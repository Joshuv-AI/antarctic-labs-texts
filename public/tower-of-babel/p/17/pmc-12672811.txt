Nomogram for predicting the overall survival and disease-specific survival of adult patients with primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type based on the SEER database
Authors: Jing Zhou, Hanyu Wang, Dan Li, Yang Cao, Gaoxiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type (PCDLBCL, LT) is a rare lymphoma with a poor prognosis. An accurate predictive model for the prognosis of these patients is lacking. Here, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 968 adult patients who were diagnosed with PCDLBCL, LT between 2000 and 2020 obtained from the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that age, marital status and radiation were independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS), while age, Ann Arbor stage and radiation were independent prognostic factors for disease-specific survival (DSS). Nomograms were constructed to predict 1-, 3- and 5-year OS and DSS. The concordance indices, receiver operating characteristic curves and calibration curves revealed that the established nomograms had good accuracy in predicting the outcomes of patients with PCDLBCL, LT. The web-based calculators were subsequently developed to provide individualized predictions for OS and DSS in patients with PCDLBCL, LT. Moreover, according to the risk scores of the nomograms, patients were divided into low-, median-, and high-risk groups. In conclusion, the nomograms and accompanying web-based survival calculators offer practical tools for individualized prediction of OS and DSS in patients with PCDLBCL, LT. Risk stratification based on these tools may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and guiding optimal treatment decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06692-5.Keywords: Primary diffuse large b-cell lymphoma, Leg type, SEER, Risk factors, Survival

Introduction

Primary cutaneous lymphomas (PCLs) are a heterogeneous group of lymphoproliferative malignancies that primarily affect the skin without evidence of lymph node, bone marrow, or other organ involvement at the time of diagnosis and account for 4% of all NHL cases annually [1]. Based on data from the United States Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry, primary cutaneous B-cell lymphomas (CBCLs) represent 25% of PCLs, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 4 cases per million people [2], and are classified into three major subgroups according to the 2008 World Health Organization and European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC): primary cutaneous marginal zone lymphoma, primary cutaneous follicle-center cell lymphoma, and primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type (PCDLBCL, LT) [3].

PCDLBCL, LT constitutes 4% of all cutaneous lymphomas and 20% of all PCBCLs, and the median age at diagnosis is 70 years [4]. The most common clinical presentation typically involves a lesion on one or both legs. The proportion of patients in whom the primary tumor appears on skin areas other than the legs is 15%−20%. According to Kraft RM et al., unlike most PCLs, PCDLBCL, LT behaves more like an aggressive lymphoma with a poor outcome and spreads to extracutaneous sites in approximately 45% of cases [5].

Due to the low incidence of PCDLBCL, LT, large clinical trials or large-scale studies are limited, and only a few studies with small sample sizes, which explore only a few variables, have been performed to investigate prognostic factors [6]. The (SEER) database contains abundant pertinent data for population-based cancer statistics, such as age, race, marital status, Ann Arbor stage, as well as details on treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, and covers approximately 30% of the United States population [7, 8]. Using data from the SEER database, we describe the clinical characteristics, prognostic factors, and treatment outcomes of PCDLBCL, LT patients and construct a prediction model for the survival of patients with PCDLBCL, LT by using a nomogram, a reliable model that can accurately predict individual survival by combining tumor-related prognostic factors [9, 10]. Based on the nomogram, we also developed a web-based prediction tool to facilitate individualized survival estimation in clinical practice. Our study aimed to provide personalized survival predictions and help clinicians select personalized treatment strategies.

Methods

Study population

Data from adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with PCDLBCL, LT were extracted from the SEER database via SEER Stat software, version 8.4.3, from 2000 to 2020. PCDLBCL was identified by the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) histologic codes (9680) and topography codes for primary cutaneous tissue (C44.0–C44.9). Staging for PCDLBCL-LT was derived from the SEER Summary Staging Manual 2000 using its standardized Summary Stage codes. These codes were systematically converted to the Ann Arbor classification – through the following mapping protocol: Localized disease (SEER code 1) corresponds to Ann Arbor Stage I; Regional disease (code 5) corresponds to Stage II or IIE and distant disease (code 7) corresponds to Stage III and IV. Patients with complete data on age at diagnosis, sex, race, Ann Arbor stage, primary site, marital status, surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, survival time, status, and cause of death were included, while those lacking any of this information or with survival time < 1 month were excluded. The endpoints of our study were overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). OS was defined as the time from diagnosis until death from any cause. DSS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death due to disease. Survival time was measured from the time of initial diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up.Institutional review board approval was not required because patient information in the SEER database is public and anonymous.

Statistical analysis

All adult PCDLBCL, LT patients were randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort at a ratio of 7:3. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using a Cox regression model based on the data of the training cohort. Subsequently, nomograms for 1-, 3- and 5-year OS as well as DSS were constructed according to the results of the multivariate analyses. To evaluate the predictive accuracy of the nomogram, the concordance index (C-index) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were applied. In addition, calibration curves with 1,000 bootstrap resampling were constructed to measure the error between the actual and predicted survival probabilities. Based on the total scores calculated by the nomogram, we divided patients into low-, median- and high-risk subgroups. Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank tests were used for between-group comparisons of survival time.

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses, nomograms, C-indices, ROC curves, calibration curves, web applications and Kaplan–Meier curves were all performed or constructed by R software (version 4.3.2) and relevant packages (“rms,” “boot,” “nomogramFormula,” “compareGroups,” “survival,” “foreign,” “caret”“survivalROC,” “survminer”, “DynNom”, “ggDCA”, “shiny”). The cutoff value was determined by X-tile software (version 3.6.1) [11] to stratify patients at different risk levels. A two-sided probability ≤ 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

From 2000 to 2020, a total of 968 patients with PCDLBCL, LT were screened from the SEER database and randomly assigned to the training cohort (n = 678) or validation cohort (n = 290). No significant difference was observed between the training and validation groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Among the included patients, most were male (52.0%), White (86.6%), and married (56.8%), and most had stage I or II disease (79.9%). The median age at diagnosis of PCDLBCL, LT was 71 years, and the patients over 80 years of age had the highest incidence (30.1%), followed by those aged 70–79 years (23.4%), 60–69 years (19.9%), 50–59 years (15.2%), and 18–49 years (11.4%). In terms of treatment, radiotherapy or chemotherapy was favored over surgery.

Independent prognostic factors

Univariate Cox analysis revealed that age, sex, race, Ann Arbor stage, marital status, surgery, and radiation significantly affected OS in the training cohort, while age, race, Ann Arbor stage, marital status, surgery, and radiation significantly affected disease-specific survival (DSS) (Table S1). These variables were further incorporated into the multivariate Cox analysis. The results showed that older age (≥ 80 years vs. <60 years: HR = 15.14; 95% CI = 8.77–26.15; P < 0.001) and being single (vs. married: HR = 1.38; 95% CI = 1.12–1.69; P = 0.003) were significantly associated with worse OS, while older age (≥ 80 years vs. <60 years: HR = 8; 95% CI = 3.82–16.80; P < 0.001) and advanced disease stage (Stage III–IV vs. Stage I–II: HR = 1.45; 95% CI = 1.04–2.01; P = 0.028) was significantly associated with worse DSS. In terms of treatment, lack of radiotherapy were significantly associated with increased mortality for both OS (HR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.04–1.66, P = 0.021) and DSS (HR = 1.82, 95% CI = 1.30–2.57, P < 0.001), indicating a survival benefit of radiotherapy (Table 2). Consistent with the multivariate Cox analysis, the results of the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that these variables were significantly associated with OS (age, marital status and radiation) and DSS (age, radiation and Ann Arbor stage) (Figure S1).

Construction and validation of the nomogram

Based on the independent prognostic factors according to the multivariate Cox analysis, we established nomograms for predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and DSS of patients with PCDLBCL, LT (Fig. 1). The accuracy of the nomograms was tested by the concordance indices (C-index). The C-index was 0.732 (95% CI: 0.708–0.757) for OS and 0.710 (0.670–0.737) for DSS in the training group, indicating the accurate discrimination ability of the nomograms. In addition, ROC curves were generated to assess the predictive ability of the nomograms. The areas under the curve (AUCs) at 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years were 0.728, 0.746, and 0.788 for OS and 0.715, 0.728, and 0.754 for DSS, respectively, in the training cohort (Fig. 2A-B), indicating the ability of the nomograms to predict survival. In addition, the calibration curve showed good agreement between the predictions and actual observations, confirming the prognostic stability of the nomograms (Fig. 3).

We further calculated the C-index, ROC curves, and calibration curves in the validation cohort to verify the nomograms. The C-index values (for OS, C-index = 0.710, 95% CI = 0.670-0.737; and for DSS, C-index = 0.738, 95% CI = 0.681-0.786), the AUC values (OS: 1-year value = 0.783, 3-year value = 0.775, 5-year value = 0.798 ; DSS: 1-year value = 0.787, 3-year value = 0.813, 5-year value = 0.824) (Figure 2C-D) and calibration curves also indicated that the prognostic nomograms we developed were effective and accurate in predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and DSS of patients with PCDLBCL, LT (Figure 4).

Risk stratification system

To further explore the predictive ability of our model, patients in the training cohort were stratified into three groups according to the scores generated by the nomograms with X-tile software: the low-risk group (total score ≤ 55.5 for OS, total score ≤ 52.0 for DSS), median-risk group (total score 55.5–95.2 for OS, total score 52.0–107.2 for DSS), and high-risk group (total score ≥ 95.2 for OS, total score ≥ 107.2 for DSS) (Fig. 5A-B). The Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed that there was a significant difference in both OS and DSS among the three groups in the training cohort (p < 0.0001). In addition, in the low-, median-, and high-risk groups, the 1-year OS rates were 95.6%, 86.5%, and 73.1%, respectively; the 3-year OS rates were 89.5%, 72.1%, and 46.1%, respectively; the 5-year OS rates were 84.9%, 59.5%, and 25.7%, respectively; the 1-year DSS rates were 96.7%, 89.4%, and 77.9%, respectively; the 3-year DSS rates were 94.1%, 78.3%, and 55.8%, respectively; and the 5-year DSS rates were 90.8%, 72.0%, and 40.7%, respectively. Significant differences in OS and DSS, along with similar 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and DSS rates, among the low-, median-, and high-risk groups were also observed in the validation cohort (Fig. 5C-F).

Development of dynamic Web-Based calculators

According to the established nomogram, we further developed a dynamic web-based survival calculator to simplify application of this nomogram (available from https://articlezhoujing.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/ for OS and https://articlezhoujing.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp-1/ for DSS) (Fig. 6). With the web-based survival rate calculator that we built, we were better able to evaluate our patients in the clinic, and thus contribute to better treatment. It is convenient to predict survival probability and disease-specific survival (DSS), along with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) by inputting their clinical features. For instance, for a 72-year-old married patient with Ann Arbor stage I and received radiotherapy, the predicted 5-year OS rate was probability 63.0% (95% CI, 57.0–71.0%), and the predicted 5-year DSS rate was probability 49.0% (95% CI, 42.0–58.0%).

Discussion

PCDLBCL, LT is a rare and aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) with a poor prognosis. The current understanding of PCDLBCL, LT relies mainly on small retrospective studies due to their low incidence. Therefore, we used the SEER database to conduct a large population-based study, and 968 patients with PCDLBCL, LT were included.

Our study revealed that the median age at diagnosis of PCDLBCL, LT was 71 years, which is similar to the data reported in previous studies [12]. Age is a well-known independent risk factor for the prognosis of various cancers [13, 14]. Our results showed that the incidence of PCDLBCL, LT increased with age, and advanced age was correlated with decreased OS and DSS, similar to previous reports on other types of DLBCL [15–17]. This correlation arises mainly because the presence of more comorbidities tends to directly impact survival time and influences treatment doses due to lower tolerability in elderly patients [18].

Marital status has been identified as an independent prognostic factor in several types of cancer, including lymphomas [18, 19]. In these malignancies, married patients tend to have better OS and DSS compared to their unmarried counterparts [20, 21]. In our study, married patients demonstrated significantly better OS than those who are single (never married, unmarried, separated, divorced, widowed or in a domestic partnership) and a tendency toward a better DSS, although no significant difference was observed. These findings are consistent with prior studies on other lymphoma subtypes, such as primary central nervous system lymphoma, primary gastrointestinal diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and mantel cell lymphoma, suggesting marital status was strongly associated with better survival outcomes [15, 16, 22].

Regarding tumor feature variables, the Ann Arbor staging system is widely used to stage NHL. We found that a high Ann Arbor stage was associated with a shorter DSS in patients with PCDLBCL, LT, as well as a tendency toward poorer OS, although no significant difference was observed.

At present, treatment approaches for primary cutaneous B-cell lymphomas (CBCLs) rely mainly on retrospective studies and expert consensus based on case reports and case series [23]. In addition to considering the type and general prognosis of the tumor (indolent versus aggressive), the patient’s age, comorbidities, and tumor location and size should be considered when choosing appropriate treatment. Due to the similar morphologic, phenotypical, and molecular genetic features as well as the clinical behavior of PCLBCL, LT and systemic DLBCL, clinicians tend to rely on the general consensus for systemic DLBCL when managing these patients [24]. Currently, R-CHOP chemotherapy followed by local radiotherapy is recommended as the first-line treatment for PCLBCL, LT [24, 25], but the treatment efficacy is still poorly understood. Given that PCDLBCL, LT predominantly affects elderly individuals (median age in the 70s), treatment decisions must account for age-related comorbidities and potential intolerance to aggressive regimens., local radiotherapy of all visible skin lesions or perhaps rituximab as single-agent therapy can be considered in such conditions. In terms of treatment, we found that radiation was significantly associated with better OS and DSS, while neither chemotherapy nor surgery had a significant effect on OS or DSS, which is similar to the findings of a previous study including 3188 patients with primary cutaneous B-cell lymphoma (CBCL) from the SEER database. In that study, there was no significant benefit of chemotherapy to the survival of patients with aggressive CBCL, including those with PCDLBCL, LT, while radiotherapy improved survival outcomes among patients with aggressive CBCLs [26]. Radiation has been considered a therapeutic option for local disease control in patients with solitary or localized disease in previous studies [27], but its role as a first-line treatment for these patients remains to be clarified. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to determine the most favorable treatment for this rare patient group with a poor prognosis.

To our knowledge, this is the first large retrospective study performed on PCDLBCL, LT to establish a predictive model. The SEER database has many advantages, such as its large population-based sample size, inclusion of a diverse population of patients, and complete survival data, which enabled us to better characterize this rare disease. The nomogram is a practical, graph-based tool that integrates clinical, pathological, and biological factors for individualized risk prediction, which are increasingly employed in the prognostic assessment of lymphoma patients [28–30]. In our study, the variables age, marital status, and radiation were included in our nomogram as independent prognostic factors for OS, while age, Ann Arbor stage, and radiation were included for DSS. Based on these variables, we constructed nomograms to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS and DSS. The models demonstrated good discriminative performance with favorable AUC values, and calibration curves indicated strong concordance between predicted and observed outcomes. Patients were subsequently stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories according to nomogram scores, which may offer useful references for clinicians to guide individualized therapeutic decisions. To improve clinical utility, we further developed dynamic web-based calculators for individualized survival prediction.

There are still some limitations in our study. First, other potential prognostic factors, such as the location and number of lesions, B symptoms and LDH levels, might have affected the survival of patients with PCDLBCL, LT, and the absence of this disease information in the SEER database could bias our results. Second, detailed chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery regimens for patients were not provided. As a result, we were unable to conduct further analysis on the impact of different treatment regimens on different risk groups. Finally, employing another independent large-scale dataset for external validation could enhance the reliability of the results. Therefore, additional high-quality studies with larger sample sizes to explore prognostic factors and treatment regimens related to PCDLBCL, LT are needed in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, age, marital status and radiation were found to be independent risk factors affecting OS in PCDLBCL, LT patients, while age, Ann Arbor stage and radiation were associated with DSS. We constructed and validated a prediction nomogram consisting of these factors that can accurately predict OS and DSS for patients with PCDLBCL, LT patients. Patients were further stratified into three risk groups to support clinical decision and personalized treatment. Moreover, web-based survival calculators for OS and DSS were created to facilitate clinical application, providing a convenient and accurate tool for prognostic evaluation.
