Translating treatment effects between correlated endpoints
Authors: Nusrat Rabbee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical researchers frequently need to answer: 'If a treatment changes endpoint 1 by X%, what change in endpoint 2 should we expect?' Current meta-analytic approaches either produce biased estimates (Daniels-Hughes) or provide correlation measures without explicit translational coefficients. We demonstrate how multivariate random-effects meta-analysis can derive the Expected Translational Association (η), which translates treatment effects across correlated endpoints, simultaneously fitting all correlated endpoints through their shared variance–covariance structure, yielding unbiased estimates and nominal confidence coverage. For convenience, we refer to this stacked implementation as SLIM (Stacked LInear Mixed Effects Model). Extensive simulations demonstrated that SLIM maintains near-zero bias and nominal coverage across scenarios, while the Daniels-Hughes approach exhibits substantial bias due to measurement-error-in-covariates, with bias persisting as sample size increases. Using the abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) dataset, we demonstrate how this approach quantifies translational associations between growth-rate and rupture-risk endpoints and enables quantitative biomarker–outcome "what if" analyses, while showing that Bayesian meta-regression approaches can underestimate risk at clinically critical ranges. The framework generalizes naturally to multivariate systems, offering a rigorous and transparent foundation for evidence synthesis in translational science.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02852-x.

Introduction

Across randomized trials, it is common practice to analyze two or more correlated aggregate-level endpoints—such as a surrogate biomarker and a definitive clinical outcome—to assess the consistency of treatment effects. Historically, this relationship has been routinely modeled using the Daniels and Hughes (DH) meta-regression approach [1, 2], which has enjoyed wide influence in surrogacy validation. Notably, the DH model rests on two critical, and often violated, assumptions: that the surrogate effect predictor is measured error-free and that the association between the surrogate and final endpoint is strictly linear. Recent work, including a notable 2025 NIH paper [3], has begun to formally quantify the bias resulting from these assumptions. Consequently, Bayesian methods employing Markov Chain Monte Chain (MCMC) offer improved heterogeneity modeling through partial pooling and informative priors. However, when implemented within a directional regression framework (regressing endpoint 2 on endpoint 1), these methods retain the underlying assumption of a parametric relationship between treatment effects. Recent applications include Inker et al. [4], who used Bayesian MCMC for a 66-trial validation of GFR slope as a surrogate for kidney failure.

The core difficulty in this type of meta-analysis is that clinical researchers must often work with aggregated data reported in the literature, which frequently includes few published studies, missing endpoint data, and significant heterogeneity in study populations and designs (see [5] and [6] for comprehensive reviews of aggregate-data heterogeneity modeling) In this setting, conventional linear mixed-effects (LME) models (such as those designed for patient-level repeated measures) are unsuitable, as they lack the fundamental mechanism to incorporate the known study-specific sample variability and covariance of the aggregate endpoint estimates. Consequently, to calculate a translational benefit—whether for designing a new study, simulating complex behavior, or achieving regulatory validation via research—requires an analytical tool capable of handling this “messy” published data without the pre-justification of a single parametric relationship between the endpoints.

To address these limitations, we propose a multivariate random-effects framework, a joint covariance-based model for multiple correlated, aggregate-level endpoints. Our framework is designed to model aggregate level data robustly, directly accommodating study-level covariates and explicitly incorporating the known sampling uncertainty (V matrix) to avoid the corruption caused by measurement error. While the Daniel Hughes method assumes a restrictive, fixed directional relationship (treatment effect on endpoint 1→ treatment effect on endpoint 2), our approach models the joint association (treatment effect on endpoint 1 ↔ treatment effect on endpoint 2), providing far greater analytical flexibility. This rigorous approach not only produces pooled estimates for each endpoint but also directly yields the Expected Translational Association (η). By providing this unbiased translational coefficient, the multivariate framework guides researchers on the precise magnitude of clinical benefit to be expected from an upstream biomarker or surrogate, thereby enabling evidence-based decision making and accelerating the understanding and proposal of disease mechanisms for future study.

Multivariate random-effects meta-analysis is well-established methodology (Berkey et al. [7]; Riley et al. [8]; van Houwelingen et al. [9]). We formalize a stacked linear mixed-effects approach that explicitly incorporates the known sampling variance–covariance matrix V, enabling extraction of translational coefficients between treatment effects. Background

Synthesizing data from multiple independently reported investigations requires a robust method for estimating the joint distribution of two or more correlated aggregate-level endpoints. This methodological challenge frequently arises when relating associations between variables, often on different scales, and across studies with varying populations or designs. The need to synthesize evidence is exemplified in manifold applications, including but not limited to, epidemiologic surveillance across centers (e.g., screening for growth rates and rupture risk rates), various clinical trials (e.g., biomarkers and clinical endpoints or an endpoint measured at different time points or a set of trial endpoints). Historically, researchers relied heavily on the DH meta-regression model devised for surrogacy validation. While influential, the DH framework is predicated on two structural assumptions that introduce a fundamental risk to scientific conclusion since: 1) the estimated effect of the outcome labeled “surrogate” is treated as an error-free predictor and 2) the underlying relationship between the two effects is structurally restricted to a simple linear form. See also DerSimonian and Laird [10] for early variance-component estimates approaches in random effects meta-analysis which has been critiqued for its method-of-moments bias and poor small-sample performance.

The statistical consequences of the restrictive assumptions of the commonly used DH model have been explored in numerous works, including a recent methodological attempt [3, 11] to correct the flaws. First, treating an estimated effect (the “surrogate”) as an error-free variable introduces measurement error bias. Second, the DH model encounters the Neyman and Scott [12] problem by treating each trial as predictor resulting in the maximum likelihood estimates of the variance components being biased. Third, the DH model imposes a linear parametric relationship between the two outcomes. While much effort has been made to correct this bias, there is a confirmation bias within the statistical domain to remain in this directional framework of surrogacy which does not serve the researcher who needs to assess the co-variation of two or more aggregate-level endpoints in a bias-free, reliable way and is less interested in directional surrogacy establishment. Also, the DH framework has often forced methodologists to abandon frequentist tools entirely and switch to Bayesian MCMC to estimate the joint distribution directly.

The complexity of these proposed solutions and workarounds stands in stark contrast to the multivariate approach we present. This framework represents a standard linear multi-level formulation that fully circumvents the Neyman and Scott problem. By modeling the joint distribution of aggregate effects and estimating relationships directly from the covariance matrix, it avoids assuming linearity or treating estimated effects as error-free fixed covariates. Its linear structure enables the use of the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator, which corrects bias in variance–component estimation. Consequently, the multivariate framework offers a clean, pragmatic and computationally feasible solution that dispenses with the complications of the DH model. This methodological robustness provides researchers with an unbiased foundation for reliably quantifying the relationships among correlated endpoints.

Model and model fitting

Multivariate meta-analysis framework for correlated endpoints

Our approach simultaneously fits all correlated endpoints using multivariate random-effects meta-analysis in a LME evidence-synthesis model. The model estimates the association between the endpoints in an exchangeable manner, explicitly avoiding the assumption of a causal, directional, or parametric relationship between endpoints. Crucially, the model incorporates the known sampling errors (V matrix) of each aggregate-level effect estimate (since they are treated as random variables), ensuring proper partitioning of variance. Comparable work (e.g., Hu & Welling 2002; [13]) either models endpoints separately or uses a vector-form multivariate likelihood,in contrast, we fit all endpoints simultaneously by stacking the data and estimating the between-study covariance G directly—without imposing a regression of one endpoint on another. We refer to this stacked linear mixed model approach as SLIM for convenience.

Input data and stacking structure

The framework accommodates heterogeneous treatment effect types as reported in trial publications. Examples include mean differences with standard errors from continuous outcomes (e.g., change in FEV1, tumor burden), log hazard ratios from time-to-event analyses (e.g., PFS, OS), log odds ratios from binary endpoints (e.g., response rate), and log rate ratios from count outcomes (e.g., exacerbation rates). In each case, the published treatment effect estimate and its standard error serve as inputs to the stacked model.

Implementation via R metafor::rma.mv()

The metafor::rma.mv() [15] function was our choice for implementing the SLIM method because it is a flexible and robust multilevel framework essential for synthesizing dependent effect sizes, regardless of whether one is working at the Individual Participant Data (IPD) or aggregate data-level. This approach enables the elegant, simultaneous fitting of multiple correlated endpoints in a single model, avoiding the well-known inefficiencies of sequential estimation. Unlike standard LME functions like nlme::lme() [16], which are limited to estimating simpler, independent error variances, rma.mv() is uniquely capable of incorporating a known variance–covariance matrix (V) of the outcomes via the dedicated V argument. This capability is crucial, as it allows the model to correctly partition the total variation into the known sampling variance and the estimated between-study heterogeneity, ensuring the true dependency structure is accounted for in the most appropriate manner for the given data (for related multilevel discussions, see [17]).

Simulation procedure and robust inference

For our proposed SLIM model in the small-K cluster setting, standard REML estimation is prone to downward bias in the standard errors of the fixed effects, a known issue that leads to inflated Type I errors [18]. To obtain robust and well-calibrated confidence intervals, we first applied Cluster-Robust Variance Estimation (CRVE) with the Satterthwaite adjustment. We then applied this identical CRVE correction to the derived mean effects of the DH model to guarantee essential methodological parity and a statistically fair comparison in our simulation.

Study-level covariates

A key strength of the SLIM framework is its ability to rigorously account for observed heterogeneity by incorporating study-level covariates directly into the model, which is a necessity when synthesizing evidence across the diverse kinds of studies found in published literature. This approach allows for formal evidence regression to adjust for differences in design factors or patient populations, providing a more precise and generalizable estimate of the overall treatment effect.

** Feasibility note**

These extensions require sufficient aggregate data. A practical rule-of-thumb is that the number of studies (K) should be at least 2–3 times the total number of parameters estimated (roughly K \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 2 × J x (1 + P), with P covariates and J endpoints).

**Clinical takeaway**

In practice, this means that adjusting for a handful of study-level factors generally requires a dozen or more trials, while very sparse evidence is best analyzed without covariate adjustment.

Applications in evidence synthesis

We are focused on modeling and inference on aggregate-level correlation of multiple endpoints in clinical studies. Often we are searching for individual study results which present the data in aggregate form, be it study-level, cohort-level, hospital-level, or center-level. From this point forward, we are asked to estimate 1) the overall mean effects for each endpoint across studies (1, 2,…), and 2) the joint co-variation among endpoints, summarized by the between-trial (R2between study), which reflects how variation in one endpoint is captured by the others while accounting for study heterogeneity and within-study variability in the aggregated estimates. This is the crux of a translational medicine question which at times need to be answered only form published literature sources of aggregate-level data with missing endpoints.

Example 1- simulations of bias and coverage

In this example, we simulate operating characteristics (bias, coverage, and RMSE) in two correlated endpoints, to contrast the performance of the multivariate framework with the traditional DH model. We took our motivation from a recent paper by [4] on meta-analysis of 66 studies of trial-level treatment effect measured on change in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) slope over 3 years as a surrogate endpoint of kidney failure. We simply used the structure of the data for each trial (a continuous surrogate endpoint [GFR slope = 3-year change due to treatment] and a continuous clinical endpoint [kidney failure = a continuous log HR (hazard ratio) due to treatment]).

For the SLIM fit, we used only a few lines of code using the R function metafor::rma.mv() to fit the model and obtain mean estimates, CI, as well as R2between study and E(Y2∣Y1). A quick check on normality distribution is necessary in this framework and it revealed that Rupture Risk needed to be log transformed (for approximately normal) and be converted to 1000 p.y. to make the two endpoints similar in range. In addition, a mice() imputation was required to incorporate centers (like Bournesmouth) that reported only one outcome, whereas this step was not necessary for the simple SLIM framework.

The Bayesian MEM model was fit using R brms::brm() to provide a comparison that is closer to the spirit of the authors, Thompson et al. who conducted the meta-analysis of the actual aggregate and IPD-level data available from the 18 centers. While this method mirrors the model fitting and results of Thompson et al. our SLIM differs in two key respects: a) it operates entirely at the aggregate level without requiring individual data and b) it does not impose a parametric or linear association between endpoints, only a variance–covariance structure of random effects.

We see from the table and graph (Fig. 3) above that the Bayesian MEM shrinks sparse groups toward the overall mean, whereas our SLIM framework recovers estimates close to the observed group averages, particularly at larger diameters where rupture data may not have a linear relationship with growth rate, thereby preserving face validity. The key practical output is answering specific translational questions. For the AAA data, we can quantify: 'If growth rate reduces by X%, what happens to rupture risk?' The translational coefficient η = τ₁₂/τ₁₁ enables these predictions with confidence intervals. Notably, at higher aneurysm diameters where clinical decisions are most critical, we observe that Bayesian meta-regression approaches systematically underestimate rupture risk, while SLIM preserves observed risk patterns.

The AAA example crystalized that our SLIM framework is a pragmatic approach that works with minimal assumptions, and aligns closely with observed data, and avoids unnecessary shrinkage, while the Bayesian MME method demonstrated the trade-offs of priors and stronger parametric (e.g., bivariate normal) coupling. The SLIM approach, while requiring the endpoints follow a roughly normal distribution at the aggregate-level, can indeed model diverse endpoints—such as rates, slopes, hazard ratios—without imposing a structural relationship, yielding estimates close to the observed study-level averages while remaining flexible across disease areas and endpoints, missing data, interpretable coefficients of covariation, and quantifiable translation coefficient between endpoints.

Discussion

Novel contributions and relationship to existing methods

The underlying statistical approach—multivariate random-effects meta-analysis with known within-study covariance—is established methodology (Berkey et al. [7], Riley et al. [8]). Our contributions are: (i) explicit formulation of the Expected Translational Association (η) to answer 'what if' questions that clinical teams routinely ask, (ii) systematic comparison showing this approach outperforms the widely-used but biased Daniels-Hughes method, and (iii) practical demonstration for regulatory and planning applications. Previous multivariate meta-analysis applications report correlation structures but do not explicitly extract translational coefficients in the form needed for sample size planning and regulatory submissions. We bridge this gap between statistical output and translational decision-making.

Study-level covariates

SLIM can also accommodate study-level covariates (e.g., drug class, dose arm, trial phase), provided the number of studies is sufficient, typically at least two to three times the number of parameters across endpoints.

Accurate and unbiased model estimates

The SLIM framework not only improves bias and coverage relative to traditional models (Example 1) but also allows direct translational interpretation (referenced as η (ETA): what an observed percent change in one endpoint implies for the expected percent change in another, with confidence intervals (Example 2). This expectational mapping makes synthesis clinically meaningful, rather than purely statistical.

Heterogeneous endpoints

Clinical research inherently involves heterogenous endpoints, such as biomarkers, time to event data, event rates, and response frequencies, and it is important to have a framework that handles most of the common data types, as well as handle them properly in the model so that effect estimates are not de/inflated in edge cases. The AAA (Example 2) data we created and analyzed shows that even with heterogeneous endpoints, such as growth rates and event risks, the framework estimates these translations reliably, while Bayesian approaches tend to impose artificial shrinkage that can obscure important edge-case signals. In clinical research, where data are often incomplete, inconsistent, or heterogenous, this flexibility is crucial.

Treatment effects

Treatment effects across different endpoints in clinical studies are standardly reported in the literature with means and standard errors, as most studies follow a standard asymptotic inferential framework to report statistical significance. Thus, SLIM expects these treatment effects—reported as means with standard errors (e.g., mean difference ± SE, log(HR) ± SE, log(OR) ± SE, log(RR) ± SE)—as inputs to its modeling framework. This aligns with conventional meta-analysis practice across endpoint types.

Handing missing data

SLIM naturally accommodates trial-level missingness when treatment effects are reported on some but not all endpoints. By contrast, directional regression approaches like Daniels-Hughes require both endpoints for every trial, forcing complete-case analysis that excludes valuable data. SLIM accepts trials reporting treatment effects on only endpoint 1 or only endpoint 2; these trials contribute to estimation of G through the multivariate likelihood, without requiring imputation or complete-case analysis. This is important because missingness at the trial level is rarely at random—publication bias implies studies are more likely to report endpoint pairs when correlated and showing strong treatment effects. Simply imputing missing endpoint values before analysis can therefore bias the data representation.

Extension to multiple treatment effects

A key strength of SLIM is its natural extension to J > 2 treatment effects. This multi-endpoint capability is essential for modern evidence synthesis, where regulatory decisions increasingly require integration of biomarker dynamics, radiographic progression, and survival outcomes within a unified framework. It shows in practice how our SLIM framework works with minimal assumptions, aligns closely with observed data, and avoids artificial shrinkage, while the Bayesian MEM version demonstrates the trade-offs of priors and stronger parametric coupling. It is a vivid, real-world proof that our approach is both robust and practical. The computation and coding burden was minimal via the metafor::rma.mv() function.

The simulation exercise of data like GFR slope change and kidney failure (example 1) exemplified that the DH framework assumes a parametric regression of the effect on Endpoint 2 given noisy Endpoint 1, inducing bias in the model estimated slopes. This approach shows high bias and its coverage fails to succeed to meet the nominal coverage rate of 95% in none of the simulation scenarios (0%). Changing the estimator of the DH approach from frequentist REML to Bayesian MCMC of that same linear parametric regression framework would not resolve the fundamental mis-specification—it primarily alters uncertainty quantification (via priors and partial pooling) rather than the directional mean structure. The issue is the imposed linearity assumption, not the inference method. By contrast, SLIM estimates a joint covariance structure for the endpoints without enforcing parametric relationships, preserving unbiasedness even under decoupled scenarios (μ2 = 0) while borrowing strength through random-effects covariance.

Conclusions

We have proposed the SLIM method as a pragmatic translational framework for synthesizing multiple correlated, aggregate-level endpoints. Our findings conclusively show that the DH method encounters model misspecifications with assumptions of an error-free surrogate predictor and a linear association between a surrogate and clinical endpoints which in turn results in substantial estimation bias, high RMSE, and low coverage. In contrast, our SLIM model shows near-perfect accuracy with negligible bias and superior precision. By correctly partitioning the within- and between-study variance using the V matrix, the model avoids corruption by measurement error and provides a robust, non-directional model of joint association.

The SLIM framework is thus positioned to be the preferred analytical choice for evidence-synthesis of aggregate-level correlated endpoints. Recent high-profile applications [4] demonstrate the continued use of computationally intensive Bayesian meta-regression,SLIM offers a simpler and more accurate alternative for normally distributed endpoints. This rigorous approach is scalable to multiple, exchangeable correlated endpoints and is well-calibrated for small number of trials. The LME naturally accommodates missing endpoints, eliminating the need for explicit imputation, a necessary safeguard against propagating unobserved publication bias where studies with weaker effects are selectively dropped. Furthermore, SLIM provides an optimal balance between noise reduction and fidelity to the true study-level heterogeneity; unlike Bayesian models which often induce excessive shrinkage and need missing data imputation as a pre-step, our frequentist approach yields estimates that remain close to the observed study-level means. Crucially, our framework provides the Expected Translational Association (η), which is an unbiased metric that converts joint statistical correlation into a direct, predictive relationship between endpoints, which is vital for clinical decision-making and regulatory or policy-level influence. These considerations align with contemporary regulatory guidance emphasizing model transparency and reproducibility [22, 23].
