Predicting household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa using supervised machine learning analysis of DHS data from 28 countries
Authors: Lidetu Demoze, Angwach Abrham Asnake, Alemayehu Kasu Gebrehana, Natnael Gizachew, Gelila Yitageasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Approximately 85% of the population in sub-Saharan Africa equating to around 894 million people depend on traditional biomass fuels such as firewood, charcoal, and agricultural waste for their cooking needs. Understanding what shapes cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa is essential for supporting clean energy transitions and advancing national policies and global goals, including Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3, 5, 7, and 13. Therefore, this study aimed to predict the key drivers of household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa using supervised machine learning techniques. This study analyzed the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data collected between 2015 and 2024 from 28 sub-Saharan African countries (N = 430,811 households) to predict cooking fuel choice using supervised machine learning. The DHS employs a multi-stage, stratified cluster sampling design, and household sampling weights were applied throughout the analysis to account for unequal probabilities of selection and non-response. Seven supervised learning models -Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Logistic Regression (LR), AdaBoost, Naive Bayes, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) were trained on 80% of the data, with 20% reserved for testing. The dataset was highly imbalanced, with a 5.4:1 ratio of unclean to clean fuels, so we applied the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) during model training to address this imbalance and implemented 10-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) metrics. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were used to identify key factors influencing the predictions. This study revealed that 84.40% of households in sub-Saharan Africa relied on unclean fuels for cooking, with significant disparities across household characteristics. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving a mean accuracy of 80.43% (95% CI: 80.17–80.67%) and a mean AUC of 0.8987 (95% CI: 0.8962– 0.9012), outperforming other machine learning algorithms. SHAP analysis identified electricity as the highest impact variable, followed by residence, TV ownership, highest education status, and wealth index. The analysis demonstrated that unclean fuels use for cooking remains highly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa. XGBoost outperformed other models in predicting cooking fuel choice. Governments in sub-Saharan Africa should prioritize improving electricity access, reducing rural–urban disparities, expanding education, and strengthening household economic conditions to promote cleaner cooking fuels. The predictive model can help policymakers and development organizations identify populations most at risk of relying on polluting fuels, enabling targeted and cost-effective interventions such as electrification programs, clean fuel subsidies, and awareness campaigns promoting clean cooking technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44937-z.

Introduction

Using solid/unclean fuels like wood, crop residues, dung, charcoal, and coal for cooking especially over open fires or with basic stoves exposes people in the household to levels of air pollution that fall between the exposure levels of second hand smoke and actively smoking1. Globally, approximately 2.1 billion people, a third of the population, cook with open fires or inefficient stoves using fuels like kerosene, biomass, and coal, causing harmful household air pollution that contributed to an estimated 3.2 million deaths in 2020, including over 237,000 child deaths, and, combined with ambient air pollution, is linked to 6.7 million premature deaths annually2.

The choice between clean and polluting cooking fuels remains a critical public health and environmental issue in sub-Saharan Africa, where a significant proportion of households still rely on traditional biomass fuels, contributing to Indoor Air Pollution, deforestation, and adverse health outcomes3.

The reliance on polluting cooking fuels remains alarmingly high across sub-Saharan Africa, with approximately 85% of the population equating to around 894 million people depending on traditional biomass fuels such as firewood, charcoal, and agricultural waste for their cooking needs4. Country-level data reveal striking disparities in cooking fuel use across sub-Saharan Africa. In Ethiopia, a staggering 92.1% of households utilize biomass fuels, while in Nigeria, this figure stands at 69%5,6.​

Urban households are more likely to adopt cleaner fuels compared to rural households in sub-Saharan Africa. For instance, in Cameroon, 66% of peri-urban households use liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) as their primary cooking fuel, whereas only 16% of rural households do the same7. Household cooking fuel choices in sub-Saharan Africa are shaped by income, education, household size, age of the households, ownership, location, and cultural preferences8–11. Wealthier, urban, and more educated households tend to use cleaner fuels like LPG, while poorer and rural households rely on biomass due to cost and access barriers8–11.

The use of polluting cooking fuels (biomass fuels), such as wood, charcoal, and animal dung has significant adverse effects on health, the environment, and socio-economic development. Health-wise, household air pollution (HAP) from these fuels is a leading risk factor for respiratory diseases, contributing to approximately 4 million deaths annually worldwide, with women and children being the most affected due to their prolonged exposure during cooking activities12–14.

Understanding the determinants of household cooking fuel choice is critical for informing national clean cooking strategies and advancing global development commitments. Transitioning from biomass to cleaner fuels directly supports multiple policy frameworks, including national energy and health sector plans, the Africa Clean Cooking Energy Solutions initiative, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), and SDG 13 (Climate Action)15–17. While previous studies have explored some determinants using traditional statistical methods, there is a notable lack of research applying supervised machine learning techniques to large-scale, multi-country datasets such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS)11,18–20. Although several studies in sub-Saharan Africa have used DHS data to examine cooking fuel use8,9,21, most have relied on conventional statistical techniques such as logistic or multinomial regression, which may not fully capture complex, nonlinear relationships among predictors.

Machine learning has increasingly been applied in environmental and public health research to improve prediction accuracy and uncover complex patterns in population data. Recent studies have used supervised machine learning methods to examine sanitation facilities, tobacco use, antenatal care, and skilled birth attendance, demonstrating the growing relevance of these approaches in analyzing health-related behaviors and service utilization22–26, but only a limited number of studies have applied supervised machine learning methods to household energy choices in the region, and none have done so using large, multi-country DHS datasets covering nearly a decade. This lack of advanced predictive modeling highlights a research gap in understanding how diverse socioeconomic, demographic, and infrastructural factors interact to shape household cooking fuel choices across sub-Saharan Africa, and this study provides evidence to inform targeted policies and interventions that promote clean cooking solutions while reducing associated health and environmental risks. Beyond identifying statistical associations, the predictive model developed in this study provides a practical tool for policymakers and development partners to identify households and population segments most likely to rely on polluting fuels. By highlighting key drivers such as electricity access, education level, and household wealth, the model can inform targeted interventions including electrification programs, clean cooking subsidies, and education-based behavioral campaigns thereby improving the efficiency and impact of clean energy policies in sub-Saharan Africa.

Addressing this gap is crucial for generating more accurate and policy-relevant insights to support clean cooking transitions in sub-Saharan Africa. Previous studies have largely relied on conventional statistical techniques, such as multilevel regression models, which typically assume linear relationships and require predefined interaction terms. These assumptions may limit their ability to capture complex and nonlinear dynamics in household energy decisions. For example, the influence of electricity access on clean fuel adoption may vary substantially across wealth groups, where electricity availability may encourage clean fuel use among wealthier households but have limited effects among poorer households due to affordability constraints. Similarly, the combined effects of education and media exposure, such as television ownership, may interact in ways that influence awareness and adoption of clean cooking technologies. Machine learning methods are better suited to capture such nonlinear relationships and complex interactions without imposing strict parametric assumptions27. Therefore, this study applies supervised machine learning techniques to identify and predict the key determinants of cooking fuel choice using Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data in sub-Saharan Africa, providing deeper insights into the drivers of household energy choices.

Materials and methods

Study period and setting

This study utilized the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data collected between 2015 and 2024 from 28 sub-Saharan African countries, namely: Angola, Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia, Gabon, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Kenya, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mali, Mauritania, Mozambique, Nigeria, Rwanda, Senegal, Sierra Leone, South Africa, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe (Fig. 1). sub-Saharan Africa is a vast and diverse region encompassing all African countries south of the Sahara Desert. It is characterized by a complex tapestry of cultures, languages, and histories, with landscapes ranging from dense rainforests and savannas to deserts and major river systems like the Niger and the Congo28. The region faces significant challenges, including some of the world’s highest rates of poverty, disease burden, and political instability, often exacerbated by the legacies of colonialism and climate change29,30. However, it is also a region of immense dynamism and potential, boasting a rapidly growing, youthful population, rich natural resources, and vibrant economies that are increasingly connected to global markets31.

Data source and sample size

The data used in this study were extracted from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), a nationally representative household survey program implemented in low- and middle-income countries32. The selection of countries and the study period was primarily based on the availability of the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data. Our objective was to include sub-Saharan African countries with publicly available, nationally representative, and methodologically comparable DHS datasets that allowed harmonized analysis across countries using similar indicators and survey designs. The DHS collects standardized data on health, nutrition, and population indicators using validated questionnaires administered through face-to-face interviews33,34.

For this analysis, we utilized the household recode (HR) files from the most recent surveys conducted between 2015 and 2024 across 28 sub-Saharan African countries. The dataset includes information on household characteristics, cooking fuel type, access to utilities, and socioeconomic indicators, providing a robust foundation for examining determinants of cooking fuel choice. The primary outcome variable household cooking fuel type was derived from the DHS household questionnaire item that asks: “What type of fuel does your household mainly use for cooking?” Response options typically include electricity, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), natural gas, biogas, kerosene/paraffin, coal/lignite, charcoal, firewood, straw/shrubs/grass, agricultural crop residues, and animal dung. In this study, these responses were categorized into clean fuels (electricity, LPG, natural gas, and biogas) and unclean fuels (kerosene/paraffin, coal/lignite, charcoal, firewood, crop residues, straw/grass, and animal dung) following previous DHS-based studies.

Access to electricity was determined using the DHS household question: “Does this household have electricity?” with response options coded as yes or no. Household ownership of assets was measured using standard DHS asset questions, including “Does any member of this household own a television?” and “Does any member of this household own a radio?” both recorded as binary variables (yes/no). Socioeconomic status was represented by the DHS wealth index, a composite indicator constructed by DHS using principal component analysis based on household asset ownership, housing materials, water supply, sanitation facilities, and other durable goods. The data used in this study are publicly available at https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm. The sample sizes varied by country, with the largest from Nigeria (40,427) and the smallest from Gambia (6,549), totaling 430,811 household observations (Table 1).

Sampling procedure

The Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) utilize a multi-stage, stratified cluster sampling design to ensure nationally representative estimates. In the first stage, census enumeration areas (EAs), known as primary sampling units (PSUs), are selected using probability proportional to size (PPS) based on recent national census frames35–38. In the second stage, a fixed number of households are systematically selected within each EA. Stratification is typically done by urban and rural areas, and sampling weights are applied to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection and non-response, allowing for unbiased national and subnational estimates39. Specifically, the weight variable was generated in Stata using the command gen wgt = hv005/1,000,000 to scale the DHS sampling weights appropriately. Weighted analyses were then conducted to reflect the population structure, including calculating the distribution of cooking fuel types by cluster.

Data management and analysis procedure

Data extraction was conducted using STATA version 17, then converted to CSV and analyzed in Python 3.12 via Jupyter Notebook. In this study, the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation technique was applied to estimate missing values for the independent variables. The percentage of missingness per variable was generally low, ranging from 0.5% to 3.2%. Missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm, with k set to 5 and using the Euclidean distance metric for similarity between observations. This approach ensured that the imputed values were consistent with the observed data distributions and preserved the integrity of the dataset for subsequent machine learning analyses. Previous research has shown that KNN imputation is a more reliable and sensitive method for handling missing data40. Descriptive statistics summarized sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and media exposure data using frequencies and percentages. To predict cooking fuel choice, the study followed Yufeng Guo’s 7 Steps of Machine Learning, using Scikit-learn for model implementation41.

Model development and validation

Target and input variables for model analysis

The target variable was the type of cooking fuel used by households in sub-Saharan African countries. It is classified into clean fuels and unclean fuels. Clean fuels include electricity, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), natural gas, and biogas, which are considered cleaner in terms of air quality and health impacts, as they produce fewer indoor air pollutants compared to traditional fuels. Unclean fuels include biomass fuels such as coal or lignite, charcoal, firewood, straw/shrubs/grass, agricultural crop residues, animal dung, and kerosene/paraffin, which are associated with higher levels of household air pollution8,42–45.

The selection of input variables for this study was informed by both theoretical relevance and evidence from previous literature examining household welfare, socioeconomic determinants, and related health and living-condition outcomes. The input variables included in the analysis were the sex of the household head (male or female), age of the household head (< 35, 35–60, and > 60 years), and the household’s wealth index (categorized as poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest)8,45. Educational level was classified as no education, primary, secondary, or higher, while marital status included never married, married, widowed, or divorced8,45,46. The place of residence was identified as either rural or urban. Household size was categorized as households with four or fewer members (≤ 4) and those with more than four members (> 4)8,45. The number of children under five was grouped as no child, 1–2 children, or three or more children (≥ 3)8,45. Additionally, variables assessing access to basic amenities included access to electricity, radio, and television (each coded as yes or no)1,45. Lastly, the place where food is cooked was categorized as inside the house, in a separate building, or outdoors1,8,46(Table 2).

Model calibration and validation procedures

Data processing involved cleaning, transformation, integration, dimensionality reduction, splitting, discretization, and feature engineering. Model validation was conducted to evaluate the performance and generalizability of the machine learning models. After preprocessing and feature engineering, the dataset was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. A 10-fold cross-validation approach was applied on the training set to internally validate the model and optimize hyperparameters, ensuring stable and unbiased performance estimates47.

In this study, we employed the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to address the issue of unequal class distributions in the dataset, which can significantly impact the performance of machine learning models. When one class is underrepresented compared to the others, the model tends to favor the majority class, often resulting in poor predictive performance for the minority class. SMOTE was used to improve model performance and ensure more accurate predictions of cooking fuel choice22,48,49.

Model selection and training

In this study, supervised machine learning algorithms were chosen for model development, given the labeled nature of the dataset, which aimed to predict cooking fuel choice based on a set of input features. Seven algorithms were implemented during the training phase: Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Logistic Regression (LR), AdaBoost, Naive Bayes, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN).

To optimize model performance and ensure fair comparison across algorithms, we conducted hyperparameter tuning using Randomized Search with 10-fold cross-validation. Randomized Search was selected due to its superior computational efficiency when working with large datasets and models with extensive parameter spaces such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and Artificial Neural Networks50. This approach enabled the algorithms to sample from a wide range of hyperparameter combinations, allowing efficient exploration of the search space while reducing computational burden.

Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a 10-fold cross-validation strategy on the training dataset. For the XGBoost model, the number of trees ranged from 100 to 300, maximum tree depth from 3 to 7, learning rate from 0.01 to 0.10, subsampling and column subsampling ratios from 0.7 to 1.0, and the gamma regularization parameter from 0 to 0.2. For Random Forest, the number of trees ranged from 200 to 500, maximum tree depth was unrestricted or limited to 10 and 20, minimum samples for node splitting ranged from 2 to 5, minimum samples per leaf from 1 to 2, and feature selection at each split used either the square root or logarithm of the total features. Logistic Regression was tuned using regularization strength values (C) between 0.01 and 10 with L1 and L2 penalties. The artificial neural network consisted of two hidden layers with 64 and 32 neurons using (Rectified Linear Unit) ReLU activation functions and a sigmoid output layer, trained with the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001), batch size of 32, and 30–50 epochs. For the Decision Tree model, maximum depth ranged from 3 to 20, minimum samples for splitting from 2 to 10, and minimum samples per leaf from 1 to 5. AdaBoost was tuned with 50 to 300 estimators and learning rates between 0.01 and 1.0, while Gaussian Naive Bayes used variance smoothing values between 1e-9 and 1e-7 (Table 3).

Random forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees and combines their outputs to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting.

Decision tree (DT) is a simple, tree-based model that splits data into branches to make predictions based on feature values.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGB) is a powerful and efficient boosting algorithm that builds decision trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects errors made by the previous ones.

Logistic regression (LR) is a statistical model used for binary or multiclass classification that estimates the probability of an outcome based on input features.

AdaBoost is another boosting algorithm that combines multiple weak learners, typically shallow decision trees, to form a strong classifier by focusing more on misclassified instances.

Naive Bayes is a probabilistic model based on Bayes’ theorem, assuming independence between features, often used for text classification and spam detection.

Artificial neural networks (ANN) are inspired by the structure of the human brain and consist of layers of interconnected nodes (neurons) that can learn complex nonlinear relationships in data, making them suitable for a wide range of classification and regression tasks.

Each of this models have specific strengths and limitations. Random Forest (RF) handles high-dimensional data and provides variable importance but is less interpretable and computationally intensive. Decision Tree (DT) is simple and interpretable but prone to overfitting. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) achieves high accuracy and handles missing data well but requires careful tuning. Logistic Regression (LR) is efficient and interpretable for linear patterns but may underperform with nonlinear relationships. AdaBoost enhances weak learners but is sensitive to noise. Naive Bayes is fast and suitable for high-dimensional data, though it assumes predictor independence. Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) capture complex nonlinear patterns but need large datasets, extensive tuning, and are less interpretable. Using this combination allowed the study to leverage complementary strengths and mitigate individual limitations.

Justification for the selection of machine learning models

Logistic regression (LR) was included as a baseline statistical model because of its simplicity and interpretability in estimating the relationship between predictors and the outcome variable. It has been widely used in public health and socioeconomic studies using survey data such as DHS to examine determinants of household behaviors. However, it assumes linear relationships and may not capture complex nonlinear patterns in the data51.

Decision tree (DT) was selected due to its ability to model nonlinear relationships and interactions between socioeconomic variables such as wealth, education, and electricity access while remaining easy to interpret for policy-related research. However, decision trees are prone to overfitting and may produce unstable results with small changes in the dataset52.

Random forest (RF) was included as an ensemble learning method that improves prediction accuracy by combining multiple decision trees and reducing overfitting. It performs well with structured survey datasets containing many socioeconomic variables, such as those in DHS data. Although it generally performs well with such data, its interpretability is lower than that of simpler models53.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) was selected because of its strong predictive performance and ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships among household characteristics and energy access variables. This makes it particularly suitable for predicting cooking fuel choice in large structured datasets. However, it requires careful hyperparameter tuning and is less transparent in interpretation54.

Artificial neural networks (ANN) were included to capture complex nonlinear patterns among socioeconomic variables, household assets, and cooking practices. Such models are useful when relationships between predictors and outcomes are highly complex, although they require more computational resources and are often less interpretable55.

AdaBoost was selected as a boosting technique that improves model performance by focusing on previously misclassified observations during training. This can improve prediction accuracy when analyzing heterogeneous household survey data. However, it can be sensitive to noisy data and outliers56.

Naïve Bayes (NB) was included due to its computational efficiency and strong performance in classification tasks with high-dimensional data such as household survey variables. Its limitation is the assumption of independence among predictors, which may not always hold in real-world socioeconomic datasets57.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was assessed using several evaluation metrics: overall accuracy (proportion of correct predictions), precision (correctness of positive predictions), recall (ability to identify all relevant cases), F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), which measures classification performance across all threshold values.

Model interpretation

To enhance model interpretability and identify key factors influencing the predictions, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated. SHAP analysis provided a detailed ranking of feature importance by quantifying each variable’s contribution to model outputs. This approach enhanced our ability to identify the most influential factors driving the use of clean versus unclean fuels.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of households in sub-Saharan Africa

A total of 430,811 households participated in this study. Household characteristics varied widely across the region, with most households headed by individuals aged 35–60 years and a majority being male-headed. Education levels were generally low, with one-third of households having no formal schooling. Nearly half of households had more than four members, and rural residence was predominant. These patterns provide important socioeconomic context that aligns with known structural disadvantages influencing energy access in sub-Saharan Africa (Table 4).

The distribution of clean and unclean fuel utilization across different categories

The use of clean fuels was consistently higher among wealthier, more educated, urban, and electrified households. In contrast, poorer, rural, and less educated households overwhelmingly depended on unclean fuels. The trend also showed clear media related and infrastructural gradients households with electricity, TV ownership, or indoor kitchens were more likely to adopt modern fuels. These patterns reinforce long-standing socioeconomic and spatial inequalities in energy access across the region (Table 6).

Comparison of AUC across models

To statistically compare the discriminative performance of the models, pairwise comparisons of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were conducted using the DeLong test. The DeLong test comparing XGBoost vs. ANN yielded a Z-statistic of 1.25 and a p-value of 0.20, which is greater than the standard 0.05 significance level. This indicates that the small difference in AUC between XGBoost (0.8988) and ANN (0.8984) is not statistically significant. In other words, although XGBoost has a slightly higher AUC numerically, the difference could be due to random variation in the sample rather than a true performance difference between the models. Practically, this suggests that XGBoost and ANN have comparable predictive performance in this dataset. The Random Forest model (AUC = 0.8642) showed a statistically significant higher performance than the Decision Tree model (AUC = 0.8357, p < 0.001) but was outperformed by XGBoost (AUC = 0.8988, p < 0.001), Artificial Neural Network (ANN; AUC = 0.8984, p < 0.001), AdaBoost (AUC = 0.8870, p < 0.001), and Naive Bayes (AUC = 0.8798, p < 0.001). The Decision Tree model performed significantly worse than all other models (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Among the top-performing models, no significant difference was observed between XGBoost and ANN (p = 0.208), while XGBoost outperformed both AdaBoost (p < 0.001) and Naive Bayes (p < 0.001). Similarly, ANN achieved significantly higher AUC than AdaBoost (p < 0.001) and Naive Bayes (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study advances household air pollution (HAP) research in sub-Saharan Africa by applying supervised machine learning to predict household cooking fuel choice, an approach that remains under-utilised in this field. To our knowledge, this is among the first region-wide studies to leverage supervised machine learning models to systematically identify, rank, and quantify the relative importance of socio-economic, demographic, and contextual determinants shaping the adoption of clean versus solid cooking fuels. While previous studies in the region have largely relied on conventional regression-based methods to examine cooking fuel choice3,8,10,18,58, such approaches are limited in their ability to capture complex, non-linear relationships and interactions among predictors. By contrast, the machine learning framework employed in this study enables a more nuanced understanding of fuel choice behaviour, revealing key drivers of clean fuel adoption and persistence of solid fuel use that may be overlooked using traditional analytical techniques. These findings offer new methodological and empirical insights with direct relevance for designing targeted and data-driven interventions to reduce HAP exposure in sub-Saharan Africa.

By integrating boosting and bagging algorithms, hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation, SMOTE balancing, and SHAP interpretability, the study captures complex nonlinear relationships that conventional models often overlook. This approach delivers more accurate predictions and clearer insights into key drivers such as electricity access, residence, wealth, education, and media exposure providing new, data-driven evidence to inform cleaner cooking policies in the region. The models also provided clearer variable importance rankings, revealing the relative contribution of socio-demographic and environmental factors with greater precision. These analytical strengths offer new insights beyond earlier work and demonstrate the added value of machine learning in understanding household fuel use patterns in sub-Saharan Africa.

This pioneering work lays the foundation for more precise and effective energy policy interventions in the region, facilitating a targeted transition to cleaner cooking technologies and addressing energy poverty in sub-Saharan Africa. Furthermore, our findings help identify the populations most likely to rely on unclean fuels, and therefore most at risk of Indoor Air Pollution. Understanding these predictors supports targeted policies to expand access to cleaner fuels among poorer and rural households. Such interventions can reduce exposure to harmful pollutants and lower IAP-related health burdens, contributing to improved household air quality and public health in sub-Saharan Africa.

Household fuel choice in the present analysis is strongly shaped by socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. Consistent with the energy transition framework, households with higher wealth status, urban residence, and access to electricity exhibited a substantially higher prevalence of clean fuel use, reflecting both affordability and infrastructure availability. Educational attainment also emerged as a key determinant, with increasing levels of education associated with a greater likelihood of transitioning to clean fuels, likely due to enhanced awareness of health risks and improved economic opportunities. Although male-headed households demonstrated higher overall fuel consumption, they were more likely to rely on unclean fuels compared with female-headed households, suggesting gender-related differences in household energy decision-making. Larger household size and the presence of young children were inversely associated with clean fuel adoption, possibly reflecting higher energy demands and cost constraints. Ownership of household assets, such as televisions, was positively associated with clean fuel use, indicating the role of socioeconomic status and exposure to information in shaping energy choices. While the location of cooking showed limited variation, households cooking indoors exhibited a slightly higher use of clean fuels, potentially reflecting safety considerations and housing characteristics. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) model emerged as the best-fitting model, achieving the highest accuracy (0.8042) and AUC (0.8988), along with strong precision (0.9696) and an F1 score of 0.8725. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) violin plot highlights the key household characteristics influencing cooking fuel choices in sub-Saharan Africa, distinguishing between clean fuels (such as electricity, LPG, and biogas) and unclean fuels (like firewood, charcoal, and dung).

Exceptionally high levels of unclean cooking fuel use were observed in Burundi, Madagascar, Sierra Leone, Malawi, Liberia, and Guinea, where more than 95% of households rely on polluting fuels, indicating a near-universal dependence on traditional biomass energy. Research has shown that in much of rural sub-Saharan Africa, low household income and the high cost of clean fuels and appliances constrain transitions away from traditional biomass, even where grid electricity exists, because both electric cooking appliances and LPG are unaffordable for poor households and supply chains are underdeveloped19,59–61. Furthermore, limited electrification, poor road and market access reduce the availability of modern cooking fuels, making locally accessible biomass fuels (wood, charcoal, dung) the default choice despite health and environmental harms18,60–62. Cultural practices and long-standing cooking norms further reinforce the use of traditional fuels, as households often prefer fuels compatible with traditional stoves and local cooking methods59–62.

Electricity emerged as the most important primary predictor of cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. Access to electricity plays a crucial role in promoting clean cooking fuel adoption, as it enables the use of electric stoves and signals broader socioeconomic development. The International Energy Agency reports that households with reliable electricity are more likely to move away from traditional biomass fuels63. Similarly, several studies highlight electricity as a key driver of fuel switching in developing countries8,10,64. However, it is important to note that electricity availability alone may not guarantee clean cooking uptake, as high tariffs, unstable supply, and the cost of electric appliances can limit the feasibility of using electricity for cooking. Thus, the affordability and reliability of electricity act as moderating factors, influencing whether households with access can realistically adopt electricity-based cooking. The World Health Organization also recognizes electricity access as essential for achieving Sustainable Development Goal 7, which aims for universal access to modern energy services2.

Similarly, place of residence was an important predictor of cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. Urban households are significantly more likely to use clean fuels such as electricity, LPG, or natural gas, while rural households predominantly rely on traditional biomass like firewood and charcoal. This disparity is largely due to better infrastructure, higher income levels, and greater access to clean energy options in urban areas65–68. In addition, access to television was another important predictor of cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. A plausible explanation for this finding is that television serves as a key channel for disseminating information about the health risks associated with unclean fuels and the benefits of clean cooking alternatives. Studies have shown that households with access to mass media are more likely to adopt modern energy technologies due to increased awareness and exposure to government or NGO-led energy campaigns69,70.

Moreover, education was another important predictor of cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. This could be due to educated individuals are more likely to be aware of the health hazards of traditional unclean fuels and the advantages of cleaner alternatives such as electricity, LPG, or biogas. Education also correlates with better income opportunities, enabling households to afford modern and cleaner cooking technologies. Several other studies have also highlighted the impact of education on cooking fuel choice10,69,71,72. Additionally, the wealth index emerged as a significant factor influencing cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. This could be due to wealthier households are more likely to afford clean fuels such as LPG, electricity, or biogas, while poorer households tend to rely on unclean fuels like firewood and charcoal due to their lower cost and greater accessibility. Likewise, other research has demonstrated how wealth affects the choice of cooking fuel10,19,70.

Additionally, two key factors that influence the choice of cooking fuel in sub-Saharan Africa are household size and the number of children under the age of five. Larger households, especially those with young children, typically require more energy for cooking, often leading them to opt for affordable and readily available fuels such as firewood or charcoal. While these traditional fuels are less clean, they are generally more economical for families with higher cooking demands. In contrast, cleaner alternatives like electricity or liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) can be costly and may not adequately meet the needs of large households. Furthermore, cultural practices such as preparing large meals for extended family members also influence the preference for fuels that are compatible with traditional cooking methods18,73,74.

Household access to radio is another important factor influencing the choice of cooking fuel in sub-Saharan Africa. This could be due to the fact that awareness campaigns broadcast via radio can promote behavioral change by informing listeners about government subsidies, improved cooking technologies, and available support programs. Moreover, households with access to radio are often more informed and connected, which tends to correlate with better socioeconomic status. This, in turn, increases their ability to afford and adopt cleaner cooking fuels75,76. Marital status is also a factor influencing the choice of cooking fuel in sub-Saharan Africa. Married individuals often live in larger households with children, increasing cooking demands and influencing a shift toward affordable fuels like firewood or charcoal. Shared financial resources in married households can also support investment in cleaner fuels like LPG or electricity. Additionally, cultural and gender roles may affect fuel preferences, with married women prioritizing safety, efficiency, and family needs. Thus, marital status plays a role in both economic capacity and cooking fuel choices77–80.

Furthermore, the place where food is cooked also influences household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa. The place where food is cooked can influence household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa due to factors like ventilation, safety, and convenience. Households that cook outdoors or in semi-open spaces may prefer traditional fuels such as firewood or charcoal, as smoke and emissions are less of a concern in open-air settings. In contrast, households that cook indoors are more likely to opt for cleaner fuels like LPG or electricity to avoid Indoor Air Pollution and its associated health risks. Additionally, the availability of cooking infrastructure such as electricity connections or gas cylinders often depends on whether the cooking area is part of a permanent structure or a temporary setup, further affecting the type of fuel used81,82.

The SHAP dependence plots provide additional insights into the relationships between household characteristics and cooking fuel choice that may not be fully captured by conventional regression models. The plot shows a clear interaction between electricity access and household wealth. While electricity access generally reduces the likelihood of relying on unclean fuels, this effect is stronger among wealthier households. Among poorer households, the reduction is smaller, suggesting that electricity access alone may not be sufficient to promote clean fuel adoption without improving household economic conditions83,84. Such nonlinear interaction effects are often difficult to detect using standard regression approaches.

The dependence plot indicates that households with television ownership tend to have lower predicted probabilities of using unclean fuels, particularly when electricity is available. This suggests that access to information through media may support the adoption of cleaner cooking technologies. The interaction between place of residence and electricity access suggests that electrification may have a stronger effect in reducing unclean fuel use in certain residential contexts85. These findings highlight how multiple household and infrastructure factors interact in influencing cooking fuel choice. Overall, the SHAP analysis reveals nonlinear relationships and interactions among key predictors, providing novel insights into the determinants of cooking fuel choice that may not be easily captured by traditional statistical models.

Implications

Implications for researchers

This study demonstrates the value of applying supervised machine learning techniques, combined with SHAP, to analyze complex public health and energy behavior data. Using a large multi-country DHS dataset, the machine learning models were able to capture nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among socioeconomic and infrastructural factors influencing cooking fuel choice. These findings highlight the potential of integrating machine learning with demographic, geospatial, and policy data to better understand household energy transitions and support evidence-based research in environmental health and sustainable development.

Implications for policymakers

The findings identify several key leverage points that policymakers can prioritize to accelerate the adoption of clean cooking fuels. Access to electricity, household wealth, education level, place of residence, and media exposure emerged as the most influential predictors of cooking fuel choice. Policymakers can use these variable importance rankings to allocate limited resources more effectively by prioritizing rural electrification programs, expanding educational and awareness campaigns, and supporting socioeconomic development in low-income communities. Targeted interventions focusing on households without electricity, those in rural areas, and those with lower wealth status may yield the greatest impact in reducing reliance on polluting fuels.

Contribution to climate and sustainability goals

The results directly support global sustainability efforts, particularly Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 7: Affordable and Clean Energy and SDG 13: Climate Action. By identifying the socioeconomic and infrastructural factors associated with continued reliance on biomass fuels, the study provides evidence to guide policies aimed at expanding access to modern energy services. Reducing dependence on solid fuels can significantly lower indoor air pollution, decrease greenhouse gas emissions, and improve public health outcomes. These findings therefore contribute to national and regional strategies aimed at achieving clean energy transitions and meeting climate commitments under international frameworks such as the Paris Agreement.

Proposed adaptation and mitigation strategies

Based on the study findings, several evidence-based strategies can support clean cooking transitions in sub-Saharan Africa:

Integrate rural electrification with clean cooking technologies: Governments should couple electrification programs with subsidies or financing schemes for electric cooking appliances, such as electric pressure cookers, to ensure that new electricity access leads to clean cooking adoption.

Target low-income households with financial incentives: Subsidies, microfinance programs, or voucher systems for liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and improved cookstoves can help overcome affordability barriers among poorer households.

Strengthen media and education campaigns: Expanding information dissemination through television, radio, and community-based education programs can increase awareness of the health and environmental benefits of clean cooking technologies.

Implementing these strategies can help accelerate clean energy transitions, reduce indoor air pollution, and contribute to healthier and more climate-resilient communities across sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion and recommendation

A vast majority (84.40%) of households in sub-Saharan Africa rely on unclean fuels. The performance of supervised machine learning techniques was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC. XGBoost emerged as the top-performing model across the board, exhibiting the highest accuracy and AUC, coupled with robust precision and F1 score, establishing it as the most well-rounded and effective solution for this task. Household access to electricity, place of residence, wealth index, television ownership, educational level, marital status, household size, number of children under five, radio ownership, and the place where food is cooked are the most important predictors of household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa.

Based on the findings, it is recommended that policymakers and sub-Saharan Africa governments should prioritize interventions targeting the key predictors of household cooking fuel choice in sub-Saharan Africa, such as improving access to electricity, promoting education, and enhancing household wealth. Efforts to increase the ownership of mass media tools like radios and televisions could also play a critical role in raising awareness about the health and environmental impacts of unclean fuel use and promoting cleaner alternatives. Furthermore, housing and infrastructure improvements that support safe and accessible cooking environments should be integrated into broader energy transition strategies. For future research, studies should explore the causal relationships between these predictors and fuel choice through longitudinal or experimental designs. Additionally, qualitative research is needed to understand cultural, behavioral, and contextual factors influencing household decisions around cooking fuels. Expanding machine learning models to include geospatial, policy, and market data could also provide a more comprehensive understanding and enable targeted, location-specific interventions.

Strength and limitations of the study

This study’s primary strength lies in its use of large, nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data drawn from multiple countries in sub-Saharan Africa, which enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings on household cooking fuel choice. The application of advanced supervised machine learning techniques represents a further methodological strength, enabling the identification of complex, non-linear relationships and interactions among socio-economic, demographic, and contextual factors that are often overlooked by conventional regression-based approaches. The incorporation of SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis allowed for the ranking and interpretation of the relative importance of predictors, improving model transparency and providing policy-relevant insights into the drivers of clean versus solid fuel use. Overall, the machine learning framework achieved strong predictive performance, demonstrating its utility for advancing evidence-based household air pollution research in the region.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of DHS data limits the ability to infer causal relationships between identified predictors and cooking fuel choice. In addition, the analysis relies primarily on demand-side variables, as DHS data do not capture key supply-side factors such as fuel prices, availability, distribution infrastructure, and the reliability of electricity or LPG supply. While variables such as urban residence and household electricity access may partially proxy these conditions, they cannot fully represent local energy market dynamics. Furthermore, although machine learning methods offer high predictive accuracy, their complexity may reduce interpretability and constrain direct causal explanation, potentially limiting straightforward policy translation. Finally, variations in survey years across countries and the exclusion of country-specific contextual variables may influence the relative importance of predictors in certain settings.

Ethics statement.

This study is a secondary analysis of previously collected and published household survey data. As such, it did not require independent ethical approval. However, each included survey obtained ethical clearance from respective national review boards, and informed consent was acquired from all participants. The specific consent procedures varied across surveys. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent do not apply to this study.
