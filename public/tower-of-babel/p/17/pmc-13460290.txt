Predictive Accuracy of Optical Evaluation for High-Risk Serrated Lesions and the Impact of a Brief Educational Intervention
Authors: Jun Young Kim, Vikas Gupta, Maria Pellisé, Sunil Gupta, Clarence Kerrison, Brian Lam, Hasib Ahmadzai, Yong Sul Kim, Eric Y. Lee, Nicholas G. Burgess, Michael J. Bourke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Real-time characterization of advanced lesions during colonoscopy informs the therapeutic approach. Sessile serrated lesions with dysplasia are a subgroup of advanced lesions that are difficult to recognize and accurately characterize. The ability of expert endoscopists to recognize these lesions is unknown; moreover, the impact of a simple training module on subsequent performance is of interest. Methods Seventy high-quality images were selected from a database of large sessile and flat polyps. Histopathology was used as the gold standard. The images were presented in randomized order to expert endoscopists and trainees. Participants were asked to predict the underlying histology. Subsequently, an educational module was delivered, and participants were asked to repeat the test using the same images in a different randomized order. Results Thirty-seven participants were included: 16 trainees and 21 experts. Baseline predictive accuracy for SSL-D was only 24.0% across the group; 18.3% for trainees and 28.2% for experts. This improved significantly, regardless of experience level, after the educational module to 69.7% ( p < 0.001) for the whole cohort; 67.5% for trainees and 71.4% for experts. Before training, SSL-D was incorrectly identified as tubular adenomas or tubulovillous adenomas in 40% of cases. Posttraining, this reduced significantly to 7.6% ( p < 0.001) for all participants. For experts, this decreased to 7.0% ( p < 0.001) and trainees to 8.3% ( p < 0.001). Conclusion SSL-D lesions are underdiagnosed by both expert and trainee endoscopists. A brief educational intervention can significantly improve the predictive accuracy and performance of experts and trainees.

Introduction

Accurate optical assessment and diagnosis are key to delivering high-quality, effective endoscopy. High-definition endoscopes and enhanced imaging technologies allow expert endoscopists to characterize and risk-stratify lesions. This facilitates real-time decision-making on the appropriateness and approach for resecting colorectal lesions. 1 2 Precise characterization underpins this process, as reflected in a 2020 European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Delphi consensus, which also stipulates the importance of training to achieve competency in optical diagnosis. 3

While adenomas account for up to two-thirds of all polyps found during colonoscopy, 4 5 sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) make up only 8.3%. 6 However, SSLs represent up to 30% of all colorectal cancer (CRC) 7 8 and are strongly associated with post-colonoscopy CRC (PCCRC). 9 SSLs may frequently be missed during colonoscopy, with proximal serrated polyp detection rates (PSPDR) varying between 1% and 18% in screening populations. 10 Compounding this, even if detected, SSLs are over four times more likely to be incompletely resected compared to adenomas, and in large SSLs (10–20 mm), the incomplete resection rate has been reported to be as high as 47.6%. 11 SSLs with dysplastic foci (SSL-D) pose specific further challenges as there is a risk of only the dysplastic component being recognized and resected, thereby potentially leaving behind a rapidly progressing residual component. 12 13 14 This highlights the importance of meticulous detection, assessment, and resection of SSLs. While we know that dysplasia within serrated lesions can be accurately identified during colonoscopy, 12 the ability of expert and trainee endoscopists is unknown, and standardized training modules are lacking. Our objective was to determine the baseline accuracy of endoscopists for in vivo histology prediction of SSL-D and the impact of a brief educational intervention.

Methods

Study Design

Seventy representative images of varying colorectal lesions were selected from a database of >10 mm sessile and flat polyps. In total, there were 18 SSLs with no dysplasia (SSL-NDs), 15 SSL-Ds, 9 submucosal invasive cancers (SMICs), 17 tubulovillous adenomas (TVAs), and 11 tubular adenomas (TAs). Images were taken with white light endoscopy without virtual or dye-based chromoendoscopy. This was created into a test module ( Fig. 1 ). The test had two sets; A and B which comprised of the exact same images in a different randomized order. Participants were asked to predict the underlying histology of each lesion from five provided options: SSL-ND, SSL-D, SMIC, and TVA. Their degree of confidence (low vs. high) and perceived quality of the images (good, moderate, poor) were rated. The images were presented to Australasian “expert” and “trainee” endoscopists during a formal educational meeting. Histopathology was used as the gold standard. Two blinded interventional endoscopists took the test module at baseline with 94.3% (66/70) of lesions correctly identified.

Sample images from test module.

Subsequently, a 60-min educational session describing the characteristic features of SSL-D was delivered via a training module ( Fig. 2 ). This module provided information on how colonic lesion assessment should be performed, followed by specific details regarding SSL and SSL-D. Participants were then asked to repeat the test using the other test set. At no stage was the histological diagnosis revealed for the test images.

Selected images from training module highlighting educational content on SSL assessment.

The training module was delivered as an interactive session with a specific focus on SSLs and SSL-Ds. Initially, the Workgroup Serrated Polyps and Polyposis criteria: clouded surface, indistinctive border, irregular shape, and dark spots within crypts 15 were detailed, and examples of SSLs were shown. Then, a further step to closely interrogate serrated lesions with both white light and narrow band imaging was highlighted. The focus was on assessing for a demarcated zone of adenomatous pit pattern amongst homogenous serrated tissue, which signifies a dysplastic focus. The training reflects the standardized imaging protocol described by Tate et al, which reported a 95.0% accuracy for endoscopically detecting SSL-D. 12 16

Participants

Endoscopists from Australia and New Zealand were invited to participate. Endoscopists were divided into either “trainee” or “expert.” Trainees were defined as endoscopists who were still undergoing training, not formally accredited and under supervision. Experts were those who were members of a national endoscopic tissue resection research group and practicing in tertiary referral centers. The number of years performing colonoscopy and the average number of colonoscopies performed per week were recorded.

Statistical Analyses

The responses from the participants were collated in a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel, Version 16.93.1; Redmond, WA: Microsoft Corporation 2025), and statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (Version 2025.05.0+496; RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Predictive accuracy was defined as lesions that were correctly identified out of the total number of lesions (i.e. sensitivity). To assess differences in baseline predictive accuracy between groups (experts vs. trainees), the independent t -test was used. Comparatively, given its dependent nature, the paired t -test was used to compare pre- and post-education accuracies. To account for the repeated measures design of participants evaluating the same images before and after training, a mixed-effects logistic regression analysis was performed. This model included a random intercept for each observer to account for intraindividual variability and repeated measures design. An image random intercept was included to account for inherently different difficulties of the images. Fixed effects for pre- and post-education, experience level, years of colonoscopy, level of diagnostic confidence and weekly colonoscopy volume were included.

Image quality ratings were evaluated for reliability using Cohen’s Kappa for intrarater agreement and Fleiss’ Kappa for inter-rater agreement. Some participants did not rate image quality, but missing data were minimal (0.48% of responses); therefore, missing values were imputed using the mode before calculating the Fleiss’ Kappa.

Results

Participants

Responses were collected from 37 participants from Australia and New Zealand, of which 21 were experts and 16 were trainees. The groups were differentiated by years of colonoscopy experience (12.8 vs. 1.4; p < 0.001) and average number of colonoscopies per week (19.2 vs. 8.5; p < 0.001).

Predictive Accuracy

Pre-education

Baseline predictive accuracy for all lesions across the cohort was 46.0% with experts demonstrating significantly better results than trainees (51.8% vs. 38.3%, p < 0.001). Overall predictive accuracy for SSLs (participants correctly identifying either SSL-ND or SSL-D as SSLs) was 57.8%, with no significant difference between experts and trainees (62.2% vs. 52.1%, p = 0.217). For SSL-ND, predictive accuracy for the whole cohort was 65.6%, and experts performed better than trainees (69.6% vs. 60.4%, p = 0.028). Overall predictive accuracy for SSL-D was 24.0%, with experts trending toward better performance (28.3% vs. 18.3%, p = 0.062) ( Table 1 )

Post-education

Overall predictive accuracy for the entire cohort increased from 46.0% to 54.4% after training ( p < 0.001). Number of years performing colonoscopy and volume did not significantly impact the effect of training.

For SSLs, the entire cohort improved from 57.8% to 85.6% ( p < 0.001). Experts improved from 62.2% to 85.9% ( p < 0.001) and trainees from 52.1% to 85.2% ( p < 0.001). After training, trainees matched experts at 85.9% and 85.2%, respectively ( p = 0.893).

For SSL-D, the entire cohort improved from 24.0% to 69.7% ( p < 0.001). A mixed-effects logistic regression model confirmed a statistically significant improvement in predictive accuracy after education with an odds ratio of 12.08 ( p < 0.001). Experts improved from 28.3% to 71.4% ( p < 0.001) and trainees from 18.3% to 67.5% ( p < 0.001). Following education, there was no difference between experts and trainees (71.4% vs. 67.5%, p = 0.44). High diagnostic confidence was also independently associated with obtaining a correct optical diagnosis with an odds ratio of 1.77 ( p = 0.001). Comparatively, specificity for SSL-D decreased from 92.8% (95% CI 90–94.7%) to 77.4% (95% CI 73.2–81.7%) ( p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed that the reduction was more pronounced in trainees than experts with a mean decrease from 90% (95% CI 87–93%) to 66% (95% CI 59%–73%) compared to 95% (95% CI 93–97%) to 84% (95% CI 78–90%), respectively.

SSL-D versus Adenomas

Before training, SSL-D was incorrectly identified as TA or TVA in 40%. Among experts, this was 35.9%, and trainees, 45.4% ( p = 0.26). Posttraining, this reduced significantly to 7.6% ( p < 0.001) for all participants. For experts, this decreased to 7.0% ( p < 0.001) and trainees to 8.3% ( p < 0.001) ( Table 1 ).

In the case of TA, the overall accuracy dropped in the whole cohort from 39.6% to 32.2% ( p = 0.036). This was seen for SMIC as well, with a significant drop from 49.9% to 42.3% ( p = 0.033). There was no significant difference for TVA. Combining overall adenoma (TA and TVA) predictive accuracies reduced from 44.2% to 41.1% posttraining, but this was not statistically significant ( p = 0.101).

Image Quality

More than 90% of images were rated as either good (62.0%) or moderate (31.4%) quality in the test modules. Intrarater reliability between the matched images in Modules A and B yielded a median kappa of 0.41 (SD = 0.15), indicating moderate agreement based on the Landis and Koch classification. However, inter-rater reliability was poor. Fleiss’ Kappa for all images across all observers was low at 0.00263 ( p < 0.001) for Module A, and 0.0159 ( p < 0.001) for set B.

Discussion

Our study confirms that SSLs are both poorly recognized and inaccurately characterized in endoscopic practice. Trainees and experts alike demonstrated limited ability to identify SSLs with dysplasia, achieving an overall predictive accuracy of only 24%. Notably, 35.8% of SSL-D were misclassified as adenomas by experts, indicating that the dysplastic component of these lesions is often the only feature recognized endoscopically. This selective recognition risks incomplete resection and subsequent rapid malignant transformation. We demonstrate how a brief educational intervention produced a marked improvement. Expert optical predictive accuracy improved by an absolute 45.8%, corresponding to an odds ratio of 12.08. Furthermore, the proportion of SSL-D misdiagnosed as adenomas fell sharply to 7.0%. Importantly, trainees achieved comparable gains following education. These findings highlight that while SSL-D remains under-recognized and incompletely characterized, targeted education can yield substantial and rapid improvements, thus underscoring the need for systematic implementation of structured training tools.

These results are consistent with existing literature. A 2024 randomized controlled trial by van Toledo et al. demonstrated a significant rise in the PSPDR from 13.6% to 17.1% following a 60-min online training session. 17 Tate et al. reported that a standardized imaging protocol using high-definition white light and narrow-band imaging achieved sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy all exceeding 90% for the optical diagnosis of SSL-D. 12 Another study showed that a brief 20-min educational session improved differentiation of adenomas and hyperplastic polyps from 47.6% to 90.8%. 18 A 2021 meta-analysis likewise demonstrated enhancement of optical diagnostic accuracy for colorectal polyps from 70.3% to 81.6% following training. 19 Collectively, these data reinforce that structured education can meaningfully improve endoscopists’ ability to detect and characterize colorectal polyps. Nevertheless, despite growing awareness of SSL-D and its association with interval CRC, there remains a relative paucity of SSL-specific educational research. Our findings directly address this gap, demonstrating that predictive accuracy for SSL-D is initially poor but markedly improvable, regardless of experience level, through focused educational interventions.

Our training module specifically targeted SSLs with a dysplastic component due to their high malignant potential. Bettington et al. reported no significant age difference between patients diagnosed with SSL-D and those with SSL-related carcinoma, 13 suggesting that SSL-D may represent a biologically advanced precursor with accelerated progression. This risk is compounded by the frequent failure to recognize or completely resect serrated lesions. 9 11 13 14 Moreover, low PSPDR has been inversely associated with PCCRC. 9 Despite substantial evidence linking missed or incompletely resected SSL-D with adverse outcomes, PSPDR continues to vary widely among endoscopists. 10 Accurate optical characterization is thus essential for selecting the appropriate resection strategy. While cold-snare polypectomy is established as the gold standard for SSL-ND resection irrespective of size, 20 larger lesions remain at increased risk of incomplete resection, 11 21 necessitating meticulous inspection and technique. In contrast, SSL-D should be managed similarly to conventional adenomas, with en bloc resection of the dysplastic area. 2 Therefore, improving competency in the optical recognition of SSL-D is a critical quality benchmark for reducing preventable cancer risk.

We acknowledge several limitations. Optical diagnosis was performed with static images, which inherently lack the depth and dynamic detail available during real-time assessment of lesions and may have impacted accuracy. As the same images were delivered in the test sets, recall bias may have affected the participants’ performance. Although diagnostic accuracy for SSL-D improved significantly across cohorts, accuracy for adenomas and cancer declined, possibly reflecting a cognitive bias introduced by the SSL-focused training content. The durability of the educational benefit and its translation into clinical performance also warrant longitudinal assessment. Image-quality ratings demonstrated moderate intrarater but poor inter-rater reliability, reflecting both subjective interpretation and the intrinsic challenge of assessing SSL morphology. Nevertheless, the study’s strengths include stratification by expertise level, the inclusion of varied histopathological subtypes, and a repeated-measures design comparing each participant with their own baseline performance. The mixed-effects logistic regression model further accounted for intraindividual correlation, strengthening the statistical validity. Importantly, the simplicity and scalability of the intervention, where a short and easily implemented educational module produced a substantial improvement in diagnostic performance, illustrate its practical utility for widespread adoption in endoscopy training.

In conclusion, baseline predictive accuracy for SSL-D was low among both experts and trainees. A concise educational module led to a clinically meaningful improvement in diagnostic accuracy for SSL-D in a controlled setting. Future research should expand to video-based assessments reflecting real-world endoscopy, evaluate the integration of artificial intelligence-assisted learning, and examine knowledge retention over time. Development of standardized SSL-specific training curricula and assessment of their impact on PSPDR, completeness of resection, and interval cancer rates are also warranted to enhance the overall quality and safety of colonoscopic practice.
