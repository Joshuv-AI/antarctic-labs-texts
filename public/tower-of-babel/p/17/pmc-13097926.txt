Amino acid metabolism-related genes signature correlates with immune infiltration and predicts clinic prognosis in osteosarcoma
Authors: Deshuang Xi, Yilin Teng, Shaobo Wang, Meng Zhang, Gang Liu, Bo Han, Yunpeng Zhang, Xiao Tian, Chunxin Cheng, Shouguo Wang, Weidong Liu, Peng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmino acid metabolism is crucial for tumor cell proliferation and survival. However, its role in osteosarcoma (OS) remains incompletely characterized. The current study aimed to investigate the role of the amino acid metabolism-associated risk signature in prognostication and immune cell infiltration in osteosarcoma.MethodsThe clinical and transcriptome data of patients with OS were retrieved using the TARGET and GEO databases. Consensus clustering of amino acid metabolism-related genes (AAMRGs) stratified the osteosarcoma patients. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression and Cox analysis were performed to build prognostic models. The expression levels of genes comprising the risk model were validated in vitro. Subsequently, the model was validated in an external cohort.ResultsThe risk model developed using five AAMRGs demonstrated appreciable ability to predict overall survival in osteosarcoma patients compared to traditional clinical factors. qPCR validated expression of the five AAMRGs comprising the risk model. Single-cell RNA sequencing and drug prediction analyses extend understanding of modeling AAMRGs in OS.ConclusionThe current prognostic signature utilizing five AAMRGs can accurately predict prognosis in osteosarcoma and may provide new insights into amino acid metabolism in OS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15746-4.

Background

Osteosarcoma (OS) is one of the most aggressive cancers in children and adolescents, which was featured by significant metastatic rates and poor prognosis [1, 2]. The early diagnosis and treatment of OS remain challenging due to its rapid progression. Approximately 15%–20% of patients have lung metastases upon initial diagnosis [3–5], this proportion of patients exhibited poor 5-year survival rates of only 20%, which was remarkably lower than that of patients without metastases (60%–70%). Relevant research progress of OS has been stagnant in the past 30 years [6, 7]. However, many efforts have been made to reveal that heterogeneity was one of the main factors contributing to the poor survival outcomes of patients with OS [8, 9]. Therefore, the development of a genome-based method of risk stratification to relieve the pain of OS patients through individualized targeted therapy has become imminent.

The proliferation of cancer is critically influenced by amino acids in ways of facilitating protein synthesis, energy production, promoting nucleotide synthesis, and maintaining cellular redox balance [10, 11]. Inhibition of tumor-associated amino acid metabolism has been demonstrated to be efficient in suppressing tumor growth in various experiments and clinical studies [12–14]. Shanyi et al. reported that the aberrant branched-chain amino acid metabolism was associated with ANGPTL4 and might be a potential therapeutic target to slow OS progression [15]. Additionally, in numerous cancers, the immune status within the tumor microenvironment (TME) can be regulated by amino acids [16]. The reduction of tryptophan in the TME could cause accumulation of kynurenine, then kynurenine and its derivatives significantly suppress immune responses, leading to tumor progression [17]. In another study, the amino acid transporter protein Slc7a5, the major methionine transporter that determines differentiation of activated T cell, was found to be overexpressed upon activation of T cells [18]. Based on these studies, we speculated that an amino acid metabolism-related risk signature might possess superior ability in predicting prognosis and distinguishing immune infiltration profiles between groups for OS patients.

To delineate the function of amino acid metabolism in OS, we systematically analyzed the clinical and immune characteristics of OS patients based on amino acid metabolism-related genome. We further constructed an amino acid metabolism-related risk signature of five genes with preferable prediction capacity, which was validated in an independent dataset and correlated with the immune status of OS patients. The present prognostic model might have application prospects in evaluation of prognosis and individualized therapy for OS patients.

Methods

Data collection

The clinical data and RNA-seq profiles of OS samples were acquired from the Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) database. The mRNA expression data of GSE21257 and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of GSE152048 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Three samples were excluded from the TARGET-OS cohort due to missing information of clinical survival. The clinicopathological features of both cohorts are presented in Supplementary Table S1. All AAMRGs were acquired from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [19] (Supplementary Table S2).

Clustering of OS samples based on molecular phenotypes

AAMRGs associated with the prognostic outcome of OS were identified using Univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.05 as threshold). Subsequently, we employed the unsupervised clustering method “ConsensusClusterPlus” in R software to identify potential clusters in OS with different molecular phenotypes of amino acids metabolism. The mRNA expression of AAMRGs that exhibited a significant association with the overall survival of OS patients were set as the matrix features.

Analysis of immune status in TME of OS samples

The ESTIMATE algorithm [20] was adopted for evaluating the activity level of TME in OS samples of different clusters or risk groups, which was quantified by immune score, stromal score, ESTIMATE score and tumor purity. We also collected genes that reflected the traits of immune infiltrating cells in tumors from the MSigDB. Then we examined the frequency of immune infiltrating cells by ssGSEA algorithm as previously described [21].

Differentially expressed genes between clusters and functional analysis

The “limma” package [22] in R software was adopted for identification differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The judging criteria for DEG were set at cutoff values of absolute fold change (FC) greater than 0.7 and an adjusted P value less than 0.05. The R packages “clusterProfiler” [23] and Metascape [24] were applied for decoding the biological processes and pathways enriched by these DEGs in OS samples. We further investigated the involvement of these DEGs in various pathways by performing Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) analysis through GSVA package of R software [25]. Genes in the canonical pathway of MSigDB database were used as the input geneset. A GSVA enrichment score was assigned to each sample and pathways with significant different enrichment values between clusters were selected through differential analysis based on limma package.

Building of prognostic model

Eligible AAMRGs for construction of the prognostic model were determined through the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) methods of “glmnet” package in R software. Then the final AAMRGs included for prognostic model were selected via multivariate Cox regression analysis and the risk score was calculated by summing the weighted expression values (Formula for risk score: 1.0173 ⋅ expression value of AFMID + 0.8599 ⋅ expression value of CKMT1B + 0.5372 ⋅ expression value of CKMT2 + (− 1.3218) ⋅ expression value of PSMC4 + (− 1.7999) ⋅ expression value of SUOX). According to the median value of all risk scores, the TARGET cohort was divided into two groups of low-risk and high-risk. The efficiency of the prognostic model is assessed by calculation of the concordance index and plotting of receiver operating characteristic curve through rms, and ROCR packages of R software.

Validation of the independency and accuracy of the present prognostic model

To test whether the prognostic model was subjected to the variation of clinical traits of OS patients, we compared the distribution of risk scores in the GSE21257 OS cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted to assess the survival difference among distinct risk groups within specific clinical subsets. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses was further performed for examining the independency and efficacy of the AAMRGs prognostic model as an indicator for OS patients’ prognosis. The prediction ability of AAMRGs risk model was additionally validated in an external dataset of GSE21257 OS cohort using methods in the above part, and the expression patterns of the constituent genes of the prognostic model in samples of GSE21257 OS cohort were displayed as a heatmap. The nomogram was built with the survival and rms packages of R software through incorporating the AAMRGs risk model and clinical data of OS patients.

scRNA-seq data analysis for OS patients in different risk groups

We used the “Seurat” package (v4.1.1) in R software to analyze the 11 scRNA-seq samples. Low quality cells were defined as those with either less than 200 detected genes or more than 10% mitochondrial genes, and were subsequently removed. Then, the data were normalized and a total of 2,000 genes exhibiting the highest variability were identified. The feature genes were selected across samples for removing batch effect and integration. After scaling of the data, the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) method was adopted for clustering cells. To identify the optimal number of clusters as well as the cell subpopulation for OS, we employed the “SingleR” package to assist in the initial definition of cell subpopulations. Then, by calculating the differentially expressed genes between clusters and reviewing literature studies on molecular markers, we defined single cell subpopulations of OS. The marker genes used for subpopulation are shown in the Supplementary Table S3. We used the “SCISSOR” (v2.0.0) package to correlate single cell sequencing data with Bulk sequence data. The cellular communication network between these cells was analyzed and visualized using the “CellChat” (v1.1.3) package in R software.

Prediction of drug sensitivity

We collected the drug sensitivity data for cancer cell lines (CCLs) using the CTRP and GDSC databases. A data quality control process such as removal of compounds containing NA records were conducted before the inclusion of drug sensitivity data. Then, the IC50 values of each drug for OS patients in low and high-risk groups were calculated using the ridge regression method through the calcPhenotype function in the “pRRophetic” R package. Drugs with therapeutic potential in both drug databases were screened out by selecting drugs with lower IC50 in higher risk group that were negatively correlated with risk scores.

Cell cultures

Human osteoblasts (OB), 143B and HOS cells were bought from Procell Life (Wuhan, China). 143B were cultivated with 1640 medium (11875093, Gibco) and HOS cells were cultivated with MEM medium (A4124802, Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. OB cells were cultured with DMEM/F-12 medium (11320033, Gibco) at 35 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (76294-180, Avantor).

qPCR assays

The expression of AAMRG gene were by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used to normalize gene expression. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9109; TaKaRa). Total RNA was synthesized into complementary DNA (cDNA) by RNA PCR kit (RR019A; TaKaRa). PCR amplification was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (22204, Tolobio) on a qPCR system (LC480 II, Roche). The PCR program was as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, then 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles. Gene expression levels were calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Results

Identification of molecular subtypes of OS based on AAMRGs

In order to depict the feature of amino acid metabolism in OS, we examined the expression patterns of key AAMRGs. Firstly, we obtained 101 AAMRGs from the MSigDB database and fifty of them were identified to be significantly correlated with the prognosis of OS patients through univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.05). Then, the patients with OS in the training cohort were classified into two subtypes based on the expression value of the fifty AAMRGs (Fig. 1a and b). Finally, 40 and 45 patients were assigned to subtype 1 and subtype 2 respectively. The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis retained the local characteristics of the original data and revealed the two-dimensional distribution of these samples in two clusters (Fig. 1c). Distinct gene expression patterns of AAMRGs in both subtypes were displayed in Fig. 1d. Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis illustrated that patients in subtype 1 had significantly longer survival than those in subtype 2 (P < 0.01, Fig. 1e).

Distinct immune infiltration and immune status in different AAMRGs-related subtypes

It has been shown that immune-infiltrating cells in the TME could modify the tumor phenotype, thus we tested the immune cell infiltration of OS. According to the results of ESTIMATE analysis, OS patients in subtype 1 had remarkably higher stromal scores compared to the patients in subtype 2 (P < 0.01), while no significant differences were found between the two subtypes with respect to immune score, ESTIMATE score or tumor purity (Fig. 2a). As shown in the heatmap for ssGSEA analysis, higher level of infiltration of immune cells was observed subtype 1 (Fig. 2b). The samples in subtype 1 displayed significantly higher infiltration of central memory CD4 and CD8 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, natural killer cells, and type 2 helper T cells. Elevated numbers of mast cells and memory B cells were also observed in subtype 1 (Fig. 2c).

Functional analysis for DEGs between two subtypes of OS samples

In order to identify the difference between the two subtypes, we evaluated the DEGs and functional enrichment between the two subgroups. A total of 379 DEGs were identified between the two subtypes, including 275 up-regulated genes and 104 down-regulated genes in subtype 2 compared with subtype 1 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S5). GO enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were associated with eukaryotic transcriptional elongation, extracellular matrix formation, ribosomal subunit composition, and RNA binding (Fig. 3b, c). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network described the intricate relationships between these DEGs and the MCODE (Molecular Complex Detection) algorithm identified four molecular expression patterns in the PPI network, all of which were closely associated with peptide chain elongation and extracellular collagen formation (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table S6).

With regard to the enrichment of these DEGs in KEGG pathways, signaling pathways related to metabolism and proliferation include endocytosis, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways were activated by these DEGs (Fig. 3e). Results from GSVA and GSEA revealed that cell proliferation and inflammation-related pathways including the EGFR SMRTE pathway, MYOSIN pathway, and PDGF pathway were enriched in subtype 1 (Fig. 3f). Activated pathways in subtype 2 correlated with cellular response to starvation, eukaryotic translation elongation, ribosomal RNA processing, activation of the epidermal growth factor receptor SMRTE pathway, MAPK3/ERK1 activation pathway, and PID VEGFR1 pathway.

Construction of prognostic model based on AAMRGs in a training cohort

Gene signatures is an appreciate tool in predict outcomes of oncology patients [26, 27]. Considering the heterogeneity of amino acid metabolism in OS, we built a prognostic model to extend the application of AAMRGs in OS. A total of 13 genes with the minimal lambda value calculated by LASSO regression analysis were preliminary reserved (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table S7). Then, five genes were further selected from the multivariate Cox regression analysis for building the risk model: AFMID, CKMT1B, CKMT2, PSMC4, and SUOX. Specifically, expression value of AFMID, CKMT1B, and CKMT2 suggested hazardous effect of them on the prognosis of OS patients (HR > 1), while expression value of PSMC4 and SUOX suggested protective effect of them on the prognosis of OS patients (HR < 1). As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the AAMRGs prognostic model enabled accurate risk stratification of OS patients. Patients in the high-risk group had shorter survival times compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 4c). The risk model based on five AAMRGs exhibited high accuracy in prediction of survival probability, displaying area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.907, 0.840 and 0.834 for one, three, and five years separately (Fig. 4d). The AAMRGs model had a superior capacity of predicting the prognosis of OS patients compared with conventional clinicopathological characteristics (Fig. 4e). The results of qPCR revealed that AFMID, CKMT1B, CKMT2 and PSMC4 were significantly overexpressed in both 143B and HOS cells, whereas SUOX were markedly under-expressed in 143B and HOS cells when compared to OB cells (Fig. 4f).

AAMRGs risk model as an independent prognostic indicator for OS patients

Subgroup analysis revealed that there were no statistically significant variations in risk scores among patients stratified by age, sex, lesion sites, or metastatic status (Fig. 5a). The prognostic model could distinctively predict the survival probability of low-risk and high-risk OS patients under different clinical classifications of age, sex, lesion site, and metastatic status (Fig. 5b). The Sankey diagram displayed the interrelationships among clinicopathological characteristics, risk scores, and prognostic situation of OS patients (Fig. 5c). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further supported that the AAMRGs risk model might be used as a standalone predictor for the prognosis of OS patients (HR = 8.961, 95%CI = 3.599–20.988, Table S8).

Different TME landscape in OS patients stratified by AAMRGs risk score

To investigate the relationship between the current prognostic model and the TME phenotypes of OS patients, the ESTIMATE algorithm was performed. The stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score were lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (P < 0.01). However, tumor purity was downregulated in the low-risk group (P < 0.01, Fig. 6a). Furthermore, immune analysis with the ssGSEA algorithm showed that greater infiltration of immune cells and up-regulated immune-related pathways were observed in the low-risk group (Fig. 6b and c). Additionally, the expression value of immune checkpoint genes was up-regulated in the low-risk group and generally inversely correlated with the risk score (Fig. 6d and e), which might correlate with better outcomes of OS patients following treatment of immune checkpoint inhibitor regimens.

Validation of the AAMRGs risk model in an external dataset

To assess the robustness of the AAMRGs risk model, we tested it on an external dataset. The GSE21257 GEO cohort was stratified into two risk groups according to the prognostic model, and the heatmap displayed the distinct expression patterns of the five candidate genes in each group (Fig. 7a). Worse survival outcomes were observed in the high-risk group, as indicated by the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (P < 0.05, Fig. 7b). In the Time-dependent ROC analysis, the risk model developed by AAMRGs performed generally well, especially in predicting one-year survival (Fig. 7c). Additionally, the connection between the AAMRGs model and TME was investigated. The low-risk group had a significantly higher stromal score (P < 0.05), immune score (P < 0.01), ESTIMATE score (P < 0.01), and significantly lower tumor purity (P < 0.01) compared with the high-risk group (Fig. 7d).

scRNA-seq data analysis for OS patients in different risk groups

The scRNA-seq can provide a more specific landscape of the amino acid metabolic profiles of osteosarcoma at the single-cell level resolution. We retrieved and downloaded a dataset containing scRNA-seq data from 11 OS patients in the GEO database (GSE152048). After quality control and integration, we identified 9 cell subpopulations (Fig. 9a and Supplementary Fig. S1). By the SCISSOR [28] algorithm, we tagged AAMRGs high-risk versus low-risk group in the single-cell data of OS patients (Fig. 9b). Metabolic analysis at the single-cell level suggested cell-to-cell heterogeneity of amino acid metabolism-related pathways in the TME. Cells associated with high-risk group of OS patients exhibited significantly greater amino acid metabolic activity, suggesting the critical role of amino acid metabolism in tumor aggressiveness (Fig. 9c). Among the identified cells, proliferating osteoblasts, chondroblasts, and multifunctional stem cells (MSCs), were mainly associated with high-risk group of OS patients. Taking clinical factors into consideration, we found that more high-risk-associated cells emerged in samples from patients with recurrent OS (Fig. 9d).

In terms of the cellular interaction network of the TME in OS, proliferating osteoblasts shared a close communication network with endothelial cells and chondrogenic cells in high-risk associated cells, and it also stimulated OS proliferation through autocrine action. The interactions between proliferating tumor cells, osteoblasts and osteoclasts were significantly enhanced in low-risk patients; however, their connection with endothelial cells was almost absent. Overall, the interactions between tumor cells, Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and MSCs were weaker in both high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 9e). As demonstrated in Fig. 9f, the overall intensity of cellular communication was higher in high-risk-related cells than in low-risk-related cells. The communication patterns of cells in different risk groups also varied. In the high-risk group, activated signaling pathways were mainly engaged in cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival. In contrast, activation of pathways associated with the cell matrix was observed in the low-risk group (Fig. 9g).

Prediction of chemotherapy response in high- and low-risk groups of OS patients

Drug prediction may provide potential treatment options for OS patients with high AAMRGs risk score. To find potential drugs, we conducted drug prediction using data from 487 drugs in the CTRP dataset and 140 drugs in the GDSC dataset, respectively (Fig. 10a). The sensitivity of patients in the TARGET-OS cohort to various compounds was predicted, following the flowchart in Fig. 10b. The correlations between the IC50s of different drugs and the risk score of OS patients were displayed in Fig. 10c and d (R < − 0.3, P < 0.05). Finally, we acquired 13 compounds in CTRP and 10 compounds in GDSC database that met the requirements of drugs with therapeutic potential for OS patients (Fig. 10e and f).

Discussion

In recent years, it has been revealed that metabolism is closely related to cancer epigenetics [29, 30]. Several studies have identified abnormalities in glucose metabolism in OS that affected the aggressive and metastatic properties of OS [31, 32]. Metabolic markers of glycolysis and lipid have been figured out to accurately predict the progression of OS [21, 33, 34]. In the current research, we focused on the dysregulation of amino acid metabolism in OS and established an AAMRGs-related prognostic model in OS patients for the first time.

A preliminary clustering of OS patients based on AAMRGs revealed two groups with different clinical progression. We noted that OS patients with higher AAMRGs expression patterns exhibited poor prognosis, lower level of immune infiltration and higher tumor purity, which implied that the tumor progression of OS might be facilitated by inhibition of immune status. Although previous studies have explained the heterogeneity of immune subtypes in OS [35], our work emphasized the heterogeneity of OS samples in aspects of amino acid metabolism and the immune landscape, which was one of the highlights of the present study. The results from consensus clustering indicated the essential role of AAMRGs in tumor immune function of OS. Further investigation of the molecular functions of DEG between the two clusters hinted that amino acid metabolism might influence protein synthesis and RNA binding activity in OS, which is consistent with the hypermetabolic nature of the tumor. In addition, pathway enrichment analysis identified the enrichment of several signaling pathways related to tumor metabolism, such as the endocytosis, MAPK, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway [36, 37], which suggested that the AAMRGs might be involved in tumor progression in OS. Based on these results, we proposed that amino acid metabolism signals were vital in regulating the progression of OS and were indicative of the prognosis of OS patients. That said, our study is based solely on transcriptomic data and thus requires validation through metabolic flux assays or targeted inhibition experiments, such as the approach employed in the study by Zheng et al. [38].

To further extend the application of the amino acid metabolism signals, a risk model was built using AAMRGs and the risk model constructed by five AAMRGs showed high accuracy in predicting the progression of OS compared with other clinical characteristics. The five genes used in the risk model have been discovered in prior researches to be related to the formation and development of a wide range of human cancers [26, 27, 39–42]. The adverse effects of AFMID, CKMT1B and CKMT2 on the prognosis of OS patients were in agreement with their reported roles in accelerating the development of human malignancies. However, the association of PSMC4 and SUOX expression with the better survival of OS patients were contradictory to their carcinogenic effects in other human cancers [42–47], which might be explained by the heterogeneity of different tumors. Though in PCR experiments, the expression pattern of PSMC4 deviated somewhat from our predictions, this discrepancy may arise from differences between experimental methodologies.

Enlightened by the distinct immune infiltration levels in clusters of OS patients with different expression patterns of AAMRGs, we investigated the difference in immune infiltration between high and low-risk groups. In this paper, it was interesting that high correlations existed between AAMRGs risk score and immune cell infiltration, which echoed with the findings in previous studies that amino acid metabolism was capable of regulating the infiltration of immune cells in TME [48–50]. Several immune cells that highly concentrated in low-risk group of OS patients such as CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, NK cells, macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) played crucial roles in eliminating tumor cells or restraining the spread of tumor cells [51–57]. Particularly, the suppressive influence of CD8 + T cells and NK cells on the progression in OS have been reported [51, 53, 54]. In our study, the low-risk group was found to be rich in infiltration of immune cells. We hypothesized that the infiltrated immune cells in low-risk OS patients exerted the cancer-fighting powers in TME, leading to restricted progression of OS and better survival conditions of OS patients. However, we should acknowledge that our study remains in the “hypothesis-generating” phase, as we have not validated concrete immunomodulatory strategies, unlike previous study which operationalizes TME modulation via exosomal engineering [58].

It has been proved that the amino acid metabolism has a broad-reaching effect on the activation and transformation of immune cell function [16]. In our study, both the innate immune signaling and adaptive immune signaling pathways; in particular, the immune checkpoints, lysis cell activity, T cell activation, and type I IFN response pathway showed a trend of diminished activation in the high-risk group. This finding suggests that the amino acid metabolism not only affected the immune infiltration but also influenced the activation state of immune cells. As previous researchers note, the unique amino acid metabolic pattern formed in TME could lead to dysregulation of immune function by interfering the energy supply of resident immune cells [59]. However, additional independent cohorts with matched multi-omic/clinical data would further strengthen our conclusions, with future efforts focused on key AAMRG gene functional experiments to validate these metabolic-immune correlations and elucidate mechanisms—aligning with methodological rigor advocated in recent multi-omic research [60–62].

The aberrant expression of immune checkpoints in two risk groups was another key point of immunal analysis in the present study. Immune checkpoints are essential in maintaining self-tolerance and restricting physiological immune reactions to avoid collateral injury [63]. Due to the ongoing generation of neoepitopes, which is the basis for immune-mediated tumor cell mortality, immunotherapies could show favorable therapeutic effects, especially in cases of genetically unstable OS [64, 65]. In our study, we found that CD274 (PD-L1) and CD276 (B7-H3) were upregulated in the low-risk group. Considering the literature evidence that PD-L1 expression affected T cell infiltration in OS [66] and overexpression of CD276 has been identified in over 90% of osteosarcoma specimens using immunohistochemistry [67], we conjectured that amino acid metabolic features may correlate with the expression of key immune checkpoint genes in osteosarcoma, and their role in immune checkpoint therapy warrants further investigation. However, this conclusion requires robust support from well-characterized clinical cohorts or definitive molecular biological studies, as shown by Zhao et al. [68].

For a thorough understanding of the molecular basis of the present AAMRGs prognostic model in OS at the single cell level, we further performed analysis of the scRNA-seq data. The scRNA-seq data provided a more specific landscape of the amino acid metabolic profiles of osteosarcoma at the single-cell level resolution. Our findings suggested a close association between high-risk AAMRGs signals and proliferating osteoblasts and chondroblasts. Previous research has emphasized the critical role of osteoblast metabolic state in determining their function [69]. The consistency between the literature evidence and the analysis results of the present scRNA-seq data demonstrated the validity of the AAMRGs prognostic model. Among all living organisms, cells communicate with each other in the context of tissue society instead of being isolated, performing distinct functions through specialized communication pathways. Cells in the TME can secrete growth factors that promote tumor growth, while tumors can also produce inhibitory ligands that enable them to evade the immune system [70]. Regardless of osteoblastic or chondroblastic OS, the majority of OS cases are classified as high-risk. Despite enhanced cellular communication between lymphocytes, tumor cells, and endothelial cells in high-risk patients, the cellular interaction patterns in the tumor microenvironment vary, reflecting the heterogeneity of OS [8, 9]. The cellular interaction network in TME of OS samples illustrated the underlying molecular basis of the different prognosis of OS patients in low and high-risk group to some extent. It is important to note that, as highlighted in previous studies, caution is warranted when interpreting in silico biological inferences. Meanwhile, we must acknowledge that the current bioinformatic analyses have not provided comprehensive evidence regarding the intercellular communication modes within the osteosarcoma microenvironment. Further cellular experiments would help to accurately characterize the features of amino acid metabolism in tumor-infiltrating cells. In addition, adopting novel research approaches such as exosome-mediated intercellular communication may enable further validation of our hypotheses [58]. These aspects require further investigation and refinement in subsequent studies to generate more robust empirical evidence.

Finally, the prediction of drug sensitivity in low and high-risk group helped providing a new approach of individualized regimen for OS patients based on drug repositioning. Compounds such as ML006, ML258 and oxaliplatin might possess therapeutic potential for OS patients.

Despite the inspiring findings of the present work, several limitations of this research should be pointed out. Although the predicting ability of the AAMRGs prognostic model has been validated in an independent cohort, predicting the overall survival of patients with OS is complex and it requires more rigorous study designs to control the effects of various factors. Thus, well-designed independent prospective trials are needed for validating the effectiveness of the AAMRGs signature.

In this study, we constructed an osteosarcoma risk scoring system from the perspective of amino acid metabolism. At the same time, we also recognize that amino acid metabolism is not an entirely independent factor in tumor pathological progression. A variety of factors such as infection and alterations in the systemic circulatory immune system can exert a profound impact on the disease itself [71–74].

Conclusions

In conclusion, we constructed a five-gene AAMRGs signature that effectively predicted the prognosis of OS patients. The AAMRGs prognostic model performed well across different clinical phenotype subgroups as an independent survival indicator for OS patients. Moreover, the different amino acid metabolism patterns correlate with the immune status and the cellular interaction network in TME of OS. The AAMRGs prognostic model in the current study may provide an important reference for tumor targeted therapy of OS.
