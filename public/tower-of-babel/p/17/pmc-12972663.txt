Self-reported job histories: potential value of the method in health policy and systems research
Authors: Bhaskar Purohit, Felix Orole, Peter S Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the absence of documented health workforce deployment policies and limited access to key job history data, existing health systems research methods are inadequate for examining policy implementation. The analysis of self-reported job histories offers a valuable research method for overcoming these limitations; however, its application and usefulness remain largely unexamined in health system and policy research, particularly in the context of health workforce and policy implementation. In this paper, we reflect on our experiences of using self-reported job histories to investigate the experiences of public sector doctors with deployment systems related to recruitment, initial posting, and transfers. We interviewed 33 public sector doctors from two Indian states to gain insight into their experiences with policies and systems related to deployment. The initial interview process revealed a pattern in which the doctors developed a work-life chronology to organize their responses. This was formalized in subsequent interviews, creating structured chronological job histories. Job histories serve as a useful and flexible research method for understanding the complexities of how health workforce deployment systems operate and are experienced by health workers. Commentary by doctors regarding these job histories revealed negative perceptions of transfer practice, disruptions in their career trajectories, and gaps in policy implementation. The recollection process of forming job histories is organic and fluid, rich in qualitative data, and its construction involves making sense of diverse work-related stories of health workers. Job histories offer flexibility for collecting data in a more structured manner through a simple set of quantitative questions. Despite their usefulness, constraints such as the inability to triangulate and recall bias exist. Beyond research, job history analysis has practical implications; it is useful for policy implementers and researchers through a more nuanced analysis of challenges related to effective workforce management systems, thereby improving workforce planning, policies, and systems.

Introduction

Health policy and systems research (HPSR) has been described as a complex research terrain with multiple challenges in conducting research (Walt et al. 2008). Deployment of the health workforce in the low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is one such research area, where researchers are confronted with practical and methodological challenges. These include hurdles in obtaining relevant policy documents (Walt et al. 2008, Purohit and Hill 2023), a lack of openness from policy actors to divulge information due to the sensitive issues investigated (Sheikh et al. 2015), limited access to policy actors (Walt et al. 2008), the absence of documented health workforce deployment policies (Abimbola et al. 2017), and the unavailability of health workforce-related data (Purohit and Hill 2024). Furthermore, the health workforce deployment systems in LMICs may operate without documented policies, or the policies are poorly implemented, the systems can be opaque and compromise public sector ethics and may be subject to corruption (Collins et al. 2000, Schaaf and Freedman 2015, Purohit et al. 2016, Abimbola et al. 2017, Purohit and Hill 2024). In such contexts, the nuances of HPSR, such as the processes involved in implementing policies and how to conduct policy analysis, cannot be comprehensively captured (Gilson 2012, Mills 2012). This also compounds the challenges in analyzing policy implementation and assessing how policy implementation is perceived and experienced by health workers.

We confronted these challenges in our larger research study, which examined the experiences of public sector doctors with deployment policies and systems related to recruitment, first posting, subsequent posting, and transfer (PT) in two Indian states. Initial documentary searches and interviews with key informants failed to identify documented policies in these areas, and presented limited access to employment data from employers (Purohit and Hill 2023, 2024). We have circumvented these obstacles through the use of two methodological strategies. The first strategy is based on our observation that, despite the absence of available documented PT policy, both employees and key informants made consistent references to the same conditions, allowing us to identify a set of accepted policy norms that apply to the PT space (Purohit and Hill 2023). In this paper, we explore the strategy to circumvent limited access to employment records, reflecting on the use of self-reported job histories to understand health workers’ perceptions of policy implementation in contexts where no documented policies or formal job history data are available.

Research context

Our larger research examined the experiences of public sector doctors with deployment policies and systems related to recruitment, first posting, and subsequent PT, including the policy implementation of PT from the doctors’ viewpoint and experiences. For this, two theoretical frameworks are pertinent. The first framework of ‘implementation fidelity’ suggests that the effective achievement of Human Resource Management (HRM) or deployment policy outcomes depends upon policies being implemented with conformity to that policy. Implementation fidelity refers to the adherence to policy, which is described as whether the policy or practice is delivered as intended or written, as well as the consistency in the frequency and duration of policy implementation (Dusenbury et al. 2003). However, from the initial data collection phase, we faced several challenges. The first was gaining access to specific policy actors, such as bureaucrats and representatives from key institutions. Another challenge was obtaining policy documents, especially those related to PT. While the study respondents, including both policy actors and doctors, believed that a policy existed, we were unable to locate or access PT policies. As a result, the lack of documented policy for PT became a significant obstacle during our research. The final challenge was the lack of access to formal health workers’ job history data from the state health department. Due to these practical difficulties, testing theories, applying the conceptual framework, and conducting implementation analyses proved to be challenging.

We overcame these challenges through two novel approaches. First, we constructed a series of norms (Table 1), which are interpreted as evidence of implied policy and have already been published (Purohit and Hill 2023). These norms were developed based on the accounts of public sector doctors regarding what the policies meant to them and were corroborated by other policy actors.

The second framework focuses on HRM performance and its underlying causal chain (Boxall and Macky 2007). The framework is explained by representing the HRM process as a linked chain, where the intended HRM practices lead to actual practices, which are filtered by how employees perceive these practices, resulting in employee reactions and, collectively, organizational performance (Boxall and Macky 2007). Thus, our research was predicated on the availability of job history data from the state health departments to examine policy implementation. In the absence of access to departmental employment records, this paper examines the use of self-reported job histories as a functional alternative. These job histories were the product of semi-structured doctors’ interviews, which provided rich, descriptive accounts of their experiences with the deployment systems, documenting chronological data as well as qualitative reflections. We saw this combination of methods as a potentially rich element of this research, allowing us to test it against the constructed policy norms and examine policy implementation from the doctors’ viewpoint. For this paper, we selected various sets of job histories and illustrated them through examples to demonstrate the variations in the job history data and how these serve as a valuable research method for uncovering critical HPSR issues that might otherwise remain unobserved using conventional approaches.

Context of job histories in the health workforce research

Life histories are a qualitative method (Witter et al. 2017), based on the story of different events in an individual's life, thus focusing on individuality, subjectivity, and a particular experience (Plummer 2001). Job histories are a subset of life histories where the interview focuses on the subject’s career or professional trajectory and main work-related events (Witter et al. 2017). Job histories have been used in various disciplines, including tourism (Ladkin 1999), the health sector, and labor market analysis (Dex 1991). Despite the use of job history as a research method in various disciplines, including health, its application and usefulness have rarely been explored in HPSR, particularly in the context of the health workforce. Studies that use job histories in health workforce-related research highlight their usefulness in understanding health workers’ experiences with policy implementation and implementation gaps in deployment systems (Kadam et al. 2016, Purohit and Martineau 2016a, 2016b, 2016c, Purohit et al. 2016, Purohit and Hill 2023, 2024, 2025). However, none of these studies discuss the practical application, challenges, usefulness, and limitations of job histories, nor how these could be utilized to overcome some of the existing limitations of current HPSR methods.

Conceptualization of job histories

For this research, we conducted 61 semi-structured interviews in two Indian states (state names anonymized and referred to as States 1 and 2). This included 28 interviews with key policy actors, 16 in State 1 and 12 in State 2, purposively recruited to ensure broad representation. The interviews with policy actors were vital for identifying previously published norms (Purohit and Hill 2023) and are not reported on directly in this paper.

We also interviewed 33 doctors—18 from State 1 and 15 from State 2, who were the main subjects of the study. The interviews focused on examining their experiences with the deployment policies and systems. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was used to include the doctors from the two states to have a representation of: (i) allopathic and Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy (AYUSH) doctors, (ii) doctors working on contracts and regular service, and (c) doctors working at Primary Health Centers (PHCs), Community Health Centers (CHCs), and District Hospitals (DHs). Due to the geographical, demographic, and socio-economic differences between the study states, there were some variations in sampling between the two states. Of the 33 frontline doctors, 15 were from State 1, which included 4 AYUSH and 6 allopathic doctors working on contracts, as well as 5 allopathic doctors working under regular employment. As State 1 is small, we ensured representation of doctors from all four districts in the state.

In contrast, State 2 is a big state with over 40 districts. We employed purposive sampling to interview 18 doctors (four AYUSH and 14 allopathic doctors working in regular service), who represented the six districts. The six districts were selected purposively to ensure representation of doctors from areas both near and far from the state headquarters. Interviews were conducted at doctors’ workplaces, except for six interviews in State 2, which took place at the state’s training center. In this paper, we focus on the job histories constructed from the interviews with doctors.

Job history information can generate quantitative and qualitative data (Ladkin 1999). In our research, job histories emerged organically from the doctors’ interviews. In the initial nine interviews with doctors, we asked doctors to explain their experiences with the deployment system. During the interview process, the doctors constructed a work-life-related chronology of their experiences as a means of structuring their responses. This was an inductive approach where job histories were grounded in the data, and thematic analysis was used to analyze the data from these initial interviews. The emerging patterns from the first nine interviews were used to construct job histories, which provided rich, descriptive accounts of their experiences with the deployment systems.

We also developed our job history process to provide both quantitative and qualitative data. We achieved this by leveraging the emerging work-life chronology pattern, adapting the interview structures for the remaining 24 interviews, and formalizing them as job history (see Supplementary material). For this, we utilized a simple format that included structured and straightforward questions, asking the doctors to recall their recruitment dates, the duration of their contracts, the locations and lengths of their recruitment, the dates of their first and subsequent postings, and so on. After capturing these key dates and events, we generated qualitative data by asking the doctors to explain their experiences, reasons, and opinions regarding these significant job-related events. The quantitative data were analyzed using simple numbers, frequencies, and percentages to illustrate some of the observed patterns.

Application of job histories

For this paper, we have selected sets of job histories to illustrate the variations in the data. These two examples demonstrate how using job history offers a valuable research method for revealing critical HPSR issues such as disruptions in doctors’ career paths, opaque systems, and gaps in policy implementation. The examples do not represent our complete findings.

Example 1—job history analysis: disrupted career trajectories, bureaucratic inefficiencies, and inequitable recruitment practices

To understand recruitment processes, particularly regarding the capture of recruitment dates and contract lengths, we utilized the quantitative questions included in the job history format. Analyzing these data revealed the variability in contract durations based on the state and the systems of medicine in which doctors were trained (allopathy and alternative systems of medicine, abbreviated as AYUSH). The analysis showed that both allopathic and AYUSH doctors from State 1 spent significantly longer periods on contracts than their counterparts from State 2 (Fig. 1). Although these insights were valuable, the personal and professional stories behind these figures were absent. To gather these individual accounts, we used the qualitative data to explore reasons, such as why doctors served longer on contracts and why these durations were notably shorter in State 2 (see selected quotes in Box 1). Combining quantitative and qualitative data enabled data triangulation, providing valuable insights and revealing key themes that might have been difficult to capture with either type of data alone. The combination of the two was compelling in highlighting key themes, such as bureaucratic inefficiencies and systemic inequities in State 1, as well as alternative recruitment solutions from State 2.

Doctors’ accounts of recruitment

“Careerwise, I have been stuck for so many years. I have no service benefits or promotions because I am a contractual employee, and it’s not my fault.” (State 1, Contractual doctor 10)

This example highlights that the job histories revealed significant systemic issues, such as the missing second vertical bar for Doctors 10 and 11 in State 1 (allopathic doctors on contract from State 1). The narrative accounts highlight systemic inefficiencies caused by the state's failure to conduct regular recruitment for many years, which has led these doctors to remain on temporary contracts for extended periods. Additionally, combining job histories with qualitative accounts reveals systemic and perceived inequalities faced by AYUSH doctors, who were unable to secure permanent positions (as shown by the missing second vertical bar for Doctors 7–9). Their stories reflect the broader policy environment and the inequities they face, as the state only recruits them temporarily to meet service needs without offering permanent positions. Therefore, combining quantitative and narrative data provided crucial insights into systemic factors, which helped explain the variation in doctors’ career progression.

Example 2—job history analysis: inconsistencies in policy implementation, political underpinning, and opaque posting and transfer systems

To understand doctors’ perspectives on the implementation of PT, we used open-ended questions. The initial interviews revealed a chronological pattern related to key job history events in how doctors described these experiences, often emphasizing their negative views of PT. Although the themes and patterns emerged from the qualitative data, highlighting issues related to PT, we faced difficulties in applying the conceptual framework and analyzing policy implementation objectively. To address this, we included a few straightforward questions, such as asking for the dates, durations, and locations of their first and subsequent postings. These were followed by qualitative questions to explore the reasons behind the varied durations and locations of postings (Box 2).

Doctors’ accounts of Postings and Transfer

“The state usually sends the recruits to the periphery, for at least three years, but that did not happen in my case. I have been posted at rural PHC, too far from my native place for over 60 months now, and other lucky doctors haven’t seen rural areas in their lives.” (State 2, Doctor 4)

“The first posting is done in rural or remote locations. I was posted at a very remote PHC, which was not my preferred posting. I was very unhappy with my posting and did not feel like working at all. But I am glad that the posting was only for 12 months.” (State 2, Doctor 5)

“According to the rule, I should be posted in a district hospital, but I have been working at PHCs for nearly twenty years now. All my past four transfers were at rural PHCs, and this is unfair.” (State 1, Doctor 2)

“I requested posting from a PHC to a DH, after duly serving for five years at PHC. However, my request was not accepted, so I decided to take matters into my own hands. I knew a politician and approached him to be posted at DH, and I succeeded.” (State 2, Doctor 3)

Combining quantitative, chronological data with qualitative reflections proved helpful in several ways. First, this enabled us to assess policy implementation by examining how the PT (as shown through quantitative data) was carried out in relation to the six policy norms established in our earlier paper (Purohit and Hill 2023). Our analysis revealed significant gaps in policy norm implementation, with all norms applied inconsistently (Purohit and Hill 2024). The job histories selected in this example (Tables 2 and 3) suggest that duration norms are applied inconsistently, with poor adherence in relation to implementation fidelity. Second, it also allowed us to evaluate how doctors experienced policy norm implementation within the same state, across two states, and between allopathic and AYUSH practitioners. More importantly, it captured policy norm implementation across each individual's employment history. As evident from Tables 2 and 3, most norms are inconsistently applied. This suggests inconsistency in both the frequency and duration of policy implementation, according to the implementation fidelity theory (Dusenbury et al. 2003). Third, it allowed us to make sense of participants’ personal stories and connect these to individual job history events and data. Finally, we identified sensitive and unexpected themes, such as opacity in operational systems, the influence of politics on PT, and power dynamics, which may have been otherwise difficult to capture. For instance, some participants shared sensitive information and openly admitted to using political connections to influence their PT.

Data gathering: using job history as a qualitative approach to produce quantitative data

We employed job history as a qualitative approach to produce qualitative and quantitative data. In the initial nine interviews, respondents organically organized their answers chronologically, reflecting their job histories. Observing this trend, we adapted our approach for the remaining 24 interviews, formalizing it with a structured job history format. Both approaches, while qualitative, offered distinct advantages and limitations. The use of a semi-structured interview guide yielded rich and detailed insights; however, it also presented some practical challenges. First, during the interview process, we were unable to gather specific data about key dates and durations for certain job-related events. This was because the interview guide was not designed to capture such quantitative details. The semi-structured interviews took more time because respondents had to recall and describe their job-specific events and deployment-related experiences. In some cases, time constraints affected how we conducted the interviews—we had to shorten some, limiting the discussion to only a few critical events in their job history.

By adopting a more structured approach for the remaining interviews, we constructed job histories through a set of structured questions followed by qualitative probing. This format provided clearer organization, with the chronological sequence of job events offering a continuous overview of key details, such as dates and durations. From a data collection and analysis perspective, it was easier to organize and synthesize the information around key themes. The sequential structure also facilitated the generation of quantitative data before qualitative probing, though this approach resulted in a less seamless flow of information compared to the semi-structured approach to collecting information.

Nevertheless, the constraints presented by the construction of job history data using a qualitative approach necessitated a follow-up interview that we were unable to conduct. Furthermore, there are constraints and reliability issues in using a qualitative approach to produce quantitative data without validation or triangulation, discussed below in the limitations. Also, PT can be a sensitive topic (Schaaf and Freedman 2015, Sheikh et al. 2015), and like life histories, job histories are highly personal accounts (Witter et al. 2017). Thus, a few respondents were concerned about being identified and feared that sharing their negative experiences could jeopardize their jobs. This was especially true for State 1, a small state with a limited number of public sector doctors, where medical professionals and senior health officials often know each other personally. As a result, some doctors chose not to participate in the study, while three declined to be audio recorded. Despite the issues of fear, we feel that most participants discussed PT issues openly.

Limitations of using job history in the research context

The use of job history has limitations. The first relates to recall bias in specifying the exact dates and durations of different postings held by doctors, which sometimes leads to individual accounts that do not always align with the aggregated data. For example, the time spent by a doctor at each posting should have summed to the total years of work. However, this was not always the case. Another limitation in analyzing the quantitative job history data is that critical individual details are lost during aggregation. For instance, Table 2 shows that out of 17 postings, only 5 (30%) met the duration norms. However, when aggregating the data by calculating the average duration across doctors, the compliance rate rises to 50%. This suggests that by aggregating the data, we risk losing individual patterns, micro-level details, personal stories, and the nuances of each respondent’s job history. A key limitation is the inability to triangulate because of the lack of job history data from the states. Additionally, the absence of documented policies created challenges in applying the conceptual framework and testing the implementation. Although we addressed these issues by establishing policy norms and utilizing self-reported job histories, a key caveat remains: we relied on the same data source—interviews with doctors—to both build policy norms and construct job histories for data triangulation and to assess implementation fidelity. This reliance on a single data source has limitations for data triangulation. Finally, other aspects of broader research, such as access to study participants and sampling limitations, posed challenges to analyzing job histories. For example, we had limited data to evaluate the perceived implementation of certain norms due to the small number of participants, and some experienced only one or two postings. In these cases, the use of job histories is restricted.

Strengths and utility of job histories to health policy and systems research

We operated in a context where official job history data from state health departments were unavailable. Our improvisation in using self-reported job histories and cross-fertilization of the quantitative, chronological data with the qualitative reflections was a rich element of the method, which provided valuable HPSR insights in several ways. First, it helped us explore the complexity of HPSR beyond just the “policy versus practice dichotomy” (Schaaf and Freedman 2015, Purohit and Hill 2024) because these issues operate at individual, political, and structural levels and shape policy outcomes (Sheikh et al. 2015). Second, job histories provided a better understanding of the sensitive nature of deployment issues, such as PT, which involve political dimensions, as well as deeper insights into the complex interactions between actors and processes involved in policy implementation. HPSR researchers argue that a better understanding of these dynamics is critical for unpacking the complex interplay and relationships between actors and agencies (Gilson 2012, Purohit and Hill 2023). Third, it provided an objective measure against which implementation fidelity could be tested. Finally, comparisons of the job histories provided valuable insights into understanding the inconsistencies in policy implementation and how these lead to perceptions of inequity (Purohit and Hill 2023, 2024).

Based on our experience, a job history can offer a framework to capture key job-related events. This has benefits, as shown in Example 2. Considering the research context, the topic, and the fear some respondents expressed about risking their jobs by speaking out against the administration, uncovering important HPSR themes and participants speaking more openly about their PT experiences was unexpected but highly valuable. Another practical benefit of collecting job histories more systematically is the ability to expand the narrative to include more detailed stories as additional variables are added to the job history data. This can illuminate key issues in HPSR research, such as power dynamics, employee centricity, corruption, and the embedded role of politics in the health workforce, which make HPSR issues difficult to investigate (Sheikh et al. 2015).

From a policy and implementation perspective, especially in contexts where documentation and data access are limited, job history provides a nuanced understanding of health workers’ perceptions and experiences with deployment systems and policy implementation. Such insights can inform more effective policy planning and implementation, addressing health workers’ negative perceptions of inconsistent policy delivery. By doing so, it has the potential to foster greater fairness and equity among staff, improving both morale and policy outcomes.

How to use job histories in health policy and systems research?

Despite these limitations, job histories can support the use of both qualitative and quantitative data, thereby improving data triangulation from various sources. We recommend supplementing self-reported job history events with qualitative accounts to enhance triangulation and rigor. We also advise that job history analysis in the public health sector be practical for policy planners and implementers. This involves moving from a basic understanding of doctors’ careers to a more detailed analysis of their job histories, combining quantitative and chronological data with qualitative reflections to gain a deeper understanding of the challenges in HPSR. Job histories should be triangulated and validated using official job history data from states when possible; however, accessing these data can be difficult. If this obstacle is overcome, evaluating the implementation of health workforce policies can be more accurate.

Conclusion

The paper contributes to the literature by demonstrating how job histories can be applied and utilized in complex HPSR environments, offering practical insights for future research. Within the methodological landscape of HPSR, the paper addresses some limitations in the existing literature, including the lack of focus on testing conceptual frameworks and identifying the most effective methods for HPSR analysis (Gilson and Raphaely 2008, Walt et al. 2008). The organic and fluid nature of job histories, grounded in qualitative data, serves as a powerful tool for narrating rich job-related stories and experiences of doctors. Additionally, self-reported job histories provide the flexibility to collect data in a more structured way, potentially revealing valuable chronological patterns. This paper highlights the potential of combining quantitative, chronological data with qualitative reflections, demonstrating how this integration enhances the job history method. It enables researchers to develop a clearer understanding of the complex and often opaque issues in HPSR, which are notoriously difficult to research or might otherwise remain unobserved using traditional approaches, especially in research contexts where documented policies and official job history data are unavailable.
