Targeting the metabolic fingerprint of amino acids to identify novel metabolic characteristics in osteosarcoma patients undergoing anthracycline treatment
Authors: Shanshan Li, Yejiao Hong, Liyan Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracycline (ANT) is a potent chemotherapeutic drug used in the treatment of solid tumors. However, its clinical use is limited by the occurrence of severe cardiotoxicity, which can lead to cardiac dilation and heart failure. Unfortunately, the exact mechanism behind this cardiotoxicity is not well understood. To address this gap in knowledge, our study aimed to investigate the changes in amino acid metabolism before and after receiving ANT treatment. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the metabolic profile, measuring plasma levels of amino acids and other metabolites. 40 Patients with osteosarcoma who were consecutively admitted to Peking University People’s Hospital for ANT treatment were included in this study. Whole blood samples were collected and analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) to quantify the levels of 48 amino acids and metabolites. Our findings indicate that levels of arginine (Arg) were found to be elevated, while threonine (Thr) levels were reduced in the anthracycline (ANT) group. A comparison of metabolites with increased concentrations, including C12:1, C10:1, C12, C10, C5DC, and C8, was conducted against a control group. Subsequently, we assessed eight differential metabolites within the ANT group utilizing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The eight identified amino acids and metabolites demonstrated significant discriminatory power in differentiating patients receiving anthracycline treatment from those who were not. An area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.650 suggests that these biomarkers possess high accuracy in identifying patients with ANT. Furthermore, the optimal sensitivity and specificity values reinforce their efficacy in accurately classifying individuals. The findings indicate that these alterations in amino acids and metabolites may serve as potential metabolic characteristics for predicting the development or presence of conditions before and after receiving ANT treatment. Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common type of primary bone sarcoma, characterized by cells derived from the bone or osteoid, as well as varying amounts of cartilage matrix and fibrous tissue. In growing long tubular bones, it is usually found in the metaphysis. Osteosarcoma can occur at any age, but it is most commonly diagnosed in individuals in their second and third decades of life1,2. The use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy has improved the 5-year survival rate of patients with early stage osteosarcoma to approximately 60–70%3,4.

ANT is a chemotherapeutic drug that is effective in treating solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. However, its use is limited due to the serious side effect of cardiotoxicity, including the development of cardiac dilation and heart failure5. The pathological manifestation of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity (AIC), first identified in 1967, is characterized by a cardiomyopathy that progressively results in heart failure6. The onset of anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy (ANT cardiomyopathy) can occur during the preclinical stage, potentially adversely affecting the cardiovascular prognosis of patients and constraining the efficacy of chemotherapeutic strategies7,8. The risk of AIC increases as the cumulative dose administered increases: 3–5% at 400 mg/m2 and 18–48% at 700 mg/m29,10. However, the mechanisms underlying AIC have not yet been fully elucidated. ANT can cause changes in resting cardiac parameters and dynamic function, a decrease in cardiovascular system parameters, such as left ventricular ejection fraction, damage to cardiac cells and structures, conduction abnormalities, vascular abnormalities, and other side effects that disrupt normal cardiac function11,12. Therefore, understanding the early characteristics of AIC is of great significance for identifying early warning biomarkers and protecting the heart from AIC.

The heart is an energy-intensive organ that relies on a steady supply of glucose, lipids and amino acid (AA) production of ATP to maintain a normal heartbeat rhythm13,14. AA are not only vital building blocks of proteins but also serve as intermediate metabolites that fuel multiple biosynthetic pathways. Disruption in AA metabolism can impair cardiac energy homeostasis and contribute to the progression of heart failure15. Indeed, ANT toxicity has been shown to disturb myocardial metabolic processes, inducing cardiac pathophysiological changes16. Supporting this, a previous study have analyzed more than 270 metabolites in patients with heart failure, and high levels of AA secretion have been found in failing hearts, indicating that various AA metabolism disorders may be related to heart failure17. L-glutamic acid is the key to inhibiting myocardial oxidative damage18. L-isoleucine and L-leucine, which are branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), have been found to play a role in the progression of cardiotoxicity and are associated with dysfunction in energy metabolism19. Therefore, we aimed to analyze the changes in AA and their metabolites in before and after receiving ANT treatment. This analysis has yielded a metabolic signature that is specific to the periods preceding and following the administration of ANT treatment, which may have potential applications in diagnostic or therapeutic contexts.

Materials and methods

Study population

Forty inpatients were recruited from Peking University People’s Hospital between February and March 2023 according to the study criteria. The inclusion criteria for the current study were as follows: (i) All patients had a diagnosis of osteosarcoma confirmed by the postoperative histology. Exclusion criteria were as follows: myocardial infarction, heart failure, myocardial disease, severe kidney disease, recent infection, severe metabolic disease, malnutrition, anemia, recent surgery, liver and hematopoietic disease, autoimmune disease, hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, end-stage patients, amino acid infusion, and any other conditions that might cause significant amino acid changes. The participants in this study were divided into the ANT and No-ANT groups according to whether or not they were taking ANT. In the No-ANT group, patients were newly diagnosed with osteosarcoma and had not had chemotherapy. Patients in the ANT group were treated with ANT chemotherapy at a dose between 250 mg/m2 and 400 mg/m2. And the patients were treated with ANT last about 5 months to 12 months.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations governing human subjects research. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Peking University People’s Hospital Ethics Committee. The need for informed consent was waived by the Peking University People’s Hospital Ethics Committee due to the use of anonymized residual clinical samples with no identifiable personal information.

Chemicals and reagents

All solvents used in this study were of high-purity grade, specifically high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade. NeoBase 2 Non-derivatized Assay Solutions, which can detect AA and their metabolites, were purchased from Wallace Oy Turku, Finland. The NeoBaseTM 2 Non-derivatized Assay Solution package included a bottle of each extraction solution and flow solvent.

Measurement of clinical indicators

Peripheral serum samples were collected from each individual and separated by centrifuging at 3500 g for 5 min. This process helps separate the serum from other blood components. All the indices—glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL- and LDL-cholesterol, creatine kinase, lactate dehydrogenase, albumin, creatinine and urea nitrogen—were determined on an AU5832 automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter). To measure these levels, the analyzer used reagents provided by the original manufacturers. The measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metabolomic analyses

we focused on patients with ANT-treated osteosarcoma and used ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) to analyze their blood samples. We employed a targeted AA metabolomics method based on ultra-performance liquid chromatography–tandem MS (UPLC-MS/MS), which allowed us to identify and quantify the amino acids (AA) and their metabolites in the samples. Metabolomic analyses were performed using a UPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All blood specimens were collected after at least 8 h of fasting into EDTA anticoagulant tubes. And whole blood samples were pretreated as follows. Whole blood was drawn into the designated position of the collection card. (i) Holes were drilled and samples were added using punching forceps to drill dry blood spots into a 96-well plate, and the quality control cloth was controlled according to blank, low control, and high control. (ii) Liquid addition: A total of 125 µL internal standard working fluid was added to each well and the 96-well plate was coated with adhesive film. (iii) Extraction: The 96-well plate with added working solution and sample was placed into a preheated shaking incubator, and extraction occurred at 45 ℃ and 750 rpm for 30 min. (iv) Transfer plate: At an absorption of 100 µ, the supernatant was transferred to the 96-well plate (this can be the same plate), which was coated with adhesive film. (v) Derivatization: The incubated 96-well plate was placed at room temperature or 4 ℃ and allowed to stand for 1 h. (vi) Machine detection.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared between groups using either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the dysregulated metabolites, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) were determined. Youden’s indices were calculated to determine the cutoff points that provided optimal sensitivity and specificity. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software Inc. www.graphpad.com), and R (version 4.1.3; University of Auckland, New Zealand https://www.R-project.org/).

A possible explanation for this may be the loss of muscle in patients with osteosarcoma prior to treatment. The metabolic signature of AA and its metabolites before and after administration ANT treatment

A total of 48 metabolites were reliably quantified in every sample. To obtain an initial overview of the dataset and to pinpoint amino acids (AAs) and their metabolites that differed most strongly between the ANT and No-ANT groups, we first performed unsupervised principal-component analysis (PCA). The resulting score plot showed a tighter clustering of samples within the ANT group, yet failed to achieve clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 1A).

Diagnostic performance of the AA and the metabolites before and after administration ANT treatment

We then evaluated the diagnostic potential of the eight differential metabolites in patients using ROC curves. As shown in Fig. 5; Table 2, Arg exhibited an AUC of 0.688 for discriminating patients in the ANT group from those in the No-ANT group, and the optimal sensitivity and specificity values were 70.0% and 70.0%, respectively. Additionally, Thr exhibited an AUC of 0.682, and its optimal sensitivity and specificity were 70.0% and 70.0%, respectively. C12:1, C10:1, C12, C10, C5DC, and C8 exhibited AUCs of 0.730, 0.795, 0.750, 0.779, 0.791, and 0.798; optimal sensitivity values of 75.0%, 60.0%, 90.0%, 75.0%, 70.0%, and 75.0%; and optimal specificity values of 60.0%, 99.5%, 50.0%, 65.0%, 85.0%, and 75.0%, respectively. These results suggest that AA metabolites have better diagnostic potential for the detection of an AIC population than AA.

The cardiac function of the patient

Figure 6 presents the results of cardiac ultrasound assessments conducted on the two cohorts. The data indicate that the dimensions of the ascending aortic arch and the aortic root have significantly improved in the ANT group; however, there has been no observable change in the left ventricular ejection fraction. The inner diameter of the ascending aortic arch serves as a reflection of cardiac contractility and the volume of cardiac output. An increase in the diameter of the ascending aortic arch may suggest heightened cardiac load or potential cardiac dysfunction. Consequently, ultrasonic measurements of the internal diameter of the ascending aortic arch can serve as a critical indicator of cardiac function and cardiovascular health. Notably, alterations in the internal diameter of the ascending aorta may manifest prior to changes in ejection fraction, which may occur as a delayed response. Therefore, monitoring variations in the internal diameter of the ascending aorta can function as an early warning indicator for AIC, facilitating prompt diagnosis and intervention in the assessment of cardiovascular disease when receiving the ANT.

Discussion

The present study employed targeted metabolomics to delineate specific perturbations in amino acid and acylcarnitine profiles associated with ANT exposure in osteosarcoma patients. Our principal finding reveals a distinctive metabolic signature characterized by elevated arginine and six acylcarnitine species (C12:1, C10:1, C12, C10, C5DC, and C8), concomitant with reduced threonine levels. Notably, these alterations manifested irrespective of demonstrable LVEF decline, suggesting that metabolic derangements may precede conventional echocardiographic manifestations of cardiotoxicity. The discriminative capacity of these biomarkers, particularly acylcarnitines (AUCs: 0.730–0.798), underscores their potential utility for early risk stratification in ANT-treated populations.

Metabolic reprogramming in ANT-induced cardiac stress

The heart’s obligatory reliance on uninterrupted substrate flux for ATP generation renders it acutely vulnerable to chemotherapy-induced metabolic perturbations20. While previous investigations have established that heart failure (HF) precipitates profound amino acid secretion and disrupted catabolism21,22, our data extend these observations by demonstrating that ANT administration alone, in the absence of overt HF, elicits quantifiable metabolic reprogramming. The concurrent elevation of multiple long- and medium-chain acylcarnitines implicates compromised mitochondrial β-oxidation—a pathophysiological hallmark of ANT-induced mitochondrial dysfunction23,24. Accumulation of these intermediates typically reflects incomplete fatty acid oxidation due to mitochondrial respiratory chain impairment or downstream enzyme inhibition, fostering a shift toward alternative fuel source utilization. This metabolic inflexibility aligns with emerging evidence that anthracyclines compromise myocardial energetics through mitochondrial DNA damage25,26 and oxidative stress27,28, thereby disrupting the delicate substrate interplay essential for contractile efficiency.

Amino acid dyshomeostasis: consequence or contributor?

The paradoxical elevation of Arg alongside Thr depletion warrants nuanced mechanistic consideration. Arg constitutes a pivotal precursor for nitric oxide (NO) synthesis via endothelial nitric oxide synthase29,30, suggesting that increased Arg availability may represent a compensatory response to ANT-induced microvascular dysfunction and oxidative stress. However, uncoupling of NO synthase activity under oxidative conditions can paradoxically exacerbate reactive oxygen species generation, potentially perpetuating myocardial injury31. Conversely, Thr reduction may reflect enhanced protein catabolism or shunting toward glycine and one-carbon metabolism pathways, which are upregulated in response to cellular stress32. Although we did not quantify branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) in our panel, previous reports have implicated BCAA accumulation in mTOR-mediated insulin resistance and contractile dysfunction22,33. The prognostic relevance of BCAA metabolic byproducts, particularly their capacity to impair insulin signaling through mTOR hyperactivation, establishes a compelling parallel with our observations of altered Thr and Arg dynamics34,35. While our findings cannot establish causality, they converge with preclinical data demonstrating that modulation of BCAA flux via branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase inhibition restores metabolic homeostasis and attenuates cardiotoxicity. Whether the identified metabolites actively propagate cardiac injury or merely reflect downstream epiphenomena of myocardial stress remains a critical unanswered question requiring functional validation.

Clinical implications and translational potential

From a clinical perspective, the superior diagnostic performance of acylcarnitines over free amino acids suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction, rather than global protein turnover disruption, may constitute the primary metabolic lesion in early-stage AIC. This has immediate implications for surveillance strategies: integrating a targeted metabolite panel into routine on-treatment monitoring could enable detection of subclinical cardiac insult weeks to months before LVEF deterioration becomes apparent. Such an approach would facilitate timely cardiology referral and preemptive initiation of cardioprotective interventions36(e.g., dexrazoxane37, ACE inhibitors38. Furthermore, the identification of specific pathway derangements opens avenues for therapeutic targeting—whether through Thr supplementation to correct amino acid imbalance or Arg manipulation to optimize NO bioavailability—though rigorous preclinical safety studies are imperative to ensure oncological efficacy is not compromised.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Several caveats temper the interpretation of our findings. First, the case-control design precludes establishing temporal relationships or causal inference; prospective longitudinal studies are essential to validate these metabolites as predictive rather than associative markers. Second, the modest sample size (n = 40) increases susceptibility to selection bias and limits generalizability across diverse ANT dosing regimens and cancer types. Third, the absence of concurrent LVEF decline or cardiac biomarker data (e.g., troponin, BNP) constrains our ability to correlate metabolic changes with definitive cardiotoxicity endpoints, a limitation imposed by the latency of clinically overt cardiac dysfunction. Fourth, potential confounders such as dietary intake, physical activity, and concomitant medications were not controlled, though stringent exclusion criteria mitigated major metabolic disturbances. Finally, the exclusive enrollment of osteosarcoma patients, typically younger than those receiving ANT for other malignancies, may limit extrapolation to older, comorbid populations with distinct baseline metabolic profiles.

Future directions and concluding remarks

In summary, this investigation provides preliminary evidence that targeted metabolic profiling can uncover early metabolic fingerprints of ANT exposure, positioning AIC as a potentially detectable and modifiable metabolic disorder rather than an inevitable structural sequela. The convergence of amino acid and acylcarnitine disturbances implicates mitochondrial β-oxidation failure as a central pathogenic mechanism, meriting exploration in mechanistic animal models and larger multi-center cohorts. Future research should prioritize longitudinal metabolomic surveillance integrated with advanced cardiac imaging (e.g., strain echocardiography, cardiac MRI) to definitively establish the predictive value of these biomarkers and inform the development of personalized cardio-oncology protocols. Until such validation is achieved, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, underscoring the urgent need for innovative strategies to preserve cardiovascular health without compromising oncological outcomes.
