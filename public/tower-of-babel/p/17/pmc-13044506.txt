Normalization of Potassium Despite 11-Deoxycorticosterone Rise During Etomidate Therapy in Adrenocortical Carcinoma
Authors: Olivia Bhattacharya, Arijit Singha, Subhasis Neogi, Subhankar Chowdhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is a rare malignancy often associated with disorganized steroid synthesis, leading to variable clinical phenotypes. Etomidate, an imidazole derivative, is a potent inhibitor of adrenal cytochrome P450-dependent enzymes, particularly 11β-hydroxylase (CYP11B1). Subhypnotic doses of etomidate are effective in reducing serum cortisol in patients with severe Cushing syndrome, a common manifestation in ACC. However, the effects of etomidate on other adrenal hormones in ACC remain poorly understood. In this report, we measured 13 steroid hormones before and after etomidate infusion using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry in a patient with ACC. Etomidate administration led to marked suppression of cortisol and aldosterone, while 11-deoxycorticosterone levels increased substantially. Interestingly, despite the elevated 11-deoxycorticosterone, serum potassium normalized, highlighting a complex interplay among mineralocorticoid precursors and active hormones in maintaining electrolyte balance.Keywords: adrenocortical cancer, etomidate, deoxycorticosterone, LC-MS/MS, kaliuretic potency, blood pressure, steroid profile

Introduction

Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is a rare malignancy often associated with disorganized steroid synthesis leading to variable clinical phenotypes. In ACC, excessive cortisol secretion can lead to severe Cushing syndrome, which is characterized by markedly elevated cortisol level (random cortisol > 41 µg/dL [SI: 1131.03 nmol/L] or a 24-hour urinary cortisol > 5-fold upper normal limit) and/or severe hypokalemia (<3.0 mmol/L). Life-threatening complications related to hypercortisolemia, which includes sepsis, refractory hypokalemia, uncontrolled hypertension, heart failure, gastrointestinal bleeding, thromboembolism and uncontrolled hyperglycemia, or ketosis, highlight the need for urgent effective cortisol-lowering interventions [1]. Etomidate, an imidazole derivative, is a potent inhibitor of adrenal cytochrome P450-dependent enzymes predominantly 11β-hydroxylase enzyme and to some extent 17α-hydroxylase and cholesterol side chain cleavage enzyme [2, 3]. Subhypnotic dose of etomidate is quite effective to reduce serum cortisol in patient with severe Cushing syndrome [4-6]. However, the effects of etomidate on other adrenal hormones in ACC remain poorly understood. Traditionally immunoassays have been used to measure serum cortisol level to assess response to etomidate therapy. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) has now replaced immunoassays as the gold standard for the measurement of steroid hormones. Although data regarding the measurement of various steroid hormones by LC-MS/MS are available, there is a notable paucity of reports that have systematically described changes in these hormones after etomidate therapy using the same platform. In this report, we measured 13 steroid hormones both before and after etomidate infusion by LC-MS/MS method in a patient with ACC.

Case Presentation

A 22-year-old female presented with a 1-year history of intermittent lower abdominal pain, amenorrhea and progressive facial hirsutism. For the previous 3 days, she had experienced painful pustular eruptions on her face and neck. She had been diagnosed with hypertension and diabetes mellitus 1 year prior, for which she was prescribed oral medications. At the time of presentation, she was taking metformin 500 mg 2 times per day and amlodipine 5 mg once per day. Additional complaints included proximal muscle weakness, thin skin, generalized weakness, fatigue, and recent unintentional weight loss. The patient was slightly restless. There was no history of fractures, galactorrhea, or voice deepening. Furthermore, no history of alternative medicine or steroid use was reported, nor any indication of adrenergic spells. On examination, her body weight was 40 kg, height 145 cm, and body mass index 19.02 kg/m2. Supine blood pressure measured 142/96 mm Hg, with no postural drop. The modified Ferriman–Gallwey score was 26/36, and pustular acneiform eruptions on her face, neck, and chest. Wide, broad-based purplish striae were also observed over the sides of her abdomen. Clitoromegaly was absent.

Diagnostic Assessment

Laboratory investigations revealed hemoglobin 10.2 g/dL (SI: 100 g/L) (reference range: 12.1-15.1 g/dL [SI: 121-151 g/L]), total leukocyte count 12 000 (reference range: 4000-11000) (neutrophil 80%, lymphocytes 20%, eosinophil 0%), urea 22 mg/dL (SI: 3.66 mmol/L) (reference range: 17-43 mg/dL [SI: 2.83-7.15 mmol/L]), creatinine 0.9 mg/dL (SI: 0.07 mmol/L) (reference range: 0.66-1.09 mg/dL [SI: 0.05-0.09 mmol/L]), fasting plasma glucose 220 mg/dL (SI: 12.21 mmol/L) (reference range: 70-110 mg/dL [SI: 3.88-6.10 mmol/L], 2-hour postmeal plasma glucose 308 mg/dL (SI: 17.09 mmol/L) (reference range: 80-140 mg/dL [SI: 4.4-7.8 mmol/L]), glycated hemoglobin 7.4% (57 mmol/mol) (normal < 5.7% [39 mmol/L]), and urine ketone negative. Hormonal evaluation (Immulite 1000, Siemens, Germany) revealed serum FSH 3.10 mIU/mL (reference range: 2.5-10.2 mIU/mL), LH 2.9 mIU/mL (reference range: 1.9-12.5 mIU/mL), cortisol 54.73 µg/dL (SI: 1509 nmol/L) (reference range: 4.3-22.4 μg/dL [SI: 118.6-617.9 nmol/L]), 24-hour urinary free cortisol- 2000 µg/24 hours (SI: 5520 nmol/24 hours) (reference range: 19.3-317.5 μg/24 hours [SI: 53.2-875.1 nmol/24 hours]), plasma ACTH 8 pg/mL (SI: 1.76 pmol/L) (reference range: 0.00-46.0 pg/mL [SI: 0.00-10.12 pmol/L]), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate > 1000 µg/dL (SI: 27.14 μmol/L) (reference range: 25.9-460.2 μg/dL [SI: 0.70-12.48 μmol/L]), total testosterone 156 ng/dL (SI: 5.4 nmol/L) (reference range: 14-76 ng/dL [SI: 0.48-2.63 nmol/L]), androstenedione 9.06 ng/mL (SI: 31.63 nmol/L) (reference range: 0.3-3.5 ng/mL [SI: 1.04-12.22 nmol/L]), prolactin 10.4 ng/mL (SI: 10.4 μg/L) (reference range: 2.8-29.2 ng/mL [SI: 2.8-29.2 μg/L]), free T4 1.34 ng/dL (SI: 17.24 pmol/L) (reference range: 0.89-1.76 ng/dL [SI: 11.45-22.65 pmol/L]), and TSH 2.5 μIU/mL (reference range: 0.55-4.78). Computed tomography scan of abdomen revealed a large heterogeneous mass (17.6 cm × 11.2 cm × 11.8 cm) with areas of necrosis and calcifications in the left adrenal gland. The unenhanced attenuation ranged from 36 to 45 Hounsfield units. On contrast-enhanced imaging, the mass demonstrated heterogenous enhancement with irregular margins. A few lymph nodes suggestive of metastasis were also noted in the retroperitoneal region (Fig. 1). Considering the clinical, hormonal, and radiological profile a diagnosis of adrenocortical carcinoma (ENSAT stage III, T2N1M0) was made.

Treatment

Metformin was discontinued, and the patient was started on basal-bolus insulin regimen for glycemic control. Low-molecular-weight heparin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole were added for prophylaxis against venous thromboembolism and Pneumocystis jiroveci infection, respectively. As metyrapone and osilodrostat were not locally available, and ketoconazole was not initiated because of elevated liver enzymes, she was shifted to the intensive care unit and etomidate injection was commenced to control the hypercortisolemia. Etomidate injection (propylene glycol, 2 mg/mL) diluted with 5% dextrose in water in 1:1 ratio to form a 1 mg/mL concentration. A bolus dose of 3 mg etomidate injection was given intravenously over 60 seconds [7], following which, blood pressure rapidly increased to 184/112 mm Hg from the baseline (142/96 mm Hg). Subsequently, infusion was started at a rate of 0.01 mg/kg/hour. The dose of etomidate was up titrated every 6 hours to target the serum cortisol level between 10 μg/dL (SI: 275.86 nmol/L) to 20 μg/dL (SI: 551.72 nmol/L). Vital parameters and Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale were monitored hourly and biochemical parameters including serum cortisol were monitored every six hours. Serum cortisol was measured by chemiluminescence assay. In addition, steroid profile was performed in LC-MS/MS assay platform at baseline and after 72 hours of etomidate infusion (Table 1). After achieving the therapeutic target cortisol, etomidate infusion was stopped at 96 hours.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Baseline pH, bicarbonate, sodium, potassium, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase were 7.47 (reference range: 7.35-7.45), 26.35 mmol/L (reference range: 22-26 mmol/L), 145.5 mmol/L (reference range: 136-146 mmol/L), 2.8 mmol/L (reference range: 3.5-5.1 mmol/L), 91 U/L (reference range: 13-33 U/L), and 198 U/L (reference range: 6-27 U/L) respectively. Serum potassium became normal (4.1 mmol/L) at 48 hours, pH became 7.42 at 72 hours, aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase improved significantly at 96 hours of etomidate infusion (59 U/L and 43 U/L, respectively). Within 48 hours of infusion, blood pressure decreased to 110/70 mm Hg, allowing discontinuation of antihypertensive medicine. Plasma glucose levels improved significantly, and subcutaneous insulin was stopped after 24 hours. Thereafter, both blood pressure and glycemic status remained stable without the need for glucose-lowering or antihypertensive therapy (Table 2).

Following initial stabilization of metabolic parameters and suppression of hypercortisolism with etomidate, the patient was transitioned to oral ketoconazole and spironolactone. As mitotane was not available, a multidisciplinary oncology team planned definitive management with laparotomy and combination chemotherapy (etoposide, doxorubicin, and cisplatin). Unfortunately, the patient died before the planned surgery.

Discussion

Our patient had severe Cushing syndrome and was treated with etomidate infusion to decrease the cortisol level rapidly. The target cortisol was achieved after 72 hours of infusion. Previous reports indicate that the median time to reach target cortisol in patients with severe Cushing syndrome caused by ACC was 48 hours (IQR: 24-86.75 hours). Additionally, patients managed in intensive care unit who received a higher starting infusion rate required less time to achieve the target [6, 8]. Serum cortisol (measured by CLIA method) reduced from 54.73 µg/dL (SI: 1509 nmol/L) to 15 μg/dL (SI: 413.79 nmol/L). The corresponding cortisol value measured by LC-MS/MS was 22.2 μg/dL (SI: 612.41 nmol/L) and 7.63 μg/dL (SI: 210.48 nmol/L), respectively. The observed difference of cortisol level between the 2 methods remains to be determined and was possibly be due to assay interferences with other steroid metabolites which includes 17α-hydroxyprogesterone, 21-deoxycortisol, and 11-deoxycortisol. 11-deoxycortisol was increased by 2-fold from the baseline; however, the kit insert of Immulite 1000 indicates that level of 11-deoxycortisol up to 1000 μg/L (SI: 2886 nmol/L) and DHEAS up to 10 000 μg/dL (SI: 271.39 μmol/L) does not interfere with the cortisol immunoassay [9, 10]. This underscores the critical importance of using LC-MS/MS for cortisol measurement, rather than immunoassays to ensure accurate dose titration.

Other hormones that were reduced by at least 2-fold from the baseline were aldosterone, DHEA, DHEAS, and testosterone; however, no significant change was observed in 17-α- hydroxyprogesterone, progesterone, and androstenedione levels. Serum 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) increased from 0.05 µg/L at baseline to 1.31 µg/L—approximately 8-fold above the upper limit of normal and 20-fold above the baseline level. In the systematic review of 46 DOC-producing adrenal tumors and hyperplasia by Asla et al, reported DOC concentrations ranged from 1.5 to 79 times the upper limit of normal, frequently associated with hypertension and hypokalemia. Thus, the magnitude of DOC elevation in our case falls within the pathogenic range [11]. Other disorders associated with elevated DOC level are 11β-hydroxylase deficiency, 17α-hydroxylase deficiency, and ACC. Furthermore, treatment with 11β-hydroxylase inhibitors such as metyrapone can also increase DOC level, potentially leading to hypokalemia [12]. In our patient, because of the rise in DOC levels, we could have expected worsening of hypokalemia; however, the potassium level was stable initially and improved subsequently with continued etomidate infusion. Mineralocorticoid action has 2 aspects: sodium retention and kaliuretic action. Mineralocorticoid receptor selectivity of cortisol is primarily mediated by 11-β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11-βHSD2), which converts cortisol to inactive cortisone. Excess cortisol can overwhelm the 11-β HSD2 enzyme activity, resulting in activation of mineralocorticoid receptor and subsequently causes hypokalemia [13]. Although the sodium retention activity of DOC is comparable to aldosterone and cortisol, the kaliuretic potential is far less compared to both of them [14]. In our patient, there was significant decrease in both the aldosterone level (4-fold) and cortisol levels (3-fold). This substantial decrease in aldosterone and cortisol may have balanced kaliuretic effects of DOC.

Our patient was restless at the time of presentation, which improved following control of hypercortisolemia. This was in line with the earlier observations where etomidate infusion was given in individuals with cortisol induced psychosis [2, 15, 16]. Furthermore, in our patient, liver enzymes were elevated before initiation of etomidate and they decreased subsequently when hypercortisolemia was controlled. However, etomidate may cause hepatotoxicity on rare occasion [17].

Though etomidate infusion therapy is relatively safe, several side effects have been documented in the literature. Sedation generally occurs at higher doses than those required to suppress cortisol production; nevertheless, level of sedation should be assessed using the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale score [18]. Adrenal insufficiency may occur in standard dose regimen and periodic monitoring of cortisol level is essential to titrate the dose of etomidate accordingly [17]. Interestingly, after bolus dose of etomidate, the patient’s blood pressure rapidly increased. Hence, lower infusion rate (0.01 mg/kg/hour) was started. Etomidate has minimal effects on cardiovascular system and reduces blood pressure by decreasing the peripheral vascular resistance. However, transient rise of blood pressure has been documented in a mice study, possibly due to alpha-2B receptor agonistic activity [19]. To the best of our knowledge, this transient hypertensive effect has not been reported earlier in humans.

This case also highlights the need for a low threshold to investigate secondary endocrine causes in young patients with hypertension or diabetes without traditional risk factors, especially when signs of androgen excess are present. Early recognition of such features may enable timely diagnosis of adrenocortical carcinoma and improve outcomes.

In conclusion, we observed that despite the rise of DOC, serum potassium normalized during etomidate infusion. This observation underscores the importance of considering net mineralocorticoid activity rather than single steroid level while monitoring electrolyte homeostasis during etomidate therapy. In addition, careful monitoring of blood pressure is warranted especially after a bolus dose.
