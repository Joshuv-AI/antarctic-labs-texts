Nothing in life is to be feared; it is only to be understood
Authors: Johanna Wiedemann, Claus Cursiefen, Peter Wiedemann
Published: 2025

Uncertainty, risk, and understanding

The phrase "Nothing in life is to be feared; it is only to be understood." is attributed to Marie Skłodowska-Curie. Fear often stems from uncertainty, which frequently arises from a lack of knowledge and understanding. Uncertainty is not an inherent feature of the world—it is rather a reflection of our relationship and interaction with it, shaped by what we know and what we do not. People's perception of risk importance can differ, even when the actual risk remains unchanged, and therefore, people may interpret the same risk differently. Probability is one way to measure and communicate uncertainty or risk. Differences in risk perception are inherent to being human and illustrate why judgment is always involved when we encounter an unknown and must make a decision on how to deal with it.1

Informed consent—more than a signature

Informed consent is "more than a signature on a piece of paper".2 The fundamental principle of medical ethics is autonomy. It protects a patient's right to make their own healthcare decisions.3 It requires providing all necessary information for informed, voluntary consent and is legally required for interventions. Valid consent involves three key criteria: patient competence, adequate information, and the absence of undue influence. Informed consent includes: a clear explanation of the diagnosis (if known), the nature and purpose of treatment, likely benefits, risks, burdens, and all reasonable alternatives, including declining treatment. This conversation should be tailored to the individual and documented. Physicians should disclose potential conflicts of interest, such as consulting for device manufacturers.2 In surgical patients, physicians providing information should have independent experience and a thorough understanding of the surgery and its potential complications. Historically, the paternalistic "doctor knows best" approach has dominated; however, medicine has shifted toward a more collaborative approach, emphasizing shared decision-making. Today, informed consent is a conversation, not a monologue; it involves clarifying the patient's understanding, exploring their goals and values, and working toward a mutually reasonable decision.4

Communicating risk clearly

People's understanding of and reactions to risk are as significant as objective risk itself. This perception can be influenced by various "risk attributes" and personal concerns, making it subjective. A proper perspective is crucial for making informed consent a more truly "informed" decision.

People's reactions to risk are shaped by information presentation. How information is presented influences patients' risk perception and decisions. Framing information as a gain makes patients more confident in treatment than when presented as a loss.5 Tailoring risk information to individual factors increases engagement and understanding, enhancing decision-making.6 Risk communication should be an open exchange using plain language.5 Instead of "endothelial keratoplasty", a doctor might say, "a surgery on the cornea, the windscreen of the eye, to replace the inner layer of it, which keeps it clear." Explaining likely and serious outcomes helps patients weigh risks and benefits. Numbers are essential; terms like "high risk" or "low risk" are less useful. Risks can be described as percentages, absolute risk reduction (ARR), relative risk reduction (RRR), and number needed to treat (NNT). Risk information should be presented in multiple ways with consistent denominators to improve comprehension. Visual aids like diagrams, charts, videos, (virtual) 3D models, and the Paling Perspective Scale, which compares medical risks to familiar risks, help patients understand complex ideas.7 The "teach-back" method—asking patients to repeat key points—helps confirm understanding.

The challenge of consent forms

Consent forms worldwide are often too long, complex, and filled with medical jargon. Studies show most are written above average reading levels.8 Many use a "one-size-fits-all" approach, failing to address specific procedures.9 Better forms should be shorter, more precise, and tailored to treatments, including diagrams and culturally sensitive language. However, improving forms is only part of the solution; understanding comes from meaningful conversation.6 Recommendations include providing (more) time for explanations and assessing patient understanding.

How to improve the consent process

Consent may be legally valid with limited patient understanding; therefore, we must ensure ethical validity. Healthcare professionals can provide informed consent effectively by allocating time for discussion, using plain language, including visuals to break down information, tailoring to patient background, checking understanding, and updating processes based on feedback. The most lasting impression patients have of informed consent is the surgeon's attitude of respect or lack thereof.10 Videos including easy-to-understand diagrams and plain information materials given to the patient well ahead of the consent conversation can be helpful, too.

Can telemedicine or AI help?

Emerging tools like telemedicine, large language models (LLMs), and virtual reality can make medical information more accessible.9 Artificial Intelligence (AI) can simplify medical jargon, create personalized explanations, and present risks clearly. Research suggests these tools improve understanding and patient satisfaction and reduce anxiety.11 However, AI can introduce errors, bias, or oversimplify critical details.12 Regarding LLMs in surgical informed consent, we must differentiate active and passive empathy. LLMs should be limited to recognizing patient distress rather than simulating empathy.9 AI should enhance, not replace, human communication. Emotional intelligence and human empathy remain crucial in patient-centered care. Transparency in AI's role, privacy protection for health information, and cultural sensitivity are essential for maintaining patient trust.

Conclusions

Informed consent is both a legal requirement and an ethical commitment. It is not simply a document but an ongoing dialogue between patient and clinician. Through thoughtful communication, personalized explanations, and careful use of new technologies, informed consent can truly empower patients to make decisions that are informed, voluntary, and in their best interest.
