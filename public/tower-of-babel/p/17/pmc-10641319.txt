FLASH Radiotherapy: A FLASHing Idea to Preserve Neurocognitive Function
Authors: Hye-Ju Jo, Taerim Oh, Ye-Rim Lee, Gi-Sue Kang, Hye-Joon Park, G-One Ahn
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH RT) is a technique to deliver ultra-high dose rate in a fraction of a second. Evidence from experimental animal models suggest that FLASH RT spares various normal tissues including the lung, gastrointestinal track, and brain from radiation-induced toxicity (a phenomenon known as FLASH effect), which is otherwise commonly observed with conventional dose rate RT. However, it is not simply the ultra-high dose rate alone that brings the FLASH effect. Multiple parameters such as instantaneous dose rate, pulse size, pulse repetition frequency, and the total duration of exposure all need to be carefully optimized simultaneously. Furthermore it is critical to validate FLASH effects in an in vivo experimental model system. The exact molecular mechanism responsible for this FLASH effect is not yet understood although a number of hypotheses have been proposed including oxygen depletion and less reactive oxygen species (ROS) production by FLASH RT, and enhanced ability of normal tissues to handle ROS and labile iron pool compared to tumors. In this review, we briefly overview the process of ionization event and history of radiotherapy and fractionation of ionizing radiation. We also highlight some of the latest FLASH RT reviews and results with a special interest to neurocognitive protection in rodent model with whole brain irradiation. Lastly we discuss some of the issues remain to be answered with FLASH RT including undefined molecular mechanism, lack of standardized parameters, low penetration depth for electron beam, and tumor hypoxia still being a major hurdle for local control. Nevertheless, researchers are close to having all answers to the issues that we have raised, hence we believe that advancement of FLASH RT will be made more quickly than one can anticipate.

RADIOTHERAPY, FRACTIONATION (HYPOFRACTIONATION VS. HYPERFRACTIONATION), AND NEUROCOGNITIVE DYSFUNCTION CAUSED BY BRAIN RADIATION THERAPY

Ionizing radiation (IR), where electrons are being accelerated in a coiled compartment of the linear accelerator (LINAC) producing a high energy as X-ray photon beam is the most widely available type of radiotherapy (RT) in the clinic [1]. When the photon penetrates in the body, it interacts with an atom, the most basic and smallest unit composing a molecule, kicking off the most outer electron from its orbit (Fig. 1) [2]. This ejected electron is able to break the covalent bond of a water molecule, which consists of approximately 70%–80% of a typical mammalian cell (Fig. 1) [2], generating various types of radicals including hydrated electron (eaq-), hydrogen radical (H•), and hydroxyl radical (OH•) as well as ions such as hydrogen ion (H+) and hydronium (H3O+), thereby yielding an “ionization event” (Fig. 1) [2]. Among these, OH• is the most electron-affinic and can interact with DNA to produce DNA radical (DNA•) (Fig. 1) [3] and this process is known as an “indirect action” of IR (i.e., producing DNA damage through series of chemical reaction) (Fig. 1). “Direct action” of IR where the high energy electron released by photon can directly attack DNA double strand is also possible (Fig. 1) [2] although it is generally believed that the indirect action of IR is more cytotoxic to cells [2]. IR-induced DNA damage (i.e., DNA• in Fig. 1) then has 2 faiths: in the presence of the molecular oxygen (O2), DNA• becomes DNA-OO• (also known as DNA damage being “fixed”), which ultimately leads to cell death (Fig. 1) [4]. However, in the absence of oxygen (e.g., under hypoxic conditions), DNA• reacts with various macromolecules within a cell, particularly SH-containing proteins thereby undergoing “hydrogen abstraction” reaction, resulting in formation of DNA-H, the original and undamaged form of DNA (Fig. 1) [4]. Because hypoxia (low in oxygen tension) has been shown to be a common feature in all solid tumors of humans as well as animals [4], this oxygen-dependency for IR-induced cell kill allows hypoxic cancer cells to selectively resist against cytotoxic action of RT [4]. To overcome this problem of tumor hypoxia, numerous attempts have been made including the use of hyperbaric oxygen (100% O2) or carbogen (95% O2/5% CO2) breathing, radiosensitizers, and hypoxia-selective cytotoxins [45], although none of these strategies has made to the clinic.

Recently, researchers and clinicians turn their interests to a different type of radiation such as proton or carbon ion RT because of their unique characteristics, offering an advantage over IR. These types of RT are known as high linear energy transfer (LET), where LET is defined as energy transferred per unit length of track [6]. High LET can directly attack DNA double strand much more efficiently than X-ray or γ-ray (which belongs to low LET) [6]. As these DNA double strand breaks are more dense in nature and complicated in its forms, they do not depend on the molecular oxygen to induce cell death [7]. Therefore high LET-type RT can potentially avoid “tumor hypoxia resistance” problem, with which IR had been struggling. Furthermore, high LET-type RT has unique physical characteristic, where the deposited energy along the track is initially much lower than X-ray but can suddenly be increased to maximal level, the phenomenon known as “Bragg Peak” (Fig. 2) [7]. This property allows one to maximize the dose delivery precisely to the deep-seated tumors such as those in the brain while sparing the adjacent normal tissues [8].

Because of the long history with the external beam RT [9] so does the radiobiological understanding with IR. In the kilovoltage era (1900–1940), LINAC suffered from limited technical capability, which can only offer 2D projection of radiation to treat tumors (Fig. 3). During this era, Regaud et al. [10] have performed the seminal radiobiological experiment where they irradiated the scrotum of ram to sterilize the testis. With a series of trial and error experiments, the investigators have realized that multiple small fraction is superb than a single large fraction of irradiation in sparing the scrotum although the both regimen have resulted in comparable efficacy of sterilization [1112]. Through this experiment, the testis has been considered as a model system for “proliferating (i.e., tumor) compartment” and the skin of scrotum as “normal tissue compartment” [12]. Based on this concept, traditional RT regimen has been implemented as “hyperfractionation of small doses” (for example, 2 Gy/fraction up to 60–80 fractions) to control the tumors as well as to preserve the normal tissue simultaneously [11]. From the megavoltage era (1946–1996) (Fig. 3) to computer-assisted era (1996–2012) (Fig. 3), there was a significant advancement in the technology derived from medical physics. Introduction of 3D treatment planning system with CT-based simulation, higher powered X-ray beams produced by LINAC, mechanical improvements including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), stereotactic radiotherapy, and image-guided radiotherapy have allowed more precise and accurate targeting of tumors [11]. The ability to deliver such high doses of radiation selectively to tumor-bearing volume has also changed the treatment regimen dramatically: “hypofractionation at larger doses” (for example, up to 60 Gy in 10 fractions in glioblastoma [13]) has shown to achieve very high local control rates [14], even to the level comparable to surgical resection in some solid tumors [15].

RT is widely prescribed to brain tumor patients including pediatric patients [16] and those with brain metastases treated with whole brain radiotherapy [17]. However, RT-induced neurocognitive dysfunction is a devastating problem, severely compromising the quality of life. Neurocognitive decline, including memory, spatial processing, and dementia can manifest many months to years after irradiation and become progressively worse [1819]. Evidence in animal model suggests that radiation-induced neurocognitive decline involves damages in multiple cell types in the brain including endothelial cells, neurons, microglia, and astrocytes causing structural and functional alterations [19]. Current attempts to reduce the radiation-induced cognitive deficits include hippocampal sparing whole brain RT (selective avoidance of the hippocampal neural stem cell compartment with IMRT), stereotactic radiosurgery (highly conformal radiation beam to treat tumors in the brain and spine), and some pharmacological interventions such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α and -γ agonists, minocycline, and memantine [19].

FLASH RADIOTHERAPY: WHAT IS IT AND WHY IS IT SO DIFFERENT?

FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH RT) is a latest RT technique to deliver certain doses of radiation in an ultra-high dose rate manner, pioneered by Dr. Loo Jr at Stanford University in US [20] and Dr. Vozenin at Lausanne University in Switzerland [21], with an initial motivation to reduce motion effects [22] during IMRT (personal communication with Dr. Loo Jr, 2015). Dr. Loo Jr’s group has initially modified clinical LINAC to produce electron beam FLASH RT [20] (Fig. 4A) while Dr. Vozenin’s group has built a custom-made LINAC (Kinetron and Oriatron) to perform their FLASH research. In the Loo Jr group setting, preclinical models including mice and cell lines are positioned on the experimental platform, which sits in the inner jaws of the head of the modified LINAC set in an inverted configuration in order to reduce source-to-surface distance (SSD) thereby maximize the dose rate (Fig. 4A) [20]. In their latest configuration (Fig. 4B), SSD has been significantly increased allowing sufficient working distance to apply the ultra-high dose-rate radiation therapy (UHDR RT) to human patients in the clinical setting [23].

Initially FLASH RT has been defined with the mean dose rate ≥40 Gy/s (whereas conventional dose rate radiotherapy [CONV RT] is ≥0.01 Gy/s) [24] although we now know that it is not simply the “ultra-high dose rate” that brings the FLASH effects. Many studies consistently emphasize that multiple parameters including total dose delivered, radiation sources (e.g., electrons, protons, heavy particles, photons), irradiated volume, overall delivery time, and pulse-related factors such as dose and dose rate per pulse, pulse size, and pulse frequency all can affect the success of bringing out the FLASH effect [24]. Indeed, there are a number of groups reporting the lack of FLASH effects even though ultra-high dose rate and other parameters suitable for FLASH RT had been utilized (Fig. 5; [2526] and references therein).

FLASH effect is a phenomenon where normal tissues including the lung [21], gastrointestinal tract [27], and brain [28] can be significantly spared from radiation-induced toxicity when irradiated with FLASH RT [24]. Interestingly, the local tumor control has been shown to be comparable between FLASH and CONV RT [2127]. Several potential mechanisms have been suggested to explain the FLASH effect. Favaudon et al. [21] have demonstrated that transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) activation, which would normally occur with CONV RT [29] does not occur with FLASH RT. Furthermore, reduction in apoptotic bronchial epithelial cells and endothelial cells has been observed in the lung irradiated with FLASH RT [21]. Similarly, Levy et al. [27] have reported decreased apoptosis and DNA damages, and increase in proliferation of the crypt base columnar cells of the abdomen of mice irradiated with FLASH RT. In the FLASH RT-irradiated brain, decreased astrogliosis and microgliosis has been observed [2830]. Regardless of all, the most convincing potential mechanism for FLASH RT is the “oxygen depletion” theory proposed by Vozenin and colleagues [2526] where the ultra-high dose rate IR can instantaneously consume the oxygen thereby making the normal tissues temporarily hypoxic hence resistant to IR-induced damages. In fact, the oxygen depletion theory has been demonstrated in 1959 by Dewey and Boag [31] where they have found that the survival curve of Serratia marcescens bacteria grown in 1% oxygen (O2) and then irradiated with ultra-high dose rate (1×108 Gy/s) in a single pulse of electron is comparable to that grown in 100% nitrogen, suggesting that 1% O2 was completely depleted by the ultra-high dose rate electron pulse. Recently, however, El Khatib et al. [32] have measured the oxygen dynamics using phosphorescence quenching method in the leg muscles of mice before and after proton FLASH RT and found that a clinically relevant single high dose (30 Gy) FLASH RT is insufficient to make tissues severely hypoxic, suggesting that there is a possible alternative explanation of FLASH effect.

IN VIVO VALIDATION IS CRITICALLY REQUIRED TO PROVE FLASH EFFECT

One essential criterion to evaluate FLASH effect is to use in vivo, but not in vitro model systems to validate the finding. Vozenin et al. [33] emphasize that the oxygen level in aerobic cells cultured at 21% O2 is too high to be depleted with FLASH RT. In fact they have stated that there are at least 13 studies reporting no FLASH effect when irradiated aerobic cells [33]. Moreover, they have suggested that 90 Gy is required to deplete such oxygen to anoxic level bringing out the FLASH effect [33]. Nonetheless, there are a couple of in vitro studies reporting the FLASH effect. For example, Berry and Hall in 1969 [34] have shown that cultured Chinese hamster cells and HeLa cells sealed in 95% air/5% CO2 for 24 hr until irradiation are more radioresistant (i.e., “FLASH effect” equivalent) when irradiated with X-ray pulses delivered in nanoseconds (mean dose rates 0.3–2.0 × 109 Gy/s) compared to those with CONV RT (1 Gy/min). Adrian et al. [35] have recently reported that FLASH effect depends on the oxygen concentration by demonstrating that clonogenic cell survival of prostate cancer cells is significantly increased at low oxygen partial pressure (FLASH effect observed at 1.6%, 2.7%, or 4.4% O2 but not at 8.3% or 20% O2). Some of in vivo model systems that have been utilized to validate the FLASH effect include zebrafish embryo model measuring the fish length, survival, and rate of edema; mouse model measuring the lung fibrosis, gastrointestinal toxicity, neurocognitive decline, and skin fibrosis/necrosis; and mini-pig model measuring skin toxicity [26]. However, despite the same model organism with similar biological endpoints, and sufficiently high enough dose rate (35–100 Gy/s), some investigators have not observed FLASH effects (Fig. 5) [3637].

NEUROCOGNITIVE PROTECTION BY FLASH RT

Because neurocognitive impairment is a detrimental side effect of RT, there has been much attempt to study whether FLASH effect would occur in the brain irradiated with FLASH RT. Montay-Gruel et al. [38] have demonstrated using novel object recognition test that memory sparing effect occurs in mice when their whole brain is irradiated 10 Gy at dose rate above 100 Gy/s (they have tested FLASH RT as 1, 3, 10, 20, 30, 60, 100, or 500 Gy/s or single pulse of 10 Gy in 1.8 µs vs. CONV 0.1 Gy/s). In their next study using the dose rate 100 Gy/s, the authors have reported that learning and memory ability is preserved at 10 Gy and 12 Gy but not at 14 Gy of FLASH RT [28]. By fixing the radiation dose at 10 Gy given in a single fraction, FLASH RT has shown the much better outcome (to the level comparable to non-irradiated control) in all of the behavioral tests that the authors have performed: hippocampal and frontal cortical learning and memory test (novel object recognition, object in place task, and temporal order task), anxiety and depression test (elevated plus maze, light-dark box text, and forced swim test), and fear extinction test (extinction test) compared to CONV RT both at short (1 month) and long term (6 months post IR) end points [28]. In their latest study, they have compared single high dose vs. multiple smaller doses of FLASH RT for the local control and survival of mice [25]. They have found that all the fractionation regimen give rise to comparable tumor response although certain doses (10 Gy single fraction, 2×7 Gy multiple fractions, and 3×10 Gy multiple fractions) are much better than others (14 Gy single fractions or 4×3.5 Gy multiple fractions) to prolong the survival of mice [25]. Again, the molecular mechanisms explaining the differential responses are not known.

In general whole brain irradiation has been reported to trigger p53-independent endothelial cell apoptosis disrupting blood-brain-barrier [39], accompanied with persistent inflammation mediated by tumor necrosis factor-alpha [40] in rodent models. Within hours of irradiation, ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM)- and p53-dependent apoptosis of neural progenitor cells have been also observed in the subventricular zone of the hippocampus in mice [4142]. Furthermore, irradiation has also shown to result in microgliosis altering the neuronal progenitor cell fate to more differentiated neurons in the subgranule zone [43]. Importantly, newly transplanted neural stem/precursor cells fail to be grafted in the irradiated hippocampus [43], indicating that radiation severely disrupts neurogenic microenvironment in the brain.

A number of studies consistently demonstrate that microgliosis is significantly reduced in the brain irradiated with FLASH RT [28304445]. These groups of researchers have also observed that dendritic spines of the neurons, which are critical for memory formation [30] are significantly preserved with FLASH RT [283044]. It is highly likely that CONV RT induces increased microgliosis, which in turn promotes shortening of dendrite spines as microglia are known to actively engulf dendrites during synaptic pruning [46]. Consistent with this hypothesis, loss of microglial phagocytic regulation ability (through microglial specific deletion of signal-regulatory protein [SIRP] alpha) has shown to result in decreased synaptic density and more severe cognitive impairment in mice [47]. FLASH RT has also been proposed to produce much less reactive oxygen species (ROS). Indeed Montay-Gruel et al. [28] have measured H2O2 levels in 4% O2 irradiated water and found that FLASH RT produces significantly less H2O2 than CONV RT. Moreover, they have led mice to breathe carbogen (95% O2/5% CO2) before and during whole brain irradiation to increase ROS levels and demonstrated that the neuroprotection by FLASH RT completely disappears [28]. Other neuroprotective mechanisms proposed with FLASH RT include maintaining blood-brain-barrier integrity including the maintenance of fluid channel aquaporin 4 and astrocytic coverage [45] and preservation of endothelial cell tight junction integrity by preventing dilation, apoptosis, and endothelial nitric oxide synthase expression [44].

CURRENT STATUS OF FLASH RT

FLASH RT has been treating cancer patients since 2019. In the first clinical trial, 6 cat patients with highly invasive and inoperable squamous cell carcinoma of the nasal planum received 25–41 Gy with FLASH RT with electron beam from Kinetron and Oriatron LINACs and have shown all complete response at 6 months follow-up [48]. The first human patient was multi-resistant CD30+ T cell cutaneous lymphoma patient treated with 15 Gy electron beam FLASH RT and has also shown complete response with no noticeable skin toxicity [49]. Based on these amazing results, there are several FLASH RT clinical trials undergoing at this moment around the world. The first FLASH RT trial is a non-randomized trial (FAST-01) with proton FLASH RT for patients with bone metastases in the extremities as a palliative purpose (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04592887) [50]. In 10 treated patients, 50% of the patients had a complete response (complete relief of pain) and 16.7% had a partial response rate [50]. Another trial is currently recruiting patients with bone metastasis to assess radiation-induced toxicity as well as pain relief with FLASH RT (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05524064). A first randomized phase II trial is also planned to evaluate FLASH and CONV RT head-to-head in patients with non-melanoma skin cancer treated with either single or fractionated doses (clinicaltrialsareana.com). There are no clinical trials planned yet with FLASH RT for brain tumor patients that these authors are aware of. However, the use of high LET radiation such as proton or heavy ion RT delivered in FLASH RT manner would offer a highly attractive clinical trial design for patients with brain tumors.

FUTURE ISSUES TO BE ADDRESSED WITH FLASH RT

Despite of amazing FLASH effects and clinical trial results with FLASH RT, there are several issues still remain to be answered.

Exact molecular mechanisms for the FLASH effect are needed to be proven

Although the oxygen depletion hypothesis prevails for the normal tissue protection by FLASH RT [33], the latest evidence suggests that it is not likely [32]. Furthermore, this oxygen depletion theory does not explain why the tumor response is comparable between FLASH and CONV RT. Spitz et al. [51] argue that there are couple of distinct differential abilities between normal tissues and tumors in handling ROS. They suggest that normal cells have lower pro-oxidant burdens and greater capacity to remove organic hydroperoxide (generated from OH• reacting with organic molecules such as lipids in tissues especially in the brain representing 30% of the tissues are polyunsaturated lipids) than tumors [51]. They further claim that normal tissues have 2- to 4-fold less levels of labile iron (Fe2+) pool (released from iron-containing protein by O2•-, which is produced from O2 reacting with eaq- and H•) compared to the tumors, hence less susceptible to Fenton-type chain reaction potentially damaging to cells [51]. Wilson et al. [26] raise an interesting (and more realistic) hypothesis where the normal tissues have higher oxygen tension (so called “physoxic”), which can be rapidly depleted in an wider range by FLASH RT, hence being protected from radiation-induced toxicity. However, tumors are hypoxic from the beginning hence the window of oxygen depletion by FLASH RT is rather small, offering no differentials in therapeutic window between FLASH RT and CONV RT [26]. Nonetheless, these are all hypotheses, which ultimately need to be proven scientifically. It seems researchers are moving on to investigate other aspects of FLASH RT such as combination with immune checkpoint blockades [5253] without understanding the exact molecular mechanism, adding another level of complication to understand the mysterious biology of FLASH effect.

Standardized parameters for FLASH RT are needed

It would be ideal to standardize the parameters of FLASH RT to produce FLASH effects in vivo. As discussed above, there are a number of groups whom have not seen FLASH effects even though they have utilized appropriate parameters and model system [25]. Pioneers in FLASH RT around the globe including Loo Jr and Vozenin groups have gathered the parameters across multi-centers trying to set up optimal beam parameters capable of achieving FLASH effect in vivo [54]. In this study, the authors have validated homogenous dose distribution using 8 MeV electron beam LINAC followed by a calculation of energy correction factor between different instrument set up that they have utilized for FLASH research at Stanford and CHUV (Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vaudois; i.e., Varian Trilogy and Oriatron eRT6, respectively) by using thermoluminescent dosimetry [54]. In the future, other instrumental set up such as protons or X-rays can be compared and validated hence one can set up FLASH RT more accurately and efficiently.

Low penetration depth of electron beam FLASH RT needs to be overcome

Initial FLASH RT such as those in [54] utilize electron beam, which is limited by the low penetration depth [55]. Because of this limitation, clinical trials have been confined to superficial tumors such as squamous cell carcinoma of the nasal planum in cat patients [48] or CD30+ T cell cutaneous lymphoma in human patient [49]. To overcome this limitation, a number of research groups now utilize proton beam as FLASH RT [565758] thereby avoiding the limitation of low energy electron. Interestingly and importantly, Loo Jr group is currently developing pluridirectional high-energy agile scanning electronic radiotherapy (PHASER) technology (Fig. 6) [59], enabling rapid delivery of megavolts X-ray beams to target deep-seated tumors offering a highly compact, economical, and power-efficient LINAC and radiofrequency power sources.

Tumor hypoxia would still remain as a resistance factor for FLASH RT

Although the normal tissues may become quite hypoxic by the oxygen depletion caused by FLASH RT thereby being spared from radiation-induced toxicity [51], tumors may not. This suggests that tumor hypoxia may remain as a major factor contributing to the treatment failure by FLASH RT. Much attempts have been made to overcome tumor hypoxia with CONV RT, for example, the use of radiosensitizers, compounds that acts like oxygen thereby fixing the IR-induced DNA damage (Fig. 1) [5]. Although this may work with CONV RT, it should not be used with FLASH RT because such agent can also sensitize normal tissues (if the normal tissues becomes severely hypoxic by FLASH RT) as well as tumors. This in turn may cause unpredictable and severe radiation-induced injuries to the normal tissues. To overcome such problem, one can exploit utilizing “radiation-activated prodrug,” experimental compounds that can be chemically converted into active cytotoxic moiety only upon irradiating tumors [60] with FLASH RT. Interestingly though, Leavitt et al. [61] have recently found that hypoxic tumors, which are artificially induced by clamping the flank tumors in mice respond better to FLASH RT than CONV RT and that FLASH RT elicit unique signaling pathways including efficient cell cycle arrest, inhibition of translation and ribosomal biogenesis, and enhanced hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling and glycolytic metabolism. It would be exciting whether this observation can also be made in more physiologically relevant hypoxic tumors in vivo.

CONCLUSION

It took 50 years to re-discover the amazing FLASH effects since the original observation of oxygen depletion in bacteria by ultra-high dose rate given in single pulses. It has now been less than 10 years since the re-discovery of FLASH effect to implement this technology to the clinics (now called as ultra-high dose-rate RT, UHDR RT). With this revolutionary FLASH RT technique, understanding of molecular mechanism responsible for FLASH effect will be critical and this would allow us to make a major step forward in cancer treatment with radiation.
