FAIM modulates HCC progression via enhancing HMGA1 interaction with CDK7 and promoting its phosphorylation level
Authors: Yuan Li, Wenna Liu, Xushen Fan, Xinyi Wu, Mingbo Cao, Xiuling Li, Amin Wurita, Suofeng Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), characterized by its aggressive trait and rapid progression, stands as one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide. Fas apoptotic inhibitory molecule (FAIM), a death receptor antagonist, impedes cell apoptosis by reinforcing protein phosphorylation and stability. High mobility group A1 (HMGA1) is reported to be subject to multiple posttranslational modification, and plays a crucial role in HCC development. However, whether FAIM facilitates tumorigenesis through modulating HMGA1 phosphorylation remains unclear.MethodsWe applied Huh7 and SNU387 cells to investigate the regulatory role of FAIM in cell proliferation and apoptosis. Next, we constructed a male BALB/C mouse model with HCC to explore the biological function of FAIM in tumor growth. Subsequently, anti-FAIM proteomic and phosphorylation proteomic profiles were performed to screen HMGA1 as the FAIM-modulated downstream phosphorylated protein. Eventually, a series of immunoprecipitation (IP) tests and in vitro kinase assay revealed the crosstalk of FAIM, cyclin-dependent kinase7 (CDK7) and HMGA1, and unveiled the vital value of FAIM in HCC pathogenesis.ResultsIn vitro and in vivo experiments validated that FAIM overexpression was advantageous for HCC progression. Thereafter, anti-FAIM proteomic profile identified that FAIM interacted with CDK7 and HMGA1. Phosphorylation proteomic profile revealed that the Ser36 of HMGA1 was a downstream phosphorylation site of FAIM. Consistently, Co-IP and cycloheximide assays verified that FAIM enhanced HMGA1 phosphorylation and stability. More importantly, recovery experiments identified that silencing HMGA1 altered the regulatory role of overexpressed FAIM in Huh7 cells. Furthermore, we transduced Flag-HMGA1WT or Flag-HMGA1S36A plasmids into overexpressed FAIM cells and demonstrated that Ser36 phosphorylation influenced HMGA1 stability.ConclusionsSummarily, we elucidated that FAIM modulated HCC progression through facilitating the interaction of CDK7 and HMGA1 as well as elevating HMGA1 phosphorylation and stability.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07148-0.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, FAIM, HMGA1, Proliferation, Apoptosis

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as a liver malignancy, is one of the most prevalent cancers [1, 2]. The pathogenies of HCC are complicated, with viral hepatitis and alcohol as the main risk factors [3–5]. Currently, a wide variety of clinical therapeutical strategies are available for HCC patients including surgical resection, liver transplantation, radiotherapy, as well as chemotherapy [6–8]. Clinically, systematic antitumor therapy is the best choice for patients in advanced or late stages. Despite advancements in medical science, HCC remains a formidable challenge due to its high recurrence rate and resistance to conventional chemotherapy. The poor prognosis and low survival rate of HCC inspires researchers to explore more effective targeted approaches. Over the recent years, as a novel strategy, in-vivo gene therapy has attracted public attention [9]. This study aims to investigate molecular mechanism related to HCC development and explore a candidate gene for radical cure of HCC.

Fas apoptotic inhibitory molecule (FAIM) is known as a vital anti-apoptotic protein which plays a crucial role in cell viability [10–12]. For instance, in rat neuronal Type II cells, FAIM-L interacted with X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein, suppressed its ubiquitination and degradation, and prevented neuron apoptosis [13–15]. Besides, FAIM expedited tumor growth and there were many investigations about its underlying function in tumorigenesis. FAIM facilitated the development of lung adenocarcinoma through promoting the binding of ULK1 and MTOR and strengthening ULK1 phosphorylation [16]. Moreover, multiple myeloma patients with FAIM overexpression manifested the shorter lifetime, and silencing FAIM attenuated cell viability induced by insulin like growth factor-1 by decreasing protein kinase B phosphorylation [17]. According to GSE46408, GSE101728 and GSE202069, FAIM expression was elevated in liver tumors compared with normal liver tissues. Nevertheless, the biological role of FAIM in HCC progression has not been explored before. Here, we will provide novel insights into how FAIM regulates HCC tumorigenesis.

High mobility group A1 (HMGA1) is a nonhistone chromatin structural protein. As an auxiliary transcription factor, HMGA1 functions by bending chromatin and recruiting other transcription factors to DNA, thereby modulates the transcription of target genes [18]. Emerging evidences have revealed that HMGA1 was implicated in the ageing, apoptosis and autophagy of cancer cells [19–21]. HMGA1 activity is regulated by multiple post-translational modifications including phosphorylation, which affects its combination with DNA [22]. In lung cancer cells, recombinant keratin 2 (CK2) facilitated phosphorylation of HMGA1 at Ser102, and the anticancer drug gefitinib enhanced HMGA1 Ser102 phosphorylation. Knockdown of CK2 or HMGA1 suppressed the survival of gefitinib-treated cancer cells, suggesting a critical role for CK2-HMGA1 axis in altering gefitinib resistance [23]. Additionally, cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) promoted the ser36 phosphorylation of HMGA1, and CDK7 facilitated cell proliferation and inhibited cell apoptosis in HCC [24, 25]. From the above adjacent paragraph, FAIM was proved to increase phosphorylation and stability of protein via fostering its interaction with another kinase. However, whether FAIM enhances the binding of CDK7 and HMGA1 to elevate HMGA1 phosphorylation level has not been investigated yet. Therefore, we will detect interaction among FAIM, CDK7 and HMGA1, as well as whether FAIM modulates HCC progression through manipulating HMGA1 phosphorylation.

In this article, we identified the functional role and molecular mechanism of FAIM in HCC pathogenesis, and laid a solid foundation for developing promising strategies for clinical HCC treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and lentiviral infection

Huh7 and SNU387 cells were obtained from iCell Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and grown in DMEM (G4511, Servive Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) as well as RPMI-1640 medium (31800, Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (11011−8611, Tianhang Biotechnology, Zhejiang, China), respectively. When Huh7 and SNU387 cells were under good growth condition and proliferated to approximately 50% confluence, they were utilized for virus infection. The lentiviral carrying empty vector was used as the negative control. Lentiviral carrying short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) or overexpressed-FAIM was applied to silence or upregulate FAIM. Puromycin was chosen to screen the stably infected cells. In addition, cycloheximide (CHX, 20 µg/ml, C112766, Aladdin, Shanghai, China), an inhibitor of protein synthesis, was added to the stably infected Huh7 cells with FAIM overexpression in CHX chase assay. The sequences of the negative control and short hairpin RNAs-FAIM were presented as follows: shNC: ccgTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTttcaagagaACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAAttttt.

Lv-Homo-FAIM-shRNA: ccgCGGAAAGAAGGGATTATTCATttcaagagaATGAATAATCCCTTCTTTCCGttttt.

Lv-Homo-FAIM-shRNA2: ccgGTCCACAAGATCGAATTTGAAttcaagagaTTCAAATTCGATCTTGTGGACttttt.

Cell counting Kit‑8 (CCK‑8)

For CCK-8 assay, a total of 5 × 103 HCC cells per well were plated in a 96‑well plate. Approximate 10 µl CCK‑8 reagent (KGA9310, Keygen Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) was added to each well at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, and then incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was detected with a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Colony formation assay

The proliferation capacity of HCC cells was evaluated by colony formation experiment. Briefly, about 3 × 102 HCC cells were seeded in a culture dish and incubated at 37 °C for 14 days. Giemsa reagent (D011-1-2, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) was applied to stain the colonies and the numbers of visible colonies were counted precisely.

Flow cytometry assay

Cell apoptosis experiment was conducted using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (KGA106, Keygen Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. HCC cells were suspended in 500 µl binding buffer and stained by Annexin V- FITC/PI iodide (volume 1:1) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. A NovoCyte cytometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was utilized to analyze the apoptosis rate of HCC cells.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIpure (RP1001, BioTeke Corporation, Beijing, China) adherence to the manufacturer’s instructions and cDNA synthesis was carried out with All-in-One First-Strand SuperMix (MD80101, Magen, Guangzhou, China). The harvested cDNAs was used as a template for FAIM amplification. PCR was performed using SYBR Green (SR4110, Solarbio). After accomplishing the reaction, relative gene expression level was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method and GAPDH was used as an endogenous standard. The sequences of primers were listed as follows:

FAIM sense: 5′-TTAGTGGGCAAAGAAACAT-3′;

FAIM sense: 5′-TTAGTGGGCAAAGAAACAT-3′;

FAIM antisense: 5′-ACCATACGTCCATAGCATC-3′;

FAIM antisense: 5′-ACCATACGTCCATAGCATC-3′;

GAPDH sense: 5′-GACCTGACCTGCCGTCTAG-3′;

GAPDH sense: 5′-GACCTGACCTGCCGTCTAG-3′;

GAPDH antisense: 5′-AGGAGTGGGTGTCGCTGT-3′.

GAPDH antisense: 5′-AGGAGTGGGTGTCGCTGT-3′.

Western blot

Briefly, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor (P0100, Solarbio). The obtained cell lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. A BCA protein assay kit (PC0020, Solarbio) was used to determine the protein concentrations of samples. Next, equal amount of protein extraction was separated on SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto the PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and then probed with the primary antibodies against FAIM (1:1000, 13005-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), cleaved caspase 3 (1:300, AF7022, Affinity, Changzhou, China), HMGA1 (1:400, sc-393213, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) and GAPDH (1:10000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was incubated in the secondary antibodies including HRP labelled-Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (1:3000, SE134, Solarbio) and HRP labelled- Goat Anti-mouse IgG (1:3000, SE131, Solarbio) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (PE0010, Solarbio).

Animal protocol and groups

Nude male mice (BALB/C, 5-week-old) acquired from Huachuang Sino (Taizhou, China) were randomly divided into four groups. About 5 × 106 Huh7 cells transduced with LV-Vector, LV-FAIM, LV-shNC and LV-FAIM-shRNA1 were injected intraperitoneally into right back of mice in the four groups, respectively. After 4 weeks, tumor volumes were calculated and tumors were collected for the subsequent experiments. Tumor volume = long diameter × (short diameter)2 × 0.5.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Tumor sections were dewaxed, rehydrated with ethanol of decreasing concentrations, followed by microwave heating in sodium citrate buffer (0.1 M) for antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was inactivated by incubating in 3% hydrogen peroxide (10011218, SinopharmChemica, Shanghai, China). Then the sections were immersed with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A602440-0050, Sangon Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 15 min at room temperature to block unspecific labeling. The sections were probed with the primary antibodies including anti-FAIM (1:100, 13005-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Ki67 (1:100, 28074-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-cleaved caspase 3 (1:100, A11319, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. Next, the sections were hatched with HRP-labeled Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, #31460, ThermoFisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) for 60 min at 37 °C. DAB (DAB-1031, Maxine, Shanghai, China) was utilized to stain the sections and hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio) was used to counterstain the nucleus. Finally, the sections were observed with a microscope system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

To validate the co-localization of FAIM and HMGA1, Huh7 cells were seeded on the slides. The cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (80096618, SinopharmChemica) for 15 min, followed by permeabilizing with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST795, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min and blocking in 1% BSA (A602440-0050, Sangon Biotechnology) for 1 h at room temperature. After incubating with the primary antibodies against FAIM (1:200, DF2492, Affinity) and HMGA1(1:100, sc-393213 M, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4 °C, cells were probed with the secondary antibodies including FITC-labeled Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, ab6717, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and Cy3-labeled Goat Anti-mouse IgG (1:200, ab97035, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Eventually, nuclei were stained with DAPI (D106471-5 mg, Aladdin) and cells were imaged with a laser scanning confocal microscope (NiKon, Tokyo, Japan).

Phosphorylation proteomics

The phosphorylated peptides were enriched by immobilized metal-chelating affinity chromatography. The peptides were first bound to the StageTip column and the phosphorylated peptide segments on the column were eluted by an elution solution. The obtained peptide segments were taken and analyzed by LC-MS. Subsequently, the samples were separated using Hypersil Gold Column (C18). The specific experiment parameters are as follows: the flow rate was 300 nl/min, composition A of mobile phase was 0.1% formic acid, and composition B of mobile phase was 80% acetonitrile. The original data was analyzed by MaxQuant software. The peptide segments with p < 0.05 and |log2FC| >1 were defined as differential phosphorylated peptides, and KEGG was used for analyzing the enriched pathways of upregulated and downregulated phosphorylated proteins.

Anti-FAIM proteomics

Firstly, IP test was conducted to gain the proteins interacted with FAIM. The prepared cell lysates were incubated with the anti-FAIM antibody‑conjugated agarose resin and then protein bound with the antibodies were eluted. Next, the obtained protein samples were incubated with trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at 37 °C overnight, followed by vortex and centrifugation. The resulting supernatant were desalted with a C18 desalination column. The obtained samples were taken and analyzed by LC-MS. Next, the samples were separately by gradient elution. Composition A of mobile phase was purified water containing 0.1% formic acid, and composition B of mobile phase was 80% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The original data was analyzed by Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software. KEGG and GO analyses were applied for revealing enriched pathways of the proteins interacted with FAIM.

Co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP) assays

In this article, we performed several Co-IP assays to detect the phosphorylation level of HMGA1 under different conditions and validate the interactions of FAIM and HMGA1, CDK7 and HMGA1, as well as FAIM, HMGA1 and CDK7. The cell lysates were obtained as the above methods mentioned. Antibodies against HMGA1, Flag, and FAIM were briefly incubated with coupling resin and then covalently coupled. The antibody‑conjugated agarose resin was hatched with cell lysates for 2 h at room temperature on a rotator. The resin was washed, and the protein complexes bound to the antibodies were subsequently eluted. Finally, proteins were separated in SDS‑PAGE gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore), followed by detection using the primary antibodies including anti-HMGA1 (1:400, sc-393213, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-Flag (1:500, AE092, ABclonal), anti-Myc (1:500, AE070, ABclonal), anti-phospho-serine (1:2000, ab9332, Abcam) and anti-HA (1:500, AE105, ABclonal) overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was incubated in the secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C and visualized with ECL reagent (PE0010, Solarbio).

In vitro kinase assay

CDK7 kinase activity toward HMGA1 was assessed by incubating immunoprecipitated CDK7 and HMGA1 with ATP in the kinase buffer (37 °C, 30 min). The immunoprecipitation steps were performed as the above described. Reaction system lacking CDK7 kinase was served as the negative control group. Phosphorylated level of HMGA1 was analyzed by western blot with anti-phospho-serine antibody (1:2000, ab9332, Abcam).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 software. Statistical significance between two groups was analyzed by unpaired t tests, and that among multiple groups was performed by one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, respectively. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

High FAIM expression in the tumor tissues of HCC patients

To explore the functional gene during HCC progression, we analyzed three databases in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) website (GSE46408, GSE202069 and GSE101728) [26]. Volcano plots and heatmaps depicted that there were conspicuous differences of gene expression between HCC and normal tissue (Fig. 1A and B). There were 646 common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the three databases, which were displayed in the Venn plot. To further investigate the roles of these common DEGs, we performed KEGG analysis, which showed that DEGs were mainly enriched in cell proliferation and death pathway. Genes associated with HCC were downloaded from Genecards websites. So far, 143 genes (set A) in the common DEGs of the three databases were not found to be associated with HCC. More interestingly, 70 genes (set B) in the set A were also in the set of genes closely related to cell proliferation and apoptosis, which also retrieved in the Genecards websites. In the set B, FAIM might be a critical gene regulating HCC progression (Fig. 1C). Thereafter, mRNA expression of FAIM in the three databases were analyzed, suggesting that FAIM level was remarkedly elevated in HCC tissue compared with normal tissue (Fig. 1D). Consistent with the above results, IHC images downloaded from Human Protein Atlas (HPA) website [27] also revealed that FAIM was highly expressed in tumor tissues of HCC patients (Fig. 1E). The patient prognosis statistical data from Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) website [28] has been shown in Fig. 1F and identified that HCC patients with highly expressed FAIM livers manifested the lower overall survival than those patients with lowly expressed FAIM livers. The pan-cancer view from The University of Alabama at Birmingham Cancer Data Analysis Portal (UALCAN) website [29] demonstrated that FAIM was widely expressed in a variety of cancers, among which FAIM was dramatically upregulated in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) (Fig. 1G). In total, FAIM level was strikingly enhanced in tumor tissues of HCC patients.

FAIM facilitates the proliferation and suppresses the apoptosis of HCC cells

The infection efficiency of the corresponding lentivirus was validated by western blot and real-time PCR. Specifically, at both mRNA and protein levels, compared to LV-Vector group, FAIM level was conspicuously enforced in LV-FAIM group, while FAIM expression was significantly reduced in LV-FAIM-shRNA1/2 group (Fig. 2A-C). The results of CCK8 detection revealed that FAIM overexpression fostered the growth of Huh7 and SNU387 cells. Additionally, the proliferative capacity shFAIM cells also verified that FAIM impairment inhibited cell growth (Fig. 2D). Colony formation assay also unveiled that FAIM facilitated the proliferation of HCC cells (Fig. 2E). Thereafter, flow cytometry assay was conducted to investigated the effects of FAIM on cell apoptosis. It was suggested that silencing FAIM promoted apoptosis of HCC cells (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, cleaved caspase 3 level was increased in shFAIM cells, which indicated that FAIM knockdown facilitated cell apoptosis (Fig. 2G). Taken together, it was demonstrated that FAIM strengthened the proliferative capacity of HCC cells and decreased their apoptosis ratio.

FAIM promotes tumorigenesis in vivo

To further determine the biological role of FAIM in vivo, male BALB/C nude mice were subcutaneously injected with Huh7 cells, which have been transduced with LV-Vector, LV-FAIM, LV-shNC and LV-FAIM-shRNA. In contrast with the control group, FAIM upregulation significantly caused tumor to expand, and FAIM downregulation abrogated tumor growth (Fig. 3A). The curve of tumor volumes provided more compelling evidence to support our conclusion (Fig. 3B). According to IHC examination, tumor tissues of mice infected with LV-FAIM manifested enhanced FAIM expression. Furthermore, Ki-67 and cleaved caspase 3 staining showed that overexpressed FAIM dramatically boosted tumor proliferation and inhibited tumor apoptosis (Fig. 3C). On the contrary, silencing FAIM repressed tumor growth and induced its apoptosis. Collectively, we concluded that FAIM facilitated tumor growth in vivo.

FAIM interacts with HMGA1 and modulates its phosphorylation level

In order to explore the effects of FAIM on controlling downstream phosphorylation, we conducted phosphorylation proteomics between Huh7 cells infected with LV-Vector and LV-FAIM. As shown in the Fig. 4A, dramatical differences existed between the two groups. Volcano plot and heatmap also displayed the notable phosphorylation differences (Fig. 4B and C). Based on analysis of phosphorylation proteomics, 119 proteins were significantly upregulated phosphorylated (log2FC > 1 and p < 0.05) and 222 proteins was strikingly downregulated phosphorylated (log2FC < -1 and p < 0.05). KEGG plot identified the enriched pathways of upregulated and downregulated phosphorylated proteins of FAIM downstream between two comparable groups, which were principally linked to cell events and metabolism (Fig. 4D). For some cases, target gene modulated the downstream phosphorylation via interacting with relevant proteins. Subsequently, Co-IP and mass spectrometry analysis were performed to detect the proteins interacted with FAIM. We found that a total of 3546 proteins were bound with FAIM. Moreover, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that the proteins interacted with FAIM mainly enriched in cell proliferation and senescence pathways (Fig. 4E). Subsequently, we screened the proteins among upregulated-phosphorylation proteins, proteins interacted with FAIM, and upregulated proteins of HCC tissue in GSE46408, GSE202069 and GSE101728. The Venn plot showed that the collection included 5 genes (Fig. 4F). Intriguingly, HMGA1 was existed in this collection, suggesting that FAIM might interact with HGMA1 and FAIM overexpression enhanced HMGA1 phosphorylation. Totally, HMGA1 was screened as a phosphorylated modification target of FAIM downstream.

FAIM influences proliferation and apoptosis of Huh7 cells via regulating HMGA1 phosphorylation

The interaction of FAIM and HMGA1 was verified by Co-IP assay (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence images showed that FAIM and HMGA1 were co-localizated in cytoplasm of Huh7 cells with FAIM overexpression (Fig. 5B). Moreover, upregulated FAIM increased the phosphorylation level of HMGA1, accompanied with enhanced HMGA1 protein level (Fig. 5C), which prompted us to suspect that overexpressed FAIM suppressed HMGA1 degradation via promoting its phosphorylation. For confirmation, CHX chase assays were carried out to detect the level of HMGA1 in cells with overexpressed FAIM or negative control treatment. In overexpressed FAIM cells, the degradation speed of HMGA1 was slower than that in control cells (Fig. 5D). Collectively, it was concluded that FAIM overexpression enhanced HMGA1 stability through reinforcing its phosphorylation. Furthermore, the decreased cleaved caspase 3 in FAIM overexpression cells was restored after silencing HMGA1 (Fig. 5E). As expected, HMGA1 knockdown increased apoptosis ratio and suppressed proliferation of HCC cells infected with LV-FAIM (Fig. 5F). In summary, FAIM manipulated proliferation and apoptosis of HCC cells via modulating HMGA1 phosphorylation.

FAIM manipulates the phosphorylation and stability of HMGA1 via modulating the interaction of CDK7 and HMGA1

FAIM overexpression elevated HMGA1 content and phosphorylation level in HMGA1WT overexpressed cells, while declined those of HMGA1 in HMGA1 S36A overexpressed cells, which indicated that Ser36 was a vital site for phosphorylation and stabilization of HMGA1 (Fig. 6A). Importantly, in vitro kinase assay revealed that CDK7 kinase directly elevated HMGA1 phosphorylation level in Huh7 cells (Fig. 6B). It was illustrated that CDK7 regulated HMGA1 phosphorylated modification. The specific crosstalk between CDK7 and HMGA1 would be further verified. Additionally, CHX chase assays also demonstrated that in cells with HMGA1 Ser36 mutation, the degradation of HMGA1 was quicker than that in cells with wild-type HMGA1 (Fig. 6C), suggesting that FAIM inhibited HMGA1 degradation through enhancing the phosphorylation of HMGA1 at Ser36 sites. Taken together, the phosphorylation of HMGA1 at Ser36 sites had a crucial impact on its stability. In order to illustrate how FAIM influences the phosphorylation of HMGA1, we found that CDK7 belonged to FAIM-interacted protein revealed by Co-IP proteomics. Co-IP assay validated the interaction of FAIM, HMGA1 and CDK7 in cells with FAIM upregulation (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, overexpressed FAIM facilitated the interaction between HMGA1 and CDK7 in Huh7 cells (Fig. 6E). In cells co-transfected with HA-FAIM and Flag-HMGA1WT, CDK7 overexpression remarkedly enhanced the content and phosphorylation level of HMGA1, while those of HMGA1 were dropped in cells co-transfected with HA-FAIM and Flag-HMGA1S36A, suggesting that CDK7 reinforced HMGA1 stability via facilitating its phosphorylation at Ser36 sites (Fig. 6F). In summary, FAIM overexpression strengthened HMGA1 stability through promoting its phosphorylation at Ser36 via interacting with CDK7.

Discussion

HCC is the most common primary liver cancer which is characterized with high incidence and poor prognosis [7, 30, 31]. Over the recent several decades, in contrast to other cancers including breast, lung, and gastric cancers, death rates of HCC-related diseases have been increased globally because of no radically curative therapies [4]. In this article, we demonstrated that FAIM inhibited HMGA1 degradation through promoting its interaction with CDK7 and enhancing Ser36 phosphorylation. Moreover, FAIM manipulated proliferation and apoptosis of HCC cells through modulating HMGA1 stability. Our findings uncovered molecular mechanism and the central value of FAIM boosting HCC progression.

Existing researches have proved that FAIM was advantageous for the survival of diverse cell lines. Huo et al. investigated that FAIM resisted Fas-induced apoptosis of hepatocytes and lymphocytes in vitro and in vivo via promoting ubiquitination and degradation of the orphan nuclear receptor [32]. Additionally, FAIM has been widely reported to facilitate tumor growth [16, 17]. Based on GSE46408, GSE101728 and GSE202069, the elevated expression of FAIM in liver tumors was displayed. Our in vitro experiments investigated that FAIM repressed apoptosis and facilitated proliferation of Huh7 and SNU387 cells. Furthermore, in liver tumor tissues, FAIM expression was positively correlated with Ki67 level and negatively correlated with cleaved caspase 3 level, which revealed the functional role of FAIM on promoting tumorigenesis in vivo. Additionally, NF-kappa B pathway is a central signaling cascade in cell inflammation, immunity and stress responses. The activated NF-kappa B is involved in enhancing proliferative capacity of cancer cells including HCC cells [33, 34]. Intriguingly, a recent study demonstrated that FAIM was able to foster neurite outgrowth through the molecular mechanism related to NF-kappa B activation [35]. According to the above findings, we speculated that FAIM might boost HCC progression through modulating NF-kappa B pathway. Thus, FAIM was a crucial regulator during HCC pathogenesis.

HMGA1 is one of the non-histone chromatin remodeling proteins and susceptible of post-translational modification. Phosphorylation level of HMGA1 (Ser99, Ser102, and Ser103) was elevated in bone marrow samples from acute myeloid leukemia patients suffered from unsuccessful chemotherapy. The binding of HMGA1 to the Baculoviral IAP repeat-containing protein 5 promoter was disrupted after mutation of phosphorylation site, leading to the significant increase of cancer cell apoptosis [36]. In Glioma Cells, HMGA1 phosphorylation at Ser36 and Ser44 was fostered by inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase subunit epsilon, which inhibited its ubiquitination and degradation [37]. Our anti-FAIM and phosphorylation proteomic analyses verified that the Ser36 site of HMGA1 was a downstream phosphorylation target of FAIM. FAIM upregulation increased phosphorylation level and protein content of HMGA1. Moreover, after Huh7 cells with FAIM overexpression was transfected with HMGA1 shorth hair RNA, cell viability was attenuated and apoptosis ratio was elevated, which suggested that FAIM influenced HCC cell proliferation and apoptosis through modulating HMGA1 phosphorylation. Flag-HMGA1WT and Flag-HMGA1S36A plasimids were transduced into overexpressed FAIM cells. Thereafter, we validated that HMGA1 Ser36 phosphorylation increased its protein stability. More interestingly, in Huh7 cells with FAIM upregulation, the content and phosphorylation level of HMGA1 was not improved by CDK7 overexpression after HMGA1 Ser36 was mutated. Emerging study has elucidated that CDK kinase family was able of elevating the Ser36 phosphorylation of HMGA1. Among these kinases, CDK7 exhibited highest probability for mediating this modification. Furthermore, our experiments verified the interaction of CDK7, HMGA1 and FAIM. It was indicated that FAIM facilitated Ser36 phosphorylation and stability of HMGA1 via enhancing its interaction with CDK7. Therefore, from the above findings, FAIM modualted HCC cell proliferation and apoptosis by impeding HMGA1 degradation via increasing the binding of CDK7 and HMGA1. Mechanically, silencing FAIM might be developed as a feasible therapy for blocking HCC progression.

For obese mice burdened with upregulated FAIM, the expression of inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) was declined, and the levels of proteins associated with glycogen synthesis was elevated [38]. Therefore, FAIM ameliorated inflammatory responses and manipulated liver metabolism. Importantly, HCC was closely linked to chronic inflammation stimulated by different etiologies including fatty liver diseases [39], which prompted us to speculate that FAIM might suppress HCC progression by alleviating inflammation. However, it was unmatched with the conclusion that FAIM facilitated tumorigenesis. Thus, more researches and clinical experiments are required to verify the elaborate value of FAIM for modulating inflammation during HCC development.

It is known that the occurrence and development of HCC involves multiple pathological processes [40–43]. Cumulating studies have revealed that HMGA1 expression was conspicuously strengthened in multiple aggressive cancers and participated in tumorigenesis [44–46]. Additionally, HMGA1 promoted cell invasion and migration of Mhcc97h cells through modulating the ILK/Akt/GSK3β pathway [47]. Collectively, we confirmed that HMGA1 was also a vital gene in HCC progression and decreasing HMGA1 level had a remarkable effect on impeding HCC development.

For the limitations and further validation steps of this study, we listed four points as follows. Firstly, tumorigenesis involves many aspects including cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration and invasion. In this article, we only investigated the effect of FAIM in the proliferation and apoptosis of HCC cells. In the future experiments, we will explore the specific role of FAIM in the migration and invasion of HCC cells. In addition, FAIM/CDK7/HMGA1 axis may only be one of the modulatory mechanisms in which FAIM participates in the development of HCC, and we will further elucidate the other molecular regulatory mechanisms of FAIM in HCC progression. Thereafter, the interaction between FAIM and HMGA1 was verified indirectly by Co-IP assay, and we will validate their direct binding through GST-pull down examination. Eventually, we did not prove the relationship between FAIM expression and prognostic survival of HCC patients through clinical samples. In the future, we will collect enough clinical samples to perform this detection.

In summary, FAIM strengthened HMGA1 phosphorylation at Ser36 and suppressed its degradation via promoting its interaction with CDK7. We unveiled the molecular mechanism of FAIM manipulating HCC progression and shed new light on clinical HCC treatment.
