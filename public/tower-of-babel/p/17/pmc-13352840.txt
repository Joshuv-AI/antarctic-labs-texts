Presenteeism, self-directed learning, and missed nursing care in the operating theatre: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mengnan Xu, Cheng Zhang, Li Zhou, Shidi Zhao, Yonglan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe operating theatre is a high-risk, high-pressure environment where patient safety and surgical results may be at risk due to missed nursing care (MNC). To date, there has been no multi-centre survey exploring MNC among operating theatre nurses in China. Even though presenteeism and self-directed learning ability have been connected to the quality of care in other settings, less is known about how they relate to perioperative MNC, especially in China.AimTo explore the current status and types of operating theatre nurses’ MNC and to determine the correlation between presenteeism, self-directed learning ability and perioperative missed nursing care.MethodsA multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted among operating theatre nurses from July to September 2025 in public hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China. Data were collected using the self-developed demographic questionnaire, the MISSCARE Survey-Operating Room Scale, the Presenteeism Behaviour Questionnaire, and the Self-Directed Learning Ability Scale. Questionnaires were distributed and collected via the “Questionnaire Star” online platform. Data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0 for descriptive and inferential statistics. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with MNC.ResultsA total of 391 validated questionnaires were analysed (response rate: 94.22%). The incidence of MNC among operating theatre nurses was found to be 34.53%. Postoperative routine tasks demonstrated the highest omission rates, whereas safety-related activities had the lowest. The most frequently reported missed tasks were “checking whether the patient is wearing graduated compression stockings during surgery” and “allowing 3 minutes for antiseptic skin preparation solutions to dry”. Human resource allocation (inadequate staffing and fatigue), material resource limitations, and poor teamwork emerged as primary contributors to missed care. Presenteeism was positively associated with the frequency of MNC, while self-directed learning ability showed a significant negative association—representing a previously underexplored relationship among perioperative nurses.ConclusionsAccording to the results of this study, missed nursing care is moderately prevalent among operating theatre nurses, which is correlated with organisational resources, presenteeism, and individual self-directed learning ability. To reduce missed nursing care, nursing managers may consider prioritising resolving staffing and material constraints, as well as addressing presenteeism. Enhancing self‑directed learning capabilities can be pursued as a long‑term professional development strategy alongside supportive cultural initiatives.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04810-w.

Background

Patient safety is a major global public health concern. Ensuring patient safety is a core priority in nursing quality management and contemporary healthcare systems [1–3]. Missed Nursing Care (MNC) is intrinsically linked to patient safety concerns, representing the gap between required care and care delivered [4, 5]. With limited time and numerous demands, nurses commonly find themselves unable to carry out all necessary interventions on time or in full. This phenomenon, termed ‘missed nursing care’, occurs when required nursing interventions are partially or wholly omitted or delayed for any reason [6].

Over the past two decades, concerns about MNCs in healthcare organisations around the world have grown. Research shows that MNC occurs widely across diverse countries, hospital types and clinical settings, with a recent meta-analysis reporting a global prevalence of 56.4% [5, 7]. The consequences of MNC are multifaceted, extending beyond patient safety to affect nurses and healthcare organisations. For patients, MNC may increase the risk of adverse events, such as medication errors, infections, falls and pressure injuries, and even prolong hospitalisation and increase the risk of complications [8–10]. For healthcare organisations, MNC contributes to extended lengths of stay and higher readmission rates [11]. Furthermore, it adversely affects the nursing workforce. There is evidence to suggest that nurses who are unable to provide comprehensive care may experience moral distress, low job satisfaction and a strong willingness to leave their jobs [9, 12, 13]. These effects can damage the professional image of nursing and undermine patient-nurse trust [14].

The operating theatre plays a critical role in surgical procedures and life-saving interventions, in which nursing care quality directly impacts surgical safety and patient outcomes [15, 16]. In this specialised, high-risk, high-pressure setting, nurses play a central role within the multidisciplinary team, coordinating team workflows, leading and participating in surgical safety checklists to check patient identity and surgical site marking, maintaining sterility of instruments and supplies, and confirming safe equipment performance [17, 18]. Accurate, timely nursing care is fundamental to perioperative patient safety and high-quality care. Missed nursing care in the perioperative period is linked to serious adverse events, such as failures in surgical safety verification, retained surgical items, intraoperative hypothermia, and surgical site infections [19, 20]. To reduce safety risks in the operating theatre, the first step is to determine which care activities are most frequently omitted and the factors associated with them [6].

Current research on MNC concentrates on general wards, identifying common missed elements such as aiding patients in mobility and positional adjustments, and monitoring and evaluating their condition at the commencement of each shift [21–25]. However, investigations regarding MNC during the perioperative period are limited. Marsh et al. [6] identified the five most common types of MNC reported by operating theatre nurses as follows: communication among team members before transferring the patient to the operating theatre (43%); suspension of activities during the surgical time out (39.9%); implementation of isolation precautions (36.1%); use of a standardised hand-over communication tool (33.7%); and reporting of abnormal laboratory results (33.4%). A national survey by Gillespie et al. [26] in Australia revealed that 41% to 68% of operating theatre nurses had experienced MNC. A study conducted in Iran revealed a low overall incidence of MNC throughout the perioperative phase. The most common types of MNC pertained to preparation and communication, whereas the reasons for MNC encompassed staffing and teamwork challenges. Ahmed et al. identified that the most common missed items during the perioperative period in Egypt were the failure to disclose allergies and the inability to execute standardised handovers [27]. While research has been undertaken on MNC during the perioperative period, their features, content, and causes may differ among countries, influenced by cultural contexts and healthcare models.

The current study adopts Kalisch’s missed nursing care model [28] to identify the factors associated with MNC in the perioperative theatres. Gillespie et al. [29] employed this framework to investigate perioperative missed nursing care, identifying nurse age and skill mix as significant factors contributing to MNC in operating theatres. Recent research has pointed to mental fatigue as an important factor in MNC among operating theatre nurses [30]. Ezgi et al. [31]found that presenteeism was positively associated with MNC. Therefore, in the conceptual framework of this study (Supplementary Figure S1), presenteeism is conceptualised as a structure factor reflecting labour resource constraints (e.g., inadequate staffing and excessive workload), whereas self-directed learning ability is conceptualised as an individual professional resource reflecting nurse competency, which may enhance nurses’ clinical judgement and adaptability in demanding clinical environments. Process was measured as missed nursing care. This study focused on the structure and process elements. Outcome indicators, including nurse and patient outcomes, were not examined in this phase of the research and will be explored in future work.

Limited evidence exists on the specific content, characteristics, and causes of MNC in the perioperative period. In particular, no studies have reported the types, prevalence, influencing factors, or management strategies for MNC in operating theatres within China. Therefore, from the perspectives of surgical and nursing quality and safety management, this study investigates the current status, types, and influencing factors of MNC in the operating theatre. The findings will assist managers and nurses in recognising frequently neglected tasks, formulating evidence-based policies and specific improvement strategies, and minimising missed care, thus improving patient safety and the quality of nursing care.

Materials and methods

Study participants and procedures

A descriptive cross-sectional online survey was conducted among perioperative nurses from nine public tertiary hospitals in Wuxi, Jiangsu Province, China, from July to September 2025. These hospitals varied substantially in size, with the number of operating theatres ranging from 11 to 36, reflecting the diversity of surgical capacities in the region and enhancing the generalisability of our findings within the tertiary hospital setting. As detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Considering the exploratory nature of the study and time and resource constraints, all perioperative nurses were invited to participate using convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical nurse practitioners working in an operating department; (2) a minimum of 1 year of clinical experience; (3) ability to read and communicate in Chinese; and (4) informed consent and voluntary participation in this study. Nurses on leave or undergoing further training during the study were excluded. To ensure rigorous reporting, the study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement.

Sample size

According to Kendall’s empirical rule [32], this study included 24 independent variables, comprising 10 variables from general information, as well as 9 dimensions from the MISSCARE survey OR (Parts A and B), 4 dimensions from the self-directed learning ability scale, and presenteeism. To ensure robust statistical validity, the sample size was calculated to be 10 to 15 times the number of variables; while also accounting for a potential 10% sample loss, the required sample size for the study should be between 266 and 400. The study included 391 participants, meeting the specified sample size requirements.

Data collection

The data were collected via an anonymous self-reported questionnaire utilising the “Questionnaire Star” online platform (https://www.wjx.cn). Upon obtaining authorisation from the nursing department directors of each hospital, survey URLs were disseminated to perioperative nurses, who were invited to participate on a voluntary basis. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality to minimise social desirability bias. The questionnaire was pilot-tested with 20 operating theatre nurses to enhance item clarity and reduce potential recall bias. The online questionnaire was configured to require responses for all questions and to prevent duplicate answers. Subsequently, two researchers verified the quality of the questionnaire and eliminated those that did not meet the completion criteria (straight-lining, patterned answers, logical inconsistencies, or failure of attention-check items). Additionally, questionnaires completed in less than 300 s were excluded based on a pilot test (fastest valid completion time = 330 s). This conservative threshold was chosen to minimise the inclusion of inattentive or rushed responses.

Ethics

The research was conducted in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the medical ethics committee of Wuxi No.2 People’s Hospital (Jiangnan University Medical Center) (approval number: WXEY-2025-103). The committee granted a waiver of written/signed informed consent due to the observational nature of the study, use of anonymous electronic questionnaires, and minimal risk to participants. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants through an implied consent mechanism: the first page of the electronic questionnaire included a clear explanation of the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature, risks (minimal), benefits, confidentiality measures, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Completion and submission of the questionnaire constituted voluntary agreement to participate. All participants completed the questionnaires on the basis of informed consent, with no coercion involved.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

The demographic characteristics considered were age, gender, marital status, level of education, professional title, years of work experience, additional education, average weekly working hours, weekly overtime, and whether there was a conflict between family and work. Family-work conflict was assessed with a single item adapted from Li et al. [33], which was originally derived from the China General Social Survey. Participants were asked the following question: “In your daily life, how often do you experience conflict between family responsibilities and work demands?” with three response options: “frequently”,“sometimes”, and “almost never”.

The MISSCARE survey-operating room

The MISSCARE Survey OR is a self-report tool with two parts: Part A is focused on the frequency of missed care, including legal, preparation, safety, communication, and postoperative routines. Five Likert-type answers are provided for each of these items: never, rarely missed, occasionally missed, frequently missed and always missed. Part B addresses reasons for missed care across four dimensions (urgency, staffing, materials, and teamwork). Response options for the reasons for each item include minor, moderate, and significant reasons, or not a reason. The Cronbach’s α coefficient ranged from 0.71 to 0.84 (Part A) and from 0.74 to 0.90 (Part B) [6]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for A and B of the Chinese version scale were 0.949 and 0.886, respectively. Permission to use the scale was granted by the authors of the Chinese adaptation and validation study [34]. Responses of ‘occasionally missed’, ‘frequently missed’, or ‘always missed’ were classified as ‘missed’, while ‘never missed’ or ‘rarely missed’ were classified as ‘not missed’ or ‘minimally missed’. For the primary analysis, this dichotomisation was chosen to facilitate clinical interpretability and maintain consistency with previous studies using the MISSCARE instrument. The average frequency for each item was also reported [26].

Self-directed learning ability scale

The Nurse Self-Directed Learning Ability Scale, created by Xiao [35] in 2007, was utilised to assess the capability of operating theatre nurses to learn and master the skills and knowledge necessary for providing nursing care. The scale consists of four dimensions: Self-Motivation Beliefs (Items 1-14), Task Analysis (Items 15-20), Self-Monitoring and Regulation (Items 21-30), and Self-Evaluation (Items 31-34), amounting to a total of 34 items. Scores vary from 1 point for “strongly disagree” to 5 points for “strongly agree”. A greater total score signifies enhanced self-directed learning capability among nurses. The aggregate Cronbach’s α is 0.944, but the Cronbach’s α for each dimension varies from 0.701 to 0.887.

Presenteeism behaviour questionnaire

The Chinese version of the Presenteeism Behaviour Questionnaire [36] was used to measure the presenteeism behaviour of clinical nurses, which included two items: “You force yourself to attend work despite feeling sick” and “You force yourself to attend work despite having physical symptoms such as headache or backache”. Subjects were asked to recall the number of times the behaviour occurred in the past 6 months, with “never” scoring 1 point, “1 time” scoring 2 points, “2-5 times” scoring 3 points, and “>5 times” scoring 4 points. The total score of the two entries is the score of presenteeism, and the higher the score, the higher the frequency of presenteeism. The Cronbach’s α for the Chinese version of the questionnaire was 0.84.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS 27.0. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Distributions for self-directed learning ability, and presenteeism were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that presenteeism scores and self-directed learning ability scores deviated significantly from normality (both P < 0.05). Accordingly, these variables were described using medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Spearman correlation was used to examine bivariate associations, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the demographic differences in MNC. Due to multiple testing, the Bonferroni correction was applied within each family of tests (chi-square tests, group comparison t-tests, and subscale correlations). Adjusted p-values are reported alongside unadjusted values in the tables. Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to analyse the factors independently associated with MNC. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analyses were entered into the multivariable model. A backward stepwise selection method based on the likelihood ratio test was applied, retaining variables with P < 0.05 in the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance, and all values were within acceptable limits. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R². The statistical significance was set at < 0.05 with two-tailed testing.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 415 questionnaires were collected initially. After screening, 391 valid questionnaires remained, with an effective response rate of 94.22%. All respondents were employed in tertiary B or higher-level hospitals. Among them, 83.89% were female, and 75.19% were married. Respondents’ ages ranged from 21 to 60 years (mean = 35.29, SD = 7.89), and they reported working in the operating theatre environment for an average of 12.08 years (SD = 8.63), with their experience ranging from 1 to 37 years. The majority, 344 (87.98%), held a bachelor’s degree. Nearly half of the participants (48.08%) reported working over 50-60 h per week. These demographics are presented in Table 1.

Associations between demographics and missed nursing care

The study revealed statistically significant differences (Table 1) across prevalence of MNC among operating theatre nurses based on age (P = 0.009), marital status (P = 0.001), professional title (P < 0.001), education level (P = 0.061), operating theatre nurse years of experience (P = 0.014), participating in the education for operating theatre specialist nurse (P < 0.001), work-family conflict/family-work conflict (P = 0.02), total scores for SDL and scores for each dimension, and total presenteeism score (P < 0.001). However, age and years of operating theatre experience showed significant associations in univariate analyses but did not remain significant after Bonferroni correction.

Discussion

Evaluating and intervening in missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses is vital for elevating nursing quality and ensuring patient safety. This research provided useful information about the frequency and types, and factors associated with missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses in China.

Frequency and types of missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses

In this study conducted among operating theatre nurses in Wuxi, China, the prevalence of MNC among operating theatre nurses was 34.53%. The findings suggest that missed nursing care remains an important issue in perioperative nursing management. The prevalence was lower than the 41%-68% reported by Gillespie et al. [26] in Australian operating theatres. Marsh et al. [6], however, found a lower mean score of 0.84 (on a 0-4 scale). Such variations may be attributable to differences in cultural backgrounds, instruments used for measurement, staffing levels, available resources, and prevailing nursing care models.

This study identified several significant gaps in perioperative nursing care items. The postoperative routine dimension exhibited the highest rate, with “timely and accurate completion of postoperative nursing documentation” and “safe postoperative patient transfer with information handover” recognised as the most commonly omitted items. During the postoperative phase, surgeons, anaesthetists, operating theatre nurses, and recovery room nurses function with distinct work rhythms and priorities. The asynchronous nature of workflow, combined with time constraints, complicates the prioritisation of the detailed and time-consuming handover process. Marsh et al. [6] similarly identified “handoff communication” as a commonly overlooked element. In contrast, safety was identified as the dimension with the lowest incidence of missed nursing care. This finding aligns with prior research [6, 27], indicating that safety-related nursing activities, due to their essential nature, exhibit high compliance across various healthcare systems worldwide. Healthcare institutions, both domestic and international, prioritise safety measures, including surgical safety checklists and surgical site marking, as essential components of quality assurance [37, 38]. The obligatory and verifiable features of these practices substantially diminish the probability of overlooked nursing care in this area. The safety dimension is directly linked to patient life safety and surgical outcomes. The professional values and moral courage of nurses are essential for maintaining patient safety [39, 40]. Nurses prioritise tasks based on professional judgement when confronted with workload pressures, placing the utmost importance on safety measures that could result in immediate severe harm. The intrinsic sense of responsibility, coupled with external policy requirements, ensures the effective implementation of safety-related nursing care.

Furthermore, in the preparation dimension, the items “checking whether the patient wears compression stockings during surgery” and “confirming that questions from the patient and legal guardian regarding the surgery (such as surgical complications, pressure injuries, etc.) have been addressed and signed off”, as well as the item in the communication dimension, “allowing 3 minutes for the patient’s skin to air-dry after disinfection”, demonstrated high omission rates, consistent with previous research findings [6, 41, 42]. Multiple factors may explain the observed patterns. First, perioperative nursing practice guidelines in China often lack clarity regarding the use of compression stockings during surgical procedures, leading to inadequate guidance for nursing staff [43]. Second, certain preventive tasks may be perceived as having minimal intermediate or measurable impacts on patient outcomes [29]. For example, compression stockings provide long-term benefits that are not immediately visible. Third, the intricacy of surgical procedures, coupled with surgeons’ concerns about possible delays in operating theatre schedules, generates significant time constraints [44, 45]. The 3-minute antiseptic drying time conflicts with efficiency pressure. Time constraints are essential for improving operating theatre efficiency; however, they concurrently heighten the risk of omissions in nursing care. These tasks may be perceived as lower priority compared to immediate life-saving interventions, increasing the likelihood of omission. The findings of this study may have important implications for the education and professional development of operating theatre nurses. Highlighting frequently missed nursing tasks may help novice nurses recognise which activities tend to be overlooked in practice, thereby shaping their understanding of task priority during early career stages and promoting a more balanced approach to both immediate and preventive care.

Consistent with previous research [46–48], this study confirms that inadequate staffing and limited material resources are major contributors to missed nursing care in the operating theatre. More than half of the nurses (54.27%) identified insufficient staffing as the primary reason for missed care, including inadequate personnel allocation, inability to ensure appropriate shift rotation, lack of backup staff, and prolonged work-related fatigue. These findings align with evidence from a systematic review of 14 studies [49] and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [50], which recognises missed nursing care as an important indicator of nursing professional staff shortages. Under staffing constraints, nurses tend to prioritise urgent or critical tasks, while other essential care activities may be delayed or omitted, particularly when long-term fatigue further compromises performance [51]. In addition, inadequate material resources, such as poor-quality supplies, delayed equipment maintenance, and the unavailability or malfunction of surgical instruments, were found to contribute significantly to missed care, consistent with findings reported by Breno et al. [8]. Teamwork and communication also emerged as important factors. Unlike some previous studies [52, 53], our findings suggest that insufficient communication between nurses and auxiliary staff leads to delays in patient transfers, operating theatre turnover, supply replenishment, and specimen handling, thereby increasing the likelihood of missed nursing care. These results provide an incentive for managers to optimise the allocation of human and material resources, enhance system functionality—particularly through improved access to information technology—and ensure adequate staffing levels to support safe and comprehensive perioperative nursing care.

Factors independently associated with missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses

MNC in operating theatres is a critical variable that is associated with self-directed learning ability and presenteeism.

This study identified a statistically significant association between higher self-directed learning ability and lower frequency of MNC in the operating theatre. However, the effect size was small (OR = 0.982). Therefore, although the association reached statistical significance (P = 0.009), its clinical significance remains uncertain. The operating theatre is characterised by advanced technologies, specialised surgical techniques, and rapidly evolving clinical demands [54]. These conditions require nurses to continuously update their knowledge and adapt their practice [55]. Self-directed learning—encompassing motivation, self-monitoring, and self-evaluation—may enhance nurses’ ability to anticipate risks, prioritise tasks, and maintain care continuity in high-pressure, resource-limited settings [27, 56]. Nevertheless, given the small effect size, this finding should be interpreted with caution regarding its practical impact. Future interventional studies are needed to determine whether improving self-directed learning contributes to lowering the frequency of MNC in the operating theatre.

The present study found that higher presenteeism among operating theatre nurses was associated with increased missed nursing care. This finding is consistent with previous research [31, 57, 58] and further suggests that inadequate staffing may be a shared underlying factor contributing to both phenomena. Presenteeism may increase the likelihood of missed care through multiple pathways. Impaired physical functioning, reduced cognitive performance, insomnia, and chronic fatigue may limit nurses’ capacity to sustain attention and prioritise tasks appropriately [59, 60]. In this context, nurses might focus on urgent or high-risk tasks, leading to the delay or incompleteness of routine but essential care. The operating theatre is a high-pressure work environment characterised by prolonged shifts and staffing shortages, which may heighten nurses’ vulnerability to presenteeism and fatigue, thereby increasing the risk of missed nursing care [30]. Organisational limitations, such as insufficient staffing and extensive documentation demands, may perpetuate this trend. Together, these findings support the importance of monitoring presenteeism in high-intensity clinical settings such as the operating theatre. Hospital administrators and policymakers should consider optimising shift schedules, ensuring adequate rest periods, implementing early identification of presenteeism. Further research is warranted to explore underlying mechanisms and to assess the effectiveness of targeted interventions in Chinese nursing populations.

Unexpected finding: Specialist nurse training and missed nursing care

Contrary to expectations, speciality nurses in this study also reported higher rates of missed nursing care. This observation contrasts with the prevailing literature [61–63], which largely emphasises the beneficial effects of continuing professional development (CPD) on professional competence and patient outcomes. The association observed here may reflect contextual, organisational factors (such as workload), role overload, unobserved confounding factors (such as hospital type, leadership responsibilities, and organisational support) or selection bias rather than the intrinsic effects of CPD itself. Speciality nurses are often selected for the additional responsibilities, such as clinical preceptorship, quality management, administrative work, mentoring or participation in complex surgical care. These expanded tasks may contribute to role overload. In clinical environments with limited human resources, this role overload may produce adverse spillover effects, such as fatigue and presenteeism, thereby heightening the risk of missed nursing care [64–66]. These results are consistent with the job demands-resources (JD-R) model, which proposes that excessive demands without adequate resources can impair performance [67, 68]. While continuing CPD is vital for enhancing nursing practice, our findings underline the need to match training programs to actual workload capacity—especially in demanding clinical areas. Given the presence of unobserved confounding factors and the absence of stratified analyses or interaction testing, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. It would be premature to conclude that specialist training is counterproductive or that it directly leads to missed nursing care. Future research using longitudinal designs is needed to clarify the temporal direction and underlying mechanisms of this association, and to examine the potential roles of confounding and unmeasured factors.

Study strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, studies in China on the measurement of perioperative missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses and its relationship with presenteeism and self-directed learning ability remain limited. This study adds to the existing evidence by reporting the prevalence of missed nursing care among Chinese perioperative nurses and identifying associated factors, particularly the associations with presenteeism and self-directed learning ability. These findings provide preliminary insights that may help guide future interventions in perioperative practice. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, convenience sampling from public hospitals in a single city limits geographical representativeness and external validity. Although multiple hospitals were involved, the sample did not include private hospitals and hospitals from other provinces. Therefore, these results should be applied to broader populations with caution. Future multicentre studies with more diverse sampling strategies are needed to validate our findings. Such studies should also consider hospital-level structural factors, including surgical volume, nurse-to-patient ratios, and operating room capacity. Secondly, the online self-reported questionnaires were employed, potentially introducing non-response, recall and social desirability biases. Although anonymity, confidentiality and pilot study were preserved to mitigate these effects, no formal assessment of response bias was performed. These potential biases should be considered when interpreting the study findings. Thirdly, work-family conflict and presenteeism were measured using brief instruments (a single-item and a two-item frequency-recall tool). These brief measures were selected to minimise respondent burden and improve the response rate in the time-constrained perioperative clinical environment. However, the limited items may compromise construct validity and underestimate the true associations with missed nursing care. In addition, dichotomisation of the MNC variable may result in some loss of statistical power. Future studies should utilise validated multi-item scales, such as the Work-Family Conflict Scale and the Stanford Presenteeism Scale-6, and ordinal or continuous scoring approaches for MNC, to provide a more robust and nuanced assessment. Finally, the cross-sectional design inhibits the determination of causal relationships or temporal sequences and does not eliminate the possibility of bidirectional influences. For example, presenteeism and MNC may influence each other reciprocally rather than following a purely linear relationship. Longitudinal, large sample or interventional studies are advised to further investigate these relationships and clarify causal mechanisms in various Chinese clinical contexts.

Implications for nursing practice

This study suggests potential directions for practice improvement that may help reduce missed nursing care in the operating theatre, thereby improving patient safety and the quality of nursing in the operating theatre.

For nursing managers

Nursing managers may prioritise the simultaneous optimisation of human and material resources. This involves guaranteeing that operating theatres are staffed with sufficient qualified nurses and are equipped with necessary material resources, including instruments and technological tools that facilitate safe nursing practices. The development of more flexible scheduling systems that account for surgical complexity, shift demands, and staff well-being could also be explored, such as an artificial intelligence-based scheduling system [69, 70].

For nursing educators

Investing in nurses’ professional development and competency enhancement appears promising. This could be achieved by strengthening continuing education programs through the integration of self-directed learning skills training, including goal setting, resource utilisation, self-assessment, and reflective practices. Incorporating online learning resources, such as virtual simulation systems, alongside workshops focused on information literacy and time management skills may further support this goal [71, 72]. In addition, implementing performance incentives or establishing career advancement pathways to enhance intrinsic motivation could be consideration.

For healthcare administrators and policymakers

Administrators and policymakers could consider fostering an open communication culture through regular team meetings, anonymous feedback mechanisms, and risk assessments to create a supportive work environment. Creating a supportive organisational culture that encourages appropriate rest and “healthy absenteeism” may also warrant consideration. These facilitate the prompt identification and resolution of potential risks associated with missed nursing care. However, any broader organisational or system-level interventions should be developed cautiously and rigorously evaluated in future multicenter interventional studies [73].

Conclusions

This study emphasises the critical concern of missed nursing care among operating theatre nurses, reporting a prevalence rate of 34.53%, which indicates potential risks to the quality of clinical nursing. The findings demonstrate that presenteeism and self-directed learning ability are independently associated with missed nursing care in the operating theatre. Within the context of this study, to reduce the missed nursing care in the operating theatre, nursing managers may consider prioritising resolving systemic constraints related to staffing and material resources. Evidence-based interventions to address presenteeism can also be implemented. Targeted efforts to enhance self‑directed learning capabilities, as a long‑term professional development strategy, should be pursued alongside supportive cultural initiatives. These efforts aim to improve perioperative patient safety.
