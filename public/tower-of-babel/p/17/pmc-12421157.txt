Immunogenicity, safety and adverse events of sequential vaccination with a 10-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV10) and PPSV23 compared with PPSV23 alone in systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Rudrarpan Chatterjee, Sai Yasaswini Kommaraju, Shincy Mettingal Ramakrishnan, Kadahalli Lingegowda Ravikumar, Amita Aggarwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSLE has increased risk of invasive pneumococcal disease due to immune dysregulation and immunosuppression. WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The safety and immunogenicity of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) are established. It is recommended that all patients with SLE undergo vaccination with sequential PCV and a 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23). In resource-constrained settings, a single-dose vaccination with the PPSV23 vaccine is a common practice. In published literature, there is a single head-to-head comparison of these two regimens that used a seven-valent conjugate vaccine in the sequential arm. This study from France, a developed country, showed that the single-dose vaccine was as effective as the sequential vaccine. In a resource-constrained country, a single vaccine may be more cost-effective, but there is at present no evidence to support the same.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this study, we found that the overall antibody response measured by ELISA of the single-dose PPSV23 is the same as sequential vaccination with 10-valent PCV, followed by PPSV23. However, on testing for functional antibodies by opsonophagocytic assay, sequential vaccination generated more functional antibodies to the most common serotypes of Streptococcus pneumoniae that cause an invasive pneumococcal disease in South Asia than PPSV23 alone.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that a sequential vaccination strategy results in better functional antibodies to common pneumococcal serotypes in South Asia, and a newly developed 10-valent conjugate vaccine should be considered for sequential vaccination in South Asian patients with SLE. In view of the overall immunogenicity being similar, if the conjugate vaccine is not available, the cheaper PPSV23 alone should still be administered. Future research should focus on long-term efficacy of these vaccines and long-term protective antibody response.

Introduction

Patients with SLE have an immune dysfunction like lymphopenia, neutrophil function defects, reduced complement levels and altered cytokine profile.1 Due to immune dysregulation, patients with SLE are at an inherently increased risk of infections.2 3 In addition, most patients are on immunosuppressive treatment which further puts them at an increased risk of infections. Invasive pneumococcal infections have been shown to be more common in patients with SLE than the general adult population in studies from developed countries with an incidence rate of 201–236/100 000 person-years.3 4 In a recent Indian study of 1354 patients, 439 serious infections were recorded in 339 patients (61.6 per 1000 person-years’ follow-up).5 Of these, 20 patients had infection due to Streptococcus pneumoniae.5 A recent nested case–control study from the UK has shown that pneumococcal vaccination in patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases protects against hospitalisation and death due to pneumonia.6

A systematic review and meta-analysis that included 14 studies on the immunogenicity of pneumococcal vaccine in patients with SLE suggested that there are only low-quality data on response to vaccine in patients with lupus.7 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) guidelines recommend universal pneumococcal vaccination in patients with SLE. The EULAR guidelines of 2019 recommend sequential vaccination with a 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13), followed by a 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23), while the ACR 2022 guidelines recommend following current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines for adult vaccination.8 9

In a large study from South India, in patients with invasive pneumococcal disease, a total of 408 isolates were obtained over a period of 11 years. Serotypes 1, 3, 5, 19F, 8, 14, 23F, 4, 19A and 6B were the common serotypes in invasive pneumococcal disease, with serotype 1 being the most common.10 Studies from India have shown the efficacy and immunogenicity of the sequential vaccine using a 13-valent conjugate vaccine in children aged 6–17 years and adults aged 50–65 years, as these groups are more susceptible to invasive pneumococcal disease.11 12

The sequential vaccination has logistical issues, as there is a gap of 12–24 weeks between the two doses. In patients with lupus, the immunosuppression to control disease flare hampers the development of an immune response. In addition, the added expense of sequential vaccination is often a limitation in developing countries. However, the evidence for sequential vaccine in SLE is very limited.13 The WHO has recently approved a newer low-cost 10-valent conjugate vaccine indigenously developed in India for resource-limited settings.14 15

We hypothesised that the immunogenicity of the sequential vaccination would be similar to single-dose vaccination. Thus, we planned to look at sequential pneumococcal vaccination using PCV10 and PPSV23 in patients with SLE to assess its immunogenicity, side effects and impact on lupus disease activity in comparison to vaccination with PPSV23 alone.

Methods

Patients

Adult patients with SLE (ACR/EULAR 2019 criteria) with no change in disease activity (clinical SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)) and background immunosuppressive drugs in the last 3 months and on a stable dose of prednisolone ≤10 mg/day were included after giving informed consent.16 This was a single-centre study from North India.

Participants in the sequential arm received 10-valent conjugate pneumococcal vaccine (Pneumosil, Serum Institute of India), followed by the PPSV23 (Pneumovax 23, MSD Pharmaceuticals) after 3 months. The second arm received only the PPSV23 vaccine. Treatment allocation was open label and non-randomised. Two cohorts of patients and a cohort of healthy controls were recruited for the study. Initially, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we were able to recruit only healthy controls from April 2020 to December 2020. We enrolled a cohort of SLE for vaccination with PPSV23 from January 2021 to December 2021 from patients attending the outpatient clinic. Although our initial plan was to compare PPSV23 alone with healthy controls, a 10-valent conjugated vaccine became available in India in 2021. Hence, we decided to enrol another cohort of SLE who received PCV10, followed by PPSV23, from January 2023 to June 2023 from patients attending the outpatient clinic to compare the sequential vaccination with the single-dose vaccine as well as healthy controls. This was done after the protocol amendment was accepted by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Samples at baseline and 12–14 weeks postvaccination were prospectively collected to assess the immunogenicity of the vaccine, the primary outcome under study, in both cohorts of patients. In addition, side effects and disease flares (Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment (SELENA)-SLEDAI flare index) were also assessed.17

40 subjects were enrolled in each vaccine cohort. In addition, 15 healthy adult subjects were enrolled for studying the response to PPSV23 alone. The sample size was determined by convenience and was based on a previous study comparing sequential vaccination to single-dose PPSV23 which had 17 and 25 patients in the two arms, respectively.13

ELISA for pneumococcal antibodies

Immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody concentrations for five pneumococcal serotypes shared by both PPSV23 and PCV10 vaccines (1, 5, 6B, 14, 19F) were measured at each study visit using a modified ELISA described by the pneumococcal reference laboratory at University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB).18 Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with a serotype-specific pneumococcal polysaccharide antigen (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, Virginia) and incubated for 5 hours at 37°C. Reference sera (007sp), quality control sera or patient specimens were preabsorbed with 5 µg/mL pneumococcal C-polysaccharide (Statens Serum Institut, Copenhagen, Denmark) and 10 µg/mL serotype 22F capsular polysaccharide (American Type Culture Collection) for 30 min at room temperature before being serially diluted. After washing plates, serially diluted serum was added, and plates were incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Plates were then washed, and alkaline phosphatase-conjugated goat antihuman IgG was added. After another 2 hours of incubation and washing, substrate (p-nitrophenyl phosphate in diethanolamine buffer, pH 9.8) was added to the plates. After a final incubation, the optical density was measured at 405 nm. Antipneumococcal antibody levels were determined in each specimen by analysis of linear regression plots compared with the reference serum (007sp).

Multiplexed opsonophagocytic assay

The functional opsonic antibodies against five serotypes were assessed as previously described by multiplexed opsonophagocytic assay (MOPA).19 Briefly, the 30 µL of serum samples was diluted serially (threefold) in opsonisation assay buffer B (OBB) and tested in duplicates. A bacterial mixture was prepared from frozen working stocks in OBB, containing approximately 50 000 CFU/mL of each of the five strains. 10 μL of the bacterial mixture was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min with shaking (700 rpm). Following incubation, 50 µL of HL60/complement (4:1) mixture was added to the wells, and the plates were incubated with shaking at 37°C/5% CO2 for 45 min. The plates were incubated on ice for 20 min, and 10 µL of the reaction mixture from each well was spotted onto Todd-Hewitt yeast (THY) agar plates. After 20 min, agar consisting of THY with 0.75% agar, along with one of the four antibiotics and 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (Sigma), was overlaid onto the plates and allowed to solidify. The plates were incubated for a period of 16–18 hours at 37°C/5% CO2. The viable colonies were counted, and opsonisation indices were calculated using opsotiter3 software (UAB, Alabama).

Analysis

A positive response to vaccination was defined as at least a twofold increase from baseline in three out of the five serotypes tested by ELISA. Overall response to vaccination was represented as proportion of all vaccinated patients developing this response by ELISA, with CIs calculated by bootstrapping. A titre of 1.3 µg/mL was taken as protective as used in other adult studies.

A positive response to a serotype by MOPA, signifying functional antibodies, was defined as a fourfold increase from baseline in the opsonisation index and a follow-up titre ≥8. Antibody titres by ELISA and MOPA were represented as geometric mean concentration and SD for prevaccination and postvaccination samples. Prevaccination and postvaccination titres were compared using a paired t-test. Χ2 test was used to test for difference between categorical variables.

We used Spearman’s rank coefficient to determine the correlation between postvaccination antibody titres by ELISA and MOPA. To identify predictors of immunogenicity for each serotype, we fitted a linear mixed effects model, with the log (fold change in antibody titre) as the outcome variable; patient’s age, disease activity (as per SELENA-SLEDAI) and prednisolone dose at vaccination as fixed effects; and vaccination schedule as a random effect. R V.4.3.3 and RStudio V.24.04.2 were used for analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design of the study.

Results

Among 40 patients in the PPSV23 arm, two were lost to follow-up, and baseline serum samples of three patients could not be traced in the laboratory as they were collected during COVID time. Among 40 patients in the sequential arm, 37 came for the next vaccine dose, while three were lost to follow-up. Among these 37 patients, one refused the second dose, one was found to be pregnant and one had the second dose delayed due to a urinary tract infection. The baseline parameters were comparable among the two groups (table 1).

Immunogenicity

Baseline data

At baseline, a significant proportion of subjects had antibody titres above 1.3 µg/mL, suggesting that they had a pre-existing protective immunity. Among the total 69 patients who finally completed the study, protective levels of antibodies were present at baseline in 28 (40.5%) for serotype 1; 22 (31.8%) for serotype 5; 22 (31.8%) for serotype 6B; 67 (97.1%) for serotype 14; and 52 (75.4%) for serotype 19 (online supplemental table 1). This was not different between the two arms.

Response to vaccination by ELISA

The immune response to PPSV23 for the primary outcome was poor in SLE (26/35, 74.28%) compared with healthy controls (15/15, 100%) (p=0.03). Patients in the PPSV23 arm had a response to at least three out of five serotypes tested in 74.28% (95% CI 60% to 89%) compared with 82.35% (95% CI 68% to 94%) in the sequential vaccination arm (table 2). There was no significant difference in the vaccine response in the two strategies.

In the subgroup of patients with a suboptimal vaccine response (less than twofold change) by ELISA, the median fold change in MOPA titres was still high. For serotype 1 (n=25), median fold change was 2.5 (1.47–6.08) for the PPSV23 arm and 2.5 (2.5–9.39) for the sequential arm. For serotype 5 (n=19), median fold change was 1.7 (1.48–1.97) for the PPSV23 arm and 104.62 (29.95–224.53) for the sequential arm. For serotype 6B (n=6), median fold change was 4.4 (1.47–11.94) for the PPSV23 arm and 1.82 (1.82–1.82) for the sequential arm. For serotype 14 (n=25), median fold change was 2.99 (1.89–6.65) for the PPSV23 arm and 7.61 (2.21–12.9) for the sequential arm. For serotype 19F (n=25), median fold change was 2.99 (1.58–12.57) for the PPSV23 arm and 5.28 (3.68–115.1) for the sequential arm.

Predictors of immunogenicity

We fitted a multivariable linear mixed effects model to predict the fold change in antibody titres for each serotype. Age was consistently a significant negative predictor of immunogenicity for all serotypes except for serotype 1 (online supplemental table S2). For each year increase in age at the time of vaccination, the fold change in postvaccination antibodies decreased by 0.962 times for serotype 5; 0.965 times for serotype 6; 0.972 times for serotype 14; and 0.962 times for serotype 19F. There were no other disease-specific predictors for immunogenicity. The intercept and residual variance of the random effect (single vs sequential schedule of vaccination) was low, suggesting that the type of vaccination did not have a significant effect on the fold change in antibody titres (online supplemental table S2).

Effect on SLE disease activity

Overall, most patients had no impact on the disease activity. In the PPSV23 arm, three minor flares were seen, of which one was arthritis and the other two were cutaneous flares. In the sequential arm, one major flare and one minor flare were seen over 6 months. The major flare was a multiorgan flare with a SLEDAI of 14, and she needed methylprednisolone pulse and change in her immunosuppressive treatment. The minor flare was haematological with worsening of thrombocytopenia. Both occurred after the second dose in the sequential arm.

Overall, there was no statistically significant change in SLEDAI postvaccination in either arm. In the PPSV23 group, the median SLEDAI at baseline was 0 (0–2), and at follow-up it was 0 (0–2). In the sequential group, the median SLEDAI at baseline was 0 (0–2), and at follow-up it was 0 (0–2).

Adverse events

In the sequential vaccination arm after PCV10 conjugate vaccination, one patient each had pain at the injection site, migraine (1 month after the dose, resolved spontaneously), fever and fatigue for 2–3 days. One patient who had neuropsychiatric SLE in the past refused the second dose of vaccine and developed Miller Fisher variant of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) 4 months after the first dose of vaccination. She was positive for antibody to GQ1b ganglioside. She was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin and other immunosuppressive agents, and she recovered. The causality could not be linked to vaccination and may be related to her basic disease. One patient had a fever after receiving the PPSV23 dose.

In the PPSV23 arm, one each had pain and swelling at the injection site, herpes zoster reactivation (after 2 months of dose), headache and fever. Among the healthy controls who received PPSV23, two patients had minor injection-site reactions.

Discussion

A comparison of sequential (PCV10 followed by PPSV23) vaccine versus PPSV23 in patients with SLE, taking PPSV23 in healthy subjects as control group, showed that both vaccine strategies are safe and effective. Patients with SLE have lower seroconversion as compared with healthy controls with PPSV23.

The criteria for response to pneumococcal vaccine are variable across studies, and it varies from a twofold or fourfold rise in antibody titres by ELISA, postimmunisation titre of >1.3 µg/mL when baseline is <1.3 µg/mL. Despite lack of strong evidence, the consensus is to consider response as a twofold rise in antibody titres in at least 70% of serotypes by ELISA.20 Since we tested for five serotypes, we have defined this as response to at least three serotypes. Even with the stringent criteria of response to at least four serotypes, the seroconversion rates in our study were 80% in healthy controls, 60% with PPSV23 and 67% with sequential vaccine.

Short-term immunogenicity was measured at 12–14 weeks postvaccination. By ELISA, all the healthy controls had significantly better response (100%) than either vaccine strategies in patients with SLE. This is similar to the data reported in previous studies.21 22 In contrast, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Pugès et al described similar seroconversion rates post-PPSV23 vaccine in 67 patients with SLE and 26 healthy controls. However, their response criteria were antibodies to serotype 23F.23 We have not tested for antibodies to serotype 23F but for the serotypes most commonly causing invasive pneumococcal disease in India.

Sequential vaccination resulted in similar immunogenicity as PPSV23 in our study (82.35% vs 74.8%). A previous study by Grabar et al also reported similar immunogenicity with 72% in PPSV arm and 76% in sequential arm.13 However, this study had a smaller sample size of 25 in the PPSV arm and 17 in the sequential vaccination arm. The slightly higher rate in the sequential arm could be due to the use of PCV10 conjugate vaccine in our study as against PCV7 conjugate vaccine in their study.13

A higher proportion of patients in the sequential arm demonstrated functional antibodies by OPA to all five tested serotypes, which underscores a potential advantage of a sequential vaccination strategy. This suggests that sequential vaccination may promote a more robust and broader immune activation in a subset of patients, although overall vaccine responses are similar to the single-dose polysaccharide vaccine. The observed positive correlations between absolute postvaccination antibody titres as measured by ELISA and functional antibody responses assessed through the MOPA further reinforce the validity of the immunological assessments used in this study. Specifically, serotypes 1, 5, 14 and 19F displayed strong correlations, indicating that higher antibody levels are associated with increased functional capacity to opsonise pathogens.24 Grabar et al showed that sequential administration of PCV7, followed by PPSV23, had a similar response (76%) compared with PPSV23 alone (72%) in patients with SLE when the seven serotypes 4, 6B, 9V, 14, 18C, 19F and 23F were tested.13 In other immunocompromised conditions, sequential vaccination showed an advantage over single vaccine administration.25 Some studies comparing OPA and ELISA tests indicated potential discrepancies between the two laboratory methods, which could be influenced by the specific pneumococcal serotype analysed.26 However, we found a positive agreement between ELISA and OPA.

Around 25–97% of patients had baseline protective titres of antibodies to different serotypes in our study which was similar to what was seen in healthy controls. Presence of high baseline titre could be a result of previous infection or nasopharyngeal carriage of the bacteria. Chowdhry et al showed that anti-dsDNA antibodies can cross-react with the pneumococcal polysaccharide leading to higher baseline titres.27 However, we did not find an association between anti-dsDNA antibody titre and baseline antipneumococcal antibodies. In our study, baseline titres did not differ between the healthy controls and patients with SLE, thus ruling out a possibility of cross-reactivity. Almost all patients had protective baseline titres to serotype 14. However, serotype 1, which is the most common pathogenic strain causing invasive pneumococcal disease in India, had the least amount of baseline antibodies. Thus, vaccination in these patients is still warranted, as the attributable mortality due to invasive pneumococcal disease is significant.

On multivariate analysis, no factor except for age was found to predict immunogenicity. It is interesting that most of the non-responders were on corticosteroids. Jarrett et al have also reported no difference in immunogenicity with different doses of steroids, but there was numerically better response in those on no steroids.21 Steroid-sparing drugs also did not affect the response. In the study by Rezende et al, the response rate to PPSV23 by ELISA was the same in the group with immunosuppressive drugs (cyclophosphamide/azathioprine/mycophenolate mofetil) and without, with less than 5 mg steroid in both groups.28 But the mean fold rise was higher in those without immunosuppressive drugs.28 Most of our patients were also on mycophenolate mofetil, followed by azathioprine and methotrexate, while none were currently on cyclophosphamide.

About 10% of patients had minor adverse events like fever, fatigue, migraine and pain at the injection site, which were similar in both groups and like that seen in the general population.29 One patient had the Miller Fisher variant of GBS with anti-GQ1b antibody, in the PCV arm, 4 months after PCV10. She also had underlying neuropsychiatric lupus with mononeuritis multiplex. Whether it was due to vaccine or neuropsychiatric lupus, the flare is still unclear. GBS has been reported post-PCV, in general population, at a median onset of 9 days (range 2–34 days) after PCV.29 However, our patient developed it after 4 months.

In contrast to the known concept, conjugate vaccine did not cause more flares than unconjugated PPSV. The flare rate over 6 months postvaccination was 6.2%. The flare rates after achievement of lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS), clinical remission and complete remission on treatment for lupus in general are 0.23, 0.12 and 0.1 per patient-year, respectively, and the rates in this study are within that range.30

The strengths of the study include that despite its small numbers, it is still the largest study to date comparing PPSV23 and sequential vaccination strategies in patients with SLE, use of both MOPA and ELISA to measure antibody response and use of healthy controls in addition. The limitations of the study include relatively small sample size, inclusion of patients in low disease activity or remission and, thus, the results not being applicable to patients with active disease. Especially, it is recommended that patients with major organ involvement, such as active lupus nephritis and lung disease in the form of interstitial lung disease (ILD) on immunosuppression, are vaccinated for invasive pneumococcal disease. However, all study patients with nephritis had inactive, clinically quiescent disease and only a single patient in each cohort had ILD. The absence of randomisation was another limitation of the study, although there was a difference between the two groups in terms of some organ manifestations; overall, all the patients had clinically inactive disease and were on similar immunosuppression. The study also excluded patients on B cell-depleting therapies, and the results cannot be extrapolated to this group of patients.
