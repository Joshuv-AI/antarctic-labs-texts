Atorvastatin pretreatment, ST-segment resolution and long-term prognosis for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with primary percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Chao Wu, Pei Gao, Tiange Chen, Haiyan Xu, Xiang Li, Yan Wang, Honglei Zhao, Zhifang Wang, Guotong Xie, Yuejin Yang, Xiaojin Gao, Jingang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe benefit of statin pretreatment before primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) on myocardial reperfusion and prognosis in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains unclear. In this study, we evaluated whether atorvastatin pretreatment could improve ST-segment resolution (STR) and long-term clinical outcomes in this setting.MethodsFrom the China Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry, we conducted propensity score matching to compare STR and 2-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE, all-cause death, reinfarction, and stroke) in 2426 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI (1213 patients per group).ResultsWithin the pretreatment group, 75, 726, 60, and 691 patients received 20 mg, 40 mg, 60 mg or 80 mg atorvastatin respectively. In the matched cohort of 2426 patients with available STR data (1213 pretreated), STR < 50 % occurred in 258 (21 %) patients in the control group versus 159 (13 %) in the pretreatment group (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 0.53; 95 % CI: 0.41–0.70). Multivariable analysis showed that atorvastatin pretreatment was significantly associated with lower 2-year MACE rates (6.9 % vs 8.7 %; adjusted HR: 0.68; 95 % CI: 0.48–0.97), which were consistent across multiple subgroups.ConclusionA single dose of atorvastatin pretreatment before primary PCI significantly improves myocardial reperfusion parameters and may be associated with long-term clinical benefits, supporting further validation in randomized trials.Keywords: STEMI, Primary PCI, Atorvastation, Pretreatment

Introduction

In the current era of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), the post-procedural no-reflow phenomenon remains a significant clinical challenge, persistently associated with adverse clinical outcomes in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [1]. ST-segment resolution (STR), an organ-level electrocardiographic manifestation of restored myocardial electrical stability after successful microvascular reperfusion, is a widely utilized and practical marker that has emerged as a key surrogate endpoint in reperfusion strategy trials [2,3]. Our previous research further established STR as an independent predictor of long-term mortality, demonstrating prognostic value beyond conventional clinical variables in real-world settings [4].

While statin pretreatment prior to primary PCI has shown promise in enhancing microvascular perfusion—as evidenced by improvements in STR and other angiographic/cardiac marker—its impact on hard clinical outcomes remains uncertain [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. The STATIN STEMI trial, which evaluated high-dose statin loading in 171 primary PCI patients, reported improved STR but no statistically significant reduction in short- or long-term major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) compared to low-dose atorvastatin [6]. Similarly, a retrospective analysis of 170 patients found that high-intensity statin loading correlated with higher complete STR rates but no significant differences in in-hospital MACE [10]. These findings suggest that prior studies may have been underpowered to detect clinical benefits due to limited sample sizes.

To address these limitations, we used data from the China Acute Myocardial Infarction (CAMI) registry to conduct a large-scale, propensity score-matched analysis. This study aims to explore the effect of statin pretreatment on improving both STR and long-term clinical outcomes for STEMI patients with primary PCI.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was a registry-based analysis involving patients with STEMI who underwent primary PCI. The patients included in the study were identified from CAMI registry from January 1, 2013 to September 30, 2014. The study design of the CAMI registry has been described previously [11]. In short, The CAMI registry is a prospective, nationwide, multicenter observational study of patients with acute myocardial infarction (MI) in China. The registry includes 3 levels of hospitals (provincial-, prefectural-, and county-level hospitals), covering all the provinces and municipalities across mainland China, which render it likely representative of routine real-world clinical practice of acute MI care in China. Each participating site was instructed to enroll consecutive patients with acute MI, within 7 days of acute ischemic symptoms with a primary clinical diagnosis of acute MI, including STEMI and non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Final inclusion criteria must meet third Universal Definition for Myocardial Infarction (2012) [12]. The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01874691) and was approved by the institutional review board of all participating hospitals.

Patients were eligible for inclusion in this study if they were patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI. The exclusion criteria for this study were the following: (1) patients treated with statin within one week before admission; (2) patients who were not treated with statins during hospitalization; (3) patients treated with other types of statin than atorvastatin; and (4) patients without any follow-up information.

Patient demographics, clinical presentation, medical history, risk factors, triggering factors, physical examination, laboratory and imaging results, transfer facility therapies, reperfusion strategies, medications, clinical events, and costs was collected using the standardized set of variables and standard definitions at each participating sites, Systematic data entry and transmission procedures, and rigorous data quality control was applied. Definitions of elements were based on the ACC/AHA Task Force on clinical Data Standards and NCDR-ACTION-GWTG element dictionary [[13], [14], [15]]. All variables were coded with CDISC, ICD-10, MedDra, and WHO-DD to make them standardized.

Intervention and outcomes

STEMI Patients were categorized into two groups: with or without pretreatment of single dose of atorvastatin prior to primary PCI procedure in the hospital. All patients also received loading dose of aspirin 300 mg and clopidogrel/ticagrelor 300–600 mg/180 mg before the procedure. Dual antiplatelet therapy was hopefully to be taken at least 1 year if there was no accident. Primary PCI was performed by a 24-hour on-call interventional team, according to standard clinical practice at each participating hospital. The Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade before and after primary PCI was evaluated by a standard method. Following primary PCI, atorvastatin, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blocker(ACEI/ARB), β-blocker, etc were applied according to Chinese guidelines and individual applications.

The primary endpoint was defined as the MACE of all-cause mortality, recurrent MI or stroke events during follow-up. The secondary outcomes was STR < 50 % at 120 min after primary PCI. The ECG Analysis has been described previously(4). Briefly, the qualifying ECGs should fulfill the following criteria: (1) ≥ 1 mm of ST-segment elevation in at least 2 contiguous leads; (2) without bundle–branch block, ventricular pacing, or rhythm. STR was evaluated based on ECGs acquired on admission and 120 min after primary PCI in local participating hospitals, measuring in the single lead with the most prominent STE on the baseline. Other outcomes included all-cause mortality, recurrent MI, stroke, bleeding, in-hospital new or worsening heart failure, and in-hospital adverse liver events. Bleeding was defined as reduction in hemoglobin concentration of ≥ 3 g/dL, use of blood transfusion, or requiring intervention (medical practitioner-guided medical or endoscopic/surgical treatment to stop or treat bleeding, including temporarily or permanently discontinuing or changing the dose of a medication or study drug) [16]. Adverse liver event was defined as elevations of liver transaminases, i.e. aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), >3 times above the upper normal limit.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes for patients in the full cohort were compared. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range and were compared using the analysis of t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared using the Pearson χ2 test. Multivariable logistic or Cox regression models were calculated in order to examine the association between pretreatment and clinical outcomes.

Given the differences in the baseline characteristics between patients with vs without atorvastation pretreatment (supplementary Table), propensity score matching was used to identify a cohort of patients with similar baseline characteristics [17]. The propensity score was estimated using multivariable logistic regression model with a group of baseline measured variables including age, sex, admission status, cardiovascular risk factors, history of cardiocerebrovascular disease, key laboratory tests, pre- and post-procedural TIMI flow, and medications at discharge, et al. We also used random imputation drawn from the sample population to impute missing values when these covariates were missing for PS matching. Optimal matching was performed with the use of a 1:1 matching protocol. Standardized differences were estimated for all the baseline covariates after matching.

In the matched cohort, paired comparisons were performed with McNemar’s test for binary variables and pairwise t test for continuous variables. We summarized the statistics using only non-missing values in Table 1. Cox model stratified on the matched pair was used for the estimation of hazard ratios (HRs) for the primary and secondary endpoints within 24-month follow-up25. Conditional logistic regression was used for the estimation of odds ratios (ORs) for the in-hospital secondary endpoints. HRs or ORs were calculated in the unadjusted model and the model adjusted for history of MI, TIMI score before primary PCI, K+, Clopidogrel (Yes/No), β-blocker (Yes/No) and ACEI/ARB (Yes/No). These variables were selected because there was still significant difference after PS-match (P < 0.05). We didn’t adjust in-hospital atorvastation maintenance dosage in the primary analysis because we found its strong correlation with the exposer/intervention (spearman’s correlation was 0.63). To assess the potential effect by the atorvastation maintenance dosage, we conducted the corresponding subgroup analysis.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed on individual-level characteristics: sex, age (<65 years vs ≥ 65 years), diabetes status (definite vs other), region (urban vs rural), location (Eastern China vs others), site of myocardial infraction (anterior wall infarction vs others), severity of coronary artery disease (single-vessel vs multiple-vessel), left ventricular ejection fraction (≤40 %, 40–50 % vs > 50 %), Killip class (class I vs others), time from symptom onset to hospital (≤6 h vs > 6 h), atorvastation pretreatment dosage (40 mg vs 80 mg only) and in-hospital atorvastation maintenance dosage (≤20 mg vs ≥ 40 mg). Tests for interaction were performed to assess for heterogeneity of treatment effect among subgroups. We used multiple imputation when these adjusted covariates were missing when calculated for HRs or ORs (number of imputation was 5). Sensitivity analysis using patients with complete information of these confounders were also conducted.

P values were two-tailed and were not adjusted for multiple testing. We judged p values less than 0.05 significant. We did all statistical analysis with R (version 3.5.1) and STATA (version 14).

Results

Study population

Overall, 26,613 patients from 108 hospitals were enrolled in the CAMI registry from January 1, 2013, to September 30, 2014. Total 7593 patients with STEMI underwent primary PCI (Fig. 1). Finally, 3956 patients met our inclusion criteria. Among these patients, 1552 patients received atorvastatin pretreatment and 2404 patients were in the control group. Within the pretreatment group, 75, 726, 60, and 691 patients received 20 mg, 40 mg, 60 mg or 80 mg atorvastatin respectively. Baseline characteristics, clinical outcomes and subgroup analysis of patients in the initial cohort before PS matching were shown in supplementary Table. After matching, there were 1213 patients in each group. The standardized differences were generally less than 10.0 % except TIMI score before primary PCI (P < 0.0001) and discharge medicine with ACEI/ARB (P < 0.0001), indicating only small differences between matched groups (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

In the PS-match cohort, 258 (21 %) vs 159 (13 %) of patients had STR < 50 % in the control and the pretreatment group (p < 0.001). Total of 190 events from 2426 patients were recorded during 10136.79 person-years at risk. Overall 106 (8.7 %) and 84 (6.9 %) patients in the control group and pretreatment group had the primary outcome respectively (p = 0.044, Fig. 2 & Table 2). For other secondary outcomes, total 146 patients died during follow-up, i.e. 82 (6.8 %) vs 64 (5.2 %) in the control and the pretreatment group respectively (p = 0.091). Overall 8 (0.6 %) vs 9 (0.7 %) had severe bleeding in the control and the pretreatment group during follow-up (p = 0.796). Total 116 (99.5 %) vs 140 (11.5 %) had in-hospital new or worsening heart failure, and 7 (0.6 %) vs 4 (0.3 %) had in-hospital adverse liver events (both p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed a PS-matched cohort of STEMI patients to evaluate the association between statin pretreatment before primary PCI and: (1) myocardial reperfusion assessed by STR, and (2) long-term clinical outcomes. Key findings were: (1) Single-dose of atorvastatin pretreatment significantly improved STR after adjusting for clinical confounders. (2) The pretreatment was associated with a reduced risk of long-term MACE, with consistent benefits across various dosing regimens and clinical subgroups.

Our results align with most prior studies [5,6,9,10], demonstrating that atorvastatin pretreatment can improve myocardial perfusion (evidenced by lower occurrence of STR < 50 % rates). The likely mechanisms—distinct from traditional cholesterol-lowering effects— include improvements in endothelial function, dilation of the coronary microvasculature, and anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic effects [18]. These may preserve cardiac function and prevent adverse remodeling by ameliorating coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) [1]. The current analysis helps reconcile previous conflicting reports regarding statins' prognostic value in primary PCI-treated STEMI [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The modest but statistically significant STR improvement we observed (9 %) provides a plausible explanation why smaller studies with limited follow-up durations might have failed to detect clinical benefits [7,[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Our larger sample size and extended 2-year follow-up period provided adequate power to demonstrate significant reductions in the composite endpoint of mortality, MI, and stroke. However, given the modest difference in STR incidence and the associated mortality gap, the benefit of atorvastatin pretreatment prior to primary PCI should not be overstated.

The selection of clinical endpoint may represent another critical factor contributing to the inconclusive evidence regarding the benefit of statin pretreatment before PCI [6,10,[19], [20], [21]]. The observed STR improvement supports the concept that statins primarily influence microvascular rather than epicardial coronary pathophysiology in STEMI. This aligns with evidence that CMD improvement affects ventricular remodeling processes more than plaque-related events which directly mediate reductions in recurrent myocardial infarction (MI), target-vessel revascularization, and stent thrombosis [6,10,19,21]. This mechanistic divergence likely explains why earlier trials failed to demonstrate significant benefits for these latter outcomes. However, the absence of dedicated assessment for heart failure—a clinically relevant endpoint closely linked to CMD [22]—represents a critical gap in current evidence. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are therefore urgently needed, particularly those designed to evaluate long-term cardiovascular outcomes, including heart failure incidence and functional status.

In this study, the atorvastatin pretreatment dosages administered to STEMI patients undergoing emergency PCI were primarily 40 mg or 80 mg. This variation may be attributed to the discretion of treating physicians, as the CAMI registry encompassed hospitals at provincial, municipal, and county levels, and no unified expert consensus regarding statin loading doses was established during the study period. Although our subgroup analysis the HRs were similar across pretreatment dosage, further studies is warranted to determine the optimal dosing.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, owing to the inherent limitations of PS matching, our results may have been due to immortal time bias or a lack of adjustment for multiple comparisons, including a residual imbalance in TIMI grade before primary PCI persisted despite propensity score matching. Therefore, our findings necessitate cautious interpretation and should be considered as generating hypotheses for future validation in randomized controlled trials. Secondly, the absence of certain key data, such as myocardial infarction biomarkers (since there is no standardized measurement for these laboratory tests across different levels in Chinese hospitals in that era), and the timing of atorvastatin administration, may introduce residual confounding and compromise the balance achieved through propensity score matching, potentially influencing the interpretation of the results. Thirdly, due to the limited number of participants at the 40 mg and 80 mg, PS-matched analysis for the direct comparison between these two dosage groups was not applied which may cause the residual confounding in the estimation of effects. Furthermore, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons because our primary analyses were hypothesis-driven and focused on a limited number of outcomes. However, the results from secondary analyses may reflect type I error and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger samples and replication across independent datasets are warranted to confirm these associations. Finally, our findings may have limited generalizability to other countries or healthcare systems with differing treatment guidelines, clinical practices, and patient characteristics.

Conclusions

Among patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI, pretreatment of single dose of atorvastatin resulted in improved microvascular reperfusion and may associated with favor long-term prognosis. These findings raised the consideration for the routine statin pretreatment prior to primary PCI among patients with STEMI. The conclusive assessment of this pretreatment strategy and its impact on clinical outcomes awaits validation from large-scale RCTs.

Clinical perspectives

What is already known on this topic

Statin pretreatment before primary PCI for STEMI has yet to receive guideline‑level endorsement, largely because prior small‑scale studies have produced inconsistent findings on its effects on myocardial reperfusion and clinical outcomes.

What this study adds

In this large propensity‑score–matched analysis, a single dose of atorvastatin pretreatment before primary PCI was associated with significantly improved post-procedural STR, suggesting a potential long‑term prognostic benefit.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

Large randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these clinical benefits, particularly those designed to assess long-term heart-failure outcomes.
