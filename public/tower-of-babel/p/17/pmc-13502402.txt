Successful immunosuppressive treatment of hypersensitivity pneumonitis secondary to lycoperdonosis in a dog
Authors: Iliana Navarro, Aida Vientós-Plotts, Isabelle Masseau, Erin Farrell, Angela Royal, Carol R Reinero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 7-year-old neutered male Cocker Spaniel with unrestricted access to rural land presented for progressive lethargy, tachypnea, and cough. Thoracic radiographs showed an unstructured interstitial pattern. Fine-needle aspirate (FNA) lung cytology identified pyogranulomatous inflammation with intralesional mushroom spores. Acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) was suspected, and treatment was initiated with oxygen supplementation and an immunosuppressive dosage of corticosteroid. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) were performed on day 6, month 2, and year 1 to track therapeutic response and assess architectural changes. Clinical signs guided prednisone tapering and discontinuation. One year later, thoracic CT and BAL confirmed re-exposure to mushroom spores with mild persistent inflammation and CT changes suggestive of mixed parenchymal and small airway disease despite absent clinical signs. This case report emphasizes the importance of rapid identification of pulmonary mushroom spores for prompt immunosuppressive treatment. Unlike prior reports of lycoperdonosis with acute, fulminating, and generally fatal outcomes, this dog developed chronic HP.Keywords: computed tomography, cytology, interstitial lung disease, mushroom, puffball, pulmonary fibrosis, small airway disease

Introduction

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an immune-mediated interstitial lung disease in people that develops after exposure to low-molecular weight compounds <3-5 μm in size.1–3 Affecting the pulmonary parenchyma and small airways (bronchioles), HP results from an immune response to an inhaled antigen in susceptible patients, emphasizing sensitization as a key factor in HP pathogenesis.4 This disease manifests itself in 2 clinical forms: (1) inflammatory or non-fibrotic HP, which is generally acute or subacute, and (2) mixed inflammatory plus fibrotic or pure fibrotic HP, which is generally chronic.1,4 There are 6 reports of HP in dogs linked to mushroom spore exposure.2,3,5–7 All dogs presented for acute respiratory distress; some had known exposure to Lycoperdon (ie, “Puffball mushroom”)2,3,5,7 and one was exposed to Gaestrum triplex (ie, “Earthstar mushroom”) spores.6 Thoracic radiography identified variable and non-specific findings ranging from normal7 lung appearance to diffuse unstructured interstitial, patchy alveolar, and structured interstitial (nodular or miliary) lung patterns.2,5,6 Computed tomography (CT), used to characterize HP in people,4,8 has not yet been reported in dogs with HP. Antemortem diagnosis was achieved by identifying fungal spores in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid in 3 dogs.2,5,6 Transtracheal aspirate7 and endotracheal lavage3 did not identify spores in another 2 dogs, suggesting localization deeper in the lung. The ideal treatment for HP in dogs is unknown, which has led to arbitrary treatment for both infection and inflammation with combinations of antimicrobial,2,5–7 antifungal,6 bronchodilator,6 non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID),7 diuretic2 and corticosteroid treatments.2,5–7 Only 3/6 reported dogs with HP survived; all of them received oxygen support and glucocorticoids, among other treatments.2,6,7 Long-term follow-up to determine if sustained or progressive injury to the lung occurs using serial CT imaging or bronchoalveolar cytology in dogs with mushroom spore exposure has not been reported.

We describe development of HP secondary to lycoperdonosis in a dog diagnosed with fine-needle aspirate (FNA) of the lung and successful resolution of clinical signs using immunosuppressive doses of glucocorticoids. Unlike prior reports lacking data on long-term sequela to mushroom spore inhalation, serial monitoring up to 1 year documented resolution of clinical signs despite cytologic evidence of re-exposure and CT evidence of continued parenchymal and small airway disease consistent with chronic non-fibrotic HP.4

Case report

A 7-year-old neutered male Cocker Spaniel was presented during the spring to the University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center for 10 days of progressive lethargy, tachypnea, and cough. The dog had free unsupervised access to rural property and was noted to dig extensively. Medical history included a left thoracic limb amputation after being hit by a car at 4 months of age.

The dog was febrile and tachypneic (39.5 °C; breaths per minute not recorded) at the primary care veterinary clinic 48 hours before referral. Thoracic radiography showed a moderate diffuse unstructured interstitial lung pattern, with patchy areas of an alveolar pattern (Figure 1A, B). A CBC and serum biochemistry profile were normal. Treatment with trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole (TMS; 36 mg/kg PO q24h) was prescribed. The dog’s tachypnea progressed, prompting thoracic radiographs by the primary care veterinarian 48 hours later (Figure 1C, D). Clinical progression of tachypnea and worsening radiographic lung abnormalities led to same-day referral to the University of Missouri Veterinary Health Center.

Physical examination upon referral identified fever (39.5 °C), tachypnea (80 breaths per minute), crackles in all lung fields, and increased respiratory effort. Point-of-care ultrasonography identified infinite B-lines in all lung fields. Serology for pathogens and heartworm antigen testing were negative (SNAP 4DX Plus, IDEXX Laboratories, Inc., Westbrook, Maine, United States). FNA cytology (Figure 2) of the lung showed pyogranulomatous inflammation with intralesional fungal spores compatible with lycoperdonosis (puffball mushroom spores).

Fine needle aspirate cytology of the lung in a dog with presumptive hypersensitivity pneumonitis secondary to lycoperdonosis. Photomicrograph of a pulmonary fine-needle aspirate. Macrophages, including epithelioid forms, and non-degenerate neutrophils predominate. Occasional lymphocytes and a single mast cell are present. Multiple circular, refractile, pale green-blue structures (arrows) measuring 3-5 micrometers in diameter are found extracellularly and within macrophages. Initial (day 0) treatment included supplemental oxygen (FiO2, 40%), a single dosage of ampicillin-sulbactam (28 mg/kg IV) and maropitant (1 mg/kg IV), a famotidine constant rate infusion (CRI, 0.3 mg/kg/h), dexamethasone (0.2 mg/kg IV q24h), butorphanol (0.4 mg/kg IV q6h), saline nebulization q4h, and lactated Ringer’s solution (LRS) at 60 mL/kg/d. Upon transfer of the case to the internal medicine service 6 hours later, ampicillin-sulbactam, famotidine CRI, and maropitant were discontinued, and dexamethasone was continued because of clinical suspicion of HP secondary to lycoperdonosis.

On day 1, the dog developed worsening tachypnea (92 breaths per minute [brpm]) and hypoxemia. High-flow oxygen support (FiO2 of 100% at 5 L/minute, escalated to 7 L/minute) resulted in a SpO2 of 96% and respiratory rate of 46 breaths per minute. High-flow oxygen support was decreased daily by 1 L/min over 3 days. Nutritional support included a low-fat gastrointestinal diet supplemented with boiled chicken, but the dog remained hyporexic despite respiratory improvement. Treatment for presumptive gastrointestinal ulceration secondary to corticosteroid treatment as a contributing factor to hyporexia was initiated with sucralfate (20 mg/kg PO q8h) and pantoprazole (1 mg/kg IV q12h) on day 3.

By day 4, the dog’s appetite improved and respiratory rate normalized (44 breaths per minute) with a SpO2 of 97% on room air. On CBC, mild non-regenerative anemia (HCT, 31.0%) and a stress leukogram were present. A serum biochemistry profile was unremarkable aside from mild hypocholesterolemia. Thoracic radiography showed resolving diffuse unstructured interstitial and alveolar lung patterns (Figure 3). An echocardiographic evaluation indicated low probability of pulmonary hypertension.

Right lateral (A), left lateral (B), and ventrodorsal (C) projections are shown. Interpretation of the ventrodorsal projection is limited by rotation and motion artifacts. A linear soft tissue opacity best seen on the right lateral projection (arrowhead) could represent a thickened pleural fissure line.

By day 5, the dog maintained a normal respiratory rate (20 brpm) on room air with return of appetite. On day 6, thoracic CT was performed to more accurately gauge response to treatment by assessing the extent of remaining disease and provide a baseline for the potential development of fibrotic HP as described in people.1,4 Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed to determine if mushroom spores were still present. The dog was anesthetized and paired inspiratory:expiratory breath-hold (I:E BH) CT9 was obtained using an Engstrom Care station medical ventilator (GE Healthcare, Madison, Wisconsin, United States) and Aquilion One Vision 640 slice CT machine (Canon Medical systems Inc., Tustin, California). Findings on inspiratory CT images included diffuse ground-glass opacity (GGO) and mosaic attenuation, and signs of small airway disease on the expiratory series (accessory lung lobe), including air trapping (Figure 4). Additional findings included subpleural interstitial thickening, traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis, peribronchovascular thickening and complete opacification (more common in dependent regions but also present in other areas).

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis using a 300-cell differential count showed 64% macrophages, 27% lymphocytes, 5% neutrophils, and 4% eosinophils. Rarely, macrophages contained a single round, pale green-blue, slightly refractile puffball mushroom spore as previously observed in lung aspirate slides. The dog was discharged on day 6 on prednisone (1.5 mg/kg/d PO) and a 7-day course of sucralfate and omeprazole.

One month later, the dog’s respiratory rate and effort were normal, and cough was resolved. Thoracic radiographs indicated resolved alveolar pattern with a persistent mild diffuse unstructured interstitial lung pattern. Prednisone was decreased to 1 mg/kg/d PO. At month 2, the dog remained asymptomatic. Before to further tapering of prednisone, repeat thoracic CT and BAL were performed. The lung parenchyma was markedly improved compared to the previous CT study, with GGO now limited to the dependent portion of the right cranial lung lobe and the left lung, especially in dependent and peripheral regions, where residual areas of complete opacification remained (Figure 5). Air trapping on expiratory CT images was now absent (not shown). Along the subpleural costal margin of the left lung, volume loss with subsequent traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis was still present (Figure 6A-B). No cytologic abnormalities were observed on BAL fluid examination. Prednisone was tapered to 0.4 mg/kg/d and discontinued 1 month later. The dog remained clinically normal with no respiratory signs.

One year after initial presentation, the dog was re-evaluated to assess subclinical progression of disease, particularly changes that could be compatible with fibrosis. A CBC and serum biochemistry profile disclosed mild, non-regenerative anemia and mild hyperphosphatemia. Paired I:E-BH CT identified new parenchymal abnormalities in the right cranial and middle lung lobes with unchanged accessory lobe patterns and almost complete resolution of the left cranial lung lobe abnormalities (Figure 6C). Bronchoscopy showed diffuse mucus hypersecretion, and BAL identified an increased total nucleated cell count of 400/μl with 85% macrophages, 7% lymphocytes, 6% non-degenerate neutrophils, 2% eosinophils, and the presence of mushroom spores. The spores were suspected to represent recurrent exposure. The owners were advised to search the property for puffball mushrooms and avoid exposure. Nebulization with sterile saline and coupage was recommended 3-4 times daily to aid in mucociliary clearance of the observed airway mucus. Prednisone (0.5 mg/kg PO q24h) was restarted to treat persistent subclinical inflammation because the puffball mushrooms were not found by the owners, preventing an avoidance strategy. Eighteen months after presentation, the dog was clinically asymptomatic.

Discussion

This case report of lycoperdonosis in a dog illustrates key features of HP, including diagnostic approach and immunosuppressive treatment, and illustrates a dissociation between resolution of clinical signs and evolution of thoracic CT and cytologic abnormalities. Rapid diagnosis was achieved by FNA cytology of the lung. With timely diagnosis, early and aggressive glucocorticoid treatment successfully managed the acute immune-mediated pathology of HP.1 Unlike thoracic radiography, CT was able to identify combined small airway and parenchymal involvement acutely, and serial thoracic CT allowed investigation of potential fibrosis,9–11 as occurs in people with HP.8,12,13 Evidence of subclinical repeated exposure with identification of mushroom spores in BAL and new lesions on CT after 1 year was documented, suggesting that mushroom spore-mediated HP can be chronic. This observation contrasts with case reports in which mushroom spore exposure-induced HP in dogs presented as an acute, fulminating disease.3,5 Even among survivors,2,6,7 no reports of follow-up more than 4 months after diagnosis are available.6 Chronic sequelae of HP in dogs may reflect the initially severe lung injury leading to structural changes and persistent low-grade inflammation, re-exposure to mushroom spores triggering ongoing damage, or both.

Five cases of lycoperdonosis2,3,5–7 and 1 case of HP secondary to Gaestrum triplex6 spore inhalation have been reported. Aside from necropsy evaluation, the diagnostic approach was variable and often required general anesthesia. Transtracheal wash failed to identify spores in 2 cases,3,7 perhaps as a consequence of disease localization in the small airways and lung parenchyma. Of the 6 HP cases, 3 were diagnosed by visualization of spores on BAL fluid examination.2,5,6 In our case, FNA cytology represented a minimally invasive alternative to identify mushroom spores. In prior reports, BAL fluid cytology showed predominantly neutrophilic inflammation2,5,6 with 1 dog having a component of lymphocytic inflammation.6 In comparing cytologic results between FNA and BAL in the dog of our report, although both techniques identified spores, FNA showed predominantly pyogranulomatous inflammation whereas BAL showed substantial lymphocytosis. In people, a constellation of clinical and diagnostic features including BAL lymphocytosis increases the index of suspicion for HP,4 which helps narrow the list of differential diagnoses and excludes infection as an alternative diagnosis.13,14 Additionally, BAL lymphocytosis is a positive prognostic indicator and predicts a better response to glucocorticoid treatment in people.13 This finding is consistent with the dog of our report having an excellent clinical response, improved thoracic CT findings, and lack of BAL lymphocytosis at the 2-month evaluation. The difference in BAL inflammation among reports of dogs with HP requires further investigation of differences in immunologic responses based on single versus repeated exposure, duration of clinical signs, and concurrent conditions such as chronic bronchitis.

Radiographic pulmonary patterns described in dogs with lycoperdonosis vary and include alveolar,3 nodular to miliary,2,5 unstructured interstitial lung patterns,3,6 and unremarkable findings.7 Thoracic radiography is also non-specific, poorly sensitive,15 and may be normal in people with HP,16,17 making CT the imaging modality of choice to identify features of HP.4,14 In the dog of our report, CT helped characterize patterns, anatomic location, and distribution of disease and provided a baseline to evaluate therapeutic response.12 In people, CT findings overlap between patients with non-fibrotic and fibrotic HP disease.4 Features of non-fibrotic HP include parenchymal abnormalities of GGO or mosaic attenuation along with at least one abnormality suggestive of small airway disease.4 In contrast, CT features of fibrotic HP can include an irregular fine or coarse reticulation with architectural distortion, septal thickening, GGO associated with traction bronchiectasis or bronchiolectasis, honeycombing, and loss of lobar volume.4,12 Initial CT findings in the dog of our report included diffuse GGO with small areas of consolidation that could represent continued inflammation, regional alveolar collapse and damage, or early fibrosis. The airway involvement observed at this stage included peribronchovascular thickening and air trapping.9 It was unclear if subpleural interstitial thickening and architectural distortion with volume loss around dilated bronchi and bronchioles at the periphery of the lungs (ie, traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis) could have represented fibrosis. Although traction bronchiectasis and traction bronchiolectasis are most often associated with interstitial fibrosis, insufficient data is available in dogs to confirm that these changes definitively reflect fibrosis.9 Dramatic resolution of these changes on the CT scan performed 1 year later in the dog of our report (Figure 6) suggest they were likely the result of collapsed alveoli tethered to airway walls pulling them open and not irreversible fibrosis. As has been shown previously in a cat,18 characteristic patterns representing fibrosis in people may not be identical in veterinary species, emphasizing the need for caution in interpreting changes observed on CT scans. Recheck CT 1-year post-presentation identified resolution of previous CT abnormalities, while new lesions supportive of parenchymal and small airway involvement without clear CT signs of fibrosis were noted.

Reappearance of mushroom spores in BAL cytology likely indicated recurrent exposure with ongoing lung injury, most consistent with chronic non-fibrotic HP. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis classification in people has shifted to emphasize the presence or absence of fibrosis, because it correlates more with prognosis and treatment response than temporal onset of disease.1,4,8,12–14 Chronicity in the dog of our report likely reflects recurrent exposure with new subclinical distribution of lung injury. Thorough evaluation of the property for sources of repeat exposure was advised, because avoidance is a key component of treatment and correlates with prognosis in people with HP.1,4,8 Considering imaging and cytologic evidence of new architectural changes and subclinical inflammation, a low dose of glucocorticoids also was recommended.

Conclusion

In conclusion, HP in dogs is a rare disease secondary to inhalation of mushroom spores. Definitive diagnosis is supported by exposure to mushroom spores, clinical signs of labored respiration, imaging findings, and lymphocytic or neutrophilic inflammation with visualization of fungal spores (typically <3-5 μm).1–3 Fine needle aspirate cytology confirmed lycoperdonosis in the dog of our case report and may be considered in dogs unstable for general anesthesia. Serial thoracic CT imaging documented subgross anatomic abnormalities including evidence of parenchymal and small airway disease. Serial BAL showed puffball mushroom spores during the initial presentation with fulminant clinical signs, absence of spores at a 2-month evaluation in conjunction with resolution of clinical signs, and asymptomatic reappearance of spores 1 year later. Although, historically, prognosis has been guarded,3,5 our report suggests a role for early immunosuppression to resolve severe acute clinical signs. Long term follow up in our case showed that inhalation of mushroom spores cannot only lead to acute, severe HP, but repetitive exposure can cause chronic HP, even in absence of clinical signs.
