Phytoplankton community dynamics and their role in naphthenic acid transformation within a constructed wetland system treating oil sands process-affected water
Authors: Dilini M. Atugala, Lisa M. Gieg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bitumen extraction from oil sands surface mining in northern Alberta generates vast amounts of oil sands process-affected water (OSPW) containing naphthenic acid fraction compounds (NAFCs) that require treatment due to their persistence and toxicity. The use of constructed wetland treatment systems (CWTS) is a promising strategy for reducing NAFC concentrations in OSPW. Photosynthetic microalgae and cyanobacteria are present in CWTS, yet their contributions to this ecosystem and NAFC degradation remain understudied. We investigated the temporal dynamics of phytoplankton communities within a pilot CWTS treating OSPW to characterize these organisms and their potential contributions to NAFC transformation. Phytoplankton taxa identified were comparable to those found in natural boreal lakes, including Chlorophyta, Cryptophyta, and photosynthetic Stramenopiles/marine algae (e.g., Chrysophyceae, Xanthophyceae, and Eustigmatophyceae) and Cyanobacteria. Water chemistry, particularly pH, calcium, magnesium, sodium, temperature, electrical conductivity, and NAFC concentrations, played a key role in shaping phytoplankton communities. While magnesium and calcium supported long-term algal growth and resilience, NAFC concentrations strongly influenced early-stage community composition. As NAFC concentrations declined over time, their direct impact lessened, highlighting phytoplankton adaptability to shifting environmental conditions. Over the operational season, NAFC concentrations in the CWTS decreased by approximately 35%, accompanied by a shift in NAFC class distribution. The toxic O2 class declined, while higher-oxygenated, less toxic classes (O3, O4, O5, O6) increased, suggesting microbial and algal-mediated transformation. Several algal genera exhibited significant correlations with heteroatom composition at different time points, with Ankistrodesmus, Scotinosphaera, and Chloroidium significantly associated with NAFC biodegradation products at the end of the CWTS operating season. These findings suggest that phytoplankton may contribute to NAFC transformation, supporting their contribution to degradation of OSPW components in constructed wetlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00876-w.

Introduction

Oil sands process-affected water (OSPW) is a byproduct generated during the separation of bitumen from surface extracted oil sands [98]. This process produces large volumes of OSPW contaminated with a complex mixture of organic and inorganic pollutants, including naphthenic acid fraction components (NAFCs), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, phenols, bicarbonates, metals, and salts [49]. NAFCs, a diverse group of carboxylic acids with varying carbon chain lengths and structures, are particularly concerning due to their persistence and toxicity in aquatic environments [72]. Traditionally, naphthenic acids (NAs) have been defined as alkyl-substituted acyclic and cycloaliphatic carboxylic acids with the formula CnH2n+zO2, where n indicates the number of carbon atoms (ranging from 7 to 30), and z represents hydrogen deficiency due to ring cyclization [31]. Additionally, more complex variants such as oxy-NAs (CnH2n+zOx) or those containing sulfur (CnH2n+zSOx) or nitrogen (CnH2n+zNOx) are prevalent in OSPW [4]. NAFC concentrations in OSPW typically range from 20 to 120 mg/L [45], and these compounds have been shown to cause both acute and chronic toxicity in aquatic organisms, including fish [123], invertebrates [10], and algae [42].

As the oil sands industry expands, the increasing volume of OSPW presents a waste management challenge. Under the Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act (2002), OSPW is subject to a strict no-release policy and must be remediated as part of mandatory mine closure requirements. In line with government regulations, all fluid tailings in Canada must be ready for reclamation within 10 years of a mine’s closure [5]. This has spurred significant interest in developing strategies to reduce the levels of NAFCs and the overall toxicity of OSPW.

Traditional methods of treating OSPW, such as adsorption, advanced oxidation, coagulation/flocculation, filtration, and photodegradation are often costly, energy-intensive, and may not fully degrade toxic components like NAFCs [110, 114]. Therefore, the need for effective, resilient remediation strategies that mitigate the impacts of OSPW on aquatic ecosystems has become increasingly urgent. Among these strategies, constructed wetland treatment systems (CWTS) represent a promising solution. CWTS are engineered to replicate the physical, chemical, and biological processes of natural wetlands within more controlled environments, offering a versatile, cost-effective, and relatively low-maintenance approach for treating various types of contaminated water. These systems have been successfully employed to treat wastewater from industrial facilities, municipalities, agricultural operations, and landfill leachates [95, 139]. CWTS have demonstrated effective removal of a wide range of pollutants, including nitrogen [129], phosphorus [138], heavy metals [51], pathogens [7], antibiotics [78], and persistent organic pollutants such as petroleum hydrocarbons [24], organic solvents, pesticides [58], and pharmaceuticals [89]. The removal of contaminants in CWTS is driven by a combination of processes, including naturally occurring microbial degradation, plant-mediated transformations, sorption, and chemical reactions [4].

Phytoplankton, a polyphyletic group of microalgae, and phototrophic Cyanobacteria are integral to the functioning of wetlands. These unicellular, photosynthetic organisms contribute to aquatic food webs, drive biogeochemical cycling by fixing carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus, and provide dissolved oxygen and organic matter to ecosystems [103]. Beyond their ecological roles, phytoplankton are increasingly recognized as key components in modern wastewater treatment, including CWTS, due to their capacity for pollutant removal. They can thrive in diverse environmental conditions and efficiently remove toxins through biosorption, bioaccumulation, and biodegradation [97]. Their cell walls contain functional groups such as carboxyl, hydroxyl, and sulfate moieties, which facilitate the binding of metal ions and organic pollutants via biosorption, a process that occurs in both living and dead cells. Additionally, the surface charge and porosity of phytoplankton aid in biosorption [130]. In living cells, phytoplankton can actively transport and accumulate pollutants, such as heavy metals, phosphates, and pesticides, a process called bioaccumulation [97]. This ability, coupled with their capacity for biodegradation, positions phytoplankton as vital tools in comprehensive wastewater treatment approaches, where they can mineralize or transform pollutants into less harmful intermediates. Biodegradation occurs both extracellularly, where extracellular polymeric substances excreted by microalgae mineralize pollutants in their dissolved state and function as an external digestive system, and intracellularly, where enzymatic reactions further break down pollutants.

Studies have documented the resilience of phytoplankton strains in oil sands leases and their ability to degrade oil sands NAs and model NAs. For example, in Base Mine Lake (BML), the first full-scale end pit lake for the oil sands mining industry in Canada, the phytoplankton community at the phylum level resembled that of natural boreal lakes after several years of operation. Dominant groups included Chlorophyta, Cryptophyta, and Cyanophyta, with smaller contributions from Bacillariophyta, Ochrophyta, and Euglenophyta [39]. In 2012, a research group isolated 21 phytoplankton strains from oil sands leases near Fort McMurray. Of these, 18 strains tolerated acid-extractable organics (AEOs) at concentrations up to 100 mg/L (as assessed using electrospray ionization mass spectrometry), while Stichococcus sp. uniquely reduced AEO marker ion concentrations across all tested levels (10, 30, and 100 mg/L; [118, 147]). Furthermore, Navicula sp. demonstrated the ability to degrade a model NA, 4-methylcyclohexaneacetic acid, while Selenastrum sp. achieved limited removal of oil sands NA mixtures extracted in the laboratory [48]. Dunaliella tertiolecta was shown to biodegrade four single-ring model NAs via β-oxidation and exhibited tolerance to double-ring model NAs [113]. Similarly, Chlorella vulgaris demonstrated significant tolerance to both model NAs and oil sands NAs [14]. A mixed photosynthetic community enriched from OSPW and dominated by the algae Chlorellales and Scenedesmus was found to biodegrade the diamondoid NA adamantane-1-carboxylic acid [108].

Despite these advancements, the precise role of phytoplankton in influencing NAFC transformation and degradation within CWTS remains poorly understood. Characterizing the composition and structure of phytoplankton communities in CWTS specifically designed to treat OSPW is a critical first step toward optimizing these systems for effective mine closure. To address this knowledge gap, we assessed the phytoplankton community associated with a pilot CWTS treating OSPW. Specifically, we examined how vegetation (cattail and sedge) in shallow areas and water depth (deep vs. shallow areas) influenced these communities. Additionally, we explored the relationship between water chemistry variables and phytoplankton dynamics and postulated the role of phytoplankton in contributing to changes in the NAFC heteroatoms present in OSPW during an operational season. These results help to inform CWTS optimization and support the development of effective, bioremediation-based strategies for OSPW.

Materials and methods

Site description and sampling

The Kearl treatment wetland (KTW), built in 2013, is located at the Imperial Oil Kearl oil sands mine site near Fort McMurray in northern Alberta, Canada (57°26′00″ N, 111°8′31″ W). This system, detailed extensively in prior studies [22–24], is a pilot-scale CWTS with a free water surface flow design. It spans an area of 7600 m2 and comprises six sequential cells, arranged as three shallow cells (0.4 m deep) interspersed with three deeper cells (1.7 m deep) (Fig. 1). Shallow and deep cells are separated by overflow banks that are designed to flood during times of high water flow. The system is structured with a slight slope of 0.014% to facilitate the movement of water through all cells. The shallow cells are densely populated with a variety of plant species, primarily common cattails (Typha latifolia) and water sedge (Carex aquatilis) [23]. In contrast, the deeper pools are largely dominated by submerged vegetation, with a narrow band of emergent plants along the perimeter (covering approximately 5% of the area) where water is shallower. The rooting medium consists of a 300 mm upper layer of non-compacted peat over a 200 mm bottom layer of compacted peat, both derived from the original muskeg displaced at the site. This peat structure overlays a geosynthetic clay liner, which is covered by a non-woven geotextile for added stability [22]. The wetland is operated with a designated retention period of 14 days, with water being pumped at a nominal rate of 5 L/s. At the beginning of each operational season, the KTW is drained to maintain a water depth of less than 0.5 m in the deeper cells. This study assessed samples collected from the 2022 operating season of the KTW. Water samples were collected at seven time points: Days (D)-0, 0, 7, 14, 28, 42, and 98. The initial sampling point (Day-0, runoff water) was taken prior to the addition of OSPW to the system at the start of the season. Subsequent samples were collected once the system was filled with OSPW (D0) and then at several intervals thereafter (Day 7 = 7 days post-initial OSPW introduction, Day 14 = 14 days post-introduction, etc.). Specifically, the sampling dates were as follows: D-0 (May 18), D0 (June 18), D7 (June 25), D14 (July 2), D28 (July 16), D42 (July 30), D98 (September 24). Water samples were gathered in triplicate from three deep pools and two shallow areas of the wetland along both northern and southern transects (Fig. 1). Due to low water levels from evapotranspiration, the wetland was supplemented with fresh OSPW on July 8 (D19) and again on August 28 (D71) of the sampling campaign. Water samples were collected in 1 L Nalgene bottles, which were pre-cleaned with bleach, from each deep pool (Forebay South (FB-S), Forebay North (FB-N), Deep Pool 1 South (DP1-S), Deep Pool 1 North (DP1-N), Deep Pool 2 South (DP2-S) and Deep Pool 2 North (DP2-N)) and shallow area cells (Shallow Area 1 (SA1), Shallow Area 2 (SA2), Shallow Area 5 (SA5) and Shallow Area 6 (SA6)) (Fig. 1) and then shipped on wet ice to the University of Calgary for processing as described below.

Phytoplankton community analysis via gene amplicon sequencing

The water samples were filtered through 0.2 μm PES membrane filters prior to DNA extraction which was conducted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentrations were quantified with a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and adjusted to the appropriate concentration (ng/ul). The DNA extracts were shipped on dry ice to the Centre d’expertise et de services, Génome Québec (Montréal, Canada) for library preparation and sequencing using the Illumina MiSeq platform (v3 600 cycle kit).

Phytoplankton identification was conducted using PCR amplicon sequencing with primers targeting the V4 region of the 18S rRNA gene for eukaryotic microalgae and the V4-V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene for cyanobacteria. The V4 region of the 18S rRNA gene was amplified with primer pairs 565F (5′-CCAGCASCYGCGGTAATTCC-3′) and 948R (5′-ACTTTCGTTCTTGATYRA-3′) [131]. For cyanobacteria, the V4-V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 926R (5′-CCGYCAATTYMTTTRAGTTT-3′) [106].

Library preparation for Illumina sequencing on DNA extracts from water samples was conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, PCR reactions were made up to 25 μl by mixing 2.5 μl of 10X Buffer with 15 mM of MgCl2 (Qiagen), 1.25 μl of DMSO (Roche), 0.5 μl of 10 mM dNTP mix (NEB), 0.1 μl of 5U/μl HotStar Taq (Qiagen), 0.15 μl of each 10 μM HPLC-purified primer (Invitrogen), 19.35 μl of H2O and 1 μL of DNA. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 96 °C for 15 min; 33 cycles of 96 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min; and a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized on E-Gel 2% agarose gel (Invitrogen), using the ChemiGenius Bioimaging System (Syngene), and then purified with AMPure XP reagent (Agencourt, Beckman Coulter Life Science) according to Illumina’s protocol, with gentle pipetting as the preferred option for mixing. Dual-index barcodes were added using the Nextera XT Index kit v2 (Illumina Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Once purified with the AMPure XP reagent, indexed PCR products were quantified using the SYNERGY Mx system (BioTek Instruments) and pooled at equimolar ratio. The libraries were then normalized at 2 nM, pooled, and denatured in 0.05 N NaOH. The pool was diluted to 8 pM using HT1 buffer and were loaded on a MiSeq and sequenced for 2X300 cycles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A phiX library was used as a control and mixed with libraries at 15% level. The MiSeq Control Software (MCS) version was 2.5.0.5 and RTA version was 1.18.54. Program bcl2fastq v1.8.4 was then used to demultiplex samples and generate fastq reads.

Bioinformatics and data analyses

Bioinformatic analyses for the 18S and 16S sequence dataset obtained from water DNA extracts were performed using the DADA2 1.26.0 [19] bioinformatic pipeline. Option files for each gene analysed can be found in the Additional file 1: Tables S1 and S3. Briefly, cutadapt software was used to trim primers and Illumina adaptor sequences from all fastq files [87]. Then the truncated amplicon dataset was subjected to denoising method, which allowed for filtering, dereplication, merging paired end reads, and chimera identification. This resulted in inferences of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignment of 18S and 16S rRNA sequences was then performed using the PR2 18S rRNA database v. 5.0.1 [47] and the SILVA 138 database [112], respectively. ASVs unassigned at the domain/kingdom, supergroup and division/phylum level were filtered out. The resulting filtered ASV tables, which contained a list of ASVs detected across all samples, their taxonomic assignment, and their abundance in each sample, was used as input material to derive microbial community parameters (i.e. alpha and beta diversity, differential abundance analyses, etc., described below). For analyses focused on community composition, ASV abundances were converted to relative sequence abundances. Because rRNA gene copy number varies among taxa and PCR amplification can introduce bias [153], phytoplankton and cyanobacterial abundance is interpreted in a compositional context as relative community structure rather than absolute organismal abundance or biomass. Accordingly, downstream statistical analyses emphasize relative differences in phytoplankton and cyanobacterial community composition across sampling dates and locations.

As described in Additional file 1: Tables S2 and S3, the 18S rRNA and 16S rRNA gene datasets were filtered to remove non-microalgae and non-cyanobacterial sequences, respectively.

Microbial community analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.3) with the vegan [100] and phyloseq [94] packages. Visualizations were carried out with ggplot2 [145], corrplot [144], and EnhancedVolcano [17]. Alpha diversity of different sampling dates and locations was assessed using three different metrics: observed richness, Shannon, and Simpson [68]. Significant differences between alpha diversity measures were tested using pairwise Wilcoxon tests with False discovery rate p value corrections. Beta diversity of different sampling dates and locations was assessed using PERMANOVA test on a robust Aitchison distance matrix [68]. A pairwise PERMANOVA was used as a post-hoc test to determine which sampling dates and locations were different from another. Principal component analysis was used for visualization of the beta diversity.

Constrained analysis in the form of redundancy analysis (RDA) [18] was performed to assess the explanatory power of various water chemistry datasets (Additional file 1: Table S5 and Figure S1) on phytoplankton community composition. Phytoplankton abundances were first agglomerated at genus level, and the top 20 most abundant genera were selected for analysis. These data were transformed using the Robust Aitchison method to ensure accurate representation of compositional relationships.

Inverse RDA [18] was used to evaluate how NAFC heteroatom composition responded to phytoplankton communities aggregated at the genus level. The NAFC concentrations and heteroatom compositions used in this analysis were determined by Vander Meulen et al. [136]. The heteroatom matrix was standardized (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1), while phytoplankton community counts were Hellinger-transformed and aggregated at the genus level to reduce the number of explanatory variables, ensuring they were fewer than the response variables. The dataset was further refined by subsetting both response and explanatory variables to include only samples from Day 0 (D0) and subsequent time points. Explanatory variables were limited to genera identified as differentially abundant (DA) through DESeq2 analysis at the genus rank, with a log2FoldChange > 2 and adjusted p-values < 0.0001. Parsimonious inverse RDA models were constructed by selecting explanatory genera based on visual inspection of correlation matrices and forward selection using the ordistep function. Results were visualized using RDA ordination plots, providing insights into the relationships between NAFC heteroatom composition and phytoplankton communities.

Results

Phytoplankton community at division/phylum level in KTW

Community composition, based on 18S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, identified a total of 7078 ASVs classified into 9 supergroups, 23 divisions/phyla, 38 subdivisions/subphyla, 110 classes, 206 orders, 329 families, and 684 genera. Among these, microalgae divisions such as Cryptophyta (0–97.8%), Chlorophyta (0.2–99.4%), photosynthetic Stramenopiles (0–12.6%) as well as non-microalgal divisions like Opisthokonta (1.1–74.1%) and Alveolata (0.5–35%) were frequently detected, each contributing a relative abundance of greater than 1% (Figure S2). However, microalgae constituted only 31% of the total 18S eukaryotic community. After filtering out non-microalgal ASVs, 2180 ASVs remained, which were classified into 4 supergroups, 9 divisions, 14 subdivisions, 37 classes, 77 orders, 108 families, and 252 genera. Similarly, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing revealed a total of 21,279 ASVs, spanning 66 phyla. Of these, Cyanobacteria, although a relatively minor component (5.8%), comprised 1241 ASVs and showed relative abundance ranging from 0.3% to 59.2% across the wetland (Figure S3).

In the microalgae community, the occurrence frequency of major taxonomic divisions was first examined to identify the most consistently detected groups across samples. Chlorophyta emerged as the most frequently detected division, accounting for 45.8% relative abundance of the total community sequenced, followed by photosynthetic Stramenopiles (31.1%) and Cryptophyta (12.6%) (Table S1). While Chlorophyta and photosynthetic Stramenopiles were the most consistently detected divisions in the KTW ecosystem, Cryptophyta played a significant role in enhancing the diversity and functional complexity of the microalgae community. The remaining 10.5% of the community was composed of taxa affiliated with divisions such as Streptophyta, Rhodophyta, Haptophyta, Glaucophyta, Rhodelphidia, and photosynthetic Alveolata (Dinoflagellata), further contributing to the diversity of the microalgae assemblage (Table S1).

Temporal and spatial dynamics of the phytoplankton community composition in KTW

Shifts in microalgae dominance across taxonomic levels

Division level

At the division level, Chlorophyta exhibited fluctuations in relative abundance over time across both the shallow and deep areas of the wetland. On D-0, before the system was filled with OSPW, Chlorophyta were relatively more abundant in the deep areas. However, throughout the 2022 operational season, their relative abundance was consistently higher in the shallow areas (Fig. S4). Notably, a comparatively high relative abundance of Chlorophyta was observed in all compartments, except SA1 (Fig. 2). The presence of Chlorophyta was moderate at D0 across all compartments. From D0 to D28, their relative abundance gradually increased, peaking at D28 in all compartments, suggesting their growth during this period. However, by D42, their relative abundance began to decline, and the composition of Chlorophyta resembled that at D14, suggesting a stabilization in their relative abundance. Cryptophyta also exhibited notable temporal changes in relative abundance across the compartments, with their patterns mirroring those of Chlorophyta throughout the KTW operating season. They were particularly more abundant in the deep areas, except at D-0 (Fig. S4). From D0 to D14, Cryptophyta maintained a consistently high relative abundance. However, by D28, their relative abundance suddenly decreased. At D42, an increase was again observed, like the levels seen at D7 and D14. By D98, the relative abundance of Cryptophyta had drastically increased across all compartments, overtaking the entire algal community, indicating a dominant presence (Fig. 2).

Photosynthetic Stramenopiles showed clear changes in relative abundance over time, with their highest relative abundance observed in shallow areas during mid-season (Fig. S4). Initially, their presence was noticeable in several compartments, particularly in deep pools (FBs and DPs) and SA1 at D0. Over time, their relative abundance fluctuated, with a notable increase observed at D14 and D28, especially in SA5 and SA6. By D42, their presence had stabilized, and by D98, their relative abundance decreased in most compartments, except for SA5 and SA6, where they continued to maintain a substantial presence (Fig. 2).

Genus level

At the genus level, temporal changes in relative abundances of microalgae were observed across the wetland compartments. Cryptomonas (Cryptophyta) exhibited a clear decline in relative abundance from D0 to D28, while maintaining stability between D7 and D14, followed by a surge by D42; this shift was particularly noticeable in the DP1-N compartment. Their relative abundance then experienced a sudden increase, becoming highly abundant towards the end of the season. The second highest most relative abundant cryptophyte, Komma, was more common at D42 and in deep pools (FB-S, DP2-S, DP2-N) at D14 and D28. The relative abundance trends of Choricystis, (Chlorophyta) mirrored those of Cryptomonas. Choricystis increased from D0 to D7, declined slightly at D14, surged at D28, and a subsequently decreased towards the end of the season. These trends suggest a possible linear pattern that could be further clarified with additional time points. The combined relative abundance of Chlamydomonas (Chlorophyta), uncultured Chlamydomonadales, and unclassified Chlamydomonadales exhibited a more pronounced temporal trend. These taxa were particularly abundant from D0 to D42, peaking at D42, especially in FB-N, FB-S, DP2-N, DP2-S, SA5 and SA6. However, by D98, their abundances dramatically diminished across most compartments, except in SA6. Notably, Chlamydomonadales were of high relative abundance at D-0, particularly in both FB compartments and SA5 and SA6, where they accounted for 70–95% of the relative abundance of the entire microalgae community (Fig. 3).

The relative abundance of Chlorococcum (Chlorophyta) increased gradually from D0, peaking around D42, before experiencing a marked decline by D98 although, it remained detectable in SA6 until the end of the operating season. Chlorochytrium (Chlorophyta) demonstrated a gradual increase in relative abundance from D0 to D14 and fluctuated for the remaining time points. It remained consistently detectable across all compartments from D0 to D42 and became particularly abundant in shallow areas by D98. Mychonastes (Chlorophyta) exhibited an initial surge in relative abundance, peaked at D7, persisted until D42, and then experienced a pronounced decline in relative abundance by D98 (Fig. 3).

The relative abundance of Coelastrum (Chlorophyta) increased up to D42, especially in shallow areas, but was virtually absent in all compartments except SA5 and SA6 by D98. Scenedesmus (Chlorophyta) showed a gradual increased from D0, with relatively higher abundance in shallow areas by D42, but it declined by D98, remaining detectable in SA5. Similarly, Oophila (Chlorophyta) increased from D0 to D14, reaching its highest relative abundance in SA5 and SA6, then decreased by D28, maintaining a stable presence until D98, with relatively higher abundance observed in SA6 (Fig. 3).

Actinastrum (Chlorophyta) was detectable in all compartments from D0 up to D7 but declined in relative abundance by D14 and remained relatively stable till D28. By D42, it was only observed in SA1 and SA5 and was no longer detectable by D98. Paulschulzia (Chlorophyta) increased from D0 to D14, reaching high relative abundance in DP2 at D14, then decreased by D28, and stabilized through D42 and D98 in SA5 and SA6 (Fig. 3).

Other genera within Chlorophyta, including Chlorodium, Oocystis, Oocystella, Chlorogonium, unclassified Chlorellales, and unclassified Trebouixiphyceae were relatively more abundant in the early stages of the operating season but gradually diminished in relative abundance at later time points. A total of 66 other Chlorophyta genera, each contributing less than 1% relative abundance, were consistently detected from D0 to D42, but their presence drastically diminished by D98, except in compartments SA5 and SA6 (Fig. 3).

Tribonema, the most abundant genus in the division photosynthetic Stramenopiles, appeared at D14, increased by D28, and declined by D42. It was relatively abundant in SA5 and SA6 during this period, but by D98, it was only detectable in SA6. Other photosynthetic Stramenopiles, composed of 100 genera, each contributing less than 1% relative abundance, showed fluctuating trends from D0 to D98, with relatively higher abundances in SA5 and SA6 at later time points (Fig. 3). The persistence of these organisms in shallow areas was likely driven by favorable nutrient conditions, enhanced by the presence of plants and increased light availability in these zones.

Cyanobium PCC-6307 was relatively abundant in both deep and shallow areas, particularly at the early time points (D0 to D28); subsequently its relative abundance gradually decreased from D14 to D98 (Fig. 4). Depth-dependent variations in phytoplankton divisions over time

Deep and shallow areas of the KTW revealed notable differences in the relative abundances of phytoplankton taxa. Shallow areas consistently exhibited higher relative abundances of Chlorophyta across all time points, except at D28, where their levels were nearly equal. Photosynthetic Stramenopiles had higher relative abundance in shallow areas at all time points, except at D0. In contrast, deep areas consistently showed higher relative abundance of Cryptophyta throughout the study period. Members of the Cyanobacteria were of comparatively high abundance in deep areas during the early stages up to D28 but became relatively dominant in shallow areas at later time points (Figs. S3 and S5).

Alpha diversity

The alpha diversity significantly fluctuated in the KTW over the course of the operational season as revealed by both 18S and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Microalgae species diversity gradually increased from D-0 to D14, followed by a slight reduction at D28, and peaking at D42, and then a significantly declining by D98. D14 and D42 were the time points with the highest species diversity, while D98 showed the lowest diversity across all indices (Fig. S6A). Similarly, the cyanobacterial community demonstrated an increase in diversity and evenness from the start until D42, when diversity indices reached their highest values. However, a sharp decline was observed at D28, followed by a slight decrease after D42, culminating in lower diversity indices by D98 (Fig. S6C).

In terms of location, some compartments significantly contributed to the alpha diversity of microalgae community in the wetland water. Based on both Shannon and Simpson diversity indices, SA6, FB-N, SA5 were ranked as the top three locations with the highest species diversity (Table S2). Among these, SA6 stood out as the most diverse sampling location (Fig. S6B). DP2-S and DP2-N displayed mid-level diversity across both indices, while FB-S, SA1, SA2, DP1-S, and DP1-N were ranked lower in diversity (Table S2). For the cyanobacterial community, the analysis highlighted that the shallow areas of the KTW, particularly SA6 and SA5, consistently exhibited the highest diversity and evenness. In contrast, the deep pools, especially DP1-S and DP1-N, showed lower diversity and evenness, with SA1, SA2, FB-S, and FB-N exhibiting intermediate values (Fig. S6D).

Discussion

Phytoplankton diversity and the influence of time and location

The observed increase in alpha diversity at D0 compared to D-0 (runoff water) (Fig. S6A) likely reflects the introduction of OSPW, which brought a complex chemical matrix, enriched with nutrients, metals, hydrocarbons, and NAFC to the existing algal taxa in the constructed wetland. The transition to this new chemical environment fostered community changes. The influx of OSPW may have also activated dormant taxa and expanded ecological niches, while changes in water chemistry, such as increased pH, conductivity, and ion concentrations, further promoted diversity.

Sampling time (e.g., time of exposure to OSPW) exerted the strongest influence on the phytoplankton community structure in the KTW. The gradual increase in alpha diversity from D0 (June 18) to D42 (July 30) with a decline observed at D28 (July 16) and further at D98 (September 24), reflects dynamic shifts in both algal and cyanobacterial communities. We postulate that these trends were driven by the reduction in NAFC concentrations [136], and variations of environmental factors, including nutrient availability, light intensity, water chemistry, and ecological interactions like competition and predation [76, 151]. Peaks in diversity at D14 and D42 suggested periods of ecological stability during late spring and summer, when favorable environmental conditions such as elevated temperature due to the onset of summer and optimal water chemistry including electrical conductivity, presence of magnesium, sulfate and bicarbonate (Fig. 7C and E, Table S9C and E) supported a diverse range of species [11]. In contrast, the decline in diversity by D98, coinciding with the onset of fall, may indicate environmental stress, resource depletion, or intensified competition, resulting in reduced species richness and evenness [44, 70, 124]. The addition of fresh OSPW to the KTW on Day 19 of its operation likely contributed to the reduction in phytoplankton community diversity observed by D28. Resulting changes in water chemistry, including increased NAFC levels, altered nutrient levels, and the presence of metals and hydrocarbons likely disrupted phytoplankton community balance.

Both alpha and beta diversity varied significantly across locations, with SA6 and SA5 exhibiting the highest species diversity and being significantly different from all other wetland compartments, including deeper pools and forebays. These shallow cells are densely vegetated with common cattails (Typha latifolia) and water sedge (Carex aquatilis), likely enhancing habitat complexity, nutrient cycling, and oxygen availability [86, 119]. Differences in light exposure, water flow, and nutrient distribution between shallow and deep zones further shaped community composition [8, 83], with shallow areas offering niches that support higher species richness and evenness. Conversely, deeper pools such as DP1-S and DP1-N exhibited lower diversity, likely due to reduced light penetration and fluctuating oxygen levels. The overlap in community structure between other locations suggests relatively limited spatial heterogeneity in phytoplankton diversity within the wetland, which aligns with previous research indicating that microbial communities in constructed wetlands can exhibit both localized and broader landscape-scale diversity patterns [95].

Phytoplankton community composition in KTW

At the division level, the phytoplankton community was predominantly composed of Chlorophyta, Cryptophyta, and photosynthetic Stramenopiles/marine algae (e.g., Chrysophyceae, Xanthophyceae, and Eustigmatophyceae) and Cyanobacteria. This phytoplankton composition is characteristic of natural boreal lakes or similar freshwater environments in Canada [99, 109] as well as northern lakes in Sweden and Finland [104] Russia, Greenland and Alaska [33]. A study of 75 boreal and temperate lakes and reservoirs in Alberta similarly found comparable phytoplankton communities in both natural and constructed aquatic systems [80], suggesting that local phytoplankton populations establish rapidly within CWTS, demonstrating their resilience and adaptability to these environments. Interestingly, a similar community composition was observed in Base Mine Lake, Canada’s first full-scale end pit lake designed for oil sands reclamation [39]. The groups of phytoplankton found in the KTW were also identified as the most tolerant groups of phytoplankton in studies of Athabasca oil sands contaminated water in Alberta [111]. In contrast, tropical aquatic environments are typically dominated by diatoms (Bacillariophyceae) from photosynthetic Stramenopiles, Chlorophyta and Cyanobacteria, but cryptophytes are generally absent or present in lower abundance [60, 62]. Notably, cryptophytes have been reported in extreme environments such as the Rocky Mountains and Antarctica [21], where they persist under harsh conditions, surviving winter beneath ice cover with minimal solar radiation for photosynthesis.

In the KTW, Chrysophyceae exhibited moderately lower relative abundances (0–7.7%), while Bacillariophyceae (photosynthetic Stramenopiles), Dinoflagellata (photosynthetic Alveolata), and Euglenophyceae (Discoba) were also relatively scarce (less than 1%) (Figs. S4 and S12). A comparable trend was noted in BML [39]. These groups are typically more abundant and stable in natural boreal lakes in Canada [99], indicating that their low relative abundance in the KTW may have been constrained by suboptimal water quality conditions. Chrysophyceae are commonly associated with oligotrophic to mesotrophic waters with low salinity (conductivity < 40 µs cm−1) and acidic pH (< 7.0) [125]. Indeed, the prevailing water quality in the KTW, with an average conductivity of 1500 µs cm−1 and pH above 7, appears unsuitable for their optimal growth and proliferation. Additionally, Chrysophyceae are recognized for their sensitivity to environmental stressors [125] and are particularly vulnerable to oil contamination [16, 27, 37, 69] and elevated turbidity [16]. Similarly, Bacillariophyceae (diatoms) are known for their susceptibility to oil contamination [37, 50, 127]. However, studies have demonstrated that both Chrysophyceae and Bacillariophyceae can persist in challenging environments. For instance, Chrysophyceae have been reported in marine oil-contaminated mesocosms [125], oil-polluted peat bogs [16], and microcosms treated with NAs [50]. Paniagua-Michel and Banat [105] highlighted the role of diatoms in bioremediation of different types of petroleum hydrocarbons and their tolerance to oil-contaminated environments. Several pollution-tolerant diatom species, such as Cyclotella [28], Nitzschia [29, 48], and Amphora [74, 105], were detected in KTW, albeit in minimal abundance. Dinoflagellates exhibit diverse responses to oil contamination. Some studies highlight their sensitivity to oil exposure [37], while others document their ability to tolerate and persist under oil-affected conditions [43, 102]. Euglenophyceae, which typically flourish in warm, nutrient-rich, slightly acidic waters and can form dense blooms [132], were also scarce, presumably due to the KTW’s cooler temperatures, low nutrient availability, slightly alkaline pH, and high turbidity. Collectively, these conditions appear to have restricted the growth of sensitive algal groups, explaining their low relative abundance in the KTW.The major algal genera in KTW included Cryptomonas and Choricystis, with other abundant and enriched genera (Fig. 3, Figs. S10 and S11) discussed below. Cryptomonas and Choricystis, frequently observed in boreal lakes [35, 82, 109, 128] were also identified as major genera in BML [39] and have been detected in other oil-impacted environments [48, 118]. Different species of Chlamydomonas, Scenedesmus, and Mychonastes were identified as indicator species in BML compared to an associated freshwater lake, Beaver Creek Reservoir [39]. Chlamydomonas spp. and Oocystis spp. have been identified as tolerant taxa in oilsands tailings ponds, containing moderate naphthenate levels (8–21 mg/L) [85, 111]. Scenedesmus has emerged as a highly resilient genus across various studies and conditions. It has demonstrated survival and adaptation in environments with high NA extract concentrations (10–100 mg/L) from fresh OSPW, showing the ability to modify acid extractable organics [118]. Furthermore, Scenedesmus can degrade Deepwater Horizon crude oil [26] and enhance the degradation of aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbons, particularly high molecular weight polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, when paired with oil-degrading bacteria [93]. Scenedesmus was also identified as a highly tolerant, co-flocculating microalga capable of remediating harsh rare earth element mine wastewater [150] and has demonstrated near-complete removal of zinc and copper [152]. Scenedesmus and Chlamydomonas sp. were abundant in crude oil-contaminated sites in Río Negro, Argentina [81]. McCauley [92] reported that oil toxicity severely impacted plankton and sediment macrofauna, but Ankistrodesmus, Chlamydomonas, Closterium, and Scenedesmus persisted in a petroleum-polluted muddy river. Chlorococcum sp, Tetracystis sp., Chlamydomonas sp., were identified as the most abundant species in gold mine tailings storage facilities in the Northwest province in South Africa [102]. Tribonema sp., a filamentous microalga, effectively purifies petrochemical wastewater with low organic carbon content, such as secondary-treated effluent [56, 77]. Further, species of Ankistrodesmus, Scenedesmus and Chlamydomonas degrade petroleum hydrocarbons such as benzo(a)pyrene [143] and phenanthrene [73]. Scenedesmus, Chlamydomonas, Coelastrum, Mychonastes, Chlorogonium, Tribonema and unclassified Trebouxiophyceae were recorded as pollutant tolerant genera present in heavily wastewater polluted waterbodies in India [60].

Based on relative abundance (and given the caveat of 18S amplicon sequencing biases and gene copy numbers, [153]), the most abundant cyanobacterial genera identified in the KTW include Cyanobium PCC-6307, unclassified Chloroplast, Phormidium IAM M-71, Planktothrix NIVA-CYA 15, Lyngbya PCC-7419, and Synechocystis PCC-6803. Members of these genera have also been isolated from freshwater reservoirs [20, 96, 117, 128], suggesting that these cyanobacteria are particularly suited to bitumen-impacted habitats. Notably, Cyanobium/Synechococcus and Planktothrix were recognized as characteristic taxa in BML [39] with Cyanobium PCC-6307 further identified as a dominant genus in demonstration end pit lakes also designed for tailing ponds reclamation [15]. Phormidium sp. has been extensively studied for its potential in wastewater treatment [2] and can consume various toxic pollutants, including heavy metals, in mixed industrial effluent containing hazardous substances from multiple industries [65, 141]. It has also been shown to effectively reduce the toxicity of pharmaceutical wastewater, including laboratory and production waste [126], remove pollutants from tannery wastewater [34], treat toxic compounds and dyes in textile industry wastewater [91], and manage swine wastewater [25]. Moreover, it is among the most abundant genera found in gold mine tailings storage facilities [101]. Further, Phormidium, Planktothrix and Synechocystis species were isolated from a wastewater treatment plant located in the north of Portugal [88]. Phormidium sp., also predominant in microbial mats on crude oil polluting the Arabian Gulf coasts, contribute to oil degradation [6]. Cyanobacterial mats, including Synechocystis sp., have demonstrated the ability to thrive in oil-containing microcosms, where they engage in potential metabolic cooperation and contribute to hydrocarbon transformations [75]. Another study revealed efficient degradation of the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon pyrene by Synechocystis sp. in the presence of constructed artificial bacterial consortium [107]. These cyanobacteria are also capable of tolerating and degrading various pesticides and herbicides [121] and have heavy metal removal capability [67]. Lyngbya was identified as a dominant species in treatment of domestic wastewater generated from the village of Sanghol, India [66] and a waterbody heavily polluted with petroleum oils [92].

Several studies have examined phytoplankton communities in simulated constructed wetlands (CW) or mesocosms, highlighting their important role in contaminant removal. In greenhouse-scale mesocosms treating OSPW, the dominant microalgal classes included Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae, with genera such as Mychonastes and Chlorococcum [135], showing a community composition comparable to that observed in KTW (Figs. S12 and 3). Although Chlorella species have been frequently used in CW mesocosm studies for wastewater remediation [9, 71, 140], they were detected at very low relative abundance (< 1%) in KTW and thus excluded from further analyses. Conversely, Scenedesmus species also present in KTW have been tested in simulated CWs for their ability to remove selenium from river water through volatilization and accumulation [55] and for their efficiency in nitrogen and phosphorus removal via algal assimilation and ammonia volatilization. Collectively, these findings underscore the role of microalgae in nutrient and contaminant removal in CW systems. Furthermore, Silveira et al. [120] demonstrated that integrating microalgae (predominantly Chlorophyceae), anaerobic reactors, and constructed wetlands can simultaneously achieve wastewater treatment, nutrient recovery, and renewable energy generation, emphasizing a scalable and environmentally sustainable treatment approach. Importantly, Cyanobacteria are also prevalent in CWs treating wastewater [84] and in conventional wastewater treatment systems [2], and their combined activity with microalgae may further enhance nutrient and contaminant removal. This suggests that integrative microalgal–cyanobacterial processes could potentially improve OSPW bioremediation efficiency in northern CW such as KTW.

The most prevalent phytoplankton taxa identified in the KTW in this study are recognized as significant contributors to food webs in various ecosystems. Cryptophytes, such as Cryptomonas, are widely regarded as high-quality nutritional sources for zooplankton [12, 40], ciliates [38] and bacteria due to their rich nutrient content. Similarly, chlorophytes like Choricystis and Scenedesmus are consumed by ciliates, heterotrophic or mixotrophic nanoflagellates [20] and herbivorous zooplankton because of their high lipid content [59]. Diatoms and cyanobacteria such as Lyngbya spp. and Planktothrix are also major food sources for zooplankton [148]. However, some species of cyanobacteria pose potential toxicity risks, while contributing to ecological functions within the KTW system. For example, Planktothrix supports freshwater food webs but is difficult for zooplankton and protists to consume due to its filamentous structure and cyanotoxin production. Additionally, Synechocystis PCC-6803 exhibits RNase activity, suggesting roles in RNA degradation and toxin-related transcriptome changes, which may influence community dynamics and trophic interactions [61].

Overall, the KTW hosts a boreal, pollution-tolerant phytoplankton community adapted to OSPW-impacted environments, with both structural similarity and functional divergence from natural lakes and constructed wetlands in other regions.

Phytoplankton seasonality

Many genera in the KTW experienced temporal fluctuations, with periodic increases and decreases at various time points. Cryptomonas reached its peak in early autumn, consistent with observations from BML [39] and freshwater reservoirs [13, 115, 146]. Their preference for the deeper, colder regions of boreal lakes, characterized by low light conditions and relatively high availability of dissolved nutrients, has been described by Gervais [41] and Becker et al. [13]. The high availability of nutrients in KTW may be influenced by the dying-off of plants at the end of the operational season. Choricystis, on the other hand, peaked in abundance during the spring and early summer months (May to July) in the KTW, aligning with findings from other studies [39, 52, 53]. Additionally, genera belonging to chlorophyte classes, such as Chlorophyceae and Trebouxiophyceae, and photosynthetic stramenopiles like Chrysophyceae and Xanthophyceae, exhibited peak relative abundances during the warmer months in KTW, consistent with findings from previous research [39, 142]. Cyanobacterial blooms, typically associated with water column stratification [137], elevated nutrient levels, and higher water temperatures [32], are most often observed in late summer or early autumn in Canadian freshwater systems [32, 64, 79, 80]. However, in the KTW, their abundance was notably higher during spring and early summer, contrasting with the established patterns reported for Canadian freshwater ecosystems. This discrepancy could be attributed to various factors, with one possible explanation being the increased grazing pressure by Ciliophora and Crustacea [63] in late summer (Fig. S13A and B).

Influence of water chemistry on phytoplankton composition

Water chemistry variables, including pH, calcium, magnesium, sodium, temperature, electrical conductivity, and NAFC concentrations significantly influenced algal community structure in the KTW. Their influence evolved over time, reflecting both short-term responses to contaminant addition and long-term adjustments to changing environmental conditions. Long term drivers such as magnesium and calcium concentrations significantly influenced phytoplankton composition from D0 to D98, supporting algal growth and resilience through their roles in cellular processes and ion balance, aligning with previous studies on the importance of these ions in freshwater systems [122, 133, 134]. NAFC concentrations strongly correlated with the community structure at D0 and continued to influence community structure at D7 and D28, highlighting the role of NAFC in shaping early-stage algal composition. Genera such as Tetracystis, Chlorochytrium, Chlorococcum, unclassified Chlamydomonadales, Actinastrum and Tribonema were positively correlated with NAFC, suggesting these algae may possess mechanisms to tolerate or degrade/utilize NAFC as carbon or energy sources [149]. However, this hypothesis requires further testing. Further, the reduction in NAFC concentrations over time appeared to lessen its direct influence on algal composition by D98. Such adaptability likely conferred a competitive advantage in NAFC-contaminated environments.

In the early stages (D0, D7, and D14) the CWTS operation, pH emerged as a significant driver of community structure (Figs. 6A, B, S8A and Table S9A, B, C), consistent with its role in regulating algal metabolism and nutrient availability [1]. Short-term responses were also evident with sodium and electrical conductivity, which significantly influenced community structure at D0, D28, and D42. These variables may reflect immediate ionic changes from OSPW additions, shaping phytoplankton responses to fluctuating salinity and ion concentrations. By contrast, temperature, a key driver at D14 and D42, likely regulated metabolic activity and favored thermally tolerant genera, as seen with Oophila and Tribonema. These observations align with research emphasizing the role of temperature in structuring algal communities under changing thermal conditions [3, 57].

Phytoplankton contributions to NAFC transformation

By D98, NAFC concentrations in KTW decreased by 35% compared to D0, accompanied by a shift in NAFC class distribution. The toxic O2 class declined, while higher-oxygenated, less toxic classes (O3, O4, O5, and O6) increased, likely due to microbial biodegradation, photodegradation and plant-microbial associated mechanisms [116]. The persistent presence of the toxic O2 class at D0, and its absence at later time points suggests that degradation processes began after D0.

None of the genera identified as positively correlated with heteroatoms have been directly recognized in the literature as NAFC degraders. However, some have been reported to degrade petroleum hydrocarbons [73, 143] or to be present in wastewater treatment systems [30, 36, 54] and tailings storage facilities [101].

Despite the lack of direct evidence, the observed positive correlation between these algal genera and higher-oxygenated NAFC heteroatom classes suggests a possible role for these algae in the transformation or modification of NAFCs. This relationship may reflect algal-mediated processes, wherein algae could contribute to the oxidation of less oxygenated NAFCs, potentially leading to an increase in higher-oxygenated forms. Algal photosynthesis may also enhance oxidative conditions by increasing oxygen availability, which could facilitate heterotrophic bacterial degradation pathways [90, 96].

Additionally, the observed correlation could reflect an adaptive response, where these algal taxa tend to persist in environments with more oxygenated NAFCs, potentially due to tolerance mechanisms or the ability to utilize these compounds as a carbon source. These patterns suggest that these algal genera may play a role in the natural attenuation of NAFC contamination in OSPW. Furthermore, the strong positive correlation between some algal genera and the N3OS and N2O3 classes indicates a potential association with nitrogen and sulfur cycling, which are processes likely involved in the transformation of complex NAFCs [46]. Future research examining the NAFC biodegradation abilities of various algal taxa, their role in N and S cycling, and algal–bacterial interactions is warranted to test such hypotheses.

Conclusion

This study highlights the strong temporal influence on the phytoplankton community dynamics within the KTW. Shallow areas (SA6 and SA5) exhibited significantly higher species diversity than deep pools and other shallow regions (SA1 and SA2). The phytoplankton taxa identified in KTW are comparable to those found in natural boreal lakes, suggesting their potential role in the local food web. Their presence in previously studied oil-impacted environments also indicates a possible association with OSPW-impacted systems.

Water chemistry played a key role in shaping phytoplankton composition, with long-term drivers such as magnesium and calcium supporting algal growth and resilience, while NAFCs significantly influenced early-stage community structure. Over time, the reduction in NAFC concentrations lessened their direct impact, highlighting phytoplankton adaptability to shifting environmental conditions.

Phytoplankton may play a role in NAFC attenuation in OSPW through oxygenation and biodegradation processes. Their correlation with higher-oxygenated NAFCs and potential involvement in nitrogen and sulfur cycling suggests a possible contribution to NAFC transformation, warranting further investigation into their bioremediation potential. As such, identifying the important phytoplankton in constructed wetland treatment systems allows for their targeted isolation to elucidate NA biodegradation pathways. Exploring the functional contributions of key phytoplankton groups, particularly their NA biodegradation capabilities, will help refine their application and monitoring in OSPW remediation strategies. Thus, future research should focus on continuous monitoring of phytoplankton dynamics in KTW to better understand their composition, diversity, and ecological roles.
