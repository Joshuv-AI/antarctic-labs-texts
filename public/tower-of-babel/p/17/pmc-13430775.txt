Clinicopathological and molecular determinants of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors: toward precision risk stratification
Authors: Kai Xuan Cheong, Lu-Ting Zhang, Ya-Zhou Wang, Wei Gan, Chen-Chen Liu, Ying Ying, Mao-Zhen Peng, Lin-Hui Tang, Liang Liu, Wen-Quan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs) are clinically heterogeneous, and conventional risk stratification based on tumor size, grade, and stage often fails to capture the full spectrum of biological behavior, especially among small (≤ 2 cm), non-functioning tumors where management remains controversial.Main bodyMolecular profiling has revealed that the most frequently altered gene, MEN1, carries prognostic information beyond its mutation status. MEN1‑mutant tumors with recurrent loss of heterozygosity across multiple chromosomes follow an aggressive course, whereas those with isolated chromosome 11 loss remain indolent. Besides, the increasing multi-omic profiling has shown that DNA methylation and transcriptomic classification powerfully refine prognosis beyond traditional staging. Different subtypes of MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX co-mutated tumors exhibiting ALT-driven chromosomal instability and a high risk of recurrence even in sub-2 cm low-grade lesions. Metastatic disease the same genotype is associated with remarkably prolonged survival and superior response to PRRT. These studies provide a systems-level view of tumor biology in PanNETs and could aid in classifying distinct subtypes. Integrating these molecular layers with conventional clinicopathological parameters offers a refined framework for risk-adapted management. Hence, this review discusses the latest research findings that build upon the traditional classification protocols and examines the prognosis value of traditional and novel molecular targets. Furthermore, it mainly emphasizes the urgent need to incorporate not just only tumor size, stage and grade but also molecular characteristics as they offer valuable insights into tumor heterogeneity and is recognized as a critical aspect in risk stratification and guiding therapy. Perspectives and actionable suggestions have been addressed for the development of more accurate and reliable classification systems and predictive models in the future. In this context, this review has proposed an estimated risk stratification by integrating clinicopathological-molecular findings and has suggested risk-adapted management, which requires further validation.ConclusionOverall, integrating molecular features, such as MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX mutation patterns, ALT status, and multi-omic classifier with traditional clinicopathological parameters is essential to resolve the biological heterogeneity of PanNETs and to enable precise, risk-adapted clinical decision-making.Keywords: Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, Clinical classification, Molecular classification, Subtype, Multi-omics

Introduction

PanNETs is the second most common pancreatic tumor after pancreatic adenocarcinoma, yet it is still considered a relatively rare subtype, accounting for no more than 3% of all pancreatic neoplasms. It displays heterogeneous features and possesses distinct biological behavior [1–3]. However, several research had suggested that the annual prevalence of this tumor has been increasing dramatically over the past few decades, although the incidence rate of PanNETs only comprised not more than 1 per 100,000 individuals [4, 5]. Over the years, the World Health Organization (WHO) grading and the TNM staging system have offered valuable perspectives into the behavior and prognosis of tumors [6, 7]. Fortunately, with recent advancements in clinical and molecular level analysis, the understanding of PanNETs has expanded and the different biological behaviors of different subtypes have been identified based on histopathological features, site of origin, and molecular profiles [3, 8–11]. Such breakthroughs are crucial in enhancing diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and therapeutic decision-making. While several therapeutic strategies have been incorporated for PanNET management, which includes the use of somatostatin analogs (SSAs), peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), everolimus, sunitinib, and alkylating-based chemotherapy [12], they are commonly prescribed based on standardized criteria instead of applying according to specific tumor characteristics. Hence, the increasing prevalence of PanNETs has raised significant clinical concerns recently due to insufficient understanding on the underlying drivers leading to its complexity and a lack of well-established stratification system that address all crucial aspects to better guide treatment and prognosis prediction despite paramount research on PanNETs has progressed these years. For example, patients with small size or low grade PanNETs potentially exhibit malignant features and cause poor prognosis has raised concerns in the clinical field [13]. The question of whether surgical intervention is necessary for these smaller tumors, especially in patients with either indolent or aggressive disease remains controversial. As a result, clinicals may encounter difficulties and struggles in providing optimal care to patients, further highlights the critical need for a personalized treatment strategy by developing a more precise classification protocol. Therefore, in this review, we discuss the recent discoveries on the clinicopathological and molecular aspects of PanNETs, including several novel molecular targets and the chromosomal instability events within traditional genetic alterations. We aim to provide insights into their clinical implications, and prognosis value, which could guide the integration of molecular markers into clinical frameworks for the sake of revising current guidelines and improving patient outcomes by avoiding undertreated or overtreated patients.

Recent advances in clinicopathological factors

Functioning vs. non-functioning PanNETs

It is widely recognized that PanNETs are classified into functioning (F-PanNETs) and non-functioning tumors (NF-PanNETs), with NF-PanNETs representing 60–90% of PanNETs [14], yet this distinction may obscure more biological reality than it reveals. F-PanNETs typically secrete hormones or bioactive substances, leading to clinical symptoms and may also contribute to carcinoid syndrome. F-PanNETs encompass insulinomas (30–40%), gastrinomas (16–30%), glucagonomas (< 10%), VIPomas (< 10%), and somatostatinomas (< 5%), each defined by distinct hormonal hypersecretion syndromes [15]. In contrast, NF-PanNETs, which are often diagnosed at a later stage, do not produce excessive hormones like F-PanNETs. Therefore, this characteristic often leads to a higher likelihood of late diagnosis, typically only after the manifestation of clinical symptoms arising from mass effect with local invasion or distant metastases, with the liver being the most common site of metastasis [16].

Previous studies have demonstrated a better prognosis and treatment outcomes in the mostly benign F-PanNETs compared to NF-PanNETs [17–20]. The stark contrast in recurrence rates after R0 resection, specifically 79% for NF-PanNETs versus 6.9% for insulinomas does not merely quantify a difference, it suggests a fundamental biological chasm [17]. One plausible interpretation is that insulinomas are rarely intrinsically indolent; rather, their hormonal leads to early detection, truncating their natural history before metastasis can occur. This gains traction when examining metastatic insulinoma, which shares clinicopathological features and a poor prognosis with NF-PanNETs, potentially worse than other non-metastatic F-PanNETs [18, 21], where it warrants a re-evaluation of the assumption that F-PanNETs are inherently less aggressive. Specifically, this pattern suggests that the favorable prognosis typically attributed to insulinoma is not an intrinsic biological property of the tumor, but rather a consequence of early clinical detection driven by hypoglycemic symptoms. When this detection advantage is eliminated, the clinical trajectory of insulinoma converges with that of NF-PanNETs. This pattern is consistent with evidence from glucagonomas, wherein tumor burden and metastatic spread, rather than the mere presence of hormonal hypersecretion, emerge as the dominant prognostic determinants [22]. However, clinical convergence in outcome does not necessarily imply biological equivalence. The observation that metastatic insulinoma and NF-PanNET share a similarly poor prognosis raises a critical but unresolved question like: does this reflect shared underlying molecular mechanisms, or do distinct oncogenic pathways converge on a similarly aggressive phenotype? It is believed that a subset of insulinomas that presents with or develops metastasis must harbor distinct molecular drivers of aggressiveness, yet current evidence cannot distinguish between these possibilities due to the largely uncharacterized molecular basis of metastatic insulinoma.

It is widely recognized that insulinomas almost exclusively harbor mutations in Yin Yang 1 (YY1), which are rarely found in non-insulinoma PanNETs [23, 24]. Nonetheless, the alteration rate of YY1 in insulinomas only represents an approximately 20% of cases, regardless of its indolent or malignant characteristics. Critically, no study has demonstrated that YY1 mutation status carries any prognostic value. Therefore, YY1 status cannot serve as a biomarker for risk stratification, nor does it explain the aggressive behavior observed in metastatic insulinoma, highlighting the necessity of alternative molecular driver search for prognostic utility. Hong et al. [25] provided a key observation in this direction by demonstrating that YY1 mutations are confined to a copy-number-neutral genomic subtype, whereas YY1-wild-type insulinomas show a high prevalence of copy number variation (CNV) amplifications, including chromosomes 7, 3p, 5q, and 13q, defining a distinct CNV-amplified subtype. Notably, Hong et al. did not correlate these subtypes with metastatic status or clinical outcomes. Whether either of these subtypes is enriched among metastatic insulinomas, and whether specific amplifications confer invasive or metastatic capacity, remains entirely unknown.

The current management protocols for F-PanNETs as reflected in existing guidelines remain limited. SSAs are recommended as the first-line treatment option for all F-PanNETs [26, 27]. Depending on clinical severity, medical therapies such as diazoxide and/or everolimus may be considered for patients with insulinoma anti-diabetic medications are recommended for glucagonoma to manage hyperglycemia; and high-dose proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are used for gastrinoma to regulate gastric acid hypersecretion. This symptom-driven approach, while clinically necessary, has likely contributed to a relative neglect of molecular characterization, particularly for rare NiF-PanNETs. The low incidence of these tumors has resulted in insufficient research and limited focus on integrating relevant molecular alterations that could guide targeted therapies.

WHO grading

The World Health Organization (WHO) grading system, based on Ki-67 index and mitotic rate, remains an essential clinical tool for estimating tumor behavior and guiding treatment decisions in PanNETs. Under the 2019 classification, well-differentiated PanNETs are stratified into G1 (Ki-67 < 3%, mitotic rate < 2/2 mm²), G2 (Ki-67 3–20%, mitotic rate 2–20/2 mm²), and G3 (Ki-67 > 20%, mitotic rate > 20/2 mm²) [28]. The prognostic value of Ki-67 is substantial, with 5-year survival rates of 75.8%, 58.4%, and 35.1% for G1, G2, and G3, respectively [29].

However, the clinical utility of Ki-67 as a standalone decision-making criterion is increasingly questionable. While guidelines from European Neuroendocrine Tumour Society (ENETS) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) rely heavily on tumor grade to guide treatment, the relationship between Ki-67 and other clinical factors such as tumor-related symptoms, burden, and growth rate remains controversial. This controversy is not inconsequential. On the contrary, it underscores a fundamental constraint of the current grading system in that tumor behavior is a complex, multifactorial phenotype that a single proliferation index cannot fully capture. Consequently, the current guideline-driven approach, which often manages G1 and G2 tumors similarly, may be systematically producing suboptimal outcomes for a subset of patients [28]. To improve classification by grades, existing research has increasingly incorporated molecular aspects including biomarkers and gene expression. Most of these efforts have focused on evaluating the distinctions between well- and poorly-differentiated PanNETs, providing valuable clinical and molecular insights that are considered sufficient for accurately distinguishing G3 PanNETs from NECs. However, as noted above, the near-equivalent management of G1 and G2 tumors current guidance represents a persistent gap remains insufficiently nuanced.

The separation of G2 PanNETs into lower and higher proliferative subgroups carries significant clinical implications and has now been validated by several independent studies, serving as a telling example of this inadequacy. Specifically, G2 is increasingly subdivided into G2a (Ki-67 3–<10%) and G2b (Ki-67 10–≤20%) [30, 31]. The findings across studies are consistent and striking, showing that G2b tumors behave more like G3 PanNETs, with markedly higher rates of lymph node metastasis (65.1% vs. 41.7%), distant metastasis (58.5% vs. 26.1%), perineural invasion (64.3% vs. 42.3%), and vascular invasion (86% vs. 59.3%). Most dramatically, liver metastasis rates reach 58.5% in G2b versus only 6.1% in G1. Moreover, G2b is associated with significantly worse overall survival compared to G2a. Therefore, these findings indicate that managing G1 and G2 PanNETs under a single category is no longer tenable, as they clearly exhibit distinct levels of malignancy. On the basis of this evidence, it is therefore reasonable to propose that patients with G2b may require treatment strategies more aligned with those used for G3 PanNETs, though prospective validation remains necessary.

This finding prompts a further question whether G3 should be subgraded as well? The current distinction between well-differentiated G3 PanNETs and poorly-differentiated NECs is clinically necessary, as their treatment paradigms differ substantially. However, existing data show considerable overlaps with approximately 32% of G3 tumors have Ki-67 > 55%, while 33% of NECs fall below 55% [32]. Despite the fact that Ki-67 and tumor grade clearly correlate with population-level survival, current guidelines that primarily rely on tumor grade cannot guide therapy with sufficient confidence for individual patients, especially those near grade boundaries. In such borderline cases, the risk of misclassification is high, and the consequences are clinically significant. This limitation underscores the need for an integrated framework that incorporates additional biomarkers and clinical parameters to refine risk stratification at the individual level.

Tumor size

Tumor size is incorporated into the AJCC TNM staging system for PanNETs. For large PanNETs (> 2 cm), surgical resection is broadly accepted as the standard of care unless prohibitive surgical risks or comorbidities exist [33]. The NCCN guidelines provide specific recommendations for NF-PanNETs and F-PanNETs (Fig. 1), particularly for non-functional tumors larger than 2 cm, invasive, or involving lymph nodes, a pancreatoduodenectomy with lymph node removal is advised for tumors in the pancreatic head, while distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy is preferred for those in the distal pancreas. While management of large tumors is relatively uncontested, the optimal approach for small (≤ 2 cm), asymptomatic NF-PanNETs remains highly debated [34–36]. It is also worth noting the predictive value for malignancy in these small tumors is limited and inconsistent, raising a fundamental question of whether small NF-PanNETs should be observed or resected?

Therapeutic algorithms for pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors suggested by National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. In this figure, it is suggested that G1 and G2 are classified as one, and were given similar treatment options

Several studies have reported minimal or no growth over years in small NF-PanNETs, suggesting that non-operative management is safe and feasible [37–39]. On this basis, the NCCN guidelines primarily recommend active surveillance for most small NF-PanNETs (≤ 2 cm). This approach avoids unnecessary pancreatic surgery with its attendant morbidity. However, a growing body of evidence challenges the assumption that small size equates to indolent behavior (Table 1). While the majority of small NF-PanNETs are predominantly classified as G1, a notable subset of an approximately 18% carry a higher grade, which are G2 or beyond, reflecting greater variability in their biological behavior. Within this cohort, lymph node metastasis (N1) rates reach 3.1% for G1 and 15% for G2, with microvascular invasion observed in 14% [34, 40]. More strikingly, even tumors ≤ 1 cm and occasionally < 5 mm, have been reported to harbor lymph node metastases [46]. Several studies have found no significant correlation between tumor size and the prevalence of lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, high WHO grade, or distant metastasis [46, 47]. Besides, small tumors located in the pancreatic head appear to carry a worse prognosis and higher metastatic potential than those in the body or tail [45, 48].

These conflicting data indicate that tumor size alone is an unreliable predictor of biological behavior. The majority of small NF-PanNETs are indeed indolent, which explains the favorable outcomes of surveillance cohorts. However, a clinically meaningful minority behave aggressively despite their small size. Current guidelines that base management primarily on a 2 cm threshold fail to distinguish these two populations, leading to potential overtreatment of indolent tumors or undertreatment of aggressive ones. The challenge, then, is to identify which small NF-PanNETs warrant surgery despite their size. A practical proposal would be to move away from a size-only threshold toward a risk-stratified algorithm. For small NF-PanNETs (≤ 2 cm), resection is indicated when any of the following are present: G2 grade, pancreatic head location, suspicious radiological features (hypo-attenuation) [49], or patient preference after informed discussion. For tumors ≤ 1 cm, surveillance is appropriate given the low metastatic risk and the lack of demonstrable survival benefit from surgery in this subgroup [50].

Several key gaps remain, including the urgent need for prospective validation of a risk-scoring system that integrates tumor size, grade, radiological features, and anatomical location. The natural history of observed small NF-PanNETs that later progress remains poorly characterized. Moreover, the role of molecular biomarkers in predicting the aggressiveness of small tumors has yet to be explored. Given this evidence gap, clinicians must balance the non-zero risk of metastasis, even in subcentimeter tumors, against the morbidity of pancreatic resection. Guidelines should explicitly acknowledge that the current 2 cm threshold offers pragmatic guidance but lacks a precise biological justification.

Morphological variants

The 2022 WHO classification recognizes the classical histopathological morphology of PanNETs as monotonous cells with round nuclei, salt‑and‑pepper chromatin, and organoid architectures (trabecular, nested, glandular, or insular patterns) [7, 51]. Beyond these conventional features, several morphological variants such as oncocytic, rhabdoid, lipid‑rich, intraductal, hepatoid, and pleomorphic—have been described, mostly in case reports [52–55]. However, the critical question is whether they carry independent prognostic or diagnostic value that should influence clinical management remain to be elucidated.

Xue et al. [56] provided a more systematic analysis, demonstrating that variants such as oncocytic, hepatoid, lipid-rich, and plasmacytoid patterns are associated with more aggressive disease and frequently presented in advanced grades and stages of PanNETs. Despite relatively low Ki-67 indices (1.8–7.2%), these tumors presented with large sizes (4.0–5.3 cm) and high rates of lymph node (39%) and distant metastasis (18%). This dissociation between proliferation index and metastatic potential is striking. It suggests that the standard Ki‑67‑based grading system may underestimate true biological aggressiveness of these morphological variants. Notably, these aggressive variants shared common cytological features like abundant cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. These may serve as visual clues for pathologists to flag these cases for closer clinical follow-up, even when the grade appears low. In contrast, pleomorphic, paraganglioid, and ductuloinsular patterns appear to follow a more indolent course, with lower Ki-67 indices, smaller tumor sizes, and reduced metastasis rates compared to average PanNETs [57, 58]. Thus, this divergence reflects that morphological heterogeneity is biologically meaningful as it represents a dimension of tumor behavior that current guidelines fail to account for.

Distinguishing well-differentiated G3 PanNETs from poorly-differentiated NECs remains a persistent diagnostic challenge. According to Sigel et al. [59], G3 PanNETs can be distinguished from NECs by several morphological features, including abundant cytoplasm, a lower nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio, and lower frequencies of nuclear pleomorphism, angulated nuclei, apoptosis, and necrosis [59, 60]. G3 PanNETs can also be distinguished from G2 PanNETs by several morphological features. G3 tumors show higher frequencies of nuclear tangles, nuclear pleomorphism, angulated nuclei, apoptosis, and necrosis. In contrast, G2 PanNETs more frequently exhibit round nuclei fine chromatin, and plasmacytoid morphology [57, 61]. Although these descriptive findings are valuable, the evidence base supporting their routine use remains thin, and their integration into clinical decision-making is currently premature.

Moving beyond descriptive cataloging requires prospective validation of morphological variant status as an independent predictor, development of a simple reproducible classification (e.g., aggressive vs. classical features), and the exploration of shared molecular drivers in aggressive variants. Until such evidence emerges, clinicians and pathologists should recognize that oncocytic, hepatoid, or lipid-rich morphology may signal potential aggressiveness, warranting closer surveillance or reconsideration of conservative management, regardless of Ki-67 index. Conversely, classical or indolent variant morphology in small, low-grade tumors supports active surveillance. Guidelines should explicitly acknowledge that morphological variants, though currently understudied, carry prognostic implications that should not be ignored.

Genomic to translational stratification in PanNETs

The heterogeneity of PanNETs is further reflected in their molecular landscape, which encompasses distinct biomarkers and genetic profiles carrying a variety of mutations (Fig. 2). Recent advances in high-throughput technologies including next-generation sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, and RNA sequencing have substantially improved our understanding of the molecular drivers of PanNETs development.

Overview of the mutational landscape of PanNETs. Key driver genes of PanNETs and their respective functional information are ordered from most to least prevalent

Although the majority of PanNETs are sporadic, several hereditary syndromes increase susceptibility to tumorigenesis, collectively accounting for less than 10% of cases. These syndromes include multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1; 30–80% of familial cases), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; 12%), tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC; 2%), and Von Hippel–Lindau (VHL; <1%), with MEN1-related PanNET being the most common cause of syndrome-related deaths [62]. Soczomski et al. comprehensively compared germline and sporadic PanNETs, revealing that sporadic tumors are larger (3.88 ± 2.8 cm vs. 2.44 ± 1.92 cm) and have higher metastasis rates (55% vs. 22%) than their germline counterparts [63]. The molecular alterations in PanNETs have been well characterized. MEN1 mutations account for the largest proportion (44%), followed by DAXX (25%), ATRX (17.6%), and genes involved in the mTOR signaling pathway, including PTEN (7.3%), TSC2 (8.8%), and PIK3CA (1.4%) [64]. Copy number loss of CDKN2A and other chromatin remodeling genes, such as SETD2, ARID1A, CHD8, and DNMT1 has also been described [65]. I In well-differentiated G3 PanNETs, the mutational profile retains alterations typical of lower-grade PanNETs but additionally shows loss of TP53 (100%) and, rarely, RB1 (12%), suggesting genetic overlap with NECs. Notably, however, studies distinguishing G1 from G2 PanNETs on the basis of genetic profiles remain lacking.

Understanding the distinct genomic profiles and biomarker expression across different clinical parameters is critically important. Current classification systems and prognostic models rely primarily on clinical factors, largely overlooking the molecular heterogeneity of PanNETs. As a result, some patients may be undertreated or overtreated due to the absence of molecularly guided treatment recommendations in existing guidelines. This review therefore underscores the emergence of integrating molecular alterations into clinical frameworks to establish a more accurate, personalized risk stratification system for PanNETs.

Core driver mutations of PanNETs

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1)

MEN1, located on chromosome 11q13, encodes the menin protein and represents the most frequently altered tumor suppressor gene in PanNETs. Over 1,600 germline and somatic mutations have been reported, with no clear mutation hotspot and approximately 75% being inactivating [66]. Frameshift and nonsense mutations are associated with MEN1 reduction in sporadic PanNETs [67], while frameshift and missense mutations are the most prevalent germline mutations in MEN1 [66, 68]. MEN1 inactivation occurs through a combination of genetic and epigenetic events, including loss of heterozygosity (LOH) and promoter hypermethylation, leading to biallelic loss [69].

A critical distinction with prognostic potential lies in the pattern of LOH. Recurrent LOH affecting 10 additional chromosomes (1,2,3,6,8,10,11,16,21,22) alongside chromosome 11 is predominantly associated with somatic MEN1 mutations and predicts poor outcomes, including higher metastasis rates [67, 70, 71]. In contrast, LOH restricted to chromosome 11, without involvement of the other 10 chromosomes, is linked to lower Ki‑67 indices, reduced metastatic potential, and better clinical outcomes. This observation suggests that the genomic context of MEN1 loss, not just the mutation itself, determines aggressiveness. Therefore, patients with the widespread LOH pattern are suggested for intensified surveillance or early adjuvant therapy, whereas those with isolated chr11 LOH might be managed conservatively.

Besides genomic alterations, epigenetic interactions introduce an additional layer of complexity in MEN1-mutant PanNETs. Likas et al. [72] found that patients with MEN1 mutations alone (without concurrent DAXX or ATRX mutations showed a lower prevalence of the 10-additional-chromosome LOH pattern and, notably, higher methylation of methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT). MGMT promoter methylation is clinically relevant because it predicts better response to temozolomide and improved prognosis [73, 74]. This prompts a key question whether MEN1-mutant tumors without DAXX or ATRX co-mutation may be more likely to benefit from temozolomide-based therapy, whereas those with DAXX or ATRX mutations may not. However, the mechanistic link between MEN1 and MGMT methylation remains unexplored and warrants investigation.

Beyond LOH patterns and MGMT regulation, the specific exon location of MEN1 mutations offers another readily accessible molecular feature with potential prognostic value, as exon location can be easily obtained from any standard sequencing report. Exon 2 mutations are the most frequently detected in MEN1-mutants [63, 75, 76]. Studies revealed that patients harboring these specific exon 2 mutations present with a more advanced and aggressive disease phenotype, characterized by a significantly higher risk of distant metastasis and an earlier onset of PanNETs in younger individuals compared to those with mutations in other exons. Therefore, incorporating exon 2 mutation status into risk calculators could refine prognostic predictions for PanNET patients. For instance, individuals carrying exon 2 mutations might be considered for upstaged surveillance or more intensive follow up, even in the absence of other high-risk features. Exons 3 and 10 also show elevated mutation frequencies and likely impair menin function, although their clinical impact remains less characterized, representing a missed opportunity for more refined molecular stratification. A remaining gap that should be taken into consideration is the lack of prospective validation of exon 2 mutations as an independent prognostic marker in PanNETs. Current evidence is derived solely from retrospective cohorts without multivariable adjustment for grade, stage, or treatment. Future studies should therefore determine whether exon 2 mutations retain independent prognostic significance after accounting for LOH patterns and MGMT expression or methylation status. This determination is necessary to establish whether incorporating exon status into routine risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Death domain associated protein (DAXX) / alpha thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome x-linked (ATRX)

DAXX and ATRX form a complex that deposits histone H3.3 at telomeres, preventing telomere dysfunction and alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) [64]. Mutually exclusive inactivating mutations in either gene are the primary drivers of ALT-positive in PanNETs, and ALT-positivity has been consistently associated with higher metastatic rates and poorer survival. Specifically, patients with DAXX or ATRX loss have 5-year disease-free survival of only 40% and 10-year disease-specific survival of 50% [77–80]. While most studies link ALT positivity to DAXX or ATRX mutations, recent evidence shows that 21.4% of metastatic sub-3 cm NF-PanNETs with ALT-positive retain normal DAXX orATRX expression [81]. This finding is clinically important because it implies that alternative pathways to telomere maintenance exist possibly through other chromatin remodelers or telomerase reactivation, and these may confer similar aggressive behavior.

Recently, the notion that DAXX or ATRX loss defines aggressiveness only in large or high-grade tumors is challenged by data showing that small NF-PanNETs (≤ 2 cm) with DAXX or ATRX loss have 5-year recurrence-free survival of 65% vs. 60% and 10-year of 32% vs. 40% compared to those without loss [79]. Moreover, 93.3% of metastasized sub-3 cm NF-PanNETs are ALT-positive [81]. These observations argue that ALT-positivity status should be considered an independent risk factor regardless of tumor size or grade, and current surveillance guidelines that rely heavily on size thresholds may underestimate risk in small, ALT‑positive tumors [80].

Unlike MEN1 where exon 2 mutations define an aggressive subset, specific mutation regions in DAXX or ATRX that correlate with prognosis have not been identified. Three new missense hotspots in the histone-binding domain of DAXX (amino acids 178–389) and five missense mutations in ATRX impairing ATPase activity have been reported [82, 83], but whether these differentially affect ALT activation or clinical outcomes is unknown. This represents a failure to establish a genotype-phenotype correlation. However, the link between DAXX or ATRX loss and chromosomal instability is well established [84]. A key advance comes from classifying NF-PanNETs into CNV deletion, amplification, and neutral subtypes [25]. The deletion subtype carries the highest mutation rates for DAXX or ATRX (67.6%), MEN1 (58.8%), and mTOR-related genes (50%), and shows frequent tumor suppressor gene inactivation (74%) compared to amplification (5%) or neutral (25%) subtypes. Consequently, this CNV deletion subtype particularly when harboring DAXX or ATRX mutations, exhibits the greatest aggressiveness and worst prognosis. Importantly, tumors with concurrent DAXX or ATRX mutations and CNV (regardless of amplification or deletion) have a significantly higher risk of relapse within 2 years. Collectively, these findings support a refined risk stratification that moves beyond tumor size and grade, where prospective studies are now needed to validate the proposed framework.

Aristaless-related homeobox (ARX) and pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (PDX1)

ARX and PDX1 are master transcription factors that direct pancreatic endocrine differentiation into alpha cells and beta cells, respectively. In PanNETs, their expression serves as a surrogate for the cell-of-origin and has emerged as a powerful prognostic classifier independent of tumor size or grade. Both ARX and PDX1 represents a clear prognostic dichotomy. Across multiple cohorts, ARX+ NF-PanNETs consistently exhibit higher aggressiveness, poorer prognosis, and higher relapse rates compared to PDX1 + tumors, which typically behave indolently and are often associated with benign insulinomas [85, 86]. Crucially, tumor size and grade do not correlate with ARX or PDX1 expression, meaning that a small, low‑grade ARX+ tumor may carry a worse prognosis than a larger, higher‑grade PDX1 + tumor. Therefore, lineage status should be incorporated into routine pathology reporting as an independent stratification factor.

Beyond canonical alpha-like (ARX+) and beta-like (PDX1+) tumors, epigenetic profiling has identified an intermediate subtype that co-expresses or shifts between these lineages. This intermediate group, predominantly ARX+ (86%), displays the most aggressive behavior. Specifically, 71.4% present at T3/T4 stage, 72.8% have high grade, and 52.2% experience relapse [85, 87]. Future research area should investigate this subgroup, given its highly aggressive nature and potential as therapeutic targets. From a clinical perspective, this subgroup should be flagged for intensive surveillance regardless of tumor size or grade, as current risk models that rely solely on proliferation indices would miss a substantial proportion of high-risk patients. Besides, evidence suggests that alpha-like PanNETs may transition to intermediate tumors upon progression, accompanied by elevated copy-number aberrations [87]. While it is clearly aggressive, no prospective study has defined its molecular drivers, optimal surveillance intensity, or potential targeted therapies. However, this observation carries significant implications, as lineage switching may introduce therapeutic vulnerabilities. For instance, intermediate tumors could retain sensitivity to alpha-cell- or beta-cell-specific pathways, though this possibility has not yet been investigated.

Phosphatase and tensin homolog on chromosome 10 (PTEN) and tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2)

PTEN and TSC2 are negative regulators of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, yet their low prevalence in NF‑PanNETs has limited robust study. Downregulation of either gene correlates with shorter overall and disease-free survival, but neither is an independent prognostic indicator, underscoring that solitary assessment of PTEN or TSC2 lacks clinical utility [88, 89]. From a stratification perspective, this means these markers should only be used in combination with other molecular features (e.g., MEN1, DAXX or ATRX, CNV subtypes) rather than alone. Notably, a case report described a TSC2-mutant PanNET that responded exceptionally to capecitabine/temozolomide, by achieving a nearly complete response to chemotherapy and has no recurrence 3 years after R1 resection [90]. Although unvalidated, this raises the testable possibility that TSC2 mutations or other mTOR pathway alterations might identify a subset sensitive to alkylating agents. Similarly, missense mutations in PTEN and TSC2 are most common [64, 91], but whether specific variants (e.g., TSC2 R905Q associated with mild tuberous sclerosis [92]) carry distinct prognostic or predictive significance remains unknown. Integration into current frameworks would require prospective studies that collect PTEN/TSC2 mutation status alongside treatment exposures and outcomes. Importantly, these rare mutations may lead to underdiagnosis of TSC, as patients often do not meet diagnostic criteria yet exhibit moderate to severe clinical features. Overall, the roles, mechanisms, and clinical implications of mTOR-related gene mutations in PanNETs remain largely unelucidated.

Co-mutation patterns and their clinical implications

To refine PanNETs stratification, researchers have assessed how co-mutations collectively impact tumor progression and prognosis (Table 2). While the individual driver genes discussed earlier are generally linked to aggressive features, the clinical significance of their co-occurrence is more complex and context-dependent. Notably, mutations in MEN1 together with DAXX or ATRX have been associated with disparate prognostic outcomes. From a critical perspective, these findings are not contradictory but complementary. They reveal that the same molecular alterations, particularly loss of MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX have opposite prognostic effects depending on disease stage and treatment context. In metastatic disease, co-mutations in MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX are consistently associated with longer overall survival in tumors larger than 2 cm. Indeed, patients with PanNETs harboring these mutations show remarkably favorable outcomes, including a 100% survival rate at ten-years and a median overall survival that has not been reached, compared to 43.63 months in wild-type patients (log-rank test, p = 0.047) [64, 93]. This is likely because DAXX or ATRX loss drives ALT positivity and genomic instability, making tumor cells more susceptible to DNA-damaging agents such as PRRT. Additionally, among those receiving PRRT with metastatic well-differentiated, the presence of these co‑mutations was linked to superior progression‑free survival (28.43 vs. 9.83 months) [94]. However, it is important to note that only one study has proposed this treatment benefit specifically in the context of these co‑mutations, thus requiring further validation. In localized, resected disease, however, the same instability increases the risk of relapse and recurrence [95, 98]. Therefore, co-mutation status does not carry an intrinsic favorable or unfavorable prognostic value, indeed, its clinical interpretation depends entirely on disease stage and treatment context.

Transcriptomic analysis has revealed three subtypes of MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX with distinct biology (hypoxia/glycolysis, proliferative, and immunosuppressive) [98]. The most clinically actionable finding is that the immunosuppressive subtype is significantly smaller (< 2.5 cm, p = 0.023) yet carry a metastasis-like signature and display aggressive behavior. Therefore, size alone is insufficient to rule out malignancy in co-mutated PanNETs, making molecular subtyping essential. Besides, primary samples with co-mutations in MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX exhibit more frequent recurrent loss or LoH across ten additional chromosomes, along with chromosome 11 loss compared to tumors with MEN1 mutations alone. Furthermore, this co-mutated subgroup shows MGMT hypomethylation, a molecular alteration that suggests reduced tumor sensitivity to alkylating agents and is therefore associated with a poorer prognosis [72].

Another remarkable finding is that the combined loss of MEN1, ATRX, and PTEN drives the development of high-grade tumors (Ki-67 > 20%) [99]. In addition, the combined loss of TSC2 and ATRX or PTEN is associated with significantly shorter recurrence-free survival, establishing TSC2 and ATRX as independent prognostic factors for RFS (HR 10.1) [97]. Notably, a single gene mutation in TSC2, as previously mentioned, is not an independent prognostic factor. This raises the question of whether the interactions among MEN1, DAXX, or ATRX mutations drive a unique evolutionary trajectory that potentially enhances immunogenicity and explains the better outcomes seen in metastatic disease or if PTEN might play a key role in driving malignancy when these mutations occur concurrently. From a stratification perspective, this unresolved question has direct consequences. If the favorable prognosis of metastatic co‑mutated patients is driven by enhanced immunogenicity, these patients might be ideal candidates for immune checkpoint blockade. Conversely, if the poor outcomes in localized disease are driven by PTEN co‑loss, then PTEN status should be used to stratify co‑mutated patients into low‑risk (PTEN wild‑type) and high‑risk (PTEN mutant) subgroups. However, besides Avanthay et al. (2025) which revealed MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX co-mutated immunosuppressive subgroup is highly enriched in immune components, including allograft rejection, interferon γ and inflammatory response, or IL2 and IL6 signaling [98], the hypothesis that MEN1/DAXX or ATRX mutations increase immunogenicity is speculative, as related studies that compare the immune microenvironment of co-mutated tumors remain scarce. Thus, this highlights the importance and advantages in exploring combinational mutations, as they offer critical insights that could enhance the stratification of PanNETs for better identification of high-risk patients.

The observation that MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX co‑mutated PanNETs uniformly express ARX and show PDX1 promoter hypermethylation is more than a descriptive finding. It establishes that loss of DAXX or ATRX locks tumors into an alpha‑like state, which is intrinsically more aggressive than beta‑like (PDX1+) tumors [95]. This implies that ARX positivity alone, regardless of mutation status, should raise clinical suspicion. Among ARX+ tumors, those with DAXX or ATRX loss have significantly larger tumor size (median 3.0 vs. 1.9 cm) and higher Ki-67 index (2.5% vs. 1%) compared to ARX+ tumors with wild-type DAXX or ATRX [86]. As DAXX or ATRX loss is associated with ARX-positivity, this suggests a strong correlation between ALT positivity and ARX expression Consistent with this, ALT‑positive tumors have a 4.2‑fold higher recurrence rate than ALT‑negative tumors (34.4% vs. 8.2%), even when small and low‑grade [100]. Moreover, 88.9% of ALT-positive cases are ARX+, reinforcing that ALT positivity is predominantly an alpha-cell lineage phenomenon. More strikingly, 67% (62/92) of intermediate PanNETs (ARX + and PDX1+/ARX- and PDX1-) harbored mutations in MEN1 and/or DAXX or ATRX genes [87].

From a stratification perspective, this finding has direct clinical implications. ARX immunohistochemistry and ALT positivity testing should be incorporated into routine pathology workups for all PanNETs, regardless of size. A small (< 2 cm), low‑grade (G1) tumor that is ARX + and ALT‑positive warrants the same intensive surveillance as a much larger, higher‑grade tumor. Notably, no prospective study has correlated the mutation status of intermediate PanNETs with long-term outcomes. Consequently, it remains unknown whether intermediate tumors with MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX mutations differ in recurrence rate or treatment response from those without these mutations.

Multi-omics integration redefines tumor subtypes

The understanding of PanNETs heterogeneity is being fundamentally reshaped by multi-omics approaches, which provide a systems-level view that transcends single-gene or mutation-based classifications (Table 3). By revealing functional interactions between genetic alterations, metabolic states, and the tumor microenvironment, multi-omics offers a more in-depth reflection of tumor behavior. It is crucial to recognize that multi-omics adds a layer of biological insight that can refine risk stratification and uncover therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Across several pivotal proteotranscriptomic and proteogenomic studies, molecular subtypes defined by distinct metabolic programs and genetic profiles have consistently emerged. The alpha-like subtype, characterized by a high frequency of MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX mutations, exhibits elevated metabolic pathways including oxidative phosphorylation and a dependency on mitochondrial metabolic programs [101]. This finding suggests a potential vulnerability to agents targeting oxidative metabolism, a hypothesis that awaits clinical validation. A comparable cluster, designated C2 in another study, shared these metabolic and mutational features, reinforcing the robustness of this phenotype across analytical platforms [102].

In contrast, PDX1 + PanNETs generally display a progenitor-like state and lack the overt proliferative features of more aggressive subtypes. Integrated analyses have challenged the conventional view that PDX1 + tumors are uniformly indolent. Strikingly, a RAS-driven subset was identified within the PDX1-high subtype, despite its lower overall mutational burden compared to other subtypes [101]. This exposes a latent oncogenic potential that would remain hidden without integrated profiling, underscoring the importance of multi-omics for uncovering true biological drivers.

A distinct proliferative subtype has been identified that contains both PanNECs and PanNETs with poorly differentiated histology. The poor survival in this subgroup supports a biological continuum that traditional grading obscures, suggesting that this molecular signature may be a hallmark of high-grade disease. Marked by strong activation of cell-cycle-related gene sets (E2F targets, G2/M checkpoints, MYC targets), this subtype corresponds closely to the C3 cluster identified using proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiles [102]. Notably, these tumors often exhibit aggressive clinical features and high proliferative signaling in the absence of classical PanNET driver mutations (MEN1, DAXX, ATRX). This indicates that proliferative deregulation can occur independently of these well-known alterations, a critical nuance for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making.

Finally, a stromal- or mesenchymal-enriched subtype displays increased extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) activation, and immune signaling, coupled with a high frequency of VHL alterations, hypoxia, and glycolytic reprogramming [101, 103]. Parallel features were observed in the P2/P3 and C4 subgroups in other cohorts, which similarly demonstrated VHL mutations, enhanced hypoxia, increased glycolysis enzymes, and reduced oxidative phosphorylation [102, 104]. Despite differences in analytical methodologies, these classifications show remarkable consistency in biological interpretation, underscoring a fundamental convergence of tumor phenotypes. Of note, DAXX mutations were reported as a superior classifier of high-risk disease, being exclusively enriched in the aggressive P2/P3 subgroups alongside elevated copy number alterations, specifically chromosome 7 amplifications, whereas MEN1 mutations lacked this discriminatory power [104]. While earlier studies centered on chromosome 11 alterations, multi-omics integration pinpoints the previously underappreciated aggressive genomic marker of chromosome 7.

Focusing on the immune signaling component of our stromal or mesenchymal subtype, tumors with EMT activation and immune signaling, features that align with the C1 proteomic subtype [102], were significantly enriched in the immune‑suppressive subgroup. This immune‑suppressive subgroup was characterized by activated TGF‑β signaling, M2 macrophage polarization, and elevated inhibitory immune checkpoints including PD‑L1, IDO1, and TIM3. These features have direct implications for future therapy management in patients with the stromal- or mesenchymal-enriched subtype. The co-expression of these multiple suppressive mechanisms argues against single-agent PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, as such tumors typically exhibit primary resistance to monotherapy. Additionally, the elevated expression of IDO1 and TIM3 offers further actionable vulnerabilities, clinical trials incorporating IDO1 inhibitors or anti‑TIM3 antibodies, particularly in combination with PD‑1 blockade, could be considered for biomarker‑selected patients. While this immune-suppressive subgroup is associated with high-risk disease, successful implementation of such combination regimens could meaningfully improve outcomes in this poor-prognosis population. Prospective trials in NF‑PanNETs should therefore stratify patients by this stromal or mesenchymal immune signature and evaluate combinatorial immunotherapies that directly target the identified suppressive pathways.

Beyond molecular subtyping, multi-omics approaches uncover deeper biological functions impacted by somatic mutations. By correlating copy-number alterations with mRNA, protein, and phosphoprotein levels, this approach can reveal the intricate effects of somatic mutations on upstream and downstream regulatory networks, thereby providing directions for targeted therapy development [102]. Furthermore, multi-omics enables the identification of clinically relevant prognostic biomarkers. For example, three signature proteins, including GNAO1 (guanine nucleotide-binding protein subunit alpha) and INA (alpha-internexin), were associated with favorable patient outcomes, whereas VCAN (versican core protein) exhibited the opposite trend. Importantly, VCAN was significantly upregulated in the C1 subgroup, which had the worst outcome in the same cohort. Its detectability in plasma further supports its potential as a non‑invasive biomarker for early cancer detection and monitoring. However, the actual role of VCAN in PanNETs has not been elucidated. Collectively, these findings exemplify how multi‑omics integration bridges mechanistic discovery with translational utility, linking molecular phenotypes to prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers. While the WHO grading system remains an indispensable prognostic tool, the emerging multi-omics data compellingly demonstrates that a single histological grade can harbor tumors with fundamentally different biological drivers and risks, highlighting a critical distinction obscured by current grading.

While multi-omics focuses on molecular data, radiomics provides a complementary, non-invasive window into tumor biology. Earlier feature-based studies using computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) successfully identified key prognostic indicators such as large tumor diameter, irregular shape, low apparent diffusion coefficient ratio, non-uniform enhancement, that help differentiate low- from high-grade PanNETs [105, 106]. While these features provided valuable insights, however, they rely on human interpretation and are inherently limited. Radiomics builds on this foundation by objectively capturing and quantifying intricate subvisual patterns of tumor heterogeneity and phenotypes that are otherwise imperceptible. This shift from qualitative to quantitative assessment is not merely incremental, it represents a paradigm change in how we extract prognostic information from routine imaging.

A recent study demonstrated an automated tool for detecting small PanNETs on CT scans, training a machine learning model on radiomics features to achieve a sensitivity of 90% and an AUC of 0.87 [107], showcasing its potential for accurate, automated screening of small tumors. Beyond detection, radiomics has proven highly effective for tumor characterization, such as determining grade and aggressiveness. A radiomic model using multivariate logistic regression offered an objective perspective on PanNET grading, providing a critical non-invasive tool for managing small tumors (≤ 2 cm), where traditional imaging and biopsy often fail to reliably predict biological aggressiveness [108].

More recently, investigations have integrated radiomics with deep learning signatures to produce powerful predictive models for tumor grade, lymph node metastasis, and other aggressive pathological features [109, 110]. For example, one study predicted lymph node metastasis in both ≤ 2 cm and > 2 cm NF-PanNETs with an AUC of 0.93. The clinical implication is clear. Patients with a low radiomic deep learning signature (RDPS) for lymph node metastasis may be candidates for minimally invasive enucleation or surveillance regardless of tumor size, whereas a high RDPS score necessitates standard resection [110]. In another significant development, a model combining a pathomics score (Ki-67 heterogeneity), a deep learning radiomic score, and clinical factors achieved near-perfect prediction of postoperative liver metastasis after R0 resection (AUC 0.985) [109]. These findings demonstrate an advanced, non-invasive method to assess tumor biology by quantitatively capturing complex tumor texture and heterogeneity. Despite its considerable promise, radiomics remains an emerging field. To date, most models have been developed using retrospective, often single-institution cohorts and have not undergone prospective validation. Their generalizability across different CT scanners, reconstruction protocols, and patient populations is therefore unproven. Therefore, multi-omics and radiomics serve as powerful complements that refine risk stratification, uncover therapeutic vulnerabilities, and enable non-invasive prediction of tumor behavior.

Conventional laboratory diagnostic markers

PanNETs express both specific and non‑specific neuroendocrine biomarkers that are essential for accurate diagnosis. Specific markers like insulin, gastrin, glucagon define functional syndromes, but the majority of PanNETs are non‑functional and do not secrete these hormones. Therefore, immunohistochemical profiling of non‑specific markers such as chromogranin A (CgA), synaptophysin (Syn), neuron‑specific enolase (NSE), and insulinoma‑associated protein 1 (INSM1) is critical for distinguishing tumor subtypes (Fig. 3), as their expression levels could vary among them. However, CgA has limited prognostic value due to varying optimal cut‑off values and potential false positives [111, 112]. Its expression is stronger in non‑β‑cell tumors, which helps differentiate CgA‑weaker insulinomas [113]. Similarly, the prognostic significance of Syn in NF-PanNETs remains doubted. INSM1, a second-generation biomarker, offers sensitivity comparable to CgA and Syn [114] but does not significantly aid in subtype classification, as its expression is high in lower-grade tumors and markedly reduced in higher-grade PanNETs, which reflects a similar trend to the traditional markers [115–117].

Somatostatin receptors (SSTR1, SSTR2A, SSTR3, SSTR4, and SSTR5) are well-established targets for both diagnosis (⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT) and therapy (SSAs, PRRT) in PanNETs. Approximately 75% of tumors express at least three SSTR subtypes, with SSTR1 and SSTR2A broadly expressed, SSTR3/5 moderate, and SSTR4 poor [118]. Expression patterns vary by hormonal cell type. Specifically, 100% of gastrinomas and glucagonomas express SSTR2A, 100% of somatostatinomas express SSTR5, while insulinomas show heterogeneous SSTR expression, with SSTR4-positive cases being almost exclusively insulinomas [118, 119]. However, the prognostic and predictive value of SSTR expression is far from settled. Some studies report higher SSTR2A and SSTR5 expression in lower grade/stage tumors (G1/G2, stage I/II) and an association with better survival, while others find no correlation [120, 121]. Moreover, no differences in expression profiles have been found between primary and metastatic tumors or between well- and poorly-differentiated cases [122]. This means that SSTR expression alone cannot reliably separate high‑risk from low‑risk patients.

These inconsistent data suggest that SSTRs have limited diagnostic and prognostic utility, where their primary value lies in guiding therapy. Three main SSAs with different SSTR binding affinities are currently available (Table 4) [123]. Although most PanNETs express SSTR2A at high frequency, a clinically important subset of G1/2 and even G3 tumors lacks SSTR2A expression, rendering SSTR2-directed agents such as octreotide and lanreotide ineffective. This observation underscores an urgent need to study SSTR subtypes beyond SSTR2A, including their roles in tumor progression and potential crosstalk between receptor pathways that may explain divergent clinical outcomes. Future research should therefore focus on three areas. First, investigate the functional consequences of SSTR co-expression patterns to refine patient selection for SSAs and PRRT. Second, explore mechanisms that restore or upregulate SSTR expression in low-expressing tumors, including gene therapy or epigenetic drugs. Third, identify molecular alterations affecting SSTR signaling, such as the truncated somatostatin 5 splicing variant SST5TMD4, which promotes malignancy by disrupting SSTR2 signaling [124], and the epigenetic silencing of the natural antisense transcript SSTR5-AS1, which leads to SSTR5 downregulation [125]. Addressing these gaps could transform SSTR profiling from a simple diagnostic tool into a framework for personalized therapy and risk stratification.

Existing drawbacks and perspectives on the integration of clinical and molecular classifications

From the preceding discussion, it is evident that although advanced research has identified novel clinical criteria and molecular markers, significant evidence gaps remain for both clinical and molecular aspects of PanNETs, particularly for NF‑PanNETs, which are more aggressive and prevalent. The heterogeneous nature of NF‑PanNETs makes accurate risk stratification a critical challenge, largely because current frameworks lack comprehensive integration of molecular and clinical parameters.

Reliance on traditional grading and staging systems has been a major factor limiting prognostic accuracy. While these tools have provided useful prognostic information for decades, they fail to account for molecular heterogeneity. Consequently, current guidelines have gradually become less reflective of the true biological complexity of PanNETs. Despite the discovery of many biomarkers, their prognostic value, clinical relevance, and applicability remain poorly validated. This highlights a critical research gap that increases risk of underestimating or overestimating tumor behavior, leading to suboptimal treatment decisions. For example, low-grade and low-stage tumors can sometimes exhibit more severe pathological features than suggested by grade and size alone [126, 127]. because patients may carry specific genetic mutations (e.g., DAXX or ATRX loss, ALT positivity) that are not captured by traditional parameters. Thus, developing an integrated clinicopathological and molecular classification is not merely desirable but necessary.

Besides, factors distinguishing G1 from G2 PanNETs remain unclear, yet this distinction could be critical for precisely predicting tumor behavior and developing personalized treatment plans, as current guidelines recommend nearly similar management for both grades. Moreover, pancreatic duct dilation has been identified as an independent prognostic factor associated with shorter overall survival and higher relapse risk [128, 129]. However, it has not yet been integrated into the AJCC or ENETS TNM staging systems. From a research perspective, molecular studies should investigate whether pancreatic duct dilation correlates with specific molecular alterations (e.g., VHL mutations, hypoxia signatures), which could lead to predictive models that combine imaging signs with genomic data.

Furthermore, the clinical management of small PanNETs presents a specific dilemma. The clinical management of small PanNETs (≤ 2 cm) presents a specific dilemma. Numerous studies have confirmed that even sub-2 cm tumors, particularly those ≥ 1.5 cm, can demonstrate significant aggressiveness, suggesting that surgical resection should be considered active surveillance in certain cases. However, conservative management of small PanNETs limits tissue availability, which in turn precludes the genomic discoveries needed to uncover molecular drivers of aggression and refine guidelines beyond size‑based surveillance. Therefore, it may be necessary to consider prospective biopsy protocols for small but radiologically suspicious lesions.

While existing studies have provided understanding into the molecular landscape of PanNETs, the associations between common genetic alterations and biomarker expressions remain incompletely understood. Of note, there is a notable lack of co-mutational studies tracking how these alterations evolve across disease stages. The differential therapy sensitivities (e.g., SSTR expression, FDG uptake, responses to targeted agents), and etc. remain to be systematically characterized in localized, advanced, and metastatic PanNETs. Such stage-dependent molecular shifts are critical for interpreting imaging results and optimizing treatment sequencing. For instance, while DAXX or ATRX loss and ALT positivity are linked to higher grade and metastasis, it remains unclear how these markers correlate with SSTR2 expression or FDG avidity across tumor sizes (e.g., < 2 cm vs. >2 cm), nodal status, or MGMT promoter methylation. Similarly, thresholds distinguishing localized from advanced PanNETs lack integrated molecular validation, as the current staging is purely anatomical, and radiomic features of aggression have not been systematically mapped to co-mutation patterns. Most critically, nearly all studies have been cross-sectional and primarily in primary tumors, prospective longitudinal cohorts tracking how MEN1, DAXX, and ATRX co-mutations evolve under therapy or during progression are absent.

The importance of addressing these gaps becomes even more apparent when considering the small subset of patients with atypical molecular expression patterns. For example, patients with ARX − and PDX1 − tumors, accompanied by ISL1 − and CDX2−, demonstrated lesser expression of SSTR2 (p < 0.001) [86], reducing the likelihood of detection by SSTR-based molecular imaging and limiting the effectiveness of SSA therapies that target SSTR2. Given that most available treatments are designed for patients expressing typical PanNET biomarkers, this subgroup risks being neglected. Therefore, deeper investigation into the molecular interplay and pathological features of atypical PanNETs is urgently needed to ensure these patients receive optimal care. Nonetheless, despite increasing contributions to understanding PanNET biology, the scarcity of reliable disease models remains a key challenge. Widely used cell lines such as BON-1 and QGP-1 have limited ability to represent the diversity of PanNETs [130]. Hence, more disease models that capture distinct tumor phenotypes and genotypes are required to enable a comprehensive study of a particular subtype of PanNETs. Such models would enable subtype‑specific drug screening and the development of targeted therapies.

The integration of artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning and deep learning, has advanced tumor detection, classification, and outcome prediction across various cancers [131–134]. Despite the significant promise driven by multi-omics and radiomics in refining the stratification of PanNETs, their clinical translation faces substantial challenges. One of the main drawbacks of the multi-omics approach is its critical reliance on invasive tissue acquisition, which is often scarce for small or surgically complex PanNETs. Moreover, integrating high-dimensional data requires profound computational infrastructure and complex analyses, raising concerns about reproducibility across different settings. Radiomics faces parallel challenges in model generalization and technical standardization, as the radiomic features are highly sensitive to variations in image acquisition protocols and analytical procedures, it has called into question whether a model developed in one institution can be reliably applied elsewhere.

Given the profound capability of these omics approaches to elucidate tumor heterogeneity, future efforts should focus on establishing a “Radiomic‑Omic” atlas that correlates specific imaging phenotypes with underlying molecular subtypes. This will require large‑scale, multi‑institutional studies adhering to standardized protocols for both data acquisition and analysis. The integration of radiomic and multi‑omic data could illuminate the biological mechanisms driving imaging patterns, transforming them into reliable, interpretable biomarkers for different PanNET subtypes.

Given these multifaceted limitations, a paradigm shift in risk stratification is warranted. To address these gaps, an integrated clinicopathological–molecular risk stratification framework is proposed (Fig. 4). This model conceptually combines conventional clinical parameters (e.g., tumor size, grade, and morphology variant) with key molecular alterations (e.g., MEN1 and DAXX or ATRX loss, ALT positivity, ARX/PDX1 lineage status) and emerging radiomic features. The aim is to move beyond size- and grade-based classification toward a more precise identification of biologically indolent versus aggressive tumors, thereby improving prognostic accuracy and guiding risk-adapted management strategies. Critically, this model is conceptual and requires prospective validation before clinical application as it is presented as an evidence-informed, hypothesis-generating model that synthesizes current retrospective and translational findings. Prospective, multi-institutional studies with standardized protocols are therefore urgently required to validate its predictive performance, assess its reproducibility across diverse populations, and determine its applicability in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

PanNETs exhibit significant heterogeneity in clinical behavior and molecular underpinnings, challenging current diagnostic and management guidelines. Accurate prognosis and treatment response prediction require refining existing parameters like tumor grade, size, and staging by integrating a deeper understanding of molecular frameworks, including genetic alterations and biomarkers. The limited predictive reliability of conventional systems underscores the necessity to link clinicopathological features with molecular components. Future strategies should prioritize the development of integrated radiomic-omic models with standardized protocols and predictive models to enable non-invasive risk stratification and guide personalized interventions. This includes considering surgical resection for tumors exceeding 1.5 cm with DAXX or ATRX mutations when radiomic or molecular features suggest aggressive potential, shifting from active surveillance to risk-adapted management for optimizing patient outcomes.
