Efficient Emulsifier-Free Emulsion Copolymerization of Functional Nanoparticles Using Biobased Itaconic Acid
Authors: Preeyaporn Chaiyasat, Netnapha Kamlangmak, Chutinun Thongbai, Amorn Chaiyasat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization of functional copolymer nanoparticles via a free radical mechanism using potassium persulfate (KPS) as the initiator at 70 °C under. The copolymerization involved styrene (S) with either methacrylic acid (MAA), a conventional petrochemical-derived monomer, or itaconic acid (IA), a renewable monomer derived from biomass fermentation. IA exhibited superior water solubility and functionality due to its two carboxylic acid groups per molecule, enhancing polymerization rate and particle stability. The optimal synthesis’s P­(S-IA) nanoparticles were achieved using 8 mol % IA with pH adjustment at 30 min after initiation, enabling high solid content preparation while maintaining colloidal stability. It demonstrated significantly improved performance over P­(S-MAA) and polystyrene (PS), including complete monomer conversion (∼100%) within 5 h, reduced average particle size (∼200 nm), and minimal coagulation (∼10 wt %) at 30 wt % solid content. In contrast, PS systems exhibited coagulation levels of up to 20% at only 10 wt % solid content, whereas P­(S-MAA) presented coagulation levels of up to 38% under similar conditions. These findings highlight the potential of IA-based functional nanoparticles for ion separation and environmental remediation as precursors for hybrid materials in photocatalysis.

Introduction

Emulsion polymerization via micellar nucleation is commonly used for its efficiency and particle size control, but it requires surfactants, which may remain in final products , posing issues for applications demanding high purity or biocompatibility. Emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization , addresses these concerns by eliminating surfactants, enabling clean-surfaced particles suitable for biomedical and catalytic uses. In emulsifier-free polymerization, particle formation proceeds via homogeneous nucleation. In this mechanism, a water-soluble initiator, such as potassium persulfate, ,, decomposes in water to form free radicals. These radicals react with slightly water-soluble monomers, such as styrene, − forming oligomeric chains. Once the growing chains reach a critical chain length (J critical) and become insoluble in water, they self-assemble into colloidal particles. − The hydrophilic parts orient outward, while the hydrophobic segments fold inward. Electrostatic repulsionprovided by the initiator or ionic comonomersstabilizes the particles and prevents aggregation. − By contrast, micellar nucleation requires surfactant concentrations above the CMC to form micelles. In this case, monomer molecules are solubilized within the micelles, and polymerization begins once the initiator-derived radicals form hydrophobic oligomers (Z-mers) that migrate into the micelles to continue chain growth. ,,, Despite its efficiency, this approach is less suited for applications requiring minimal surfactant residue.

In emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization, hydrophobic monomers like styrene (S) are typically copolymerized with hydrophilic, carboxylic acid–based comonomers such as acrylic acid (AA) or methacrylic acid (MAA). − These comonomers improve colloidal stability, mechanical strength, adhesion, and compatibility with water-based systems. However, they are derived from petrochemical sources, and limited environmental compatibility remains a concern. Biobased monomers like itaconic acid (IA) have gained attention in response to sustainability demands. , IA, which is produced through fermentation using agricultural feedstocks such as starch, contains two carboxylic groups and a vinyl bond. This structure enhances water solubility and provides dual functionality, making IA a promising comonomer for synthesizing functional copolymers. It is an attractive alternative to AA or methacrylic acid MAA in developing functional polymers. ,, IA-based copolymers have recently shown potential to improve polymerization kinetics and colloidal stability and enable functional performance in water-based coatings and adsorptive nanomaterials. −

In most reported systems utilizing conventional comonomers, such as MAA or AA, challenges such as coagulation, low conversion, or poor particle stability still persistparticularly under high solid content and pH-sensitive conditions. ,,, Although there is growing interest in biobased alternatives, emulsifier-free polymerization systems employing itaconic acid (IA) remain underexplored, especially under pH-controlled and high solid content conditionsboth critical for industrial scalability and long-term performance.

Therefore, in this context, we investigate the emulsifier-free emulsion copolymerization of styrene with biobased IA and compare its performance to conventional MAA and S-only systems. This study aims to (i) evaluate the effects of IA versus MAA on polymerization rate, particle size, and colloidal stability, (ii) optimize IA content and pH adjustment to minimize coagulation and enhance particle uniformity, and (iii) assess the capability of IA-containing systems to tolerate high solid content formulations. The results are intended to establish a sustainable strategy for producing surfactant-free functional nanoparticles with coatings, ion exchange, and environmental remediation applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Styrene (S) (Sigma-Aldrich; purity, 99%), methacrylic acid (MAA) (Merck; purity 99%), and itaconic acid (IA) (Sigma-Aldrich, purity, 99%) as monomers were purified by passing through a column packed with basic aluminum oxide to remove the contained inhibitors and then kept in a refrigerator before use. Potassium persulfate (KPS) (BDH; purity, 99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (Univar; purity, 99%), hexadecane (HD) (Sigma-Aldrich; purity 99%), and hydroquinone (Sigma-Aldrich; purity, 99%) were used as received. Deionized water used in all experiments was obtained from a water purification machine (Chendu Haochun Equipment, China) with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ cm.

Preparation of Copolymer Particle

Effect of Comonomer Type

To evaluate the influence of comonomer type on polymerization rate and colloidal stability, three systems were synthesized through emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization using S alone (PS), S with MAA, and S with IA. Monomers were dissolved in deionized water at uncontrolled pH conditions before being added to a round-bottom Schlenk flask. After that, the flask was closed with a silicone rubber septum and degassed using several N2/vacuum cycles, finally, the flask was filled with N2. It was placed into an oil bath at a stirring rate of 500 rpm with a magnetic stirrer at 70 °C. After 30 min, KPS solution (using water) was injected into the round-bottom Schlenk flask as an initiator. The polymerization was carried out for 24 h under N2 atmosphere. Monomer conversion, particle size, and morphology of the obtained polymer particles were studied. Characterizations

Gravimetry was employed to quantify monomer conversion. Emulsion samples (approximately 1.0 g) were extracted from the reactor and immediately transferred to an aluminum cup for triplicate weighing. The liberated liquid was evaporated in an oven at 80 °C after the addition of numerous droplets of hydroquinone solution (1 wt %). Until the desiccated polymer reached a consistent weight, the sample was dried. The monomer conversion was determined by comparing the weight of the desiccated polymer to that of the original monomer, as in eq . %Conversion=WP×Wtotal×100WE×WM 1 where W P is the polymer weight (g) after drying, W E are the polymer emulsion weight (g) before drying, W total is the total weight (g) of the emulsion before polymerization, and W M is the monomer weight in the recipe.

The weight-average diameters (d w) were determined at a 165° angle at room temperature using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Delsa Nano C, Beckman Coulter, Germany). In concentration mode, the polymer particles (approximately 6 wt %) that were extracted from the reactor were directly triplicate measured. Zeta potential was employed to quantify the surface charge. Before measurement, the particles were attenuated to approximately 2 wt % in 1.0 mM of NaCl. The number of polymer particles (Np) was calculated using the following eq . NP=WPVP·DS 2 where V P (43πr3) is the polymer particle volume (cm3), r is the weight-average particle radius (cm), D S is the PS density (1.045 g/cm3) and W P is the total mass polymer (g/mL).

The morphology of the polymer particles was observed by a Transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-1230, JEOL). The particles of ca. 1 wt % dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid and washed with several drops of deionized water. Then, it was dried at room temperature in a desiccator before observed with a TEM.

Styrene has a significantly lower solubility in water (∼0.03%) compared to MAA (∼9.0%) and IA (∼8.3%), resulting in a lower initial monomer concentration in the aqueous phase. This limits the radical propagation and nucleation rate, leading to slower polymerization. Additionally, PS particles are stabilized only by the sulfate groups (−SO4 2–) from the initiator, , which is insufficient to prevent aggregation. As a result, particle coagulation occurs, as evidenced by the high coagulation percentage (∼20%) shown in Figure , which reduces the number of polymer particles (N p). The polymerization rate in emulsion polymerization is directly proportional to N p, and this reduction negatively impacts the overall reaction rate. Furthermore, the low initial monomer concentration in the aqueous phase likely limits particle nucleation, further contributing to the decrease in N p. The P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) systems exhibited a faster polymerization rate, achieving 100% conversion within 5 h. This is attributed to the higher water solubility of MAA and IA and their ability to enhance colloidal stability through ionizable carboxyl groups, resulting in low coagulation (∼2%).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis confirmed that the synthesized polymer particles exhibited a spherical morphology in all cases, as shown in Figure a–c. The PS particles displayed a uniform size distribution (Figure a′), with an average particle diameter (d w) of approximately 260 nm at 68% conversion. In contrast, both P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) particles showed bimodal size distributions (Figure b′,c′), with larger and smaller d w of ∼270 and ∼60 nm for P­(S-MAA), and ∼320 and ∼70 nm for P­(S-IA), respectively. However, smaller particles were not clearly observed in the TEM micrograph of P­(S-MAA), while they were distinctly present in P­(S-IA). This may be attributed to the higher water solubility and stronger acidic character of IA, which contains two carboxylic acid groups per molecule, leading to enhanced ionization and increased electrostatic stabilization in the aqueous phase. , These effects can promote secondary nucleation during the early stages of polymerization under acidic conditions, resulting in a bimodal particle size distribution. Furthermore, the distinct visibility of small particles in the P­(S-IA) system suggests better colloidal stability and reduced aggregation, which aligns with prior studies on IA-induced stabilization. Although P­(S-IA) exhibited the highest initial polymerization ratereaching 77% conversion within 3 h (Figure ), compared to 49% for P­(S-MAA) and 21% for PSthe resulting d w was considerably larger, while the N p was lower than those observed for P­(S-MAA), as shown in Figure . In addition, the coagulum contents of P­(S-IA) and P­(S-MAA) prepared at 10 wt % solid content under uncontrolled pH conditions were not significantly different. This observation can be attributed to IA’s relatively lower copolymerization reactivity compared to MAA, leading to less incorporation of IA into the polymer particles during the early nucleation stage. Moreover, the incomplete ionization of IA’s carboxylic groups under the uncontrolled pH conditions (approximately pH 2.0–2.5), given its pK a 1 and pK a 2 are 3.58 and 5.55, respectively, reduced the electrostatic stabilization efficiency of the system. As a result, some secondary particles may have adsorbed onto the surfaces of larger, growing primary particles rather than forming discrete entities, ultimately leading to a lower total particle number (N p) in the P­(S-IA) system relative to P­(S-MAA).

Zeta potential at the particle surface is an important indicator of colloidal stability. Particles exhibiting zeta potential values greater than ±30 mV are generally considered electrostatically stable due to sufficient repulsive forces that prevent aggregation. , In this study, all particle systemsPS, P­(S-MAA), and P­(S-IA)showed surface zeta potentials around −30 mV at about a pH of 2 (uncontrol pH condition), indicating good baseline stability (Table ). Interestingly, despite differences in functional group content, the absolute zeta potential values of the three systems were relatively similar (−36, −38, and −28 mV for PS, P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA), respectively). The negative surface charges observed in all three polymer particles are likely attributed to the presence of sulfate ions (SO4 2–) derived from the KPS initiator. In contrast, the carboxylic acid groups of P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) are expected to remain mostly protonated under the experimental conditions, as their pK a values (5.2 for MAA and 3.58 for IA) are significantly higher than the system’s pH (∼2). To examine the presence of carboxylic acid groups on the surface of P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) particles, the emulsions of all three polymers were subjected to pH adjustments from approximately 2 to 4 and 8. Zeta potential measurements were then performed at each pH level, as summarized in Table . The results revealed the minimal change in zeta potential was observed with varying pH for PS, containing only sulfate end groups (−SO4 2–) derived from the KPS initiator. In contrast, P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) particles exhibited increasingly negative zeta potentials as pH increased due to the gradual deprotonation of carboxylic acid groups. For P­(S-MAA), no change was observed between pH 2 and pH 4, indicating minimal dissociation of the MAA carboxyl groups below their pK a (∼5.2). A more pronounced shift occurred between pH 4 and pH 8, reaching −17 mV. P­(S-IA), on the other hand, demonstrated stronger pH sensitivity. Its carboxyl groups dissociated at lower pH values due to a lower pK a. The zeta potential decreased by −9 mV between pH 2 and pH 4, and showed a more significant shift (−30 mV) at pH 8 compared to P­(S-MAA). This behavior reflects the lower pK a and the higher number of carboxyl groups in IA, contributing to greater surface charge and improved stability at elevated pH.

The experimental results indicate that incorporating functional monomers during the synthesis of PS-based particles enhances the efficiency of polymer particle formation compared to using styrene alone. Furthermore, the presence of two carboxylic acid groups in a single IA molecule results in a higher density of surface carboxyl groups in P­(S-IA) particles compared to P­(S-MAA). These additional functional groups work with the SO4 2– ions, derived from the initiator, to improve colloidal stability by preventing particle aggregation. The proposed particle formation mechanisms for all three systems are illustrated in Figure (the symbol “M” represents all monomers, including S, MAA, and IA). In the initial stage of emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization, radicals generated from the decomposition of potassium persulfate in the aqueous phase react with monomers dissolved in water. As polymer chains grow, they reach a critical chain length at which they become insoluble in water and self-assemble to form primary nuclei or “pre-particles.” This homogeneous nucleation process in the aqueous phase is a well-established characteristic of emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization. ,−

Schematic diagram of emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization of PS, P­(S-MAA), and P­(S-IA) particles. “M” denotes S, MAA, and IA. IA provides two carboxylic acid groups per molecule, resulting in higher surface charge density and improved colloidal stability compared with S or MAA systems.

Once preparticles are formed, monomer diffusion from the monomer droplets becomes the dominant growth mechanism. As polymerization proceeds inside the particles, the monomer concentration in the polymerizing particles decreases relative to that in the monomer droplets. This concentration gradient drives the continuous diffusion of monomers across the aqueous phase into the polymerizing particles, ensuring sustained particle growth throughout the reaction. IA or MAA, being more hydrophilic than styrene, readily partitions into the water phase and diffuses into the growing particles, where they preferentially localize at the particle–water interface. Their carboxylic acid groups contribute to developing negative surface charges, particularly after pH adjustment, thereby improving colloidal stability.

The differences among PS, P­(S-MAA), and P­(S-IA) particles are evident in their surface charge characteristics. PS particles acquire weak negative charges solely from sulfate end groups derived from the initiator. In contrast, P­(S-MAA) and P­(S-IA) particles exhibit higher negative surface charge densities due to ionized carboxyl groups. Notably, IA, possessing two carboxylic acid groups per molecule, imparts a higher surface charge density than MAA, resulting in superior colloidal stability and reduced coagulation, as supported by our experimental results.

Effect of IA Amount

This study investigates the influence of varying IA content (0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 mol % relative to styrene) on polymerization kinetics and particle stability. As shown in Figure a, increasing IA concentration led to a notable enhancement in the polymerization rate, including 4 mol % IA significantly improved conversion compared to the control (0 mol %), indicating that IA promotes faster initiation and particle formation due to its carboxyl functionality. However, stabilization at this concentration was insufficient during the later stages of polymer growth. Under these conditions, the stabilization efficiency was comparable to that of PS particles stabilized by SO4 2– ions. This behavior indicates that a low IA content may result in a reduced number of stable nuclei. Consequently, particle coagulation decreases the N p, lowering the polymerization rate. When the IA content was increased to 8, 12, and 16 mol %, the polymerization proceeded more rapidly, achieving higher monomer conversions during the early stages of the reaction.

All samples were measured in triplicate.

At higher IA concentrations (12 and 16 mol %), the polymerization rate was initially faster than that observed at 8 mol %, likely due to the greater availability of IA, which promoted nucleation. However, after approximately 3 h, the reaction rate dropped, as evidenced by the reduced slope of the conversion curveindicating the onset of particle coagulation. This behavior can be attributed to excessive nucleation, which generated many tiny particles that could not be sufficiently stabilized. The resulting increase in total surface area required a higher quantity of stabilizing functional groups; when these were insufficient, particle aggregation occurred. This reduced the N p, eventually decreasing the polymerization rate. In contrast, at 8 mol % IA, the conversion reached ∼99% within 5 h without a steep decrease in slope. This indicates that particle nucleation was well balanced with stabilization, allowing sufficient −SO4 2– and carboxyl groups to maintain colloidal stability as particle size increased with conversion. The role of IA concentration in minimizing coagulation is further supported by the data presented in Figure b. The proportion of coagulated mass relative to the total monomer decreased significantly from 20% at 0 mol % IA to just 2% at both 8 and 12 mol % IA. However, this value slightly increased to 4.7% at 16 mol %, suggesting that while IA enhances colloidal stability, an excessively high concentration may result in overcrowding of initial nuclei, ultimately promoting particle aggregation. The particle size progression as a function of monomer conversion, shown in Figure c, further supports this observation. Across all conditions, particle size gradually increased with monomer conversion but tended to decrease after 3 h due to particle coagulation. Notably, at 8 mol % IA, the particle size continuously increased throughout the polymerization, indicating suppressed coagulation and stable growth.

Therefore, based on the lowest degree of particle aggregation and the highest polymerization rate observed at 8 mol % IA, this concentration was optimal for the emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization of P­(S-IA). Under these conditions, the pH of the synthesis system was adjusted to alkaline to deprotonate the carboxyl groups on the P­(S-IA) particle surface, thereby enhancing colloidal stability throughout the polymerization.

Effect of Solid Content

In industrial-scale polymer production, both time and cost are critical factors. Achieving high-throughput often requires increasing the monomer concentration in the reaction system. In emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization, however, higher monomer content can lead to challenges such as particle agglomeration and reduced colloidal stability.

Based on previous results, pH influenced the ionization of carboxylic acid groups on the surface of P­(S-IA) particles. Therefore, in this step, the effect of pH on the synthesis process and colloidal stability of P­(S-IA) particles was investigated. The study was conducted by adjusting the pH to 10 before polymerization or 30 min after initiating the reaction. pH is fundamental in emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization, primarily when employed by functional comonomers like IA or MAA. They contained ionizable carboxylic acid groups, whose degree of dissociation significantly influences their solubility, migration into polymer particles, and reactivity during copolymerization. In the case of pH adjustment before the polymerization, functional monomer IA or MAA dissolved fully in the aqueous phase. Most functional monomers are not incorporated into the hydrophobic polymer cores, leading to poor stabilization and increased particle aggregation (>40% for both IA and MAA). In contrast, adjusting the pH 30 min after the polymerization begins significantly improves colloidal stability. Under the optimal condition for P­(S-IA) synthesis using 8 mol % IA, the polymerization proceeded smoothly, with minimal particle coagulationapproximately 0.5%which is notably lower than the 2% observed under uncontrolled pH conditions. The resulting polymer particles had an average diameter of approximately 200 nm with a narrow size distribution (Figure a,b). Moreover, tiny particles based on secondary nucleation were not observed, unlike in uncontrolled pH conditions. The rationale for delaying pH adjustment lies in the behavior of IA. IA remains largely undissociated during the nucleation phase by initiating the polymerization under acidic conditions (pH ∼ 2), enabling its efficient diffusion into the growing polymer particles before significant aggregation occurs. When the pH is subsequently adjusted to ∼10, the incorporated IA dissociates, introducing surface charges that enhance electrostatic stabilization and markedly improve colloidal stability. This delayed dissociation mechanism not only supports faster polymerization kinetics but also helps to minimize coagulation.

Additionally, a comparison was conducted between P­(S-IA), P­(S-MAA), and PS particles synthesized under identical conditionswith pH adjustment at 30 min after polymerizationto evaluate their coagulation behavior across various solid contents, as illustrated in Figure c. In the absence of a functional monomer, PS particles exhibited a high coagulation percentage of approximately 20%. In contrast, particles containing MAA or IA showed significantly improved stability. While the difference in coagulation was minor at 10% solid content, the advantage of IA became more pronounced at higher concentrations. At 20 and 30% solid content, P­(S-MAA) particles showed 9 and 38% coagulation levels, respectively, whereas P­(S-IA) particles demonstrated much lower values of 1 and 10%.

However, when the solid content was further increased to 40%, P­(S-IA) particles also experienced considerable coagulation (36%), indicating that IA could no longer provide sufficient stabilizationlikely due to limitations inherent in batch emulsion polymerization at such high concentrations. Nevertheless, the results clearly demonstrate that IA, a biobased monomer containing two carboxylic acid groups per molecule, offers superior stabilization efficiency compared to conventional MAA.

These findings confirm that IA-based systems not only facilitate rapid polymerization but also maintain excellent colloidal stability at elevated solid contents, making them promising candidates for high-throughput, surfactant-free polymer production processes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effective use of biobased itaconic acid (IA) as a functional comonomer in emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization of styrene. Compared to conventional methacrylic acid (MAA), IA significantly enhanced polymerization performance due to its higher hydrophilicity and dual carboxylic functionality. Through systematic optimization, the optimal formulation was found to be 8 mol % IA with pH adjustment to ∼10 at 30 min after initiation and monomer content up to 30 wt %. Under these conditions, the polymerization achieved nearly complete monomer conversion (∼100%), low coagulated mass (∼0.5–10) and uniform particle sizes (∼200 nm) with narrow size distribution. These results reflect excellent colloidal stability and efficient particle formation without surfactants. Furthermore, the IA-based system outperformed the MAA-based and styrene-only systems, especially under high solid content conditions, validating IA’s superior stabilizing efficiency. These findings provide practical guidance for designing sustainable and scalable latex synthesis processes and suggest that P­(S-IA) latexes hold strong potential for advanced applications such as environmentally friendly coatings, functional surface treatments, and adsorptive materials.
