Preliminary evaluation of beta-endorphin alone and in combination with adrenocorticotropic hormone for the diagnosis of pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction in equids
Authors: Paula Billmann, Andy E Durham, Garance Christen, Vinzenz Gerber, Josef Gross, Nathalie Fouché
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited published data exist regarding the diagnostic utility of beta-endorphin (β-END) for pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID), either compared with or in combination with adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH).Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo evaluate β-END as a potential diagnostic test for PPID in comparison with ACTH and to explore the diagnostic performance of combined tests.AnimalsNinety-eight equids aged 19-32 years, owned by the foundation “Stiftung für das Pferd,” were included.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in January 2025. Haircoat abnormalities were scored as absent, mild, or pronounced, and plasma β-END and ACTH concentrations were measured. Horses were classified based on haircoat abnormalities, with pronounced haircoat abnormalities defining the PPID group (n = 39) and absence of haircoat abnormalities defining the control group (n = 59). Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for individual and combined diagnostic tests. Previously established ACTH cut-offs were combined with study-derived maximum Youden-Index cut-offs.ResultsBeta-endorphin showed an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of 0.88 for PPID diagnosis, with a sensitivity of 76.9% and specificity of 93.2%. Corresponding AUROCs, sensitivities, and specificities were 0.87, 71.8%, and 88.1% for ACTH measured by chemiluminescent assay (CLA), and 0.83, 79.5%, and 69.5% for ACTH measured by immunofluorescent assay (IFA). Combining β-END at 115 pg/mL with ACTH (CLA) at 24.3 pg/mL yielded 88% sensitivity and 89% specificity.Conclusions and clinical importanceThese preliminary findings support further evaluation of β-END as an adjunct diagnostic test for PPID.

Introduction

Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction (PPID) is a progressive endocrine disorder, affecting 20%-25% of aged horses (>15 years), caused by degeneration of dopaminergic neurons within the hypothalamic–pituitary axis.1–6 As equine life expectancy continues to increase, PPID is encountered with greater frequency, underlining the critical need for timely recognition and accurate diagnosis to improve affected horses’ quality of life.7,8

Pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction is most consistently associated with coat abnormalities, including generalized hypertrichosis and delayed shedding, and is accompanied by elevated concentrations of pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)-derived peptides such as adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), and beta-endorphin (β-END).9–15 In clinical practice, diagnosis and monitoring rely largely on the interpretation of clinical signs alongside plasma ACTH concentrations.11,12 Several analytical techniques are validated for equine ACTH measurements, including radioimmunoassay,14 immunofluorescent assay (IFA),15 and chemiluminescent assay (CLA),16 with the CLA being the most commonly employed method for routine diagnosis of PPID.11,12

Despite its widespread use, ACTH testing does not always allow clear diagnostic classification of PPID. Clinical signs might be mild or difficult to distinguish from changes attributable to normal aging, and ACTH concentrations frequently fall within the equivocal range, thereby limiting diagnostic confidence.17–19 In such cases, the thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulation test can improve diagnostic accuracy.1,19–21 However, ACTH responses to TRH stimulation are also influenced by season, which might affect test interpretation and diagnostic performance if seasonally appropriate thresholds are not applied.22 Accordingly, current BEVA primary care guidelines emphasize the use of seasonally adjusted reference intervals and recognize the value of the TRH stimulation test for detecting early-stage PPID.7 Despite its diagnostic utility, the TRH stimulation test has practical limitations. The protocol requires the use of an unlicensed product, paired plasma samples, and strict adherence to sampling point, since ACTH concentrations change rapidly after TRH administration and deviations in timing might affect result interpretation.23 These limitations have prompted continued interest in additional biomarkers that better reflect pars intermedia activity and might complement ACTH in diagnostically challenging cases.

Since PPID originates in the pars intermedia, altered secretion of POMC-derived peptides represents a biologically plausible target for diagnostic test development. Early studies in the 1980s demonstrated changes in several of these peptides in horses with PPID, leading to subsequent investigations beyond ACTH.24–26 While α-MSH has been investigated in several studies, its lower sensitivity and limited practicability for routine, large-scale testing limit its clinical usefulness compared with ACTH.4,5,27–29 Beta-endorphin also has been examined in horses with PPID,24–26 yet its diagnostic value relative to ACTH, and its potential value when used in combination with ACTH, remain insufficiently understood.

The aim of this study was therefore to explore the diagnostic potential of β-END, both alone and in combination with ACTH, for the diagnosis of PPID. The first objective was to assess the diagnostic potential of β-END as a diagnostic test for PPID in comparison with ACTH. The second objective was to determine whether combining β-END and ACTH concentrations improves discrimination between horses with PPID and controls.

Materials and methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted in January 2025. Research was approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the Cantons of Jura and Berne, Switzerland (JU/37075/2024). Owner consent for each horse was provided by the foundation for retired equids, “Stiftung für das Pferd.” Coat abnormalities were scored by veterinarians (G.C., N.F., and P.B.) in a cohort of 113 aged horses (≥18 years) as absent, mild, or pronounced using the scoring system described in Appendix 1. After exclusion of 15 horses with mild coat abnormalities, 39 horses with pronounced and 59 horses without coat abnormalities were included. Blood samples were obtained via venipuncture. Beta-endorphin concentrations were measured using a validated ELISA kit (β-endorphin kit S-1134, BMA Biomedicals).30 Adrenocorticotropic hormone concentrations were quantified with 2 validated methods: an automated CLA (Immulite 2000 XPi, Siemens Healthineers)16 and IFA (AIA-900, Tosoh Bioscience).15 Information on grouping is shown in Figure 1; details are provided in Appendix 1.

Data analysis was performed using NCSS (NCSS 2024 Statistical Software (2024). NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA. https://www.ncss.com/software/ncss/) and RStudio (Posit team (2025). RStudio: Integrated Development Environment for R. Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA. https://posit.co/) with R (version 4.4.3; R Core Team. R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing (Vienna, Austria: R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2025). https://www.r-project.org/). Receiver operating characteristic analyses were conducted, and cut-offs were determined using the maximum Youden index. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios of β-END were compared with those of ACTH (CLA and IFA). Sensitivity and specificity corresponding to laboratory-defined ACTH cut-offs (CLA ≥ 28.2 pg/mL; IFA ≥ 24.3 pg/mL; Durham, unpublished data) were derived.

Diagnostic test combinations were assessed: (1) β-END and ACTH (CLA); (2) β-END and ACTH (IFA); and (3) ACTH (CLA) and ACTH (IFA). Predictive probabilities from logistic regression models (Data S1, Supporting Information) were subjected to ROC analyses. The CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio was calculated, and group differences were evaluated using a Mann–Whitney U test. The ratio was explored because the assays differ in cross-reactivity with ACTH fragments.31–34 The diagnostic utility of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio was assessed using ROC analysis.

Finally, ACTH (CLA and IFA) laboratory-derived cut-offs were combined with the β-END and CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio cut-off. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated using true positive (TP), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), and true negative (TN) classifications.

Results

Study population and biomarker concentrations

The PPID group included 39 horses (23 mares and 16 geldings), with ages ranging from 20 to 32 years. The most represented breed was Warmblood (n = 15) followed by Baroque breed (n = 7), Freiberger (n = 5), Icelandic horse (n = 3), Thoroughbred (n = 3), Draft horse (n = 2), Quarter horse (n = 2), Haflinger (n = 1), and Pony type (n = 1). The control group included 59 horses (26 mares and 33 geldings) with ages ranging from 19 to 32 years. The most represented breed was Warmblood (n = 22), followed by Freiberger (n = 13), Arabian horse (n = 9), Haflinger (n = 5), Baroque breed (n = 4), Pony type (n = 2), Draft horses (n = 1), Icelandic horse (n = 1), Quarter horse (n = 1), and Thoroughbred (n = 1). Descriptive statistics including medians and IQRs for age, β-END and ACTH (CLA and IFA), as well as significance of group comparisons are displayed in Table 1. Horses in the PPID group were significantly older than horses in the control group (P < .001). Beta-endorphin and ACTH (both CLA and IFA) concentrations were significantly higher in the PPID group compared to the control group (all P < .001). There was a positive correlation between loge-transformed ACTH concentrations measured by CLA (r = 0.86, P < .001) and IFA (r = 0.81, P < .001; Data S2, Supporting Information).

Evaluation of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio

A significant difference in the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio was found between the groups, with the PPID group presenting a significantly higher ratio (1.09 [median]; 0.83-1.58 [IQR]) than the clinical control group (0.78 [median]; 0.70-0.84 [IQR] P < .001; Figure 2). The ROC analysis revealed a high specificity comparable to that of β-END, whereas sensitivity was found to be lower than that of any diagnostic test alone (Table 5). Combination of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio with ACTH measured by CLA, ACTH measured by IFA and β-END classified most horses as concordantly positive or concordantly negative. The combination of CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio with ACTH measured by CLA showed high specificity but lower sensitivity, while the combination with ACTH measured by IFA showed more balanced sensitivity and specificity, but produced the highest number of discordant or intermediate results. The combination of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio with β-END showed the highest specificity among the 3 approaches, with only a few discordant or intermediate classifications (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides preliminary findings on the diagnostic performance of β-END in comparison to ACTH, evaluates whether combining diagnostic tests improves classification of PPID, and addresses the clinical relevance of assay-dependent differences in ACTH measurements.

The results of this study suggest that β-END reflects PPID-associated pituitary dysfunction and provides diagnostic information comparable to ACTH. The marked increase in β-END concentrations in PPID horses, together with the strong correlation between β-END and ACTH measured by both the CLA and IFA, supports the biological relevance of β-END in horses with PPID. Currently, no single diagnostic test allows definitive differentiation between PPID and age-related endocrine or clinical changes. Nevertheless, accurate diagnosis remains essential, as pergolide treatment is lifelong and ideally avoided in horses that are not affected. In this context, the identification of a complementary diagnostic test is of considerable clinical interest.

The value of multi-biomarker approaches is well illustrated in Parkinson’s disease, a progressive neurodegenerative disorder in which a single diagnostic test does not reliably reflect disease presence or stage. Multiple reviews emphasize that combinations of diagnostic tests provide superior and more robust diagnostic performance compared with individual markers alone.35–37 Such approaches are considered essential to address disease heterogeneity and to improve early diagnosis, staging, and prognostication. These principles are directly relevant to PPID, which similarly represents a progressive neurodegenerative condition with heterogenous clinical expression.

In this study, β-END demonstrated good ability to distinguish between diseased and non-diseased horses, as AUROC values were comparable to both ACTH assays. Furthermore β-END was characterized by high specificity and the highest positive likelihood ratio among individual diagnostic tests, whereas ACTH measured by IFA showed high sensitivity but lower specificity. This pattern suggest that β-END might be particularly useful for increasing diagnostic confidence when concentrations are above the proposed cut-off, while a negative result alone is less suitable for excluding PPID. In a study of 63 horses comparing diagnostic performance of ACTH, α-MSH, and β-END, a substantially higher β-END cut-off of 428 pg/mL was reported, yielding a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 84%.38 The marked differences from the present findings are most likely attributable to methodological factors. In that study, horses were assigned to PPID or control groups based on a dexamethasone suppression test, and β-END concentrations were measured using a radioimmunoassay.38 The diagnostic performance of ACTH has been evaluated extensively across multiple studies.17,19,39,40 In the present dataset, plasma ACTH measured by the CLA showed a sensitivity of 72% and a specificity of 88%, in agreement with the findings of a recent meta-analysis.17 These values were slightly lower than the median sensitivity (75.5%) and specificity (95.2%) reported by an earlier study.19

Given that neither ACTH nor β-END alone provide perfect diagnostic accuracy, combining biomarkers represents a logical approach to improving diagnostic classification. In the present study, most biomarker combinations did not substantially outperform β-END alone. Combinations including β-END maintained high specificity and high positive likelihood ratios but generally did not meaningfully increase AUROC or sensitivity compared with β-END alone. An exception was observed when β-END, using the proposed cut-off of ≥ 115 pg/mL, was combined with the balanced cut-off values established by a testing laboratory for ACTH (IFA) of ≥ 24.3 pg/mL (Durham, unpublished data). This combination improved the balance between sensitivity and specificity in the present cohort, suggesting that combined interpretation might be useful in selected diagnostic contexts. Although encouraging, these findings are preliminary and cohort specific. Further studies in larger and more diverse populations, ideally including horses at earlier stages of the disease, are required to determine whether this combined approach is clinically applicable and provides a reproducible diagnostic advantage.

Within the scope of this study, differences between immunoreactive ACTH concentrations measured by CLA and IFA were evaluated. Adrenocorticotropic hormone concentrations measured by CLA are generally higher than those measured by IFA.15,31,33,34 This observation was supported in the PPID group in our study, in which the median CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio was 1.09. In contrast, the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio was significantly lower in the control group, with a median value of 0.78, indicating higher ACTH concentrations measured by the IFA than by the CLA. These findings suggest that assay-dependent differences in ACTH concentrations are not random but might reflect differences in the detection of ACTH-related peptides between groups. Both the CLA and IFA detect ACTH1-39; however, the CLA cross-reacts with corticotropin-like intermediate peptide (CLIP; ACTH18-39),31–34 whereas the IFA was found to additionally detect corticotropin inhibiting peptide (ACTH7-38).31 The higher ACTH concentrations measured with the CLA in the PPID group are therefore plausibly explained by higher CLIP concentrations, which are elevated in horses with PPID.25,26 In contrast, the higher ACTH concentrations measured by IFA in the control group were unexpected and warrant further investigation, although they might be related to higher immunoreactivity of ACTH1-39 with the IFA compared to CLA.31

The marked difference in CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratios observed between the groups suggests that this assay-derived ratio might provide supplementary diagnostic information. In the present study, the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio demonstrated a high specificity (92%) alongside moderate sensitivity (64%), indicating that an increased ratio may support a diagnosis of PPID, whereas a lower ratio is less suitable for excluding PPID. The combination of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio with β-END showed the highest specificity among the evaluated ratio-based approaches, suggesting that concordant abnormalities in β-END and assay-dependent ACTH measurements might provide particularly strong diagnostic support for PPID. Because both ACTH assays are already widely available, further evaluation of the CLA/IFA [ACTH] ratio might represent a practical approach to refining ACTH-based diagnostic interpretation.

All horses included in this study were aged over 18 years and maintained under comparable management conditions allowing direct comparison between clinically normal aged horses and those classified as having PPID. As expected, horses in the PPID group exhibited significantly higher plasma concentrations of both β-END and ACTH.13

Group allocation in this study was based on subjectively assessed haircoat abnormalities, specifically generalized hypertrichosis and delayed shedding, which are regarded as pathognomonic clinical features of PPID.1,5,16 This approach was chosen deliberately to permit an unbiased comparison between β-END and ACTH, as ACTH concentrations were kept masked during classification. Nevertheless, this classification strategy represents an important limitation, because PPID diagnosis ideally integrates clinical findings with endocrine testing.7 Misclassification therefore cannot be excluded, particularly in the control group, where horses with early or subclinical PPID might have been included. Furthermore, haircoat abnormalities were assessed at a single time point during winter, when seasonal coat variation and observer interpretation might affect classification. The addition of a TRH stimulation test would likely have improved diagnostic classification; however, this was not feasible in the present cohort because of the requirement for an unlicensed product and the animal use constraints associated with repeated sampling.

The present study was conducted in a specific, well-defined study cohort of aged horses with clear clinical phenotypes and at a single time of year. A central assumption of the study design was that overt coat abnormalities reliably define a PPID group, while older horses without winter haircoat abnormalities likely do not have PPID. While this approach minimizes diagnostic uncertainty, it introduces substantial bias into both cut-off derivation and performance estimates. Consequently, the resulting cut-offs are not applicable to a general clinical population, particularly for horses with intermediate or early presentations which are the cases in which diagnostic tests are most clinically valuable. The results should therefore not be considered broadly generalizable, and clinicians should exercise caution when applying these thresholds to such horses. This is particularly relevant because the proposed cut-offs were derived and evaluated in the same data set, which might have resulted in overly optimistic estimates of diagnostic accuracy.

Seasonal and circadian influences also need to be considered. Like ACTH, β-END concentrations in horses appear to be affected by biological variation. Significantly higher β-END concentrations have been reported in horses with PPID during autumn, suggesting a seasonal effect that might influence interpretation of β-END concentrations and that season-specific cut-off values might be required, analogous to those established for ACTH.39,41 Ideally, the present cohort would have been evaluated at multiple time points, including spring and autumn, with repeated documentation of clinical signs and endocrine testing. Such an approach would have allowed a more complete assessment of the diagnostic value of β-END across different seasons and disease stages.

The diagnostic potential of β-END as a diagnostic test for PPID should be interpreted in the context of the present study design. Because PPID classification was based on pronounced haircoat abnormalities, the diagnostic performance of β-END might have been influenced by the use of a phenotype closely linked to pars intermedia dysfunction. Beta-endorphin is secreted from the pars intermedia as a proopiomelanocortin-derived peptide together with ACTH, α-MSH, and other related peptides.1 Although β-END itself is unlikely to directly contribute to hypertrichosis, increased β-END concentrations might reflect broader pars intermedia POMC dysregulation and co-secretion of peptides more directly involved in haircoat regulation, particularly α-MSH.1 This could explain why β-END might be closely associated with haircoat abnormalities, potentially more so than ACTH, without implying a causal role of β-END in coat changes. Thus, the apparent diagnostic value of β-END in the present study might partly be related to the haircoat-based case definition, and validation in horses classified independently of haircoat phenotype is required.

Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory. They do not allow firm conclusions regarding the diagnostic utility of β-END, alone or in combination with ACTH, in horses with early or mild PPID. This distinction is clinically important because diagnostic tests are most valuable before characteristic features such as generalized hypertrichosis become apparent, when the diagnosis is often less apparent.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory study shows that β-END has diagnostic performance comparable to ACTH and might provide additional diagnostic information, particularly because of its high specificity and high likelihood ratio. Selected combinations of diagnostic tests maintained a favorable diagnostic profile, although their added value over individual tests requires further validation. The findings also highlight assay-specific differences in ACTH measurement that may be clinically relevant for diagnostic interpretation. As the present results mainly reflect horses with more advanced phenotypes, they should not yet be directly implemented into clinical practice. Rather, they provide a rationale and methodological framework for future longitudinal studies aimed at validating β-END and related diagnostic approaches for detection of PPID at earlier disease stages.
