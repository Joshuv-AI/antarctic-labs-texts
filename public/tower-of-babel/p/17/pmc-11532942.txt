Transcriptome signature of juvenile Litopenaeus vannamei cultured under different salinity levels in response to Vibrio harveyi infection
Authors: Zulaikha Mat Deris, Thinh Dinh Do, Shumpei Iehata, Mhd Ikhwanuddin, Md Asaduzzaman, Cher Chien Lau, Yantao Liang, Min Wang, Yeong Yik Sung, Chao Li, Li Lian Wong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Rapid changes in abiotic and biotic factors can impose physiological stress on shrimp.•RNA-seq analysis was conducted on the hepatopancreas to assess the effects of these factors on Litopenaeus vannamei.•Shrimp maintained at 20 ppt demonstrated the highest immune response following Vibrio harveyi infection.•Both V. harveyi and salinity stress independently or in combination, activate a broad spectrum of immune and stress regulatory genes, highlighting their potential role in the development of effective prophylactic strategies for L. vannamei.

Introduction

The Pacific white shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei is the most commonly cultured shrimp species, accounting for about 50 % of total cultured crustaceans worldwide [1]. Because of its fast growth, high yield, strong environmental adaptability, and suitability for high-density farming, L. vannamei aquaculture brings high economic benefits to the local farmers and supports the development of shrimp farming in many Indo-Pacific countries [2]. In Malaysia, an estimation showed that 90 % of the shrimp production was contributed by L. vannamei [3]. However, the rapid changes in abiotic and biotic factors may result in physiological stress of L. vannamei, leading to economic losses in the shrimp farming industry [4]. Salinity is an important environmental factor related to the physiological function, growth, and survival of shrimp [5]. It is reported that L. vannamei is a wide salinity tolerant species [2]. However, rapid alteration in salinity may lead to osmotic imbalance, metabolic disorders, and suppressed immunity in L. vannamei [4,6]. In addition to abiotic stressors, L. vannamei is sensitive to biotic factors such as viral and bacterial pathogens. Vibrio spp. are among the most virulent pathogens in shrimp aquaculture [7]. Together with V. parahaemolyticus, V. harveyi is one of the main causing pathogens for Acute Hepatopancreatic Necrosis Disease (AHPND) in L. vannamei [3]. The outbreak of V. harveyi infection usually results in mass mortality of shrimp, threatening the socio-economic status of shrimp farmers. As an invertebrate, L. vannamei lacks adaptive immune system and uses innate immune system as their primary defense mechanism against disease infection [8]. Therefore, understanding the innate immune system of V. harveyi under these stressors is critical steps for improving shrimp farming.

RNA-seq is a powerful tool for the identification of genes and proteins related to stress response mechanisms in shrimp [[7], [9]]. Recently, RNA-seq has been widely applied in the discovery of differentially expressed gene under biotic and abiotic pressures in cultured shrimps. Comparative transcriptomics data from previous studies indicated that salinity stress triggers higher differential expression of genes related to osmoregulation, antioxidation, immune defense [2,7]. Meanwhile, gene expression of the shrimp infected with Vibrio spp. is closely linked with the immune system [10,11]. These findings have provided fundamental information on transcriptional responses of L. vannamei to single stressors of either salinity tolerance or pathogenic infection. However, multiple stressors may concurrently occur in practical aquaculture. Despite this fact, the understanding of L. vannamei responses to multiple stressors is still limited. Therefore, investigation of differential gene expression of L. vannamei under multiple stressors will elucidate the mechanism of transcriptional responses of shrimp in cultured conditions, leading to enhancement of aquaculture production.

Considering the importance of L. vannamei survival to multiple stressors, the present study was performed to investigate the transcriptional responses of shrimp towards salinity alteration and disease susceptibility. In the present study, L. vannamei were reared under different levels of salinity and challenged with V. harveyi. RNA-seq was then employed to generate transcriptomic data of V. harveyi infected and control groups, in which their differential expression genes were compared. The findings of this study could provide basis of regulatory genes associated with shrimp tolerance and adaptation to both biotic and abiotic pressures important for shrimp selective breeding program.

Methodology

Shrimp culture and pathogenic Vibrio harveyi challenge test

Juveniles of Litopenaeus vannamei (average of initial weight = 2 ± 0.04 g, mean ± SE) were obtained from a local shrimp farm and were acclimatized for 7 days in six different tanks (500 L) at density of 240 shrimp individuals in the hatchery of the Institute of Aquaculture Tropical and Fisheries Sciences (AKUATROP), Universiti Malaysia Terengganu (UMT). Prior to different salinity treatments, shrimp were acclimated to 5 ppt, 20 ppt and 30 ppt (as a control salinity) through a daily decrement of 1.5 ppt for a two-month cultivation period. The shrimp were fed a commercial diet (Protein: 45 % and Fat: 6 %; Cargill, USA) for three times daily based on 10 % of their body weight. To maintain good water quality, the feed leftover was removed once in a day using sterile syphoning method, while 50 % of the shrimp culture water were changed once in every three days. Water quality parameters were recorded twice a week and maintained at a temperature of 26–28 °C, pH 7.5–7.9, dissolved oxygen of 4.8–6.4 mg L−1, and total ammonia nitrogen of < 0.02 mg L−1.

Pathogenic Vibrio harveyi (BB120) strains was obtained from the Institute of Marine Biotechnology (IMB), UMT. Pathogenic V. harveryi was cultured on tryptic soy agar (TSA; Miller) with 1.5 % sodium chloride (NaCl) for 24 hour (h) at 35 °C. Bacterial colonies were then grown to stationary phase at 35 °C for 24 h in tryptic soy broth (TSB) containing 1.5 % NaCl. After being harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 x g for 15 min, V. harveyi cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then resuspended with the rearing water of L. vannamei. Prior to pathogenic challenge test, the growth curve, pathogenicity test and species confirmation were conducted on V. harveyi. The density of V. harveyi was determined using spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Each salinity treatments were divided into two groups, notably the control/non-infected (C) and V. harveyi infected groups (VH), each consisting of three replicates (n = 3). The pathogenic challenge test used approximately 2.7 x 107 CFU mL-1 pure culture of V. harveyi which was selected based on our preliminary test which is determine the pathogenicity of V. harveyi based on virulent gene and haemolysin test (unpublished data). The immersion method was applied to mimic the natural bacterial infection route [12], where L. vannamei individuals (VH group) were immersed in 500 L of culture water containing 50 mL of 2.7 x 107 CFU mL-1 of the bacterial suspension for 1.5 h before they were transferred back to experimental tanks. Shrimp survivability was recorded, and moribund shrimp individuals were collected in triplicates following pathogenic challenge test prior to RNAseq sequencing analysis. The shrimp was aseptically dissected to extract hepatopancreas which were preserved in RNAlater until total RNA extraction. Although no mortality were recorded, passive behaviour such as slow swimming behaviour, gasping for air and lethargy were observed in the infected shrimp. All the experimental procedures followed the guidelines approved by the Biosecurity and Ethic Committee of Universiti Malaysia Terengganu.

Total RNA extraction and RNA sequencing

Total RNA of shrimp hepatopancreas was extracted by using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of the total RNA were quantified using A260/280 nm ratio by Biodrop (BioDrop, UK) and 1 % denatured electrophoresis gel was used to analyse the total RNA integrity before RNA sequencing. Prior to library preparation for RNA sequencing, the integrity and quantification of total RNA were assessed using RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). To prepare the cDNA libraries, a total amount of 1 μg high quality total RNA per sample was used as an input material for the RNA sample preparation and three replicates of each treatment group were used for sequencing (RNA pools, i.e., 5VH, 5C, 20VH, 20C, 30VH and 30C). Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following manufacturer's recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X) or by using sonication with Diagenode bioruptor Pico for breaking RNA strands. First strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (RNase H-). Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease or polymerase activities. After adenylation of 3′ ends of DNA fragments, NEBNext Adaptor with hairpin loop structure were ligated to prepare for hybridization. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 150 bp∼ 200 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then 3 μL USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min followed by 5 min at 95 °C before PCR. PCR was then performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers and Index (X) Primer. PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality was assessed on Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a Bot Cluster Generation System using PE Cluster Kit Bot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform and paired-end reads were generated. All the sequences data were submitted to GenBank under accession numbers PRJNA953667.

Assembly, functional annotation, differentially expressed genes (DEGs), GO and Kegg pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

Raw reads generated using the Illumina platform were firstly processed with fastp to remove adaptors, ambiguous sequences, and low-quality reads [13]. Following this, the clean reads were aligned with reference genome (GCF_003789085.1_ASM378908v1) using HISAT2 software to produce unigenes using Stringtie. gffcompare was used to compare Strintie assemblies to reference annotation files [14].

The following is the downstream pipeline applied specifically for pooled samples in this study. The gene abundance was quantified in RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) by featureCounts [15]. The R package, edgeR was used to conduct differential gene expression analysis [16]. edgeR is designed to prioritize sensitivity in detecting true differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by minimizing the False Discovery Rate (FDR). It is also well-suited for experiments involving pooled samples, as it effectively identifies all potential candidate DEGs [[16], [17], [18]]. The read counts of all libraries were adjusted to Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) in edgeR. Comparative analysis of two conditions was performed and the p-value were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg methods [19]. Corrected p-value of 0.005 and |log2(Fold Change)| of 1 were set as the threshold for significant differential gene expression. The principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using RStudio software 4.2.1 to observe the correlation between the samples. The DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis by using clusterProfiler, with cutoff value set at corrected adjusted p-value (p-adj) ≤0.05 [20].

Validation of differentially expressed genes by qPCR

To validate the RNA sequencing data, approximately 100 ng of total RNA were reverse transcribed into cDNA using ReverTra Ace™ qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Japan). 10 qPCR primers which were designed using PrimerQuest (https://sg.idtdna.com) are listed as in Table S1. qPCR was performed on a Mx3005P QPCR System (Agilent Technologies, USA). To test the efficiency of the primers, a standard curve was generated using 10-fold serial dilutions of cDNA for all targeted and housekeeping genes before proceed with qPCR validation. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 2x of SensiFAST SYBR Lo-ROX mix (Bioline, USA), 400 nM forward and reverse primers, 2 μL of cDNA template, and 6.4 μl of sterile ultrapure DNase/RNase-free water (Life Technologies, USA). qPCR was performed with initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 53 °C for 11 s and 72 °C for 19 s, then continuous heating at 55 °C to 95 °C for melting curve analysis to verify the specific amplification of a single PCR amplicon in the qPCR reactions.

Expression level of each targeted gene was normalized according to the expression of the EL gene (housekeeping gene) for 3 biological samples (3 individual shrimp) with triplicates of technical samples for each treatment. The transcript levels of each targeted gene for control and treatment groups were compared and converted to relative fold changes by 2–∆∆Ct method [[21], [22]]. Data statistical significance were estimated by using t-test in SPSS v.25, which flagged at p-value < 0.05.

Results

Transcriptome sequencing and quality assessment

Illumina high-throughput sequencing was performed on the hepatopancreas RNA of both Vibrio harveyi infected and non-infected groups of L. vannamei cultivated under different salinities (Table 1). A total of 3.38×108 raw reads with an average length of 100 bp were generated from each groups. After removing the raw reads with adapter sequences, ambiguous nucleotides, and contamination, a total of 1.36×108 clean reads were contained for each library, with GC content of 45.8–47.6 %, and these included 5C, 20C and 30C (C group) reads for the three 2.04×107, 2.18×107 and 2.37×107 libraries and 2.28×107, 2.69×107,and 2.07×107 reads for the three 5VH, 20VH, and 30VH (eg: VH group) libraries, respectively. The Phred score of 30 (Q30) exceeded 92.8 %, indicating that the sequencing data are of high quality. Identification and analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

To identify the DEGs involved in the V. harveyi infection response in L. vannamei cultured under three salinity levels, we performed detailed analysis of the up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs of the three pairwise comparison groups (5VH vs 5C, 20VH vs 20C, and 30VH vs 30C). A total of 5,725 DEGs were identified in 5C versus 5VH, of which 2,443 DEGs were up-regulated and 3,282 DEGs were down-regulated (Fig. 1). When the control groups were compared with the V. harveyi infected groups, 3,643 and 1,560 DEGs were detected in 20 ppt (20C and 30VH) and 30 ppt (30C and 30VH), including 1,346 and 2,297 up-regulated genes, and 761 and 799 down-regulated genes, respectively (Fig. 3). 8,530 common DEGs were found in all pairwise comparisons of the V. harveyi infected groups, and 1,351 DEGs were not common among the analyses as shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. 2). Validation of RNA-Seq data by real-time PCR quantification

To verify the reliability of RNA-Seq data, we designed specific primers to quantify the expression level of 10 DEGs in L. vanammei hepatopancreatic tissues via qPCR. The qPCR result demonstrated that the expression levels of all tested DEGs were comparable to the RNA-Seq quantification (Figures S1, S2, and S3), indicating that our sequencing data analysis is valid. Our qPCR data also demonstrated that genes involved in immunity were up-regulated in shrimp after infection with V. harveyi. These genes which are associated with pathogenic Vibrio spp. included: Leucine (Leu), c-type lectin (c-t), von-willebrand factor (Von), perlucin-like protein (Per), serine/theonine phosphate 6 regulator (ST), calpain 5 (Cal), ADP-ribosylation factor like (ADP), carboxypeptidase B (Car), mucin-5AC-like (MU), and pancreatic lipase-related protein 2 (PL). PCA analysis was performed with the normalized counts to investigate the clustering patterns of the experimental groups. The parameters of the first and second principal components were 0.64 and 0.22, respectively. PC1 revealed 64 % variability in the data, while PC2 revealed 22 % differential clustering (Fig. 9). Our PCA analysis indicated a clear difference between the transcriptome profiles of V. harveyi infected (VH) and control (C) groups of L. vannamei hepatopancreas, given that these groups were grouped in distinct clusters. However, 30C was clustered together with the infected groups, instead of grouping together with the other control subgroups. In fact, considerable transcriptome variation can also be observed when the control subgroups of 5C fell in the positive direction of both PC1 and PC2, and 20C fell in the negative direction of the PC2 axis. Nevertheless, our results indicated that the transcriptome data are of high quality, as the infected subgroups were clustered together accordingly, despite the separate clustering of the control subgroups. To determine the expression dynamic patterns of the experiment groups, a correlation heat map based on the abundance of DEGs in the respective groups was drawn. Discussion

Salinity is a key ecological variable that shrimp have to cope with throughout its life cycle in the coastal and estuarine zones, considering that most metabolic and physiological processes require stable osmotic-ionic environment [23]. Shrimp possess a vital organ, hepatopancreas, which connect both metabolic and immune functions such as energy metabolism, digestive enzyme synthesis, nutrient absorption and detoxication [24,25]. Notably, salinity fluctuations have been known to suppress physiological changes, growth and immune system of shrimp, leading to an increased susceptibility of L. vannamei to pathogens [[26], [27], [28]]. Physiological adaption of shrimp towards varying salinities is directly linked to osmoregulatory process which have cost large amount of metabolic energy [29]. Numerous studies have documented that osmoregulation processes is initiated as the first response to maintain intracellular homeostasis for optimal survival and growth of shrimp exposed to salinity changes [[30], [31], [32]]. On the other hand, innate immunity and stress regulation act as the key reaction towards pathogenic infection in shrimp challenged with different salinity stress. In the present study, we analyzed the DEGs and metabolic pathways involved in osmoregulation and transport activity, immune and stress regulation, energy metabolism, growth, chitin binding, gonadal development, and metal ion binding and toxicity, unique to each salinity level and infected treatments to explore the potential mechanisms of different salinity on the immune response to V. harveyi infections. Our DEG, GO and KEGG data showed strong interaction of the hepatopancreas with metabolism and immune systems, as a collective response against V. harveyi infection and long term salinity stress (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7).

Osmoregulation and transport activity

Salinity fluctuations disrupt ion exchange process between shrimp haemolymph and ambient environment, urging shrimp to regulate osmolyte concentrations via two main osmotic control mechanisms. Anisosmotic extracellular regulation (AER) is important for osmolality maintenance of body fluid, active ion transportation and adaptive water permeability, while intracellular isosmotic regulation (IIR) regulates amino acids as osmolytes [33,34]. Ion exchange of various osmotic balances such as ion symporters, ion transporters/cotransporters, and transporter protein are not only between intracellular and extracellular regions, but also between various organelles and cytoplasm in cells [[32], [35], [36], [37]]. Conversely, biosynthesis or degradation of amino acids modifies the intracellular osmotic pressure [38,39]. Overall, the majority of genes related to ion transport proteins and transport related enzymes were up-regulated, proving that ion channels and free amino acids are essential osmotic effectors in L. vannamei [40,41]. One of the most important transport-related enzymes which was significantly upregulated at low salinity (5 ppt) in this study is carbonic anhydrase X6, which enhances the supply of counterions for NaCl uptake and catalyses the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide [42,43]. In addition, the significant expression of ABC transporter G in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt, indicated its important role in transporting biological molecules, specifically cholesterol across the cell membrane using ATP [44]. ABC transporters have been documented to be involved in numerous biological processes such as osmoregulation, nutrient transport, multidrug resistance transportation and antigen presentation [45,46]. In particular, voltage-dependent anion-selective channels is highly associated with the hepatopancreas [44]. In the present study, voltage-dependent l-type calcium channel subunit beta-2-like is significantly upregulated at 30 ppt (Table 2), to mediate calcium exchange between the mitochondria (MT) and cytoplasm [47]. Ion transporter like sodium-dependent nutrient amino acid transporter 1 were differentially expressed among the shrimp groups of different salinities (Table 2). This ion transporter facilitates the transport of amino acids to the cell in a sodium-dependent manner [48,49]. It is presumed that the Na+/K+-ATPase and total ATPase activities were higher in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt than at 30 ppt, although the expression of solute carrier family 23 and solute carrier organic anion transporter family 5 showed contradictory pattern which was also observed in other study [50].

Immunity and stress regulation

The degree of damage caused by pathogens largely rely on the host's immunity [51,52]. Litopenaeus vannamei, solely depend on innate immunity as defence mechanism in response to pathogens [34], showed higher growth when maintained at salinity closest to their isosmotic point (30 ppt) than 20 ppt, but exhibit highest susceptibility to V. harveyi at the same salinity level. The DEGs and pathways related to these mechanisms corroborated with those reported in other studies [[39], [53], [54], [55]]. It is notable that phagocytosis, endocytosis, apoptosis, nodule formation, and encapsulation of pathogens have been highlighted as integral parts of immunity response to pathogen infection in shrimp [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. We observed immune-related genes involved in phagocytic activity and c-type lectin were up-regulated and down-regulated, respectively in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt after exposure to V. harveyi infection (Table 2). In particular, the immunity level was highest in shrimp cultured at 20 ppt, followed by 5 ppt and 30 ppt, as reflected by the highest number of down-regulated immune-related genes detected in shrimp reared at 30 ppt and enrichment of cellular immunity related proteins (lysosome, phagosome, endocytic) at 5 ppt (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Similar pattern was also found in L. vannamei cultured at lower salinity which exhibited lower immune parameters with decreased resistance towards Vibrio infection [[60], [61]]. Both salinity levels (5 and 30 ppt) lead to significant decline of immune activity factors through intercellular signalling pathways, such as c-type lectin, resulting in the inhibition of innate immune responses such as phagocytosis and stress regulation processes. This result also observed in previous studies, highlighted that salinity changes impacted haemolymph osmosis, induced degradation of plasma membrane, inhibited cytoskeleton modification and intracellular ion transport which further suppress cell viability and phagocytic activities in L. vannamei [[61], [62]].

Increasing evidences have revealed the critical roles of phagocytosis in shrimp cellular homeostasis maintenance and immune response by the uptake and degradation of infectious pathogens [[63], [64], [65]]. The cascade of phagocytosis is initiated by recognition and binding of a target particle to the cell surface receptors, followed by activation of diverse signalling pathways, cytoskeleton modification, membrane invagination, coated vesicle formation, directed intracellular vesicular transport, fusion of phagosome with endosome and lysosome to form phagocytic cup, and finally destruction of engulfed target by low pH, hydrolysis, and radical formation [66,67]. Following engulfment and internalization of targeted particles, phagosomes are formed and move through early to late endocytic membrane compartments to construct the phago-lysosomal pathway [68]. The enhancement of phagocytic activity could effectively inhibit pathogen infection in L. vannamei, resulting in significant reduction of mortality which subsequently enhances haemocytic phagocytosis that protects our shrimp from V. harveyi infection [69,70]. In our study, the significant up-regulation of glutamate receptor 3 coupled with G-protein in shrimp reared 20 ppt further emphasized its key function in antibacterial immunity against V. harveyi invasion by triggering the downstream phagocytosis response [69].

Lysosomes is essential in the metabolic homeostasis maintenance via removal of cellular material through endocytic and autophagic processes [71]. The expression of NPC intracellular cholesterol transporter 2 (NPC2) was significantly up-regulated in shrimp reared at 5 ppt post V. harveyi infection, which act in concert with NPC1 to transport intracellular cholesterol and other lipids (such as sphingolipids) from the lysosomal compartment [72]. NPC2 is a small soluble glycoprotein which strongly binds to cholesterol and other sterols [73]. Additionally, the expression of tropomysin in the present study might induced the interaction between actin and myosin (Table 2), resulting in the formation of actin filaments in striated muscles in shrimp that promoted cytokinesis of immune cells and antigen formation against pathogen invasion [74].

Under both abiotic (salinity) and biotic (V. harveyi) challenge, we observed significant differential expression of stress regulating genes between the shrimp groups (Table 2). The release of stress hormones is believed to be a proto-stress response [75]. The expression of c-type lectin 2 in the hepatopancreas of shrimp cultured at 5 ppt were found to be significantly lower after V. harveyi infection (Table 2). The low salinity stress may likely suppress the antibacterial activity and the hemagglutinating activity by inhibiting c-type lectin gene expressions in the shrimp [[61], [76]]. Our KEGG and GO analysis have also demonstrated that V. harveyi infection has significantly induced the expression of antioxidant heat shock 70 kDa protein (HSP70) to suppress the physiological stress inflicted by this pathogen at shrimp cultured 30 ppt (30VH) (Figs. 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8). Together with epsin 2 of the endocytosis pathway, HSP70 facilitates the release and recycle of clathrin to form clathrin-coated endosome for membrane fusion and invagiantions, followed by transportation of exogenous cargos to the subcellular sites [[77], [78], [79]]. Moreover, HSP has also been reported to play integral role in modulating the expression of virulence associate-LPS biosynthesis of GDP-mannose dehydratase in shrimp exposed to low salinity stress (5 ppt). The induced GDP-mannose dehydratase triggered the expression of brain protein I3 that further induce the expression of tumor necrosis factor -alpha (TNF) for apoptosis/ cell death process, as a cellular immune response towards V. harveyi infection [80,11]. Our result also showed that ribosomes were the most strongly induced organelles by low salinity, with six ribosomal protein related genes significantly up-regulated in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt (Table 2). This indicated that hypoosmotic rearing condition could lead to metabolic changes by stimulating protein synthesis to supply cytokines and immune factors in response to low salinity [[54], [81], [82]].

Energy metabolism

When shrimp detects changes in the ambient salinity or pathogenic infection, signal transduction network will be activated, in which signals are sent to osmo-effectors in shrimp to acclimatize themselves to salinity change and shrimp requires extra energy to modulate and stimulate ion transport [[32], [43], [83], [84]]. In the present study, the additional energy of L. vannamei at lower salinity level (5 ppt and 20 ppt) might derive from the high efficient metabolism of nutrient substance [85]. In the metabolic-related cluster (osmo-effectors), we observed that hepatopancreas might acts as the centre for carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolic processes, purine metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis and other metabolic processes (Table 2).

Pathogens exploit host cellular metabolic machinery to maintain their life cycles by reprogramming carbohydrate various aspects of host central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis, glutaminolysis and lipid synthesis [[86], [87]]. Carbohydrate metabolism pathways such as those glycolysis/glucongeogenesis, and glycosaminoglycan are complex biochemical process that involves synthesis, breakdown and interconversion of carbohydrates that provide extra energy for growth and alleviation of osmotic stress in shrimp exposed to stress conditions, such as salinity and pathogenic challenge (Fig. 8 and Table 2) [[88], [89], [90]]. When shrimp are exposed to these stressors, glycolysis pathway is also induced with increased glucose intake to obtain energy [86,91,92]. Under stress condition, shrimp tend to utilize protein as an energy source metabolized by gluconeogenesis pathway to maintain osmotic pressure and reduce the loss of amino acids from muscle during salinity acclimation to promote shrimp growth [[27], [42]]. In fact, protein synthesis is increased in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt and 20 ppt, with the increased production of ATP and decreased consumption of ATP (Table 2), which is reflected by the increased expression of mitochondrial import inner membrane translocase subunit TIM44-like [93]. Low salinity (5 ppt) reduces salt diffusion from shrimp cells to the external environment, resulting in swollen cells [82]. Given that large changes in the cell volume could disrupt normal cell function or lead to cell death, shrimp regulate the concentration of intracellular osmotic level by adjusting the intracellular pool of amino acid compounds [94,95]. Under low salinity stress the dramatic increased in biosynthesis of free amino acids and the decreased fatty acids metabolism are both involved in osmoregulation of L. vannamei [[32], [50]].

While lipid metabolism play an indispensable role in osmoregulation [[50], [86]], sphingolipids function to allow salt transport and protect cell surfaces against harmful environmental factors including pathogen by maintaining the mechanical and stability and chemical resistance of the plasma membrane lipid [96,97]. Ceramide synthase 2-like was significantly up-regulated in the sphingolipid biosynthesis pathway when shrimp were cultured at 5 ppt and 20 ppt and exposed to V. harveyi infection. Likewise, O-acyltransferase which stimulate lipolysis capacity and catalyze lipid synthesis by converting diacylglycerol and fatty acyl CoA to triacylglycerol [98], was also significantly upregulated in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt and 20 ppt. Similar to Li et al., [43], the enrichment of aminoglycan metabolic process in low-salinity ambient osmoregulation (5 ppt) may likely aim to improve osmoregulation capacity through increased enzymatic activity or modification of gill permeability. The total consumed lipid was higher for osmotic regulation at low salinities [[98], [99]].

Growth

Considering that the isosmotic point of the body fluid in L. vannamei is approximately 718 mOsm kg-1, this euryhaline species is reported to achieve optimum survival and growth rate at salinity above 20 ppt [[26], [30], [100], [101]]. In the present study, L. vannamei cultured at 30 ppt was found to exhibit the highest growth gene expression compared to those cultured at 5 ppt, suggesting 30 ppt is closest to the shrimp hemolymph isotonic point, while 5 ppt is stressful for the shrimp [[6], [26]]. As salinity changes, the deviation from the isosmotic point in the shrimp body fluid can cause adverse effects on their growth performance [39]. At isosmotic deviated salinity levels such as 5 ppt in the present study or hypersaline condition in previous studies [38,102], a large proportion of metabolic energy may be required for osmoregulation, leaving less energy reserved for shrimp growth [[6], [50], [54]].

Chitin binding

Enrichment of chitin binding protein in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt suggested that this salinity level promote the molting of shrimp which increase the susceptibility of the shrimp towards V. harveyi infection. Chitin binding protein (CBD) is the main component in the formation of new epidermis in shrimp, particularly during molting stage [[103], [104], [105]]. In fact, consistent with those documented in shrimp infected with WSSV, CBD in the present study may likely be involved in the V. harveyi infection, by interacting with V. harveyi and mediated the invasion process [106]. The high expression of chitinase 10 in shrimp cultured at 20 ppt, has increased the antibacterial defence function of shrimp, regulating their innate immune responses towards V. harveyi infection [[11], [107], [108]]. Conversely, chitinase was found to be lowly expressed in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt, indicating that long term low salinity stress inhibits expression of chitinase, resulting in immunodepression in shrimp [[93], [107]]. Chitinase is a key enzyme which digest chitin for the growth, molting and immune function of shrimp [44]. Given that molting is deeply affected by salinity, chitinase is assumed to be closely associated with salinity [107,108].

Gonadal development

Overspent of energy for osmoregulation following salinity stress may adversely affect gonadal development in shrimp [[76], [109]]. In fact, previous study showed that salinity closest to the isosmotic point of crustacean may accelerate gonadal development due to reduction of energy consumption on osmoregulation, resulting in more nutrients being directed to gonadal development [110,111]. It was also reported that some ions such as calcium which are present in brackish water could stimulate their gonadal development [[109], [112]]. The significant expression of Apolipoprotein d-like in shrimp cultured in 30 ppt, has further proved that shrimp cultured near their isosmotic point (brackish water) could increase the binding of cholesterol, an important precursor for the biosynthesis of gonadal development related hormones [113,114]. However, the expression of most gonadal development genes were significantly down-regulated in shrimp cultured at 20 ppt (Table 2). These findings were also congruent with previous studies indicating that gonadal development genes of L. vannamei such as vitellogenin, Dmc1, proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), cyclophilin A and shrimp ovarian peritrophin (SOP) was significantly inhibited at this salinity ambient [76].

Metal ion binding and toxicity

Salinity affects the physiology of the L. vannamei, particularly their osmoregulatory processes, altering the ion exchange mechanism including metals [32]. This is due to the fact that water chemistry can greatly modify the bioavailability and the toxicity of a metal [115]. Euryhaline shrimp are more sensitive to metal bioavailability and toxicity at a lower salinity than a salinity closer to their isosmotic point [[115], [116]]. This negative co-relationship between salinity and metal toxicity have also been observed in our study, where the expression of metal ion binding genes were significantly upregulated in shrimp cultured at 5 ppt (Table 2) which is also consistent with those found in other crustacean such as blue crab Callinectes sapidus and mudflat fiddler crab Minuca rapax [117,118].

Advantages and limitations of pooled samples prior to RNA sequencing

In this study, raw samples were pooled prior to total RNA extraction and subsequent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Pooling samples before RNA-seq has been employed in previous research, demonstrating that this method does not compromise the ability to detect differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [119]. Furthermore, pooling can reduce within-group variability, enhancing the detection of biological effects even with smaller sample sizes. It also mitigates noise associated with low-abundance genes [18]. Additionally, this approach offers cost efficiency in sequencing, which is particularly beneficial for researchers facing budget constraints [[119], [120]].

However, using pooled RNA samples can pose limitations, such as the loss of information regarding individual variability in gene expression [119]. This issue can be addressed by validating the expression of targeted genes using quantitative PCR (qPCR). As the gold standard for gene expression analysis, qPCR provides detailed information on gene expression levels across individual samples and allows for statistical comparisons [[119], [121]]. Previous studies have successfully applied sample pooling in RNA-seq to investigate DEGs in crustaceans, including shrimp, crabs, and mollusks [[122], [123], [124], [125], [126]]. Therefore, pooling RNA samples is a scientifically valid approach that significantly reduces costs associated with library preparation and sequencing.

Conclusion

This is the first study on the comparative transcriptome analysis of Litopenaues vannamei in response to abiotic (salinity) and biotic (pathogen) challenge. We performed RNA sequencing analysis and detected differential expression genes in the hepatopancreas of L. vannamei cultured at 3 different salinities (5 ppt, 20 ppt, 30 ppt) and exposed them to V. harveyi infection. Higher differential expression was found in genes related to osmoregulation and transport activity, immune and stress regulation, nutrient metabolism, growth, chitin binding, gonadal development, and metal ion binding and toxicity, and these might therefore be involved in the integrated salinity tolerance and pathogen resistance mechanism of this species. Overall, shrimp reared at 30 ppt have the fastest growth and gonadal development rate, but are highly susceptible to disease infection. Both V. harveyi (biotic) and salinity (abiotic) stressors work in concert or separately in activating both immune and stress regulator genes, by accelerating the expression of a series of immune genes in 30VH shrimp in response to pathogenic challenge. On the other hand, shrimp reared at 5 ppt are prone to slow growth and gonadal development, osmotic imbalance, metal toxicity, and high degree of immunodepression. Genes in the osmoregulation and transport activity pathways, work together to maintain osmotic balance in the 5VH shrimp. Low ambient salinity (5 ppt) can augment the bioavailability and toxicity of free ionic metal concentration and also promote the enrichment of chitinase for molting processes in shrimp, leading to increased susceptibility of shrimp towards V. harveyi. Shrimp cultured at 20 ppt were observed to have the highest immunity level, but are deemed to possess intermediate levels of growth, osmoregulation and nutrient metabolism genes. Therefore, providing ample energy by nutritional manipulation such as increasing lipid and carbohydrate composition in shrimp diet maybe the key approach in improving the growth performance and physiological fitness of L. vannamei cultured at 20 ppt. With this, culturing shrimp at 20 ppt maybe a potentially beneficial approach in striking the profitable balance of faster shrimp growth with higher susceptibility towards disease outbreak, particularly Vibrio causative diseases. Collectively, our study may provide valuable high-quality transcriptome and empirical data that will facilitate future molecular research on the salinity and pathogenic stress adaptation of shrimp. All the up-regulated protein-coding genes could be potential candidate markers for future functional studies including RNAi and knockdown analyses to develop efficient prophylactic management strategies for L. vannamei sustainable production.
