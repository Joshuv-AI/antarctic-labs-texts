Are rapid diagnostic tests reliable for the detection of malaria in the emergency department?
Authors: Chloé Moran, Saoirse Coyne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA short systematic review assessed the diagnostic accuracy of malaria rapid diagnostic tests in febrile adults from malaria-endemic regions. A database search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane and Google Scholar returned 138 papers. Six papers met the inclusion criteria and were included in our analysis.

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) showed high sensitivity for Plasmodium falciparum detection, ranging from 75% (95% CI 56.6% to 88.5%) to 100% (95% CI 95.1% to 100%), with specificities of 96.6%–99.4% (95% CI 98.9% to 99.8%). For non-falciparum malaria, RDT sensitivity was as low as 58.3%, but specificity remained consistently high at 99.7%–100%.

The key characteristics of each study are summarised in table 1. Collectively, the evidence indicates that RDTs enable prompt bedside detection of malaria, facilitating earlier diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment. However, their lower sensitivity for non-falciparum species increases the risk of false-negative results in these specific cases.

Results

P. falciparum, Plasmodium falciparum; RDT, rapid diagnostic test.

Clinical scenario

A patient presents to the emergency department with fever and myalgia 5 days after returning from a malaria-endemic region of Nigeria. They have no past medical history. An RDT for malaria is performed at triage and is negative. You wonder whether malaria can be excluded on this basis, or whether further expert analysis is still required.

Three-part question

In (patients who have been exposed to malaria) are (malaria RDTs) acceptable for (the detection of malaria in the emergency department)?

Search strategy

EMBASE and MEDLINE were searched using the Ovid interface. The results were limited to adult participants only and papers in English. The following search strategy was used:

(exp Malaria/ OR exp Plasmodium/ OR malari$.mp. OR (malaria* or plasmodium or falciparum or vivax).mp.) AND (exp Rapid Diagnostic Test$/ OR exp Point of Care Test$/ OR (rapid diagnostic test* or RDT* or antigen test or immunochromatographic test* or point of care).mp.) AND (exp Emergency Department/OR (emergency hospital or emergency room or ED or emergency service).mp.) AND (exp Adult$/)

Duplicates were removed using Ovid’s ‘Remove Duplicates’ function. An additional search using Google Scholar was conducted, using keywords from the Ovid search. No date or language filters were used.

Search outcome

Overall, 138 papers were identified using our search strategy. 23 of these papers were identified through the supplementary Google Scholar search. 124 of the papers were excluded on abstract review as they were irrelevant to our three-part question. Case reports, reviews, letters and conference abstracts were excluded. 14 papers underwent full-text review. Eight papers were further excluded—seven did not address our question, and one was a preprint. Six papers were used in the final analysis, which included three retrospective cohort studies and three prospective cohort studies. The results are summarised in table 1.

Comments

Our search returned six studies relevant to our three-part question.1–6 These studies included patients from malaria-endemic regions presenting to the emergency department.1–6

The studies demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy of RDTs for P. falciparum, the species associated with the highest mortality.1 2 4–6 Reported sensitivities for detection of P. falciparum ranged from 75% to 100%, with specificities ranging from 96.6% to 99.4%.1 2 4–6 RDT performance was notably poorer for the detection of non-falciparum malaria species; sensitivity for non-falciparum malaria ranged from 58.3% to 99.9%, although specificity remained high at approximately 99.5%–100%.1 2 Clinicians working in non-falciparum endemic regions should therefore exercise more caution when using RDTs.

A recent, large retrospective cohort study supports RDTs as a reliable adjunct to microscopy, especially where microscopy may be delayed.4 Nevertheless, initial RDT-based diagnostic strategies reduce the time to diagnosis and treatment initiation in suspected malaria cases.4

Clinical bottom line

Emergency medicine clinicians should use RDTs as the first-line diagnostic test for suspected malaria. A positive RDT should prompt immediate treatment. However, due to reduced sensitivity for non-falciparum species, RDTs must not replace microscopy. Expert microscopy is required to confirm infection and identify species. Treatment should not be delayed while awaiting results. A negative RDT does not exclude malaria, and microscopy is still indicated where clinical suspicion remains.
