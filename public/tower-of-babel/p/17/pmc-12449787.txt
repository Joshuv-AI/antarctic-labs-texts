An Enigma of N-termini dependent protein degradation
Authors: Lina Alhourani, Alaa M. Elgohary, Richard P. Fahlman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The N-Degron rules that target proteins for degradation via their N-terminal sequences has expanded to encompass most N-terminal sequences. These destabilizing N-termini include many sequence combinations with the initiator methionine intact, including the recently reported N-terminal methionine followed by a basic residue. Despite the diverse sequences reported for N-Degron recognition and degradation system wide proteomic analysis has currently not observed these rules with endogenous proteins. Here we report on these apparent inconsistencies by validating the degradation of reporter proteins with N-terminal MK- and MR-sequences and also investigating global endogenous protein turnover by proteomics. In addition to verifying the reported degradation of proteins with MK- and MR- N-termini we have also identified an additional sequence dependency where an acidic residue following the basic residue inhibits protein degradation. Global protein degradation analysis using a metabolic labelling approach with azidohomoalanine failed to observe trends in cytoplasmic protein stability that correlates to the N-terminal sequence. Together we have been able to reproduce the apparent contradictory results reported using different methodologies. This included the use of different recombinant protein reporters to investigate MK- and MR- N-termini dependent protein degradation and an alternative proteomic method to quantify global protein degradation. This highlights the need into further investigations to complete our understanding of the mechanisms and roles of N-Degron pathways.

Introduction

The N-degron rule describes the association of a protein's stability and degradation can be a result of the identity of its N-terminal amino acid residue[[1], [2], [3]]. Many branches are associated with this approach, including the Arg-N End rule [4,5], Ac–N End rule [6,7], Glycine N end rule [8,9], and a proline branch [10].

Within the broader context of eukaryotic N-degrons, N-terminal methionine is a potential degradation signal depending on the identity to the penultimate residue. The N-degron can be generated but excision of the N-terminal methionine or when methionine is retained at the N-termini. The N-terminal Methionine Excision (NME) mechanism is responsible for removing methionine from proteins that are followed by amino acids such as Glycine, Alanine, Serine, Cysteine, Threonine, Proline, and Valine [11,12]. These residues may undergo degradation as described by the Ac–N end rule [6]. For proteins that have retained the N-terminal methionine (Nt-Met) and the penultimate residue is a hydrophobic residue (Ф) such as Leu, Phe, Ile, Trp, or Tyr, the protein is reportedly degraded as a result of MФ-degrons [13]. These degrons exhibit a mutual interaction between the Ac–N end rule and the Arg-N end rule pathways (which controls the ubiquitin degradation of proteins with arginylated N-terminal) [14]. Additional branches of the N-end rule, such as the Glycine N-degron which governs proteins with MG- N-termini [8,9] and the Proline N-end rule which controls proteins with MP- N-termini, play a role in the complex regulation of protein breakdown [10].

Within the context of N-degrons is another complexity of an apparent lack of complete conservation between yeast and mammals. Many of the N-Degrons described above were initially identified in S. cerevisiae with some examples not being observed or validated in mammalian systems. A recent expert review summarizes the known destabilizing N-termini in these two biological systems [2], highlighting the Arg/N-degron pathway and the GASTC/N-degron pathway in mammals. With the complexity of N-degrons there remains questions about specific N-terminal sequences. For example, a previous report [8] has previously indicated that in a mammalian cell line model proteins with a penultimate basic residue are also targeted for degradation. Timms et al. used global protein stability (GPS) technology to identify patterns that induce instability, particularly the residues located at the N terminus of the peptide [8]. This work revealed that N-terminal sequences with penultimate lysine, arginine, glycine, and cysteine were identified as the most destabilizing. This highlights some of the differences between mammalian and yeast systems as MK- N-termini has been previously demonstrated to be stable in S. cerevisiae [13].

In contrast to prior reports with reporter proteins, global proteomic analysis of protein degradation has failed to identify similar relations of N-termini to protein stability [15]. This inconsistency between mechanistic investigations and what is observed at a cellular level for endogenous proteins warrants follow up investigations.

Here we describe our investigations on MK- and MR-degrons of recombinant proteins and by global proteomic analysis. We have been able to recapitulate the previous reports on MK- and MR-degrons with recombinant proteins and similarly our proteomic analysis on protein stability did not observe this degradation with endogenous proteins. Further analysis has revealed this may in part explained by additional sequence requirements for the MK- and MR-degrons.

Materials and methods

DNA plasmids. Plasmid for the ubiquitin-ΔN-Lyn-3 × Flag fusion expression has been previously reported (Eldeeb & Fahlman, 2016) (Addgene#78960). The N-terminal residues were altered by site-directed mutagenesis (SDM) to generate the MKT- MRT- and MRD- N-terminal residues as described in this report. Plasmid (pReciever-M14) for PKC-δ-3 × Flag expression was obtained from GeneCopoeia. The N-terminal MKP-mutant was generated by SDM. SDM was performed as previously described [16,17].

Cell culture and Cycloheximide Chase assays. HeLa cells were obtained from ATCC. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) (SIGMA#F1051) to 10 % and Penicillin were added to DMEM at the time to culture HeLa (37 °C and 5 %CO2). 5 × 105 cells were seeded in untreated 12 wells plate. the following day the cells were transfected with plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen #11668027) as recommended by the supplier. 18 h after transfection a CHX chase was initiated by the addition of 100 μg/mL of cycloheximide (SIGMA #01810-1G). At the indicated time points, cells were lysed with 150 μL of lysis buffer, 50 mm Tris (pH 6.8), 8 % glycerol (v/v), 0.016 % SDS (w/v), 0.125 % β-mercaptoethanol (v/v), 0.125 % bromophenol blue (w/v). Lysis was completed by sonication for 10 s at a 30 % (1 W) amplitude. Samples were then resolved by SDS-PAGE.

Electrophoresis and Immunoblotting. Antibodies for immunoblotting were anti-FLAG® M2 (Sigma-# F1804), anti-tubulin (Santa Cruz Bio. #SC-53646) and anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling #2118S). The secondary antibodies were either goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit conjugated with IRDyes® (LI-COR). Cell Lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE on 10 % gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (LI-COR Biosciences). Precision Plus All Blue protein pre-stained standards (Bio-Rad# 1610373) were used as molecular weight markers. Immunoblotting was performed as previously described [17]. Immunoblotting for biotinylated proteins was done in a one step blotting procedure with IRDye® 800 CW labeled streptavidin (LICOR #926–32230) as recommended by the supplier. Visualization was performed on an Odyssey® Infrared Imaging System.

Azidohomoalanine pulse chase for proteomic analysis. HeLa cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes until they reached approximately 80 % confluence upon which cells were washed 2 × with methionine-free DMEM (Gibco #21013-024). Metabolic labelling with 50 μM l-azidohomoalanine (AHA)(vector laboratories #CCT-1066) in methionine-free DMEM supplemented with 10 % dialyzed FBS (Cytiva #SH30079.01), 0.2 mM Cysteine (SIGMA#8755), and 2 mM l-Glutamine (GIBCOBRL#21051-016). Control cells were cultivated for the same duration in DMEM with 10 % FBS. Following labeling, cells were washed twice with DMEM and the experiment initiated by the addition of DMEM with 10 % FBS. Cells were then lysed at 0, 3, 9 or 27 h after the addition of the media. Cells were washed with PBS And lysed by at addition of 100 μL 1 % SDS in PBS containing an EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Cat# 1873580). Lysates were sonicated on ice for 2 min (cycles: 5 s on, 2 s off, with 35 % amplitude). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 RPM for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein content was determined by a BCA assay (Pierce #23227).

For each sample, 250 μg of protein were utilized in the click reaction (400 μL total volume in PBS) which included 50 mM TCEP, 50 mM CuSO4, and 5 mM Biotin-Alkyne (CCT-TA105). The click reaction proceeded at room temperature in the dark with continuous agitation. The samples were clarified by centrifugation and the supernatant was precipitated overnight with trichloroacetic acid (20 % final concentration). The samples were pelleted by centrifugation and the pellet washed with 95 % ice-cold ethanol and resolubilized with 8 M Guanidinium chloride (200 μL). Solubilization with heat-freeze-vortex cycles, as previously described [18] was done to maximize solubilization. Samples were diluted 2 × with PBS. Then the biotinylated protein was captured on avidin-agarose (Pierce #20219) overnight with agitation. Capture efficiency was evaluated by spotting the samples (2 μL) before and capture on nitrocellulose membrane and dried prior to immunoblotting. Upon achieving an efficiency above 70 %, the beads were collected and subjected to an on-bead trypsin digestion as previously described [18].

Mass spectrometry. Peptides were separated and analyzed using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific) and an EASY-Spray capillary HPLC column (ES75150, Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode with a resolution of 60,000 and m/z range of 350–1700. Multiply charged ions were fragmented via HCD dissociation with an NCE of 28, and spectra of their fragments were recorded in the orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000. Data was processed using Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Scientific, version 3.0) and the database (Uniprot UP000005640) was searched using SEQUEST (Thermo Scientific). Search parameters included a strict false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01, a relaxed FDR of 0.05, a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.01Da. Peptides were searched with carbamidomethyl cysteine as a static modification and oxidized methionine and deamidated glutamine and asparagine as dynamic modifications.

The Raw data of analysis is available at ftp://massive.ucsd.edu/v07/MSV000096670/and the raw quantified protein lists are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Data analysis. Extracted ion intensives from Proteome Discoverer were used for quantification. After background subtraction of the no AHA control data a global normalization was applied. Only proteins that have two or more peptides were used for the comparison among groups. The fraction remaining were calculated using the initial time zero to determine the fraction remaining of proteins for half life determination. A maximal half life of 100 h was set as this was four-fold of the time of the experimental data collection.

This reporter being an N-terminal ubiquitin fusion protein enables the generation of either canonical or non-canonical N-termini [19](Fig. 2A). Twenty-four hours after transfection, cycloheximide (CHX) (100 μM final) was added to the cells to inhibit protein synthesis. Cells were lysed at 0, 2, 6 and 8 h after the addition of CHX. To investigate whether the utilization of the ubiquitin fusion technique impacted this observed degradation of MK- N-termini or whether degradation was inherent to the reporter Lyn kinase, a alternative construct was investigated. Here we utilized an expression plasmid for full length PKC-δ with a C-terminal triple FLAG tag with either MF- N-terminal sequence or the mutant MK- N-termini.

As described above, these PKC-δ expression plasmids were transiently transfected into HeLa cell and the amount of protein remaining after CHX addition was determined by immune blotting. As shown in (Fig. 2D), the mutant with the MKP- N-termini resulted in degradation after 2 h CHX treatment. Demonstrating the resulting protein destabilization by the MK- N-termini is independent of the reporter protein.

Impact of Inhibitors on MK- and MR- Protein Degradation. The prior report describing the degradation of proteins with MK- and MR- N-termini revealed that these N-termini are targeted to the proteasome by direct recognition by the UBR1 and UBR2 E3 ubiquitin ligases [8]. Here we verified this proposed mechanism by investigating the degradation of the ΔN-Lyn in the presence of chemical inhibitors.

The instability of MK- and MR- ΔN-Lyn was investigated in the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132. As described above, HeLa cells were transiently transfected with the two plasmids and were additionally treated with 10 μM MG132 (or DMSO control) upon the addition of CHX. Cell lysates were harvested at 0 and 8 h after treatment and the amount of ΔN-Lyn remaining was determined by immune blotting. While previous work demonstrated that MK- and MR-are directly recognized by the E3 ligases, the canonical substrates for the UBR domain containing E3 ligases are N-terminal basic residues [22]. MK- and MR- N-termini are not known substrates of methionine aminopeptidases (MetAP) but their activity could potentially lead to N-Terminal K- or R-residues for direct recognition by the Arg N-End rule pathway [4]. To investigate this possibility the stability of N-terminal MK- and MR-proteins were investigated in the presence of the MetAP inhibitors fumagillin and bestatin [23,24].

When bestatin (50 μg/mL) or fumagillin (5 μg/ml) are added to cells expressing MK- or MR-ΔN-Lyn in addition to CHX no change in protein degradation is observed. As shown in (Fig. 3B), neither inhibitor had an impact on ΔN-Lyn protein stability, which is in agreement with prior reports indicating the MK- and MR- N-termini are directly recognized by the E3 ligases [8].

Proteome Wide Analysis of Protein Degradation. While the above-described experiments align with prior reports regarding the instability of proteins with MR- and MK- N-termini [8], prior proteome wide investigations on protein degradation failed to observe system wide degradation of endogenous proteins as predicted by the various branches of the N-Degron pathways [15]. Here we investigated global degradation using an alternative proteomic approach to evaluate protein stability.

An approach adapted from previous reports with metabolically labelling with the methionine analogue, azidohomoalanine was utilized [25,26]. For this study 16 dishes (10 cm) of HeLa cells on 80 % confluence were labeled overnight with DMEM media (with 10 % Dialyzed FBS) where 50 μM AHA replaced methionine. Four dishes were treated with normal DMEM are background controls. After labeling the media was replaced with DMEM (with 10 % FBS) and the cells were washed and lysed at 0, 3, 9, and 27 h after media replacement (four dishes at each time point) as outlined in (Fig. 5A). AHA labeled proteins were then biotinylated with biotin-alkyne. Samples were then isolated on streptavidin beads where the capture efficiency verified by spotting 2 μL of sample before and after the addition of beads and quantified by probing with labeled streptavidin to ensure labeled protein depletion (Fig. 5B). reveals that the capture efficiency was >70 % for all samples prior to proceeding with on bead trypsin digestion and LC-MS/MS analysis. The resulting identified and quantified protein lists were corrected for the proteins identified in the no AHA controls (Supplemental Table 2). Of the 2817 identified proteins, 811 were identified to be exclusively cytoplasmic or nuclear in their location. These proteins were then grouped by their N-terminal residues (Fig. 5C) and then their individual stability was determined by their fraction remaining over the time course of the experiment. As shown in (Fig. 5D), no correlations of N-termini with protein stability is apparent with the exception of MC-, a known Arg-N-End rule substrate [27,28]. As with a previous report [15], no generalized instability is observed with N-termini as predicted by N-Degron pathway.

To investigate whether the degradation of proteins with MK- and MR- N-termini may be dependent on whether the endogenous proteins were in complexes, we cross referenced our data to that of a proteomic experiment characterizing protein complexes [29]. Here we identified proteins with MR/MK- N-termini in our dataset that were also detected in the proteomic study on protein complexes, when proteins with MK- and MR- N-termini are grouped according to whether they are in protein complexes, no obvious correlation with protein stability was noted (Fig. 5E).

In addition to the data presented here, we have also investigated other available data sets on protein degradation [30], which we analyzed in a similar fashion to that described above. Again, this did not reveal any generalized protein degradation that is correlated with a protein N-termini (data not shown) despite using an alternative proteomic technique to that described here.

Discussion

In this study we have utilized multiple recombinant proteins to verify that N-terminal MK- and MR-sequences result in protein degradation. While this degradation appears generally independent of the protein sequence as a result of the two reporter proteins (Fig. 2) being degraded by the inclusion of these N-terminal sequences, the identity of the third residue was also determined to influence degradation.

Validation of MK- and MR- N-degrons in Reporter Proteins. Consistent with prior findings using Global Protein Stability (GPS) assays [8], we confirmed that N-terminal MK- and MR-sequences destabilize recombinant proteins. Using ΔN-Lyn and PKC-δ reporters bearing MK- and MR- N-termini, we observed accelerated degradation following CHX treatment when contrasted to the stable MV- control N-termini, which is in agreement with these N-termini acting as degradation signals (Fig. 2). This effect was observed with a second unrelated reporter construct suggesting that degradation was independent of the remaining protein sequence. Of note with the MV- control N-termini which we observe to be stable, as this N-termini is stable in mammalian cells [2], but has been reported to be degraded in yeast [13].

Our results show that MK-mediated degradation is not impacted by the addition of MetAP inhibitors (Fig. 3), which implies that the N-terminal methionine retention is essential for their recognition. The retained N-terminal methionine is in agreement with the previous report by Timms et al. [8].

Influence of the Third Residue on N-terminal Degradation. A novel observation from our study is that the identity of the third residue modulates degradation mediated by MK- and MR- N-termini. Specifically, substitution of the third residue with an acidic amino acid, like aspartic acid resulted in protein stabilization (Fig. 4). This finding suggests that a negatively charged residue at this position inhibits recognition by N-degron pathway components, possibly by charge neutralization or disrupting interaction with the UBR box domain [22,31]. This observation opens the possibility of post translational modifications, such as phosphorylation potentially modulating the recognition of proteins with N-terminal MK-/MR-degrons.

Proteasome Dependency. Degradation of the reporter constructs was inhibited by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, supporting the role of UPS in MK-/MR-mediated degradation (Fig. 2A). These results, together with the ineffectiveness of MetAP inhibitors like bestatin and fumagillin (Fig. 2B), further affirm the direct recognition of intact MK-/MR- N-termini by E3 ligases, bypassing aminopeptidase processing as previously reported [.

Proteome-Wide Degradation Patterns Do Not Reflect Reporter Findings. Despite clear degradation of MK-/MR-bearing reporters, our AHA-based pulse-chase proteomic analysis failed to detect a similar correlation in endogenous proteins (Fig. 5). This finding corroborates previous large-scale studies by which showed no broad relationship between N-terminal identity and protein half-life [30]. While proteins with an MC- N-termini exhibited the predicted instability (Fig. 5D) [32], no general pattern of degradation was seen for MK-, MR-, or other destabilizing N-terminal sequences (Fig. 1). One explanation for this discrepancy may be the effect of protein complex formation. In recombinant overexpression systems, the abundance of nascent proteins may exceed the availability of binding partners, rendering N-termini more accessible for degradation. In contrast, endogenous proteins may exist predominantly in complexes that sterically shield the N-terminus from the UPS, as has been proposed and discussed regarding the Ac/N-end rule pathway [2,33,34]. However, our cross-referencing with complex formation data did not reveal an association between complex status and stability of MK-/MR-bearing proteins (Fig. 5E) [34]. An alternative explanation is that N-terminal degron accessibility is conditionally regulated, potentially influenced by conformational changes, post-translational modifications, or molecular chaperones. This highlights the importance of degron exposure in the N-end rule, an area that has been previously [[33], [34], [35]] discussed but further investigations are warranted.

Together our data is consistent regarding the current dichotomy of mechanistic investigations into N-Degron protein degradation and what is observed at a proteome wide scale. There are current hypothesis including steric shielding to why this disparity is observed [34]. These alternative models regarding the accessibility of the N-termini still need to be further explored but it may be revealing an overlooked cellular process that may be modulating proteostasis. Creating a hypothesis that there may be a mechanism for cells to degrade excess protein, which would have broad implications in our understanding of development and disease.
