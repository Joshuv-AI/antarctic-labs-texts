Five Mycena species that induce seed germination in the mycoheterotrophic orchid Gastrodia
Authors: Chigusa Yugeta, Kosuke Nagamune, Michiru Kitahara, Kozue Sotome, Yuki Ogura-Tsujita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The fungus genus Mycena is saprotrophic, but several lineages form orchid mycorrhizae or associate closely with plant roots. To elucidate which Mycena species form mycorrhizal associations with orchids, fungal isolates of five Mycena species (M. epipterygia, M. haematopus, M. polygramma, M. pura, and Mycena sp.) from four sections (Calodontes, Fragilipedes, Hygrocyboideae, and Lactipedes) were cultured with the seeds of mycoheterotrophic orchids, including Gastrodia confusa, G. elata, G. nipponica, and G. pubilabiata. All five Mycena species stimulated seed germination in all four Gastrodia species. Mycena haematopus and M. polygramma had a high affinity for Gastrodia and induced protocorm formation in all of them and tuberization in seedlings of two of them. Phylogenetic analyses indicated that Mycena species that associate with Gastrodia are dispersed throughout the genus and do not form a monophyletic group. Gastrodia species differ in fungal specificity, with G. confusa having a narrow specificity for specific Mycena species, whereas G. pubilabiata and G. nipponica have a broader specificity with multiple Mycena lineages and non-Mycena species, Cyanotrama gypsea and Collybiopsis dichroa. Our results imply that phylogenetically diverse Mycena taxa associate with Gastrodia species as orchid mycorrhizal fungi.

1. Introduction

Mycena (Pers.) Roussel is one of the most species-rich genera in the order Agaricales, with about 600 species (Wijayawardene et al., 2020). Mycena are litter- and wood-decaying fungi found in a wide range of habitats and climatic zones (Harder et al., 2023). However, some species may invade and interact with living plant roots (Harder et al., 2023; Thoen et al., 2020), with life histories that are intermediate between free-living saprotrophs and symbiotic fungi, making them a valuable genus for studying the evolutionary switch from a saprotrophic to a biotrophic mode. Mycena galopus (Pers.) P. Kumm. formed ericoid mycorrhizal-like structures with Vaccinium plants (Grelet et al., 2017). Several Mycena species formed mantle-like structures around the root tips and showed intercellular growth in root epidermis and cortex of Betula pendula Roth seedlings (Thoen et al., 2020). Additionally, some lineages have formed orchid mycorrhizae (Kitahara et al., 2022; Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2009; Xu & Guo, 1989), but it is unclear which taxa are involved in orchid mycorrhizal associations.

Intracellular hyphal coils, typical hyphal structures in orchid mycorrhizae, containing Mycena species have been reported in Gastrodia R. Br. (Li et al., 2020; Xu & Mu, 1990) and the epiphytic orchid Dendrobium officinale Kimura & Migo (Zhang et al., 2012). Gastrodia elata Blume is associated with Mycena species in the juvenile stage (Xu & Guo, 1989; Yu et al., 2023) although it switches to Armillaria fungi for further development (Xu & Guo, 2000). Mycena species have been detected in mycorrhizal roots of G. appendiculata C.S. Leou & N.J. Chung, G. flavilabella S.S. Ying, G. fontinalis T.P. Lin, G. nantoensis T.C. Hsu & C.M. Kuo ex T.P. Lin (Lee et al., 2015), G. confusa Honda & Tuyama, G. nipponica (Honda) Tuyama, G. pubilabiata Y. Sawa (Kinoshita et al., 2016; Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2009), G. confusoides T.C. Hsu, S.W. Chung & C.M. Kuo (Li et al., 2022), and Wullschlaegelia aphylla (Sw.) Rchb.f. (Martos et al., 2009). In a previous study, we successfully conducted in vitro seed germination of G. confusa, G. elata, G. nipponica, and G. pubilabiata with two Mycena strains (Kitahara et al., 2022). These results imply a strong symbiotic relationship between Mycena and Gastrodia species.

However, it is difficult to identify the exact Mycena species involved in orchid mycorrhizal associations. Most Mycena strains from orchids have been identified only by DNA sequences and not at the species level. Although a few studies have successfully identified species via induction of fruiting bodies in culture, such as M. osmundicola J.E. Lange (Xu & Guo, 1989), M. anoectochili L. Fan & S.X. Guo (Guo et al., 1997), M. dendrobii L. Fan & S.X. Guo (Guo et al., 1999), and M. orchidicola L. Fan & S.X. Guo (Fan et al., 1996), the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of the ribosomal RNA gene and nuclear-encoded large subunit rRNA gene (LSU) sequence information necessary for molecular phylogenetic analysis have not been published for these Mycena species, making it difficult to understand their phylogenetic position. Symbiotic culture with orchid seeds and Mycena isolates from morphologically identified fruiting bodies would allow identification of the Mycena species involved in orchid mycorrhizal associations and their correct taxonomic position. However, only a few studies have demonstrated orchid seed germination by fungal isolates from fruiting bodies (Liu et al., 2022).

In this study, to identify the Mycena taxa involved in orchid mycorrhizal associations, five Mycena species belonging to four different sections of the genus were used for symbiotic seed germination tests with four Gastrodia species. The fungal isolates were obtained from basidiospores of field-collected fruiting bodies that were morphologically identified (Table 1). The Mycena isolates were M. haematopus (Pers.) P. Kumm. in Sect. Lactipedes, M. polygramma (Bull.) Gray in Sect. Fragilipedes, M. epipterygia (Scop.) Gray in Sect. Hygrocyboideae, and M. pura (Pers.) P. Kumm. and Mycena sp. in Sect. Calodontes. Among them, M. polygramma, M. epipterygia, and M. pura have been found to show intercellular colonization and/or form mantle-like structures in Betula roots (Thoen et al., 2020). Mycena pura is a litter decomposer (Maas Geesteranus, 1989), while M. haematopus is a wood decomposer (Maas Geesteranus, 1988). We tested whether such ecological characteristics affect orchid mycorrhizal associations. A molecular phylogenetic analysis was also performed using the sequences of the five Mycena species and Mycena strains previously detected or isolated from Gastrodia.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Fungal isolates

The five Mycena isolates were from the Tottori University Fungal Culture (TUFC) collection, obtained from basidiospores of fruiting bodies. The basidiomata of TUFC101683 have the characteristics of Sect. Calodontes, including a raphanoid odor and smooth pileipellis. Furthermore, this species is morphologically similar to M. pura and M. pearsoniana Dennis ex Singer, but differs in the absence of pleurocystidia and amyloid basidiospores, respectively. Therefore, we assigned this species to Mycena sp. Cyanotrama gypsea and Collybiopsis dichroa were isolated and molecularly identified using the methods described by Kitahara et al. (2022). The strains and sequences of the ITS region were deposited in the NITE Biological Resource Center (NBRC115254, NBRC115274) and the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) (LC843753, LC843754), respectively (Table 1). The ITS sequence of isolate NBRC115254 shared 99.6% sequence similarity with Cyanotrama gypsea (KT203292) while that of NBRC115274 shared 99.2% sequence similarity with Collybiopsis dichroa (OL467256).

For phylogenetic analysis of the Mycena species, the partial regions of ITS and the LSU were sequenced. PCR amplification was performed using the primer combinations ITS5/ITS4 (White et al., 1990) for the ITS region and LR0R/LR5 (Vilgalys & Hester, 1990) or CTB6/LR5-F (Garbelotto et al., 1997; Tedersoo et al., 2008) for the LSU region. PCR and sequencing reactions were performed using KOD FX Neo (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and a BigDye Terminator v3.1 cycle sequencing kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sequences were determined using the Fasmac DNA sequence service (Kanagawa, Japan). Obtained sequences were assembled with ATGC ver. 7 software (Genetyx, Tokyo, Japan) and deposited in DDBJ under accession numbers LC843748-LC843752 for ITS and LC822037-LC822041 for LSU.

2.2. Phylogenetic analyses of Mycena species

The LSU sequences of the five Mycena species were used for phylogenetic analysis. The sequences from Gastrodia-associated Mycena species from GenBank and reference sequences from Chew et al. (2015), Jin et al. (2024), and Liu et al. (2022) were added to the analysis. The sequences from two Mycena isolates (Mycena sp. 1, Mycena sp. 2) used for symbiotic germination tests in a previous study (Kitahara et al., 2022) were also included in the analysis. Multiple alignments were conducted using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004) implemented in MEGA v.7.0.26 (Kumar et al., 2016). The phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with MEGA v.7.0.26 software, and Bayesian inference (BI) with MrBayes (version 3.2.7) (Ronquist et al., 2012). The GTR+G+I model for ML analysis and GTR+G model for BI analysis were chosen as the best-fit nucleotide substitution models by MEGA v.7.0.26 and Kakusan 4 (Tanabe, 2011), respectively. Bootstrap (BS) analysis of the ML tree was performed with 1,000 BS replicates. MrBayes analysis involved two sets of four Markov chain Monte Carlo runs composed of three heated chains and one cold chain, with the topology sampled after every 100 generations. In total, 1,500,000 generations were analyzed until the average standard deviation of the split frequencies was < 0.01. After convergence, the first 25% of primary topologies were discarded as burn-in; then a 50% consensus tree was constructed using the remaining trees and posterior probabilities (PPs) were calculated. Sequence alignments were deposited in TreeBase under accession no. TB2:S31707 (http://www.treebase.org/). To confirm the phylogenetic position of Mycena sp. (TUFC101683), an additional ML tree was constructed with ITS sequences using the same method as described above. The representative sequences of the M. pura species complex published in Harder et al. (2013) and the epitype of M. pearsoniana (Harder et al., 2012) were also included in the analysis.

2.3. Symbiotic seed germination test

The seeds of four Gastrodia species were symbiotically cultured with the five Mycena strains and two isolates from Gastrodia roots (Table 1), as described in Kitahara et al. (2022). The mature Gastrodia seeds were collected from natural habitats and kept at 5 °C after drying with silica gel. The fungal isolates were subcultured on 2% malt extract agar medium (1.5% agar) for 2-3 wk. Dead leaves of Quercus glauca Thunb. were cut into 2 × 2-cm discs and autoclaved for 2 h. The sterilized discs were placed on the subcultured plates of each fungal isolate and incubated for an additional 2-3 wk. The seeds were sterilized in 5% Ca(ClO)2 with 200 µL Tween-20 for 15 min and sown on 1.5% water agar medium plates. Fungus-colonized discs were transferred to these water agar plates. Approximately 20-140 seeds were sown on each plate, and each treatment was replicated five times. Petri dishes without fungal inoculum were prepared as a control. The plates were incubated for 5 mo at 25 °C in the dark, and the seeds were counted under a stereomicroscope. As shown in Figure 1, seed development was assigned to five developmental groups following Kitahara et al. (2022): ungerminated; Stage 1 (protocorms 0.2-3 mm long); Stage 2 (protocorms 3-10 mm long with developed roots); Stage 3 (seedlings > 10 mm long); and Stage 4 (showing tuber development). Because G. elata did not develop roots, its germination stages were limited to the three protocorm classifications (0.2-3 mm, 3-10 mm, and > 10 mm long).

Fig. 1 - Seed development of Gastrodia confusa (A), G. nipponica (B, E), G. pubilabiata (C, D), and G. elata (F). Seed development was assigned to the following five stages. (A) Stage 0: Ungerminated seed, (B) Stage 1: Protocorms 0.2-3 mm long, (C) Stage 2: Protocorms 3-10 mm long with developed roots, (D) Stage 3: Seedlings > 10 mm long, (E) Stage 4: Tuber development. Gastrodia elata protocorm (F) did not develop roots, and thus its germination stages were limited to the three protocorm classifications (0.2-3 mm, 3-10 mm, and > 10 mm long). R: Root, T: Tuber.

3. Results

All five Mycena species stimulated seed germination in all four Gastrodia species, while controls showed no germination (Table 2). Mycena haematopus and M. polygramma had a high affinity for Gastrodia and promoted seed germination in all four species, reaching Stage 4 in G. pubilabiata and G. nipponica. Mycena epipterygia also induced germination to Stage 1 or higher in all four species, reaching Stage 3 in G. pubilabiata. Mycena pura and Mycena sp. had less affinity than others and developed to Stage 1 or 2 in three Gastrodia species. Among isolates from orchid roots, Cyanotrama gypsea and Collybiopsis dichroa promoted germination and the seeds developed to Stage 1 or higher in all Gastrodia species except G. elata, with the greatest effect on G. pubilabiata and G. nipponica, reaching Stage 4.

Phylogenetic analyses showed that Gastrodia-associated Mycena species are not biased toward a particular lineage but rather are widely dispersed within the genus (Fig. 2). The section Fragilipedes includes M. polygramma (isolate no. TUFC101077) used in this study, and M. citrinomarginata Gillet (accession nos. OM228762, OR754294) and M. subpiligera L.N. Liu (accession no. OM228765), which induced seed germination in G. elata in Liu et al. (2022), but these are not monophyletic. In addition, in our tree Mycena sp. was not related to M. pura (TUFC101474) or M. pura (FN394636) (Fig. 2, Supplemantal Fig. S1).

4. Discussion

A wide range of Mycena lineages are symbiotic with Gastrodia species. Symbiotic culture experiments showed that five Mycena species within four sections induced Stage 1 or higher germination in all four Gastrodia species studied (Table 2). These five Mycena species, along with other Gastrodia-associated Mycena, formed a polyphyletic group in molecular phylogenetic analyses (Fig. 2). Thus, our results imply that diverse Mycena lineages are involved in Gastrodia associations. Thoen et al. (2020) showed that M. polygramma, M. epipterygia, and M. pura colonize intercellularly and/or form mantle-like structures in Betula roots, indicating that these Mycena species also exhibit symbioses with orchids. In addition, we found that both leaf-litter and wood-decaying Mycena species are symbiotic with Gastrodia species. The M. polygramma and Mycena sp. isolates used in this study were obtained from fruiting bodies on decayed wood and litter, respectively. These results indicate a diversity of carbon sources in mycoheterotrophic Gastrodia.

The results of the symbiotic culture experiments clearly show that the Gastrodia species have different fungal specificities during their early developmental stages and that the available Mycena species vary by orchid species (Fig. 3). Gastrodia pubilabiata and G. nipponica have lower specificity than other species, and developed to Stage 4 not only with multiple Mycena species but also with non-Mycena fungi including Cyanotrama gypsea, Collybiopsis dichroa, and Marasmiaceae sp. 1. On the other hand, G. confusa developed to Stage 4 with only a single Mycena species, demonstrating its high mycorrhizal specificity for a particular lineage. These differences in fungal specificity during seed germination correlate well with those during the adult stages. Gastrodia pubilabiata is predominantly associated with Mycena, Marasmiaceae, and Omphalotaceae, whereas G. nipponica is associated with these families as well as with ectomycorrhizal fungal groups (Kinoshita et al., 2016; Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2021). On the other hand, G. confusa is associated exclusively with Mycena (Kinoshita et al., 2016; Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2009). These results imply that these Gastrodia species do not exhibit a change in fungal specificity between germination and adult stages. The differences in fungal specificity among Gastrodia plants may affect their habitat preferences and distributions. Mycoheterotrophic species are completely dependent on mycorrhizal fungi as a carbon source, so their distribution is more restricted by fungal distribution than is that of photosynthetic species. A Mycena specialist, G. confusa, is found almost exclusively in bamboo thickets in Japan (Kinoshita et al., 2016), implying that its habitat may be strongly restricted by mycorrhizal fungi. On the other hand, the generalists G. pubilabiata and G. nipponica are distributed in more diversified habitats, including Castanopsis forest, Cryptomeria japonica plantations, and bamboo thickets (Kinoshita et al., 2016). Although Gastrodia species have mycorrhizal associations with a wide range of Mycena lineages, they have diversified into specialists and generalists, which may have contributed to the great diversification of the genus, one of the most species-rich among mycoheterotrophic plants.

Gastrodia elata is associated with Mycena during seed germination, but a switch to Armillaria is required for further development (Xu & Guo, 2000). Symbiotic germination has been observed between G. elata and various Mycena species, such as M. osmundicola (Xu & Guo, 1989), M. anoectochili (Guo et al., 1997), and M. dendrobii (Guo et al., 1999). Jin et al. (2024) showed that the sequences of five Mycena taxa detected from naturally germinated seeds of G. elata were included in a single clade. However, our results indicate that polyphyletic Mycena species are involved in the germination of G. elata seeds, as suggested by Harder et al. (2023), who reported that root-invasive Mycena are a completely polyphyletic group with members found throughout the Mycena clade. In our symbiotic culture experiments, the development of G. elata stopped at Stage 3 (Table 2; Fig. 1F). Transplantation of germinated protocorms onto Armillaria-infected wood allows continued growth and tuber development (Park et al., 2012). Presumably, the association with Armillaria is necessary for growth to Stage 4 or higher. The switching of fungal partners may appear to be a significant limitation for G. elata establishment in natural habitats, because plants need to employ at least two different fungal partners throughout their life history. However, our data indicate that G. elata can form symbiotic relationships with a variety of Mycena species, facilitating its germination. In addition, Armillaria, the largest fungus in the world, spans up to 965 ha and produces abundant rhizomorphs in the soil (Ferguson et al., 2003; Smith et al., 1992), which improves the chances of successful colonization. The ability of G. elata to switch partners is likely not a significant limiting factor. Indeed, this species is widespread throughout cool temperate regions, from the far east of Russia to the Himalayas (Chen et al., 2009).

Our phylogenetic analyses highlight some taxonomic issues within Mycena (Fig. 2). The Mycena section has been defined based on morphological characteristics (Maas Geesteranus, 1980) and is often not supported by molecular phylogenetic analysis (Chew et al., 2015; Perry et al., 2020). That is true of our analysis using LSU sequences. Mycena citrinomarginata, and M. subpiligera, which induce seed germination in G. elata (Liu et al., 2022), and M. polygramma were assigned to Sect. Fragilipedes based on the morphological characters of fruiting bodies. Kitahara et al. (2022) concluded that Mycena sp. 1 (LC651188) from G. pubilabiata belongs to Fragilipedes because the tree based on ITS and LSU regions showed that Mycena sp. 1 formed a monophyletic clade with other Fragilipedes species such as M. abramsii (Murrill) Murrill and M. aetites (Fr.) Quél. In our analyses, these Fragilipedes species did not form a monophyletic clade (Fig. 2). Fragilipedes is polyphyletic (Chew et al., 2015) and will need to be revised in the future. Another issue concerns the ambiguous phylogenetic position of our isolate of Mycena sp. (TUFC101683). This species is morphologically similar to the two species within Sect. Calodontes, M. pura and M. pearsoniana, but our additional phylogenetic analysis with ITS sequences showed that Mycena sp. is not closely related with any taxa within the M. pura complex reported in Harder et al. (2013) and authentic M. pearsoniana (Harder et al., 2012) (Supplemental Fig. S1). Mycena sp. (TUFC101683) could be an undescribed species within Sect. Calodontes and further study is needed.

In conclusion, a variety of Mycena lineages are involved in orchid mycorrhizal associations, although the available fungal lineages vary among orchid species. Mycena species are frequently found in mycoheterotrophic Gastrodia species as the main fungal partners (Ogura-Tsujita et al., 2021) but they are also found in photosynthetic orchids (Herrera et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2012) and promote seed germination (Guo et al., 1997, 1999) and plant growth (Zhang et al., 2013). These results imply that Mycena species may be associated with diverse orchid species, although they are minor fungal partners. Such minor associations or endophytic infections may opportunistically switch to symbiosis during the evolutionary process, which is referred to as the “waiting room hypothesis” (Selosse et al., 2022). The fungal partner for the ancestor of Gastrodia may have switched to Mycena species. The unique genomic structures of Mycena species (Harder et al., 2024) imply that a mixture of saprotrophic and biotrophic lifestyles may be widespread in a variety of Mycena lineages, and such a lifestyle could be the first step toward the evolutionary switch from saprotrophic to biotorophic nutritional modes.
