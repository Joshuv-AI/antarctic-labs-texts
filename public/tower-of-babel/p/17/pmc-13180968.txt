Pomolic acid alleviates CCl4‑induced liver fibrosis in mice by suppressing β-arrestin 2-mediated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization
Authors: Xuan Zhu, Yuhan Zhou, Mingyu Ruan, Xuebin Yang, Gang Pei, Jin Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver fibrosis is a wound healing process in response to various chronic liver injuries characterized by the replacement of normal liver by collagen fibers formed from extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in scarring of the organ. Liver macrophages, a highly heterogeneous and plastic immune cell population, are pivotal in human health and disease, widely involved in the advancement and reversal of liver fibrosis. Interleukin-4 (IL-4) and/or interleukin-13 (IL-13)-induced pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization play a crucial role in promoting liver fibrosis through the secretion of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a key profibrotic cytokine. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic potential and underlying mechanism of pomolic acid (PA) in mitigating carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis. The results indicated that PA effectively hindered pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and the release of TGF-β, thereby ameliorating the fibrotic progression. Mechanistically, PA disrupted β-arrestin 2-IRG1 interaction, consequently impeding the ubiquitination-related degradation of IRG1, and ultimately suppressed fatty acid oxidation (FAO)-mediated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. Furthermore, PA also demonstrated excellent efficacy in combating oral submucosal fibrosis (OSF). In conclusion, our study confirmed that PA inhibited the advancement of fibrosis by repressing β-arrestin 2-induced pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. Introduction

Liver fibrosis is a progressive condition characterized by the accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) and the gradual loss of functional liver tissue1. It is a dynamic and potentially reversible response to chronic liver damage that can advance to cirrhosis and, ultimately, hepatocellular carcinoma if untreated2. As a result, investigating the underlying pathological processes and therapeutic interventions for liver fibrosis is crucial in both clinical practice and medical research. However, there is currently a lack of targeted pharmacological interventions for liver fibrosis, so the development of novel treatment approaches is imperative to address this condition effectively.

Macrophages, as integral components of the innate immune system, serve as the frontline defense mechanism essential for preserving tissue homeostasis, coordinating immune reactions, and promoting tissue regeneration3. These versatile cells derive from circulating monocytes that infiltrate tissues and transform into macrophages with specific phenotypic and functional characteristics dictated by their microenvironment4. Macrophages consist of diverse subpopulations with varying cellular functions. Comprehending the distinct identities and roles of these cells is crucial for elucidating the processes involved in liver fibrosis. Liver macrophages are commonly categorized as pro-inflammatory /pro-fibrotic macrophages or monocyte-derived macrophages/Kupffer cells, depending on the classification used5. Both pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic macrophages are significantly increased during liver fibrosis6. Pro-inflammatory macrophages, commonly known as “classically activated” macrophages, primarily participate in pathogen defense and inflammation promotion by producing significant amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines aid in the activation of other immune cells, thereby amplifying the overall inflammatory response. In contrast, pro-fibrotic macrophages, termed “alternatively activated” macrophages, are essential for tissue repair, wound healing, and inflammation resolution. Pro-fibrotic macrophages facilitate the clearance of cellular debris and apoptotic cells, promote tissue repair, and possess characteristics that contribute to vascular lesions, scarring, and tissue fibrosis7. In the initial phases of liver fibrosis, the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) by inflammatory cytokines through pro-inflammatory macrophages is implicated in exacerbating the fibrotic condition. As liver fibrosis advances, pro-fibrotic macrophages play a more important role than pro-inflammatory macrophages, and they produce pro-fibrotic factors such as TGF-β after the stimulation of interleukin-4 (IL-4) and/or interleukin-13 (IL-13) to promote liver fibrosis8,9. Thus, pharmacological strategies aimed at regulating pro-inflammatory macrophage and pro-fibrotic macrophage functions in the context of liver fibrosis could offer a practical method for mitigating fibrotic mechanisms.

Pomolic acid (PA) is a triterpenoid that derives from the fruit of Euscaphis japonica (Thunb.) Dippel, commonly referred to as “Ye Ya Chun” in traditional Chinese medicine. PA exerts diverse biological activities such as anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antibacterial effects10–12. Notably, PA has attracted attention for its ability to prevent fibroblast activation and renal interstitial fibrosis13. Furthermore, PA exhibited anti-liver fibrotic activity by suppressing HSC proliferation14. Nonetheless, whether PA could directly repress pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and the precise mechanism remains incompletely elucidated. In this study, we identified PA as a novel inhibitor of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization through an in vitro preliminary screening aimed at reducing the progression of liver fibrosis. Meanwhile, we have elucidated a potential mechanistic association between PA and the β-arrestin 2/IRG1/STAT6 pathways in preventing pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. In addition, we also found that PA has a certain anti-oral submucosal fibrosis (OSF) effect. In conclusion, our findings highlight the potential of PA as a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of fibrotic diseases.

Raw264.7 culture

RAW264.7 murine macrophages, obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China), were cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. For pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, cells were stimulated with 1 μg/ml LPS for 48 h. For pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/ml IL-4 for 48 h.

Bone marrow-derived macrophage isolation and culture

Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and the tibias and femurs were removed. Bone marrow cells were collected by flushing the femoral and tibial bones and were cultured in 1640 (Procell, Wuhan, China) containing 10% FBS, 1% P/S, with the addition of 20 ng/mL M-CSF (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) for 2 days. Subsequently, the attached cells were collected as bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and utilized for the subsequent culture procedure. For pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS plus 20 ng/ml IFN-γ for 48 h. For pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/ml IL-4 plus 20 ng/ml IL-13 for 48 h.

THP-1 culture

The human monocytic cell line THP-1 was cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. To differentiate THP-1 monocytes into macrophage-like cells, they were treated with 50 μg/ml PMA for 24 h, followed by a 24-h rest period in fresh complete medium. For pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, the differentiated macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS plus 20 ng/ml IFN-γ for 48 h. For pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/ml IL-4 plus 20 ng/ml IL-13 for 48 h.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability following treatment with IL-4 and PA was assessed via a CCK-8 assay. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. After treatment with varying concentrations of PA and IL-4 for 48 h, the cells were incubated with a mixture of 10 μL CCK-8 reagent and 100 μL fresh medium for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured for each well using a microplate reader (Biotek, US).

Flow cytometric analysis

To identify BMDMs and Kupffer cells, cells were stained with antibodies against CD11b and F4/80. For functional analysis, IL-4-induced Raw264.7 macrophages were stained for both surface CD86 (APC-conjugated antibody, 30 min, 4 °C) and intracellular CD206 (PE-conjugated antibody). Specifically, after surface CD86 staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by intracellular CD206 staining in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. Additionally, Kupffer cells were separately stained with a PE-conjugated CD206 antibody alone to assess their pro-fibrotic phenotype. Following washing, all cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter Cytomics FC 500). Data were processed using FlowJo software. Pro-fibrotic macrophages were typically characterized by high CD206 and low CD86 expression.

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence staining procedure was conducted as follows. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at 4 °C, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for an additional 15 min, and then blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature to minimize non-specific binding. Primary antibodies against α-SMA, Collagen 1, and CD206 (all at 1:200 dilution) were applied and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated to either cyanine3 or FITC (1:100) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature under light-protected conditions. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI prior to mounting. All fluorescent images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was isolated from RAW264.7 cells and liver tissues by lysing in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mmol/L protease and phosphatase inhibitors (30 min). After determining protein concentrations via a BCA kit, 20 μg of protein lysate per sample was loaded for separation on 8–12% SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred to PVDF membranes (300 mA, 60 min). The membranes were subjected to a 20-min blocking step and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: α-SMA (1:2000), Collagen I (1:2000), TGFβ (1:5000), Arg1 (1:5000), CD206 (1:1000), PPARγ (1:1000), CD36 (1:2000), CPT1 (1:2000), p-STAT6 (1:1000), STAT6 (1:1000), IRG1 (1:1000), and ubiquitin (1:5000). This was followed by a 1 h incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) at room temperature. Immunoreactive signals were developed with an ECL substrate (UVITEC, UK) and quantified using ImageJ software.

After treatment with or without IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and PA (1.25, 2.5, 5 μM) at 37 °C for 48 h, total RNA was isolated and quantified using the Simgen Ultrapure Total RNA Extraction Kit. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit. Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP)

For immunoprecipitation assays, cell lysates were prepared using ice-cold binding buffer and clarified by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatants were immunoprecipitated with an anti-β-arrestin2 or IRG1 antibody overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G-coupled nanomagnetic beads were then used to pull down the immune complexes. Following extensive washing, the precipitated proteins were eluted and analyzed by western blot.

CRISPR-Cas9 system

To generate β-arrestin-2 knockout in Raw264.7 cells, a CRISPR-Cas9 system was employed. Specific guide RNAs (gRNAs) were cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector using the BsmB1 restriction site. For lentivirus production, HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 60–70% (0.6–0.7 × 106 cells/well) and transfected using the Lentiviral Packaging Kit (Arcegen, AS3509550) via the polyethylenimine (Polysciences, 02371) precipitation method. The culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 6 h post-transfection. Viral supernatants were harvested after 48 h and used to infect the target Raw264.7 cells. Alternatively, some cells were directly transfected with the gRNA plasmids. Successfully transduced cells were selected by treatment with puromycin (Gibco, A1113803).

Constructs

Expression constructs generated for this study were prepared by standard molecular biology techniques, and coding sequences were entirely verified.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

Cells were detached using trypsin–EDTA and subsequently incubated with 50 μM PA or DMSO control for 4 h at 37 °C under a CO2 atmosphere. Following PBS washes, the cell pellets were resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors. Aliquots of this suspension were heat-treated in a PCR thermocycler (3 min) and then cooled to 25 °C. The resulting pellets were processed for membrane protein extraction by resuspension in specific lysis buffer and undergoing three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen. The lysate was centrifuged at 18,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant, containing soluble proteins, was analyzed by western blot.

Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS)

Raw264.7 cells were collected and lysed with Mem-PER membrane protein extraction lysis buffer. The lysates, diluted to 5 mg/mL protein concentration, were subjected to a 3-h incubation at 4 °C with rotation in the presence of 50 μM PA or an equivalent volume of DMSO. Pronase (Sigma-Aldrich, 10165921001) was then introduced, and the samples were rotated for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by heat inactivation through boiling. The resulting samples were prepared for western blot analysis.

Sample collection and processing

Upon completion of the treatment regimen, mice were fasted for 24 h with free access to water. Blood was collected by retro-orbital puncture and centrifuged (3000 r/min, 15 min) after a 2-h clotting period. The resulting serum supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C. Following euthanasia by cervical dislocation, tissue samples were systematically processed. One portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological evaluation, while the remainder was snap-frozen and stored at − 80 °C for molecular analyses.

Serum biochemical tests

The biomarkers of serum ALT, AST, and Hyp were measured by kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The levels of TNF-α, IL-β, and IL-6 were detected using ELISA kits (MultiSciences (Lianke)Biotech Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions, with a microplate reader (Biotek, USA) at 450 nm to detect the sample contents.

Histopathological analysis

After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. The sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for general histology. Collagen distribution and muscle fiber architecture were evaluated using Masson’s trichrome stain. All slides were digitally scanned and visualized with a panoramic scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Kupffer cell extraction

Liver tissues were minced, washed with PBS, and digested overnight at 4 °C with 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV. The digested suspension was filtered through a 100 µm strainer and centrifuged (400 g, 5 min). Kupffer cells were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using 45% Percoll (900 g, 15 min). Cells were fixed with 4% PFA, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD68 primary antibody, followed by a fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and samples were imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

Results

PA significantly inhibits pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization rather than pro-inflammatory macrophage

Our initial investigation revealed that oleanolic acid (OA), ursolic acid (UA), and PA are the predominant triterpenoid compounds present in Ye Ya Chun15. Hence, to elucidate their impact on macrophage polarization, we employed the Raw264.7 mouse mononuclear macrophage cell line. Initially, we assessed the cytotoxicity of these compounds using a CCK-8 assay. Our results, depicted in Fig. 1A, demonstrated that OA and UA did not elicit any significant cytotoxic effects at the designated concentrations. Conversely, PA at a concentration of 5 μM notably affected Raw264.7 cell viability. Consequently, we proceeded to investigate the influence of 5 μM of the aforementioned monomers on macrophage polarization in subsequent experiments. Subsequently, we investigated their influence on pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization induced by 1 μg/ml LPS. Western blot analysis revealed that LPS notably upregulated iNOS and CD86 expression in pro-inflammatory macrophages. However, treatments with OA, UA, and PA did not alter the expression of iNOS and CD86 (Fig. 1B-D). Pro-inflammatory macrophages exhibited pronounced proinflammatory behavior, prompting us to assess the levels of NO and TNF-α in the culture medium supernatant, which were unaffected by the treatments (Fig. 1E-F). Remarkably, the polarization of pro-inflammatory macrophages was significantly linked to glycolytic activity. Consistent with the aforesaid findings, we evaluated changes in glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes such as HK-II, PKM2, LDHA, and end products16, finding no effect on pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization following treatment with these compounds (Fig. 1G-K). Subsequently, we investigated their impact on pro-fibrotic macrophages. The polarization of pro-fibrotic macrophages induced by IL-4 was distinguished by heightened expression of Arg1 and CD206, along with the secretion of the profibrotic factor TGFβ. This polarization was unaffected by OA and UA but was reversed by PA intervention. In addition, we employed flow cytometry as an alternative method to assess the membrane expression of CD86 and CD206. The results obtained were generally consistent with those derived from western blot analysis (Fig. S1A). Furthermore, to rigorously validate the universality and potential translational relevance of PA in regulating macrophage polarization, we systematically verified its effects in more physiologically relevant models: primary mouse bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) (Fig. S1B-G) and human monocyte-derived THP-1 (Fig. S1H-M) macrophages. The regulatory effect of PA on macrophage polarization was consistently and reproducibly confirmed across these different species and cellular origins. In essence, PA demonstrates pronounced inhibition of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization while minimally influencing pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization.

PA suppresses pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by impeding FAO in macrophages

Liver macrophages exhibit diverse roles and significant phenotypic flexibility based on their polarization, local microenvironment, and fibrosis stage17. Of note, TGF-β plays a pivotal role in liver fibrosis by activating HSCs, facilitating their transformation into profibrotic cells, and inducing ECM deposition, thereby driving fibrotic progression18. Hence, in the following section, we will explore the impact of PA on pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization in greater depth. A concentration gradient of PA was established to assess its dose-dependent inhibition of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, with 5 μM PA suggesting superior efficacy in preventing pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. The transcription factor STAT6 serves essential roles in regulating pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, where phosphorylated STAT6 forms dimers that translocate to the nucleus, binding to promoters of pro-fibrotic macrophage-related genes (Arg1, YM1, CD206) to facilitate pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization19. Our findings revealed that IL-4 dramatically upregulated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization markers such as p-STAT6, Arg1, CD206, and TGFβ, effects that were dose-dependently reversed by PA treatment (Fig. 2A-C). Pro-fibrotic macrophages predominantly rely on mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) for energy20, in contrast to pro-inflammatory macrophages that favor glycolysis. Our data proved that PA effectively decreased FAO, as evidenced by reduced expression of PPARγ, CD36, and CPT1 (Fig. 2D-G). Additionally, co-administration of FAO agonists (pioglitazone, PGZ) counteracted the inhibitory impact of PA on pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO (Fig. 2H-N). At the same time, immunofluorescence results showed that PA drastically reduced CD206 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2O). These results indicate that PA modulates macrophage metabolic reprogramming to inhibit pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by suppressing FAO.

PA prevents FAO through upregulating the IRG1-itaconic acid axis

In light of the critical component in itaconic acid in modulating macrophage function21, we explored the involvement of IRG1/itaconic acid in the suppression of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by PA. Our findings revealed a distinct increase in IRG1 expression following PA treatment (Fig. 3A, B), prompting the utilization of IRG1-IN-1 (50 μM) and the itaconic acid derivative 4-OI (100 μM) in subsequent investigations. Noticeably, IRG1-IN-1 effectively counteracted the anti-pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization induced by PA, as evidenced by elevated levels of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization markers such as p-STAT6/STAT6, Arg1, CD206, and TGFβ (Fig. 3C–G), as well as PPARγ, CD36, and CPT1, proteins associated with FAO (Fig. 3H–J). Encouragingly, treatment with 4-OI reversed these outcomes. Immunofluorescence staining for CD206 was consistent with the western blot findings (Fig. 3K). Collectively, our data denote that PA hinders pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization through the IRG1-itaconic acid pathway.

PA increases IRG1 expression by hindering β-arrestin 2-mediated ubiquitination.

In this chapter, we aim to delve into the precise mechanism by which PA upregulated IRG1 expression. Initially, we assessed the impact of PA on IRG1 mRNA levels and found no significant alteration (Fig. 4A), prompting an investigation into the potential influence of PA on IRG1 protein degradation. The primary cellular pathways for protein degradation encompass the autophagy lysosomal pathway and the ubiquitin proteasome pathway22. To elucidate the mechanism of IRG1 protein degradation, we employed inhibitors targeting these pathways, namely CQ (50 μM) to hinder lysosomal degradation and MG132 (5 μM) to impede proteasomal degradation. Treatment with CQ did not block IL-4-mediated IRG1 degradation, as evidenced by comparable IRG1 expression levels between the IL-4 + PA group and the IL-4 + PA + CQ group, indicating that the autophagy lysosomal pathway is not involved in IRG1 degradation. Moreover, no significant disparity in IRG1 protein expression was observed between the IL-4 + PA group and the IL-4 + MG132 group, implying that PA inhibits IRG1 degradation to a similar extent as MG132. Importantly, IRG1 expression in the IL-4 + PA + MG132 group showed a pronounced increase compared to the IL-4 + PA group, emphasizing the role of the ubiquitin proteasome pathway in mediating IRG1 protein degradation (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, PA reduced IL-4-induced overall ubiquitination associated with pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization (Fig. 4D), further supporting the involvement of the ubiquitin proteasome pathway in regulating IRG1 degradation. Taken together, these results underscore the essentiality of the ubiquitin proteasome pathway in controlling the degradation of IRG1 protein (Fig. 4B, C). Recent research manifests that the ubiquitination of IRG1 mediated by β-arrestin 2 plays a role in steatohepatitis linked to metabolic dysfunction23. Our investigation revealed an upregulation of β-arrestin 2 expression during pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, unaffected by PA exposure (Fig. 4D). We postulated that PA might impact the β-arrestin 2-IRG1 interaction. Indeed, our CO-IP result findings supported this hypothesis, illustrating that PA treatment prevented the pull-down of IRG1 by β-arrestin 2 (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, we observed that PA treatment prevented the pull-down of ubiquitin by IRG1. Therefore, our data in this context indicate that PA disrupts IRG1 ubiquitination degradation by impeding the β-arrestin 2-IRG1 interaction.

PA targets β-arrestin 2 to block pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization

Given the important role of β-arrestin 2 in regulating pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by PA, we delved deeper into the underlying mechanism of PA inhibition of pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by manipulating β-arrestin 2 expression levels. Initially, we assessed pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO following β-arrestin 2 overexpression (OE-Arrb2) (Fig. 5D). Enhanced expression of β-arrestin 2 exacerbated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO in the presence of IL-4, as evidenced by elevated levels of p-STAT6/STAT6, Arg1, CD206, TGFβ, PPARγ, CD36, and CPT1. This observation suggested that increased β-arrestin 2 expression intensified pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. However, despite this, PA treatment continued to weaken pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO in the context of OE-Arrb2 alongside IL-4 stimulation (Fig. 5A–C and E). Subsequently, we investigated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO upon β-arrestin 2 knockout (KO-Arrb2) (Fig. 5I). Remarkably, silencing β-arrestin 2 markedly attenuated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and FAO, with PA failing to decrease these processes in the setting of β-arrestin 2 knockdown combined with IL-4 exposure (Fig. 5F–H and J). These findings further supported the notion that PA inhibited pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by impeding the interaction between β-arrestin 2 and IRG1. Subsequently, we investigated whether β-arrestin 2 is a target of PA. We employed CETSA, a label-free binding assay, to confirm the interaction between PA and β-arrestin 2 by monitoring alterations in protein thermostability induced by compound binding. Our findings revealed that PA diminished the thermal denaturation of β-arrestin 2 within the temperature range of 42 °C to 62 °C, implying that PA binds to β-arrestin 2 and triggers conformational modifications that render it less susceptible to thermal denaturation (Fig. 5K, L). Furthermore, we validated the binding of PA to β-arrestin 2 and its enhanced resistance to protease degradation through DARTS western blot analysis. PA markedly enhanced protein stability under 1:1000 pronase (10 mg/ml) protease cleavage conditions. (Fig. 5M). In summary, the above results proved that PA hindered pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by binding to β-arrestin 2, thereby preventing β-arrestin 2 from interacting with IRG1.

PA alleviates liver tissue damage and liver fibrosis in CCl4-exposed mice

Given that PA has a potent inhibitory effect on pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization in vitro, we subsequently investigated its anti-liver fibrosis effects. To assess the effectiveness of PA, a liver fibrosis model was induced through intraperitoneal administration of 1 ml/kg CCl4, with SM (100 mg/kg) serving as the positive control. Liver damage was evaluated by analyzing serum AST and ALT levels and examining histological alterations in liver lobules via HE staining. The model group exhibited substantial increases in AST and ALT levels, along with pronounced hepatocyte swelling and disrupted arrangement, as well as extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. Conversely, treatment with PA or SM significantly ameliorated liver injury in mice (Fig. 6A, B, and H). In view of the diminished inflammatory cell infiltration in liver tissue, we proceeded to investigate the expression of proinflammatory factors. ELISA analysis revealed a substantial increase in serum levels of TNF-α, IL-β, and IL-6, which were significantly reduced following administration of PA and SM. Masson staining, Sirius red staining, and enhanced expression of α-SMA, collagen 1, and TGFβ in liver tissue indicated collagen deposition and abnormal collagen fiber proliferation in the livers of mice in the model group, suggesting liver fibrosis. However, administration of PA or SM significantly reversed this effect. In summary, PA potently ameliorates CCl4-induced liver damage, inflammatory infiltration, and liver fibrosis in mice.

PA reduces pro-fibrotic macrophage infiltration in liver fibrosis

In the preceding section, it was demonstrated that PA exhibited notable antifibrotic properties. Subsequently, we examined the effect of PA on the polarization of primary hepatic macrophages during liver fibrosis. PA markedly impeded pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization, as proven by reducing the expression of Arg1 and CD206 in liver tissue. Conversely, it did not significantly influence pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, as evidenced by the lack of effect on iNOS and CD86 expression (Fig. 7A, B). Notably, PA had a minimal impact on hepatic glycolytic flux but hindered pro-fibrotic macrophage infiltration by suppressing FAO. This was supported by the unaltered expression of HK-II, PKM2, and LDHA, alongside the inhibition of PPARγ, CD36, and CPT1 expression (Fig. 7C–J). Furthermore, while PA did not modulate β-arrestin 2 expression in liver tissue, it impeded STAT6-mediated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization by enhancing IRG1 expression (Fig. 7K–N). The flow cytometry results of CD206 single staining showed that PA also inhibited pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization (Fig. 7O and P). These outcomes were consistent with those obtained in vitro, suggesting that PA could mitigate liver fibrosis by impeding pro-fibrotic macrophage infiltration in liver tissue (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Liver fibrosis is the result of abnormal scar formation in the liver during the healing process following chronic liver injury24. It is characterized by the excessive accumulation of ECM components, particularly collagen fibers, leading to the disruption of the liver tissue structure25. This pathological progression is primarily mediated by the persistent activation of HSCs26. Currently, regulating liver immune response has gradually become a hot topic in anti-fibrosis treatment27.

Among the various immune cells involved, macrophage-based anti-fibrotic therapies have been extensively studied and have shown reliable potential for treating liver fibrosis28. In the initial phase of liver damage, circulating monocytes differentiate into pro-inflammatory macrophages, which then migrate to the site of injury. On the one hand, these pro-inflammatory macrophages secrete numerous cytokines that directly activate quiescent HSCs to facilitate excessive extracellular matrix deposition29. On the other hand, they can recruit more immune cells by releasing chemokines to exacerbate the liver fibrosis30. As liver fibrosis progresses, pro-fibrotic macrophages play a more important role, which upregulate tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP-1) and downregulate matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) to inhibit matrix degradation, ultimately exacerbating liver fibrosis9.

Metabolic reprogramming is a pivotal factor in regulating macrophage function17,31. Research indicates that pro-fibrotic macrophages, not pro-inflammatory macrophages, predominantly utilize oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and FAO for sustained energy supply. Studies have demonstrated that the transcription factor PPARγ plays a critical role in regulating the polarization of macrophages towards the pro-fibrotic macrophage phenotype by controlling FAO32. It upregulates the expression of fatty acid transporters like CD36, facilitates the uptake of free fatty acids (FFA), and boosts the expression of mitochondrial FAO pathways (such as CPT1A) to drive macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory and pro-repair pro-fibrotic macrophage phenotype.

Recent studies have identified the immune metabolite itaconic acid (ITA), produced by activated macrophages via IRG1, as a powerful endogenous “brake” for pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. Specifically, ITA exerts its inhibitory effects by covalently modifying signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), a key transcription factor downstream of the IL-4/IL-13 signaling pathway that is critical for pro-fibrotic macrophage gene expression. This modification blocks nuclear translocation of phosphorylated STAT633, thereby disrupting the transcriptional programs required for full pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization and its associated pro-repair functions, including efficient matrix remodeling. This antagonism of ITA highlights the complex metabolic regulation of macrophage fate.

In the present study, PA significantly improved CCl4-induced liver damage, inflammatory infiltration, collagen deposition, and abnormal collagen fibre proliferation in mice. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of β-arrestin 2 due to abnormal regulation has been implicated in liver fibrosis34,35. Here, our results indicated that PA inhibited the interaction between β-arrestin 2 and IRG1 by binding to β-arrestin 2 directly, which blocked the ubiquitination and degradation of IRG1. The upregulation of IRG1 assuaged liver fibrosis by decreasing the flux of FAO and impeding STAT6-mediated pro-fibrotic macrophage polarization. This study enhances the understanding of the IRG1/STAT6 axis in regulating macrophage function. It is the first report on the pharmacological mechanism study of β-arrestin 2 as a viable drug target. Furthermore, PA demonstrates excellent efficacy in combating oral submucosal fibrosis. Overall, this study offers a novel perspective on understanding the potential mechanism through which PA exerts its anti-fibrosis effects.
