Intraindividual Discordance Between Remnant Cholesterol and Low‐Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Associated With Incident Stroke: Results From 2 National Cohorts
Authors: Cancan Cui, Pingan Li, Yitian Qi, Jiayin Song, Tianjiao Han, Xinyun Shang, Lois Balmer, Chen Sheng, Yining Zha, Zhonghang Xu, Xu Wang, Zhiyuan Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe atherogenic effect of remnant cholesterol is being increasingly acknowledged. This study aimed to explore the association of discordance between remnant cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol with stroke onset using 2 Chinese national cohorts.Methods and ResultsWe studied 11 139 participants from CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) and 5993 participants from CHNS (China Health and Nutrition Survey) aged 45 years or older. The discordance between remnant cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol was defined using the difference in percentile units (>15 units). There were 988 (8.9%) and 128 (2.1%) stroke events reported during follow‐up in the 2 cohorts. Elevated remnant cholesterol was significantly associated with a higher risk of total stroke in 2 cohorts. After adjusting for remnant cholesterol level, the discordantly high remnant cholesterol group was significantly associated with an increased stroke risk (CHARLS: subdistribution hazard ratio [sHR], 1.31 [95 CI, 1.10–1.55]; CHNS: sHR, 1.84 [95 CI, 1.15–3.08]) compared with the discordantly low group. Consistent results were shown even among those with optimal low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level.ConclusionsThe discordance between remnant cholesterol and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, representing the intraindividual discrepancy, is significantly associated with stroke onset among Chinese adults.

Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide, and the related economic burden of treatment and poststroke health care is substantial. 1 Cholesterol metabolism plays a key role in cardiovascular diseases; however, the association of cholesterol and stroke is much less clear. 2 The population‐attributable risk of total stroke caused by hypercholesterolemia was reported to be much lower than that of myocardial infarction. 3 A low level of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was associated with an increased stroke risk, while the population‐attributable risk of low HDL‐C was low. 4 Epidemiological studies found no consistent relationships between cholesterol levels and overall stroke risk, such as total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and HDL‐C. 5 , 6 This is likely explained by the different effects of cholesterol on ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke. 7 , 8 Individuals with low LDL‐C levels (<70 mg/dL) may still be at high risk of stroke. 9 CGPS (Copenhagen General Population Study) also reported that the proportion of ischemic stroke attributable to elevated non–HDL‐C was double that attributable to elevated LDL‐C. 10 In this context, emerging evidence suggests the atherogenic effect of remnant cholesterol on the residual risk of ischemic stroke. 11 , 12 , 13

Remnant cholesterol is the cholesterol content of triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins (ie, the intermediate‐density lipoprotein and very LDL in the fasting state). 14 There are ethnic differences and heterogeneity of cholesterol patterns. 15 Previous studies have investigated the effects of remnant cholesterol on carotid atherosclerosis, stroke, and complications of diabetes in the Chinese population. 12 , 16 , 17 Of note, a recent study suggested that the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C could predict the risk of composite cardiovascular diseases. 18 The discordance index characterizes the relative distribution difference of remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C in addition to the absolute remnant cholesterol levels. Our previous study reported the effect of discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C on arterial stiffness. 19 However, the effect of the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C on stroke remains unknown.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association of remnant cholesterol and its discordance with LDL‐C in terms of stroke onset using 2 Chinese national cohorts. We hypothesized that the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C could contribute to the residual risk stratification of stroke onset beyond remnant cholesterol level.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

The current study used data from a subcohort of CHNS (China Health and Nutrition Survey) and a subcohort of CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) of participants older than 45 years. The CHNS is a prospective cohort study and an international collaborative project between the Carolina Population Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and the National Institute for Nutrition and Health (formerly the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety) at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. 20 CHNS collected disease history and physical examination data from 1991 to 2015, covering 9 provinces that vary in geography, economic development, public resources, and health indicators. A multistage, random cluster process was used to select the samples surveyed in each of the provinces. Our study included participants in 2009 as baseline because blood samples were collected and tested at this time, and follow‐up surveys were repeated in 2011 and 2015.

CHARLS is an ongoing open cohort, aiming to collect a high‐quality nationally representative sample of Chinese residents aged 45 and older to serve the needs of scientific research on middle‐aged and older individuals. 21 The baseline national wave of CHARLS was initiated in 2011 and followed every 2 years, including approximately 10 000 households and 17 500 individuals in 150 counties/districts and 450 villages/resident committees. CHARLS collected data on demographics, health status, and functioning and blood biomarkers. Our study included participants in 2011 at baseline and repeated the follow‐up surveys in 2013, 2015, and 2018. Baseline and follow‐up processes are shown in Figure S1.

We excluded 221 participants who were younger than 45 years; 271 had a baseline diagnosis of stroke and 216 lacked available lipid data. In the CHNS cohort, we included participants 45 years and older and excluded 121 who had a baseline diagnosis of stroke and 65 who lacked available lipid data. Finally, a total of 11 139 and 5993 participants were recruited in the analyses for CHARLS and CHNS, respectively. This study was reported following the Strengthening of Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.

Laboratory Measurement

Triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, and glucose were measured by an enzymatic colorimetric method using the Olympus Automatic Biochemical Analyzer (Hitachi 747). The coefficients of variation were <4.0% for triglycerides and HDL‐C, <3.0% for total cholesterol, and LDL‐C <5.0% for fasting glucose. Concentrations of high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein were measured by the immunoturbidimetric method. Glycated hemoglobin was measured by ultrahigh‐performance liquid chromatography. Serum creatinine was measured by the picric acid method in CHNS and the rate‐blanked and compensated Jaffe creatinine method in CHARLS. The detailed information of sample collection, transportation, storage, and analysis has been previously described 22 and on the CHARLS website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). Non–HDL‐C was calculated as total cholesterol minus HDL‐C. Remnant cholesterol was defined as non–HDL‐C minus LDL‐C.

Data Collection and Definition

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, residence, education level, and marriage status), smoking status, current drinking status, and history of diseases (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease or myocardial infarction, and stroke) were collected via questionnaires at every 2‐year follow‐up. The core CHARLS questionnaires include sections on demographics, family structure and changes, health status and functioning, general health, physician‐diagnosed chronic illnesses, lifestyle, and health‐related behaviors. Detailed information has been previously reported in the cohort profile. 21 In brief, smoking status was divided into never, former, and current. Residence type was grouped into rural and urban. Education level was divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary. Marriage status was grouped into married and other. Anthropometric measurements were performed for height, weight, waist circumference, lower right leg length and arm length, lung capacity, grip strength, speed of repeated chair stand, blood pressure, walking speed, and balance tests. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure are presented as the average of 3 measurements. 21 Hypertension was defined as systolic pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or a self‐reported diagnosis history of hypertension. 23 Diabetes was defined as fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5 mmol/L, or self‐reported diagnosis of diabetes. 24 Diagnosis history of stroke (including ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke and excluding transient ischemic attack) was self‐reported and collected at each follow‐up survey.

Discordance Definition

We calculated the discordance by the remnant cholesterol percentile minus the LDL‐C percentile using the cumulative distribution function separately in each cohort. We defined discordance as a difference of >15 in percentile units (remnant cholesterol percentile minus LDL‐C percentile). The population was classified into 3 groups based on previous studies: the discordantly low group (remnant cholesterol percentile minus LDL‐C percentile ≤−15 percentile units), the concordant group (remnant cholesterol percentile minus LDL‐C percentile within 15 percentile units), and the discordantly high group (remnant cholesterol percentile minus LDL‐C percentile ≥15 percentile units). The discordance represents the relative distance considering the population distribution (percentile unit) between 2 related markers (such as cholesterol lipids and estimated glomerular filtration rate values), which has been widely used in population studies. 18 , 19 , 25 , 26

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study population are described using mean (SD) for normally distributed variables, median (25th–75th percentile) for skewed continuous variables, and frequency (proportion) for categorical variables. The distribution of remnant cholesterol is presented using a density plot and box plot, stratified by the presence or absence of incident stroke.

The Fine and Gray competing risk model, accounting for the competing risk of death and loss to follow‐up, was used to explore the longitudinal association between remnant cholesterol and incident stroke. We measured the follow‐up time as the time elapsed from the date of the last interview to either the date of diagnosis of stroke or the date of the latest interview or the date of death, or loss to follow‐up, whichever came first. Remnant cholesterol was analyzed both as a continuous variable and a categorical variable grouped using quartile levels and a clinical cutoff point (30 mg/dL). 27 Subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) and 95% CIs were calculated. Model 1 was primarily adjusted for age (years, continuous) and sex (male, female); model 2 was further adjusted for residence (rural, urban), marriage (married, others), education (primary, secondary, third), body mass index (kg/m2, continuous), smoking (never, past, current), drinking (current, none), hypertension (yes, no), diabetes (yes, no), coronary heart disease or myocardial infarction (yes, no), serum creatine (mg/dL, continuous), and high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (mg/L, continuous). We evaluated the association between the percentile discordance of remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C with incident stroke, further adjusting for remnant cholesterol level to evaluate the relationship between percentile discordance and incident stroke beyond the effects of remnant cholesterol itself. The dose–response relationships of the percentile discordance with stroke risk were visualized using a restricted cubic spline function using 3 knots at the fifth, 50th, and 95th percentiles.

We performed multiple sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the analysis using 5‐iteration imputed data sets by the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method and pooled the results. The detailed missing numbers of covariates are shown in Table S1. Second, we also examined the association among those with optimal LDL‐C (<100 mg/dL). Third, we reanalyzed the association between discordance in remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C with incident stroke among the whole population of the CHNS cohort. Fourth, we performed a cross‐sectional analysis at baseline, including individuals with stroke diagnosis history or those lost to follow‐up.

All other statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

The institutional review boards of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety and Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention approved CHNS. CHARLS was approved by the institutional review board of Peking University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Remnant Cholesterol and Stroke Onset

There were 988 stroke events reported during a maximum follow‐up of 7 years in CHARLS, and there were 128 stroke events reported during a maximum follow‐up of 6 years in CHNS. Figure S2 shows the positive dose–response relationships between remnant cholesterol and incident stroke. We observed that remnant cholesterol was significantly associated with incident stroke after adjusting for traditional risk factors (sHR per 10 mg/dL, 1.04 [95% CI, 1.01–1.06 of CHARLS], and 1.10 [95% CI, 1.01–1.18 of CHNS]) as shown in Table 2. Using 30 mg/dL as the cutoff point, individuals in the high remnant cholesterol group had a significantly increased risk of stroke (sHR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.09–1.43 of CHARLS], and 1.78 [95% CI, 1.17–2.70 of CHNS]).

Sensitivity Analysis

The pooled results remained consistent using imputed data sets (Table S1). Among individuals with optimal LDL‐C levels (<100 mg/dL), we found that those with high remnant cholesterol levels (≥30 mg/dL) also had an increased risk of stroke. The percentile discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C was significantly associated with total stroke, as shown in Table S2. When we additionally included individuals younger than 45 years in the CHNS cohort, the results were largely consistent, as shown in Table S3. In the cross‐sectional analysis, there were 271 and 121 participants reporting a history of stroke events in the CHARLS and CHNS cohorts, respectively. There was a significant cross‐sectional association of discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C with prevalence of stroke (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that remnant cholesterol was consistently associated with the risk of total stroke using 2 Chinese national cohorts. Our findings demonstrate that individuals with a high discordance of remnant cholesterol versus LDL‐C have an excess risk of total stroke, even among those with optimal LDL‐C levels. Moreover, assessment of the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C contributes to the risk stratification and primary prevention of stroke beyond the remnant cholesterol level itself.

Triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins can penetrate and accumulate in the arterial wall and subsequently cause atherosclerosis and low‐grade inflammation. Remnant cholesterol is regarded as the most pathogenic component of triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins. 14 , 28 , 29 A study found that very LDL‐C explained one‐half of the myocardial infarction risk but not very LDL triglycerides. 30 Previous cohort studies have confirmed the atherogenic effect of remnant cholesterol on arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease, 18 major adverse cardiovascular events and all‐cause mortality. 31 Genetic studies have also supported the causal associations of remnant cholesterol with ischemic heart disease, 32 , 33 , 34 myocardial infarction35 and aortic valve stenosis. 36 Remnant cholesterol reclassifies the risk of ischemic heart disease and myocardial infarction and considerably improves risk prediction. 37 The fact is that the effect of cholesterol should be heterogeneous in terms of stroke. 2 Varbo et al and Wadström et al reported that remnant cholesterol increases the risk of ischemic stroke. Li et al found that the variability of remnant cholesterol is associated with incident ischemic stroke. 12 Our previous study investigated the causal effects of genetically predicted remnant cholesterol on stroke subtypes. 38 Notably, the current findings indicate that the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C is another significant risk factor for total stroke, as demonstared in 2 Chinese cohorts. Sun et al demonstrated the positive association of LDL‐C with ischemic stroke and an equally strong inverse association with intracerebral hemorrhage. 7 A review concluded that a higher level of LDL‐C seemed to be associated with a lower risk of hemorrhagic stroke. 8 In contrast, 2 Mendelian randomization studies concluded that LDL‐C was not significantly associated with ischemic stroke, despite randomized trials showing a reduction in the risk of ischemic stroke with LDL‐C lowering. 39 A Chinese cohort study reported that very LDL‐C concentrations (≤40 mg/dL) were significantly associated with increased risks of both ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke among adults with optimal LDL‐C levels, 9 which was also observed in the supplementary analysis of a Danish cohort. 13 Taken together, these findings indicate that remnant cholesterol could be a better predictor of ischemic stroke and total stroke than LDL‐C. Another study also found that remnant cholesterol, but not LDL‐C, was associated with all‐cause mortality risk. 31 Remnant cholesterol could stratify individuals at a higher risk of total stroke even among those with optimal LDL‐C levels. There existed a distinct distribution of remnant cholesterol between the CHARLS and CHNS cohorts (median, 20.1 mg/dL versus 13.5 mg/dL). The geographic difference among the surveyed participants and the higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes in CHARLS could possibly explain the higher median level of remnant cholesterol. There existed a difference regarding the incidence rates of stroke between 2 cohorts. The biggest difference is the residence and education level, with 81% of CHARLS participants coming from rural areas, resulting in lower education attainment. Stroke burden in China has remained particularly high in rural areas according to a nationwide population‐based survey. 40 There is a large difference in stroke prevalence and incidence among different regions in China attributable to different diets and cultures. In addition, the higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, as significant risk factors of stroke, in CHARLS could possibly explain the higher incidence of stroke.

The discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C has been gradually applied to predict the risk of cardiovascular diseases. 18 Fu et al found that within the high and low LDL‐C subgroups, high baseline remnant cholesterol was associated with a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events among patients with type 2 diabetes. 41 Similarly, a previous study investigated the interaction between remnant cholesterol and inflammation levels on new‐onset stroke and indicated the effect of remnant cholesterol. 42 To our knowledge, our study investigated the longitudinal association of the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C with stroke for the first time. We found that people in the discordantly high remnant cholesterol versus LDL‐C group had an increased risk of incident stroke. Among individuals with optimal LDL‐C levels, the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C appeared to further stratify the high‐risk group for stroke. When remnant cholesterol and its discordance with LDL‐C were mutually adjusted in the regression models, we found that the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C was still significantly associated with the risk of stroke, suggesting that the discordance of remnant cholesterol could provide an additional index for the risk stratification of total stroke beyond remnant cholesterol level.

There are potential mechanisms underlying the association of remnant cholesterol with stroke. Remnant particles accumulate in the arterial wall, potentially propagating local and systemic inflammation and vascular damage. 43 Remnant cholesterol represents a causal risk factor for low‐grade inflammation, possibly attributable to triglyceride‐rich lipoprotein degradation and uptake into macrophage foam cells in the arterial intima. 44 A study claimed that remnant cholesterol causes both low‐grade inflammation and ischemic heart disease, whereas LDL‐C causes ischemic heart disease without inflammation. 45 There has been increasing interest in interventions for lowering remnant cholesterol, including eicosapentaenoic acid, fibrates, and gene silencing therapy. 46 A randomized crossover study concluded that statins significantly reduced remnant cholesterol levels in addition to LDL‐C, 47 which potentially explains why statin therapy is likely to have a net benefit for the prevention of overall stroke beyond the intervention of LDL‐C. 7 , 48 Regarding the discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C, epidemiological studies have evaluated the effects on arterial stiffness 19 and composite atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. 18 Discordance analyses also show that participants with discordantly high remnant cholesterol had a higher risk of incident stroke, suggesting that the cholesterol content of remnants may modify stroke risk information beyond the LDL particle burden. Findings suggest that remnant cholesterol is a significant predictor of stroke considering the distinct effect on macrophages, vascular walls, and key plasma lipid regulatory proteins such as apolipoprotein C3. 49 , 50 The underlying mechanism of interaction between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C remains to be further clarified.

Strength and Limitations

This study used 2 national cohorts to investigate and validate the association of discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C with incident stroke. The results should be interpreted in the context of limitations. First, remnant cholesterol concentration was not directly measured but obtained by calculation, which may deviate from the actual level and lead to measurement errors. 51 However, the calculated remnant cholesterol is closely correlated with the directly measured concentration and has been widely used in most population studies. Second, data on stroke subtypes were not available in the 2 cohorts, making it impossible to determine whether remnant cholesterol is more strongly associated with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. The effect of remnant cholesterol on ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke warrants further cohort studies. In addition, stroke diagnosis history was based on questionnaire and self‐reported, which could cause information and recall bias. However, several previous studies and international aging surveys (such as HRS and ELSA) have validated the feasibility of self‐reported outcomes in population‐based research. Third, a causal association could not be claimed in the current study because of the observational design. Clinical trials are needed to evaluate the benefits of further lowering remnant cholesterol for the primary prevention of stroke, particularly in individuals with discordantly high remnant cholesterol levels. Fourth, there exists potential confounding bias attributable to the lack of information on specific lipid‐lowering medication such as statins, which are closely associated with stroke onset.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, this study found that discordance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C is significantly associated with stroke onset, even among individuals with optimal LDL‐C levels. The findings suggest that the distribution distance between remnant cholesterol and LDL‐C is a potential risk factor for the primary prevention of stroke.
