Detecting Adverse Events Following Acute Care Encounters among Veterans Prescribed Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs
Authors: Amber J Hackstadt, Amy M Perkins, Jesse O Wrenn, Thomas J Reese, Melissa D Rubenstein, Robert Winter, Chad A Dorn, Matthew R Martino, Jin H Han, Michael J Ward, Michael E Matheny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo model the occurrence of adverse events (AEs) among Veterans prescribed nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) during emergency department and urgent care encounters within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) care setting. NSAIDs can increase the risk for gastrointestinal, renal, and cardiovascular AEs, particularly in older adults, patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), or patients with polypharmacy.MethodsWe applied eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) modeling and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) methods along with logistic regression to model the occurrence of AEs in a cohort of Veterans prescribed NSAIDs seen between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2023. The outcome was defined as a composite of acute coronary syndrome, acute kidney injury, allergic reactions, gastrointestinal bleeding, or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) within 2 to 30 days following the encounter. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values and calibration plots. Observed-to-expected (OE) ratios for AEs were calculated.ResultsWe identified 1,112,819 acute care encounters for 736,677 unique patients and a total of 50,278 (4.5%) AEs. The XGBoost and LASSO regression models performed well across time, with AUC values ranging from 0.77 to 0.79. GERD and a diagnosis of CKD stage 4 or 5/dialysis were identified as having a harmful association with AEs. The OE ratios varied by VHA care site.ConclusionsThe risk adjustment models had good discrimination performance across time and allowed us to identify significant risk-adjusted site variation. The models may be useful in clinical decision support and population health interventions with NSAIDs.Keywords: machine learning, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, emergency department, urgent care, adverse events

The Bottom Line

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are commonly used medications but have significant adverse events (AEs) associated with them. We created risk adjustment models in a national cohort of Veterans prescribed NSAIDs during emergency department and urgent care encounters. This study highlighted that a composite outcome was successfully modeled for NSAID AE risk assessment and works to address that very low event rates in risk adjustment models frequently limit their utility and accuracy. The models maintained performance over time and may be used in multiple ways to help identify areas for improvement in addressing pain with NSAIDs.

Introduction

Background

Up to 28% of all emergency department (ED) and urgent care (UC) visits are related to adverse drug events.1, 2, 3, 4 Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the most commonly prescribed drug class in the acute setting and are a significant source of adverse events (AEs).5, 6, 7 Although these medications seem innocuous compared with alternative analgesics (ie, opioids), NSAIDs are high risk for gastrointestinal (GI), renal, and cardiovascular AEs, particularly in older adults and patients with polypharmacy or kidney disease.8, 9, 10, 11 Older Veterans seen in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) care have greater medical needs than younger patients, given their higher rates of concomitant medications and mental health conditions.5

Modeling the occurrence of AEs associated with acute care encounters in which NSAIDs were prescribed can provide useful information to be used in interventions aimed at reducing the occurrence of AEs. Risk adjustment models can be tailored and focused to provide valuable information to providers about individual health care encounters or operate at a population health level. Risk adjustment models, even models that are for population use, benefit from temporal surveillance and updating to maintain model performance over time, as shifts can occur over time with clinical practice change and occurred at a larger scale during the pandemic.12,13

Importance

There have been few models detecting AEs following NSAID exposure. Most have evaluated individual classes of AEs, such as acute kidney injury (AKI), gastric ulcers, or cardiovascular events.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Our analysis considered a composite outcome and looks at a national cohort of Veterans, which increases event rates and guards against poor model performance due to very low event rates, though it must be balanced against more heterogeneous risk factor associations.

Goals of This Investigation

To build on previous studies, we developed multiple risk adjustment models to detect a composite adverse event outcome following NSAID prescriptions in a national cohort of Veterans seeking emergent or urgent care. These models can be used to identify outliers, either sites or providers, that have higher or lower than expected AE rates. The risk-adjusted models are intended to be added to an intervention that provides feedback to providers, CRAFT (Care Review, Assessment & Feedback Tool).5,14 We compared the benefits and limitations of the risk adjustment models and evaluated their performance across time. We used these models to provide risk-adjusted observed-to-expected ratios (OEs) and report important factors associated with AEs in this national Veteran population.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We used a retrospective observational cohort to create risk adjustment models for AEs among Veterans seen in the acute care setting (all ED and UC visits) in the VHA care setting from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2023, and prescribed NSAID medications.

Qualifying Encounters

We included encounters for patients ≥18 years of age. Encounters resulting in hospital admission were excluded, as were encounters for Veterans who did not survive longer than 1-day post-encounter. The Institutional Review Board and the Research and Development Committee of the Tennessee Valley Healthcare System VA approved this study (gov.IRBNET 1573619).

Data Sources

We used the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) data set, which contains electronic health records (EHRs) and claims data from VHA care sites.22 VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure (VINCI) hosts a data warehouse that contains information about unique patients' inpatient and outpatient encounters, demographics, diagnoses, treatments, and procedures.

Composite Adverse Event Outcome

The primary outcome of interest was a composite outcome of observed AEs within 2 to 30 days after the acute care visit. The AEs included in the composite outcome were acute coronary syndrome (ACS), acute kidney injury (AKI) stage 2+, allergic reaction, GI bleeding, or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). These are well-known AEs that have been associated with NSAID exposure.5,6,23 With the exception of AKI, AEs were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. AKI was identified using baseline and post-index estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) serum creatinine level definitions.23 See Table S1 for details. To identify incident AEs, we used a lookback exclusion period. Details on this exclusion period can be found in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Patient Characteristics and Care Setting Variables

We created several patient and care setting variables to include as potential covariates. We included information available up to the day following the acute care encounter for model development. The outcome ascertainment window began 2 days after the encounter. We included patient demographic characteristics (eg, age, sex, marital status, education, and race), patient comorbidities (eg, Charlson comorbidity score and cardiovascular disease), and patient medication variables (eg, anticoagulants, glucocorticoids, and diuretics). As an additional covariate, we considered a 3-tier system to classify the appropriateness of each NSAID prescription. The 3-tier system was derived from clinical guidelines and developed by a team of physicians and pharmacists. It includes “tier 3: likely inconsistent with guidelines,” “tier 2: potentially inconsistent with guidelines,” or “tier 1: consistent with guidelines.”23,24 Prescriptions were assigned to the highest tier that was met. See Table S2 for the complete list of candidate variables, including patient demographic characteristics, and Table S3 for full definitions and background references for the NSAID tiers. We summarized the AE rates by NSAID tier and by sex and age groups.

Data Analysis and Risk Adjustment Models

We implemented and evaluated the performance of 2 modeling approaches to estimate the probability of an AE. The first approach used eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and was a data-driven approach to obtain the estimated probabilities.25 The XGBoost model provided a flexible model that accounted for interaction terms as part of the modeling framework and has been used in other NSAID AE models.26,27 The XGBoost algorithm provided a score that indicates how valuable each feature was in the construction of the model (importance score) and was used to identify influential variables for the AE outcomes.

The second approach used a logistic regression model along with a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) approach for variable selection.28 Once the variables were selected using the LASSO approach, a logistic regression model was fitted to the data. It has the advantage of providing odds ratios (ORs) to quantify the estimated odds of an AE for different levels of the variables of interest and has been used in other NSAID AE models.17,29 More details about these approaches, including how missing data is handled, can be found in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Each modeling technique was developed from a single calendar year of data and first applied on acute care encounters during that same year by bootstrap sampling of the development data sets. The models were then applied (scored) on the following year of data. The yearly update aligns with a practice of yearly full remodeling to perform model temporal updating and was performed to help guard against calibration drift.30 Because we considered acute care encounters and not individual patients, Veterans could enter the study cohort more than 1 time and have correlated observations. Thus, we used 95% percentile bootstrapping to create CIs. The evaluation metrics described below were applied to each set of paired training year and subsequent year of data. The same set of potential covariates is considered for each of the training years of data for both the LASSO and XGBoost regression models.

Model Evaluation

We evaluated the discrimination of the models using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) curve (AUC) values.31 AUC values allow us to quantify each model’s ability to discriminate between patients who will experience an AE and those who will not.32 AUC values range from 0 to 1, with larger values suggesting better discrimination. We also evaluated the model by using calibration plots, calibrated slope, and the intercept of the calibration curve.33, 34, 35 If there was perfect agreement, the calibration curve would be a diagonal line with a slope of 1.32,33,35 As a sensitivity analysis, we looked at model performance using sex subgroups (male vs female). Percentile bootstrapped CIs were created for the AUC values, calibrated slope, and intercept of the calibration plot.36,37 More details about the model evaluation approaches can be found in Supplementary Appendix 3.

Observed-to-expected Ratios

We evaluated the observed vs expected rates of AEs to evaluate coarse calibration performance for the population and to look at AE rates by VHA care site. We summed the probabilities of the scored models to get a risk-adjusted expected number of AEs and compared it to the observed number of AEs using an OE ratio.38,39 Percentile bootstrapped CIs were created for the OE ratios. See Supplementary Appendix 3 for more details. All modeling and model evaluation were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1).40

Results

Study Cohort and Characteristics

We identified a total of 1,112,819 acute care encounters among 736,677 unique patients where an NSAID was prescribed (Table 1). There were 745,909 (67.0%) patient encounters with the NSAIDs prescribed identified as consistent with guidelines (tier 1), 191,813 (17.2%) identified as potentially inconsistent with guidelines (tier 2), and 175,097 (15.7%) identified as likely inconsistent with guidelines (tier 3). Table S2 gives summary statistics describing the potential covariates for the 1,112,819 acute care encounters. There were 975,346 (87.6%) ED visits and 137,473 (12.4%) UC visits.

Adverse Events

We identified a total of 50,278 (4.5%) AEs occurring between 2 and 30 days of the acute care visit where an NSAID was prescribed, with most of the AEs being GERD (41,946 [3.8%] of encounters) followed by GI bleeding (3391 [0.3%] of encounters; Table 1). The AE rates differed by NSAID tier, with the highest rate of AEs (6.4%) observed for the likely inconsistent tier and lowest for the consistent tier (3.9%). We summarize the AE counts by sex and age groups in Table S4.

Limitations

Caution should be used when extrapolating results to other populations that are not well represented, especially for the non-veteran population. However, the process and framework we utilized in developing and evaluating the models are reproducible in other settings and environments. We used ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes as opposed to individual chart reviews, as chart reviews would not be feasible. It may be difficult to distinguish incident cases from a previous diagnosis, such as with GERD. Lookback windows were used to help identify new diagnoses. The risk adjustment models focused on NSAID prescribing patterns and identified if any NSAID was prescribed during the encounter. We did not focus on dosage information or include the specific type of NSAID prescription in our models, though these variables could be included in future models depending on the analysis goals. To reduce the computational burden, we used mean imputation to address missingness in the logistic regression model. In future work, we plan to use multiple imputation to guard against violations of the missing completely at random assumption.

Discussion

The XGBoost and LASSO models performed well when estimating the probability of AEs, and the estimated rates were close to the observed rates for all years. The AUC values were all 0.77 or above. This study highlighted that a composite outcome was able to be successfully modeled for NSAID AE risk assessment and works to address that very low event rates in risk adjustment models frequently limit their utility and accuracy. However, some individual AE types were quite rare, and this limited the ability to estimate the risk of each AE element individually. The performance across sites and over time may be impacted by the presence of an aggregate outcome, where underlying AE elements have different etiologies. Modern machine learning methods, such as XGBoost, can account for differential and interaction terms to allow for complex modeling to address a portion of this concern, but residual noise and heterogeneity remain.

Risk adjustment models can be tailored to provide valuable information to providers about individual health care encounters or operate at a population health level to provide feedback for populations and health care systems. For example, risk adjustment models can provide risk-adjusted rates for providers or facilities to account for heterogeneity in patients. In future work, we plan to integrate provider-level risk adjustment into the multicomponent intervention CRAFT tool. As noted in Figure 2, there were several facilities that had higher or lower than expected rates of AEs based on the risk adjustment conducted in this work. These types of models can provide information both to further improve risk adjustment modeling to account for factors that health system leaders would like to see included in the risk adjustment and to provide potential signals for further evaluation among outlying sites (in both directions). The VHA care site is continuing to work to include data on VHA-purchased care for Veterans from community providers, VA Community Care Network (CCN), in the OMOP dataset. We hope to include CCN data to further improve the risk adjustment models.

The LASSO model identified factors that were associated with AEs in this study and found an association between a history of GERD and AE. A history of GERD was also identified as the most influential variable using the XGBoost algorithm. The LASSO regression also identified an association between AE and a diagnosis of CKD stage 4 and CKD Stage 5/dialysis. These findings were consistent with prior literature and emphasize the importance of provider awareness of patient comorbidities prior to prescribing NSAIDs in the acute care setting where providers newly encounter patients and may not be familiar with their history. This study reinforces results found in another study that found Veterans with CKD continue to be prescribed NSAIDs at high rates.41

In summary, the risk adjustment models had good discrimination performance across time and allowed us to estimate the probabilities of AEs while adjusting for patient and care setting characteristics. Significant site-level variation in OE ratios may indicate care variation. Lastly, the risk adjustment models may be used in multiple ways to help identify areas for improvement in addressing pain with NSAIDs.
