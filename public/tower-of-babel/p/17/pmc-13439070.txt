System-level characterisation of hybrid LEO-terrestrial link performance under Ka-band propagation and interference constraints
Authors: Sujatha Rajkumar, Aditya Menon, Siddhanth Gupta, Indrasen Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybrid integration of Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite systems with terrestrial networks is a critical facilitator for beyond-5G connectivity, especially in situations where terrestrial coverage is constrained by cost or feasibility. At Ka-band frequencies, link performance is extremely susceptible to propagation impairments, including rain attenuation and atmospheric absorption, as well as terrestrial interference resulting from coexistence with ground-based networks. This study offers a simulation-driven, system-level analysis of hybrid LEO-terrestrial downlink performance. A Monte Carlo framework integrates established propagation models, free-space path loss, ITU-R P.676 atmospheric absorption, ITU-R P.838 rain attenuation, and Rician small-scale fading with a distance-dependent, spatially distributed terrestrial interference formulation. The emphasis is on identifying performance regimes and reliability limitations resulting from the cumulative effects of environmental and interference factors, rather than suggesting new channel models. The study measures received power, SNR, SINR, outage probability, bit error rate, and spectral efficiency at different satellite altitudes, carrier frequencies, rainfall intensities, and levels of interference. The results show that the satellite signal weakens with increasing slant distance, causing a transition from noise-limited to interference-limited operation, and demonstrate how rain attenuation degrades Ka-band link reliability. This work further illustrates that sustaining link reliability under adverse conditions requires adaptive adjustment of performance thresholds. The simulated SNR and capacity values for clear-sky conditions are verified against published Ka-band link budget benchmarks and ITU technical data. The results offer system-level perspectives for the design and planning of hybrid satellite-terrestrial communication systems.

Introduction

The integration of non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) with terrestrial cellular infrastructure will play an important role in enabling communication systems beyond 5G and 6G3,19. Hybrid satellite-terrestrial architectures working in the Ka-band (24–32 GHz) can potentially extend coverage, improve reliability, and support high-capacity services in urban, rural, maritime, and aeronautical scenarios. On the other hand, the fact that satellite and terrestrial transmissions may occur simultaneously results in highly complex propagation and interference interactions, which have a profound impact on link performance. Among the factors affecting the performance of these hybrid systems are free-space path loss, atmospheric absorption, rain attenuation, small-scale fading, and aggregate terrestrial interference. Moreover, these impairments have combined effects: when the satellite signal becomes weak at very long slant ranges, the level of terrestrial interference that dominates total link quality also rises.

Consequently, the system undergoes a shift from being noise-limited to interference-limited. While this regime transition is well-understood at a conceptual level in satellite link-budget theory23, its quantitative characterisation under the simultaneous influence of Ka-band atmospheric absorption, ITU-R-consistent rain attenuation, Rician fading, and spatially distributed terrestrial coexistence interference remains insufficiently characterised. Such a characterisation has immediate implications on link-budget design, reliability analysis, and the selection of interference mitigation techniques.

Recent papers have concentrated on proposing novel transmission techniques7,9, devising resource allocation mechanisms5,18, and conducting propagation-aware performance analysis14,16. While these contributions provide valuable insights at the signal-processing or network level, they typically rely on simplified or abstract channel assumptions and do not provide a structured characterisation of performance trade-offs under realistic, combined Ka-band propagation and interference conditions15. provides a stochastic-geometry-based coverage perspective addressing outage probability under combined channel conditions; however, it relies on network-topology abstractions rather than structured identification of propagation-induced regime transitions.

This paper addresses the identified gap by presenting a systematic, simulation-based characterisation of hybrid LEO–terrestrial link performance in the Ka-band. The key novelty lies in two complementary contributions: (i) the systematic quantitative characterisation of the noise-limited to interference-limited regime boundary for Ka-band LEO downlinks under the joint action of geometric path loss, ITU-R-consistent atmospheric absorption and rain attenuation, Rician fading, and spatially distributed terrestrial interference; and (ii) regime-aware design insights grounded in outage probability analysis and reliability-constrained operation. Related work

Researchers have investigated how satellite and terrestrial systems can be integrated within 5G and beyond-5G networks. Related work falls into three categories.

Architectural and standardisation studies

Surveys3,8,10 outline NTN architectures, 3GPP standardisation progress (Releases 17–18), and open challenges in mobility management and interoperability21. These works provide a broad system perspective but do not quantitatively assess physical-layer performance under realistic Ka-band propagation impairments.

Resource management and interference mitigation

Works1,2,4–6,9 investigate edge computing, beamforming, massive MIMO, and machine-learning-based optimisation for hybrid satellite-terrestrial networks. These approaches demonstrate performance gains at the network or signal-processing level but often rely on simplified or abstract channel assumptions, particularly with regard to atmospheric absorption and rain attenuation. The present work complements this body of literature by providing a propagation-realistic, simulation-based characterisation of the operating regimes that these resource management strategies must contend with.

Propagation-aware performance analysis

Studies14,16examine atmospheric effects, rain-induced attenuation, and channel statistics for LEO satellite links15. presents a hybrid satellite-terrestrial performance model addressing outage probability under combined channel conditions; however, its analysis relies on network-topology abstractions rather than the structured identification of propagation-induced regime transitions that characterise the present work. ITU-R Recommendations P.676, P.838, and P.83911–13 provide the standardised models for atmospheric gas absorption, rain attenuation, and rain-height geometry, respectively. Many existing works consider these impairments in isolation or focus on satellite-only scenarios without evaluating their combined impact under hybrid coexistence conditions.

Positioning of the present work

Despite the advances described above, the quantitative delineation of the noise-to-interference regime boundary for Ka-band LEO downlinks under the simultaneous influence of ITU-R-consistent atmospheric and rain propagation impairments and spatially distributed terrestrial coexistence interference remains insufficiently characterised. While the conceptual existence of this transition is established in satellite link-budget theory23, its precise location as a function of satellite altitude, carrier frequency, and interferer density has not been comprehensively quantified. The present work provides this characterisation through a controlled, simulation-based framework.

System and channel model

System description and assumptions

The scenario considered includes a single LEO satellite offering downlink communication to a single user equipment (UE). Apart from the intended satellite signal, the UE receives co-channel interference from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{int}}$$\end{document} terrestrial 5G base stations (gNBs) using the same spectrum. The satellite downlink in the Ka-band is assumed to experience primarily line-of-sight (LOS) propagation. The model is designed to isolate and characterize the sensitivity of the satellite downlink to geometric and environmental propagation impairments in the presence of hybrid coexistence conditions. Network-level factors (handover, load balancing, traffic variation, inter-cell scheduling) are intentionally omitted to ensure a controlled parametric link-level study. The implications of this abstraction are discussed in Section "Scope and limitations".

Atmospheric absorption

Atmospheric attenuation due to oxygen and water-vapour absorption is modelled according to ITU-R Recommendation P.67611. The specific attenuation coefficient is3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \gamma _{\textrm{atm}}(f) = \gamma _{\mathrm {O_2}}(f) + \gamma _{\mathrm {H_2O}}(f) \quad \mathrm {[dB/km]}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and the total attenuation along the slant path is4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\textrm{atm}}(f,d) = \gamma _{\textrm{atm}}(f)\,d. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}At 28 GHz the combined specific attenuation under standard atmospheric conditions is approximately 0.06–0.08 dB/km, increasing significantly at higher moisture levels.

Rain attenuation

Rain-induced attenuation is modelled using ITU-R Recommendation P.83812. The total rain attenuation over the slant path is6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\textrm{rain}}(f,d,R) = \gamma _R(f,R)\,d_{\textrm{eff}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the effective rain path length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\textrm{eff}}$$\end{document} is computed from the ITU-R P.839 rain-height model13 as7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d_{\textrm{eff}} = \frac{h_R - h_s}{\sin \theta }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_R$$\end{document} the rain height (obtained from P.839 latitude-dependent tables), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_s$$\end{document} the station altitude above sea level, and θ the elevation angle. Small-scale fading

Small-scale fading on the satellite link is modelled using a Rician distribution, which is appropriate for LOS-dominated Ka-band LEO channels. The complex channel coefficient is10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} h = \sqrt{\frac{K}{K+1}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{K+1}}\,\frac{X+jY}{\sqrt{2}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where K is the Rician K-factor and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X,Y \sim \mathcal {N}(0,1)$$\end{document} are independent Gaussian random variables. The instantaneous fading power gain is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|h|^2$$\end{document}.

Doppler frequency shifts arising from LEO satellite motion are not modelled in the present framework. At Ka-band with LEO orbital velocity v ≈ 7.5 km/s, the peak Doppler shift is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_D = v f_c / c \approx 700$$\end{document} kHz at 28 GHz, which exceeds 7% of a 10 MHz channel bandwidth. Doppler compensation in deployed systems is handled by dedicated synchronisation algorithms; its omission here introduces a potential SNR estimation error bounded by the residual post-compensation Doppler spread, which is typically below 1 kHz and contributes less than 0.1 dB to effective SNR degradation for the reference waveform parameters21. This omission is therefore judged acceptable for the propagation-regime analysis presented here and is identified as a direction for future refinement.

Summary of channel model

The model combines deterministic path loss (FSPL, atmospheric absorption, rain attenuation with ITU-R P.839 rain-height geometry), Rician small-scale fading, and a spatially distributed distance-dependent terrestrial interference model. Together, these components support controlled, parametric link-level evaluation of hybrid Ka-band LEO coexistence scenarios.

Performance metrics and simulation methodology

Scope and limitations

The simulation is deliberately limited to the link level. Effects at higher layers (scheduling, handover, mobility, traffic variation) are beyond the scope of this work. The single-satellite geometry does not account for diversity effects from simultaneous visibility of multiple satellites, which could reduce outage probability in dense constellations. The static geometry assumption does not account for the time-varying Doppler spread produced by LEO orbital velocity; as quantified in Section "System and channel model"-G, the resulting SNR estimation error is bounded at below 0.1 dB for standard synchronised receivers21. The interferer spatial model uses a uniform distance distribution as a first-order approximation; a PPP-based model is identified for future work. Link adaptation and power control are also not incorporated. These omissions serve to isolate propagation-regime effects.

Results and discussion

The following presents a structured evaluation of hybrid LEO-terrestrial link performance under varying geometric, environmental, and interference conditions. The analysis focuses on identifying dominant performance regimes and the transitions between them. All results are obtained through Monte Carlo averaging over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^5$$\end{document} independent realisations.

Baseline validation

Before presenting parametric results, the simulation framework is validated against published benchmarks. Under clear-sky conditions (zero rainfall), a satellite altitude of 600 km, a carrier frequency of 28 GHz, an elevation angle of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$45^{\circ }$$\end{document}, and a channel bandwidth of 20 MHz, the model yields a slant range of approximately 871 km and a received SNR of 20.3 dB. This is consistent with Ka-band LEO link budget estimates reported in14 for comparable system parameters and with the attenuation values specified in ITU-R P.67611 and P.83812. The specific atmospheric attenuation at 28 GHz produced by the model (≈0.07 dB/km) matches the P.676 tabulated value for standard atmosphere within 5%. Rain-induced attenuation at 25 mm/h (≈2.5 dB/km at 28 GHz) agrees with P.838 tabulated coefficients to within 3%. These checks provide confidence in the correctness of the simulation implementation.

This regime transition is a central finding of this paper. It establishes that interference mitigation strategies (beam coordination, frequency reuse management) are most effective in the interference-dominated region at large slant ranges, whereas link-budget optimisation (transmit power, antenna gain, coding rate) is the primary performance lever in the noise-limited region at short to moderate slant ranges. Two principal trends are evident. First, at a given frequency, a larger number of active interferers shifts the outage transition curve toward shorter distances, reducing the usable slant range for a given reliability requirement—consistent with the SINR floor imposed by aggregate interference in Fig. 3. Second, the relative ordering of the frequency curves at low interferer density reflects the interaction between atmospheric attenuation (which scales with frequency) and the frequency-independent fixed-power terrestrial interference in the adopted model: at 24 GHz, lower atmospheric attenuation maintains a somewhat higher satellite signal, yet the interference contribution represents a larger relative fraction of the SINR denominator at low interferer counts and moderate distances, producing a non-monotone frequency ordering. These observations confirm that both carrier frequency and interference density are first-order determinants of the feasible operating range and must be treated jointly in reliability-constrained design.

The adaptive threshold heuristic of (17) is applied here solely to illustrate the qualitative direction of margin recovery: relaxing the SINR criterion in proportion to rainfall intensity reduces the frequency of threshold crossings near the regime boundary, demonstrating the principle of environment-aware link management without constituting a validated system result.

Discussion

The simulation results collectively characterise hybrid LEO–terrestrial link performance across two distinct operating regimes, separated by a quantifiable boundary whose location depends on satellite altitude, carrier frequency, and interference density.

Noise-limited regime: quantitative characterisation

At short to moderate slant ranges—satellite altitudes of 300–600 km and elevation angles above approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$45^{\circ }$$\end{document}—the link operates in a noise-limited condition. Transitioning from 24 GHz to 32 GHz at a fixed 600 km altitude reduces the received SNR by approximately 4–5 dB, consistent with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\log _{10}(f_2/f_1) \approx 2.5$$\end{document} dB FSPL frequency scaling supplemented by the higher atmospheric absorption at 32 GHz (≈0.10 dB/km vs. ≈0.07 dB/km at 28 GHz per ITU-R P.67611). Rain attenuation is the dominant environmental impairment at Ka-band: at 28 GHz and 25 mm/h rainfall, the ITU-R P.838-consistent specific attenuation of ≈2.5 dB/km12 applied over a typical effective rain path of 3–5 km produces a total rain fade of 7.5–12.5 dB—a loss one to two orders of magnitude greater than the atmospheric absorption contribution (≈0.07 dB/km × 5 km = 0.35 dB) over the same path. In this regime, link-budget improvements (transmit power, antenna gain, receiver noise figure) are the primary performance levers.

Interference-limited regime: regime boundary and design implications

As slant distance increases, terrestrial interference becomes the dominant constraint. The regime transition is operationally defined as the slant distance at which the SNR-to-SINR divergence exceeds 3 dB. For the baseline configuration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{int}} = 5$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{t,i} = 30$$\end{document} dBm, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{\textrm{int}} = 3.5$$\end{document}, interferer distances 100–2000 m), this transition occurs within the 600–800 km slant range at 28 GHz. Doubling \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{int}}$$\end{document} from 5 to 10 advances the outage transition distance (at a 5 dB SINR threshold) by approximately 100–150 km at 28 GHz. In the deep interference-limited regime, where the interference-to-noise ratio (INR) substantially exceeds 10 dB, a 6 dB increase in transmit power yields less than 1 dB SINR improvement, confirming the law of diminishing returns for link-budget strategies when interference is the binding constraint. These quantitative regime boundaries define distinct sets of effective design levers for each operating condition and constitute the primary contribution of this work.

Limitation: single-satellite geometry

A significant limitation is the single-satellite assumption. In dense LEO constellations with multiple simultaneously visible satellites, multi-satellite selection diversity would reduce outage probability relative to the single-link results presented here, most substantially in the interference-limited regime. Quantifying this diversity gain under the present propagation framework is identified as a direction for future work.

Design insights for hybrid satellite-terrestrial systems

Regime-aware system design

The results establish that hybrid LEO-terrestrial links operate in fundamentally different performance regimes depending on slant distance and interference density. Interference mitigation strategies (beam coordination, frequency reuse management, spatial separation) are most impactful in the interference-limited regime, whereas link-budget improvements (power amplifier upgrades, higher-gain antennas, lower-noise receivers) are most valuable in the noise-limited regime.

Ka-band propagation margin provisioning

Ka-band operation introduces significant margin sensitivity to rain attenuation. At 28 GHz and a rainfall rate of 50 mm/h (exceeded approximately 0.01% of the time annually in tropical ITU-R P.837 rain zones), specific rain attenuation reaches approximately 5–6 dB/km12. Applied over an effective rain path length of 3 km per the ITU-R P.839 model13 at mid-latitudes, this yields a total rain fade margin requirement of 15–18 dB. Link budget designs must provision fade margins calibrated to local rain statistics and should consider site diversity or uplink power control as operational countermeasures.

Reliability-constrained operation

The outage analysis shows that system reliability is fundamentally shaped by the joint effect of propagation impairments and coexistence interference. Rather than applying a fixed signal quality threshold, operators should consider dynamic threshold adaptation tied to current or forecast weather conditions. The rainfall-dependent threshold of (17) illustrates this principle qualitatively; more rigorous implementations would leverage real-time link monitoring to adjust coding rate, transmit power, or modulation order.

Interference sensitivity at low link margins

The SINR CDF and outage results show that a modest increase in active terrestrial interferers can substantially degrade reliability in the interference-limited regime. This motivates coexistence-aware deployment planning, interference coordination agreements between satellite and terrestrial network operators, and dynamic spectrum sharing mechanisms2,18.

Directions for multi-satellite constellation design

The interference-limited regime identified here represents the scenario where multi-satellite selection diversity would be most beneficial: routing traffic through a satellite at a more favourable geometry (higher elevation, shorter slant range) would shift the operating point back toward the noise-limited regime. These findings motivate future work on constellation-level diversity analysis under the present propagation framework; no constellation design conclusions are drawn from the single-link simulation results of this paper.

Conclusion

This paper presented a simulation-based, link-level characterisation of hybrid LEO-terrestrial communication performance in the Ka-band. A Monte Carlo framework integrating established propagation models—FSPL, ITU-R P.676 atmospheric absorption, ITU-R P.838 rain attenuation, ITU-R P.839 rain-height geometry, Rician fading, and a spatially distributed distance-dependent terrestrial interference model—was used to evaluate received power, SNR, SINR, outage probability, BER, and spectral efficiency. The framework was validated against published Ka-band LEO link budget benchmarks14 and ITU-R tabulated attenuation values11,12.

A key outcome of this work is the identification of a practical regime transition criterion, enabling system designers to estimate whether a hybrid link will operate in a noise-limited or interference-limited regime based on link budget and deployment parameters without requiring exhaustive simulation. The central finding is that hybrid LEO–terrestrial link performance is governed by two distinct operating regimes whose boundary is quantifiable from simulation. At short to moderate slant ranges (below approximately 700–900 km depending on carrier frequency and interference density), the system is noise-limited: rain attenuation is the dominant environmental impairment, introducing 7.5–12.5 dB of total path loss at 25 mm/h rainfall over a 3–5 km effective path at 28 GHz. At large slant ranges, where the aggregate terrestrial interference power exceeds the thermal noise floor (INR above approximately 10 dB), further link-budget improvements yield less than 1 dB SINR gain per 6 dB of additional transmit power, confirming a hard transition to interference-limited operation. Doubling the number of active terrestrial interferers from five to ten advances the outage transition distance by 100–150 km at 28 GHz, illustrating the first-order sensitivity of regime location to coexistence density.

Design insights include regime-aware resource allocation, provisioning of Ka-band fade margins (15–18 dB at 50 mm/h rainfall), interference-aware coexistence planning2, and leveraging multi-satellite diversity as a countermeasure in the interference-limited regime.

Future extensions include: multi-satellite constellations with diversity combining; stochastic geometry-based interference modelling; Doppler compensation and adaptive modulation and coding; and hardware-in-the-loop validation. The present findings offer a reproducible, quantitative baseline for these extensions and for link-budget design in practical hybrid Ka-band satellite-terrestrial systems.
