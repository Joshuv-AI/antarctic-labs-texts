Tailoring Management of Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction Through Sex-Specific Insights
Authors: Hack-Lyoung Kim
Published: 2025

Heart failure, the final common pathway of almost all cardiovascular diseases, imposes a major societal burden due to its high prevalence in aging populations and its poor prognosis.1) Based on left ventricular ejection fraction, heart failure is classified as either heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) or heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Unlike HFrEF, where the advent of multiple novel agents has led to encouraging improvements in patient outcomes, HFpEF remains a major clinical challenge, with few therapies shown to improve long-term prognosis.2) Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying HFpEF, and the translation of this knowledge into strategies that enhance patient outcomes, is of critical importance. In this regard, increasing attention has been directed toward sex differences,3) which are increasingly recognized as clinically relevant in HFpEF. HFpEF is more prevalent in older women, who tend to present at an advanced age with distinct risk factor profiles compared to men, highlighting the clear epidemiologic disparities between sexes.4) Despite this strong signal, sex-specific insights into HFpEF remain underexplored in clinical practice and underrepresented in clinical trials. More specifically, sex-specific risk factors for long-term outcomes among patients with HFpEF remain poorly understood, and existing studies have yielded inconsistent results.5)

The study by Ahmed et al.,6) analysis of the U.S. National Readmissions Database provides valuable evidence. By examining more than 350,000 patients hospitalized with HFpEF, the authors demonstrate that sex influences not only baseline characteristics but also readmission patterns and mortality following hospitalization. Women were more frequently admitted with atrial fibrillation, diastolic heart failure and respiratory complications, whereas men had a greater burden of ischemic heart disease and systolic failure. Advancing age was associated with a higher risk of readmission among women but a lower risk among men. Chronic kidney disease and diabetes consistently emerged as significant predictors across both sexes.6)

These findings have several important clinical implications. The results highlight the inadequacy of applying uniform strategies to the care of patients with HFpEF. The concept of a single typical or average HFpEF patient is misleading, since women and men experience distinct biological processes, develop different comorbid conditions, and follow clinical trajectories that diverge over time. Women often develop HFpEF in the setting of long-standing hypertension, vascular stiffness, and metabolic disturbances, whereas men more commonly present with HFpEF driven by ischemic heart disease and structural myocardial injury.7) These contrasting pathways indicate that therapeutic interventions and preventive measures should be tailored to the underlying mechanisms rather than applied in the same way to all patients. The study also reinforces the necessity of adopting preventive strategies that take sex into account. In women, more aggressive management of hypertension and atrial fibrillation may help reduce future admissions and improve long-term outcomes. Such an approach may include early detection of blood pressure elevation, strict adherence to antihypertensive regimens, and timely rhythm or rate control in atrial fibrillation. In men, on the other hand, better recognition and treatment of ischemic heart disease is critical, since coronary artery disease contributes more strongly to the clinical course of HFpEF in male patients. Addressing coronary risk factors, ensuring optimal use of statins and antiplatelet therapy, and considering timely revascularization when indicated may all provide benefits.

Some important limitations affect the interpretation of this study. Administrative coding cannot fully reflect the diverse nature of HFpEF. Echocardiographic and treatment data were not available, and the retrospective design means that causality cannot be proven. Even with these limitations, the large size and representative nature of this dataset strengthen the conclusions. The sex-specific patterns described in this study are also consistent with findings from smaller studies conducted in different parts of the world.

The next step is clear. We need to apply precision medicine in HFpEF by conducting trials that can clearly show sex-based differences and by including more older women who make up most of the HFpEF population. This will help us move beyond a uniform approach to treatment. The study shows that sex differences in HFpEF are not small details but are key to understanding the disease and guiding therapy. By recognizing and addressing these differences, we can make care for HFpEF more effective.
