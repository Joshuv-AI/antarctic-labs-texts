Near-peer mentorship for newly qualified doctors; what are the benefits, and what methods can be used to overcome the pitfalls?
Authors: Patrick McGown, Jennifer Forshaw, Lisa Gould
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccess to mentorship is frequently cited as a priority for doctors, however formal mentorship programmes throughout training are lacking for residents. Near-peer mentorship is a faculty-light option to potentially bridge this mentorship gap, however the literature in the clinical postgraduate setting is not comprehensive. We aim to evaluate the benefits and pitfalls of near-peer mentorship in a postgraduate setting.Materials and MethodsThis study conducted a narrative review based on previously published frameworks. An exhaustive search of the literature was conducted using PubMed and OVID Medline. The findings were analysed using Framework synthesis, in which the pre-existing framework of benefits and pitfalls of near-peer mentorship were applied. After allocation to the frameworks’ overarching themes, data was analysed thematically.ResultsAcross 10 identified studies (one quantitative, three qualitative, six mixed-methods), near-peer mentorship was perceived to be beneficial (72−100% approval), with 85−99% of mentors and mentees desiring continuation of schemes at their units (seven UK-based, two Australian, one Canadian). The main themes identified were Mentee benefits, including careers advice and development of transferable skills; Mentor benefits, including leadership and organisational skill improvements; Organisational benefits, including reduced faculty workload and an enhanced sense of community. Pitfalls included a perceived lack of mentor expertise, shortage of time and resources, and unsupportive mentoring relationships.DiscussionThis research suggests that near-peer mentorship offers benefits for the mentee, mentor and organisation, if care is taken to mitigate the potential pitfalls. The main benefits versus traditional senior faculty mentorship derive from the concept of social and cognitive congruence, whereby a more closely relatable tutor is better able to tailor learning in terms understood by the tutee. Practical recommendations to optimise near-pear mentorship include mentorship pyramids, matching of pairings within the same clinical sites, and a hybrid matching approach with the option for mentee-led selection.

Introduction

A mentor is defined as a ‘teacher or peer that supports the learner’s development over a certain period of time’ ([1], p. 258), and access to mentorship is frequently cited as a top priority for junior doctors [2,3]. The traditional mentorship model involves a dyad between an expert and a learner, however in recent years it has become under–used across the postgraduate medical setting, with increased clinical demands, competing time constraints, and frequent changes in clinical placements for trainees reducing the drive for mentors to take on mentees [4].

A potential alternative to the classical expert–learner mentorship model is near–peer mentoring. The definition of near–peer mentorship is derived from Olaussen et al. [5] as two participants (mentor and mentee) at a similar training level but at least one academic year apart, with a teacher to student ratio of 1:1 or 1:2. It has been extensively studied in both undergraduate settings [6] and in academic medicine [7], however the literature in the clinical postgraduate setting is less comprehensive [8]. In this literature review, we consider ‘newly qualified doctors’ to be those within five training grade years of graduation.

The over–arching theory behind near–peer mentoring is Schmidt and Moust’s [9] concept of social and cognitive congruence, whereby a more relatable tutor is better able to tailor learning in terms understood by the tutee. While a consultant supervisor has a higher level of knowledge, what they regard as basic may not be perceived as such by the novice [10]. Thus, the expert may not be best placed to convey understanding to the learner [11]. Meanwhile, the near–peer can better understand the struggles of the learner having recently overcome them themselves [12], may seem more approachable [13,14], and creates a less threatening learning environment [15].

Materials and methods

Conceptual framework

The conduction of this narrative study followed the SALSA (Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, and Analysis) framework [16].

Stage 1: search

The search strategy followed the five–stage search process: initial search (scoping search), conduct search, bibliography search, verification, and documentation [17]. This study searched two medical and educational databases: PubMed and Ovid Medline for literature on near–peer mentorships (see Figure 1). Additionally, using the snowball sampling technique, the research team hand–added some related studies from citations [18].

Search strategy.

The main search terms were ((peer) OR (near–peer)) AND ((trainee) OR (junior doctor) OR (postgraduate) OR (foundation doctor) OR (foundation year)) AND ((mentor) OR (mentorship) OR (mentoring)).

Exclusion criteria: No date ranges were placed as exclusions, but only English–language publications were considered. Any papers regarding ‘group mentorship’, articles involving non–clinicians, or where mentorship was targeted solely towards learning a specific clinical skill were excluded.

On the PubMed search 231 results were found, and these were filtered manually by abstract to assess exclusion criteria. Of the 20 remaining articles, 14 were excluded after reading the full article as they did not relate to newly qualified doctors, leaving six papers. The same process was performed using OVID Medline, and one further article was included, plus an additional three found on reference tracking. Thus, ten articles are included in this review (see Figure 2). Seven are UK–based, two pertain to mentorship schemes in Australia, and one from Canada. One is quantitative, three qualitative, and six are mixed–methods. For SANRA [19] quality metric data please see Appendix 1.

Study populations.

Stage 2: appraisal

This report used a modified version of the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) tool [20], originally derived from the CASP qualitative tool [21].

Stage 3: synthesis

This study adopted a framework methodology [22], predefining benefits and pitfalls as the overarching themes, and employing thematic synthesis to identify common themes from within those categories [23]. The thematic synthesis process followed three stages: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding [24].

Data was gathered and separated into each of the two categories, benefits and pitfalls. After this, a researcher produced codes by reading the studies line by line. For studies that had already undergone thematic analysis, the researcher incorporated their existing codes into the new set of codes. Aligned with the interpretivist paradigm, the researcher acknowledged that the existing codes reflected the beliefs and interpretations of the original research teams. The second step was axial coding, which used the codes generated from initial coding, and checked the same coding across all the materials. The purpose of axial coding was to maintain the consistency and to prevent missing codes from early works.

Stage 4: analysis

This study used narrative analysis to delve into the themes generated from syntheses. The purpose was to explain the differences and similarities in the different papers [25].

The first step was to summarize the main characteristic and findings of the study [25], which includes the aim of the research, research type, main findings, strengths and limitations of each studies, and most importantly, the related concepts from the themes collected from thematic synthesis. The second step was to compare and to contrast findings [25]. These results are examined in more depth in the discussion section. A coding tree with a full list of codes, sub–themes and themes is listed in Appendix 2.

This study used both deductive and inductive methods to generate our findings. For framework analysis, an inductive process generated the sub–themes for the overarching themes of benefits and pitfalls. For thematic synthesis, a deductive process focused on the common and important themes across different studies.

Benefits for the mentee

The literature is resoundingly positive regarding the mentee experience of near–peer mentorship, with all ten articles describing multiple benefits. Commonly reported advantages for the mentee included careers advice or portfolio development [26–30], increased networking [8,31], and improved transferable skills such as workload and time management [26,27,31–34]. Indeed, mentees felt the near–peer mentorship scheme was of value (72−100%) or should continue (85−99%) in all seven studies where asked (see Figure 2 for numerical breakdowns). This strongly suggests that near–peer mentorship delivers a favorable role in training for the mentee.

The most frequently cited positives from a mentee perspective were the approachability and camaraderie of the near–peer as a mentor [32]; Eisen et al. [8], and their recent experience was viewed as a highly desirable characteristic [27,30,32–34]. Moreover, the only article which directly compared different types of mentors found that mentees were more satisfied with the mentorship relationship with a near–peer than faculty mentor [33]. However, this study population size was small with no matching of cohort populations, so generalizability is limited.

Benefits for the mentor

Benefits for the mentor were explored in less depth, being mentioned in only six of the ten studies. Nevertheless, from a mentor’s perspective, the literature was again overwhelmingly favorable towards near–peer mentorship. The main benefits noted were improved organizational skills [27,34], increased reflection [29], new leadership experience [27,31,32], and improved transferable communication and teaching skills [31,34].

Benefits for the organization

Benefits specific to the organization were discussed relatively sparingly in the literature. Enhanced support networks and an improved sense of community were reported [26,30,31], plus the reduction of faculty workload, with mentors widely used for advice instead of supervisors [26,28,31,32]. However the literature did not explore the consequences when a near–peer mentor lacked the expertise to deal with issues raised. Whilst this implies saving of time and workload for consultant supervisors, no objective outcome measures were recorded to measure the strength of this result.

Pitfalls

Having relayed the benefits of near–peer mentorship above, we shall now review the pitfalls. Thematic analysis resulted in the creation of three main themes: a perceived lack of mentor expertise; a shortage of time and resources; and unsupportive mentoring relationships.

Perceived lack of mentor expertise

A perceived lack of mentor expertise was the predominantly mentioned pitfall of near–peer mentorship, both in terms of giving appropriate advice [8,34], and in overcoming difficulties encountered [29,33]. The only contrasting opinion to the effectiveness of mentor training was in the study by Webb et al. [34], where the authors suggest that training did not benefit a cohort of possible mentors.

Time and resources

Difficultly meeting up in pairs was expressed as the largest barrier to mentorship throughout the literature. This was most often due to time constraints [29,31,32] and work schedule clashes [8,27,30].

Unsupportive mentoring relationships

A poor mentor–mentee rapport was mentioned as a barrier to successful mentorship across several studies [29,31,34]. However, there was a low reported frequency of occurrence. Studies either used program–led or mentee–led matching methods to limit the likelihood of unsupportive mentoring relationships.

Discussion

The findings suggest several benefits to mentoring: it is evident that mentees perceived near–peer mentorship schemes as valuable (72−100%) and were in favor of scheme continuation (85−99%), across all seven studies where asked (see Figure 2).

Additionally in undergraduate settings, replacing faculty with near–peer teaching has been shown to reduce time burden on staff without any statistical difference in learning outcomes on assessment [35,36]. This would be useful to explore in the postgraduate setting.

As can be seen by Figure 4, this review included a range of research studies, from quantitative survey/Likert–scale tests to focus groups and some free text coding. All studies included use self–perceived evaluation methods. This is important to note because self–scoring does not necessarily equate to demonstrable improvement. For example, in a different population receiving near–peer tutoring (not mentoring), statistically significant improvements have been demonstrated in perceived exam preparedness after near–peer tutoring, but without a significant difference in assessment scores [37]. Therefore, whilst basing scheme success on self–perceived improvement does provide subjective evidence, it does not necessarily equate to measurable objective improvement.

Evaluative methods.

There were mixed findings regarding participants’ views on preferred type of mentorship. There was mentee preference for informal mentorship in the study by Havard & Baker [8]. Yet the predominant standpoint in the broader literature is that mentees and mentors are more likely to sustain a relationship [38] and feel satisfied with it [39] within formalized rather than informal schemes. Havard & Baker’s [8] study was representative of the views of five female mentees, so whilst it provided some depth of experience in this issue it would be interesting to explore the experiences of other groups.

One study randomly allocated participants [26], which sorted applicants to mentored and non–mentored cohorts. They used a focus group evaluation with thematic response analysis, saturation of ideas, and member–checking. Overall, the randomization provides additional evidence in favor of near–peer mentoring; mentees reported high satisfaction with the scheme including increased career and personal support, whilst in the non–mentored cohort participants felt that having a mentor would have been beneficial.

An assumption in mentorship schemes is that volunteer mentors provide better outcomes as they are more motivated [40]. Of the identified studies, all had volunteer mentors except Vulliamy and Junaid’s [30], and therefore the predominance of volunteer mentors in the literature may inflate mentees perceptions of near–peer mentoring versus perceptions if non–volunteer mentors are used. Despite reporting positive outcomes with non–volunteer mentors, feedback in Vulliamy and Junaid’s [30] study suggested a more targeted approach to identify those in need of mentorship. This implies that the implementation of an all–inclusive near–peer mentorship program in formal training may not be warranted, however further research with larger cohort sizes in non–volunteer mentorship should be performed, particularly within under–served areas such as underrepresented groups where the lack of current research presents an equity gap, or specifically for trainees in difficulty.

Alongside the numerous advantages noted above, the literature also underlines several pitfalls with near–peer mentorship. The main issues highlighted include a lack of expertise, the resource and time burden, and the subject of unsupportive mentorship relationships.

Lack of expertise

To overcome a lack of expertise, mentorship training or the provision of resources was employed in several studies, and was widely found to be helpful [26,31,34]. Notably, in several of the studies citing a lack of near–peer expertise as a drawback, mentor training was not explicitly stated to have taken place [8,29,33]. This implies that the training of mentors can reduce the perceived lack of mentor expertise and this should be considered in programs going forwards.

In the study by Webb et al. [34], where the authors suggest that training did not benefit a cohort of possible mentors, the population this refers to are mentors who underwent training yet never signed up to the scheme, and thus cannot accurately reflect whether the training was beneficial as it was never put into practice.

A perceived lack of mentor expertise can be somewhat negated if the near–peer mentor is not the only support available. A suggested method of providing assistance to near–peer mentors is through a mentorship pyramid model, whereby mentors have their own mentor for advice [11]. Surprisingly, the appointment of a consultant with mentoring oversight was only mentioned in the study by Lakra et al. [28]. It was perceived favorably, however only the abstract for this paper was available so therefore it is difficult to critique this intervention in more detail.

In the Canadian study by PlyleyCory and Flexma [33], those in the peer–mentored cohort expressed the desire for a faculty mentor as well as a near–peer. However, in contrast to the other near–peer mentorship schemes, the population in this study lacked a formal consultant supervisor, and so feedback is likely a reflection of that shortcoming in the trainee support pathway.

Time and resources

From the literature it can be argued that job location had the largest impact on time pressures of mentorship meetings. The studies by Eisen et al. [31] and Peterson & Monaghan [32] encompassed a similarly wide geographic area, yet the Eisen et al. [31] study did not match together any participants working in the same hospital site, and subsequently found disparate locations presented a barrier to meeting up. Conversely, in Peterson & Monaghan’s [32] study, mentorship pairings were matched based on job and location similarities, leading to a high meet–up rate between pairings. Therefore, when matching mentorship pairs, programs should use job location as a principal factor to reduce time pressure concerns.

Whilst it can be anticipated that set–up costs of mentorship programs are time and resource heavy [31], this should reduce for future years. Information packs could be re–used, and there would be a proportion of returning mentors which would reduce the training burden. Furthermore, high proportions of mentees in the literature express interest in becoming mentors themselves [29,31,32,34] and they would already be aware of the requirements of the program. However, only the study by Szabo et al. [29] documents outcomes from a follow up period of more than one year, which makes it hard to determine long term yield from such programs.

Where more intensive training was employed, it came at the cost of limiting participants below the total interested number of applicants [31]. Therefore, a medium ground of time–efficient and cost–effective training should be found, although this was not specifically studied in any of the papers.

Unsupportive mentoring relationships

A poor mentor–mentee rapport was mentioned as a barrier to successful mentorship across several studies [29,31,34]. However, there was a low reported frequency of occurrence. Studies either used program–led or mentee–led matching methods to limit the likelihood of unsupportive mentoring relationships.

A program–led matching approach was most widely used [26,28,32,34]. In this approach, program leads used perceived beneficial factors such as gender and job location to facilitate a congruent pairing, but participants had no input. This may be considered less onerous than the mentee–led approach which is potentially why it was more widely used. It also has the added benefit of extensively searching for suitable matches which the mentee may not be able to do [41]. However, feedback regarding this method within the literature indicates that mentees would have liked to choose their mentors [27].

Unsupportive mentorship relationships are not confined solely to near–peer mentorship, and occur across the wider mentorship literature, with mentee–led matching the preferred method to achieve an optimal mentoring relationship. It facilitates congruence, and mentorship pairings can feel more invested [7,38,39,42]. Nevertheless, only the studies by Eisen et al. [31] and Lakra et al. [28] incorporated this, which may be due to practical constraints. As Cowan and Flint [27] point out, to maximize mentorship potential by starting at the beginning of the academic year, free–choice matching is challenging because pairings do not know each other at the outset. Using a small window at the start of the academic year for mentees to select their mentor could act as a compromise [27]. Whilst this idea has merit it has not been explored to enable further analysis. However, there may be value in a hybrid approach whereby mentorship pairings are first filtered by clinical site and specialty, then optional mentee–led matching is performed within the opening few weeks of the academic year, and program–led matching completes mentorship pairings for those who remain unmatched at the end of this period.

Conclusion

This review has found a clear narrative for the benefits of near peer mentoring schemes that occur across all three themes of mentee, mentor and organization. In particular there is a strong case for mentee benefits, including receipt of careers advice and gains in time and workload management skills. Benefits are apparent to a lesser degree for the mentor and organization, such as improved leadership and communication skills, and a reduction in supervisor workload. The pitfalls identified in the study, which include perceived lack of mentor expertise, shortage of time and unsupportive relationships, may be mitigated by three recommendations from this research, such as congruence within mentorship pairings, a mentorship pyramid scheme, and the training of mentors.

This study is limited by small sample numbers (only 10 articles) and the inclusion of English–language publications only, with English terminology search terms, which may have led to a reduced scope of publications identified.

There are clear gaps in the literature which merit further study, such as targeted mentorship programs, particularly those for unrepresented student groups. Detailed investigation into the effectiveness of a mentorship pyramid model would also be an informative area for future research [19].
