Oncolytic Newcastle disease virus promotes tumor cell death via the anoikis effector Bit1 translocation
Authors: Yang Qu, Sainan He, Liya Shen, Ying Liao, Xusheng Qiu, Lei Tan, Cuiping Song, Ning Tang, Yingjie Sun, Chan Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Anoikis is a specialized form of programmed cell death triggered by the detachment of cells from the extracellular matrix (ECM). Tumor cells that develop resistance to anoikis acquire the ability to detach, migrate, and colonize distant sites, ultimately leading to the formation of metastatic tumors. Bit1 (Bcl-2 inhibitor of transcription 1), a key effector of anoikis, is released into the cytoplasm upon loss of cell attachment and activates a caspase-independent pathway of apoptosis. Newcastle disease virus (NDV), a pathogen that poses a significant threat to the poultry industry, has also emerged as a promising oncolytic virus capable of selectively targeting and killing tumor cells. However, whether NDV can induce the death of anoikis-resistant tumor cells by activating Bit1 remains unclear. In this study, we utilized physical methods to induce cell suspension as a positive control for anoikis and further examined the expression and cellular localization of Bit1 following NDV infection in tumor cells. The results indicated that both viral infection and cell suspension resulted in partial cell death, accompanied by the translocation of Bit1 from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm and a reduction in its protein levels. Notably, Bit1 expression was found not to significantly affect viral replication. These findings suggest that NDV infection promotes tumor cell death by activating Bit1 translocation, mirroring the effects observed during cell suspension-induced anoikis. In addition, in vivo experiments demonstrated that NDV effectively inhibits the metastasis and growth of melanoma in mice, and that overexpression of Bit1 in tumor cells accelerates this process. This study provides novel insights into NDV-induced tumor cell death and identifies potential targets for understanding the mechanisms of oncolytic virus action.

Introduction

Anoikis is a form of programmed cell death that occurs when cells become “homeless”, specifically when the extracellular matrix (ECM) is lost from contact with the cell surface. This mechanism serves as a critical safeguard to prevent detached cells from adhering to inappropriate matrices, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis within tissues (Gilmore, 2005; Kakavandi et al., 2018). Anoikis plays a pivotal physiological role in processes such as tumor progression and metastasis, tissue remodeling, and wound healing (Providence et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2024). Tumor cells, however, can disseminate to other sites by resisting anoikis, leading to the formation of invasive tumor cells. Given that the disruption of anoikis regulation contributes to the growth and malignancy of many solid tumors, restoring anoikis sensitivity is an important therapeutic strategy for reducing tumor invasiveness and metastasis (Wang et al., 2024). It has been shown that classical apoptosis pathways regulate the process of anoikis, including the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway and the extrinsic death receptor pathway. When cells detach from the ECM, the loss of integrin-mediated survival signals leads to the recruitment and activation of pro-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 protein family, such as Bax and Bak, which lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and membrane permeabilization. This process results in the release of cytochrome C and the subsequent activation of downstream caspases (Frisch, 1996; Bondar and Mcconkey, 2002). The death receptor pathway depends on the binding of death ligands (Fas or TRAIL) to death receptors and utilizes the formation of a death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) to activate the downstream caspase-8. In addition to directly activating the downstream caspase protein family to trigger cell death, caspase-8 also disrupts mitochondrial stability by cleaving the pro-apoptotic protein Bid, thereby connecting the extrinsic and intrinsic death pathways (Rytömaa et al., 1999; Han et al., 2023). Both pathways converge on the activation of caspases, ultimately leading to cell death.

In non-classical caspase-independent pathways, the integrin-dependent apoptotic pathway is primarily mediated by mitochondrial Bit1 (Bcl2-inhibitory transcription factor 1) protein (Jan et al., 2004). Following the loss of integrin-mediated cell adhesion, Bit1 is released into the cytoplasm, where it forms a complex with the transcriptional co-regulator amino-terminal enhancer of split (AES) and subsequently induces caspase-independent apoptosis. Studies have demonstrated that caspase inhibitors fail to suppress Bit1-mediated apoptosis, and no caspase activation is observed in Bit1-transfected cells (Jan et al., 2004). These findings indicate that the Bit1 pathway may represent a caspase-independent anoikis mechanism in tumor cells. Furthermore, this evidence suggests that the Bit1 apoptotic pathway could serve as a promising therapeutic target to overcome anoikis resistance, particularly in tumor cells with impaired caspase activity. The role of Bit1 in anoikis has been confirmed across various tumor cell lines. Studies have shown that exogenous Bit1 expression can overcome tumor cell resistance to anoikis, whereas knockdown of endogenous Bit1 enhances cellular sensitivity to this process (Biliran et al., 2008; Brunquell et al., 2012). Intriguingly, research in adherent cells reveals that Bit1 overexpression paradoxically inhibits apoptosis and promotes cell survival through upregulation of Bcl-2 gene transcription (Griffiths et al., 2011). Since the acquisition of anoikis resistance critically determines tumor transformation and metastatic potential, reduced Bit1 expression in tumor cells may facilitate cancer progression, particularly in developing advanced tumorigenic and metastatic capabilities (Karmali et al., 2011; Massagué and Obenauf, 2016). Studies have shown variations in Bit1 expression levels between tumor cells and normal tissues. Bit1 is upregulated in esophageal and oral cancers but downregulated in lung cancers (Fan et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2014). These opposing expression patterns likely reflect Bit1's role in regulating cancer metastasis, though its exact anti-metastatic mechanisms require further clarification.

Oncolytic viruses are considered to play a significant role in anti-tumor effects primarily through their preferential replication in tumor cells, oncolytic activity, and the immune response activated following oncolysis, demonstrating considerable potential for the treatment of malignant tumors (Bommareddy et al., 2018). Newcastle disease virus (NDV), due to its specific cytotoxicity towards tumor cells and minimal impact on normal cells, has facilitated the development of a genetically engineered oncolytic virus NDV variant for the treatment of various tumors (Zhong et al., 2025). Our previous studies have confirmed that NDV induces tumor cell death through multiple mechanisms, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, and parthanatos (Li et al., 2019; Qu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024). Therefore, it is essential to further investigate the mechanisms underlying NDV induced tumor cell death. In this study, tumor cells in suspension as a positive control for anoikis. The results indicated that both NDV infection and cell suspension induced down regulation of Bit1 expression. NDV infection also induced the translocation of mitochondrial Bit1 to the cytoplasm, and the expression of Bit1 could promote NDV-induced tumor cell death. These findings suggest that NDV can activate the Bit1-mediated anoikis pathway, further elucidating a new mechanism by which NDV induces tumor cell death.

Results

Both cell suspension treatment and NDV infection induced cell death

Under normal physiological conditions, the weakening or loss of interactions between cells and the ECM can lead to anoikis. Based on this principle, an anoikis model was simulated by culturing adherent cells in suspension. Cell culture plates were pre-coated with poly-HEMA to prevent proper adhesion of non-small cell lung cancer cells (A549 cells). Cellular morphology and viability after suspension were examined using optical microscopy and a cell counter. Microscopic observation revealed that suspension treatment resulted in visible cellular damage (Fig. 1A and B). Moreover, with increasing treatment duration, the cell aggregation rate progressively decreased (Fig. 1C), accompanied by a corresponding reduction in cell viability (Fig. 1D). Further analysis of apoptosis levels indicated that suspension treatment significantly promoted the progression of cell death (Fig. 1E and F). This effect was similar with the induction of apoptosis in A549 cells by the virulent NDV strain Herts/33, the moderately virulent strain Mukteswar and the attenuated strain LaSota (Fig. 1G and H). Furthermore, our previous experiments have demonstrated that NDV can induce apoptosis in various tumor cell types (Qu et al., 2024). In the attenuated NDV LaSota infection model expressing green fluorescent protein (EGFP-LaSota), fluorescence microscopy directly confirmed that apoptosis occurred specifically in infected cells (Fig. 1I and J). Translocation of Bit1 from mitochondria to cytoplasm induced by both cell suspension treatment and NDV infection

Bit1-mediated anoikis is triggered by the release of Bit1 from the mitochondria and its translocation to the cytoplasm, where it forms a complex with AES (Jan et al., 2004). Subsequently, the expression levels of Bit1 in mitochondrial fractions were examined following suspension treatment and NDV infection. Consistent with expectations, both suspension treatment (Fig. 2A and D) and NDV infection (Fig. 2B and E) resulted in an increase in cytoplasmic Bit1 and a decrease in mitochondrial Bit1 in A549 cells. Similarly, NDV infection in normal lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B also increased the expression of Bit1 in the cytoplasm while reducing its levels in the mitochondria (Fig. 2C and F). Interestingly, both suspension treatment and NDV infection resulted in a decrease in the total Bit1 protein level. Furthermore, immunofluorescence experiments confirmed these results, showing that NDV infection promotes the translocation of Bit1 from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm (Fig. 2G and H). It is important that NDV strains with varying virulence were able to induce the translocation of Bit1 from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm (Fig. 2I and J). Bit1 promoted tumor cells death induced by NDV infection

To further investigate the role of Bit1 in promoting NDV-induced tumor cell death, we transfected A549 cells with siRNA targeting Bit1 to knock down its expression. Both tested siRNAs effectively inhibited Bit1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A and B). Knockdown of endogenous Bit1 suppressed NDV infection-induced tumor cell death (Fig. 3C and D). Similarly, exogenous expression of Bit1 promoted NDV-induced tumor cell death (Fig. 3E and F). Interestingly, in uninfected cells, Bit1 knockdown reduced the basal cell death rate, while Bit1 overexpression promoted it, suggesting that Bit1 is a potential target for inducing tumor cell death. Previous studies have demonstrated that the multiple forms of cell death induced by NDV favor viral replication, such as apoptosis, ferroptosis and parthanatos (Li et al., 2019; Kan et al., 2021; Qu et al., 2024). Therefore, we investigated whether NDV-mediated changes in Bit1 expression affect viral replication. Although knockdown of Bit1 inhibited NDV-induced cell death, it had no effect on viral replication, as evidenced by no significant differences in NDV nucleoprotein (NP) expression (Fig. 3G and H) and viral titers in the cell supernatant (Fig. 3I). Similarly, Bit1 overexpression promoted NDV-induced cell death but did not enhance NDV NP expression (Fig. 3J and K) or viral particle production (Fig. 3L). Cytosolic translocation of Bit1 was required for its pro-apoptotic function

The above results have demonstrated that NDV infection induces the release of Bit1 from mitochondria into the cytoplasm and that Bit1 promotes NDV-induced cell death. To further investigate the relationship between Bit1 translocation and cell death, we constructed plasmids for overexpressing Bit1 tagged with green fluorescent protein (GFP). Based on the methods described previously (Jan et al., 2004), transfection with a Bit1 construct, in which its mitochondrial localization sequence was disrupted by N-terminal GFP insertion, resulted in its cytosolic expression (Fig. 4A). We transfected A549 cells with both Bit1 (cyto) and Bit1 (mito) plasmids and observed their localization through indirect immunofluorescence assay. The results indicated that inserting the GFP tag at the N-terminal of Bit1 effectively localized Bit1 to the cytoplasm (Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis also confirmed that Bit1 (cyto) and Bit1 (mito) localized effectively to the cytoplasm and mitochondria, respectively (Fig. 4C). We next measured the viability of A549 cells after transfection with Bit1 (cyto) and Bit1 (mito). The results indicated that Bit1 (cyto) reduced cell viability (Fig. 4D). Further investigation into the role of Bit1 localizations in cell death revealed that overexpression of Bit1 (mito) did not induce apoptosis (Fig. 4E and F). However, Bit1 (cyto) effectively promotes NDV-induced apoptosis (Fig. 4G and H). Similarly, transfection with Bito (cyto) and Bit1 (mito) had no effect on viral replication (Fig. 4I–K). Both cell suspension treatment and NDV infection induced Bit1 degradation

An interesting finding was that the levels of Bit1 protein were reduced in suspended A549 cells and in normal bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells, as detected by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, Bit1 protein expression was significantly lower in BEAS-2B cells than in A549 cells. Consistent with the above results, NDV infection also reduced Bit1 protein levels in both A549 and BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 5C and D). The decrease was more pronounced overtime (Fig. 5E–G), which may represent an emergency compensatory response by the cells to resist death. Similarly, the virulent NDV strain Herts/33, the moderately virulent NDV strain Mukteswar and the attenuated NDV strain LaSota all induced Bit1 degradation (Fig. 5H and I). To further elucidate the relationship between Bit1 translocation and degradation, we exogenously expressed differentially localized Bit1 plasmids and monitored their expression after NDV infection. The results indicated that the cytosolic Bit1 is more susceptible to degradation (Fig. 5J and K). This suggests that although NDV infection activates Bit1 and promotes its cytoplasmic translocalization, tumor cells degrade the active Bit1 in the cytoplasm to resist NDV-induced anoikis. NDV infection effectively inhibited melanoma metastasis in mice

To further validate that NDV triggers anoikis in tumor cells via Bit1 activation, we established an in vivo tumor metastasis model by injecting mouse melanoma B16 cells into the tail veins of mice. NDV was used to infect both wild-type (WT) and stable overexpressing Bit1 (Bit1++) B16 cells. At 18 h post-infection, an equal number of cells from each group were injected into the mice. On day 14 post-injection, mice were treated with NDV On day 21, lung tissues were harvested and examined for tumor metastasis (Fig. 6A). Compared to the control group, inoculation with B16 cells resulted in tumor formation in the mouse lungs (Fig. 6B). Pathological examination revealed that inoculation with melanoma cells disrupted the lung tissue, characterized by widespread alveolar loss and replacement with diffusely proliferating tumor cells (Fig. 6F). As expected, NDV treatment significantly reduced lung tumor metastasis and formation. Interestingly, Bit1 overexpression further reduced lung metastasis and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of NDV in inhibiting metastatic progression (Fig. 6C and D). Consistent with these findings, inoculation with NDV-infected Bit1++ cells effectively prolonged mouse survival (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, pathological examination showed that NDV treatment effectively suppressed tumor growth in the lungs (Fig. 6G and H). Discussion

NDV is a highly infectious pathogen that affects over 250 species of poultry, causing an acute, febrile, and septicemic disease (Zhang et al., 2023). As a notable oncolytic virus, it can specifically recognize and infect tumor cells, ultimately leading to cell swelling and destruction (Sinkovics and Horvath, 2000). In this study, we found that NDV infection is consistent with cell suspension treatment, triggering cell anoikis by inducing the cytoplasmic translocation of Bit1, while the activation of Bit1 does not interfere with viral replication and proliferation. Similarly, in vivo experiments confirmed that NDV treatment can effectively alleviate pulmonary metastasis of melanoma. Furthermore, cells degrade Bit1 that translocates to the cytoplasm in order to resist the occurrence of anoikis. In summary, these results demonstrate that NDV can trigger tumor cell death by activating the Bit1 pathway, providing a new mechanism for NDV-induced cell death and offering new targets for oncolytic virus-based cancer therapy.

Under normal physiological conditions, when the interaction between cells and the extracellular matrix weakens or adhesion is lost, it triggers anoikis (Grossmann, 2002). However, some malignant tumor cells can localize in lymph nodes or distant metastatic lesion through lymphatic and blood circulation systems, continuing to develop. This process is a crucial step in cancer progression and the metastasis of tumor cells, often accompanied by resistance to anoikis. Bit1, a novel protein associated with anoikis discovered in 2004 (Jan et al., 2004), exerts pro-apoptotic effects by downregulating Bcl-2 expression. When cells are grown in suspension, Bit1 is involved in regulating anoikis; it is released from mitochondria and translocates to the cytoplasm, where it forms a complex with AES, thereby stimulating caspase-independent apoptosis. Studies have shown that Bit1 is the only cell death-inducing factor negatively regulated solely by integrins. When integrin α5β1 binds to fibronectin in the extracellular matrix, it can inhibit the pro-apoptotic function of Bit1 (De Pereda et al., 2004). In this study, we found that although treatment of cells in suspension can induce the release of Bit1 from mitochondria, a significant amount of Bit1 is degraded. This may be due to surviving cells degrading Bit1 to acquire resistance to anoikis. Similarly, NDV infection also leads to the degradation of Bit1, which may be a compensatory mechanism of tumor cells. After Bit1 is translocated to the cytoplasm, its degradation of Bit1 may help resist the occurrence of anoikis. Further investigation is required to explore the mechanisms and reasons behind NDV infection-induced degradation of Bit1.

In this study, we demonstrated that knocking down endogenous Bit1 inhibits NDV-induced cell death, while overexpressing exogenous Bit1 promotes it. However, changes in Bit1 expression levels appear to have little effect on NDV-induced apoptosis, suggesting that the death regulation involving Bit1may not be the primary type of cell death induced by NDV. Our previous research has confirmed that NDV infection can induce cell death through various pathways, including apoptosis, necrosis, and ferroptosis (Li et al., 2019; Kan et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025). Investigating the primary mechanisms of cell death caused by NDV infection is of significant importance. However, current detection methods limit our ability to accurately quantify the contribution of each cell death mechanism. Additionally, the dominant death phenotypes may vary depending on factors such as cell type, time of infection, and viral strain. This underscores the complexity and diversity of the mechanisms of cell death triggered by NDV.

Cell death and viral proliferation are mutually influencing and constraining processes. On one hand, the host limits viral infection and proliferation through cell death as a self-protective defense mechanism (Everett and Mcfadden, 1999). Conversely, viruses can deplete host resources, induce cell death, and promote viral replication and progeny virus release (Ampomah and Lim, 2020; Turpin et al., 2022). However, in this study, we found that knocking down or overexpressing Bit1 can regulate the cytotoxic effects of NDV on tumor cells, but has no impact on viral replication and progeny virus proliferation. In fact, our previous study has shown that inhibiting any apoptotic pathway impedes NDV replication and progeny virus proliferation (Qu et al., 2024). This may be due to the fact that Bit1-mediated anoikis is not the primary form of cell death induced by NDV, or it may result from the inherent resistance of tumor cells to anoikis. This phenomenon may provide insights into how oncolytic viruses exert tumor-killing effects while alleviating the host's antiviral response.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies that NDV infection triggers cell death by inducing the release of Bit1 from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm, drawing an analogy between the effects of cell suspension treatment and NDV infection. Specifically, the knockdown of endogenous Bit1 inhibits NDV-induced cell death, while the overexpression of cytoplasmic Bit1 effectively promotes it. Moreover, both cell suspension treatment and NDV infection lead to the degradation of cytoplasmic Bit1, which may reflect a mechanism of tumor cells resistance to anoikis. Interestingly, Bit1-mediated regulation of cell death does not affect NDV replication. Importantly, in vivo experiments demonstrate that NDV therapy can effectively alleviate tumor metastasis and growth. These findings provide strong evidence for elucidating NDV's oncolytic mechanism, advance our understanding of novel pathways in NDV-mediated tumor killing, and offering valuable insights and foundations for future research in oncolytic virotherapy.

Materials and methods

Cells, viruses and antibodies

A549, BEAS-2B, 293T and BHK21 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The NDV velogenic strain Herts/33 and the attenuated strain LaSota was obtained from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control in Beijing, China. The moderately virulent strain Mukteswar was obtained from Yangzhou University. The virus strains were propagated by inoculation into the allantoic cavity of 9-day-old embryonated SPF eggs. Following incubation at 37 °C for 72 h, the virus-containing allantoic fluid was harvested and titrated using the median tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) assay, as described in the virus titration section.

A monoclonal antibody against NDV-NP was prepared in our laboratory. Rabbit monoclonal antibody anti-Bit1 (TA380561) was purchased from OriGene (Wuxi, China). Rabbit monoclonal antibody anti-β-actin (AC006), mouse monoclonal anti-β-tubulin (AC021), goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS014), and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (AS003) were purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). Rabbit monoclonal anti-Bit1(3543), anti-Tom20 (42406), anti-Flag (2368), anti-HA (3724) and anti-GFP (2956) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA).Alexa Fluor goat anti-rabbit-488 (A11034) was obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Cell suspension treatment

Poly-2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (poly-HEMA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. It was dissolved in 95% ethanol to prepare a 12 mg/mL solution, which became completely liquid and highly fluid. The dissolution process was accelerated on a 37 °C shaker. For instance, each well of a six-well plate was treated with 1 mL of poly-HEMA solution, placed in a biosafety cabinet to dry overnight, and washed twice in PBS before use. The cells were then seeded evenly on the treated cell culture plates for subsequent experiments.

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis

Cells were cultured to a monolayer on 6-well tissue culture plates. After treatment according to experimental requirements, apoptosis was detected using an apoptosis detection kit (C1062L, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 ×g for 5 min, washed once with PBS, and then resuspended in 195 μL of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer and 10 μL propidium iodide (PI) binding buffer. After staining with Annexin V-FITC and PI in the dark at 25 °C for 20 min, samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman, California, USA) with FlowJo software.

Transfection of plasmids or siRNAs

According to the manufacturer's instructions, cells were transfected with plasmids or siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Specifically, 2 μg of plasmid was mixed with 4 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (mass/volume ratio = 1:2), or 4 μL of siRNA was combined with 4 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (vol/vol ratio = 1:1) in 0.2 mL of Opti-MEM (Gibco) and incubated for 5 min. After mixing, the solution was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form lipid-plasmid complexes. Finally, the complexes were added to a 6-well tissue culture plate. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 6 h, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated for an additional 24 h (for plasmid transfection) or 36 h (for siRNA transfection) prior to viral infection. The Bit1-flag, Bit1 (cyto) and Bit1 (mito) plasmids were prepared in our laboratory as previously described (Jan et al., 2004). The siRNA targeting endogenous Bit was purchased from Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China). The siRNAs of human Bit1 (siBit1#1: GGGACUGACUGUAAGUUUATT; siBit1#2: GACCAGCAGACCUAAUUGATT) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China).

Mitochondria isolation assays

Mitochondrial proteins were isolated and extracted using a cell mitochondria isolation kit (C3601; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, the cells were resuspended in the mitochondrial extraction reagent provided with the kit. Then, the resuspended cells are homogenized using a micro-homogenizer, and the homogenate is cooled on ice for 15 min. After the cooling step, the homogenate was centrifuged at 600 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and subjected to a second centrifugation at 11,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cytoplasmic fraction (supernatant) and the mitochondrial fraction (pellet) were collected. The mitochondrial and cytoplasmic components were then quantified using the BCA method.

Cell viability detection

The cell suspension was evenly seeded on 96-well plates at a volume of 100 μL per well and then cultured in an environment of 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 12–18 h to allow the cells to adapt and grow adherently. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the incubator for 1–4 h. The CCK-8 kit was purchased from YEASON (40203ES60, Shanghai, China). After the incubation was completed, the absorbance value at a wavelength of 450 nm was determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader. Record and analyze data.

Animal experiments

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from JieSi Jie Laboratory Animal Co.,Ltd.(Shanghai, China). All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the animal facilities of Shanghai Institute of Veterinary Medicine. A total of 0.2 mL of B16 melanoma cells were injected via the tail vein of mice, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS buffer, thereby establishing an in vivo model of pulmonary metastasis. The animal experiments were conducted as illustrated in Fig. 6A. All animals were recorded as deceased when they were close to death for the establishment of survival curves, and euthanasia was performed using carbon dioxide.

Quantification of lung metastases

Lung tissue was excised from mice, and the number of metastatic foci on the lung surface was counted to assess the degree of lung metastasis. Additionally, the lung tissue was fixed in formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Systematic sectioning of the embedded lung was performed at a thickness of 3.5 μm at the maximum cross-section, with an interval of 50 μm between each section. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) to detect lung metastasis. The extent of tumor growth was evaluated by measuring the size of the largest metastatic foci in the sections (Wu et al., 2025).

Western blotting

The processed cells were washed three times with cold PBS and then lysed using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime). The lysate was centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (P0010, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated on an SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane. The NC membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with an appropriate primary antibody. After overnight incubation at 4 °C and three washes with TBST, the NC membrane was incubated with an enzyme-linked secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using the Tanon 4600 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Bio Tanon, Shanghai, China). Protein band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). The data were normalized to the corresponding β-actin loading control to determine the relative expression levels of the target proteins.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded on coverslips in 12-well plates and processed according to experimental requirements. The coverslips were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Blocking was performed with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 45 min. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 15 min. Between and after each incubation step, the monolayer cells were washed three times with blocking buffer. Finally, the cells were washed once with PBS and observed using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software. The colocalization of mitochondria and Bit1 was quantified by the percentage of overlap between the punctate green fluorescence (Bit1) and the total red fluorescence (mitochondrial), measured across multiple cells.

Virus titration

Viral titers were quantified by 50% TCID50. Briefly, BHK21 cells were seeded on 96-well plates. The virus-containing suspension was serially diluted 10-fold, then 100 or 300 μL/well was added to the 96-well plates, respectively, for 1 h at 37 °C. Unbound virions were removed by washing with PBS. Maintenance medium (DMEM containing 2% FBS) was added to the 96-well plates 3–5 days post-infection, and the TCID50 was calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and expressed as means ± standard deviations of at least three independent experiments. Significance was analyzed with two-tailed independent Student's t-tests between the two groups.
