Oxalic acid vaporization: effectiveness against Varroa destructor (Mesostigmata: Varroidae) and safety for Apis mellifera (Hymenoptera: Apidae)
Authors: Mustafa Bozkus, Carolyn Breece, Hannah Lucas, Nathalie A Steinhauer, Ramesh R Sagili, Johanne Brunet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxalic acid (OA) is a promising tool for controlling varroa mites (Varroa destructor Anderson and Trueman), a devastating pest of the European honey bee (Apis mellifera L.). Applying OA as a vapor inside honey bee colonies has been gaining popularity among beekeepers. We conducted a 2-year field study to evaluate the effectiveness of OA in controlling Varroa and assess the potential negative impacts of OA on honey bee larval development. Different doses of OA (1, 2, and 4 g in 2021; 3 and 4 g in 2022) were applied once a week over 3 wks in August at Oregon State University’s apiary in Corvallis, OR. Each experimental group included 8 honey bee colonies, with control groups receiving no OA. The Varroa levels in colonies treated with 1 g OA (the current label dose in the US) and 2 g OA were not significantly different from each other and the control group. In both years, 4 g OA effectively suppressed the Varroa population growth but also appeared detrimental to the honey bee larval development. Our findings suggest that while the legally permissible dose of 1 g OA per brood chamber in the United States is insufficient for effective Varroa control, higher doses (3 and 4 g) can significantly suppress Varroa population growth but may also increase larval mortality, highlighting a trade-off between Varroa control and colony health.Keywords: colony health, mite infestation, larval mortality, organic acids, sublimation

Introduction

Honey bees (Apis mellifera L., Hymenoptera: Apidae) are among the most important and economically valuable insects worldwide (Aizen and Harder 2009). They pollinate approximately one-third of the world’s crops, including fruits, nuts, and vegetables (Allsopp et al. 2008). Thus, honey bees are a critical part of the global food system (Aizen and Harder 2009, vanEngelsdorp and Meixner 2010, Brittain et al. 2013).

Since the early 2000s, the mortality of honey bees has become a growing concern worldwide, prompting efforts to document and track the alarming rates of colony losses (Pirk et al. 2014, Liu et al. 2016, Antúnez et al. 2017, Kulhanek et al. 2017, Brown et al. 2018, Requier et al. 2018, Castilhos et al. 2019, Bruckner et al. 2023, CAPA 2023, Gray et al. 2023, Aurell et al. 2024). These colony losses have been attributed to a combination of factors such as parasites and diseases, climate change, pesticide use, and habitat loss (Potts et al. 2010, vanEngelsdorp and Meixner 2010, Goulson et al. 2015, Havard et al. 2020).

The varroa mite (Varroa destructor Anderson and Trueman, Mesostigmata: Varroidae) (hereafter, Varroa) is an obligate ectoparasite of honey bee colonies, prevailing nearly everywhere apiculture is practiced (Rosenkranz et al. 2010, Traynor et al. 2020, Reams and Rangel 2022). In the United States, its importance as a leading driver of colony mortality is linked to both its high prevalence (Traynor et al. 2016, Abban et al. 2024) and the severity of its damage, which, if left untreated, typically results in colony collapse (Boecking and Genersch 2008, but see Locke 2016). At the colony level, the damage of high mite infestation is called varroosis, or Varroa disease. However, it is difficult to dissociate the direct effects of the Varroa parasitism from the damage caused by the viruses they transmit (Martin 2001, Yang and Cox-Foster 2007, Boecking and Genersch 2008, Carreck et al. 2010, Nazzi et al. 2012, Francis et al. 2013). Varroosis signs vary with infestation rate, which typically starts low in the spring and peaks in early fall (Wegener et al. 2016, Traynor et al. 2020) due to a contraction of the bee population (Calis et al. 1999) and increased mite migration from nearby colonies (Peck and Seeley 2019).

At the individual bee level, physiological damages have been documented in both brood and adult bees (for reviews, see Genersch 2010, Rosenkranz et al. 2010, Noel et al. 2020, Traynor et al. 2020). Varroa feeds on the bees’ fat body tissue (Evans and Cook 2018, Ramsey et al. 2019) as well as their hemolymph (Han et al. 2024). Through their feeding, the mites injure their host repeatedly, resulting in scar tissue and increased risk of bacterial infections (Kanbar and Engels 2003). Parasitism of the bee during its pupal development is most damaging, as it weakens the bees’ immune system (Yang and Cox-Foster 2005, Yang and Cox-Foster 2007, Nazzi et al. 2012), decreases their size, weight and performance (Duay et al. 2002, Duay et al. 2003, Kralj and Fuchs 2006, Kralj et al. 2007, Yang et al. 2021), and shortens their lifespan (Amdam et al. 2004, van Dooremalen et al. 2012), all of which leads to a decline in colony size and ultimately colony loss (Genersch 2010, Genersch et al. 2010, van Dooremalen et al. 2012, Wegener et al. 2016).

Those mites’ feeding events are also when viral infections are transmitted (Lamas et al. 2023), bypassing the usual barriers of vertical or horizontal transmission of viruses between bees (de Miranda and Genersch 2010, Genersch and Aubert 2010, Martin et al. 2012). Much—or arguably, most—of the damage from Varroa infestation is attributed to their associated viruses (Martin 2001, Yue and Genersch 2005, Yang and Cox-Foster 2007, Carreck et al. 2010). Because viral titers correlate with mite levels (Francis et al. 2013) and more virulent strains are favored through Varroa (Ryabov et al. 2014), early and consistent mite control is the most effective way to prevent viral outbreaks (Carreck et al. 2010, Francis et al. 2013, Whitehouse et al. 2025).

Beekeepers have attempted to address Varroa infestations through increased proactive and reactive management (Haber et al. 2019), increasing costs—though those can be offset through improved colony health and survival (Tubene et al. 2023).

Varroa control methods have evolved through several waves of innovation driven by the mite’s resistance to chemical treatments (Lodesani et al. 1995, Elzen et al. 1999, Pettis 2004, Rinkevich et al. 2017), concerns over accumulation of residues in hive products (Wallner 1999, Bogdanov 2006, Benito-Murcia et al. 2021, Albero et al. 2023), and the appeal for more sustainable beekeeping practices (Dietemann et al. 2012, Danka et al. 2013, Locke 2016). While not all innovations have proven successful (Bartlett 2022), the pursuit of effective alternatives has remained steady, with several promising approaches emerging (Reams and Rangel 2022, Posada-Flórez et al. 2025, Whitehouse et al. 2025). Beekeepers have used both chemical and non-chemical methods since Varroa began spreading globally, aiming to keep mite levels below harmful thresholds (Bailey and Ball 1991). By the early 2000s, those various approaches were integrated into a framework combining mechanical, biological, and cultural controls; intensive monitoring (ie Integrated Pest Management [IPM]); and limiting synthetic chemicals to last-resort solutions based on economical thresholds (for reviews of IPM as it relates to Varroa, see Jack and Ellis 2021, Bartlett 2022). Although certain aspects of IPM have been well integrated by beekeepers (eg monitoring), chemical products remain the primary line of control for the majority of beekeepers (Haber et al. 2019, Thoms et al. 2019, Deguine et al. 2021).

Organic acids (eg oxalic acid [OA], formic acid) and essential oils (eg thymol) were incorporated in the IPM framework as “softer” chemicals, preferable to synthetic chemicals, with reduced risk of residue accumulation in hive products and reduced risk of development of resistance in mites (see reviews in Rosenkranz et al. 2010, Jack and Ellis 2021). Such products have been used in Canada and Europe over the past few decades and have been shown to effectively control Varroa populations (Al Toufailia et al. 2015, Giusti et al. 2017, Menzies et al. 2019, Sabahi et al. 2020, Qadir et al. 2021). Interest in these alternatives has grown, especially in light of recent reports indicating the spread of resistance in mites leading to a substantial decrease in the effectiveness of the synthetic products most commonly used by US beekeepers (Rodríguez-Dehaibes et al. 2005, Rinkevich 2020, Hernández-Rodríguez et al. 2021, Millán-Leiva et al. 2021, Lester 2023, Bahreini et al. 2025). In addition, adverse effects in bees have been documented, particularly alterations in honey bee physiology. For example, some chemical control options for Varroa could adversely affect bees by disrupting their natural microbiome and immune system function, ultimately leading to long-term negative impacts on bee health and survival (Rouzé et al. 2019, Cuesta-Maté et al. 2021, Benito-Murcia et al. 2024).

Although often considered less toxic, organic products (non-synthetic substances, not to be confounded with organic compounds containing carbon) vary in safety depending on formulation and application method (Ames et al. 1990, Smith and Perfetti 2020). Some organic products used for mite control have been shown to be less likely to cause harm to organisms outside the hive, potentially making them environmentally safer than conventional miticides (Sabahi et al. 2017). However, other studies have demonstrated that, when used as Varroa control, formic acid, also an organic compound, may have detrimental effects on honey bee workers, queen survival, and brood area, especially under unfavorable conditions, but even within the label-recommended temperature range (Bolli et al. 1993, Westcott and Winston 1999, Giovenazzo and Dubreuil 2011, Pietropaoli and Formato 2019).

Amongst organic acids, OA was registered for the control of Varroa in the United States in 2015, and its use has been increasing since 2013 (Haber et al. 2019). OA is a natural constituent of honey, does not leave residues in bee hive products (Bogdanov et al. 2002, Giovenazzo and Dubreuil 2011, Maggi et al. 2016), and does not seem to lead to resistance issues (Maggi et al. 2017, Kosch et al. 2024). OA can be an effective miticide (Imdorf et al. 1997, Charrière and Imdorf 2002, Al Toufailia et al. 2015, Kulhanek et al. 2023). However, OA has been shown to have detrimental effects in the hive depending on the conditions of use (Higes et al. 1999, Rademacher et al. 2017, Sabahi et al. 2020, Tellarini Prieto et al. 2024), particularly on larval health (Gregorc et al. 2004, Sabová et al. 2019, Terpin et al. 2019), though other studies have not shown negative impacts when looking at colony size and productivity or overwintering success (Papežíková et al. 2016, Jack et al. 2021, Berry et al. 2022).

OA can be applied through several application techniques—spraying, trickling, vaporization, and strips (Charrière and Imdorf 2002, Bahreini 2003, Rademacher and Harz 2006, Maggi et al. 2016). Vaporization (also called sublimation when using pure OA) appears to be most effective, as it resulted in higher mite mortality at lower doses (Al Toufailia et al. 2015). This technique can eliminate approximately 97% of Varroa in broodless colonies and was associated with lower bee and queen mortality even months after application (Al Toufailia et al. 2015, Gregorc et al. 2017). Repeated application of OA using the vaporization technique is essential for effectively targeting a larger proportion of Varroa when brood is present in the hive (Rosenkranz et al. 2010, Giovenazzo and Dubreuil 2011, but see Berry et al. 2022), as OA acts mainly on the dispersing mites rather than the mites under cell cappings (Gregorc and Planinc 2001, Gregorc and Planinc 2002). Alternatively, applications should be combined with a forced brood break (Jack et al. 2020, Evans et al. 2021, Berry et al. 2023, Kovacic et al. 2023). OA miticide labels state a legal limit of 1 g vaporized per brood chamber (US EPA 2024). However, previous studies have shown that the legally allowed 1 g dose of OA is ineffective in controlling Varroa and provides efficacy similar to that observed in the no-chemical control group (Al Toufailia et al. 2015, Jack et al. 2020, 2021, Berry et al. 2022). Few previous studies have focused on the efficacy of different doses of OA to control the Varroa (Al Toufailia et al. 2015, Jack et al. 2021, Berry et al. 2023), while others compared the frequency of application rounds (Jack et al. 2020, Berry et al. 2022).

The primary objective of this study was to determine the optimal dosage of OA using the vaporization method that achieves the most effective reduction in Varroa populations while ensuring the safety of developing honey bee larvae.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

The experiments were conducted in July and August of 2021 and 2022. All experimental honey bee colonies were located at Oregon State University Lewis Brown Horticulture Farm (coordinates: 44.54890, -123.21531) and comprised of 2 deep 10-frame Langstroth hive bodies with solid bottom boards. A 5-cm spacer was placed between the bottom board and the hive body on all colonies. During the experimental period, average temperature and RH were 20.5 °C and 60 ± 1% in 2021 and 21 °C and 65 ± 1% in 2022, respectively.

Prior to the initiation of the experiments, all experimental colonies were equalized to ensure similar size and strength. Varroa infestation levels (mites/100 bees) in all experimental colonies were estimated by way of alcohol wash (Dietemann et al. 2012, Jack et al. 2021) prior to the initiation of the experiment. Experimental colonies were grouped into blocks based on their Varroa levels, such that each block (4 in 2021, 3 in 2022) contained colonies with high, moderate, and low mite levels. Then each block was randomly assigned to 1 treatment.

In the 2021 experiment, we had a total of 32 experimental colonies (4 treatments and 8 colonies per treatment). The following 4 treatments were used: (i) 1 g OA, (ii) 2 g OA, (iii) 4 g OA, and (iv) untreated control. In 2022, we had a total of 24 experimental colonies (3 treatments and 8 colonies per treatment). The following 3 treatments were used: (i) 3 g OA, (ii) 4 g OA, and (iii) untreated control. We note that, based on the 2021 results, which indicated that 1 and 2 g OA were ineffective in controlling Varroa, these groups were discontinued. A 3 g OA group was added to assess whether effective control could be achieved with a lower dose than 4 g.

OA Application

OA (ApiBioxal, Chemicals Laif S.P.A., Vigonza, Italy) was applied to the experimental colonies using the vaporization method by way of a VARROX-Vaporizer (OxaVap, South Carolina, United States) consisting of a heater, metallic support, and 3 m of cable with 2 battery clips. When secured to a 12-V car battery, the VARROX-Vaporizer heated the OA around 150 to 160°C, converting OA dihydrate to OA vapor. A designated dose of OA was placed in the reservoir of the VARROX-Vaporizer, and the device was placed inside the colony between the spacer and the solid bottom board. The entrance was then covered with wet towels to minimize the escape of OA vapors. The VARROX-Vaporizer device was activated for 2 min. After OA was vaporized entirely, the vaporizer was removed from the hive. The hive entrance remained closed with a wet towel for 10 additional min to increase the exposure of the OA. The process was repeated once a week for 2 additional weeks. This timing (3 applications at 7-d intervals) was selected to reflect common practices among beekeepers in the region. The schedule was based on informal consultations with both commercial and backyard beekeepers who regularly use OA vaporization and have adopted this application frequency in their operations.

Larval Mortality Assessment

In 2022, before the first OA application, 4 honey bee colonies were randomly selected from each OA application group. Approximately 20 brood cells containing healthy honey bee larvae (aged between 2 and 4-d old (after egg eclosion), which corresponds to between second and fourth instar larvae) were selected in each colony, and their locations were marked on acetate sheets overlaid on the frames. One day after this selection, the first OA doses were applied. The selected brood cells were then inspected on the day before the second OA application—7 d after selection—for larval survival (capped brood cell = survived; empty, egg, or young larvae = did not survive). This process was repeated for the second and third OA applications as well (for a total of 3 successive rounds per colony), each time selecting cells the day before OA application and recording survival 7 d later. We acknowledge that the original intent was to select a proportional amount of second and fourth instars to introduce some age variability—capturing potentially more susceptible younger larvae without risking near-complete mortality that could occur if only very young instars were used. However, due to variability introduced by personnel assistance during the marking process, the actual cohort likely included a mix of larvae aged approximately 2 to 4 d (second to early fourth instars). We did not analyze age-specific mortality rates, as only the overall larval survival (number of cells with a live larva after 1 wk) was recorded.

Varroa Evaluation

Before and after the experiment, we collected 2 separate samples of approx. 300 adult bees from each of the experimental colonies. These adult bees were shaken from frames from the brood nest (where the majority of the nurse bees and newly emerged bees are located) (Dietemann et al. 2012, Jack et al. 2021) and placed into 160 ml sample jars. The jars were transported to the lab and prepared for a varroa mite count. Jars were filled with 40% ethanol until all bees were submerged. Bees were shaken in a shaking incubator (New Brunswick Scientific Excella E24 Incubator Shaker) for 30 min at 250 rpm. Next, the varroa mites dislodged from the bees were counted and recorded. One hundred adult bees were individually counted and weighed, and the weight was used in comparison with the total sample weight to provide an estimation of the number of bees in the sample. The infestation level was calculated as (# mites/# adult bees estimated) × 100 = infestation rate per 100 bees, or % infestation. In the models, the number of mites and number of adult bees estimated are both used (as an answer variable and offset value), so that the predicted value is expressed as % infestation. At each sampling event, 2 infestation samples were collected simultaneously from each colony. Rather than averaging these values, which would obscure within-colony variability, we retained both and treated them as repeated measures in the statistical model to avoid pseudo-replication (see “Read” in the model description below).

Colony Strength Parameters

Colony strength parameters, ie the number of frames of bees and brood in all experimental colonies, were estimated visually in the field (Delaplane et al. 2013) both pre-treatment (1 wk before the first application) and post-treatment (meaning ∼4 wk apart, as shown in the graphical abstract). Each side of the frame was standardized as a complete 100% area, with full coverage of adult bees on 1 side of a frame considered 100% of the frame, and full coverage on both sides totaling 200% coverage. This approach defined a frame with 200% coverage as a singular entity, recognized as a “frame of bees” (FOB) in our evaluation. For example, if the total area covered by bees on all frames in the colony was 1,200%, this was equivalent to 6 FOBs within the respective colony. Frames of brood (FOBr) were estimated by adding up the area taken by both capped and open larvae.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses and visualizations were performed and created using R Statistical Software (v 4.3.2; R Core Team 2023). Figures were made using the package “ggplot2” (v 3.5.0; Wickham 2016). Statistical significance was assessed at an α = 0.05 for all tests. Each year was analyzed separately. Of the 32 colonies selected in 2021, 1 colony (2 g OA group) was removed from the study due to loss of the queen in that colony.

Larval Survival

Differences in larval mortality rate across the different doses of OA and the control group were tested using a generalized linear mixed-effect model with binomial error distribution and logit link function. Model Evaluation and Selection

The models were evaluated using Q–Q plots and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for residuals and a nonparametric dispersion test on simulated and fitted residuals, using the package “DHARMa” (v 0.4.6; Hartig 2022) (model diagnostics are shown in online Supplementary Data S1).

The interaction effect (indicating a difference between treatment levels in their effect over time) and both main effects of “Treatment,” “Time”—and “Round” (for larval survival only)—were tested using a three-pronged approach: (i) Wald test on the parameter estimates from our full model, using the “Anova” function from the “car” package (v 3.1.2; Fox and Weisberg 2019), (ii) Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRTs) on a step-wise simplification of the full model to compare nested models (refitted with ML) and identify the most parsimonious model, using the “anova” function from the “stats” package (v 4.3.2; R Core Team 2023), and (iii) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) scores comparison between the full models and its simplified version to select the best-fitting model. After determining the minimum adequate model based on a consensus of the 3 approaches (model selection provided in online Supplementary Data S1), we estimated the effect size of the remaining significant terms, and their 95% confidence intervals (CI), using the “emmeans” package (v 1.10.0; Lenth et al. 2020). We used a compact letter display to simplify interpretation of multiple comparisons (in which 2 or more means sharing the same grouping symbol indicates that the model could not show them to be different). In the case where the pairwise comparisons were too difficult to interpret, we focused on comparing pre- vs post- for each treatment (∼ Time|Treatment).

Results

OA Vaporization Treatment Effectiveness

Significant differences were observed in Varroa infestation levels between the pre-treatment and post-treatment periods across different treatment groups in 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1).

Similarly, in the 2022 study, the interaction between “Treatment” and “Time” was again significant (LRT: χ2 = 24.7, df = 2, P < 0.001), indicating that the change in mite loads between pre- and post-treatment depended on the treatment group (Figs 1b and 2). The post-treatment results showed that Varroa infestation levels were suppressed significantly in both the 3 g OA treatment (Estimate [95% CI] = 0.04 [0.02 to 0.06] at baseline, 0.04 [0.02 to 0.07] post-treatment) and the 4 g OA treatment (Estimate [95% CI] = 0.04 [0.03 to 0.07] at baseline, 0.05 [0.03 to 0.08] post-treatment), compared to the control group which increased from baseline to post-treatment (Estimate [95% CI] = 0.04 [0.02 to 0.07] at baseline, 0.13 [0.08 to 0.21] post-treatment) (Fig. 1b). These findings suggest that higher doses of OA (3 g and 4 g) were effective in suppressing the growth of the Varroa population (Fig. 2).

Colony Strength

Frames of Bees

In 2021, we assessed the impact of different OA treatments (1, 2, and 4 g) compared to a control on honey bee colonies by examining the number of frames of bees before and after a 3-wk treatment period (4 wk apart total). The analysis revealed no difference in the change in colony size between treatments (LRT: “Time: Treatment” interaction: χ2 = 0.34, df = 3, P = 0.952, Information Criteria and Wald test results shown in online Supplementary Data S1), as well as no significant difference in size between colonies based on the treatment group (LRT: “Treatment” main effect: χ2 = 1.65, df = 3, P = 0.647) (Figs 3a and 4, Supplementary Table S1). However, there was a significant effect of the “Time” of the measurement (pre-treatment vs. post-treatment) on the number of frames of bees (LRT: χ2 = 13.1, df = 1, P < 0.001), meaning that overall, colony size decreased between pre- and post-treatment measurements (Estimate [95% CI]: 8.8 frames [8.1 to 9.6] pre-treatment, to 7.4 frames [6.6 to 8.1] post-treatment).

Larval Survival

The monitoring of larval mortality across different OA treatment groups in 2022 revealed a dose-dependent effect on honey bee larvae that varied between rounds of application (interaction term between “Treatment” and “Round”: LRT: χ2 = 23.6, df = 4, P < 0.001, Information Criteria and Wald test results are shown in online Supplementary Data S1) (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S2). In particular, the dose-dependent effect on honey bee larvae survival after 1 wk was mostly driven by “Round1” (the first of 3 consecutive applications of OA in the colony). Results indicated that the highest larval mortality occurred in the 4 g OA treatment group in “Round 1,” with a survival probability of 0.38 [0.15 to 0.69], significantly lower than the control group for that same “Round 1,” which had a survival probability of 0.94 [0.78 to 0.99]. The 3 g OA group exhibited an intermediate effect in “Round 1,” with a survival probability of 0.79 [0.51 to 0.93], not significantly different from either the control or 4 g OA group (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S2). In “Round 2” and “Round 3,” differences between treatment group survival levels were not significant in post-hoc multiple comparisons (Fig. 7); though we note that when contrasts were applied for each round individually rather than over all pairwise comparisons, “Round 3” showed both 3 g (0.69% [0.38 to 0.89]) and 4 g (0.69% [0.38 to 0.89]) had significantly lower larval survival rates compared to the control group (0.97% [0.85 to 0.99]) (online Supplementary Data S1).

Discussion

Given the potential for Varroa to develop resistance to synthetic chemical treatments, exploring alternative strategies for combating this devastating parasite is imperative. Promising pathways are being explored to increase the arsenal of tools available—including mechanical, biological, and cultural controls (Bartlett 2022, Whitehouse et al. 2025)—while reducing beekeeper’s singular reliance on synthetic chemicals. OA presents a promising option for managing Varroa infestations in honey bee colonies as part of an IPM framework. Here, we evaluated the effectiveness of different vaporized OA dosages in controlling Varroa populations and examined the impacts on honey bee colony health, including larval mortality.

Our figures placed Varroa loads in the context of the commonly used benchmarks of 3% and 5% in order to facilitate interpretation. We note that we use the term “benchmark” rather than “threshold” to reflect the informal and evolving nature of these infestation levels, which are commonly referenced in extension materials but lack standardized validation across regions and studies. In the context of IPM, the use of Economic Thresholds (ET) is critical for signaling when to initiate control methods to prevent pest density from reaching the Economic Injury Levels (EIL), assuming the control method will reduce pest density (Stern et al. 1959). Though IPM principles require the use of EIL and ET, those are sorely lacking in the context of honey bee Varroa management (Jack and Ellis 2021). In any case, true ETs are also dependent on product effectiveness (Pedigo et al. 1986), which was the focus of this study for different doses of OA by vaporization in our geographic region. However, we note that this study was not designed to define a formal ET, which would require integrating treatment efficacy with colony performance and economic data. The mention of ETs is intended solely to provide IPM context for interpreting our results. Previous attempts to produce an ET for Varroa in comparable regions and seasons reported thresholds of 14 mites/100 bees (August, Washington State, 1999 and 2000) based on ether rolls and fluvalinate or coumaphos treatments (Strange and Sheppard 2001), and 4 mites/100 bees (late August, Canadian prairie region, 1994 and 1995) based on alcohol washes and fluvalinate, coumaphos, or formic acid treatments (Currie and Gatien 2006). The mite levels observed in this study during July/August in Oregon were not unusual, with pre-treatment averages of 4 – 5 mites per 100 bees and post-treatment averages ranging from 4–5% to 10–13%.

The combined results from both OA vaporization studies (2021 and 2022) show that while the legally permissible dose of 1 g OA per brood chamber in the United States is insufficient for effective Varroa control, higher doses (3 and 4 g) can significantly suppress Varroa population growth but may also increase larval mortality. Both the 3 g (tested in 2022) and 4 g (tested in both years) doses (applied 1 wk apart for 3 wks) successfully suppressed Varroa population growth, as opposed to the lower dosages of 1 and 2 g, which showed no difference in Varroa growth from the untreated control group. This finding is somewhat consistent with the findings of another recent study examining the effectiveness of various OA doses. Jack et al. (2021) found an inverse relationship between the OA dose and Varroa infestations: specifically, a 4 g OA dose resulted in the strongest decrease in Varroa infestation, followed by 2 g, then 1 g (though 2 g was not significantly different from either). However, in that study, mite infestations dropped from pre-treatment to post-treatment for all treatment groups except the control, which increased (Jack et al. 2021). This was not the case here, where the Varroa infestation of the 3 and 4 g groups remained stable compared to the other groups, for which mite levels increased. Though a difference between the treatment and control group indicated treatment effectiveness, it is difficult to consider it a treatment success when Varroa loads were not significantly lowered post-treatment but only remained stable. Berry et al. (2022) made the same observation while testing the legal dosage of 1 g per brood box in a repeated application study: though there was a significant difference with the untreated group, in which Varroa loads increased over time, 7 applications of 1 g OA at 5-d intervals were not enough to significantly reduce Varroa loads. In another recent study, researchers found that 4 applications of 4 g OA, spaced 5 to 7 d apart, was the optimal timing associated with significant reductions in Varroa infestations (Prouty et al. 2023). It appears that optimizing the dose of OA, timing and repetition of application is crucial and should perhaps be done at a regional level to determine the most efficacious regimen for that time and region. Ultimately, this study confirmed previous findings that the current legal dose of 1 g of OA per brood box is ineffective at reducing Varroa infestations and further demonstrates that it fails to prevent Varroa infestation levels from increasing over time.

A previous study found that very high doses of vaporized OA (20 g) can have negative effects on adult bee populations, including increased worker mortality (Tellarini Prieto et al. 2024), though interestingly, no significant effect on brood production was noted. While our study did not use such high doses, these findings underscore the importance of using OA in optimal amounts to avoid potential harm to honey bee colonies. Information about toxicity to larval and adult honey bees is critical to make OA a viable and effective option for Varroa control. To evaluate the impact of treatment on honey bee colony health, we measured colony size (FOB) and brood area (FOBr) before and after treatment. Additionally, we assessed larval mortality during the first week following each treatment application. Previous studies have also examined adult bee populations and brood areas, reporting either no differences between treated and control colonies (Papežíková et al. 2016, Al Toufailia et al. 2018, Jack et al. 2021, Berry et al. 2022) or better outcomes in OA-treated colonies (Prouty et al. 2023)—findings that are difficult to reconcile. In this study, we found no difference in the change over time of adult bee counts (FOB) between treatment groups, but we did find a significant difference in the area of brood (FOBr) in 1 of the 2 years of the study. In 2021, all groups, including the control group, showed a reduction in FOBr from pre- to post-treatment; that change was not associated with the treatment group. At the same time, FOB also decreased during the 4-wk experimental period, which seems to indicate the impact of seasonal patterns rather than the OA treatments themselves. In 2022, we found no significant difference in FOB between treatment groups, nor a change in FOB over time. However, colonies treated with 3 or 4 g of OA showed reduced FOBr post-treatment, whereas no such reduction was apparent in the control group. This year-to-year variation mirrors inconsistencies reported in other studies where treatment groups showed no clear differences in colony size and brood area compared to controls. Honey bee colonies may compensate for lost brood by increasing brood production, masking subtle or transient larval mortalities when only looking at colony-level brood area. As a result, the absence of detectable impacts at the colony level should be interpreted with caution, as it does not necessarily indicate the absence of an effect. This observation motivated our use of acetate sheets to closely monitor larval mortality in the second year of our trial, providing a more sensitive and direct measure of treatment effect on brood health.

Previous studies have documented the toxic effects of OA on honey bees, in particular larval health. Field studies have suggested that liquid application of OA is associated with negative effects on brood development (Higes et al. 1999) and other sublethal effects likely to affect colony productivity (Schneider et al. 2012). Toxicity appears to be resulting from induced cell death (Gregorc et al. 2004). Toxicological essays were conducted to establish the lethal (oral and dermal) concentrations of OA (Rademacher et al. 2017, Sabová et al. 2019, Terpin et al. 2019). Ingestion of OA was shown much more toxic to bees than application on the cuticula (Rademacher et al. 2017). In comparison to trickling applications, OA applied by sublimation was shown to have lower toxicity, as shown by caged bee lifespan and histological observations (Papežíková et al. 2017). To our knowledge, this study is the first to specifically investigate the effects of OA vaporization on exposed honey bee larvae under in-hive conditions. Across all application rounds, larval survival 7 d after exposure to the 4 g OA treatment averaged 60%, compared to 90% in the control group. However, the treatment effect was most pronounced during the first application round. In the first round of OA application, the percentage of larvae alive 7 d after exposure to the 4 g treatment was only 38%, significantly lower than the 94% survival of the control group. The percentage of surviving larvae in the 3 g group was intermediate (at 79% survival) and not significantly different from either the 4 g or the control group. The variation in effect size between rounds was unexpected, given that each round involved newly selected cohorts of second to fourth instar larvae. One possible explanation is survivorship bias in the later rounds: by the time larvae were selected for rounds 2 and 3, they had already undergone early development (3 d as eggs, followed by 2 to 4 d as larvae) in colonies that had previously received OA treatment. Although OA residues are believed to be generally short-lived, it is conceivable that residual or delayed colony-level effects from the earlier application influenced larval development, resulting in a pre-selection effect that diminished the observable mortality in subsequent rounds. Previous toxicological larval cultures had already indicated that larvae were more sensitive to OA exposure (in sugar water diet) than adult bees (Terpin et al. 2019). In addition, second instar larvae were more sensitive than third and fourth instars, based on OA lethal oral concentrations (Terpin et al. 2019). We used a mix of second- to fourth-instar larvae to balance sensitivity to OA exposure with the risk of excessive mortality. However, the age-specific survival rates were not analyzed independently. A similar study design following larvae in marked cells exposed to OA by trickling (3% w/v OA in 50% sugar solution) showed between 6% and 13% of larvae removed 7 d after exposure (Hatjina and Haristos 2005)—percentages considerably lower than those observed in this study (though the control groups also showed lower mortality rates compared to the present study). We note—though the authors did not test for that difference—that the percentages of larvae removed were higher in the first application compared to the second application (13 d later) in that study as well (12.6% vs. 9.5% for “younger” larvae and 10.6% vs. 5.6% for “older” larvae) (Hatjina and Haristos 2005). We recommend that future assessments of OA treatments incorporate larval health evaluations, particularly when brood is present in the colony, as those assessments might be more sensitive than the estimations of the change in frame counts of uncapped and capped brood.

In conclusion, while OA offers a viable option for Varroa management, the dosage and application frequency must be carefully calibrated to balance treatment effectiveness and colony health. Our findings suggest that while the legally permissible dose of 1 g OA per brood chamber in the United States is insufficient for effective Varroa control, higher doses (3 and 4 g) can significantly suppress Varroa population growth—though they did not significantly reduce the levels post-treatment—but also increase larval mortality, highlighting a trade-off between Varroa control and colony health. Future studies should explore OA treatments across diverse locations and seasons to develop comprehensive guidelines that maximize Varroa suppression while minimizing adverse effects on honey bee colonies.
