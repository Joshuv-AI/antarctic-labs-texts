Association of Long‐Term Trajectories in Obesity Indices With Neuroimaging Metrics and Cognition: A Population‐Based Study
Authors: Yanbo Liang, Zhiming Lu, Xiaoqing Yuan, Mingze Xu, Ying Hui, Shouling Wu, Zhenchang Wang, Ning Wu, Yuntao Wu, Han Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAccurate assessment of gradual age‐related weight gain, a common phenomenon, is crucial for identifying brain health risks. This study investigated the associations between long‐term trajectories in obesity indices and brain structure and cognition.MethodsThis study included 1114 participants from the Kailuan cohort with ≥ 3 follow‐up visits during 2006–2018. The trajectories of body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR) were identified using group‐based trajectory modelling. Cognition was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. Associations of trajectories in obesity indices with neuroimaging metrics and cognition were examined using generalized linear models. Mediation analyses were performed to assess the associations between trajectories in obesity indices, neuroimaging metrics and cognition.ResultsParticipants with high‐stable WC trajectory were associated with lower grey matter volume (GMV) (β = −0.331; 95% CI: −0.522 to −0.139) and higher white‐matter hyperintensity (WMH) (β = 0.423; 95% CI: 0.166 to 0.680). The high‐stable BMI trajectory showed similar associations. The high‐stable WC and WHR trajectories were associated with lower cognition (β = −0.909; 95% CI: −1.701 to −0.116; β = −0.543; 95% CI: −1.034 to −0.052). GMV atrophy and high WMH statistically partially explained the association between high‐stable WC and WHR trajectories and cognitive decline.ConclusionsPersistent central obesity is associated with adverse brain structural changes, and WC and WHR trajectories are associated with cognitive decline. Preventing persistent central obesity may help preserve brain health and cognition.Keywords: brain health, central obesity, cognition, long‐term trajectory, neuroimaging

Introduction

Brain health and cognitive issues are receiving increasing attention [1]. Identifying modifiable risk factors is of great significance to preserve brain health. Global obesity rates continue to rise, with central obesity being particularly common among Asian populations [2]. Convincing evidence has revealed obesity may pose a threat to neurological health, thereby affecting cognitive function [3, 4, 5].

Body mass index (BMI) is a commonly used indicator for evaluating obesity status [6]. Despite its widespread use, BMI does not account for variations in body fat distribution that may better explain obesity‐related health risks [7]. Central obesity, often measured by waist circumference (WC) and waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR), is an alternative indicator of adiposity distribution. Importantly, WC and WHR have sex‐specific clinical cutoffs because body fat distribution and visceral adiposity accumulation differ between women and men. Therefore, sex‐stratified analyses may help clarify whether the associations between obesity‐index trajectories and neuroimaging or cognitive outcomes are consistent across women and men [8, 9, 10, 11]. In addition, most previous studies have considered obesity status only at a single time point, which makes it difficult to fully capture the longitudinal dynamic evolution characteristics of obesity and accurately reveal the potential impact of longitudinal changes in body size on brain structure and cognitive function [3, 12, 13, 14].

In real‐world populations, most individuals gradually become obese with advancing age [2, 15, 16]. The data from three large U.S. prospective cohorts indicated that participants gained an average of 1.52 kg every four years [17]. Nevertheless, excess adiposity remains a significant health risk [18]. A recent study focused on the associations between variability in BMI and WHR and brain health, and the results suggested that long‐term maintenance of BMI stability was crucial for maintaining brain health [12]. However, the BMI variability of both perennial obese and perennial thin populations may be at a low level, and existing research cannot distinguish the differences in brain health between the two groups through variability indicators, making it difficult to clarify the impact of long‐term obesity on the brain [19, 20, 21]. Therefore, there is still a lack of research on the long‐term evolutionary trajectories in different obesity indices and their relationship with brain health.

This study aimed to explore the associations of long‐term trajectories in obesity indices (BMI, WC, and WHR) with neuroimaging markers and cognitive function utilizing data from a large, Chinese community population‐based study. We assumed that individuals with persistently high obesity‐index trajectories have poorer brain structural integrity and cognitive function. In addition, this study further performed sex‐stratification analysis for BMI, WC, and WHR.

Methods

Study Participants

The Kailuan Study (KLS) is a multicentre, population‐based prospective cohort study conducted in Tangshan, Northern China since 2006 [22]. The participants are primarily employees of Kailuan (Group) Co. Ltd. and local residents. According to standardized protocols from 11 local hospitals, demographic questionnaires, clinical information, and laboratory tests were prospectively collected by trained research staff every 2 years [23]. Since 2020, a subset study of the KLS, the Multi‐modality MEdical imaging sTudy bAsed on KLS (META‐KLS) was established to recruit participants to perform brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations [24]. Participants in the META‐KLS are characterized by a long‐term follow‐up since 2006 and have undergone one brain MRI examination since 2020. Detailed descriptions of the rationale, study design, standardized protocols, staff training, equipment calibration, database development, and quality control procedures of the META‐KLS have been published previously [12]. As of September 2022, a total of 1195 participants had completed brain MRI examination once.

This study is based on the META‐KLS. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing basic information for identification, such as age and sex; (2) missing or incomplete brain MRI data; and (3) a history of cancer, stroke, or traumatic structural alterations of the brain. Timeline and flowchart of the study.

The META‐KLS study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital (IRB number: 2021002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This cohort study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Obesity Indices and Covariates Assessments

The anthropometric measurements were obtained by well‐trained medical staff using standard procedures. Body weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes, and standing height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wall‐mounted stadiometer. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest at the end of a normal expiration. Hip circumference was measured at the level of the maximum gluteal protuberance. WHR was calculated as WC divided by hip circumference.

Standardized questionnaires were used to collect information including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption and physical activity. Seated blood pressure was measured after at least 5 min of rest using calibrated sphygmomanometers. Fasting venous blood samples were analysed in certified laboratories for fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), following uniform protocols. History of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia was ascertained from self‐report, medication use and medical records.

Brain MRI Acquisition and Processing

MRI examinations were performed from 2020 to 2022. All brain MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0 T MRI scanner (GE Healthcare 750 W, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) with an eight‐channel phased‐array head coil at Kailuan General Hospital to ensure the reliability and comparability of the imaging data. The sequences collected in the present study included a three‐dimensional (3D) BRAin VOlume (BRAVO) based on high‐resolution T1‐weighted imaging (T1WI) for volumetric assessment, a 3D fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence for white‐matter hyperintensity (WMH) quantification, and conventional T2‐weighted and diffusion‐weighted imaging for exclusion of major structural lesions. The parameter settings of sequences were as follows. 3D‐BRAVO‐T1WI: bandwidth = 41.67 KHz, flip angle = 15 degrees, slice thickness/space = 1 mm/1 mm, repetition time = 6.7 ms, echo time = 2.6 ms, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, field of view = 25.6 × 25.6 cm2, number of excitation = 1; T2WI: bandwidth = 62.5 KHz, flip angle = 142 degrees, slice thickness/space = 5 mm/6 mm, repetition time = 5842 ms, echo time = 103 ms, acquisition matrix = 416 × 416, field of view = 24 × 24 cm2, number of excitation = 1.5.

Global brain macrostructural volumes were derived from 3D‐BRAVO T1WI sequences. Supratentorial brain tissue volumes, including total brain volume (TBV), grey matter volume (GMV), white matter volume (WMV), cerebrospinal fluid volume (CSFV), and total intracranial volume (TIV), were quantified using an automated processing pipeline based on Statistical Parametric Mapping software and the CAT12 toolbox (http://www.neuro.uni‐jena.de). Cerebral parenchyma was defined as the sum of GM and WM volumes. Additionally, volumes of Alzheimer's disease–related regions—specifically the hippocampus (HIP) and parahippocampal gyrus (PHG)—were extracted using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) 90 atlas. To account for individual differences in head size, all regional volumetric measures were normalized relative to TIV and expressed as percentages. Continuous variables were transformed into z‐scores to approximate a normal distribution. WMH were segmented on FLAIR images using a semi‐automated pipeline with visual quality control. Total WMH, periventricular WMH (PWMH), and deep WMH (DWMH) volumes were quantified, normalized for TIV, and transformed.

Cognitive Assessment

Cognitive function was evaluated using the complete 30‐point Chinese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Beijing version) [25]. Face‐to‐face assessments were conducted by senior psychiatrists from the Kailuan Mental Health Center in strict accordance with the official scoring protocol on the same day as neuroimaging data acquisition between 2020 and 2022. The MoCA yields a maximum total score of 30 and assesses seven distinct cognitive domains. To mitigate potential educational confounding, an additional point was added to the total score for individuals with fewer than 12 years of formal education. A total score of ≤ 25 was defined as indicative of cognitive impairment. All raters were blinded to participants' clinical information during the cognitive evaluation.

Long‐Term Trajectories in Obesity Indices

Before trajectory construction, values outside the mean ±3 standard deviations at each measurement wave were treated as missing. Participants were retained for trajectory modelling only if they had at least 3 valid measurements for the corresponding obesity index. Long‐term (2006–2018) trajectories were then independently conducted for BMI, WC, and WHR using group‐based trajectory modelling (GBTM), a finite‐mixture approach that estimates group‐specific polynomial functions of time by maximum likelihood. Trajectory models were fitted in R using the ‘trajeR’ package, specifying a censored normal (CNORM) distribution for these continuous adiposity measures. Time since baseline was used as the time scale. For each adiposity measure, we evaluated candidate models with 2–5 trajectory groups and linear, quadratic, or cubic time terms. The optimal number of trajectory groups and polynomial order were first determined based on lower BIC and AIC values, while requiring at least 5% of participants per group. The adequacy of classification was then further evaluated using the average posterior probability (AvePP), with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable (Tables S1 and S2).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were examined for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages. The clinical characteristics and neuroimaging markers across different trajectory groups were compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ 2 tests for categorical variables, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Associations between different trajectory groups and brain MRI markers were assessed using generalized linear models. Model 1 did not correct for any covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for history of diabetes, hypertension, TC, TG, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C based on Model 2. To provide clinical interpretation of the results, the group with the low‐stable/increasing trajectory was used as a reference. Sex‐stratified analyses were further performed for BMI, WC, and WHR trajectories. Sex × trajectory interaction terms were further tested in the full models to evaluate effect modification by sex. To address the multiple testing problem arising from the large number of exposure and outcome variables, False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied for all regression analyses involving multiple regional brain measurements. A FDR‐corrected p for trend < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Voxel‐wise differences in GMV across trajectory groups were first examined using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) implemented in Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) based on Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM). Age and sex were included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory, with a voxel‐level threshold of p < 0.05 and a cluster‐level threshold of p < 0.05. When a significant effect was observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed to identify differences between the low‐stable/increasing and high‐stable trajectory groups.

To explore the potential mediation, we first evaluated whether each adiposity‐index trajectory was associated with MoCA score in the fully adjusted model. Because WC and WHR trajectories showed significant associations with MoCA score after full adjustments, mediation analyses were restricted to WC and WHR trajectories. We estimated effects of WC and WHR trajectories on MoCA score through brain markers using the product‐of‐coefficients approach and evaluated significance with Sobel tests. The total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated based on 5000 bootstrap samples.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (4.2.1; R Development Core Team) and Python (3.11.5; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). Statistical significance was set as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05.

Association Between BMI Trajectory and Brain Health

We conducted a comparison based on the five BMI trajectories. The results showed that the high‐stable BMI trajectory exhibited lower brain macrostructural volume, as well as higher CSFV and WMH (all p < 0.05) when compared to the low‐stable BMI trajectory (Table S3).

In fully adjusted models, across the five BMI trajectories, the high‐stable BMI trajectory was associated with lower GMV (β = −0.194; 95% CI: −0.356 to −0.031) and greater WMH burden (β = 0.219; 95% CI: 0.001 to 0.436) (Table 2; Figure S1). Voxel analysis showed that compared with the low‐stable BMI trajectory, the high‐stable BMI trajectory was associated with widespread GMV reduction, especially in regions such as the temporal cortex, insula, and frontal lobe (Figure 3A).

Association Between WC Trajectory and Brain Health

After comparing the four WC trajectories, we found that compared with the low‐increasing WC trajectory, the high‐stable WC trajectory exhibited lower brain macrostructural volume, as well as higher CSFV and WMH (all p < 0.05) (Table S4).

Sex‐Stratified and Interaction Analyses

To further evaluate potential sex differences, we performed sex‐stratified analyses and sex × trajectory interaction tests for BMI, WC, and WHR trajectories. The results are presented in Tables S6–S8.

For BMI trajectories, the sex × trajectory interaction was significant for MoCA score (p for interaction < 0.001), whereas interactions for most neuroimaging outcomes were not statistically significant.

For WC trajectories, several associations were observed among women, whereas corresponding associations among men were generally not statistically significant. However, the sex × WC trajectory interaction tests were not statistically significant for these outcomes, including MoCA score (p for interaction = 0.193).

For WHR trajectories, sex‐stratified analyses showed several associations among women and selected associations among men. Interaction analyses showed significant sex × WHR trajectory interactions for white matter volume (p for interaction = 0.030) and MoCA score (p for interaction = 0.045).

Mediation of the Association Between WC and WHR Trajectories and Cognition by Neuroimaging Markers

Because WC and WHR showed significant associations with MoCA score after full adjustments, mediation analyses were restricted to WC and WHR trajectories. The WC and WHR trajectories were used as the independent variable X, and the z‐transformed MoCA score was used as the dependent variable Y. The results showed that decreased GMV and increased WMH statistically mediated the cross‐sectional association between high‐stable WC and WHR trajectories and lower MoCA score (Figure 4). Discussion

Brain health and cognitive preservation have emerged as critical public health priorities amid global ageing and rising obesity prevalence [26]. While obesity has been linked to adverse neurological outcomes, most prior research has relied on cross‐sectional measurements of obesity indices, failing to capture the dynamic, longitudinal nature of weight changes over time [27]. The present study utilized data from a large community‐based study to investigate the relationships between long‐term trajectories of BMI, WC, and WHR with MRI‐derived neuroimaging markers and cognitive function, and further explored potential mediating mechanisms and sex‐specific differences. We found that higher trajectories of obesity indices were associated with lower brain volumes, higher CSFV and WMH, which were more significant among women. And compared to BMI, WC more stably reflects the unfavourable fat distribution patterns associated with brain atrophy and WMH. Mediation analyses suggested that GM atrophy and greater WMH mediated a substantial proportion of the association between high‐stable WC and WHR trajectories and cognitive decline. Taken together, these findings linked long‐term trajectories of central obesity indices to brain structural changes and cognitive decline.

In real‐world populations, gradual weight gain and progressive adiposity accumulation with advancing age are common [16, 17]. In this context, our findings demonstrated that persistent central obesity, defined by high‐stable trajectories of WC, was significantly associated with reduced GMV and increased WMH. In contrast, BMI trajectories only exhibited weak associations, indicating that central obesity may serve as a more robust predictor of brain structural integrity than general obesity. These results aligned with the notion that body fat distribution, rather than overall weight, was a key driver of obesity‐related health risks [28, 29]. The observed GMV atrophy in individuals with persistent central obesity indices may stem from multiple pathways [30, 31]. Excess visceral fat is a major source of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and adipokines, which can cross the blood–brain barrier to induce neuroinflammation, impair synaptic plasticity, and promote neuronal loss [3, 32]. Additionally, central obesity is closely linked to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, which contribute to cerebral microvascular dysfunction, reduced cerebral blood flow, and impaired nutrient delivery to the brain, all of which exacerbate GMV atrophy and WMH accumulation [19, 33].

The superior predictive value of central obesity indices (WC) over BMI for brain health was consistent with previous studies and underscored the clinical relevance of body fat distribution [34, 35]. BMI, a measurement of general obesity, does not differentiate between fat or muscle mass, nor does it reflect visceral fat accumulation. In contrast, WC directly captures central obesity, which is strongly associated with insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and systemic inflammation, which are key factors driving cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative changes [11, 27]. Our results supported the use of central obesity metrics in clinical and public health settings to identify individuals at higher risk of brain structural deterioration and cognitive decline, beyond what BMI alone can achieve.

In the sex‐stratified analyses, some associations between obesity indices trajectories and adverse brain or cognitive outcomes appeared more evident among women. However, sex × trajectory interaction testing indicated statistically significant effect modification only for selected outcomes, particularly cognitive function and white matter volume. This finding was consistent with evidence suggesting sex‐specific vulnerabilities to obesity‐related neurological risks [12]. A potential explanation may be that oestrogen exerts neuroprotective effects by regulating glucose metabolism, reducing inflammation, and maintaining the integrity of the blood–brain barrier [34]. However, during midlife and beyond, declining oestrogen levels in women may diminish this protection, rendering them more susceptible to the harmful effects of central obesity [36]. Additionally, women tend to accumulate more visceral fat relative to subcutaneous fat after menopause, which may amplify metabolic and inflammatory stress on the brain [37]. Socioeconomic and behavioural factors, such as differences in physical activity, dietary patterns, and healthcare‐seeking behaviours, may also contribute to sex disparities. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these sex‐specific patterns.

The mediation analysis further revealed that GMV atrophy and increased WMH statistically partially explained the association between high‐stable WC and WHR trajectories and cognitive decline. Both neurodegenerative‐like tissue loss and vascular brain injury may jointly contribute to the observed association between trajectories of central obesity indices and cognitive performance [38, 39]. Our study suggested that reducing central obesity indices may slow down brain structural impairment, thereby protecting cognition. It is critical to reiterate that this mediating effect is a statistical association. We emphasize that the results are derived from a cross‐sectional community cohort and require validation in independent, longitudinal samples before they can be applied in routine clinical screening.

There are several advantages of this study. First, it utilized data from a large, population‐based cohort with long‐term follow‐up and assessments of obesity indices at multiple time points. Second, multi‐modality brain MRI acquisition also allows for the accurate assessment of brain health. Notably, our study also examined the association at the voxel level, revealing the effect of trajectories of persistent obesity indices on brain sub‐regional structures precisely [40].

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population was predominantly from a community in Northern China, which may limit the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. And they were recruited from the META‐KLS cohort, which inherently introduces selection bias. Only participants who were healthy enough to undergo MRI were included. The study results may therefore be more applicable to community‐dwelling adults with relatively good physical condition. Second, despite the availability of longitudinal follow‐up clinical data, the single cross‐sectional neuroimaging and cognitive assessments limit the ability to infer the temporal causal relationship between obesity indices, neuroimaging metrics and cognition. Longitudinal repeated measurements of neuroimaging and cognitive indicators will be the focus of our subsequent research to further clarify the causal association among these variables. Third, the MoCA assessment, while widely used, primarily measures global cognition. Future studies incorporating domain‐specific cognitive tests (e.g., memory, executive function) may provide more nuanced insights into the impact of obesity index trajectories on specific cognitive abilities. Finally, the trajectory modelling relied on biennial measurements of obesity indices, which may not capture short‐term fluctuations in weight.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that persistent central obesity assessed via long‐term WC and WHR trajectories was associated with GM atrophy, increased WMH, and cognitive decline. These associations may be partially mediated by structural brain changes, highlighting the importance of addressing central obesity to preserve brain health. Our findings underscored the clinical utility of monitoring trajectories of long‐term central obesity indices, rather than single‐time‐point measurements, for identifying individuals at risk of neurological decline. Avoiding persistent central obesity may be a key modifiable strategy for maintaining brain health and cognitive function across the lifespan.
