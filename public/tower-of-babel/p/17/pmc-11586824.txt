Dual‐Seed Strategy for High‐Performance Anode‐Less All‐Solid‐State Batteries
Authors: Yeeun Sohn, Jihoon Oh, Jieun Lee, Hyunjae Kim, Insu Hwang, Gyeongho Noh, Taeyong Lee, Ji Young Kim, Ki Yoon Bae, Taegeun Lee, Nohjoon Lee, Woo Jun Chung, Jang Wook Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterest in all‐solid‐state batteries (ASSBs), particularly the anode‐less type, has grown alongside the expansion of the electric vehicle (EV) market, because they offer advantages in terms of their energy density and manufacturing cost. However, in most anode‐less ASSBs, the anode is covered by a protective layer to ensure stable lithium (Li) deposition, thus requiring high temperatures to ensure adequate Li ion diffusion kinetics through the protective layer. This study proposes a dual‐seed protective layer consisting of silver (Ag) and zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles for sulfide‐based anode‐less ASSBs. This dual‐seed‐based protective layer not only facilitates Li diffusion via multiple lithiation pathways over a wide range of potentials, but also enhances the mechanical stability of the anode interface through the in situ formation of a Ag–Zn alloy with high ductility. The capacity retention during full‐cell evaluation is 80.8% for 100 cycles when cycled at 1 mA cm−2 with 3 mAh cm−2 at room temperature. The dual‐seed approach provides useful insights into the design of multi‐seed concepts in which, from a mechanochemical perspective, various lithiophilic materials synergistically impact upon the anode‐less interface.

Introduction

All‐solid‐state batteries (ASSBs) have attracted a great deal of attention because they contain non‐flammable solid electrolytes (SEs), which enhance their safety. ASSBs also provide the potential for a substantial increase in energy density through their utilization of lithium (Li) metal anodes.[ 1 ] Although oxides, sulfides, polymers, chlorides, and hybrids thereof have been investigated as materials for SEs,[ 2 ] sulfide SEs have emerged as a focal point because their high ionic conductivity is even comparable to that of liquid electrolytes. In addition, sulfide SEs can be sintered at room temperature owing to their inherently soft nature.[ 3 ] These intrinsic mechanical properties of sulfide SEs are beneficial for ensuring intimate interparticle interaction such that the interfacial resistance can be effectively managed once the cell assembly is adequately pressurized. Therefore, the community has placed the highest priority on sulfide‐based SEs in its pursuit of commercialization. Representative sulfide SE families include Li7P3S11 (LPS),[ 4 ] Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS),[ 5 ] and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl).[ 6 ] From an energy density viewpoint, the thickness of the SE layer represents a crucial parameter. The realization of a defect‐free SE layer as thin as the separator in liquid‐based commercial lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) is considerably challenging.[ 7 ] This unique feature of ASSBs has been the driving force behind research on high‐capacity anode materials such as silicon (Si),[ 8 ] Li metal,[ 9 ] and various alloys.[ 10 ] Along this direction, an anode‐less cell configuration that utilizes only a current collector without any active material on the anode can offer unparalleled energy density.[ 11 ] However, the lack of an active material induces spatially uneven Li plating and stripping during cycling;[ 12 ] thus, warranting long‐term cyclability and safety is non‐trivial. The uneven surface morphology of metallic Li often evolves to form dendrites and thus amplifies side reactions with the electrolyte to further adversely impact upon the cyclability. A promising solution to this problem presented by anode‐less configurations entails the introduction of thin protective layers on the anode current collectors without overly compromising the energy density.[ 13 ]

Many anode‐less protective layers incorporate lithiophilic metals such as silver (Ag) to facilitate the nucleation of Li toward uniform Li deposition.[ 14 ] Representatively, an anode‐less electrode composed of silver‐carbon (Ag‐C) maintained 89% of its full‐cell discharge capacity after 1000 cycles by reversibly plating and stripping the Li beneath the Ag‐C layer. This was attributed to the ability of Ag to stably nucleate Li and suppress dendritic growth.[ 15 ] In addition, the carbon layer serves as a protective layer to effectively restrict direct contact between the plated Li metal and the sulfide SE. In spite of the remarkable performance and energy density demonstrated by implementing this protective layer, the given ASSB cell requires an operating temperature above 25 °C to overcome the energy barrier for Li ion migration through the protective layer. Hence, the protective layer serves as a double‐edged sword.

To enhance the longevity of room‐temperature anode‐less ASSBs, we propose a dual‐seed strategy that involves the integration of zinc oxide (ZnO) and Ag to form the protective layer. This design promotes uniform Li deposition during charge and minimizes void formation during Li stripping, both of which are crucial for improving the charge–discharge reversibility (Figure 1A). The dual‐seed design facilitates multistep lithiation owing to the different lithiation potentials of Ag and ZnO. Under these circumstances, the Li diffusion constant (DLi) is drastically increased by driving multiple alloying reactions over a wider potential range, which provides a large number of lithiation pathways. During delithiation, the Ag–Zn alloy is spontaneously formed and endows the SE|anode‐less interface with ductility[ 16 ] to minimize the formation of voids and therefore enhances the cyclability. We propose a principle for designing dual seeds for anode‐less ASSBs such that both seeds form alloys with Li at different redox potentials for facile Li diffusion, and the two seeds also form an alloy among themselves. This alloy has the desired mechanochemical properties for establishing a sustainable SE|anode‐less interface over cycling.

Results and Discussion

ZnO is well‐known for its affordable synthesis and propensity to react with Li, which allows for sequential conversion and alloying processes.[ 17 ] Ag is recognized for its role as a nucleation seed for Li, for effectively suppressing Li dendrite formation, and for affording uniform Li plating.[ 18 ] Based on these reaction mechanisms with Li, ZnO, and Ag were selected as the constituent materials of the protective layer for a dual‐seed anode‐less electrode. To ascertain the superiority of the dual‐seed strategy, the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode was compared with the respective ZnO and Ag single‐seed electrodes. All the anode‐less electrodes, with a thickness of 4 µm, were fabricated using the slurry casting method, which involved the application of a mixture of ZnO and Ag nanopowders or each of these components individually to stainless steel (SUS) foils. The Ag and ZnO nanopowders that were utilized had particle diameters of 30 and 50 nm, respectively. The thickness of this anode‐less protective layer is significantly lower compared to that of conventional composite anodes based on graphite or Si. Theoretical calculations of the volumetric energy density of an anode‐less ASSB cell, assuming a pouch cell configuration, show a substantial increase (Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information), with this increase becoming more pronounced as the electrode stack increases (Figure S1, Table S3, Supporting Information). X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed their typical crystalline structures (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the electrodes fabricated via the slurry casting method revealed that Ag and ZnO were uniformly dispersed on their individual single‐seed electrodes (Figure S3, Supporting Information) and that the mixture of these two components was well dispersed on the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode (Figure S4, Supporting Information). These observations suggest that nanoparticle dispersion can be readily achieved through wet processing, even when different types of nanoparticles are mixed.

The electrochemical performances of the AgZnO, Ag, and ZnO anode‐less electrodes were evaluated by testing the anode‐less|SE|Li all‐solid‐state half‐cells at room temperature (25 °C). Each cell was operated with a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 (Figure 1B−D). More specifically, Li was plated for 3 h from the point at which the cell voltage reached 0 V in the first cycle. The dual‐seed AgZnO‐based half‐cell exhibited the highest initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of 92.24% (Figure 1B), and the ICEs of the Ag and ZnO single seed‐based half‐cells were 92.08% and 74.07%, respectively. While the ZnO electrode experienced short‐circuiting as early as the 3rd cycle, and the Ag electrode operated for 60 cycles, the AgZnO electrode exhibited stable charge−discharge behavior for more than 260 cycles (Figure 1C), equivalent to 1600 h (Figure 1D). Additionally, the AgZnO electrode exhibited an impressive average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.27% for 260 cycles. The superior performance of the AgZnO electrode can be attributed to its unique mechanisms in both the Li plating and stripping processes.

We first describe the mechanism whereby Li plating occurs and the relevant benefits of employing the dual‐seed approach, which are twofold: increased Li diffusivity (DLi) and reinforced mechanical integrity. Each of these advantages is elaborated upon below. The introduction of Ag and ZnO seeds manifests the reactivity with Li under ambient conditions, thereby lowering the nucleation overpotential of Li and promoting uniform Li deposition. Throughout the lithiation process, both seeds engage in alloying reactions to sequentially generate Li–Zn and Li–Ag alloys as the cell voltage decreases to 0 V versus Li/Li+. The Li alloying reaction with Ag is known[ 19 ] to occur in the range of 0.02−1.0 V (vs Li/Li+) despite the reaction mainly taking place below 0.1 V, whereas the Li alloying potential of Zn lies in the range 0.18−0.5 V (vs Li/Li+).[ 20 ] As Ag single‐seed electrodes typically alloy with Li predominantly within the potential range of 0.02−0.15 V, Li deposition in the AgZnO electrode can be described as a multistep lithiation phenomenon as follows. Initially, ZnO undergoes a conversion reaction (reaction (1) below), succeeded by the alloying reaction of Zn with Li (reaction (2) below). Ag also alloys with Li at its own redox potential (reaction (3) below). Hence, once the formation of the Li–Ag alloy is initiated, the Li–Zn and Li–Ag alloys emerge concurrently as the voltage diminishes to ultimately predominantly generate the Li–Ag alloy. These successive alloying reactions and stripping processes are summarized by Equations (1−4):

The multistep lithiation processes: (1) 2Li+ZnO→Zn+Li2O (2) Li+Zn→Li−Zn (3) Li+Ag→Li−Ag

Delithiation processes: (4) Li−Zn+Li−Ag→Li(stripped)+Ag−Zn

Thus, the well‐mixed AgZnO offers a greater number of reaction pathways for Li over a wider potential range, contrary to the single Li seed, which functions in a confined potential range. The multistep alloying process also elevates DLi of the anode‐less layer as detailed in the next paragraphs.

The cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles demonstrate the alloying/dealloying potentials during Li plating/stripping of the AgZnO, Ag, and ZnO anode‐less electrodes. The CV peaks of AgZnO (Figure 2A) lie between those of ZnO (Figure 2B) and Ag (Figure 2C), indicative of a multistep lithiation process during Li deposition involving both Ag and ZnO. Furthermore, the consistent shape of the CV profiles from the 2nd and 3rd cycles (Figure S5, Supporting Information), akin to those of the 1st cycle, suggests the persistency of the Li plating/stripping processes (Reactions 1−4) within the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode. The Ag and ZnO single‐seed electrodes exhibited similar cycle‐independent CV profiles, indicating that the single‐seed electrodes also induced repeated deposition. Notably, for the ZnO single‐seed electrode, however, the peak intensity was found to increase with increasing cycling. This observation points to the limited impact of the ZnO single‐seed electrode on the lifetime, as the phenomenon is a well‐known sign of soft shorting, where Li filaments grow into the SE layer.[ 21 ]

The higher DLi in the AgZnO electrode was directly evident from the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) analysis (Figure 2D; Figure S6, Supporting Information). This analysis is based on the rationale that the Li diffusivity in the anode‐less electrode is closely associated with uniform Li deposition and the stability of the SE|anode‐less interface. Although accurately measuring the diffusion coefficient of AgZnO through GITT is challenging due to the multiple‐phase reactions of AgZnO, it is still reasonable to use it for comparing samples that operate based on similar mechanisms. In each step of the GITT analysis, Li plating continued for 1 min, followed by a subsequent rest period of 10 min to monitor the voltage relaxation. For the anode‐less layers, Li ions are reduced to Li metal on the surface of the anode‐less electrode, whereupon they diffuse through the metal‐alloy layer during the lithiation process. DLi is calculated using Equation (5):[ 22 ] (5) DLi=49π×ΔEsΔEt2×rp2tp where ΔE t is the voltage drop during the relaxation step, ΔE s is the voltage change during the current pulse step, r p denotes the radius of the particles within the electrode, and t p represents the duration for which a current pulse is applied. The change of r p was assumed to be negligible. The voltage profiles of the GITT analysis confirmed the multistep lithiation process of the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode, consistent with the CV profiles. The DLi for the three different anode‐less electrodes are comparatively summarized in Figure 2E,F, which show the dependence of DLi on the Li alloying potential of each metal. These GITT results show that the DLi within AgZnO is clearly superior to that of individual Ag and ZnO in their respective redox potential regimes. In the voltage range for Li–Zn alloy formation (0.1−0.6 V), the DLi of AgZnO was found to be higher than that of ZnO by 1−11 times (Figure 2E). In the lower potential regime of 0−0.04 V where Zn and Ag both form an alloy with Li, the DLi of AgZnO turned out be up to 10.2 times higher compared to that of Ag alone (Figure 2F), which reveals the advantage of the multiple Li alloying reactions from a kinetics perspective.

The morphological alterations of the AgZnO electrode were monitored during Li plating and stripping by employing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and the corresponding energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses (Figure 3 ). The samples used for the SEM/EDS analysis were prepared under the same operating conditions as those employed for the half‐cells described in Figure 1. In the pristine state, the anode‐less protection layer was uniformly and smoothly deposited on the SUS foil, and Ag and ZnO elements were evenly distributed therein (Figure 3A). Upon inspection of the surface after 1 cycle of Li plating‐stripping, micrometer‐scale Zn agglomerations related to the formation of Li–Zn alloy were observed (Figure 3B). Cross‐sectional images and EDS elemental mapping of the newly fabricated cell verified the seamless contact between the AgZnO electrode and the SE layer before cycling (Figure 3C). After Li deposition, Li plating was observed to have occurred throughout the AgZnO layer, a signature of the spatially uniform involvement of the dual seeds in the Li nucleation and subsequent plating processes. Consistent with the top‐view image in Figure 3B, the cross‐sectional EDS image confirmed the local confinement of the Li–Zn alloy, with its domain sizes measuring 1−2 micrometers (see Zn map in Figure 3D). Because the Li‐Zn alloy is known[ 23 ] for its considerable strength, the formation of this alloy would be anticipated to enhance the mechanical properties of the electrode by providing local strengthening points and therefore rendering the electrode resilient to pressure change during cycling.[ 24 ]

Cross‐sectional SEM and the corresponding energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) images (C) of the pristine state, D) after the 1st Li plating, and (E) after the 1st Li stripping.

On the contrary, examination of the cross‐section after depositing Li on the ZnO single‐seed electrode revealed that, upon lithiation, the Zn particles were not agglomerated at all (Figure S7, Supporting Information), in contrast with the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode. Therefore, the agglomeration of Zn in the AgZnO dual‐seed electrode cannot be solely attributed to the inherent properties of ZnO; rather, the agglomeration must originate from the multiple alloying processes with Ag and Zn. Indeed, both elements undergo distinct alloying processes such that Ag forms a solid solution with Li, whereas Zn forms an alloy with Li mainly through an intermetallic mechanism, as indicated in the phase diagrams[ 25 ] of Li–Ag and Li–Zn alloys (Figure S8, Supporting Information). In the solid solution process, a phase boundary is not usually formed between the Li‐absent and Li‐present phases and therefore results in superior reaction kinetics.[ 20 , 26 ] By contrast, the intermetallic alloying process gives rise to the formation of a clear phase boundary owing to the high miscibility gap between the two phases and thus its kinetics is inferior. Furthermore, the Li‐Ag alloying process leads to the supersaturation of Ag, which promotes the further diffusion of Ag over the Li–Ag alloy medium. Although these sequential processes facilitate the dispersion of Ag throughout the deposited Li plate after the 1st lithiation, the supersaturated Ag, in turn, serves as a barrier for Zn diffusion, and is the origin of the local agglomeration of Li‐Zn alloys, unlike the ZnO single‐seed case. Subsequent Li stripping led to the complete disappearance of the Li metal layer, and the components of the protective layer were recovered at the SE‐SUS interface (Figure 3E). To further clarify that this Zn aggregation is due to the kinetics difference of the seeds, SEM‐EDS analysis was conducted after Li deposition at a low current density of 0.1 mA cm−2 (Figure S9, Supporting Information). These images revealed significantly smaller Zn aggregates, indicating that the Zn aggregation is influenced by the lithiation kinetics. The reduction in agglomerate size can be attributed to the longer duration of Zn diffusion in the Li deposition layer at the lower current density before Ag becomes supersaturated, leading to a more uniform distribution of Zn.

The effect of dual‐ and single‐seed integration on the volume change of the cell during Li plating and stripping was investigated by fabricating ASSB full‐cells with LiNbO3‐coated LiNi0.7Co0.15Mn0.15O2 (NCM711) cathodes, and the pressure changes were monitored using a digital press instrument (Figure 4A,B). For this analysis, pressure of 20 MPa was applied to each cell after fabrication, and once cycling began, the thickness of the cell was fixed while the pressure change was recorded. Accordingly, the volume change of the cell can be detected by monitoring the pressure change in real time. The operation mechanism of the digital press used in this study is illustrated in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). An NCM711 cathode with 3.8 mAh cm−2 was utilized, and the operating current density was 0.2C (1 mA cm−2). In particular, the pressure increase during the first charge and the pressure increase after 10 cycles were defined as ∆P1 and ∆P2, respectively, as denoted in Figure 4A,B, to correlate the pressure change with the cell behavior at different cycling points.

Variation in the stack pressure of A) NCM711|SE|AgZnO and B) NCM711|SE|Ag all‐solid‐state full‐cells during galvanostatic cycling at 0.2C (1 mA cm−2). XPS and XRD profiles of AgZnO electrodes upon Li plating and stripping: C) XRD patterns of AgZnO electrodes in pristine, lithiated, and delithiated states; D) XPS Zn 2p profiles and E) XPS Ag 3d profiles of the same cells.

The values of ∆P1 of the dual‐ and single‐seed cells were +3.6 and +3.1 MPa, respectively. The positive value in this analysis corresponds to the volume expansion of the cell and reflects the formation of a Li plating layer on the anode, which occupies additional volume, upon extraction of the Li ions from the cathode. The considerably different ∆P1 between the two cells points to their distinct levels of volume expansion, even in the first charge, under the same full‐cell and current density conditions. The greater ∆P1 of the dual‐seed cell compared to that of its single‐seed counterpart can be explained by the fact that for the given fixed cell thickness, the Li plating for the dual‐seed cell was more compact and hence resulted in a larger charge capacity. This finding is aligned with the aforementioned higher DLi in the dual‐seed layer, and is the result of the multiple lithiation pathways therein.

Notably, ∆P2 has the opposite polarity for the two cells: a positive value (+0.5 MPa) for the dual‐seed cell and a negative value (−0.8 MPa) for the single‐seed cell. The negative ∆P2 of the single‐seed cell resulted from void formation and the penetration of Li metal into the SE layer, both of which jointly lower the pressure. Moreover, in the case of the single‐seed cell, the stack pressure decreased significantly below the initial point even after the first discharge, implying that void formation was severe even in the first cycle. As the degree of void formation is closely related to capacity degradation through an increase in the interfacial resistance, the observed negative stack pressure in the first several cycles indicates the notable mechanical degradation of the single‐seed cell from the very beginning of cycling.

Aside from the mechanical properties, the changes in the chemical composition of the AgZnO electrode were examined using X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses, as shown in Figure 4C−E. Figure 4C shows the XRD patterns of the AgZnO electrode before and after plating−stripping. In the XRD analysis, the pristine AgZnO electrode exhibited peaks corresponding to pure ZnO, Ag, and the current collector, as expected. At the end of Li plating, the peak associated with metallic Li (Li0) was detected at 36.2°, whereas the multiple peaks assigned to ZnO and Ag, respectively, disappeared due to alloy formation. After Li stripping, the peak of metallic Li also disappeared, indicating the complete stripping of the metal. Furthermore, the peaks assigned to the Ag–Zn alloy emerged, which implied the spontaneous formation of the alloy between Zn and Ag when they are in close proximity during Li stripping.

The XPS results paint a consistent picture. The Zn 2p spectra (Figure 4D) exhibited two peaks of pure ZnO at 1044.5 eV (Zn 2p1/2) and 1021.4 eV (Zn 2p3/2) of the AgZnO electrode in the pristine state. Upon lithiation, in which ZnO underwent sequential conversion and alloying reactions with Li, the formation of the Li–Zn alloy and Li2O was evident from the shifts of the two peaks to 1042.8 and 1019.9 eV, respectively, which correspond to the lower oxidation state of Zn. This observation can be understood based on the electronegativity difference among the three elements: Zn (1.65), Li (0.98), and O (3.44). Specifically, the Zn with the divalent oxidation state in ZnO experienced a decrease in its oxidation state upon alloying with Li, which has a lower electronegativity compared to Zn. This reduction in oxidation state of Zn results in a shift of the two Zn 2p peaks to lower binding energies. Conversely, after delithiation, the formation of the Ag–Zn alloy was supported by Zn peak shifts toward higher binding energies (1043.98 and 1020.98 eV), which can also be explained by the same logic based on the electronegativity; the peak shifts are linked to the higher electronegativity of Ag (1.93) compared to that of Li (0.98), which modifies the relative oxidation state of Zn to that of these two elements. Regarding the Ag 3d spectra (Figure 4E), the peaks exhibited a similar tendency with negative shifts to 373.02 and 367.12 eV after lithiation, and this is attributed to the transformation into the Li‐Ag alloy. After delithiation, the peak shift in the positive direction reflected the formation of the Ag–Zn alloy.

The dealloying of Li from the Li–Zn and Li–Ag alloys led to the formation of the Ag–Zn alloy, as was detected by the XRD and XPS analyses. The formation of the Ag–Zn alloy originates from the Li deposition process involving the use of dual seeds. More specifically, when Li is stripped during discharge, Li dealloys from the Li–Ag alloy, which enables Ag to re‐diffuse around in the remaining Li deposit. Toward the end of the discharge, when the majority of Li leaves the electrode, the diffusing Ag encounters Zn to form the Ag–Zn alloy near the SE|anode‐less interface (Figure 5 ). The overlapped positions of Ag and Zn at the interface after the 1st Li stripping (Figure 3E) offer further support for the facile formation of the Ag–Zn alloy at the SE|anode‐less interface. The presence of this Ag–Zn alloy at the interface is anticipated to contribute to impede void formation at the interface because the alloy is known to be soft and ductile.[ 16 ] Consequently, the dual‐seed electrode is more capable of maintaining a continuous and stable interface as compared to the Ag single‐seed case because the high modulus of Ag prevents it from hindering the void formation as much. To further ascertain the feasibility of achieving self‐healing property, additional electrochemical evaluations were conducted under a reduced stack pressure of 5 MPa. The AgZnO‐based half‐cell was operated at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 3 mAh cm−2, while the Ag‐based half‐cell was subjected to the same current density but with a lower areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Even under these unfavored conditions, the AgZnO dual‐seed exhibited more stable charge–discharge behaviors over prolonged cycles, revealing the effective self‐healing ability of the dual‐seed strategy for improved practicality.

Schematic illustration of SE|anode‐less interfaces using A) AgZnO dual‐seed and B) Ag single‐seed electrodes.

The SE|anode‐less interfaces of the Ag single‐seed and AgZnO dual‐seed electrodes were examined after 50 cycles using X‐ray microscopy (XRM) combined with micro‐computed tomography (Figure 6A−D).[ 27 ] The contrast mapping from the XRM analysis was utilized to evaluate the homogeneity and continuity of the interface because each material transmits X‐rays at a different level. Compared with the Ag electrode (Figure 6A), the AgZnO‐based half‐cell exhibited less contrast differences at the SE|anode‐less interface (Figure 6B), implying its more homogenous interface. This interfacial feature is attributed to the synergistic effect of the higher DLi during Li plating and the formation of the Ag–Zn alloy during Li stripping, as described above. The increasing formation of voids and by‐products at the SE|anode‐less interface of the Ag electrode was responsible for obstructing mass transport and increasing the interfacial resistance.[ 28 ] The more prominent contrast observed for the Ag electrode in the x‐y 2D XRM images of the SE|anode‐less interface stemmed from the formation of voids, and pointed to significant contact loss at the interface (Figure 6C). In contrast, the smoother, less patterned interface of the AgZnO electrode highlighted its more sustained solid‐solid contact for facile Li transport across the interface (Figure 6D).

X‐ray microscopy (XRM) images of the A) Ag and B) AgZnO electrodes after 50 cycles. SE|anode‐less interfaces of the C) Ag and D) AgZnO electrodes. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) plots of ASSB half‐cells with E) Ag and F) AgZnO electrodes after 1, 5, 10, and 15 cycles.

To further validate the distinct SE|anode‐less interfaces between the two samples, we subjected the half‐cells to electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis after cycling (Figure 6E,F). The Nyquist plots were fitted to the equivalent circuit depicted in Figure S12 (Supporting Information). The x‐axis intercept corresponds to the bulk resistance of the SE (RSE), whereas the amplitudes of the semicircles in the high and low‐frequency ranges represent the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) resistance (RSEI) and charge transfer resistance (R CT), respectively.[ 29 ] As cycling progressed, the RSEI of the Ag electrode increased more significantly (Table S4, Supporting Information), indicating more severe side reactions at the SE|anode‐less interface caused by the growth of dendritic Li. Remarkably, the RCT of the Ag‐based cell increased by 118% after 15 cycles, whereas that of the AgZnO‐based cell increased by a more modest 31%. R CT serves as an indicator of the physical contact between cell components, and its elevation suggests a breakdown of these contacts.[ 30 ] The combined evidence provided by the XRM images and EIS analysis underscored the ability of the dual‐seed strategy to maintain robust mechanical stability at the SE|anode‐less interface, which is beneficial for stable cycling.

The electrochemical performance of the dual‐seed AgZnO electrode was assessed in a full‐cell configuration by pairing with a NCM711 cathode with an areal capacity of 3.8 mAh cm−2. During the initial formation cycle in which the cell was charged and discharged at 0.05 and 0.2C (1C = 5 mA cm−2), respectively, an initial discharge capacity of 149.21 mAh gcathode −1 was measured (Figure S13, Supporting Information). The rate capability of AgZnO was evaluated by varying the charging and discharging rates from 0.1 to 1C (Figure 7A,B). AgZnO exhibited a capacity of 108.35 mAh g−1 even at the high 1C rate, which corresponds to 5 mA cm−2, without short‐circuiting. In contrast, when tested under the same conditions, the single‐seed Ag electrode was not able to sustain current densities above 0.3C owing to short‐circuiting (Figure S14, Supporting Information). These distinct full‐cell critical current densities (CCDs) between the two cells reconfirm the impact of the dual‐seed on the reliability of the performance even at higher current densities.

During extended cycling at a current density of 0.2C, the AgZnO electrode delivered stable performance over 100 cycles (Figure 7C). The capacity retention after 100 cycles was 80.8% (Figure 7D), and the average CE throughout the given cycling period was 99.5%. In contrast, the single‐seed electrodes experienced premature soft short‐circuits under the same operating conditions; the Ag‐ and ZnO‐based full‐cells short‐circuited at the 61st and 3rd cycles, respectively (Figure 7E,F). Although the Ag‐based cell exhibited a higher ICE of 94.06% compared to the 91.08% of the AgZnO‐based cell owing to the formation of Li2O during the conversion reaction of ZnO, which to some extent is accompanied by inevitable irreversibility, the discharge capacity of the Ag‐based cell decayed more rapidly. The Ag‐based cell retained only 77.36% after 60 cycles, with an average CE of 99.37% during this time, due to the aforementioned interfacial degradation. The charge−discharge voltage profiles of the three full‐cells are presented in Figure 7D−F. The NCM711‐AgZnO electrodes were employed to assess the performance of a large‐scale pouch cell with an active area of 12 cm2. The cell exhibited a stable voltage profile during charge–discharge cycling (Figure S15, Supporting Information) under an applied pressure of 2.7 MPa. Thickness changes across six evenly divided regions of the cell were monitored throughout the charging process (Figure S16, Table S5, Supporting Information). The maximum deviation in thickness change across all sections was consistently within 3 µm, while the rate of thickness increase per unit deposition capacity averaged 5 µm cm2 mAh−1. This value well matches the theoretical thickness of Li metal, confirming that a significant portion of the system's capacity was attributed to Li metal deposited on the anode. Overall, the dual‐seed strategy, which contributes to uniform Li deposition and stabilizes the anode interface during cycling, ensures the stable long‐term lifespan of anode‐less ASSB full‐cells, even with high‐loading cathodes at room temperature.

Conclusion

We introduced a dual‐seed strategy for sulfide‐based anode‐less ASSBs operated at room temperature. This strategy addresses the chronic issues of anode‐less ASSBs that stand in the way of reliable room‐temperature operation, such as sluggish Li diffusion through the protective layer during Li deposition and the formation of voids at the interface during Li stripping. The alloying reactions with Ag and Zn involve multistep lithiation over a wide potential range, which expedites Li diffusion for facile Li deposition. Whereas Ag supersaturates in the Li deposit and spreads therein by utilizing its mobile characteristics, Zn forms local agglomerates. During Li stripping, highly mobile Ag encounters Zn to form the Ag–Zn alloy with high ductility, thereby minimizing the formation of voids to produce a mechanically sustainable SE|anode‐less interface. Overall, this study offers a useful design principle for dual‐ or multiple‐seed protection layers. By engaging in their alloying processes with Li as well as among themselves, these layers enhance the charge−discharge reversibility of sulfide‐based anode‐less ASSBs at room temperature by improving their mechanochemical properties.

Experimental Section

Cell Fabrication

The Ag and ZnO‐based electrodes were prepared by formulating slurries containing Ag nanopowder (Alfa‐Aesar), ZnO nanopowder (Sigma‐Aldrich), and 5 wt.% Spandex binder (Daesung Chemical) in NMP. The slurries were then cast on anode current collector and dried at 80 °C for 12 h, resulting in a layer with an areal mass loading of 0.48 mg cm−2. SUS foil, instead of copper (Cu) foil, was used as the anode current collectors to avoid any potential contact between the current collector and the SE during cycling. To fabricate asymmetric anode‐less|SE|Li half‐cells, the LPSCl powder was initially compressed into a pellet with a diameter of 13 mm by applying 100 MPa, after which the premade anode‐less composite layer was positioned on the SE pellet and the assembly was compressed at 450 MPa. The anode‐less electrode was transferred onto the SE pellet from the SUS foil after compression. Subsequently, the Li metal and SUS foils were attached to the side of the pellet and compressed. The cell was then positioned in a housing case and tightened to 3 N m using a torque wrench, with an external pressure of 20 MPa applied during cell testing. All‐solid‐state full‐cells were fabricated using a procedure similar to that of the half‐cells, where the anode‐less composite layer and cathode composites (prepared by mixing LiNbO3‐coated NCM711, SE, and VGCF in a weight ratio of 70:27.5:2.5) were placed on either side of the SE pellet. The whole assembly was compressed at 450 MPa, and the areal mass loading of NCM711 was 25 mg cm−2. All assembly processes were conducted in an Ar‐filled glove box.

Electrochemical Characterization

The electrochemical performance of the anode‐less|SE|Li all‐solid‐state half‐cells was evaluated using a battery cycler (WBCS3000L, WonATech). The current densities and areal capacities were 1 mA cm−2 and 3 mAh cm−2. The potential cutoff during the stripping process was 0.1 V. For the electrochemical performance of the NCM|SE|anode‐less all‐solid‐state full‐cells, constant current constant voltage (CCCV) mode was employed for charge and constant current (CC) mode for discharge in the potential range of 2.5–4.3 V using a battery cycler (MACCOR series 4000, Maccor). For the formation step, cells were initially cycled at a charging rate of 0.05C and a discharging rate of 0.1C for one cycle. Subsequently, a constant C‐rate of 0.2C was applied for both charge and discharge. During charging in CCCV mode, the voltage was maintained constant at 4.3 V once constant current charging reached this value, and this voltage level was maintained until the current density decreased to 0.05C. For the rate performance test, charge and discharge were conducted at various C‐rates from 0.1 to 1C while maintaining CCCV mode for charge and CC mode for discharge. All electrochemical measurements were performed at 25 °C. The interfacial resistances of the SE|anode‐less|Li all‐solid‐state half‐cells were measured by EIS using a battery cycler (VSP, Bio‐Logic) in the frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.05 Hz. The DLi of the ZnO, Ag, and AgZnO electrodes were measured by GITT with a battery cycler (WBCS3000L, WonATech). The GITT‐pulse duration was 1 min with a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2 and after each titration step, the cell was allowed to relax for 10 min. This particular procedure was carried out 600 times while discharging and another 600 times during the charging process. The DLi was calculated using Equation 5, which was obtained from a published report [53]. The variation in the stack pressure of the anode‐less full‐cell was measured using a digital press (TPU‐040, Tera‐Leader).

Characterization of Materials

The changes in the chemical composition of the AgZnO electrode before and after Li plating/stripping were analyzed by XPS (PHI 5000 VersaProbe, ULVAC‐PHI). The crystal structures of the AgZnO electrode before and after Li plating/stripping were examined using XRD analysis (New D8 Advance, Bruker). The characteristic peaks of all materials were obtained from the ICDD database. The surface of the AgZnO electrode was visualized by acquiring top‐view images of the AgZnO electrode using SEM (SEM‐7800F Prime, Jeol). Cross‐sectional images of the AgZnO electrode before and after cycling were acquired using SEM (JSM‐7610FPlus, Jeol) after dicing the cells with a cross‐section polisher (CP; ArBlade 5000, Hitachi). Elemental mapping was conducted with EDS (Ultim Extreme, Oxford Instruments). The 3D distribution of the interfaces was analyzed using a system that combined high‐resolution 3D X‐ray microscopy (XRM) and computed tomography (Xradia 620 Versa, Carl Zeiss, USA) installed at the National Center for Inter‐university Research Facilities (NCIRF) at Seoul National University.
