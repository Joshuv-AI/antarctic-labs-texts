Effectiveness of a diabetes educational intervention on the knowledge level of registered nurses working in a public tertiary care hospital in Karachi: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Abdullah Khan, Amjad Ali, Ameer Ullah Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes Mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder requiring effective self-management to prevent complications such as stroke, neuropathy, and nephropathy. Nurses play a pivotal role in diabetes care and education; however, limited evidence exists regarding the impact of structured educational interventions on their knowledge in Pakistan.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured diabetes education program on the knowledge level of registered nurses working in a tertiary-care public hospital in Karachi.MethodsA quasi-experimental pre–post study was conducted among 93 registered nurses at Dr. Ruth K.M. Pfau Civil Hospital, Karachi. A single 120-minute educational session was delivered by a diabetes nurse educator. Data were collected using the validated Diabetes Basic Knowledge Tool (DBKT) before and one week after the intervention. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Version 23 using the Wilcoxon signed-rank and Chi-square tests, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsParticipants were predominantly female (75%), with 57% holding bachelor’s degrees. Background

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder and one of the greatest global health challenges, affecting an estimated 589 million adults worldwide and projected to reach 853 million by 2050, a burden disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Pakistan carries one of the highest national burdens, with a prevalence of 31.4% and an estimated 34.5 million cases, ranking 4th worldwide and expected to rise further [1]. Sustained glycaemic control, including even modest reductions in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), substantially reduces diabetes-related morbidity and mortality [2] Diabetes Self-Management Education (DSME) is recognised as a core strategy by empowering patients to manage their condition and improving clinical outcomes and quality of life [3].

Nurses are central to delivering DSME, and the World Health Organization emphasises strengthening nursing education and competency to address noncommunicable diseases including diabetes [4]. However In Pakistan, opportunities for diabetes-specific training remain limited, and nurses consistently demonstrate moderate-to-low knowledge in dietary counselling, glycaemic monitoring, insulin management, and complication prevention [5–7]. Internationally validated nursing frameworks, such as Orem’s self-care model and the Roy adaptation model, are rarely applied in Pakistani public hospitals because of limited resources and the absence of standardized nurse training, leaving nurses without structured guidance for assessing patients’ educational needs or delivering interactive teaching [8]. Where educational interventions have been attempted, they remain limited in scope and are not systematically embedded within healthcare institutions [9]. Specifically, it remains unclear whether a structured educational intervention can produce measurable, domain-level gains in nurses’ diabetes knowledge within a Pakistani public-hospital setting, and whether such gains vary across nurses’ demographic and clinical-exposure profiles a gap this study was designed to address.

To address this gap, this study is among the first quasi-experimental evaluations of a structured diabetes education intervention for registered nurses in a Pakistani public-sector tertiary hospital, offering empirical evidence to inform the design of feasible, nurse-led DSME training models [10].

Objective

To determine the effectiveness of diabetes education intervention sessions on the knowledge level of registered nurses.

Operational definitions

Diabetes mellitus (DM)

A chronic metabolic disorder characterised by elevated blood glucose levels due to insufficient insulin production, ineffective insulin action, or both. Prolonged hyperglycaemia can lead to multiple acute and chronic complications affecting various organ systems.

Diabetes educational program

A structured educational intervention designed to improve nurses’ knowledge of diabetes. It includes teaching sessions focused on disease pathophysiology, dietary management, insulin therapy, recognition and management of hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia, and patient self-management support.

Nurses’ diabetes knowledge

The extent to which registered nurses understand various aspects of diabetes care, including causes, symptoms, treatment, complications, and education strategies. This knowledge enables nurses to contribute effectively to diabetes management and serve as educators in both clinical and community settings.

Study design

A quasi-experimental pre-and post-test design was employed to measure changes in nurses’ diabetes knowledge without randomization, appropriate for assessing educational intervention effects in a clinical setting. To minimize measurement bias, the intervention was delivered using standardized materials and a facilitator guide detailing objective, content, teaching methods, time allocation, and evaluation. Materials included PPT slides, and demonstrations for diet, foot care, and insulin management workshop. The Diabetes Basic Knowledge Tool (DBKT), a validated instrument, was used consistently for data collection. Convenience sampling may introduce selection bias. however, all eligible nurses present during the study period were invited to participate.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Dr. Ruth K.M. Pfau Civil Hospital Karachi, the largest tertiary care public hospital in Sindh Province, Pakistan. Nurses were recruited from multiple departments, including medicine, surgery, gynaecology, cardiology, emergency, psychiatry, orthopaedics, and intensive care units.

Sample and sampling technique

A non-probability convenience sampling method was used to recruit nurses from the duty roster. This approach was selected for practicality and time efficiency, and although it may introduce selection bias, it was appropriate for a quasi-experimental study assessing change within an accessible population.

Validity and reliability

Permission to use the Diabetes Basic Knowledge Tool (DBKT) was obtained from the corresponding author. The DBKT has been widely used to assess nurses’ diabetes knowledge in various healthcare settings and demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including a content validity index of 0.98 among Saudi nurses [11] and Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.77 to 0.94 in previous studies [13, 15]. Nurses’ knowledge levels were categorised using percentage-based thresholds adapted from the original tool and its subsequent applications.

This categorical classification was introduced to enhance interpretability and facilitate comparison across participants. Scores were classified as Poor (≤ 24; <50%), Fair (25–32; 50–65%), Good (33–39; 66–80%), and Excellent (≥ 40; >80%). These cut-offs were proportionally adjusted for the extended 49-item version to ensure consistency and comparability with the original scale.

Diagnostic criteria for diabetes

According to the ADA (2024), diabetes was diagnosed if HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (≥ 7.0 mmol/L), 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 11.1 mmol/L), or random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (≥ 11.1 mmol/L) with classic hyperglycemia symptoms [16].

Data collection procedure

Ethical approval and hospital permission were obtained before data collection. A total of 100 nurses were enrolled after providing informed consent and assigned unique identification numbers. Participants completed the demographic questionnaire and DBKT as a pre-test, attended the educational session, and completed the post-test one week later. The one-week interval was chosen to assess early knowledge retention and to allow reinforcement of newly acquired concepts, consistent with principles of sustainable learning. Previous nursing education studies have also evaluated knowledge retention one week after interventions, demonstrating that this timing can effectively capture short-term retention of newly learned material [17].

Of the 100 participants, seven nurses did not complete the post-test due to shift changes or leave. These missing cases were excluded from the final analysis, and data from 93 nurses with complete pre- and post-test responses were analysed.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were pre-specified in the study protocol. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 23. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics and knowledge scores. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were compared pre- and post-intervention using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. Subgroup analyses by demographic characteristics were performed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and associations between categorical variables and knowledge levels were assessed with the Chi-square test. Effect sizes (r) were calculated, with values interpreted as small (0.1), medium (0.3), and large (≥ 0.5). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was not applied, as the majority of between-group comparisons (Table 1) were non-significant on Kruskal–Wallis testing. Post-hoc corrections are only indicated when multiple pairwise comparisons follow a significant overall effect.

Moderate improvements were noted in diabetes management/oral hypoglycemic agents (r = 0.4), blood glucose monitoring, diet and nutrition, and foot care/diabetes complications (r = 0.3 each). Although the effect sizes were moderate, these gains reflect meaningful advancement in practical and preventive care competencies essential for holistic diabetes management.

The global knowledge score increased from a median of 21 (IQR: 17–27) pre-intervention to 27 (IQR: 22–35) post-intervention (z = 6.259, r = 0.6), demonstrating a large overall effect. This result confirms the effectiveness of the educational intervention in improving comprehensive diabetes-related knowledge among participating nurses.

Both male and female nurses demonstrated substantial gains (p < 0.001 for both); post-intervention, male nurses achieved slightly higher median scores than females, with between-group differences reaching significance (p = 0.023).

Participants aged 25–45 and 45–60 years showed significant improvement in knowledge levels (p < 0.001), with younger nurses retaining new knowledge more effectively (p = 0.005). Similarly, diploma and bachelor’s degree nurses, including one MSN-qualified participant, showed significant gains (p = 0.002 and p < 0.001, respectively). The single MSN respondent was analyzed with the bachelor’s group, as non-parametric tests cannot produce valid comparative results for a single observation. Post-intervention qualification-based differences remained significant (p < 0.001), indicating that higher academic preparation facilitated greater knowledge acquisition.

Marked improvement was observed among nurses from medical, surgical, pediatric, orthopedic, obstetric and gynecology, and other units, including intensive care. However, neurology and cardiology nurses did not exhibit significant improvement (p = 0.109 and p = 0.157, respectively). The lower post-intervention scores among cardiology nurses are notable given the strong link between diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD). As diabetes increases CVD risk two- to fourfold, cardiology nurses require stronger competence in diabetes management, glycemic control, and insulin therapy highlighting a key educational gap for targeted training.

Between-group comparisons across all clinical areas were not statistically significant (p = 0.733), suggesting that the intervention benefited nurses across departments, though improvement magnitude varied. All experience groups (1–40 years) showed significant gains (p ≤ 0.037). Pre-intervention differences were significant (p = 0.013) but diminished after the intervention (p = 0.143), indicating consistent effectiveness across varying experience levels.

Discussion

This structured educational intervention markedly improved nurses’ diabetes-related knowledge across all assessed domains, establishing the value of continuous professional development (CPD) in strengthening clinical competence. The magnitude of post-intervention gains suggests benefits extending beyond knowledge acquisition to clinical reasoning and patient education, supporting global evidence that structured, context-specific CPD initiatives help bridge the persistent knowledge–practice gap among nurses managing chronic disease [18].

The largest gain occurred in the insulin knowledge domain, reflecting that insulin therapy remains a particularly high-risk component of diabetes management, where dosing and administration errors can cause hypoglycemia or poor glycemic control. This improvement suggests the workshop’s hands-on format effectively closed a critical learning gap, consistent with evidence from China and similar settings showing that nurses often hold positive attitudes toward diabetes care but inadequate insulin-related knowledge and skills [19, 20]. The parallel improvement in the Pathology/Symptoms domain (r = 0.5) indicates deepened conceptual understanding alongside factual gains, supporting evidence that programs emphasizing pathophysiological reasoning enhance nurses’ clinical decision-making and individualized patient education [21].

Consistent with these domain-specific gains, a Malaysian primary-care study found that a six-month Continuing Medical Education course (SRCC) improved nurses’ competence across self-monitoring of blood glucose, foot care, insulin administration, and patient engagement [22]. The present study similarly observed medium effect sizes (r = 0.3–0.4) for Diabetes Management, Blood Glucose Monitoring, Diet & Nutrition, and Foot Care/Complications.

Intervention impact varied across demographic groups. Younger (25–45 years) and less experienced nurses (1–10 years) gained more than older, more experienced colleagues (p = 0.005 and p = 0.013), and degree-qualified nurses improved more than diploma holders (p < 0.001). This likely reflects differential exposure to contemporary, evidence-based learning frameworks through recent curricula, familiarity with self-directed digital resources, and stronger analytical skills rather than inherent differences in ability [23, 24]. A Taiwanese study similarly linked nursing competence to knowledge, clinical experience, and continuing education, with targeted courses enhancing care delivery [25].

These differences also point to confounding factors such as educational background, clinical exposure, and workplace learning environment [26]. Nurses with stronger academic preparation may engage more critically with new content, while frequent contact with diabetic patients allows learning to be consolidated through practice; conversely, rotating or high-acuity roles with limited chronic-disease exposure may constrain this. Tailoring CPD to nurses’ age, experience, and qualifications may therefore maximize knowledge translation into practice.

Male nurses showed greater knowledge gains than female nurses (34 vs. 26, p = 0.023) despite similar baseline scores (21 vs. 21, p = 0.929), possibly reflecting concentration in technical or procedural roles that reinforce learning through practice [27]. Sociocultural factors may also contribute; qualitative evidence suggests male nurses favor a solution-focused, practical approach, while female nurses emphasize empathetic, meticulous care [28], and differences in departmental placement and procedural involvement may further shape experiential learning [29]. These findings point to a need for gender-sensitive professional development strategies and further research into the behavioral and contextual drivers involved.

An unexpected finding was that one-third (33%) of participating nurses self-reported having diabetes and showed significantly higher post-intervention scores (p = 0.036), consistent with heightened personal motivation. This proportion exceeds national estimates 16.98% among Pakistani adults aged ≥ 20 years, peaking at 26.03% in those aged 51–60 [30] and should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect the health-aware, diabetes-exposed environment of a tertiary hospital, selection bias from convenience sampling (nurses with personal or family experience of diabetes may have been more motivated to participate), or misclassification given that diabetes status was self-reported and not clinically verified. As these explanations cannot be disentangled within the present design, the finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than firm evidence of an elevated diabetes burden among nurses.

Knowledge improved across most clinical units, with greater gains in orthopaedic, medical, paediatric, obstetrics & gynaecology, and surgical areas, while neurology (p = 0.109) and cardiology (p = 0.157) nurses improved least. In these acute, procedure-focused specialties, routine exposure to chronic disease management and diabetes education is limited, consistent with evidence that non-endocrinology and cardiovascular/intensive-care nurses often show moderate self-assessed but lower actual diabetes knowledge [31]. This pattern suggests the smaller gains reflect reduced opportunity to consolidate learning in practice rather than lower learning capacity, supporting the case for specialty-tailored rather than uniform CPD content.

Clinical and policy implications

This study demonstrates that structured diabetes education significantly improves nurses’ knowledge, highlighting the importance of integrating evidence-based training into continuing professional development programs. Hospitals should implement standardized curricula combining theory and practical skills, tailored to nurses’ experience, qualifications, and departmental needs, with special focus on high-risk areas such as insulin management. Strengthening nurse-led patient education through structured counseling and follow-up can enhance clinical outcomes, while institutional support including resources, skilled educators, and protected training time is essential for sustainability. At a national level, incorporating standardized diabetes modules into nursing curricula and CPD frameworks can promote uniform competencies, bridge knowledge–practice gaps, and improve the overall quality of diabetes care in Pakistan. These recommendations align with the Future of Nursing 2020–2030, which emphasizes preparing nurses to tackle health equity, fully utilize their expertise, and integrate evidence-based interventions into practice to improve health outcomes [32].

Additionally, the finding that one-third of participating nurses self-reported having diabetes emphasiszes the need for workplace health screening and lifestyle promotion programs. Supporting the health and well-being of nurses is not only an ethical imperative but also crucial for sustaining high-quality patient care, as healthcare workers with chronic conditions may face challenges balancing self-care and professional responsibilities.

Limitations

Despite the significant findings, this study has several limitations. First, the quasi-experimental design without randomization and the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the results to nurses in other hospitals or regions. Another limitation is that the post-intervention assessment was conducted only one week after the educational session. Although this interval allowed evaluation of short-term knowledge acquisition, it does not provide evidence regarding long-term knowledge retention or sustained application in clinical practice [33]. Future studies should consider follow-up assessments at 3–6 months to evaluate sustained impact. Relatedly, the absence of long-term follow-up means this study cannot establish whether the knowledge gained was retained over time or translated into sustained changes in clinical practice, and this should be regarded as a key limitation of the present design. Third, the study relied solely on a knowledge-based tool (DBKT) and did not assess changes in actual clinical skills, patient outcomes, or behavior, which may limit understanding of the intervention’s practical impact. Finally, the single-session format may not be sufficient to address complex aspects of diabetes care, such as advanced insulin management or chronic complication monitoring, highlighting the need for ongoing, multi-session training and follow-up evaluations.

Strengths of the study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first quasi-experimental study in Pakistan evaluating the impact of structured diabetes education on the knowledge level of registered nurses in a public hospital setting. The systematic data collection process, adherence to protocol, and high engagement from participants during teaching sessions enhanced the study’s quality. The study also explored the association of demographic variables with knowledge levels, as well as the prevalence of diabetes among nurses a unique addition to the existing body of literature.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the structured educational intervention proved highly effective in significantly improving diabetes knowledge among nurses, with particularly strong gains in insulin knowledge. The intervention’s success was evident across most demographic groups, though its impact was modulated by factors such as academic qualification, age, and clinical experience. These findings advocate for the widespread implementation of similar educational programs while also calling for a more personalized approach to professional development that addresses the specific needs of different nursing subgroups. Future research should focus on evaluating the long-term retention of this knowledge and its direct impact on clinical outcomes for patients with diabetes.

Recommendations

The findings support the implementation of mandatory, continuous diabetes education programs for nurses in both public and private hospitals. Nursing councils and academic institutions should consider revising undergraduate curricula to incorporate comprehensive content on diabetes management. Additionally, a national framework to introduce a specialized Diabetes Nurse Educator certification should be developed. Given the concerning prevalence of diabetes among nurses observed in this study, further research is warranted to explore contributing factors and to design preventive strategies for reducing the burden of diabetes in the nursing workforce.
