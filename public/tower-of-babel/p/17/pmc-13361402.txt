Cuproptosis: A Potential Target for the Treatment of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Ai Qian, Ke-Xin Hu, Ya-Wen Zhu, Ming Li, Chuan-Bing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease characterized by multi-organ involvement and broad immunological abnormalities, including persistent positive anti-nuclear antibodies. Different from many other autoimmune disorders, SLE is associated with significant disruption of copper homeostasis. Accumulating clinical evidence demonstrates elevated serum copper concentrations in SLE patients, which are closely associated with disease activity and inflammatory status. Despite advances in disease management, effective therapeutic options for SLE remain limited. Most SLE patients still require nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, and glucocorticoids, yet these treatments are frequently associated with adverse events and variable efficacy, particularly with long-term glucocorticoid use. For refractory SLE cases and patients experiencing chronic glucocorticoid-related toxicity, novel therapeutic strategies targeting cuproptosis are urgently required. Although biologic therapies have broadened available treatment options, their high cost and increased infection risk limit widespread clinical application. Its complex pathogenesis and pronounced clinical variability make personalized management strategies highly challenging. Identifying new molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets, therefore, remains a major focus in SLE research. Recent studies have revealed a newly recognised form of programmed cell death, termed cuproptosis, within SLE-related pathological processes. Copper-mediated cuproptosis is driven by enhanced mitochondrial metabolism and excessive reactive oxygen species accumulation, processes that may promote immune-cell activation and inflammatory injury in SLE. Several key regulators are involved in this pathway, including ferredoxin 1 (FDX1); the lipoic-acid synthesis and protein lipoylation enzymes lipoic acid synthase (LIAS), lipoyltransferase 1 (LIPT1), and dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD); pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHC) subunits PDHA1 and PDHB, and dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT); as well as additional regulatory factors, including glutaminase (GLS), metal-regulatory transcription factor 1 (MTF1), and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A). This review explores the relationship between cuproptosis and ten associated genes, their roles in dysregulated immune responses, and the emerging opportunities and challenges in developing therapeutic targets and clinical interventions.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is the prototype autoimmune disease characterized by involvement of multiple organ systems and the presence of diverse autoantibodies in the serum. It follows a course of fluctuating activity with episodes of relapse and remission that are difficult to control, severely affecting work, sleep, and overall physical and mental health.1,2 Due to the complexity and heterogeneity of SLE and the variability in treatment responses, a standard management approach remains under exploration.3,4 This underscores the need to identify new molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets of SLE that may offer more precise diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Disturbances in cell death pathways are associated with the pathogenesis of SLE. Impaired clearance of apoptotic cells leads to self-antigen exposure, triggering pathological autoimmune responses.5 Abnormal apoptosis in immune cells, such as lymphocytes, disrupts immune tolerance and promotes autoantibody production.6 Neutrophil NETosis (neutrophil extracellular trap) releases nuclear material and granule proteins that act as potent self-antigens and strongly drive pathological type I interferon signaling.7 Macrophage pyroptosis, driven by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway activation, releases interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and other pro-inflammatory mediators that increase tissue inflammation.8 Beyond these well-established pathways, a novel form of regulated cell death, cuproptosis, has been identified. Recent advances have characterized cuproptosis as a cytotoxic mechanism in which copper binds directly to acylated components of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, causing aggregation of these components, loss of iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster proteins, and protein-toxicity stress that leads to cell death.9

Copper is an essential trace element that serves as a cofactor for several enzymes involved in immune responses and oxidative stress. Multiple studies have reported elevated serum copper levels in SLE patients, suggesting a possible role in disease development.10 Tóth et al11 reported increased serum copper concentrations in SLE patients and a shift in copper-binding patterns: copper was predominantly albumin-bound in SLE, whereas ceruloplasmin was the major carrier in healthy individuals, suggesting disruption of metal-metabolism homeostasis during chronic inflammation. Other studies have confirmed that elevated serum copper is associated with increased SLE risk and correlates positively with C-reactive protein levels.12,13 Copper may exacerbate inflammatory reactions and sustain chronic inflammation by interacting with pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress pathways.14 Current evidence suggests that copper’s effects on SLE primarily involve its role in oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, both of which are central to disease progression.15

To better understand these relationships, a systematic literature search was conducted on cuproptosis, cuproptosis-related genes, and SLE using databases including PubMed and Web of Science. The objective was to clarify how copper metabolism and copper-dependent cytotoxic pathways may contribute to SLE and to provide a theoretical basis for developing new therapeutic strategies.

The Relationship Between Cuproptosis and SLE

Various forms of programmed cell death have been linked to the pathogenesis of SLE, and growing evidence indicates potential interactions between cuproptosis and the immunopathology of this disease. Immune infiltration studies have shown increased numbers of neutrophils, memory B cells, plasma cells, macrophages, and activated dendritic cells in SLE patients.3 Neutrophils contribute to pathology through several mechanisms, including the induction of plasma-cell–like dendritic cells that drive interferon production and promote disease progression. Although NETosis is an important antimicrobial defence mechanism of neutrophils, excessive or inadequately cleared neutrophil extracellular traps can damage tissues and stimulate inflammatory cells, thus promoting disease progression.16,17 Research has shown that reactive oxygen species (ROS) strongly promote NETosis, and the interactions between ROS and NET formation are bidirectional.18,19 Cuproptosis involves ROS generation via disruption of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, leading to oxidative stress in blood vessels and connective tissues and contributing to chronic inflammation.20 Therefore, ROS may represent a mechanistic link between cuproptosis and NETosis in SLE pathogenesis.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway is a key regulator of cellular growth, metabolism, and autophagy, and dysregulated mTOR signaling has been implicated in several autoimmune disorders, including SLE.21 Copper can interfere with autophagy through multiple mechanisms, including increased expression of autophagy-related (ATG) proteins, regulation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-mTOR signaling, and induction of oxidative stress. Moreover, deficiency of the copper-export protein ATPase copper transporting beta (ATP7B) or intracellular copper accumulation activates transcription factor EB (TFEB), a major regulator of lysosomal biogenesis, enhancing autophagic activity.22 Effector T cells also rely on mTOR-dependent metabolic pathways and exhibit increased glycolytic uptake of glucose and enhanced glutaminolysis of glutamine, which may indirectly influence cuproptosis by altering substrate availability.23

Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate through sequential enzymatic reactions mediated by enzymes such as hexokinase and pyruvate kinase, supplying ATP required for cellular activity.24 In SLE, M1 macrophages depend predominantly on glycolytic metabolism and release multiple pro-inflammatory mediators that contribute to disease progression. Co-stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-γ stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) and promotes metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis in M1 macrophages.25 Recent findings by Ou et al26 identified hexokinase domain-containing protein 1 (HKDC1), a mitochondrial-associated hexokinase that interacts with heat shock cognate B (HSCB) and the cuproptosis regulator FDX1, increasing intracellular copper accumulation and initiating cuproptosis. In LPS-stimulated THP-1 macrophages, HKDC1 knockdown altered FDX1 activity and modified the expression of copper-transport proteins ATP7B and solute carrier family 31 member 1 (SLC31A1).26 These results suggest that glycolytic reprogramming and cuproptosis may be functionally interconnected in M1 macrophages during SLE progression, providing new insight into disease-associated metabolic regulation. Potential Functions of Cuproptosis-Related Genes in SLE

PDHA1

PDHA1 may contribute to SLE development through its regulatory role in glycolysis. As the catalytic core of the PDHC, PDHA1 controls the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA within the tricarboxylic acid cycle by oxidizing decarboxylated pyruvate. Its involvement in metabolic control has been widely reported in various tumors. For example, PDHA1 plays an inhibitory role in the proliferation and aerobic glycolysis of cervical cancer cells,64 and its downregulation in gastric cancer promotes cancer cell growth, invasion, and glycolytic activity.65 In prostate cancer, knockdown of PDHA1 increases glutamine-dependent survival and drives metabolic reprogramming,66 while PDHA1 knockout in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma promotes cell proliferation, migration, and chemotherapy tolerance.67

Immune cells undergoing activation or inflammatory stimulation require rapid metabolic shifts to meet increased bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands. During activation and differentiation, these cells shift from catabolic metabolism to anabolic metabolism and rely on glycolysis to rapidly produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and metabolic intermediates.68 Pro-inflammatory effector cells in SLE—such as M1 macrophages, activated B cells, and effector T cells—prefer glycolysis for energy and intermediate products production, accompanied by increased activity of the pentose phosphate pathway and glutamine utilization, whereas the level of fatty-acid synthesis remains relatively reduced.69,70 This metabolic pattern suggests that PDHA1 may be inhibited in effector cells (such as M1 macrophages, activated B cells, and effector T cells) in SLE, favouring a glycolytic state and supporting cellular activation and expansion.

In addition to its role in metabolic regulation, PDHA1 may also affect SLE by regulating inflammatory responses. Activation of the NOD-like receptor heat protein domain-associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and the release of downstream inflammatory mediators are key pathogenic features of SLE.71 Inhibition of pyruvate oxidation through reduced activity of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 2 (MPC2) or PDHA1 increases NLRP3 inflammasome activation, indicating that impaired mitochondrial pyruvate transport and increased lactate fermentation support the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome.27 NLRP3 inflammasome activation leads to the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 2 (MPC2) and others.27 Macrophages, which drive the inflammatory cascade in SLE, are sensitive to this regulation. Loss of the silencing regulatory protein 3 (SIRT3) reduces PDHA1 activity by increasing lysine acetylation, leading to intracellular lactate accumulation and reduced macrophage pH, controlling NLRP3 and IL-1β secretion by macrophages.28 In vitro models using LPS-stimulated macrophages have further elucidated this pathway.72 Melatonin receptor 1 (MT1) reduces LPS-induced aerobic glycolysis and suppresses inflammatory responses by increasing PDHA1 expression and affecting oxidative phosphorylation.29 MT1 has already been associated with SLE pathogenesis through its effects on the Th1/Th17 balance and the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, which regulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and IL-1β, exerting anti-inflammatory effects.73

PDHA1 appears to serve as a key regulatory element linking glycolytic reprogramming and inflammation and is regulated by multiple transcriptional mechanisms. Its exact role in SLE, however, requires further in-depth research through continued mechanistic studies.

PDHB

The PDHC is a tetramer composed of two alpha subunits (PDHA1) and two beta subunits (PDHB). PDHB, as an essential catalytic component, participates in the decarboxylation of pyruvate and plays a key role in glycolysis-related energy metabolism, similar to the function of PDHA1.30 DJ-1 (encoded by PARK7), a key familial Parkinson’s disease gene, is known to act as a regulatory factor for PDH activity in CD4⁺ regulatory T cells. DJ-1 binds to PDHB and inhibits PDH phosphorylation, supporting PDH activity and oxidative phosphorylation. Given the significant dysregulation of Treg homeostasis in SLE, the DJ-1–PDHB axis represents a potential regulatory target for maintaining or restoring Treg stability.31

Xia et al32 used machine-learning approaches combined with single-cell sequencing and animal experiments to identify molecular pathways associated with cuproptosis in SLE. Their findings showed that, based on ROC curve analyses, the cuproptosis–related genes PDHB and GLS hold strong diagnostic relevance. Among all immune cells with high PDHB expression, macrophages accounted for the most significant proportion, while plasma cells showed the highest PDHB expression intensity. GLS expression was also highest in plasma cells. These observations highlight the diagnostic importance of PDHB and GLS in SLE pathophysiology.

Disruption of ERK signalling in CD4⁺ T-cell populations has been implicated in SLE pathology.74 Studies by Tang et al demonstrated that PDHB inhibits RasV12-driven ERK signalling and limits cell growth.33 This suggests that PDHB may suppress excessive lymphocyte activation in SLE by inhibiting ERK pathway activity. Reduced PDHB expression may reprogram glucose metabolism toward a glycolytic state, inhibit the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and exacerbate immune-cell dysfunction. These changes may reflect a cellular adaptation to copper-related metabolic stress.

GLS

GLS exists as two isoenzymes, GLS1 and GLS2, both of which catalyze the first step of glutamine catabolism. The functional differences between these isoforms arise from the presence of PDZ-domain-binding motifs in GLS2; their absence impairs GLS2’s ability to control ROS levels and support IL-2 production. GLS1 is required for Th17-cell differentiation, and its inhibition can suppress autoimmune pathology in animal models. Hisada et al34 reported reduced GLS2 expression in CD4⁺ T cells from lupus-prone mice and patients with SLE. Overexpression of GLS2 restored ROS balance in lupus mice and SLE patients and recovered IL-2 production by CD4⁺ T cells.

Although GLS1 and GLS2 both convert glutamine to glutamate, their downstream biological effects differ significantly. In various cancer cell lines, GLS1 upregulation supports tumor growth, whereas GLS2 expression is associated with quiescent or differentiated states.35 GLS1 is known to drive Th17-cell differentiation, and either pharmacological inhibition or genetic deletion of GLS1 has been shown to slow disease progression in MRL/lpr mice.36 GLS2 lowers ROS levels through a glutathione (GSH)-dependent antioxidant system. Excessive intracellular ROS is associated with various pathological states, including malignancies and SLE.75 Within the immune system, moderate ROS levels are required for T-cell signalling, but excessive ROS compromises T-cell survival and inhibits IL-2 production.76 Impaired IL-2 production is a key immunological defect in SLE, and restoring this pathway is considered clinically relevant.75

Studies have shown that GLS2 expression is downregulated in CD4⁺ T cells from MRL/lpr mice and SLE patients, whereas GLS2 overexpression reduces ROS and restores IL-2 production.34 These observations suggest that GLS2-centred signalling may represent a promising therapeutic direction for SLE. GLS appears to affect plasma cells, Th17 cells, and CD4⁺ T-cell subsets in SLE by regulating the differentiation of inflammatory cells and supporting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

LIAS

LIAS is a key mitochondrial enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of iron–sulfur clusters and for generating the endogenous antioxidant α-lipoic acid (LA). LA also acts as an essential cofactor in the PDHC and the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, both of which contribute to mitochondrial energy metabolism.37 As mitochondria serve as the primary source of intracellular ROS, disturbances in mitochondrial redox homeostasis are closely associated with the immunopathogenesis of SLE. Excessive oxidative stress has been shown to aggravate inflammation, trigger apoptosis, and disrupt immune tolerance, thus contributing to disease progression.77

LIAS has been identified as a copper-toxicity–related gene and may affect SLE through its role in immune-cell composition in affected tissues. Immune-infiltration analyses revealed strong associations between LIAS expression and multiple immune-cell subsets, including Treg cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, natural killer cells, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and platelets.38 These populations have well-established relevance to SLE pathophysiology, particularly in aberrant cytokine secretion, autoantibody formation, and tissue injury.78–80

Functional enrichment analyses showed that LIAS-related gene sets were significantly enriched in the JAK–STAT signalling pathway, especially within type I interferon responses.38 Genes associated with LIAS expression are widely involved in multiple central regulatory mechanisms in this pathway, including suppressor of cytokine signaling-1 (SOCS1)- and SOCS3-mediated inhibition of JAK kinase activity, Src homology region 2 domain-containing phosphatase-1 (SHP-1)-dependent JAK1 dephosphorylation, and STAT2 phosphorylation. These events shape interferon-α and interferon-β signalling, underscoring the importance of LIAS in immune regulation.39

Further analysis of two GEO datasets (GSE122459 and GSE61635) demonstrated that LIAS achieved the highest diagnostic performance among a panel of 10 cuproptosis-related genes, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 86.5%.38 This suggests that LIAS may serve as a potential biomarker for SLE. These results indicate that copper-related oxidative stress and downstream inflammatory responses associated with LIAS may contribute to disease development and carry diagnostic value.

DLAT

Autophagy is closely associated with the immunopathology of SLE. Increased numbers of autophagosomes have been reported in T cells from SLE patients, and increased autophagy may support the survival of autoreactive lymphocytes.81 Autoantibodies isolated from the serum of SLE patients can also trigger autophagy in neuroblastoma cells, underscoring the regulatory importance of this process in disease activity. DLAT, a core component of the PDHC, has been associated with autophagy induction when expressed at high levels on the cell surface; however, this evidence is primarily derived from cancer and neurological disease models, and direct experimental validation in SLE models remains lacking.32

The association between DLAT and SLE may be indirectly reflected through complications related to steroid exposure. Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a common cause of disability, and corticosteroid use is a major risk factor for its occurrence and progression. Because steroids are routinely administered in SLE management, steroid-induced ONFH (SIONFH) has become a significant clinical concern.82 Currently, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are recognised pathological features of SIONFH. Copper dysregulation may contribute to this process, as copper ions can bind to multiple lipid-acylated mitochondrial enzymes, including PDHB and DLAT within the PDHC complex. Such interactions interfere with enzyme function and impair the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the central metabolic pathway responsible for mitochondrial energy production. Dysfunction of the tricarboxylic acid cycle decreases electron transport chain activity, lowers ATP synthesis, and increases ROS generation through electron leakage to oxygen molecules.40,41 These mechanisms suggest that DLAT, through its sensitivity to copper-induced dysfunction, may participate in oxidative stress–related pathways relevant to SLE pathogenesis and its complications. However, direct evidence supporting the involvement of DLAT in SLE pathogenesis remains limited, and further investigations using SLE animal models and patient samples are required to validate these proposed mechanisms.

FDX1

FDX1 encodes a small acidic iron–sulfur protein that functions as an electron-transfer carrier for the mitochondrial cytochrome P450 system and participates in metabolic pathways involving steroid hormones, vitamin D, and bile acids.42 Evidence from Sun et al43 indicates that FDX1 supports mitochondrial stability, and its downregulation may trigger mitochondrial depolarization and oxidative stress. Excessive ROS can damage mitochondria, induce mitochondrial depolarization, and trigger mitochondrial autophagy.83 ROS can also activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through several mechanisms.84 In SLE patients, this pathway remains persistently activated, with increased secretion of inflammatory factors. Phosphorylation of PI3K activates AKT, which, in turn, regulates downstream apoptotic factors such as Bax and Caspase-3, aggravating inflammatory injury and platelet apoptosis.85

Studies in inflammatory models further highlight FDX1’s relevance to immune-mediated pathology. Che J44 reported that in IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes and monocytes, expression of cuproptosis-related genes was significantly altered, and copper-dependent cytotoxic stress increased FDX1 expression levels while promoting chondrocyte apoptosis. These observations suggest that FDX1 upregulation during copper-dependent stress may also occur in chondrocyte injury associated with SLE, supporting the possibility that copper-induced cytotoxicity contributes to joint damage in this disease.

Zhou et al45 reported a strong correlation between FDX1 and the copper transporter SLC31A1. FDX1 is a mitochondrial reductase that converts Cu(II) to the more cytotoxic Cu(I), which drives aggregation of lipid-acylated proteins and initiates cuproptosis. Its overexpression has been associated with poor outcomes in several malignant tumours, suggesting that FDX1 may serve as a therapeutic target in disorders with mitochondrial metabolic dependence.46 FDX1 also shows differential associations with immune-cell infiltration, including CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and macrophages,47 and displays close relationships with signalling pathways such as HIF-1α/mTOR, p53, and Notch.48 These pathways are widely implicated in SLE pathogenesis, suggesting that FDX1-related copper-dependent mechanisms may play an important role in disease development.

MTF1

Hypoxic microenvironments, characterized by reduced tissue oxygen concentration, are essential to the pathogenesis and progression of several autoimmune diseases. The transcription factor HIF-1α coordinates cellular responses to low oxygen and remains highly active in an inflammatory microenvironment.86 Cytokines released within inflamed regions, including granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), TNF-α, IL-1β, and interferons, support HIF-1α activity and inhibit neutrophil apoptosis.87 Neutrophils from patients with SLE show abnormal ROS formation, with elevated O2−, H2O2, and HO• levels,88 indicating that HIF-1α contributes to neutrophil accumulation and the secretion of inflammatory mediators.

A significant overlap between MTF1 and HIF-1α signalling has been reported. Dubé et al49 demonstrated that MTF1 increases HIF-1α activity, while HIF-1α improves MTF1 function, forming a reciprocal regulatory axis. Activation of MTF1 requires phosphorylation by several kinase pathways, including PKC, PI3K, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and protein tyrosine kinase (PTK).49 In SLE, these pathways do not work in isolation, but form a sophisticated signaling network. PTK acts as an upstream switch following immune-receptor engagement. PTK triggers PI3K signalling, which supports cell survival and metabolic shifts, and activates PKC, which facilitates lymphocyte activation and differentiation. PKC then activates MAPK pathways, such as JNK and the NF-κB cascade, leading to increased production of inflammatory factors, proliferation and persistence of autoreactive lymphocytes, and class-switching events that promote high-affinity autoantibody production.89,90

Metal ions play essential roles in various cellular processes, including cellular proliferation, migration, metabolic regulation, and cell death. It is worth noting that metal ions regulate the functions of many immune cells and molecules in the body and have been widely reported in recent years.50 Metallothionein, encoded by MTF1, is a cysteine-rich protein that binds zinc, cadmium, copper, and related ions with high affinity, enabling detoxification and free-radical scavenging.51,52 Serum zinc concentrations are reduced in patients with SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren syndrome, and zinc-finger proteins are closely associated with the pathology of these diseases.91 These results indicate that MTF1 participates in SLE pathogenesis by maintaining metal-ion balance, limiting oxidative stress, and regulating transcriptional programmes associated with immune activation and metal-related toxicity.

CDKN2A

Dysregulated B-cell function is a defining feature of SLE. Loss of regulatory control increases the survival of autoreactive B cells, allowing these cells to bypass tolerance checkpoints and undergo clonal expansion. They can further differentiate into long-lived plasma cells that release large quantities of pathogenic autoantibodies. These antibodies form immune complexes that deposit in tissues, cause inflammation, and contribute to multi-organ involvement.92–94 Azizan et al53 reported dysregulation of the CDKN2A–MDM2–p53 axis in SLE B cells. Expression of TP53 and CDKN2A was reduced in both resting and activated B cells, with higher reductions in patients with active or inactive disease, whereas MDM2 expression was increased, particularly in activated B cells from patients with active SLE. Reduced TP53 and CDKN2A, together with elevated MDM2, may shift the balance toward increased B-cell survival rate. CDKN2A functions as a negative regulator of MDM2, stabilizing and activating p53 by inhibiting MDM2 and p14ARF, supporting p53-mediated cell death.54,55 Altered CDKN2A expression in SLE may therefore disturb this regulatory balance and favour the persistence of autoreactive B cells.

CDKN2A encodes two tumour-suppressor proteins, p16INK4A and p14ARF, which participate in cell ageing by regulating the cell cycle and are considered hallmark molecules of this process.56 Compared with healthy individuals, SLE patients show increased CDKN2A expression,38 consistent with the close association between cellular ageing and chronic inflammation.57 Monocytes from SLE patients display a senescence-like phenotype, characterized by increased CDKN2A expression and elevated senescence-associated β -galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity.58 Upregulated genes in SLE monocytes include several ageing-related markers (CDKN2A, CDKN2B, CDKN2D) and DNA-damage-response genes (CHEK1, CHEK2), whereas downregulated genes include CDK4, CDK6, and others that drive cell-cycle progression and are directly inhibited by CDKN2A.58 Peripheral activation of SLE monocytes promotes IFN-α production through TLR7-driven plasmacytoid dendritic-cell responses and cGAS-STING-mediated pathways in monocytes, both of which exacerbate systemic inflammation.

DNA methylation changes in immune-related genes, including interferon-regulated genes, MMPs, TGF-β, STAT3, CXCL12, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10, have been studied as diagnostic markers for SLE and other autoimmune diseases.95–98 Gravand et al59 reported a significant association between CDKN2A promoter hypomethylation and disease-activity indices in SLE patients. Patients showed pronounced hypomethylation of the CDKN2A promoter compared with healthy controls, indicating that low CDKN2A methylation can serve as an alternative biomarker for evaluating disease activity. At an optimal threshold, CDKN2A methylation yielded a specificity of 77.30% and a sensitivity of 81.33% for distinguishing SLE from healthy subjects. CDKN2A methylation levels correlated positively with C3 and C4 and negatively with anti-dsDNA concentrations. DNA methylation differences were also observed between patients with onset before age 18 and those with onset after age 18. These results indicate that reduced CDKN2A methylation is associated with higher disease activity. However, confirmation in larger cohorts remains necessary due to sample-size limitations.

LIPT1

Cuproptosis occurs when intracellular Cu2⁺ binds to unstable thiol-containing proteins, causing their abnormal aggregation, mitochondrial metabolic disruption, and, eventually, cell death.99 Protein lipoylation is a two-step enzymatic process in which LA is first activated to lipoyl-AMP and then transferred to target apoproteins through a covalent reaction catalysed by the LIPT1-encoded enzyme.60 Studies in mutant cell models reported reduced LIPT1 expression, accompanied by decreased levels of lipoylated mitochondrial proteins, findings associated with defective mitochondrial function, iron accumulation, reduced membrane potential, and increased oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation.61 LIPT1 dysfunction has been identified as a limiting factor contributing to the pathogenic phenotype of SLE fibroblasts. Disturbance of cellular metabolic homeostasis in these cells exacerbates their pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic behaviour, driving disease progression and aggravating tissue injury in SLE.62 Although these findings suggest a possible association between LIPT1 and SLE pathogenesis, careful interpretation is warranted, as most mechanistic evidence regarding LIPT1 function, particularly its involvement in cuproptosis, derives from studies conducted in cancer and neurological disease models.100–104 Whether these mechanisms are directly applicable to SLE pathophysiology remains unclear. Therefore, the biological role of LIPT1 in autoimmune diseases requires further experimental investigation.

DLD

DLD encodes dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase, a core mitochondrial enzyme in energy metabolism whose dysfunction can trigger mitochondrial autophagy.32,51 DLD is shared by three major mitochondrial enzyme complexes: the PDHC, the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, and the branched α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complex. DLD is an essential catalytic core in these complexes. Loss of DLD activity disrupts these complexes and may cause mitochondrial membrane potential loss, accompanied by a sharp increase in ROS, changes known to activate the PINK1/Parkin pathway and initiate mitochondrial autophagy.63 Evidence indicates that mitochondrial autophagy contributes to the development and functional regulation of T cells, natural killer cells, and macrophages.75 In SLE, defective mitochondrial autophagy allows neutrophils to retain oxidised mitochondrial DNA (ox-mtDNA), which can be released and recognised as a potent autoantigen. This recognition supports dendritic cell activation, type I interferon production, and autoantibody formation, promoting disease progression.105 It is worth noting that the mechanisms governing mitochondrial autophagy are highly complex and involve the coordinated action of ATG proteins, mitochondrial receptors, endocytic machinery, and mitochondrial dynamics regulators. Because of this complexity, the functional status and impact of mitochondrial autophagy regulation cannot be fully inferred from isolated mechanistic studies on mitochondrial autophagy regulation and autoimmunity.106 However, DLD is likely to be an important cuproptosis-related gene with a considerable role in mitochondrial autophagy dysregulation in SLE, warranting further detailed investigation. Conclusions

Multiple cuproptosis-related genes may contribute to the pathogenesis of SLE. PDHA1 has been implicated in immune-cell metabolism and inflammatory regulation, whereas PDHB appears important for maintaining T-cell stability. GLS and LIAS are associated with antioxidant defense and oxidative-stress regulation, respectively. DLAT and FDX1 have been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic reprogramming. MTF1 and CDKN2A may contribute to autoimmune activation and cellular senescence, whereas LIPT1 and DLD participate in tricarboxylic-acid cycle regulation and mitochondrial autophagy. As a recently identified form of programmed cell death, cuproptosis has attracted increasing attention, and research in this field is progressing rapidly. A previous bioinformatics study conducted by Li et al107 identified cuproptosis-related genes and established machine-learning models for SLE prediction, highlighting IFIT3, PLSCR1, CCR1, IL1RN, and ETV7 as potential biomarkers. However, this review focuses specifically on ten core regulatory genes directly involved in the cuproptosis pathway and summarizes their biological functions and potential mechanisms in SLE pathogenesis, thereby providing a theoretical framework for future experimental validation (Figure 2). As SLE serves as a prototype of systemic autoimmune disorders, elucidating its new molecular pathways and therapeutic targets may offer fresh perspectives for the diagnosis and treatment of other autoimmune diseases. However, due to an incomplete understanding of cuproptosis mechanisms, direct clinical evidence associating cuproptosis with SLE remains lacking. Therefore, future studies should extend beyond literature-based and mechanistic analyses and prioritize experimental validation using established SLE animal models, including MRL/lpr and NZB/W F1 mice. Particular attention should be paid to FDX1 and DLAT, given their key roles in the cuproptosis pathway and their potential as therapeutic targets. Moreover, future studies should stratify SLE patients by disease activity to investigate the expression profiles of cuproptosis-related genes across different pathological conditions. Such approaches may facilitate the identification of clinically relevant biomarkers and support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies and early-phase clinical studies.
