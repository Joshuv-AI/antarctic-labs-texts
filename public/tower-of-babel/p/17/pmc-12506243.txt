A comparative analysis of the efficacy of modified double-bundle suspension fixation versus single-bundle suspension fixation for acromioclavicular joint dislocation treatment
Authors: Dan Wen, Pei Zhang, Yaojia Lu, Sheng Meng, Wenyong Fei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare clinical and radiologic outcomes between modified double-bundle suspension fixation and single-bundle suspension fixation for the treatment of acute acromioclavicular joint (ACJ) dislocation.MethodA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of 68 patients with ACJ dislocation treated in the orthopedics department of our hospital between May 2020 and May 2024. Patients were categorized into two groups according to their surgical techniques: 30 patients received the modified double-bundle suspension fixation technique (MDB group), while 38 patients underwent the traditional single-bundle suspension fixation technique (SB group). The study statistically analyzed and compared clinical baseline characteristics, surgical parameters, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS); scores, Constant-Murley Shoulder Function Score (Constant-Murley score). On the plain radiograph, the coracoclavicular (CC) interval ratio (CCIR)was measured to evaluate maintenance of CC interval fixation. Postoperative complications, including reduction failure, titanium plate slippage, or bone resorption around the titanium plate, were also documented.ResultNo statistically significant differences were observed in preoperative data between the two groups (P > 0.05). The MDB group showed longer average surgical duration compared to the SB group (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found in incision length, intraoperative blood loss, or postoperative hospitalization duration (P > 0.05). Postoperatively, at day 2, 3 months, 6 months, and the final follow-up, the MDB group demonstrated significantly better VAS scores, Constant-Murley scores, and CCIR measurements than the SB group (P < 0.05). During the follow-up period, no complications were observed in the MDB group, whereas the SB group experienced 8 cases (8/38) of complications, including 6 cases of lost reduction, 1 case of titanium plate displacement, and 1 case of bone dissolution. According to the classification, one case was classified as type Ⅲ, while the remaining cases were classified as type IV/V.ConclusionCompared with single-bundle fixation, the modified double-bundle fixation technique for ACJ dislocation demonstrates significant advantages in preventing postoperative loss of reduction, particularly in cases of high-grade ACJ. Additionally, it is associated with reduced postoperative pain and superior recovery of shoulder joint function. Moreover, compared with existing double- bundle fixation techniques, this surgical approach exhibits a diminished suture-bone abrasion between the suture and bone tunnel, which is worth further study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09214-6.

Background

For acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation, the traditional mainstream surgical method is clavicle hook plate fixation technique. However, more and more studies have shown that the titanium plate suspension fixation technique for treating ACJ dislocation can more effectively restore shoulder joint function, significantly reduce postoperative pain, and eliminate the need for a second operation to remove internal fixation [1–3]. Consequently, this surgical approach has gradually become popular and has become one of the mainstream treatment plans for acute ACJ dislocation.

The titanium plate suspension fixation technique can be further categorized into single-bundle suspension and double-bundle suspension techniques. The single-bundle suspension technique involves positioning one titanium plate on the clavicle and another on the base of the coracoid process, with the two plates being suspended and fixed using a single set of high-strength sutures (Fig. 1 A). While this surgical approach is relatively straightforward to perform, it exhibits poor stability in the horizontal instability. Additionally, the single set of sutures is subjected to high tension, which increases the risk of postoperative complications such as suture breakage leading to loss of reduction and bone resorption due to titanium plate subsidence [4, 5]. In contrast, the mainstream double-bundle suspension technique entails placing two titanium plates on the clavicle and one on the base of the coracoid process, with the components being suspended and fixed using two sets of high-strength sutures (Fig. 1B). These two sets of sutures form a relatively stable “V” shape within the CC space, thereby providing enhanced stability to the reconstructed CC ligament. To minimize the incidence of these complications, we have developed an improved version of the existing suspension technique. Building upon the single-bundle technique, the clavicular bone tunnel is repositioned slightly medially (medial clavicular bone tunnel) while maintaining alignment with the coracoid bone tunnel. A set of high-strength sutures is then used for suspension and fixation. Subsequently, an additional bone tunnel (lateral clavicular bone tunnel) is created at the lateral end of the clavicle, through which another set of sutures is passed and routed around the base of the coracoid process. Finally, both sets of sutures are secured in the notches of the titanium plate at the coracoid end (Fig. 1 C). This surgical approach effectively integrates the two sets of sutures to function synergistically, achieving robust double-bundle suspension and fixation while maintaining relative simplicity in execution and significantly reducing the “suture-bone abrasion”. For clarity, we refer to this technique as the “modified double-bundle suspension fixation technique” (Fig. 1D).

In this study, we evaluated and compared the clinical and radiologic outcomes of the modified double-bundle suspension fixation technique (hereafter referred to as “modified double-bundle fixation”) and the standard single-bundle suspension fixation technique (hereafter referred to as “single-bundle fixation”) in patients with ACJ dislocation. We hypothesize that the modified double-bundle fixation technique may provide improved clinical and radiological outcomes when compared to the single-bundle fixation technique.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: ① Unilateral, acute and closed ACJ dislocation (injury duration < 3 weeks); ② Patients aged between 18 and 70 years; ③ ACJ dislocation classified as Rockwood type III to V; ④ Ability to comply with postoperative rehabilitation exercises and provision of complete follow-up data.

Exclusion Criteria: ① Presence of ipsilateral clavicle fracture, scapula fracture, brachial plexus injury, or axillary nerve damage; ② History of prior fracture, rotator cuff injury, or surgery involving the same-side shoulder joint; ③ Patients diagnosed with rheumatism, rheumatoid arthritis, or other bone metabolic disorders; ④ Evidence of significant osteoporosis (T-score < −2.5).

General information

This study was conducted as a retrospective analysis. Clinical data were collected from 68 patients with acute ACJ dislocation admitted to our department between May 2020 and May 2024. All patients were randomly assigned to groups using a lottery system. Among these patients, 38 underwent the traditional single-bundle suspension fixation technique (hereafter referred to as the “SB group”), while 30 received the modified double-bundle suspension fixation technique (hereafter referred to as the “MDB group”). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, age, injured side, cause of injury, or time from injury to surgery (P > 0.05; see Table 1). This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital (Approval No.: J-202066). All patients provided written informed consent prior to the procedure.

Surgical methods

Single-bundle fixation Group(SB group)

Cervical plexus nerve block or general anesthesia was administered. The patient was positioned in a beach chair position, and standard disinfection and draping were performed. A longitudinal arc-shaped incision measuring 4 ~ 6 cm was made extending from the clavicle to the coracoid process. The deltoid fascia sleeve was dissected and partially incised to expose the superior surface of the clavicle and the coracoid process. The deltoid muscle was retracted laterally to expose the conjoined tendon and the tip of the coracoid process. The conjoined tendon was longitudinally split to allow finger access to the base of the coracoid process, with care taken to avoid injury to the medial vessels and nerves of the coracoid process. Bone tunnel positioning was then performed: the exit on the coracoid side was centered at the base of the coracoid process, while the entry on the clavicle side was located approximately 3.5 cm medially from the distal end of the clavicle [8], ensuring that the drilled bone tunnel was as vertical as possible. With the assistance of a dedicated positioning device (Arthrex, USA), the surgeon used a 2.5 mm K-wire to drill the bone tunnel and placed a set of high-strength sutures. Titanium plates (Ligta Model F360180, China/Arthrex Model AR-2270, USA) were secured at both the entry and exit points of the bone tunnel, and the high-strength sutures were passed through their respective grooves. The assistant pressed the acromion to temporarily reduce the ACJ, and the surgeon tightened the suture and tied a knot above the clavicle (Fig. 1A). (Fig. 1A). Intraoperative C-arm X-ray fluoroscopy confirmed the reduction of the ACJ and the proper positioning of the titanium plates. The incision was irrigated, hemostasis was achieved, the deltoid fascia sleeve was repaired, and the incision was closed and dressed aseptically.

Modified double-bundle fixation group (MDB group)

The surgical incision, approach, intraoperative instruments, and internal fixation devices used in this group were identical to those in the SB group. This technique involved creating three bone tunnels: the medial clavicle bone tunnel, the lateral clavicle bone tunnel, and the coracoid bone tunnel. The establishment of the coracoid bone tunnel and the medial clavicle bone tunnel followed the same procedure as in the SB group, except that the medial clavicle bone tunnel was slightly shifted medially (4 cm medially from the distal end of the clavicle). Using a positioning device (Arthrex, USA) for precise alignment, the entry point should be perpendicular to the distal supraclavicular surface, while the exit point aligns with the base of the coracoid process. To ensure the medial clavicle tunnel and coracoid tunnel remain aligned in a straight line (simulating conical ligament reconstruction), the drill bit must tilt forward by 10°ཞ15° and outward by 5°ཞ10° [9]. The lateral clavicle tunnel is positioned 2.5 cm medial to the distal clavicle [10, 11], with the drill bit oriented toward the base of the coracoid process (for trapezius ligament reconstruction). For this purpose, the drill bit requires a forward tilt of 20°ཞ30° [9]. A set of high-strength sutures was introduced into the medial clavicle bone tunnel and the coracoid bone tunnel, while another set of high-strength sutures was introduced into the lateral clavicle bone tunnel and passed around the base of the coracoid process (Fig. 1E). The surgeon simultaneously placed both sets of sutures into the grooves of the same titanium plate (Figs. 1 C and 1 F), tightened the sutures, and ensured that the titanium plate adhered closely to the base of the coracoid process. Subsequently, a titanium plate was placed above each of the medial and lateral clavicle bone tunnels, and the high-strength sutures were inserted into their respective grooves. The assistant reduced the ACJ, and the surgeon tightened the sutures to ensure that the two titanium plates adhered tightly to the superior surface of the clavicle and were securely fixed with a knot (Fig. 1D). It should be noted that if the two groups of patients had fracture or bore failure during the operation, they were switched to the traditional clavicle hook plate fixation technique. These complications and countermeasures are routinely listed in the ethical approval of this study.

Postoperative management

Both groups of patients were required to wear shoulder and arm fixation braces for 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively. Within the first 2 weeks after surgery, pendulum exercises of the shoulder joint were initiated. From weeks 3 to 6, rehabilitation focused on passive movements, including elevation, abduction, and external rotation of the shoulder joint. Between weeks 6 and 8, active shoulder joint movements were gradually introduced to progressively restore the normal range of motion. Resistance training was incrementally incorporated into the regimen from weeks 8 to 12. Patients resumed daily activities at 3 months postoperatively but were advised to avoid weight-bearing activities. At 6 months postoperatively, patients were permitted to engage in light physical labor or contact sports as tolerated.

Efficacy evaluation

The surgical incision length, operation duration, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative hospital stay were recorded and compared between the two groups of patients. The VAS score for pain and the Constant-Murley Score was assessed and compared preoperatively and postoperatively in both groups. The VAS score ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity; the Constant-Murley score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better recovery of shoulder function. Anteroposterior X-ray images of the shoulder joint were obtained regularly before surgery, postoperatively, and during follow-up to evaluate the quality of reduction and the ability to maintain reduction after surgery.

The CCIR was used to evaluate CC interval fixation maintenance on plain radiographs [12]. The calculation method of the CCIR is: a frontal X-ray of the uninjured shoulder joint was taken before surgery to measure the distance between CC intervals, and the distance between CC intervals of the injured shoulder was compared with it based on this (CCIR = CC of the injured side/CC of the uninjured side) [12]. The CC interval distance was measured between the upper border of the coracoid process and the lateral aspect of the conoid tubercle. Considering the small size of the data and the measurement differences, according to Professor Park’s suggestion: reduction failure was defined as CC interval widening more than 100% compared to the CC interval of the uninjured shoulder [12]. Two specialists who were not informed of the study used a digital caliper to measure the parameters on the X-ray image of the shoulder joint in the picture archive and communication system, and then averaged their measurements. Postoperative complications, including reduction failure, cortical fixation button failure, bone dissolution, and coracoid process fracture, were documented.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) was used for the analysis. Discussion

Some studies [14, 15] have confirmed that single-bundle suspension fixation only provides vertical stability and fails to offer horizontal stability, leading to complications such as loss of reduction and fixation failure in many patients with ACJ dislocation. Scheibel [16] et al. reported that over 43% of patients who underwent single-bundle suspension fixation experienced horizontal instability of the ACJ after surgery. We believe that the reasons for the easy loss of reduction in this technique are as follows: on the one hand, for patients with high-grade ACJ dislocation (such as Rockwood type IV and V) [13], there is obvious horizontal instability in the ACJ after injury. Therefore, even if complete reduction is achieved during surgery, due to the insufficient restriction of horizontal displacement by the single-bundle suture, loss of reduction is likely to occur after surgery [6, 7]; on the other hand, during early rehabilitation exercises, the suture may generate a " suture-bone abrasion” within the bone channel, which may result in loss of reduction and functional impairment [4]. Mazzocca et al. [17] confirmed from an anatomical perspective that the conoid ligament and trapezoid ligament can respectively limit the upward and posterior displacement of the ACJ, and the trapezoid ligament plays an important role in limiting the posterior displacement of the ACJ [18]. The single-bundle fixation technique only simulates the reconstruction of conical ligament, resulting in its weak control ability for horizontal displacement [15]. We used modified double suspension technique to simulate the reconstruction of conical ligament and trapezius ligament by suturing two groups of lines in both directions, so as to achieve more powerful horizontal displacement control.

The results of our study were highly consistent with those of the appeal study: at all postoperative follow-up intervals, the CCIR values of patients in the MDB group were better than those in the SB group (Table 4).From the second day after surgery to the last follow-up, CCIR increased by 66% in SB group, while only 36% in MDB group, which also indicates that modified double bundle suspension technology has a stronger ability to maintain ACJ reduction. There were no cases of failure to reduce, titanium plate displacement or bone dissolution around the plate in group MDB, while there were 8 patients with complications in group SB. Further analysis revealed that Type IV and V patients had significantly higher complication rates than Type III patients (7 vs. 1). This also suggests that in high-grade ACJ dislocations, double-bundle fixation technology should be used as much as possible.

Although some studies have suggested that single-bundle suspension fixation and double-bundle suspension fixation are biomechanically equivalent [19], we argue that this conclusion represents only an initial, static analysis. Over time, sutures subjected to continuous high tension inevitably experience creep, and the prolonged exposure to surrounding tissue fluid further compromises their mechanical strength. This phenomenon is a critical factor contributing to the relatively high incidence of reduction failure observed after suspension fixation in ACJ repair. In the single-tunnel fixation technique, a single group of sutures bears all the stress between the coracoid process and the clavicle, placing both the sutures and titanium plates under significant tensile stress [4, 5, 20]. The greater the degree of reduction loss, the higher the tension exerted on the fibrous structure of the CC ligament, which may result in postoperative shoulder discomfort or even pain for patients [21]. In contrast, the modified double-bundle internal fixation employs two groups of sutures to share the stress between the coracoid process and the clavicle, thereby reducing postoperative pain and discomfort. Civan et al. [22] demonstrated through a biomechanical study that the double-bundle plate fixation model exhibits superior stability and more uniform stress distribution compared to the single-bundle plate fixation model, with the sutures in the double-bundle model exhibiting a longer expected lifespan. In our study, the MDB group patients’ pain and shoulder joint function were better than the SB group at all postoperative follow-up time points, which also confirmed this point.

In the currently prevalent double-bundle suspension fixation technique, two sets of sutures are suspended in a “V”-shaped configuration within the CC space. This structural arrangement inherently results in a pronounced “suture-bone abrasion” between the sutures and the bone tunnels (Fig. 1B) [6, 23]. Wellmann et al. [7] posited that the non-parallel alignment between the sutures and the tunnels is the primary reason for the inevitable cutting of the sutures. Sun et al. [24] and Seo et al. [25] further demonstrated that the angle of the CC tunnel is positively correlated with postoperative reduction loss and tunnel enlargement. The present surgical technique builds upon and refines the existing double-bundle approach. By aligning the medial clavicular bone tunnel and the coracoid bone tunnel along the same straight line, the likelihood of impact and cutting between the medial group of sutures and the bone tunnels is substantially diminished. Similarly, the lateral group of sutures passes exclusively through the lateral clavicular bone tunnel, which is oriented toward the base of the coracoid. The parallel alignment between the bone tunnel and the suture significantly reduces the probability of mutual abrasion.

Precautions

① This article does not include people under 18 years old and over 70 years old. Since patients under 18 years old are minors, bone tunnel drilling above the clavicle and coracoid process may damage the epiphysis, and a considerable part of patients over 70 years old choose conservative treatment because of low functional requirements, inclusion of this group may lead to selection bias.②Unlike other scholars who use a 4.0 mm drill bit to create bone tunnels, in this surgery, a 2.5 mm Kirschner wire is used to drill the bone channels. This is because the bone channels only need to accommodate the folded fine steel wire and sutures, which can significantly reduce bone loss [26]. Smaller bone channels can also prevent the titanium plate from sliding out and provide a larger force-bearing area for the titanium plate on the clavicle, reducing local pressure. ③ The medial clavicular bone channel and the coracoid bone channel should be as straight as possible. The surgeon can use a dedicated positioning device to drill both bone channels at once, or adopt a segmented drilling method, which is easier to achieve success in one attempt. If the latter method is used, according to our team’s previous research, it is recommended to position the exit point of the coracoid bone tunnel 5 mm anterior to the center of the base of the coracoid process along the axis of the coracoid process [27]. ④ Before tying the knots to fix, repeatedly pull the sutures to allow the titanium plate on the coracoid side to self-adjust its direction and stably adhere to the base of the coracoid process. At the same time, tighten the double-bundle sutures, first tying the knot to fix the sutures at the medial clavicular bone channel, and then tying the knot to fix the sutures at the lateral clavicular bone channel. ⑤ It is crucial to avoid over-reduction, otherwise the suspension system will bear excessive tension.

Limitations

First, the sample size in this study remains relatively limited, and further validation of the clinical efficacy of this surgical technique requires multi-center studies with larger sample sizes. Second, this study still follows the traditional practice of establishing clavicle tunnel at 4 cm and 2.5 cm distal to the clavicle [10, 11], but this fixation method cannot fully consider the individual differences of patients. A previous study has demonstrated [9] that the absolute position of the CC ligament’s attachment on the clavicle surface exhibits significant inter-individual variability. Third, the horizontal stability of the ACJ was not quantitatively assessed in this study because it was very difficult to achieve. Although some scholars [28] attempted to assess this parameter using dynamic axillary radiographs in the supine position, we consider this method to be labor-intensive, requiring multiple imaging sessions, and providing limited accuracy. Considering that loss of ACJ reduction is inevitable if significant horizontal instability exists, this study did not use observation of the CC interval to indirectly assess horizontal stability. Fourth, due to high patient satisfaction with the treatment outcomes, most patients were unwilling to undergo additional MRI examinations, resulting in a lack of imaging data that could directly reflect ligament healing.

Conclusion

Compared with single-bundle fixation, the modified double-bundle fixation technique for ACJ dislocation demonstrates significant advantages in preventing postoperative loss of reduction, particularly in cases of high-grade ACJ. Additionally, it is associated with reduced postoperative pain and superior recovery of shoulder joint function. Moreover, compared with existing double-bundle fixation techniques, this surgical approach exhibits a diminished suture-bone abrasion between the suture and bone tunnel, which is worth further study.
