New generic classification for Korean Peucedanum L. (Apiaceae) species based on deep phylogenomic study
Authors: Yeseul Kim, Kyeonghee Kim, Heewon Ji, Yun-Joo Kang, Inkyu Park, Jun-Ho Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeucedanum L. (Apiaceae) is a species-rich genus with taxonomically complex relationships and longstanding uncertainties in its circumscription. In particular, the phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic positions of Korean Peucedanum taxa remain unresolved due to morphological convergence and limited molecular evidence.ResultsTo clarify these relationships, we generated complete plastome sequences for 13 accessions representing 12 taxa in Korea, including Sillaphyton podagraria (formerly P. insolens). Comparative plastome analyses revealed differences in plastome size, inverted repeat boundaries, and repeat content between Peucedanum and S. podagraria. Phylogenomic reconstruction confirmed the polyphyly of Peucedanum s.l., resolving three distinct clades (A, B, and C). Ten Korean taxa formed a monophyletic group corresponding to Kitagawia, whereas P. japonicum clustered with Sinopeucedanum. Mericarp anatomical characteristics further supported these phylogenetic relationships, showing distinct combinations of vittae, oil ducts, and crystal types among the clades. Molecular dating analyses suggested that rapid diversification within Apioideae occurred from the Late Miocene (ca. 8.1–8.2 million years ago, MYA) to the Quaternary, likely driven by climatic and topographic changes. The three clades subsequently diverged at approximately 3.50, 3.27, and 2.29 MYA, respectively.ConclusionsOverall, an integrative approach combining plastome structural variation, mericarp morphology, phylogenetic relationships, and divergence time estimates supports the transfer of Korean Peucedanum taxa to Kitagawia (except for P. formosanum due to a discrepancy with previous findings). Furthermore, it supported the reassignment of P. japonicum to Sinopeucedanum. These results highlight the importance of combining molecular and morphological evidence to resolve taxonomic ambiguities and to elucidate patterns of lineage diversification within Apiaceae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08754-w.Keywords: Peucedanum, Kitagawia, Mericarp morphology, Phylogenomics, Plastome evolution

Background

The taxonomy of Peucedanum L. (Apiaceae Lindl. = Umbelliferae Juss.) has been controversial for centuries [1–4]. Approximately 200 species of the genus Peucedanum have been recorded worldwide, including in Europe, Africa, Asia, and North America, since the genus was first reported by Linnaeus in 1753 [5, 6]. Compared to other genera, Peucedanum has ternately or pinnately compound leaves and distinguished mericarps with winged marginal ribs and filiform dorsal ribs, as well as non-lignified internal layers of the mesocarp parenchyma [3]. However, there are substantial morphological differences between the species, which reveal variations in the shape of the mericarps, the degree of development of calyx teeth, and the presence or absence of a stem. In addition, molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed that this genus is polyphyletic [7–10]. Therefore, many researchers using morphological, micromorphological, molecular, and chemical composition data are actively collecting comprehensive data to resolve the complex phylogenetic relationships of the genus Peucedanum s.l [11–15]. Pimenov and Leonov (1993) [6] mentioned that there are most likely only eight to ten European species that are considered to represent the ‘core Peucedanum,’ leaving all the other species to be reclassified into several genera other than Peucedanum.

In regional studies of North America, 51 species previously comprising the entire genus Peucedanum were transferred to the genus Lomatium Raf. based on their ecological and morphological characteristics [16–18]. Recently, 49 species from South Africa were reassigned to six genera, three of which were newly established on the continent [9]. The distinguishing features of African species include developmental traits, such as growth habits and seasonal growth patterns, as well as morphological characteristics of the leaves and fruits [9]. In East Asia, Pimenov (1986) [3] defined the genus Kitagawia Pimenov, based on the presence of elongated, lignified cells on the mesocarp surface, and has so far moved ten species from Peucedanum to Kitagawia.

The genus Kitagawia is distinguished from Peucedanum s.s. by its mesocarp parenchyma cells with lignified inner layers of the mericarps [3]. In addition, the genus Kitagawia does not contain quercetin and kaempferol flavonoids or their derivatives unlike Peucedanum [3]. After the morphological and chemical study of the section Selinoides DC. from northern, eastern, and central Asia, Pimenov reported 10 Kitagawia species using new combinations from the genus Peucedanum [K. baicalensis (Redowsky ex Willd.) Pimenov; K. eryngiifolia (Kom.) Pimenov; K. formosana (Hayata) Pimenov; K. komarovii Pimenov; K. litoralis (Vorosch. & Gorovoj) Pimenov; K. macilenta (Franch.) Pimenov; K. pilifera (Hand.-Mazz.) Pimenov; K. praeruptora (Dunn) Pimenov; K. stepposa (Y.H.Huang) Pimenov; K. terebinthacea (Fisch. ex Trevir.) Pimenov] [3, 19]. However, some molecular studies have confirmed that the genus Kitagawia is not monophyletic based on the DNA sequences of plastid coding genes and nuclear internal transcribed spacer (ITS) with external transcribed spacer (ETS) regions [20, 21].

In Korea, 4–11 Peucedanum taxa have been recorded by various authors (Fig. 1; Table 1). In 1952, Nakai [22] reported six species and two variations of Peucedanum from Korea: P. terebinthaceum (Fisch. ex Trevir.) Ledeb. var. terebinthaceum, P. terebinthaceum var. flagellare Nakai, P. elegans Kom., P. japonicum Thunb., P. coreanum Nakai, P. paishanense Nakai, P. hakuunense Nakai, and P. austriacum (Jacq.) W.D.J.Koch (Table 1). Subsequently, Chung (1956) [23] rejected P. terebinthaceum var. flagellare (= P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum), P. hakuunense, and P. austriacum from Nakai’s list, and newly added P. deltoideum Makino ex Y.Yabe in Korea (Table 1). Lee T.B. (1980) [24] did not recognize P. deltoideum and P. paishanense in his publication; however, he redescribed a species that Nakai (1952) [22] had listed for Korean Peucedanum before, P. hakuunense (Table 1). Although Pimenov included four species from Korea in the genus Kitagawia, W.T. Lee (1996) [25] did not accept Pimenov’s new genus concept and reported five species of Peucedanum in Korea (Table 1). Notably, W.T. Lee (1996) [25] listed only P. insolens Kitag. [= Sillaphyton podagraria (H.Boissieu) Pimenov] together with other Peucedanum species, but treated P. coreanum and P. hakuunense as synonyms of Dystaenia takesimana Kitag., and P. paishanense, respectively, and P. terebinthaceum var. flagellare as synonym of P. terebinthaceum (Table 1). Afterward, a list of specific species and their descriptions was published in North Korea, adding P. litorale Vorosch. & Gorovoj and P. formosanum Hayata which are distributed in the northern regions of the Korean Peninsula, but not in the southern part (S.J. Lee, 1998 [26]) (Table 1). Despite the aforementioned reports, B.Y. Lee (2007) [27] recorded only four species in Korea (Table 1). Kim (2019) [28] and Kim et al. (2022) [29] presented nine species and two varieties of the genus Peucedanum, including three newly discovered Peucedanum species (P. chujaense K.Kim, S.H.Oh, Chan S.Kim & C.W.Park, P. miroense K.Kim, H.J.Suh & J.H.Song, and P. tongkangense K.Kim, H.J.Suh & J.H.Song). However, they did not address the relationship between Peucedanum and Kitagawia from Korea.

Although plastomes generally exhibit lower nucleotide substitution rates than nuclear genomes, they are valuable resources for studying plant evolutionary history because of their relative stability and conserved structure [30–33]. Most angiosperm plastomes exhibit a quadripartite organization with large and small single-copy regions and two inverted repeats and are maternally inherited with limited recombination [34–36]. Structural rearrangements, particularly expansions or contractions at inverted repeat (IR) boundaries, can alter genome size and gene order, providing insights into plastome evolution and lineage diversification [37–41]. Large-scale comparative analyses enabled by recent sequencing technologies allow the systematic investigation of these structural variations across diverse plant lineages [42–45]. However, the systematics of several Apiaceae genera remain unclear.

Most recently, 13 species of Chinese Peucedanum were transferred to two new genera, Sinopeucedanum B.N.Song, C.K.Liu & X.J.He, and Shanopeucedanum B.N.Song, C.K.Liu & X.J.He, based on detailed phylogenetic analyses of plastome sequences and nrDNA ITS sequences [21]. These studies commonly conducted detailed morphological and/or molecular analyses to revise the genus taxonomically. However, the taxonomic positions of several species worldwide remain to be confirmed. As for the Peucedanum species from Korea, studies focusing on new national records or on morphological and molecular comparisons among species are limited [13, 14, 28, 29, 46]. Despite these revisions, many Peucedanum species still require systematic reassessment. In particular, Korean Peucedanum species have not yet been systematically studied in detail.

The East Asian Monsoon System (EAMS), characterized by humid summers and dry winters, plays a major role in driving genetic diversification among East Asian plant lineages [47, 48]. During the Neogene and Quaternary, tectonic and climatic events, including the uplift of the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau (QTP) and the intensification of monsoonal circulation, profoundly influenced the evolution of temperate taxa, including members of Apioideae Thorne ex P.Royen [49, 50]. A temporal framework is essential for understanding how these paleoenvironmental factors have shaped lineage diversification. Although phylogenetic studies on Apioideae have improved our understanding of subfamily diversification [50–52], research has focused mainly on Chinese endemics and studies specifically targeting Peucedanum remain limited. In particular, the phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic placement of Korean Peucedanum species remain unclear, with only a few studies examining their morphological and molecular characteristics [13, 14, 28, 29, 46]. Clarifying their systematic positions will provide novel insights into the taxonomy and evolutionary history of Peucedanum and enhance our understanding of biogeographic diversification in East Asia.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive plastome analysis to clarify the phylogenetic positions and evolutionary history of Korean taxa historically treated as Peucedanum, including S. podagraria, a Korean endemic formerly classified as P. insolens. We also examined mericarp morphology to support the systematic investigation. The specific aims were to: (1) sequence and characterize 13 plastomes across 12 taxa, including one accession each of the 11 Peucedanum taxa and two accessions of Sillaphyton podagraria; (2) compare plastome structures and genetic features among these taxa; (3) infer the phylogenetic relationships of Korean taxa historically treated as Peucedanum within a comparative framework including allied genera, and estimate divergence times among the resulting clades; and (4) reassess the taxonomic status of the Korean taxa and propose revisions where warranted. Overall, this study provides new insights into the evolutionary history of Korean Peucedanum taxa and helps resolve their longstanding taxonomic ambiguities.

Results

Plastome structure and organization

A total of 13 complete plastomes, comprising 11 from Peucedanum taxa and two from Sillaphyton podagraria, were assembled using high-quality reads (Fig. 2; Additional File 1: Fig. S1, Tables S1–S3). Plastome sizes were highly conserved within Peucedanum (147,087–148,315 bp), whereas S. podagraria exhibited relatively larger plastomes compared to Peucedanum taxa (156,422–157,461 bp). All plastomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, with an overall GC content of approximately 37%. Within Peucedanum, the lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions showed only minor variation (Fig. 2; Additional File 1 Table S3). Compared with S. podagraria, the plastomes of Peucedanum were characterized by shorter IR regions and correspondingly longer LSC regions, whereas SSC lengths were similar between the two genera. Consistent with this pattern, four genes (ycf2, trnI-CAU, rpl23, and rpl2) were present as single copies in the LSC of Peucedanum plastomes but were duplicated within the IR regions of S. podagraria. Peucedanum plastomes contained 114 unique genes (80 protein-coding, 30 tRNA, and four rRNA genes), whereas those of S. podagraria plastomes contained 113 unique genes (79 protein-coding, 30 tRNA, and four rRNA genes) (Additional File 1: Tables S4 and S5).

Codon usage patterns were highly similar across all analyzed plastomes. Relative synonymous codon usage revealed a strong preference for A/U-ending codons, consistent with the overall low GC content of chloroplast genomes (Additional File 1: Fig. S2 and Table S6). GC content at the third codon position was particularly low, and indices such as Codon Adaptation Index (CAI) and Effective Number of Codons (ENC) indicated weak codon usage bias across taxa.

Patterns of repeat elements and sequence divergence among 13 plastome sequences

A total of 68–80 SSRs were identified across the 13 plastomes (Additional File 1: Fig. S3 and Table S7). Mononucleotide repeats predominated in all taxa, followed by dinucleotide repeats (Additional File 1: Table S7). Tandem repeats ranged from 13 to 37 among 11 Peucedanum plastomes, whereas the two S. podagraria plastomes contained 19 and 22 tandem repeats, respectively (Additional File 1: Fig. S3 and Table S8). Dispersed repeats were mainly of forward and palindromic types (Additional File 1: Fig. S3 and Table S9). Although overall repeat composition was broadly similar, the total length of dispersed repeats was greater in S. podagraria (Additional File 1: Table S9). In addition, the two S. podagraria plastomes differed by more than two-fold in total dispersed repeat length (2,203 and 4,471 bp).

Comparative mVISTA analyses showed that sequence divergence was consistently higher in non-coding regions than in coding regions across all taxa (Additional File 1: Fig. S4). Within Peucedanum, sequence variation was limited to a small number of intergenic spacers, including atpH–atpI, trnT-GGU–psbD, trnL-UAG–rpl32, ndhF–ycf1, trnD-GUC–trnE-UUC, and ycf2–trnL-CAA. Notably, P. japonicum exhibited distinct variations in the trnD-GUC–trnE-UUC and ycf2–trnL-CAA regions (Additional File 1: Fig. S4). In S. podagraria, variability was observed across multiple non-coding regions, including trnK-UUU–rps16, trnE-UUC–trnT-GGU, ycf2–trnL-CAA, trnL-UAG–rpl32, and rpl32–ndhF (Additional File 1: Fig. S4).

Structural and sequence variation, IR boundary dynamics

The structural features of the 21 plastomes (including publicly available sequences), grouped into clades A, B, and C, were examined using locally collinear block analyses (Additional File 1: Fig. S5). Gene order was largely conserved across all taxa, with a single inversion detected in the trnY–trnD–trnE region of P. medicum Dunn and P. japonicum (clade B) (Additional File 1: Fig. S5). Apart from this inversion, no additional rearrangements were detected. Despite the overall structural similarity, reduced sequence identity near IR regions was observed in P. praeruptorum Dunn and P. harry-smithii Fedde ex H.Wolff var. grande (K.T.Fu) R.H.Shan & M.G.Sheh, associated with insertion events (Additional File 1: Fig. S6).

To assess sequence-level divergence among clades A, B, and C, nucleotide diversity (Pi) was calculated for coding genes and non-coding regions (Additional File 1: Fig. S7). In both datasets, the LSC and SSC regions exhibited higher Pi values than the IR regions. Among coding genes, matK consistently showed the highest variability, whereas several non-coding regions, including trnK–rps16 and ndhF–rpl32, displayed elevated nucleotide diversity. Overall, nucleotide diversity between clades A and B was lower than that observed between clades A and C or clades B and C.

Plastome size variation was primarily associated with expansion and contraction of IR regions at the junctions with single-copy regions. Three major IR boundary patterns were identified (Additional File 1: Fig. S8). In clade A, the LSC/IR junction was typically defined by ycf2, with variable extension into the IRa region among species. Clade B exhibited two boundary types, including a distinct pattern in P. mashanense R.H.Shan & M.L.Sheh and P. medicum, in which rpl23 crossed the LSC/IR boundary. In clade C, the LSC/IR junction was defined by rps19, which extended into the IRa region. Across all taxa, ycf1 was consistently located at the IR/SSC boundaries, indicating a conserved IR–SSC junction structure. These boundary patterns corresponded to substantial variation in IR length among clades. Clade A exhibited relatively short IRs, clade B showed intermediate to expanded IRs, particularly in P. mashanense and P. medicum, and clade C possessed the longest IR regions overall (Additional File 1: Fig. S8). Together, these results indicate that the three major IR boundary types broadly correspond to phylogenetic clades, although some structural heterogeneity exists within clade B.

Phylogenetic relationships of Peucedanum and related species

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted on 49 taxa, with three species from the family Araliaceae Juss. designated as outgroups [Aralia continentalis Kitag., Panax japonicus (T.Nees) C.A.Mey., and Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides Colenso] (Fig. 3; Additional File 1: Table S10). The ML and BI analyses yielded identical tree topologies, with most nodes showing strong support (Fig. 3). Although a few nodes exhibited moderate-to-low support values (45/0.70 and 60/0.95), the overall support values for the clades were robust (Fig. 3). In the phylogenetic tree, the genus Peucedanum was resolved into two well-supported clades (clades A and B; support values were 100/1 for each clade), thereby demonstrating that the Korean taxa historically treated as Peucedanum are not monophyletic (Fig. 3). Of the 11 Peucedanum taxa, 10 (P. formosanum, P. paishanense, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum, P. litorale, P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, P. tongkangense, P. miroense, and P. elegans) were clustered into clade A (Fig. 3). P. japonicum was placed in the phylogenetically distant clade B (Fig. 3). The two S. podagraria sequences were grouped with another S. podagraria (= P. insolens) sequence, which was downloaded from the NCBI (clade C) and placed near the base of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3). S. podagraria 2, which had larger cauline leaflets and inflorescences, was resolved as being more closely related to the downloaded sequence (GenBank accession no. KU041143.1) (Fig. 3). Clade A included P. elegans as the earliest taxon that could be separated. P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum did not cluster with its original variety, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum; instead, it grouped most closely with P. miroense (Fig. 3). In addition, P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, and P. tongkangense formed a separate subclade (Fig. 3). P. formosanum, P. paishanense, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, and P. litorale were grouped in a terminal subclade (Fig. 3). Clade B was divided into two subclades: one comprised P. medicum and P. mashanense, which were recently transferred to the genus Shanopeucedanum; the other included P. praeruptorum, P. harry-smithii var. grande, P. ampliatum K.T.Fu, and P. japonicum, which were recently transferred to the genus Sinopeucedanum, and P. longshengense R.H.Shan & M.L.Sheh (Fig. 3). Saposhnikovia divaricata (Turcz. ex Ledeb.) Schischk. occupied an intermediate position between the Sinopeucedanum and Shanopeucedanum subclades within clade B, possibly due to homoplastic insertion (Fig. 3) [8]. Clade C included P. insolens and S. podagraria (Fig. 3). Within this clade, P. insolens clustered more closely with S. podagraria 2 than S. podagraria 1 (Fig. 3).

In addition to Peucedanum, phylogenetic analysis revealed that species within the genera Angelica L. and Heracleum L. are phylogenetically distributed across multiple clades (Fig. 3). Specifically, Angelica polymorpha Maxim., A. gigas Nakai, and A. dahurica (Hoffm.) Benth. & Hook.f. ex Franch. & Sav., and A. acutiloba (Siebold & Zucc.) Kitag. occupied phylogenetic positions intermediate to clades A and B, whereas A. paeoniifolia R.H.Shan & C.C.Yuan and A. sinensis (Oliv.) Diels was positioned between clades B and C. Within the first group, A. polymorpha and A. gigas were closely related, and A. dahurica clustered with Glehnia littoralis (J.G.Cooper) F.Schmidt ex Miq. A. acutiloba was more distantly related to these species, with Pimpinella rhomboidea Diels and G. littoralis intervening. Similarly, three species within Heracleum L. were resolved into two distinct subclades, confirming its polyphyly (Fig. 3). H. candicans Wall. ex DC. was closely related to Semenovia transiliensis Regel & Herder, followed by H. bivittatum H.Boissieu, whereas H. moellendorffii Hance showed a closer relationship with Arracacia xanthorrhiza Bancr. than with other Heracleum species.

Mericarp characteristics for each clade

All mericarps from the seven Peucedanum species shared characteristics such as a slightly to strongly compressed dorsal outline, one dorsal rib, two lateral ribs, two slightly winged marginal ribs, flat to slightly convex or slightly concave endosperms, and no epicuticular wax (Table 2; Fig. 4). Fruit anatomical and micromorphological characteristics showed that five Peucedanum species within clade A (P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, P. miroense, P. terebinthaceum, and P. tongkangense) had an ellipsoid to oblong-ellipsoid shape, absent or prismatic crystals in mesocarp, 1 to 4 vittae in each vallecula (furrow), 2, 4, or 6 to 12 vittae on commissure, lacking mesocarp oil ducts, except for P. chujaense (which has 10–14), and rib oil ducts, except for P. miroense (having five rib oil ducts), glabrous, or with and sparse tubercles or simple trichomes (Table 2; Fig. 4A–F). Other Peucedanum species within clade B (P. japonicum and P. praeruptorum) had ovate-ellipsoid to ovoid shapes, druse crystals in the mesocarp, 3–9 vittae in each vallecula, 5–6 or 15–18 vittae on the commissure, 17–18 or 43–50 vittae overall, 18–20 mesocarp oil ducts in P. japonicum and 5 rib oil ducts in P. praeruptorum, densely pubescent surfaces with verrucate-surfaced simple trichomes (Table 2; Fig. 4G–L). S. podagraria within clade C had a narrowly ovoid to ellipsoid shape, absent crystals in mesocarp, 1–3 vittae in each vallecula, 2–4 vittae on commissure, 6–12 vittae overall, absent mesocarp and rib oil ducts, and glabrous surface (Table 2; Fig. 4M–O).

Divergence time estimation and lineage diversification

Based on divergence time estimation, the stem age of the family Apiaceae, corresponding to its divergence from family Araliaceae, was estimated at 76.37 MYA (95% HPD: 74.14–78.88) (Fig. 3A; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). In the phylogenetic tree, the genus Centella L. (subfamily Mackinlayoideae Plunkett & Lowry) was recovered as the basal lineage, with its divergence estimated at approximately 75.96 MYA (95% HPD: 73.69–78.83) (Fig. 3A; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). The subfamilies Apioideae and Saniculoideae Burnett diverged at approximately 58.63 MYA (95% HPD: 57.87–59.46) during the Paleocene (Fig. 3A; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Within the subfamily Apioideae, the most recent common ancestor of clades A, B, and C was dated to 26.52 MYA (95% HPD: 25.50–27.48), indicating that diversification among these lineages began in the Oligocene. Clades A and B, corresponding to the tribe Selineae Spreng., diverged at approximately 6.10 MYA (95% HPD: 5.54–6.64), near the Miocene–Pliocene boundary, whereas clade C diverged substantially earlier from the lineage leading to clades A and B (Fig. 3A; Additional File 1: Fig. S9).

Among Korean Peucedanum s.l. taxa, 10 of the 11 taxa were assigned to clade A, whereas P. japonicum was placed in clade B together with other Peucedanum taxa and Saposhnikovia divaricata (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Divergence within Korean Peucedanum s.l. primarily occurred from the Pliocene to the Pleistocene, with clade A and clade B separating at approximately 3.27 MYA (95% HPD: 2.89–3.67) and 3.50 MYA (95% HPD: 3.05–3.97), respectively (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Within clade A, lineage divergence occurred at multiple time points spanning the late Pliocene to the Pleistocene. In clade B, P. japonicum diverged from its closest relative, P. longshengense, at approximately 1.32 MYA during the early Pleistocene (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). In clade C, the two accessions of Sillaphyton podagraria diverged at approximately 2.29 MYA (95% HPD: 1.72–2.87) (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9).

The lineage-through-time (LTT) plot showed a gradual accumulation of Apioideae lineages, followed by a marked increase in lineage number at approximately 8.04–8.21 MYA, indicating accelerated diversification during the Late Miocene (Fig. 5B; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Most speciation events within Apioideae were concentrated from the Late Neogene to the Quaternary.

Evolutionary rates

To evaluate differences in selective pressures among clades A, B, and C, site models (M1a vs. M2a; M7 vs. M8) and branch-site models were applied separately to each clade. Site model comparisons (ΔlnL > 0, p < 0.05) detected significant evidence of positive selection only in ycf1 of clade A (Additional File 2: Tables S11 and S12). BEB analysis identified codon 1306 (K) as a positively selected site under both M2a and M8 models, with posterior probabilities of 0.957 and 0.981, respectively. Branch-site model analyses, in which each clade was designated as the foreground branch (Additional File 2: Table S13), revealed no significant signals of positive selection in clade A. In contrast, rbcL in clade B and ycf2 in clade C showed significant evidence of positive selection (LRT, p < 0.05; BEB posterior probability ≥ 0.95). In clade B, a single positively selected codon (codon 247, C; posterior probability = 0.990) was identified in rbcL, suggesting a localized selective event. In clade C, ycf2 exhibited 16 positively selected codons (BEB posterior probability ≥ 0.95), indicating more extensive positive selection.

Discussion

Comparison of plastome features between Peucedanum and Sillaphyton

The plastome size ranged from 147,087 to 148,315 bp in Peucedanum and from 156,422 to 157,461 bp in S. podagraria, with the latter being approximately 9 kb larger (Fig. 2; Additional File 1: Fig. S1). The gene content of the 11 Peucedanum taxa was within the range previously reported for the genus [10, 12] and was largely similar to that of S. podagraria, while differences in IR length and gene arrangement were observed. In Korean Peucedanum s.l., four genes, rpl2, rpl23, trnI-CAU, and ycf2—which are typically duplicated in IR regions [36]—were present as single copies within the LSC region. Similar copy number variations have been reported in other Peucedanum plastomes, reflecting lineage-specific IR expansion and contraction [10, 12].

Codon usage analysis across the 13 plastomes revealed that the effective GC3s values were consistently below 0.5, indicating a strong AT bias (Additional File 1: Fig. S2), which is consistent with patterns commonly observed in angiosperms [53, 54]. Most genes showed a weak codon usage bias, with only minor variation among taxa (Additional File 1: Fig. S2). These results indicate that codon usage patterns are largely conserved within these plastomes and are mainly shaped by compositional constraints, particularly AT-richness, with limited influence of selective pressures [55, 56].

Repetitive elements and regional sequence variation

Plastome repeats contribute to structural stability, sequence variation, and genome rearrangements [57–59]. They are classified as SSRs, tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats. SSRs, ranging from mono- to hexanucleotides, were the most abundant, with 69–80 identified in 11 Peucedanum taxa, mostly mononucleotide repeats (Additional File 1: Fig. S3 and Table S7) [12, 60, 61]. Tandem and dispersed repeats varied among taxa. P. japonicum had the highest number of tandem repeats (37) and the longest total length (1,490 bp), while dispersed repeats contributed variably to plastome size. Although total dispersed repeat length correlated with plastome size when comparing Peucedanum and S. podagraria, this pattern was inconsistent within Peucedanum, suggesting that plastome size is influenced by repeats, IR expansion/contraction, insertions, deletions, and other structural modifications [62, 63].

Plastomes of land plants generally exhibit highly conserved structures, making comparative alignment a valuable approach for detecting sequence variations [64]. The plastomes of Korean Peucedanum s.l. generally showed high levels of sequence conservation across the 11 taxa examined (Additional File 1: Fig. S4), reflecting the patterns observed in a previous study [12]. Sequence variation was mainly concentrated in the LSC and SSC regions, with the IR regions showing a comparatively lower variability (Additional File 1: Fig. S4). In addition, the non-coding regions exhibited higher sequence divergence than the coding regions (Additional File 1: Fig. S4), a pattern commonly observed in other angiosperms [65]. The plastome of S. podagraria exhibited markedly higher sequence divergence than those of Peucedanum taxa, underscoring its phylogenetic distinctiveness. Among these variable regions, ycf2–trnL-CAA, trnL-UAG–rpl32, and rpl32–ndhF are promising candidate loci for species identification and molecular barcoding.

Sequence divergence and IR-boundary shifts among three clades

The plastomes of the 11 Peucedanum taxa and S. podagraria included in our phylogenetic analysis were generally well conserved. A notable exception was the inversion of the trnY–trnD–trnE region in P. medicum and P. japonicum (Additional File 1: Fig. S5), consistent with a previously reported rearrangement pattern [10] and similar to that observed in Angelica [66]. Pairwise comparisons among the three clades (A, B, and C) indicated that sequence variability was greater in non-coding regions than in coding regions, and higher in the LSC and SSC regions than in the IR regions, a trend widely documented in angiosperms [67]. Among coding regions, nucleotide diversity was relatively high in matK [10, 12], with elevated variation also observed in rpoC2, rpoA, rps8, and ycf1. Identified non-coding hotspots included trnK–rps16, petA–psbJ, ndhF–rpl32, rpl32–trnL, and rps15–ycf1, consistent with previous findings [10]. Although some sequence divergence was observed in hotspots and non-coding regions, Pi values for all protein-coding genes remained low (< 0.04), indicating high conservation [50].

Structural variation in Peucedanum plastomes is closely associated with the expansion and contraction of IR regions. Based on IR length, the 21 analyzed plastomes were categorized into three structural types: clade C harbored the longest IRs and the largest plastomes (156–157 kb), P. medicum and P. mashanense in clade B exhibited intermediate sizes (152–154 kb), and the remaining species, including clade A and other members of clade B, had the smallest plastomes (147–148 kb). These observations demonstrate that IR expansion and contraction are important factors contributing to plastome length variation in angiosperms [68, 69]. Structural changes were primarily concentrated at the LSC/IRa and IRb/LSC junctions, whereas the IR/SSC boundary remained largely conserved, with ycf1 typically located at this junction [10, 12].

IR contractions coincide with reductions in gene copy number. Clade C retained two copies of all four genes (ycf2, trnI-CAU, rpl23, rpl2), whereas clade A and most members of clade B retained only one copy each. P. medicum and P. mashanense (clade B) also retained single copies of rpl23 and rpl2 (Additional File 1: Fig. S8). These patterns indicate that IR boundary shifts directly influence plastome size and gene copy number [52], reflecting lineage-specific plastome dynamics and differential genomic stability within Peucedanum. Similar IR boundary shifts have been documented in other Apioideae genera, including Angelica and Ligusticum [66, 70, 71]. The widespread occurrence of IR boundary changes underscores their significance as key drivers of plastome evolution and contributes to the phylogenetic complexity observed within the subfamily, partially explaining the non-monophyly revealed in our phylogenetic analyses.

Perspectives on lineage diversification from time-calibrated phylogenies

We estimated the Apiaceae–Araliaceae divergence at 76.37 MYA (95% HPD: 74.14–78.88) in the Late Cretaceous (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Although it is intermediate between previous estimates of 68.88 MYA [50] and 81.06 MYA [72], the broadly overlapping confidence intervals confirm a Late Cretaceous origin. The subfamily Mackinlayoideae (represented by the genus Centella) was estimated to have diverged at approximately 75.96 MYA, consistent with the range reported in a previous study [(89.7–)79.4(–70.1) MYA] [73]. Among the analyzed taxa, the genus Centella represents the earliest-diverging lineage within the family Apiaceae, with a divergence time comparable to that of the split between Apiaceae and Araliaceae, which occurred in the Late Cretaceous (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Although Centella possesses schizocarpic fruits, unlike the drupes or berries typical of Araliaceae, it is distinguished from most Apiaceae taxa by the absence of vittae in the mericarps. The divergence between the subfamilies Apioideae and Saniculoideae was estimated at 58.63 MYA, consistent with prior studies (54–64 MYA) [50, 74, 75] and temporally close to the Paleocene–Eocene boundary. This period included major warming events, such as the PETM (~ 56 MYA) and EECO (~ 53–49 MYA) [76], which likely facilitated poleward range expansion and niche emergence. Subsequent climatic oscillations during the Middle to Late Eocene [76–78], coupled with the uplift of the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau (QTP) and Himalayas starting around 50 MYA [79], likely drove lineage diversification. Similar to Gentiana Tourn. ex L [80]. and Saxifraga Tourn. ex L [81]., our findings support the hypothesis that the QTP and adjacent regions served as primary centers of evolutionary radiation for Apiaceae [19, 49, 50, 75, 82].

The most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of clades A, B, and C diverged approximately 26.52 MYA, followed by the separation of Meeboldia delavayi (Franch.) W.Gou & X.J.He and S. podagraria at 23.61 MYA (Fig. 5; Additional File 1: Fig. S9). Both events coincide with the Late Oligocene warming [76, 83]. Subsequently, the divergence of the tribes Apieae Takht. ex V.M.Vinogr., Careae Baill., and Pyramidoptereae Boiss. aligned with the Mid-Miocene Climatic Optimum (17–15 MYA) [84], suggesting that elevated global temperatures facilitated these early lineage diversifications. Following this, the Late Miocene was characterized by progressive global cooling, continental aridification, and episodic uplift phases of the QTP [49, 76, 85–88]. These combined environmental shifts likely enhanced ecological heterogeneity and habitat fragmentation, creating favorable conditions for further speciation.

The uplift of the QTP drove the intensification of the East Asian Monsoon System (EAMS), particularly during phases around 15.5–13 MYA and 8–7 MYA [89–95]. These climatic shifts likely reinforced ecological barriers, promoting lineage diversification. Notably, our LTT plot reveals an accelerated diversification rate starting from ~ 8 MYA, coinciding with this EAMS intensification and QTP uplift. This pattern mirrors the Pliocene diversification trends in Asian Apioideae [19, 50] and likely accounts for the polyphyly and incomplete lineage sorting observed in Peucedanum, Angelica, and Heracleum. Subsequently, Clades A, B, and C diverged between 3.50 and 2.29 MYA, a period marked by intensified climatic fluctuations [96]. Most species within these clades radiated during the Quaternary, driven by the ecological isolation associated with repeated glacial–interglacial cycles [97, 98].

Taxa in clades A and B share similar East Asian ecological niches, likely subjecting them to comparable selective pressures that conserve plastome structure. Given the MRCA estimate of ~ 26.5 MYA, key chloroplast functions were likely stabilized early and maintained by purifying selection. Furthermore, the relatively recent divergence within each clade allowed limited time for mutation accumulation. Consequently, the low sequence divergence reflects a combination of purifying selection, recent speciation, and rapid radiation, which constrains phylogenetic resolution [99]. Despite this limited sequence divergence, plastome data clearly revealed the non-monophyly of Peucedanum. While our comprehensive sampling of Korean taxa establishes a primary temporal framework, future inclusion of broader intermediate lineages will help further refine the precision of these divergence-time estimates.

Selective pressure analysis among clades A, B, and C

Selection pressure analysis provides insights into the evolutionary dynamics of plastid genes, with the nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rate ratio (ω) indicating functional constraint or adaptive change [100]. In our site model analysis, only ycf1 in clade A showed evidence of positive selection. ycf1 is proposed to encode Tic214, a component of the chloroplast protein translocation machinery [36, 101], and its considerable length may partly explain frequent detection of adaptive sites [102, 103]. In the branch-site model, positive selection was detected in rbcL in clade B and ycf2 in clade C. rbcL encodes the large subunit of RuBisCO [36] and has been reported to evolve adaptively in response to ecological pressures [104, 105]. ycf2, one of the largest chloroplast genes in angiosperms, is suggested to form part of a large AAA-ATPase complex [106, 107], and has also shown positive selection in other species [108, 109]. Except for ycf1, rbcL, and ycf2, the chloroplast genes in clades A, B, and C are subject to strong purifying selection, consistent with the overall conserved nature of angiosperm plastid genomes [50, 110].

In summary, plastome patterns observed among taxa historically treated as Peucedanum, including low sequence divergence, strong purifying selection, and structural diversity at the LSC/IR boundaries, suggest that recent diversification and rapid radiation have shaped both molecular and structural evolution in these lineages. These patterns, likely influenced by incomplete lineage sorting and past climatic events such as global cooling, Central Asian aridification, and intensification of the East Asian monsoon, help explain the complex phylogenetic relationships and limited resolution observed among these taxa.

Phylogenetic relationships of Peucedanum in Apioideae: Support for three distinct clades with mericarp anatomy and taxonomic reassessment

Our plastome-based phylogeny corroborates previous plastome- and nrDNA-based studies, demonstrating that Peucedanum s.l. is polyphyletic [10, 12, 20, 21, 50, 111, 112]. Three well-supported clades were identified within the Peucedanum taxa analyzed. In clade A, all observed Peucedanum taxa, excluding P. japonicum, included P. formosanum, P. paishanense, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum, P. litorale, P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, P. tongkangense, P. miroense, and P. elegans (Fig. 3). Three taxa formerly transferred to the genus Kitagawia, P. formosanum (≡ K. formosana), P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum (≡ K. terebinthacea), and P. litorale (≡ K. litoralis) formed a single subclade together with P. paishanense which has been treated as a synonym of Kitagawia terebinthacea (≡ P. terebinthacea) with strong support (BS = 100, Fig. 3).

To validate the phylogenetic affinity between Korean Peucedanum s.l. taxa and foreign representatives of Kitagawia, two non-Korean species, K. baicalensis and K. praeruptora, were additionally included in the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. S10). The resulting tree showed that 10 Korean Peucedanum s.l. taxa, together with the two foreign species, formed a well-supported monophyletic group (clade A; Fig. S10), supporting the taxonomic placement of the Korean Peucedanum s.l. taxa within Kitagawia. Notably, P. elegans was recovered as the sister taxon to the two foreign species, suggesting a particularly close phylogenetic affinity.

For P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, five samples were analyzed, including four additional sequences obtained from NCBI (PX549446, MT671397, PX549447, and NC_010400) (Fig. S10). The phylogenetic analysis revealed that these samples were divided into two distinct subclades: three individuals, including the sequence newly generated in this study formed one clade, whereas the remaining two individuals formed another (Fig. S10). This pattern may indicate cryptic diversity within P. terebinthaceum.

The mericarp morphology of clade A is represented by P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, P. miroense, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, and P. tongkangense (Table 2; Fig. 4). In the present study, clade A is distinguished from species of clade B by having an elliptic to narrowly elliptic mericarp outline (vs. ovoid or nearly ovoid), prismatic crystals dispersed in the mesocarp or absent (vs. druse crystals present in the mesocarp), and by lacking oil ducts and trichomes; however, tubercles are sparsely distributed on the surface (Table 2; Fig. 4). Moreover, clade B including P. japonicum [≡ Sinopeucedanum japonicum (Thunb.) C.K.Liu & X.J.He] and P. praeruptorum [≡ S. praeruptorum C.K.Liu & X.J.He] was distinguished from other species by the combination of present oil ducts and densely pubescent surfaces with verrucate simple trichomes (Table 2; Fig. 4).

From this study, P. formosanum clustered with P. paishanense, P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum, and P. litorale, which were treated as belonging to the genus Kitagawia by Pimenov (1986) [3] based on their ecological and anatomical traits. In contrast, P. formosanum has been listed as a member of Sinopeucedanum by Song et al. (2025) [21]. To assess the inconsistency between our results and those reported by Song et al. (2025) [21], we downloaded two chloroplast genome sequences of P. formosanum from NCBI (NC_032364 and PX549440) and conducted an additional phylogenetic analysis (Fig. S10). Of the three P. formosanum samples included, two sequences, including the one newly generated in this study, formed a monophyletic clade with other members of Kitagawia, in agreement with Pimenov (2017) [19] (Fig. S10). By contrast, the remaining sequence clustered with Sinopeucedanum, consistent with the interpretation of Song et al. (2025) [21] (Fig. S10). Consequently, inconsistencies in the delimitation of this species remain a persistent challenge within P. formosanum. We therefore refrain from making a taxonomic treatment of P. formosanum in the present study. Resolving its taxonomic identity will require further investigation of this taxon.

A previous micromorphological study by Kim et al. (2025) [13] confirmed that P. paishanense showed an independent pattern of micromorphological evolution, distinct from that of its close relatives. P. paishanense had the highest cell density of epidermal leaf cells among the Korean Peucedanum s.l. and uniquely displayed densely scaled epicuticular wax on both leaf surfaces [13]. In addition, P. paishanense has distinctive characteristics, including stomatal rims on both leaf surfaces [13]. The plastome sequence of P. paishanense provides strong evidence that this taxon is independent.

Three other taxa, P. chujaense, P. hakuunense, and P. tongkangense, clustered into distinct subclades within clade A (Fig. 3). They are commonly endemic to Korea and inhabit small islands, mountainous areas, and rocky cliffs along rivers, respectively [28, 29]. These three species also share the same number of vittae in their mericarps, three or four per vallecula [13]. These anatomical features could serve as useful evidence for defining these subclades as distinct phylogenetic groups. P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum was the closest relative to P. miroense among the observed taxa (Fig. 3). P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum was defined as having triangular terminal leaflets and a truncated leaflet base compared with P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum (≡ Kitagawia terebinthacea). Recognition of P. terebinthaceum var. deltoideum as a separate taxon is clear; however, its affinity with P. terebinthaceum var. terebinthaceum requires further investigation.

P. elegans was the basal lineage of clade A (Fig. 3). Along with the distinctive morphological features of P. elegans, including 2- or 3-pinnately compound leaves with linear ultimate segments bearing spined tips, the plastome sequence revealed that it is an independent taxon. An earlier study on the micromorphological characteristics of leaf surfaces also supported the recognition of P. elegans as a well-defined taxonomic entity [13]. Pimenov (1986) [3] proposed a rational argument that a new correct name for P. elegans Kom. should be assigned to Kitagawia komarovii Pimenov, because it is a later homonym of P. elegans (Hoffm.) Sweet, and has clear morphological characteristics that make it a member of the genus Kitagawia. In this study, P. elegans Kom. was closely grouped with other taxa included in the genus Kitagawia.

P. japonicum was grouped as a member of clade B with P. praeruptorum, P. harry-smithii var. grande, and P. ampliatum (Fig. 3). Those four taxa have been previously reported as members of the genus Sinopeucedanum based on a phylogenetic study of plastid protein-coding gene sequences [21]. The mericarps of P. japonicum and P. praeruptorum were distinguished from other taxa based on the presence of druse crystals dispersed in the mesocarp and the densely pubescent surfaces of the mericarp with simple trichomes (Table 2; Fig. 4). These morphological characteristics could be useful for the delimitation of the genus Sinopeucedanum.

P. praeruptorum has been treated as Kitagawia praeruptora (Dunn) Pimenov. However, recent molecular phylogenetic studies have reported that P. praeruptorum has failed to recover as a monophyletic lineage with other Kitagawia taxa [10, 21]. Song et al. (2025) [21] combined P. praeruptorum with Sinopeucedanum praeruptorum based on a molecular phylogenetic analysis. Comparable results were obtained in our study, supporting the transfer of P. praeruptorum to S. praeruptorum, as proposed by Song et al. (2025) [21].

P. mashanense and P. medicum were clearly distinguished by unique patterns of the LSC/IR boundary, in which rpl23 crossed the border between the two regions. In addition, both species were located near the subclades of P. praeruptorum and P. japonicum in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5). Our results are congruent with Song et al. (2025) [21], supporting recognition of Sinopeucedanum and Shanopeucedanum as distinct lineages.

As with the molecular phylogenetic data, the number of vittae was considered reliable for identifying taxa within the Apiaceae. Lei et al. (2022) [20] suggested that core Kitagawia species and subclade II species could be distinguished (solitary vitta in each furrow, two vittae on the commissure vs. 3–5 vittae in each furrow, 6–8 vittae on the commissure). However, in clade A, Kitagawia species had one to four vittae in each vallecula (furrow) and two, four, or 6–12 vittae on the commissure. Clade B, Sinopeucedanum, had 2–9 vittae in each furrow and 5–18 vittae in each commissure. Thus, the number of vittae should be assessed more broadly across all species belonging to Peucedanum s.l., such as Kitagawia, Sinopeucedanum, and Shanopeucedanum, to define the full range of variation.

Clade C included one P. insolens and two samples of S. podagraria (1 and 2). P. insolens clustered closely with S. podagraria 2, whereas S. podagraria 1 formed a sister group with type 2. This pattern corroborates previous studies [10, 12, 19, 21] proposing the transfer of P. insolens to S. podagraria and highlighting the phylogenetic distinction between the two S. podagraria samples. The two samples also showed differences in dispersed repeat profiles (number, length, and type) and tandem repeat content (Additional File 1: Fig. S3). Morphologically, S. podagraria type 1 displayed a distinct dwarf form, characterized by markedly smaller leaflets and rays than type 2 during the same flowering period. Considering genetic and morphological differences, the taxonomic status of the dwarf form warrants further evaluation. Additional sampling is necessary to determine whether this variation represents a genetically fixed character, ecotype, or an infraspecific or distinct species.

Taxonomic treatments

Based on our multifaceted, in-depth analyses of plastome sequences, we concluded that all Peucedanum taxa occurring in Korea, except P. formosanum, should be treated as either Kitagawia or Sinopeucedanum. Accordingly, we propose the following taxonomic treatments. To provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genus Kitagawia, we present its generic description. Additionally, a dichotomous key to the Korean species of Kitagawia, prepared based on their morphological characteristics, is provided.

Conclusions

In this study, we sequenced and analyzed the complete plastomes of 11 Korean Peucedanum taxa and Sillaphyton podagraria. Comparative analyses revealed clear differences between Peucedanum and S. podagraria, particularly in the configuration of LSC/IR boundaries. Phylogenomic reconstruction resolved three distinct clades within Peucedanum s.l., and mericarp anatomy and micromorphology, one of the most diagnostic traits in Apiaceae, further supported these molecular relationships. Molecular dating suggests that diversification in Apioideae began in the Late Miocene, with subsequent divergence of the three clades (A, B, and C) occurring from the Pliocene to the early Pleistocene. These divergence-time estimates further support the distinct phylogenetic identities of the three clades. Based on these findings, we support transferring the nine Korean taxa—K. chujaensis, K. hakuunensis, K. komarovii, K. litoralis, K. miroensis, K. paishanensis, K. terebinthacea var. terebinthacea, K. terebinthacea var. deltoidea, and K. tongkangensis—to Kitagawia. Furthermore, P. japonicum, long recognized as morphologically distinct, is strongly supported as belonging to Sinopeucedanum. However, the clear delimitation and well-defined taxonomic placement of P. formosanum remain challenging. This species, which remains taxonomically unresolved, requires further in-depth study. Nevertheless, this study clarifies the evolutionary history of Korean Peucedanum taxa, refines their taxonomy, and provides a foundation for future systematic and phylogenomic research on Kitagawia and other Apiaceae lineages.

Methods

Taxon sampling

To conduct phylogenetic analysis based on plastome data, 13 samples representing 11 Peucedanum taxa, which are recorded in Korea, and S. podagraria were included (Additional File 1: Table S14). For genomic DNA extraction, a single cauline leaf sample was collected from each herbarium sheet (except for P. chujaense and P. japonicum) that was deposited at herbarium of Seoul National University (SNU) and Korean Herbarium of Standard Herbal Resources (KIOM) (herbarium acronyms referred to Thiers 2026 [113]; Additional File 1: Table S14). For P. chujaense and P. japonicum, fresh leaves frozen at − 70 ℃ were used in the experiment. Additionally, two samples (obtained from each of the two different morphological types) were collected from S. podagraria. One type (S. podagraria 1) had relatively small leaflets and short rays (dwarf type), whereas the other type (S. podagraria 2) had relatively large leaflets and long rays (common type) (Additional File 1: Table S14). Peucedanum coreanum, which is distributed only in North Korea, was excluded from this study because we could not obtain taxon leaf samples. In contrast, P. formosanum, a controversial taxon that does not grow in South Korea, was included in this study using dried specimens collected from regions adjacent to Korea (Additional File 1: Table S14). In addition, for the mericarp morphological comparative analysis, five samples representing four taxa of Peucedanum and S. podagraria were collected from the herbarium materials, same as for molecular analysis (Additional File 1: Table S14). We examined the mericarps of a single representative taxon from each clade to characterize the mericarp anatomy and micromorphology in each group.

Plastome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [114], which is optimized for plastome sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA Nano kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and most samples yielded approximately 4 Gb of data on the Illumina HiSeq X platform (Illumina). S. podagraria 1 was sequenced on a MiSeq platform (Illumina), producing approximately 2 Gb of data (Additional File 1: Table S1). Low-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.36 [115]. High-quality paired-end reads (Phred score ≥ 20) were then used to assemble the plastomes with CLC Genome Assembler v4.06 beta (CLC Inc., Aarhus, Denmark) under default parameters. This process yielded complete sequences for 13 samples (Additional File 1: Table S2). Gene annotation was performed using GeSeq [116]. tRNA genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE [117] and ARAGORN [118]. Based on the final annotations, linear and circular genome maps were generated using OGDRAW [119].

Codon usage bias analysis

Protein-coding genes (PCGs) were extracted from 11 Peucedanum taxa and S. podagraria for codon usage analysis. After removing duplicate genes, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated using CodonW v1.4.4 [120]. The effective number of codons (ENC) and the GC content at the third synonymous codon position (GC3s) were also computed to assess codon usage bias and base composition preferences.

Identification of repetitive sequences

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified using MISA [121] with parameters specified in the ‘misa.ini’ configuration file. The minimum number of repeats was set to 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3 for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexa-nucleotide motifs, respectively. The maximum allowable interruption between two SSRs in a compound microsatellite was set to 20 bp. Forward, reverse, palindromic, and complementary repeats were detected using REPuter [122], with a minimum repeat size of 30 bp and a Hamming distance of 3. Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder [123], with the matching weight and mismatching penalty set to 2 and 7, respectively. The minimum alignment score and maximum period size were set as 50 and 500, respectively. Only tandem repeats with a sequence identity > 90% were considered. Customized Python scripts were used to process and analyze repeat data.

Plastome comparison and variation analysis

Structural comparisons of the plastomes were conducted using mVISTA in Shuffle-LAGAN mode [124]. Whole-genome alignments were performed using the progressiveMauve algorithm implemented in the MAUVE plugin within Geneious Prime v2025 to detect gene rearrangements and assess synteny patterns [125, 126]. In addition, AliTV [127] was used to visually compare genome structures and support the interpretation of structural variations. To identify variable regions, alignments of PCGs, introns, and intergenic spacers were generated using MAFFT v7 [128], and nucleotide diversity (Pi) values were calculated with DnaSP v6.12.03 [129]. Regions longer than 300 bp were selected for analysis, and the results were visualized using R. IR region expansions and contractions were examined manually using Geneious Prime v2025 [126].

Phylogenetic reconstruction

The phylogenetic tree was reconstructed based on complete plastome sequences from 49 taxa, 37 of which were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and 12 newly obtained sequences in this study (Additional File 1: Table S10). In total, 37 taxa were selected as representatives of each clade in the Apiaceae phylogeny [50]. To clarify the phylogenetic position of Korean taxa historically treated as Peucedanum, we defined three operational units for comparative analysis: Clade A (Kitagawia), Clade B (Shanopeucedanum and Sinopeucedanum), and Clade C (Sillaphyton). Although recognized as distinct genera, they were analyzed within a unified framework to explicitly demonstrate the polyphyly of Peucedanum s.l. Multiple sequence alignment of the plastomes was performed using MAFFT v7 [128]. From these alignments, 71 conserved PCGs shared across all taxa were identified. Each gene was individually aligned and concatenated for each taxon to generate a super-matrix for phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The best-fitting nucleotide substitution model (GTR + I+G) was selected under the Akaike information criterion (AIC) using jModelTest v2.1.4 [130], and was applied to both ML and BI analyses (Additional File 1: Table S15). ML analysis was conducted using RAxML v8.2.12, incorporating rate heterogeneity modeled by four discrete gamma categories [131]. Node support was assessed via 1,000 bootstrap replicates. BI analysis was performed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [132]. Two independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were conducted, each comprising four chains (three heated and one cold). The analyses were run for 1,000,000 generations with sampling every 100 generations to ensure adequate mixing and convergence.

Mericarp anatomy and micromorphology

All materials were dehydrated in a tertiary butyl alcohol (TBA) series and processed for paraffin embedding using an automatic tissue processor (Leica EG1150H; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) to investigate transverse sections of the mericarp. The tissue blocks were sectioned using a manual rotary microtome (HistoCore MULTICUT; Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) and 8–12 μm sections were placed onto glass slides. The sections were then double-stained with Fast-Green FCF and safranin O solutions using an automatic slide stainer. The permanent slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner (3DHistech Pannoramic Desk II DW; 3DHistech Kft., Budapest, Hungary). The images were then observed and photographed using the CaseViewer software v2.4.0 (3DHistech Kft.).

For observations using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM), the mericarps were dehydrated using an ethanol series (70%, 90%, 95%, and 100% ethanol) at room temperature for 1 h at each ethanol concentration. The dehydrated material was immersed in liquid CO2 for critical point drying (CPD) (SPI-13200JE-AB; SPI Supplies, West Chester, PA, USA) and subsequently mounted on aluminum stubs using a double-sided adhesive conductive carbon disk (05073-BA; SPI Supplies). All samples were gold-coated using an ion-sputtering device (208 h; Cressington Scientific Instruments Ltd., Watford, UK) and observed using a low-voltage field-emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-7600 F; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV and a working distance of 5–10 mm. Mericarp terminology followed that of Plunkett et al. (2018) [133] and Wojewódzka et al. (2019) [134].

Divergence time estimation

A total of 71 conserved PCGs shared among 49 taxa, representing the main clades of the three subfamilies of Apiaceae (Apioideae, Saniculoideae, and Mackinlayoideae) were selected for divergence time estimation. The analysis was conducted using a calibrated Yule model in BEAST2 v2.6.7 [135] and the GTR + I+G substitution model was applied, as determined by jModelTest v2.1.4 [130] (Additional File 1: Table S15). Fossil calibration points were incorporated based on previous studies. The divergence between Apiaceae and Araliaceae was constrained using a normal distribution with a mean of 60.19 million years ago (MYA) and a standard deviation of 6.6 MYA [136]. Additional priors were assigned as follows: Saniculeae, 54.17 MYA (95% HPD: 45.55–66.44); Bupleureae, 41.46 MYA (95% HPD: 36.93–48.04); and Oenantheae, 28 MYA (95% HPD: 22.8–33.97) [50]. The input XML file was generated using BEAUTi v2.6.7. The MCMC analysis was run for 100 million generations, with sampling every 1,000 generations. Convergence and sampling adequacy were evaluated in Tracer v1.7.2 [137], ensuring that all effective sample size (ESS) values exceeded 200. The maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was summarized using TreeAnnotator v2.7.3 [135], with mean node heights and 10% burn-in. The final consensus tree was visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 [138].

Detection of positive selection

To investigate signals of positive selection, selection pressure analyses were performed by estimating nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution rates and their ratio (ω = dN/dS) across 78 shared PCGs (excluding ycf15) from species grouped into clades A, B, and C based on the phylogenetic tree constructed in this study. For each clade, the phylogenetic tree used for codon-based analysis was exported from MEGA v11 [139] in the Newick format and analyzed using codeml in PAML v4.10.8 [140]. Site models (seqtype = 1, model = 0, Nsites = 0, 1, 2, 3, 7, 8) were used to detect variation in ω among codon sites within each clade. Likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) were conducted to compare the following nested models: M0 (one-ratio) vs. M3 (discrete), M1a (neutral) vs. M2a (positive selection), and M7 (beta) vs. M8 (beta plus positive selection). Positively selected sites were identified using the Bayes Empirical Bayes (BEB) method in models M2a and M8. Sites with posterior probabilities ≥ 0.95 were considered significant and are marked with a single asterisk (*), while those with posterior probabilities ≥ 0.99 are marked with a double asterisk (**). These sites correspond to the site class with ω > 1. To detect lineage-specific positive selection, branch-site models were implemented using EasyCodeML [141]. Each clade (A, B, and C) was designated as a foreground branch to identify sites unique to that lineage under positive selection.
