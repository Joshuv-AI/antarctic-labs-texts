Preoperative Health During a Pandemic: An Observational Study Assessing Lifestyle Changes in Patients Associated With COVID‐19 in Western Australia
Authors: Nadine Radin, Dana Hince, Jim Codde, Paola Chivers, Dale W Edgar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimHealthcare service disruptions resulting from the COVID‐19 pandemic led to substantial changes in surgical pathways across public hospitals in Western Australia (WA). These disruptions, compounded by restrictions and containment measures, influenced the health and lifestyle behaviours of the WA community, including patients awaiting surgery. This study aimed to assess changes in self‐reported modifiable lifestyle risk factors among patients during COVID‐19.MethodsThis observational study analysed hospital patient data across three seasonally matched calendar periods: Period 1 (January–March 2019 vs 2020), Period 2 (March–July 2019 vs 2020), and Period 3 (July–December 2019 vs 2020). Multivariable regression models were used to examine differences between these COVID‑impacted periods and preoperative self‑reported modifiable risk factors. The assessed risk factors included body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol and drug use, malnutrition risk, physical function, and frailty.ResultsData from 2340 patients were analysed. There were no indicated changes between 2019 and 2020 cohorts in relation to body mass index (BMI), current smoking status, alcohol, or drug use, poor function or frailty risk. Change was detected in past smoker, with a higher proportion of individuals in Period 2 (Mar to Jul 2020), (8% more in 2020, 95% CI3% − 14%, p = 0.005) and Period 3 (Jul to Dec 2020) (7% more in 2020, 95% CI: 2% − 12%, p = 0.003). Also, a greater proportion of patients assessed ‘at malnutrition’ risk in Period 3 (13% more in 2020, 95% CI: 6% − 19%, p = 0.013).ConclusionThis is the first Australian COVID‐19 study to report potential pressure on the preadmission pathway for nutrition care during the COVID‐19 period, with a higher proportion of patients reported to be at risk of malnutrition in later 2020. An increase in reporting of recent past smoking was observed, potentially attributable to heightened awareness of respiratory vulnerabilities associated with the virus. This study offers valuable insights to inform future pandemic planning, optimise resource allocation, and refine nutritional screening priorities. The COVID‐19 pandemic has shaped a new path for innovative preoperative strategies and possible motivations for behaviour change that enhance the development of targeted prehabilitation efforts.Keywords: anaesthesia, COVID‐19 pandemic, lifestyle, perioperative, prehabilitation, preoperative health

Introduction

In March 2020, Australian hospitals faced unprecedented disruption as the COVID‐19 pandemic escalated nationally. The first confirmed Australian case was recorded on 25 January, with Western Australia (WA) reporting its first death on 1 March [1]. By late March, nationwide restrictions were implemented, mirroring international strategies to curb transmission and preserve healthcare capacity [2, 3].

Globally, surgical services were forced to restructure, prioritising urgent and oncological procedures while postponing elective surgery [2, 3, 4]. A systematic review estimated that over 28 million elective procedures were cancelled or delayed worldwide [5]. In WA, like other Australian services, all non‐urgent and semi‐urgent surgeries were suspended to safeguard resources [6, 7]. Although community case numbers were ultimately lower than anticipated [8, 9], hospitals then faced a surge in rebooked procedures, and preadmission consultations shifted largely to telephone.

Beyond surgery, COVID‐19 restrictions had wide‐ranging consequences for population health. International and Australian studies reported reduced physical activity, poor dietary habits, increased alcohol and tobacco consumption, and greater psychological distress, particularly among younger adults and those experiencing financial strain [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. These changes were attributed to limited access to fitness facilities, altered food supply, increased screen time, home‐delivered alcohol, and employment insecurity [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. In Australia, rises in alcohol and tobacco use were strongly linked to coping with stress and anxiety [18].

Delays in surgery are known to impair function, wellbeing, and quality of life, with uncertainty and poor communication further compounding patient distress [19, 20, 21]. In Australia and New Zealand, the recommended perioperative pathway emphasises comprehensive assessment of physical and psychological health to optimise outcomes and recovery [22]. Consultations led by multidisciplinary teams are also viewed as “teachable moments” to address modifiable risks such as smoking, alcohol, diet, and physical inactivity [23, 24, 25]. These factors are increasingly incorporated into multimodal prehabilitation programs that combine exercise, nutrition, and psychological support, with evidence of improved surgical outcomes and resilience [26, 27, 28, 29, 30].

Despite the importance of preoperative health, routine data collection is limited by reliance on paper‐based forms, leaving the prevalence of risk behaviours in surgical populations poorly characterised.

This study aimed to describe body mass index (BMI), substance use, nutritional risk, physical function, and frailty in patients scheduled for colorectal and upper gastrointestinal surgery across 2019 and 2020 and secondarily, explore differences between periods impacted by the COVID response in WA. By examining health behaviours during pandemic‐related service disruptions, we sought to inform strategies for more resilient perioperative care and prehabilitation in future healthcare crises.

Methods

Study Design

This observational cohort study assessed changes in modifiable preoperative health behaviours among patients scheduled for colorectal (CR) or upper gastrointestinal (UGI) surgery at a tertiary hospital in Perth, Western Australia. The onset and progression of COVID‐19 in WA acted as a natural intervention, enabling comparisons across matched calendar periods in 2019 and 2020.

Three 2020 periods were defined to align with WA's healthcare responses:

Period 1 (1 Jan–7 Mar): pre‐pandemic baseline.

Period 1 (1 Jan–7 Mar): pre‐pandemic baseline.

Period 2 (8 Mar–20 Jul): confirmed local cases which resulted in Category 2 (semi‐urgent) and Category 3 (non‐urgent) surgeries postponed or cancelled; reduced theatre capacity; preadmission clinics moved to telephone.

Period 2 (8 Mar–20 Jul): confirmed local cases which resulted in Category 2 (semi‐urgent) and Category 3 (non‐urgent) surgeries postponed or cancelled; reduced theatre capacity; preadmission clinics moved to telephone.

Period 3 (21 Jul–31 Dec): theatre scheduling and elective surgery bookings resumed with COVID‐19 screening protocols.

Period 3 (21 Jul–31 Dec): theatre scheduling and elective surgery bookings resumed with COVID‐19 screening protocols.

The same calendar dates from 2019 were used as comparators to control for seasonal effects.

Patients and Setting

Eligible patients were 18 years and older and scheduled for CR or UGI surgery during the study period. All available preadmission paperwork was included, with final sample size determined by record completeness. Ethics approval was granted by the South Metropolitan Health Service HREC (RGS5977) including a waiver of consent to utilise the previously collected audit data for the purpose of this study, and by The University of Notre Dame Australia HREC (Ref 2024‐163).

Data Sources and Measures

Patient information was extracted from routine preadmission clinical forms: the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ; 2014 and 2019 versions), Nursing Preadmission History Risk Screen, Anaesthetic Record, Preoperative Check, and Nursing Admission and Risk Assessment. Data were audited from the digital medical record and entered into a REDCap database.

Statistical Methods

Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, including age, were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) due to non‐normal distribution. χ² and t‐tests were used to compare age, sex, and surgery group between patients with complete versus missing data; representativeness and statistical testing for DASI and frailty measures reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Regression models assessed associations between outcomes and calendar periods (2019 vs. 2020). Multinomial logistic regression was used for categorical outcomes (BMI, smoking) and binary logistic regression for dichotomous outcomes (drug use, alcohol, malnutrition risk). For physical function and frailty outcomes, logistic regression models were restricted to data from the 2020 calendar months as noted above, as these measures were not collected in 2019. Regression models were adjusted for age, sex, and surgery group in separate models. Accordingly, Supplementary Table S4 presents separate adjusted estimates for age‑adjusted, sex‑adjusted, and surgery‑group‑adjusted models. Relative risk ratios (RRR) and odds ratios (OR) estimated for all models are available in Supplementary Table S4. Wald χ² tests assessed interactions; pairwise comparisons were performed when p < 0.05. Proportions and differences were reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Analyses were conducted in Stata V18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patients

Of 2,353 identified patients, 2,340 met inclusion criteria (12 excluded for age < 18; one for incomplete records). Among those included, 1,185 (50.6%) underwent procedures in 2020. Overall, 1,698 (72.6%) underwent colorectal (CR) procedures and 642 (27.4%) upper gastrointestinal (UGI) procedures. The proportion of UGI cases was higher 344 (53.6%) in 2020 than in 2019, 298 (46.4%). The cohort's median age was 57 years (IQR 39–71), and 1,103 (47.1%) were female.

Body Mass Index

Of 2,185 with recorded height and weight, 86 (3.9%) were underweight, 621 (28.4%) healthy, 747 (34.2%) overweight, and 731 (33.5%) obese. Consistent with Figure 2, the proportion of patients in each BMI category did vary across calendar period differentially between years (no year by Period interaction was found between year and calendar periods for BMI categories, Wald χ² (6) = 9.77; p = 0.135). Patients with completed BMI were more likely in the UGI pathway (p < 0.001).

Smoking

Among 2,303 patients, 415 (18.0%) reported current smoking, 526 (22.8%) past smoking, and 1,362 (59.1%) no smoking. Past smokers were mainly long‐term (83.8%, n = 441), compared with (53) 10.1% recent quitters. The proportions of patients reporting smoking status across time Periods are presented in Table 1. Smoking status differed by year by Period interaction (Wald χ² (4) = 11.13; p = 0.025), limited to the past vs. non‐smoker comparison (Wald χ² (2) = 6.98; p = 0.031). Specifically, past smoking increased in 2020 Period 2 (absolute risk difference +8%; 95% CI 3–14; p = 0.005) and Period 3 (absolute risk difference +7%; 95% CI 2–12; p = 0.003) compared with 2019. Patients with completed smoking status data were younger (mean [SD], 55.2 [19.3]) than those with incomplete status data (mean [SD], 62.9 [16.6]; t = 37.6; p = 0.008).

Alcohol Use

Of 2280 respondents, 1206 (52.9%) consumed alcohol. Proportions reporting alcohol use across time Periods are presented in Table 1. No year by Period interaction was detected (Wald χ² (2) = 5.10; p = 0.078). Among the 148 patients who responded to the modified AUDIT‐C, 86 (58.1%) scored ≥ 4, indicating likely misuse. No year by Period differences were observed (Wald χ² (1) = 0.01; p = 0.930). Differences between patients with this assessment and the broader cohort reporting alcohol use without assessment for sex, age and surgery group are presented in Supplementary Table S3. The subset of patients who completed the AUDIT‐C tool were observed to be significantly older than those who had confirmed alcohol use (p < 0.001). Additionally, patients in the UGI surgery group were more likely to have completed the AUDIT‐C (p = 0.031).

Drug Use

Of 2046 respondents, 143 (7.0%) reported recreational drug or opioid use, most commonly opioids (50.5%, n = 55) and cannabis (39.4%, n = 43). Other substances included cocaine (5.5%, n = 6) and miscellaneous drugs (4.6%, n = 5). Proportions reporting drug use across time Periods are presented in Table 1. Prevalence did not differ across years (Wald χ² (2) = 2.22; p = 0.330). Patients who recorded non‐prescribed drug use were more prevalent among patients in the UGI surgery group (p < 0.001).

Nutritional Status

Of 1107 patients screened with the MST, 18.1% (n = 200) were at risk of malnutrition. Proportions of patients at risk across time Periods are presented in Table 1. A year by Period interaction was observed (Wald χ² (2) = 8.75; p = 0.013), with higher risk in 2020 Period 3 than 2019 (absolute risk difference +13%; 95% CI 6–20). Patients with completed malnutrition status data were older (mean [SD], 59.2 [17.8]) than those with missing data (t = 2337.9; p < 0.001) and were more frequently from the upper gastrointestinal (UGI) surgery group (p < 0.001).

Physical Function (2020 Only)

Of 297 patients eligible for the PHQ in 2020, 191 completed the DASI. One‐third (33.0%, n = 63) scored < 30, indicating reduced functional capacity and higher risk with surgery. The proportion ‘below threshold’ increased between Periods 1 and 3 shown in Table 2 but were not statistically observed (Wald χ² (2) = 0.93; p = 0.628).

Discussion

This study examined preoperative health behaviours in colorectal and upper gastrointestinal surgical patients in WA during 2019–2020. No differences were observed for BMI, current smoking, alcohol, or drug use. No change was observed in Periods among those reporting physical function and frailty. However, a higher proportion of patients reported past smoking in mid‐to‐late 2020, and more patients were reportedly at nutritional risk in late 2020. These findings remained after adjusting for age, sex, and surgery group. To our knowledge, this is the first Australian COVID‐era study investigating lifestyle risk factors in a surgical cohort.

Nearly 70% of patients were overweight or obese, consistent with WA and national prevalence data [38, 39]. A Queensland inpatient study reported similar obesity rates [40], highlighting the need for systematic inpatient monitoring. Obesity increases perioperative risks, complicates management, and contributes to higher costs [41].

Eighteen percent of patients in this study cohort were current smokers, higher than community rates [42, 43]. Multiple international and Australian pandemic impact studies [13, 17, 44] report increase in smoking associated with existing smokers, lower education, younger age and psychosocial stressors. The observed 38%–49% percent rise represents a change in the prevalence of recent quitting in past‑smoking history among surgical patients, and suggests a meaningful alteration in individual's risk profiles within a relatively short period. Greater past smoking in 2020 likely reflects the increased awareness of respiratory risks with COVID‐19 and likely effectiveness of public health messaging [13, 17, 44, 45, 46, 47]. In surgical settings, where clinical encounters are often “teachable moments,” reinforcing smoking cessation may be particularly impactful [25].

The prevalence of alcohol use (53%), and misuse rates (58% of those who responded to screening) were concerning but unchanged across periods. COVID‐related international and Australian surveys show mixed patterns, with stress‐related increases offset by reductions in other groups [48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54]. WA surveillance similarly reported variability [48]. Long‐term misuse is linked to poorer surgical outcomes, underlining the importance of consistent screening [55, 56, 57].

Drug use (7%) matched WA community estimates [42], and stable across time. Population surveys noted reduced illicit use but increased cannabis during early 2020 [13]. Although not highly prevalent, non‐prescribed substance (and alcohol) use remains relevant for perioperative planning, particularly in the elderly and in regards to managing post‐operative cognitive states [58].

Nutritional risk (18%) was similar to international surgical cohorts [59], but prevalence increased, rising from 14% in 2019 to 27% in late 2020, during comparable periods, representing a near doubling of patients meeting at‑risk criteria as theatre recovery and surgical activity returned to pre‑pandemic scheduling. Hospital‐acquired malnutrition affects up to 20%–50% of hospitalised patients [60, 61]. Surgical patients are particularly vulnerable to poor nutritional status due to a combination of clinical and social factors [60, 61, 62]. Preoperative fasting, a routine part of surgical preparation, can contribute to nutrient depletion, especially when prolonged unnecessarily. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, this vulnerability was further exacerbated by food insecurity, including disruptions to food supply, reduced access to nutritious meals and financial hardship [10]. Rescreening protocols for hospital‐acquired malnutrition are conducted during hospitalisation to monitor patients at risk, particularly older adults [61, 62]. Preoperative prehabilitation support the early identification of nutritional decline and timely intervention [61, 62].

Nearly half of the cohort lacked documented MST assessments, highlighting systemic gaps in the nutritional screening pathway. Notably, patients who had completed MST screening were older and more likely to have undergone UGI surgery, suggesting that screening may have been selectively applied based on perceived clinical risk rather than consistently integrated into the care pathway. Strengthening nutrition screening and intervention pathways could mitigate adverse outcomes.

Physical function and frailty data, available for the 2020 cohort only, indicated that approximately one‐third of patients were at risk. Although differences across calendar periods were not statistically observed, the findings remain clinically relevant due to the established association between poor functional capacity or frailty and adverse postoperative outcomes [63, 64, 65]. Frailty patterns may also reflect case‐mix changes during the restricted period, when Category 3 (non‐urgent) patients were under‐represented and Category 1–2 procedures predominated. Category 3 patients are typically younger and less frail; hence their reduced inclusion likely contributed to a higher frailty profile in the 2020 cohort. This acuity‐driven shift, combined with incomplete PHQ administration, suggests that frailty estimates should be interpreted with caution. Selection bias is also likely, as patients who completed the assessments were not representative of the broader cohort; they tended to be older and more frequently from the UGI surgery group, which may reflect clinical prioritisation due to the greater complexity of UGI procedures involving access to the oesophagus or stomach near the diaphragm. This is supported by Supplementary Table S3, which demonstrates that patients completing PHQ‐based assessments were older and disproportionately from the UGI pathway.

The study results should be considered with respect to its limitations. While the results provide valuable insights, they may not fully represent all surgical patients across Western Australia, as the study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital. However, tertiary hospitals often manage complex cases and diverse patient populations, which can offer meaningful indicators of broader trends. Data using self‐report is known to introduce recall and social desirability bias, particularly for alcohol use. As with similar studies using self‐report tools, this may lead to underreporting, and therefore the results could underestimate the true prevalence [66]. PHQ administration errors limited frailty and functional capacity comparisons, however provided better understanding of the cohort completing the assessments. Observational design precludes causal inference. However, the results offer valuable insights into associations and patterns that can inform future research, clinical practice, and policy development.

Clinical and Policy Implications

The findings highlight the value of consistent lifestyle risk screening in perioperative pathways. Opportunities include:

Leveraging preadmission consultations as “teachable moments” for cessation and behaviour change [25].

Leveraging preadmission consultations as “teachable moments” for cessation and behaviour change [25].

Implementing robust nutrition screening and targeted dietetic support or telehealth counselling to address malnutrition risk.

Implementing robust nutrition screening and targeted dietetic support or telehealth counselling to address malnutrition risk.

Embedding validated tools (AUDIT‐C, MST, DASI, EFS) within electronic systems to reduce missing data and administration errors.

Embedding validated tools (AUDIT‐C, MST, DASI, EFS) within electronic systems to reduce missing data and administration errors.

Planning scalable tele‐prehabilitation services to maintain continuity during future healthcare disruptions.

Planning scalable tele‐prehabilitation services to maintain continuity during future healthcare disruptions.

This study highlights several areas for future investigation. First, linking lifestyle factors such as BMI, substance use, and malnutrition with surgical outcomes (complications, length of stay, readmissions) would provide stronger evidence for targeted interventions and allocation of resourcing to specifically explore and deploy strategies to reduce nutritional risk. Extending digital PHQ data collection across subsequent years would allow real‐time monitoring of trends and alignment with national patterns.

Second, health economic evaluations could assess the fiscal impact of prehabilitation and lifestyle risk modification, particularly in reducing length of stay and hospital‐acquired complications. Comparative analyses across urgency categories may clarify how delays affect outcomes and resource use.

Third, integrating sociodemographic data could identify disparities in access to preoperative optimisation, informing equity‐focused service delivery.

Finally, qualitative studies exploring patient motivations, barriers, and responses to prehabilitation would complement quantitative analyses and guide implementation of supportive patient‐centred pathways.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into preoperative health behaviours during the COVID‐19 pandemic in WA. While BMI, alcohol, and drug use remained stable, higher rates of past smoking and increased nutritional risk were evident in 2020. These shifts highlight both vulnerabilities and opportunities for intervention.

Embedding robust, standardised screening tools and adopting digital platforms can improve data quality and continuity of care. Innovative strategies, such as remote dietary counselling, targeted cessation messaging, and scalable tele‐prehabilitation, should be considered to enhance perioperative pathways. Findings from this study can inform resource allocation, clinical planning, and policy development to strengthen surgical care resilience in future healthcare disruptions.
