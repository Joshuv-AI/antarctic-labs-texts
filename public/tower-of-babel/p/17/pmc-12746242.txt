Early prediction and warning of MODS following major trauma via identification of cytokine storm: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Panpan Chang, Rui Li, Jiahe Wen, Guanjun Liu, Feifei Jin, Yongpei Yu, Yongzheng Li, Guang Zhang, Tianbing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeEarly mortality in major trauma has decreased, but MODS remains a leading cause of poor outcomes, driven by trauma-induced cytokine storms that exacerbate injuries and organ damage.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 79 major trauma patients (ISS >15) treated in the National Center for Trauma Medicine, Peking University People's Hospital, from September 1, 2021, to July 31, 2023. Patients (1) with ISS >15 (according to AIS 2015), (2) aged 15–80 years, (3) admitted within 6 h of injury, (4) having no prior treatment before admission, were included. Exclusion criteria were (1) GCS score <9 or AIS score ≥3 for TBI, (2) confirmed infection, infectious disease, or high infection risk, (3) pregnancy, (4) severe primary diseases affecting survival, (5) recent use of immunosuppressive or cytotoxic drugs within the past 6 months, (6) psychiatric patients, (7) participation in other clinical trials within the past 30 days, (8) patients with incomplete data or missing blood samples. Admission serum inflammatory cytokines and pathophysiological data were analyzed to develop machine learning models predicting MODS within 7 days. LR, DR, RF, SVM, NB, and XGBoost were evaluated based on the area under the AUROC. The SHAP method was used to interpret results.ResultsThis study enrolled 79 patients with major trauma, and the median (Q1, Q3) age was 51 (35, 59) years (52 males, 65.8%). The inflammatory cytokine data were collected for all participants. Among these patients, 35 (44.3%) developed MODS, and 44 (55.7%) did not. Additionally, 2 patients (2.5%) from the MODS group succumbed. The logistic regression model showed strong performance in predicting MODS. Ten key cytokines, IL-18, Eotaxin, MCP-4, IP-10, CXCL12, MIP-3α, MCP-1, IL-1RA, Cystatin C, and MRP8/14 were identified as critical to the trauma-induced cytokine storm and MODS development. Early elevation of these cytokines achieved high predictive accuracy, with an AUROC of 0.887 (95% CI 0.813–0.976).ConclusionTrauma-induced cytokine storms are strongly associated with MODS. Early identification of inflammatory cytokine changes enables better prediction and timely interventions to improve outcomes.

Introduction

Major trauma remains the leading cause of death among individuals under 45 years of age, often associated with severe tissue damage and massive hemorrhage.1 Advances in medical technology, including damage control resuscitation and surgery, have significantly enhanced treatment options, boosting survival rates in cases that were previously considered fatal in the early stages of trauma care. However, despite timely intervention, some patients still have a high risk of mortality. Research indicates that the early occurrence of a severe systemic inflammatory response can aggravate initial injuries and lead to MODS, which is a crucial factor in poor outcomes and unfavorable prognoses.2,3

The early inflammatory response to severe trauma is driven by sterile inflammation initiated by DAMPs released from injured cells. These include DNA, RNA, heat shock proteins, and extracellular matrix fragments.4,5 Unlike PAMPs, which are infection-derived, DAMPs activate the innate immune system in sterile conditions. In some cases, excessive activation can result in a cytokine storm.2 This cascade increases vascular permeability, reduces blood pressure, and impairs coagulation, leading to the onset of MODS.6, 7, 8 Simultaneously, the immune system may be suppressed to increase the risk of infections and sepsis.9 Evidence indicates that an intense inflammatory response is associated with poorer outcomes and higher mortality rates.6,10, 11, 12 This immune dysregulation not only worsens patient survival but also prolongs ICU and hospital stays, significantly increasing healthcare costs.13

Despite the well-known risks of cytokine storm and MODS, accurately predicting their onset, timing, and severity remains a clinical challenge. Emerging research is gradually illuminating these complex phenomena. Some studies explore immune cell alterations,14 others focus on the relationship between miRNA and prognosis,15 and some investigate specific inflammatory factors or HMGB1 concerning MODS prognosis.16,17 However, the specific cytokine storm following major trauma remains poorly understood.

Few studies have systematically examined the cytokine storm following major trauma or its impact on patient prognosis. Moreover, effective early warning strategies remain absent in clinical practice. Cytokine storms vary across different conditions due to distinct disease mechanisms.18 In major trauma, the inflammatory response is primarily driven by tissue damage and hemorrhage. As a result, traditional markers such as C-reactive protein and IL-6 become less reliable for evaluating infections or general inflammatory activity.19

This study aimed to investigate the cytokine storm triggered by major trauma and develop a model for early identification of patients at high risk for MODS. A total of 90 inflammatory cytokines were screened, and 10 key cytokines were identified as strongly associated with MODS. These cytokines can be detected from the first blood sample on admission, enabling timely and accurate risk prediction. The proposed model enhances understanding of MODS pathogenesis and provides a practical tool for early detection and intervention. Early identification of high-risk patients helps to protect organ function and prevent complications. Timely intervention also shortens ICU and hospital stays, reducing healthcare costs of trauma patients.

Methods

Study design and population

In this prospective observational cohort study, serum inflammatory cytokine data at admission were analyzed to develop machine learning models for predicting MODS development within 7 days. The study population included major trauma patients treated at the National Trauma Medical Center of Peking University People's Hospital between August 2021 and December 2023. Patients (1) with ISS >15 (according to AIS 2015), (2) aged 15–80 years, (3) admitted within 6 h of injury, (4) having no prior treatment before admission (defined as hemostasis, minimal crystalloid fluid resuscitation, and initial immobilization) were finally included. Patients were excluded if they had received treatments that affect cytokine levels, such as blood transfusions, corticosteroids, or immunosuppressive drugs before admission. Exclusion criteria were (1) GCS score <9 or AIS score ≥3 for TBI, (2) confirmed infection, infectious disease, or high infection risk, (3) pregnancy, (4) severe primary diseases affecting survival (e.g. unrespectable tumors, hematological diseases, acute cardiovascular or respiratory diseases, HIV infection), (5) recent use of immunosuppressive or cytotoxic drugs within the past 6 months, (6) psychiatric patients, (7) participation in other clinical trials within the past 30 days, (8) patients with incomplete data or missing blood samples.

The enrolled patients had 2 mL of peripheral blood (first blood) drawn at the initial laboratory test after admission to the hospital, followed by holographic analysis of inflammatory cytokines. Pathophysiological parameters were also collected during hospitalization. This study strictly adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People's Hospital (Ethics Number: 2021PHB406-001). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their families.

Data collection

Comprehensive demographic data, medical history, trauma type, injury mechanism, and pathophysiological data, including vital signs, organ function biomarkers, blood gas, and complete blood counts, among a total of 39 parameters (Supplemental Table 1), were collected for all patients. Patients were followed up daily for the first 10 days, and then followed up once on day 14 and day 28 after trauma. SOFA scores were evaluated at each time point, and MODS was defined as a SOFA score of ≥5 for at least 2 consecutive days after 48 h at admission.6,7 Categorical variables included sex and MODS status. Other variables were standardized using the Z-score method, ensuring a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1.

Fresh blood samples (2 mL) were collected from patients into yellow-top tubes and kept at 4 °C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was carefully collected and stored in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes at −80 °C. For analysis, samples were thawed on ice. Inflammatory cytokine profiling was performed using BioLegend's LEGENDplex™ assay kits (Supplemental Table 2), following the manufacturer's instructions. The kit facilitates multiplex cytokine profiling, enabling the simultaneous quantification of multiple cytokines from small sample volumes. This highly sensitive and reproducible method, compatible with flow cytometry, allows for a comprehensive analysis of inflammatory markers while conserving limited patient samples, an essential consideration in clinical research. Data were acquired using a BD Biosciences LSR II instrument, and all tests were conducted in a standardized laboratory environment to ensure comparability. The included inflammatory cytokine and their measurement results are provided in Supplemental Table 3.

Statistical analysis

Model development

To compare the predictive performance of MODS onset within 7 days at admission, we evaluated the following machine learning models: LR for binary classification, DT for hierarchical classification, RF for enhanced accuracy via ensemble learning, SVM for optimal data separation, NB leveraging feature independence, and XGBoost using gradient boosting to strengthen predictive power. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via grid search to fine-tune each model. To enhance feature selection, RFE was applied to refine the initial set of 90 inflammatory cytokines, identifying the 10 most predictive features through 10-fold cross-validation. This widely utilized technique is particularly effective for assessing machine learning models in studies with limited datasets. By maximizing the use of each sample for both training and testing, this method reduces the risk of overfitting and enhances the model's generalizability. The dataset was partitioned into 90% for training and 10% for testing. The training set was employed for model building, hyperparameter optimization, and classification threshold calibration, while the reserved test set was used exclusively for performance evaluation. Model performance was assessed using metrics such as AUROC, ACC, TPR, TNR, BER, and F1 score. All analyses were based on Scikit-learn 1.4.1 in Python 3.9.18. All results have a 95% CI, and statistical tests were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Model interpretability

To ensure transparency and clinical relevance, SHAP were employed to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model predictions. Dependency plots were generated to visualize the relationships between individual feature values and predicted MODS risk. The use of SHAP values offers critical insights into how specific cytokines and clinical factors influence model predictions, enabling clinicians to better understand high-risk predictions and facilitate targeted early interventions. This interpretability supports trust and promotes adoption in clinical settings by providing an explainable decision-making framework. Result

Patient enrollment and characteristics

The study enrolled 82 major trauma patients, with 3 discharged early and not completing the observation period. Therefore, 79 patients completed the study and were included in the final analysis, with 52 males (65.8%). The median age was 51 (35, 59) years. Complete clinical and inflammatory cytokine data were collected for all participants. Of these, 35 patients (44.3%) developed MODS, while 44 patients (55.7%) did not. Two patients (2.5%) died in the MODS group during the observation period (Table 1). Male patients were predominant, and the incidence of MODS was higher among men. The median ISS for all patients was 21 (17, 27), with higher scores observed in the MODS group (24 (19, 29)) compared to the non-MODS group (18 (17, 24)). Similarly, the SOFA score at admission was significantly higher in the MODS group (5 (4, 8)) compared to the non-MODS group (3 (2, 4)). Traffic accidents (n = 49, 62.0%) and falls (n = 28, 35.4%) were the most common causes of trauma. The chest (n = 71, 89.9%), abdomen (n = 49, 62.0%), and external regions (n = 49, 62%) were the most frequently affected areas, as identified by the AIS. Patients in the MODS group experienced significantly longer ICU stays (median 9 (7, 12) days) compared to those without MODS (7 (6, 11) days) (p = 0.008). The MODS group also had extended total hospital stays (20 (16, 23) days, p = 0.007). Mortality within 28 days was observed exclusively in the MODS group, with 2 deaths recorded. Model performance of inflammatory cytokines for MODS in trauma patients

To evaluate the prognostic utility of the “first tube blood” inflammatory cytokine levels for predicting MODS in trauma patients, we first used all measured cytokines to predict patient outcomes. To determine the optimal model, we tested the 6 models described in the Methods section. Our analysis demonstrated that cytokine levels from the initial admission sample effectively predict MODS onset. The performance of each algorithm is presented in Fig. 2, with a comparative summary in Supplemental Table 4. LR exhibited the highest predictive accuracy, achieving an AUROC of 0.821 (95% CI: 0.714–0.937) (Fig. 3). SVM, RF, and XGBoost models also showed strong predictive ability, with AUROCs of 0.782 (95% CI: 0.718–0.863), 0.803 (95% CI: 0.748–0.870), and 0.763 (95% CI: 0.652–0.897), respectively, while the DT model had markedly lower performance than the others (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Comparison of AUROC for different algorithms, displaying the AUROC values (vertical axis) for models based on different machine learning algorithms (horizontal axis), comparing the predictive performance of inflammatory cytokines and pathophysiological parameters. Previous studies have highlighted that pathophysiological parameters can support the early detection of MODS.20 Consistent with this, we included pathophysiological parameters in our predictive models. However, across all models, the predictive performance was lower than that of the inflammatory cytokine-based models. The LR model achieved the highest AUROC of 0.744 (95% CI 0.631–0.862), which was significantly lower than the performance of the inflammatory cytokine model (Fig. 3).

Key inflammatory cytokines and model enhancement for predicting MODS

To enhance the predictive performance of our model, we identified the inflammatory cytokines most strongly associated with MODS. Given the superior performance of the LR model, we used it for marker selection. The analysis identified 10 key markers: IL-18; Eotaxin; MCP-4; IP-10; CXCL12; MIP-3α; MCP-1; IL-1RA; Cystatin C; MRP8/14. Models built using these 10 cytokines exhibited robust performance across various machine learning algorithms. Among them, the LR model again achieved the highest AUROC, reaching 0.887 (95% CI: 0.813–0.976) (Fig. 3). Performance on the training dataset is shown in Supplemental Fig. 1. This was followed by the SVM model (AUROC 0.856, 95% CI: 0.769–0.963), RF model (AUROC 0.849, 95% CI: 0.756–0.954), and XGBoost model (AUROC 0.842, 95% CI: 0.757–0.941) (Fig. 2), with a comparative summary in Supplemental Table 1. The findings demonstrate the high performance of the LR model in predicting the risk of MODS, emphasizing the relevance of these 10 inflammatory cytokines in early diagnosis and risk stratification. These cytokines are closely associated with systemic inflammation and the cytokine storm, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and subsequent organ damage.

In conclusion, these inflammatory cytokines offer significant clinical utility for early identification of MODS and effective risk assessment. As promising biomarkers, they may guide treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes in trauma settings. Future research should focus on validating their clinical applicability and investigating their specific roles in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying MODS development.

Discussion

The early systemic inflammatory response triggered by major trauma, which leads to secondary injury and MODS, remains a major contributor to poor prognosis and suboptimal treatment outcomes in affected patients.2,6 The underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood, and effective diagnostic or therapeutic strategies remain elusive. This study aims to characterize the early inflammatory cytokine storm in major trauma patients and develop an early warning system. Our findings demonstrate that a combined model incorporating 10 inflammatory cytokines, including IL-18, Eotaxin, MCP-4, IP-10, CXCL12, MIP-3α, MCP-1, IL-1RA, Cystatin C, and MRP8/14, can predict the risk of MODS. Early identification of trauma-induced cytokine storms is pivotal in predicting the onset of MODS, facilitating timely clinical interventions, reducing ICU stays, and ultimately lowering healthcare costs. This strategy significantly enhances patient outcomes. Following major trauma, patients often experience multiple distinct peaks of organ dysfunction at different intervals.7,8 Our predictive model focuses specifically on trauma-related MODS triggered by the initial inflammatory cytokine storm, deliberately excluding cases driven by subsequent immune dysregulation or infection. Furthermore, TBI has been excluded from this study due to its unique inflammatory response within the central nervous system. TBI is characterized by distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, including the activation of neuroinflammatory pathways, the disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and the release of specific cytokines. Additionally, biomarkers such as GFAP, which have already been approved by the Food and Drug Administration in the United States, offer valuable diagnostic tools for assessing inflammation associated with TBI.21, 22, 23

The manifestation of cytokine storms differs among various diseases because of the diversity in pathogenic mechanisms. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were identified as the primary drivers of the cytokine storm.24,25 In other diseases, however, the characteristics of cytokine storms may be distinct.26,27 Consequently, the concept of disease-specific cytokine storm has emerged, prompting the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies. However, trauma-specific cytokine storm remains undefined. Previous studies have highlighted the roles of IL-6 and MRP8/14, respectively, in trauma prognosis.28,29 However, their presence in other inflammatory conditions limits their diagnostic specificity and utility. To address this limitation, our model incorporates multiple inflammatory cytokines central to the trauma-induced cytokine storm. By utilizing omics technology in combination with machine learning, we identified a set of key factors that predict the onset of MODS following major trauma. This approach allowed us to define a trauma-specific cytokine storm, significantly improving diagnostic performance.

Previous studies have explored various approaches to predict the prognosis of trauma patients. Mommsen et al.30 found that IL-18 levels were significantly increased in patients who developed MODS compared to those who did not, thus indicating IL-18's potential as an early biomarker. Our findings align with this, identifying IL-18 as the most critical cytokine in our predictive model, while providing more comprehensive insights. Liu et al.20 proposed using early physiological indicators, such as the GCS and the need for respiratory support, for MODS detection, while our inflammatory cytokine-based model outperformed previous physiological models in predictive power, offering a more targeted insight into the mechanisms underlying MODS.16,31,32 Thair et al.15 proposed using an 11-RNA SepsisMetaScore to indicate early infection and sepsis risk in severely injured patients, recommending early antibiotic administration to prevent sepsis. Vicente's research demonstrated that certain key micro-RNAs, including miR-K12-10b, miR-K12-12, and EmiR-BHRF-1, were detected shortly after trauma and were associated with poor outcomes in severely injured patients.16 However, the dynamic and rapidly evolving pathophysiological environment post-trauma may limit the responsiveness of these micro-RNAs. The intricate regulatory pathway from transcription through translation, protein folding, and subsequent post-translational modifications suggests that initial micro-RNA expression may limit its influence on final effector molecules. Moreover, this biological process seems to take a relatively long time, which is not reliable in the early stage of major trauma. Consequently, simple correlation analysis may not fully explain the specific roles of micro-RNAs in post-trauma regulation. Additionally, several studies have reported early peripheral immune cell changes in trauma patients, such as a reduction in T cells and NK cells, which have been associated with increased mortality and adverse outcomes.14,33 However, relying solely on shifts in cell subpopulations as prognostic markers may overlook the complexity of the immune response following trauma. Our study focuses on cytokines, the final effector molecules directly involved in inflammation and organ dysfunction. The 10 cytokines identified in our model not only show significant predictive value for the early detection and risk stratification of MODS, but also complement existing research. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the post-traumatic immune response and its clinical significance.

The 10 cytokines identified in this study collectively drive a cytokine storm during the inflammatory response, promoting the development of MODS following major trauma. In the early stage of trauma, the release of large amounts of DAMPs triggers innate immune activation MRP8/14,10 acting as a DAMP,34 collaborates with pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-18 and MCP-1, to amplify and maintain a strong innate immune response.35,36 This pathophysiological process includes pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals.3,13 Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-18, MCP-1, MCP-4, IP-10, MIP-3α, and Eotaxin, activate immune cells, promoting chemotaxis and sustaining the immune response.34,37, 38, 39 For example, IL-18, identified as the most influential cytokine in our predictive model (Fig. 4A), drives systemic inflammation by promoting the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α, leading to endothelial dysfunction and organ damage.40 Meanwhile, IL-1RA, as an anti-inflammatory factor, counterbalances inflammation by antagonizing IL-1α and β.41 However, in the early stage of major trauma, excessive pro-inflammatory factors may overwhelm anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Chemokines, including MCP-1, MCP-4, IP-10, Eotaxin, MIP-3α, and CXCL12, recruit monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils, and T cells to damaged tissues.42,43 Overactivation of these processes can lead to persistent inflammation and organ damage. Regarding balancing tissue damage and repair, CXCL12 mediates stem cell mobilization and repair. However, when elevated along with pro-inflammatory factors, it may trigger abnormal repair mechanisms, resulting in fibrosis or irreversible organ damage.44,45 IL-18 activates NK and T cells, promoting a Th1 immune response that exacerbates local and systemic inflammation, increasing the risk of tissue damage.40,46 On the other hand, Eotaxin is associated with the Th2 immune response.47 The alternation or simultaneous occurrence of different immune responses may exacerbate immune dysregulation, potentially leading to MODS.2,48 By analyzing these interrelated factors, further exploration of the immune response alterations following trauma can be pursued.

This study has several limitations. The most significant is the relatively small sample size of 79 patients, coupled with the lack of external validation. Our strict inclusion criteria, which required an ISS >15, presentation within 6 h of injury, and no prior treatment, combined with ethical considerations, inherently limited the pool of eligible participants within the 1-year recruitment period at our national trauma center. While the models demonstrated strong performance in internal validation using 10-fold cross-validation, the lack of external validation raises concerns about their robustness and generalizability. Unfortunately, we were unable to find a suitable publicly available dataset for external validation. Future studies should focus on validating these models in external cohorts to enhance their clinical utility and ensure their applicability beyond the study sample. Despite the limited sample size, small-sample studies are commonly used in medical research, particularly in high-risk populations, where high-quality small datasets often provide more meaningful insights into key biological mechanisms than larger and less rigorous datasets.49, 50, 51 The reliability of small-sample machine learning models can be improved through techniques such as cross-validation and model optimization,52 which were employed in this study. The rarity of such high-quality datasets in this specific patient population underscores the importance of our findings and provides a strong foundation for future research with larger cohorts. To improve the robustness and generalizability of our predictive model, we conducted a sample size estimation, suggesting that approximately 130 patients are required to achieve an AUROC >0.85 with 95% confidence and 80% power. This highlights the need for future multi-center collaborations to increase sample size and further validate our findings, ensuring a stronger evidence base for clinical implementation.

Furthermore, the scope of selected inflammatory cytokines is narrow. Although we made efforts to include relevant biomarkers, some key mediators may have been overlooked, potentially limiting our understanding of the complex biological processes underlying MODS. Additionally, this study focused exclusively on inflammatory cytokines, without considering non-peptide mediators, such as lipid-derived mediators (e.g., prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and resolvins) and regulatory miRNAs, which are known to play significant roles in modulating inflammation and immune function. While these mediators were not included, their interactions with cytokines suggest a complex regulatory network in the pathophysiology of MODS. The focus on cytokines, however, was driven by their clinical feasibility, as they are easily measurable and suitable for rapid, stable monitoring, making them more applicable for clinical translation.

In conclusion, this study analyzes inflammatory cytokine changes in the “first tube blood” at admission, employing machine learning models to reveal a strong association between trauma-induced cytokine storms and MODS development. It offers an initial characterization of the early trauma-specific cytokine storm after major trauma. Early detection of key cytokine elevations enhances MODS prediction, supporting timely interventions that may reduce ICU and hospital stays and associated costs. This model demonstrates valuable clinical utility in improving trauma care.
