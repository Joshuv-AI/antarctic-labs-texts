Supplementary case reports to the current German Cardiac Society (DGK) position paper on electrical cardioversion
Authors: Nawar Alhourani, Felix Wegner, Fatih Güner, Lars Eckardt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrical cardioversion represents a cornerstone of rhythm control strategies in patients with atrial fibrillation. However, in patients with obesity and specific anatomical variations, e.g. post heart transplantation, the standard flow of this procedure may need to be altered. This includes employing technical deviations of shock delivery and paying special attention to potential, unexpected complications. In light of the current position paper of the German Cardiac Society (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Kardiologie, DGK) on electrical cardioversion of atrial fibrillation, two cases with unconventional course of elective electrical cardioversion of atrial fibrillation are presented: dual direct current cardioversion in severe obesity and the occurrence of ventricular fibrillation in the presence of heterotopic heart transplant.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Obesity, Dual simultaneous cardioversion, Heterotopic heart transplant, Ventricular fibrillation

Introduction

Rhythm control using electrical cardioversion is an integral part of national and international guidelines for the management of atrial fibrillation (AF) [1–4]. In addition to alleviating AF-related symptoms, cardioversion also plays an important role in guiding long-term antiarrhythmic therapy [5]. The success rate of electrical cardioversion depends on numerous factors including character and/or pre-treatment of AF, patient-related variables, and technical aspects such as shock energy as well as type and position of electrodes [6–11]. Obesity and anatomical variations, e.g. after heart transplantation, may require deviations from standard protocols for electrical cardioversion. In light of the current position paper of the German Society of Cardiology (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Kardiologie, DGK) [12], the following two cases offer a perspective on such modifications and highlight clinical practice-related considerations.

Cardioversion-resistant atrial fibrillation in obese patients: a therapeutic dead-end?

A 66-year-old male patient was referred to the authors’ hospital with severe exertional dyspnea due to AF recurrence despite long-term flecainde therapy at a daily dose of 3 × 100 mg. Prior to the onset of AF, he had a relatively good exercise tolerance. Neither electrocardiographic nor echocardiographic evaluations revealed findings suggestive for structural heart disease. His medical history was notable for a longstanding morbid obesity with a body mass index (BMI) of 45 kg/m2. In the presence of uninterrupted oral anticoagulation, electrical cardioversion was planned. The utilized cardioverter/defibrillator (Zoll® R‑Series [Zoll R-Series® by ZOLL Medical Corporation, Massachusetts, USA]) automatically determined the amount of shock energy delivered based on real-time thoracic impedance measurements. The initial synchronised shocks were delivered via adhesive electrodes in both antero-lateral and antero-posterior configurations with energies of 270 and 280 J, respectively. Both attempts failed to restore sinus rhythm. Further attempts implementing additional manual compression with paddles, each delivering 270 J, were also unsuccessful in both electrode positions. Based on the results of a recent randomized clinical trial [13], a dual simultaneous shock strategy was subsequently employed. This approach involves the simultaneous delivery of two shocks via two sets of defibrillation pads, thereby increasing the cumulative energy reaching the atria [13]. In our case, a total of 530 J in bilateral antero-posterior vectors (260 and 270 J, respectively) were delivered (Fig. 1). This led to an immediate restoration of a stable sinus rhythm on the first attempt. Following a short period of uneventful monitoring in the observation unit, the patient was discharged without any evidence of skin irritation at the sites of the electrodes or chest discomfort.

Synchronized cardioversion-induced ventricular fibrillation: an uncommon complication

Experimental studies conducted several decades ago clearly established that electrical cardioversion must be delivered in synchronisation with the R‑waves to avoid the iatrogenic induction of ventricular fibrillation [14]. When a shock is given during the vulnerable phase of myocardial repolarization, ventricular fibrillation can occur, corresponding to the classic “R-on-T” phenomenon. However, a synchronized shock may also trigger ventricular fibrillation in the rare situation of heterotopic heart transplant: A 60-year-old male patient was referred for elective electrical cardioversion due to symptomatic, persistent AF. In 1990, he had undergone heterotopic heart transplantation for severe restrictive cardiomyopathy secondary to endocardial fibrosis (Fig. 2). On admission, his 12-lead ECG showed AF with right axis deviation and a right bundle branch block-like morphology. Additionally, permanent parasystole with broad QRS complexes originating from the native heart was recorded. The patient was not on any regular antiarrhythmic medications. Following exclusion of left atrial thrombi via transesophageal echocardiography, the first synchronized shock of 250 J was delivered via an antero-posterior electrode configuration using Zoll® R‑Series cardioverter/defibrillator. Although the patient remained haemodynamically stabile, his ECG revealed loss of the previous parasystole with ongoing ventricular fibrillation in the native heart parallel to an independent regular ventricular rhythm in the graft (Fig. 2). To terminate the ventricular fibrillation of the native heart, a further synchronised shock to the donor heart with 200 J was administrated. Hereafter, a stable sinus rhythm of the donor heart with intermittent parasystole from the native heart was unmasked (Fig. 2). The patient was discharged after an uneventful stay in the observation unit and free from cardiac arrhythmias.

Discussion

According to a recent sub-analysis of the EAST-AFNET 4 trial, early rhythm control therapy retains its effectiveness and safety in patients with obesity [15]. Although electrical cardioversion of AF in obese patients is considered safe, it has been associated with a markedly reduced success rate, necessitating the application of special techniques with often multiple shock deliveries and prolonged procedure times [7]. One of these deviations from conventional electrical cardioversion is the delivery of dual simultaneous synchronised shocks via two sets of electrodes. In a recent randomised trial by Aymond et al. [13], cardioversion was more often successful with dual direct current cardioversion compared with single direct current cardioversion (97 of 99 patients [98%] vs 87 of 101 patients [86%]; p = 0.002) in obese patients with a mean BMI of 41 kg/m2. Patients in the single cardioversion cohort with a failed first attempt underwent successful dual cardioversion without complications (12 of 14 after second cardioversion and two of 14 after third cardioversion). This approach, being the fifth shock delivered in our presented case, also successfully and safely restored sinus rhythm. Therefore, dual simultaneous shock delivery should be considered in obese patients with failed conventional electrical cardioversion. Of note, the administration of dual sequential shocks in the case of defibrillation-refractory ventricular fibrillation may be, in a similar fashion, a second-line life-saving option [16].

Electrical cardioversion may also be challenging in patients with cardiac anatomical variations, such as after heterotopic heart transplantation, as in our second case. Heterotopic heart transplantation is nowadays a rarely performed surgical procedure and was primarily developed to preserve the recipient’s native heart in the case of graft rejection during the early pre-cyclosporine era [17]. Patients with heterotopic heart transplant require special attention during electrical cardioversion. The differentiation between the electrical activity of the native and donor hearts on surface ECG as well as the selection of an appropriate shock vector for synchronisation to R‑waves of the donor heart are crucial in the management of these cases [18]. The electrical activity of the native heart typically presents as independent QRS complexes (parasystole) and is more evident on the left precordial leads [19]. The available data on electrical cardioversion in these patients is restricted to a few retrospective analyses and case reports [18–22]. Notably, depending on the associated haemodynamic tolerance observed in the reported patients, ventricular fibrillation in native hearts was managed inconsistently. For example, in one case report, the ongoing ventricular fibrillation was tolerated by stabile and adequate haemodynamic status without further cardioversion [21]. To the authors’ knowledge and based on the available evidence, this is the first reported case of ventricular fibrillation in the recipient’s native heart induced by electrical cardioversion of the donor heart.

Practical conclusion

A dual simultaneous cardioversion may be considered in case of cardioversion-resistant atrial fibrillation in obese patients.
