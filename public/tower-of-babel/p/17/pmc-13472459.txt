Quality assessment of YouTube™ videos on gingival recession and its management: A cross-sectional analysis of content reliability and educational value
Authors: Hadeel Mazin Akram, Rasha Salah Abood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Gingival recession affects approximately 81% of people worldwide and represents one of the most common oral health issues. Increasingly, more people are using digital platforms to find information about their health, with YouTube represented as a popular source for learning about oral health topics. However, studies have not been conducted on the quality or trustworthiness of gingival recession information that is available on YouTube.Objective:To systematically evaluate the content quality, usefulness, and reliability of YouTube™ videos concerning gingival recession and its management using validated assessment tools.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional analysis was conducted using four search terms: “gingival recession,” “gum recession treatment,” “receding gums,” and “gum graft.” Twenty videos that met the inclusion criteria were independently evaluated by two calibrated dental evaluators using the Global Quality Scale (GQS), audiovisual quality assessment, and a customized usefulness index. Videos were classified by source (expert vs. non-expert) and content level (high vs. low). Statistical analysis examined the relationships between quality metrics and video characteristics.Results:Expert-created videos comprised 85% of the sample, significantly higher than previously reported for other dental topics. The mean GQS score was 3.4 ± 0.9, indicating moderate-to-good overall quality. High-content videos accounted for 70% of the sample and expert videos demonstrated significantly superior quality across all metrics (GQS: 3.5 vs. 2.7, P < 0.05; usefulness index: 8.2 vs. 5.3, P < 0.01). Content analysis revealed strong coverage of treatment procedures (80%) but significant gaps in prevention education (35%) and practical considerations (15%).Conclusions:YouTube videos concerning gum recession had better quality in terms of content than did videos pertaining to other dental subjects. Videos found on YouTube were at a high level in terms of professional input and contained a moderate-to-high amount of educational information. There was little or no information provided in YouTube videos related to prevention as well as full patient instruction. Therefore, it appears that while YouTube can be a viable source for providing patients with educational materials, there are some subject matter areas that need greater emphasis by professionals.

INTRODUCTION

Gingival recession, defined as the apical migration of the gingival margin that exposes the root surface beyond the cemento-enamel junction, is one of the most prevalent oral health conditions worldwide.[1] Recently published systematic reviews report that gingival recession is found in 81.1% of the world’s population when the threshold is set at ≥1 mm, 48.4% when it is set at ≥3 mm, and 16.2% when it is set at ≥5 mm.[2] In addition to appearance, this condition can result in various functional problems, such as dentine hypersensitivity and susceptibility to root caries and may cause tooth loss if untreated.[3]

Both modifiable and nonmodifiable factors contribute to the etiology of gingival recession. Male gender (odds ratio [OR] =1.52), smoking (OR = 1.84), alcohol consumption (OR = 2.04), dental plaque accumulation (OR = 4.26), high frenum attachment (OR = 4.58), occlusal trauma (OR = 3.20), and preexisting periodontitis (OR = 9.90) are all significantly associated in multiple systematic meta-analyses.[2] The risk factor profile is the key to both prevention and treatment planning and patient education remains the cornerstone of good clinical management. Treatment options for gingival recession have markedly changed from nonsurgical methods directed at risk factor modification to advanced surgical procedures utilizing techniques such as connective tissue grafts, free gingival grafts, and pinhole surgical technique.[34] The success of these interventions depends on patient understanding and adherence, as well as realistic expectations, which highlights the importance of thorough patient education for achieving the best results.

Patients have shifted to using online sources (including YouTube) for seeking information about their oral health. As a result, there are now many different sources of medical and dental education available via YouTube. There are currently over two billion people who visit YouTube each day, and an average of 1 billion hours of video are viewed. Therefore, the way patients search for and use health information in today’s digitally literate population is significantly affecting dentistry.[5] Patients will arrive at appointments with preconceived ideas and expectations based on what they have read or seen online.

The quality and reliability of health information in the age of social media are now considered primary concerns for healthcare professionals. Previous studies examining YouTube™ content related to various dental topics have found substantial variation in quality, with many videos not providing a full range of information and some being completely inaccurate.[67] Arslan et al. showed that the overall quality of YouTube™ videos on naso-alveolar molding was low, with an average Global Quality Scale (GQS) score of 2.3 ± 0.8, and only 58.3% of videos originated from expert sources.[8] Studies of oral hygiene practices found that 17.6% of the videos achieved an excellent quality score, even though 63% were uploaded by oral health professionals.[9] Standardized methodologies are necessary for identifying the quality of online health information in a reproducible and meaningful way. The GQS has become a well-validated instrument to appraise the educational quality of health-related videos. It offers a systematic opportunity to grade content flow, comprehensiveness, and patient usefulness.[1011] Complementary assessment tools, including the DISCERN instrument and customized usefulness indices, have been employed to provide the comprehensive quality evaluations across the multiple dimensions.[1213]

Although gingival recession is common and the use of digital health information has increased, no systematic review of the quality of YouTube™ videos on gingival recession has been published to date. The lack of information regarding gingival recession is particularly problematic given how complex it is to manage, and because misinformation may guide both patient selection and treatment options. Therefore, understanding which digital educational resources are currently available to educate patients about gingival recession is critical to supporting the development of counseling strategies and effective patient instruction of individualization.

The primary goal of this research project was to evaluate the reliability, value, and quality of educational material available in YouTube videos on gingival recession and its treatments, as measured using standardized evaluation instruments. The secondary objectives included comparing the source and quality of video content with viewer interaction/engagement data to identify the potential gaps in relevant subject matter that could benefit from input from dental professionals. This study will provide evidence-based information on the current state of digital patient education resources for patients experiencing gingival recession and contribute to the ongoing development of methodologies to improve the overall quality of health-related online content in periodontology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This observational, cross-sectional study examines the quality and reliability of YouTube videos on gingival recession and its treatment. The study design, as per prior dental YouTube (tm) protocols,[8914] was developed based upon the established methods for social media content analyses in health care. As the study evaluated only publicly available content (i.e. no human subjects), ethical approval was not required. Nonetheless, the methodology employed to analyze the digital material is consistent with the ethics of good research integrity and digital scholarship.

A full extraction was conducted via the default YouTube search algorithm in May 2025. Four key search terms were developed from the common terminology used by both patient and professional users searching for information regarding gingival recession: gingival recession, gum recession treatment, receding gums, and gum graft. These four search terms were used to ensure an abundance of relevant content and to identify the varying vocabulary used by different types of users. The relevance-based search results provided by YouTube were also utilized to maintain relevance to the typical user. Upon conducting searches with all four search terms, the first 50 video results from each search were analyzed to form a comprehensive sampling pool. Prior research validating this method of sampling has demonstrated that this method is sufficient for providing a representation of the available content, yet still practical enough to analyze fully.[1516]

Videos included in this study had to meet several inclusion criteria. (1) All video content needed to be in English, (2) All video content needs to have one of the five listed below as it’s primary focus, (3) All videos need to have an educational or informative intent, (4) All video content is related to humans and dentistry, (5) All video content needs to last at least 30 s so there will be enough information to evaluate, and (6) All video content must be available for the general public to view without restriction.

The exclusion criteria consisted of the following: (1) any video not in English, (2) any videos that are strictly promotional and do not provide any educational benefit, (3) any video content unrelated to gingival recession, (4) any video that relates to animal or veterinary dentistry, (5) any videos that appear multiple times through different searches, (6) any video with substandard audio and/or visual quality that would prevent adequate evaluation, (7) Live Streams and/or videos with technical problems, and (8) content primarily used to promote a product/service that has little to no educational benefit.

For each included video, comprehensive metadata were systematically recorded using a standardized data collection form. Video characteristics included title, URL, upload date, duration (minutes and seconds), number of views, likes, dislikes (when available), comments, and subscriber count of the uploading channel. Additional parameters recorded included video format (standard, short-form, and live), presence of captions or subtitles, and any disclaimers or professional credentials mentioned.

Calculated engagement metrics included (1) Viewing rate (%) = (Number of views/Days since upload) ×100; (2) Interaction Index (%) = (Number of likes/Number of views) ×100; and (3) Comment Engagement Rate = (Number of comments/Number of views) ×100. These metrics have been validated in previous social media health content studies and provide standardized measures of audience engagement and content reach.[1718]

Videos were systemically categorized as high or low in terms of their credibility of the source based on previously used categories for dentist YouTube (tm) reviews.[89] Expert sources would include all of the following: (1) A licensed dentist or periodontist that had been vetted; (2) Dental clinic(s), hospital(s), university(s), or other academic institution; (3) Professional dental organization(s); (4) Accredited dental school(s) and/or education center(s); and (5) Medical/dental education channel(s) that are overseen by professionals.

Nonexpert sources comprised: would include but is not limited to: (1) Patient (s) reporting experience (s); (2) Content creator(s) who do not have formal training as a dentist; (3) Commercial entity(ies) that do not have an independent review process for its content; (4) Media outlet(s) and journalist(s); and (5) Unverified individual(s) stating they are a dentist without proof of formal education/training as a dentist. Source classification and video quality assessment were performed independently by two calibrated dental evaluators using a standardized scoring form. The evaluators scored the videos separately during the initial assessment to reduce the observer bias. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus; when required, a third reviewer was consulted.

The GQS was used as an assessment tool. The GQS is a valid 5-point Likert scale.[1011] It is designed to assess how well the video quality and educationally helpfulness are in terms of the patient’s needs. The GQS will evaluate the content related to flow, patient usefulness, and the quality of the information presented. In order to score the videos using this method, the scoring will be as follows:

The GQS has demonstrated good inter-rater reliability with κ = 0.83 and construct validity in previous YouTube™ health content studies, making it an appropriate tool for this analysis.[1920] Each video was independently scored by two trained periodontists with inter-rater agreement assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Technical quality was evaluated using a three-point scale assessing audio clarity, visual quality, and overall production values.[921]

This assessment tool was validated in multiple YouTube™ health content studies and it provided a standardized approach to evaluating technical factors that may influence the content accessibility and user engagement.[722] A customized usefulness index was developed specifically for gingival recession content adapting methodologies from previous YouTube™ studies[8] and the content index included eight key items considered important for patient education about gingival recession. Each item was scored from 0 to 2, giving a maximum total score of 16 points.

The assessed items were as follows: Definition and causes of gingival recession; risk factors such as trauma, periodontitis, toothbrushing habits, genetic predisposition, and anatomical factors; available treatment options, including nonsurgical and surgical approaches; gingival grafting procedures and graft types; the role of oral hygiene in prevention, maintenance, and posttreatment care; possible complications, limitations, or recurrence; cost expectations and treatment accessibility; and practitioner qualifications or the need for specialist referral.

For each item, a score of 0 indicated that the topic was not mentioned or was inadequately covered, 1 indicated brief or partial coverage, and 2 indicated adequate and detailed discussion. Based on the total score, videos scoring 6 points or more were classified as having “high content,” while videos scoring below 6 points were classified as having “low content,” in line with thresholds used in previous dental YouTube™ studies.[823]

The data analysis was performed using Python statistical software. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all video characteristics and quality assessment scores. The continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons were made between expert and nonexpert videos, as well as between high- and low-content videos. Independent t-tests were used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for nonnormally distributed variables and categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Pearson and/or Spearman’s correlations (depending on data distribution) were calculated to examine the associations between video quality ratings, engagement metrics, and video properties. Multiple linear regressions were performed to determine which aspects are related to video quality ratings. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined as follows. For those analyses where multiple comparisons were made (and thus a need for correction), the Bonferroni method was used. To increase confidence in the results from our assessment, both raters had a background in dentistry; each rater provided independent assessments of all the videos. The raters also attended training sessions before conducting their evaluations using pilot videos to ensure that they would be evaluating against consistent criteria. To assess inter-rater agreement, Cohen’s Kappa (for categorical variables) and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC, for continuous variables) were computed. It was determined that good agreement existed if either κ ≥0.70 or ICC ≥ 0.75.

When there is disagreement, the reviewers discuss the scores until an agreement is reached, and when needed, a third reviewer was consulted. Additional standardization sessions were held during data collection to maintain consistency in the assessment process.

RESULTS

A total of 200 videos appeared in the original search results across the four search terms. After eliminating duplicate videos and based on inclusion/exclusion criteria, there remained 47 videos. From that group, 20 videos met the eligibility criteria for the detailed evaluation required by the predetermined inclusion/exclusion criteria. The video quality score was determined prior to inclusion and therefore did not influence video selection. The sample of the selected videos contained a variety of different types of content, such as educational presentation, clinical demonstration, patient testimonial, and professional discussion. The selected videos had varying degrees of reach/engagement, with view counts ranging from 10,247 to 4,300,000 (median: 185,000). The upload dates ranged from January 2008 to March 2024. Forty percent of all selected videos were published in the last 12 months. This reflects a mix of historic and current content. Videos were also highly variable in length, ranging from 0.5 to 45.2 min (mean ± SD: 8.5 ± 12.3 min). Shorter videos usually focused on one or two specific procedures, whereas longer videos provided a comprehensive educational experience [Table 1].

Expert sources were the most prevalent type in this study, comprising 85% of the total (n = 20). The sources were comprised of dentists/periodontists (n = 12; 60%), dental clinics/hospitals (n = 3; 15%), and medical education websites/videos (n = 2; 10%). The nonexpert sources consisted mostly of patient-experience videos (n = 2) and other content creators (n = 1). Content classification indicated that 14 of the videos (70%) had a high-level classification based on a usefulness index >6.

The majority of the video content-types fell into one of two categories: educational/informational content-type (n = 12; 60%), demonstration of treatments (n = 5; 25%), patient-experiences (n = 2; 10%), and promotion-related content (n = 1; 5%), as shown in Table 2. The average GQS-score for all the videos within the sample was 3.4 ± 0.9, which indicated an average-quality range from good to moderately good. The GQS-scores obtained in this study represent a significant increase compared to those reported by Arslan et al. (2013) regarding their analysis of nasoalveolar molding videos found on YouTube™, which reported an average GQS-score of 2.3 ± 0.8. The GQS-distribution for the sample videos was categorized as follows: excellent quality - n = 2; 10%, good quality - n = 8; 40%, moderate quality - n = 7; 35%, and poor quality - n = 3; 15%.

The high-quality production of expert videos was confirmed by their statistically significant superiority in terms of GQS scores as compared with nonexpert videos (P = 0.032; 3.5 ± 0.8 vs. 2.7 ± 1.2) which are associated with greater educationally valuable content for students. Videos that were classified as “high-content” yielded a substantial difference in terms of GQS scores when compared to those classified as “low-content” (P < 0.001; 3.8 ± 0.7 vs. 2.5 ± 0.8) validating an association between overall quality assessments and the extent of content coverage.

The results from the technical quality evaluation indicated good technical production quality for most of the videos evaluated, with an average Audio/Visual Quality Score of 2.7 ± 0.6. The distribution of the scores on this scale included: 14 (70%) videos that achieved good technical quality (Score = 3); 5 (25%) videos that achieved a moderate level of technical quality (Score = 2); 1 (5%) video that exhibited very poor technical quality (Score = 1). Expert-produced videos had significantly higher A/V Quality scores than non-expert-produced videos (2.8 ± 0.5 vs. 2.3 ± 0.6, P = 0.041), indicative of the professional resources typically available to dental practitioners and institutions [Table 3].

The average usefulness index score for the videos was 7.8 ± 3.2. Scores ranged from zero to sixteen. Videos produced by experts had an average usefulness index score greater than that of those made by nonexperts; specifically, 8.2 ± 3.0 versus 5.3 ± 2.1 (P = 0.009). Of all the videos included in this study, 70% contained a high amount of useful information (defined as ≥6 points), with usefulness index scores ranging from 6 to 14 points (average = 9.8 ± 2.4). Seventy percent of low-usefulness videos scored two through five points (average = 3.7 ± 1.2).

Videos also varied in their content coverage of specific topics within the educational domain of periodontal disease. Definitions and causes of gingival recession, treatment options, and gingival grafting procedures were among the most described topics (18 videos, 90%; 16 videos, 80%; 15 videos, and 75%, respectively). Practitioner credentials were referenced in 70% of the videos (n = 14), indicating good representation of practitioner professionals.

Significant content deficiencies were noted in many important topic areas. Only forty percent (n = 8) of the videos provided adequate discussion of risk factors. Given that gingival recession has multiple etiologic factors, it is surprising that so few videos provide adequate information regarding these topics. Similarly, only 35% (n = 7) of the videos adequately discussed the role of oral hygiene in prevention and maintenance. This indicates inadequate attention to preventative measures. The most alarming deficiencies were the limited descriptions of potential complications or recurrences (n = 4, 20%) and cost expectations (n = 3, 15%). These are both important considerations for patients when making decisions about dental treatments [Table 4].

Engagement data indicated a strong relationship between content quality and audience reaction. The videos with the highest quality content scores also had the highest interaction values (r = 0.412, P = 0.038), suggesting that higher-quality content is received more positively by the audience. In addition, there was a strong correlation between the usefulness score of the videos and the level of audience engagement with those comments (r = 0.385, P = 0.048), which indicates that when the video has enough information for the viewer to understand what they are seeing and make use of it, they will be more likely to engage in a conversation about the topic.

Videos with a lot of content were shown to have higher engagement on all measures than videos with very little content. High-content videos had significantly higher mean interaction indices (.42 ± 0.21%) than did low-content videos (2.8% ±1.8%, P = .041). High-content videos also had higher mean comment engagement rates (0.09% ± 0.13%) than did low-content videos (0.05% ± 0.08%, P = 0.032). This would indicate that viewers can determine whether a video’s content is high or low quality and respond appropriately in their engagement behavior [Table 5].

An analysis of quality over time showed an upward trend. The average GQS scores for videos from the last 2 years (n = 8) were higher than those of older videos (3.6 ± 0.7 vs. 3.1 ± 1.0, P = 0.048), indicating a progression toward higher-quality educational content. In addition, the mean usefulness index scores for these newer videos were higher (8.9 ± 2.8 vs. 6.8 ± 3.4, P = 0.041) and the mean AVQ scores were higher as well (2.9 ± 0.4 vs. 2.5 ± 0.7, P = 0.035).

The increase in quality may be due to growing recognition among dentists of the need for and benefits of creating and using high-quality digital educational materials, as well as technological advances that enable easier creation of such materials.

Inter-rater reliability demonstrated excellent agreement between the two independent evaluators across all quality metrics. Cohen’s kappa coefficients were 0.89 for source classification, 0.85 for content classification, and 0.82 for GQS scoring. Intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.91 for usefulness index scores and 0.88 for audio-visual quality ratings, confirming the reproducibility of the assessment process.

DISCUSSION

The results of this study provide an overview of the first systematic assessment of the quality of video content found on YouTube™ related to gingival recession and its treatment. In addition, the results highlight how much better the quality of these video presentations are as compared to previous studies related to dental content (i.e., gingival recession vs. nasaolveolar molding), demonstrating an improved mean GQS rating of 3.4 ± 0.9 as opposed to a rating of 2.3 ± 0.8 from the nasoalveolar molding content.[8] This quality enhancement appears to be driven by strong professional engagement, with 85% of videos created by expert sources compared to 58.3% in previous dental YouTube™ studies.

Many experts have created content about gingival recession, a common gum problem affecting 81.1% of people worldwide.[3] The use of Digital Platforms by dentists to educate patients about this problem is important because understanding the cause and treatment of Gingival Recession requires comprehensive education and accurate information. The experts created educational video(s) for patient viewing that were of much higher quality than those created by nonexperts. Specifically, they ranked significantly higher with respect to overall quality (3.5 vs. 2.7, P = 0.032), Audio Visual Quality (AVQ) (2.8 vs. 2.3, P = 0.041), and Coverage of Information (Usefulness Index: 8.2 vs. 5.3, P = 0.009). This study’s findings were consistent with prior studies, which have found that expert-produced health-related content viewed via social media is generally more credible and informative.[2425]

A useful index assessment of current YouTube video content regarding gingival recession has shown that there are some strong points as well as many deficits for educational purposes. According to this study, 90% of the videos contained adequate information on what gingival recession is and why it occurs; similarly, 80% contained sufficient information on how to treat it. However, in critical educational areas, a large deficit was identified. Specifically, only 40% of videos included adequate discussion of risk factors for developing gingival recession; however, numerous studies have demonstrated that there are at least three known modifiable risk factors for developing gingival recession: smoking (OR = 1.84), excessive drinking (OR = 2.04), and dental plaque accumulation (OR = 4.26).[2] This represents a missed opportunity for preventive education, particularly given that risk factor modification represents the foundation of nonsurgical gingival recession management.[2627]

The lack of information about oral health in relation to gum recession (only 35%) is a problem, as evidence shows that proper oral care helps prevent and maintain surgical outcomes. Therefore, many may not realize that gum recession is preventable and the importance of continued care. The lack of education on potential issues related to gum recession (only 20%) and cost associated with treatment (only 15%) are additional concerns; patient awareness of these issues aid in making educated decisions and setting realistic expectations, given that complete root coverage is not always an achievable goal regardless of surgical outcome. Furthermore, there are no clear indications of the cost of treatment for gum recession due to variability in insurance coverage.

The findings of this study contrast favorably with previous evaluations of dental content on YouTube™. Compared to the Arslan et al. study on nasoalveolar molding,[8] gingival recession videos showed superior quality: higher expert participation (85% vs. 58.3%), better overall quality scores (GQS 3.4 vs. 2.3), and more high-content videos (70% vs. 29.2%). This improvement may reflect the broader clinical relevance of gingival recession, attracting greater professional attention and increased investment in production. Similarly, compared to studies on oral hygiene practices, which found only 17.6% of videos achieving excellent quality despite 63% professional authorship,[9] gingival recession content showed better quality distribution, with 50% of videos achieving good to excellent ratings. This suggests that the specificity and clinical focus of gingival recession content may facilitate higher-quality educational presentations compared to broader oral health topics.

The study found that people interacted more with gingival recession content, with an average engagement rate of 3.8%, compared to the usual 1%–2% for health content.[2829] This suggests that this topic interests audiences. There is a positive link between content quality and engagement (r = 0.412, P = 0.038). This means that people can distinguish between good and bad content and respond accordingly. It shows that creating high-quality educational content is valuable.

The findings have significant implications for digital health literacy in periodontics. The predominance of high-quality, expert-created content suggests that YouTube™ can serve as a valuable supplementary resource for patient education when used appropriately. However, the identified content gaps highlight the need for more comprehensive educational approaches that address prevention, risk factors, and practical considerations alongside treatment procedures.

The current content focuses mainly on treatment details. While this information is correct, it might not help patients fully understand or make informed choices. Recent studies on digital health literacy highlight the need for balanced information. This includes both the pros and cons of treatments, costs, and other options.[3031] Right now, content seems to focus more on procedures than on overall patient education. This might make it less effective in fully preparing patients.

The positive correlation between content quality and audience engagement suggests that patients can recognize and prefer high-quality educational content. This finding supports the potential for quality-based content curation and highlights the importance of professional involvement in digital health education initiatives.[3233]

The diversity of content formats observed, including traditional educational videos, YouTube Shorts, and clinical demonstrations, reflects the platform’s evolution and adaptation to different learning preferences and attention spans. The superior quality scores observed in recent videos (3.6 ± 0.7 vs. 3.1 ± 1.0 for older content, P = 0.048) suggest ongoing improvement in content quality, possibly driven by technological advances, increased professional awareness, and platform features that facilitate better educational presentations.

The excellent inter-rater reliability achieved in this study (κ = 0.82–0.89, ICC = 0.88–0.91) validates the assessment methodology and supports the reproducibility of quality evaluation approaches for YouTube™ health content. This methodological validation is important for future research and quality monitoring initiatives in digital health education.

It is necessary to acknowledge several limitations when considering the implications of these findings. With a sample size of 20 video clips, which was sufficient for a qualitative examination on an in-depth level and consistent with prior research examining YouTube™’s health-related content, it is possible that this study does not provide a comprehensive representation of all types of YouTube™ content. In addition, due to the ever-changing nature of YouTube™ content, there will likely be variations in future analyses based on newly created videos being uploaded by users and changes to algorithmic usage.

The language restriction creates a limitation on the ability to generalize results internationally as well as culturally since YouTube is an international platform with varying quality expectations depending upon culture and language. Future studies examining quality of content on YouTube in multiple languages may be able to provide additional insight into the quality of global digital health education resources.

Although quality assessment of online videos has an unavoidable subjective component, this potential bias was reduced by using two independent calibrated evaluators, standardized scoring criteria, and inter-rater reliability testing. However, the use of validated assessment tools (GQS, DISCERN) and the development of a specific usefulness index for gingival recession content help mitigate this limitation while providing clinically relevant evaluation criteria.

The cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of content quality at a specific time point but cannot capture temporal trends or the impact of platform algorithm changes on content visibility and quality. Longitudinal studies would provide valuable insights into content evolution and the effectiveness of quality improvement initiatives.

The results have major ramifications for how professionals can create educational materials for patients using online video resources and how they may develop strategies to educate their patients about dentistry. The high quality of all content created by experts underscores the importance of including healthcare providers in creating digital educational content and suggests that dental practitioners could also consider creating videos posted on YouTube as a viable alternative for educating their patients.

The dentist or other dental healthcare provider is encouraged to review the current media environment as the part of a patient’s preconsultation education so that he/she/they may identify potential misconceptions regarding the information they have reviewed online (including YouTube), provide relevant additional information, present alternative views about treatments or expected outcomes, and assist the patient in establishing an informed decision-making process. This understanding also supports the development of high-quality educational content by dentists, which will likely increase consumer engagement with such content. The development of guidelines for content creation by dental practices and institutions would further help maximize the educational utility of their digital communications.

This study sets the stage for longitudinal studies that will explore how both content (the quality) evolves over time, how algorithms affect the information patients receive and whether exposure to certain types of content leads to an increase in patient knowledge about their own health. Comprehension studies examining the relationship between exposure to YouTube videos (and other forms of digital media) and patients’ comprehension of gingival recession are needed to assess whether current video content is effective at educating patients. Finally, research studies measuring the effects of high-quality YouTube videos on patients’ willingness to accept dental treatment, follow posttreatment instructions, and ultimately achieve positive dental outcomes can be used to demonstrate the benefits of integrating digital patient education platforms into dental offices.

Comparative studies assessing the quality of educational content posted on specific social media sites (Facebook, TikTok, and Instagram) may help identify platform-specific factors that influence both the quality of educational content and its distribution. Furthermore, comparative studies of various types of educational content (short-form vs. long-form, animated videos vs. live-action videos, patient testimonials vs. professional oral health presentations) may help establish a basis for developing and disseminating high-quality educational materials.

CONCLUSIONS

This study suggests that YouTube™ currently provides moderately useful educational content on gingival recession, with many videos produced by specialists and of moderate quality, as indicated by the mean GQS score. Expert-produced videos scored significantly higher than nonexpert videos in quality, content coverage, and production. However, important topics such as prevention, risk factors, and cost information were often insufficiently addressed. These findings suggest that YouTube™ may support chair-side patient education, but clinicians should guide patients toward reliable videos and clarify missing information.
