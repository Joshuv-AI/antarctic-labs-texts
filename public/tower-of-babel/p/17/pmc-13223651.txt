The efficacy of oral sucrose for relieving lumbar puncture pain in neonates: a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Alireza Jashni Motlagh, Sina Mashayekhipour, Mahsa Khoshnam Rad, Niloofar Khoshnam Rad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the analgesic efficacy and physiological effects of oral sucrose during lumbar puncture (LP) in term neonates.Study designThis double-blind, randomised trial enrolled 60 full-term neonates undergoing LP. Participants were randomly given 2 mL of 25% oral sucrose or sterile water 2 min before the procedure. The primary outcome was pain score change from baseline via the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS). Secondary outcomes included changes in heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂). Measurements were taken at baseline, 1 min, and 3 min post-LP.ResultsThe sucrose group showed significantly smaller changes in NIPS scores at 1 min (1.0 vs 5.0, p<0.001) and 3 min (0.0 vs 4.0, p<0.001), along with smaller HR increases (p=0.012) and SpO₂ decreases (p=0.023) at 1 min post-procedure. Clinically significant tachycardia (HR >160 bpm) occurred in 40% of controls vs 13.3% of sucrose-treated infants (p=0.045), and desaturation (SpO₂ <90%) in 26.7% of controls vs 6.7% of the sucrose group (p=0.038).ConclusionA 2 mL dose of 25% oral sucrose administered 2 min prior to LP is a safe and effective intervention that significantly reduces behavioural and physiological markers of pain in term neonates during the immediate post-procedural period.Keywords: Neonatology, Nursing, Pain

Introduction

Pain perception in neonates is a well-documented phenomenon with far-reaching neurodevelopmental consequences. Unlike older infants, neonates lack mature inhibitory pain pathways, rendering them more sensitive to noxious stimuli and less capable of modulating pain responses.1 2 Exposure to unmanaged procedural pain during this critical period has been linked to altered pain sensitivity, cognitive deficits, and emotional dysregulation later in life.3,5

Lumbar puncture (LP) remains a frequently performed and moderately to severely invasive procedure in neonatal medicine, essential for diagnosing sepsis, meningitis, and metabolic disorders.6 Despite its clinical necessity, LP elicits significant pain and physiological stress, triggering tachycardia, oxygen desaturation, and prolonged crying.7 Current guidelines recommend non-pharmacological analgesia for minor procedures, yet evidence-based strategies for moderately painful interventions like LP remain limited.8 Non-pharmacological interventions such as non-nutritive sucking (pacifier use), facilitated tucking, and breastfeeding have shown promise for minor procedures, but evidence for their efficacy during LP remains limited.9

Oral sucrose has emerged as a promising analgesic due to its ability to activate endogenous opioids via sweet-taste receptor signalling.10 While its efficacy for heel sticks11 and venipuncture12 is well-established, results for more invasive procedures have been inconsistent. A 2023 Cochrane review concluded that evidence for the efficacy of sucrose for more painful procedures like lumbar puncture is of low certainty, noting a significant lack of powered studies focused specifically on this intervention. Notably, the most recent Cochrane review on sucrose for procedural pain explicitly identified the need for further high-quality research on lumbar puncture, while another Cochrane review on non-pharmacological management excluded sucrose studies altogether, creating a critical evidence gap that our trial addresses.9 10

Materials and methods

study design and participants

This double-blind, randomised controlled trial was conducted from 1 February 1, 2023 to 30 April 30, 2023 at the tertiary neonatal unit of Kamali Hospital in Karaj, Iran. The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Alborz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.ABZUMS.REC.1401.275) and was prospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCTID: IRCT20220703055360N1; available at: https://en.irct.ir/trial/64590) before the onset of participant recruitment. Written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian for each participant. Full details of the study protocol, including patient identification and consent procedures, are available in online supplemental file 1).

We enrolled 60 term neonates (gestational age ≥37 weeks and postnatal age <28 days) scheduled for a diagnostic LP to rule out sepsis or meningitis. Preterm infants were excluded because they represent a distinct population with different pain processing mechanisms, higher vulnerability to adverse events (eg, apnoea, bradycardia) and potentially different sucrose dosing requirements. Studying this group requires separate safety and efficacy considerations beyond the scope of this initial trial. Additional exclusion criteria included: clinical instability, major congenital anomalies, known metabolic disorders, receipt of analgesic medication within 24 hours of the procedure or oral feeding within 30 min of the procedure.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control group. Randomisation was performed using a computer-generated sequence with block sizes of 2, 4 and 6 to ensure balanced allocation.

To maintain blinding, the 25% sucrose and sterile water solutions were prepared in identical, sequentially numbered, unlabeled syringes by a research pharmacist not involved in patient care or outcome assessment. The participating neonates, their parents, clinical staff and the outcome assessor were all blinded to the treatment allocation throughout the study.

Intervention and procedure

The dose of 2 mL of 25% sucrose (0.5 g sucrose) was selected based on several considerations. A 2026 Cochrane review noted significant variation in sucrose dosing across studies for venipuncture (0.1 mL/kg to 0.5 mL/kg or 0.1 mL to 2 mL), with no established optimal dose for more invasive procedures.13 However, a landmark dose-finding study by Stevens et al. (2018) in 245 neonates undergoing heel lance demonstrated that the minimally effective dose of 24% sucrose was only 0.1 mL, with no additional benefit at higher doses.14 In contrast, evidence from systematic reviews suggests that larger volumes (up to 2 mL) may be required for more invasive procedures.10 13 15 Given that lumbar puncture is more invasive and prolonged than heel lance, we selected a 2 mL dose to ensure adequate analgesic effect while remaining within safe limits (0.5 g sucrose, approximately 2 calories). No adverse events were observed, consistent with the low adverse event rate (2.0%) reported by Stevens et al across all dose groups.14

The LP was performed by a resident or attending physician according to a standardised hospital protocol. This included performing the procedure under a radiant warmer, placing the neonate in the lateral decubitus position with careful flexion, and using aseptic technique. Importantly, no topical or injectable local anaesthetic was used for any participant in this study. Pacifiers were not used during the procedure, as non-nutritive sucking was not standard practice in our unit at the time of the study and its use could have confounded the sucrose effect, given that it has independent analgesic properties.16 A team of two dedicated nurses assisted with positioning and gentle containment for all procedures. LPs were performed by a pool of 5 residents and 2 attending physicians. The number of needle insertion attempts was recorded.

Ethical Justification for Placebo Control

Placebo use for a painful procedure17 was approved by the Alborz University ethics committee.

First, at the time of study design (late 2022), there was no universally accepted standard of care for analgesia during neonatal lumbar puncture in our setting or globally. A 2023 Cochrane review explicitly identified the lack of high-quality evidence for sucrose efficacy during LP as a critical evidence gap, noting that existing studies were underpowered and had methodological limitations.9 Establishing efficacy definitively required comparison against placebo, as active-controlled trials cannot determine whether an intervention is superior to no treatment.

Second, topical anaesthetics—the most common alternative analgesic—are not routinely used for neonatal LP in many institutions worldwide, including our own at the time, due to concerns about delayed onset (60–90 min), potential vasoconstriction affecting procedural success, and inconsistent evidence for efficacy in neonates. Therefore, the “‘standard of care’” against which sucrose would be compared was effectively no analgesia.

Third, we implemented multiple safeguards to minimise infant distress: (1) Aall procedures were performed by experienced clinicians to maximise first-attempt success and minimise procedure duration; (2) Ggentle containment and positioning were provided by dedicated nurses for all infants; (3) Tthe procedure was aborted if infants exhibited signs of severe distress not alleviated by these comfort measures (though this did not occur in any case); (4) Tthe placebo (sterile water) is innocuous and posed no direct risk to infants.

Fourth, the total volume administered (2 mL) and the brief duration of the procedure (typically <5 min) minimised the duration of any potential discomfort. All infants were monitored continuously, and no adverse events occurred in either group.

At the time of study design and conduct, no form of routine analgesia—including topical anaesthetics—was standard practice for neonatal lumbar puncture in our unit or in most Iranian neonatal units. Therefore, the control group received standard care, not a substandard intervention. Nevertheless, we agree that evolving standards of care now support multimodal analgesia, and future trials should compare sucrose against active controls or within multimodal regimens rather than placebo. The study protocol, including the placebo-controlled design, was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Alborz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.ABZUMS.REC.1401.275), which specifically considered the absence of an established standard of care for neonatal LP analgesia at the time.

outcomes and data collection

The primary outcome was the change in pain score from baseline, measured using the NIPS. The NIPS is a validated 7-point scale assessing facial expression, cry, breathing pattern, arm and leg movements, and state of arousal (17).

Secondary outcomes included changes in heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), which were measured using a standard neonatal pulse oximeter.

All outcomes were recorded at three time points:

The NIPS was not assessed during the needle insertion itself due to logistical constraints, as the physician’s position often obscured a clear view of the infant’s face and body, which is essential for a valid NIPS assessment. Therefore, we focused on capturing the immediate post-procedural pain response. A single trained researcher, blinded to group allocation, performed all NIPS assessments from an unobstructed viewpoint to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

A pre-trial sample size calculation was performed. Based on a pilot study, we estimated a large effect size (d=0.8) on the NIPS score. With an α of 0.05 and a β of 0.2 (power of 80%), a sample size of 26 per group was required. We enrolled 30 per group to account for potential attrition.

Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics, Version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The normality of data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. As outcome data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used. The presentation of demographic data (eg, weight, age) as median (IQR) was chosen due to their non-normal distribution. Baseline demographic data and outcome measures are presented as median and IQR. Intergroup comparisons of continuous variables were made using the Mann-Whitney U-test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squareχ² test. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p- values are reported throughout.

The study population consisted of infants admitted to the tertiary neonatal unit for various medical conditions who subsequently required LP for suspected sepsis. The majority (76.7% in control group, 73.3% in intervention group) underwent LP for evaluation of late-onset sepsis (≥72 hours of age), while the remainder were evaluated for early-onset sepsis (<72 hours of age). Common underlying conditions included suspected pneumonia (n=12), hyperbilirubinaemia requiring phototherapy (n=18), maternal chorioamnionitis (n=8) and rule-out meningitis in infants with non-specific symptoms (n=22). None of the infants were critically unstable at the time of procedure, per exclusion criteria.

No adverse events, such as choking or aspiration, were observed in either study group.

Pain and physiological outcomes

Neonates in the sucrose group exhibited significantly attenuated pain responses compared with the control group. The median change in NIPS score from baseline was substantially lower in the sucrose group at both 1 min post-LP (1.0 (IQR: −1.0 to 2.25) vs 5.0 (IQR: 3.0 to 6.0), p<0.001) and 3 min post-LP (0.0 (IQR: −1.0 to 1.25) vs 4.0 (IQR: 2.0 to 5.0), p<0.001).

The physiological responses mirrored the behavioural findings (figure 3). At 1 min post-LP, the sucrose group showed a significantly smaller median increase in HR and a smaller median decrease in SpO₂ compared with controls (p=0.012 and p=0.023, respectively). By 3 min, these parameters began to return toward baseline in both groups. Changes in RR did not differ significantly between the groups at any time point (table 2).

At 1 min post-LP, clinically significant physiological changes were more common in the control group. Tachycardia (HR >160 bpm) occurred in 12/30 (40%) of control infants compared with 4/30 (13.3%) of sucrose-treated infants (p=0.045). Oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ <90%) was observed in 8/30 (26.7%) of controls vs 2/30 (6.7%) of the sucrose group (p=0.038). Tachypnea (RR >60 breaths/min) occurred in 10/30 (33.3%) of controls and 6/30 (20%) of the sucrose group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.38). By 3 min post-procedure, these differences persisted but were attenuated, with 5 controls (16.7%) and 1 sucrose-treated infant (3.3%) still exhibiting desaturation (p=0.19).

Although the difference in median weight between groups did not reach statistical significance (3.2 kg vs 2.9 kg, p=0.086), we conducted exploratory analyses to assess whether weight influenced outcomes. Spearman correlation analysis showed no significant association between weight and ΔNIPS at 1 min (ρ=−0.12, p=0.36) or 3 min (ρ=−0.08, p=0.55) in either group. Similarly, weight did not correlate with changes in HR or SpO₂. These findings suggest that the observed analgesic effect of sucrose was independent of infant weight within the range studied.

Discussion

In this double-blind, randomised controlled trial, we found that a 2 mL dose of 25% oral sucrose administered 2 min prior to LP was a statistically and clinically significant analgesic for term neonates in the post-procedural period. The intervention resulted in meaningful reductions in behavioural pain scores and promoted greater physiological stability, as evidenced by attenuated changes in heart rate and oxygen saturation. Clinically, these effects translated to a threefold reduction in the proportion of infants experiencing tachycardia (40% vs 13.3%) and a fourfold reduction in oxygen desaturation (26.7% vs 6.7%) immediately following the procedure.

Our findings align with and strengthen the existing evidence supporting sucrose for procedural pain relief.10 18 The magnitude of the effect we observed is particularly noteworthy. Our results, demonstrating a significant reduction in both behavioural and physiological pain markers, confirm that sucrose is a potent and vital component of analgesia for lumbar puncture. This is consistent with quality improvement initiatives that have successfully increased its use as a standard of care. For instance, a project in a paediatric emergency department by Balay et al achieved 100% compliance in using at least one analgesic for infant LPs, with oral sucrose being one of the two most commonly used methods alongside topical lidocaine.19 While previous studies have shown mixed results for more invasive procedures,20 our data demonstrate a clear benefit for LP. This suggests that sucrose is not only effective but also a potent intervention for moderately painful procedures in this population.

We must also acknowledge the ethical considerations of conducting a placebo-controlled trial for a painful procedure. While our study provides valuable evidence that fills a critical gap identified in Cochrane reviews, the standard of care is evolving. Quality improvement initiatives have successfully increased analgesic use, and evidence now suggests that multimodal analgesia—combining sucrose with local anaesthetics and non-pharmacological comfort measures—represents best practice.21 Therefore, while our study robustly confirms the efficacy of sucrose as a single agent, its most powerful application in clinical practice is likely as a key component of a combined analgesic strategy. We have detailed our ethical justification and safeguards in the Methods section (2.3.1).

An important clinical consideration regarding the administration of oral sucrose prior to LP is its potential effect on the interpretation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) glucose levels. Theoretical concerns exist that a sucrose-induced spike in blood glucose could translate to falsely elevated CSF glucose, potentially masking hypoglycorrhachia. However, human physiology does not support this concern in the context of our protocol. The equilibration of glucose between systemic circulation and the CSF across the blood-brain barrier is a delayed process, typically taking 30 to 120 min to reflect changes in serum levels.22 Given that sucrose is administered merely 2 min prior to the procedure in our study, there is physically inadequate time for any transient blood glucose changes to be reflected in the CSF drawn during the puncture. Furthermore, the 2 mL dose of 25% sucrose provides only 0.5 g of carbohydrate, which is minimal. Therefore, clinicians can confidently use oral sucrose for LP analgesia without compromising the diagnostic reliability of the CSF glucose analysis.

The optimal sucrose dose for LP remains to be determined. We selected 2 mL of 25% sucrose based on previous literature suggesting higher doses may be needed for more invasive procedures. However, dose-finding studies specifically for LP are lacking. Future research should investigate whether lower doses (eg, 0.5–1 mL) provide equivalent analgesia, which would further minimise any theoretical risks and reduce cost. The concentration of 25% was chosen as it is the standard preparation available in our setting and has been most extensively studied.

The strengths of this study include its rigorous double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled design, which minimises bias. We used a validated pain assessment tool (NIPS) and supplemented it with objective physiological data, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the neonatal pain experience. The inclusion of clinically significant thresholds (eg, tachycardia >160 bpm, desaturation <90%) enhances the clinical interpretability of our findings.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the use of a placebo control group—which reflected the standard of care in our unit at the time (no routine analgesia)—was methodologically sound for establishing efficacy. However, given that multimodal analgesia is increasingly recognised as best practice, future trials should compare sucrose against active controls or within multimodal regimens rather than placebo. Second, it was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, our population was restricted to term neonates, and the results cannot be extrapolated to preterm infants, who may have different analgesic responses to sucrose and higher risk of adverse events. Future multicentre research should specifically investigate optimal dosing strategies, safety profiles and efficacy of sucrose for LP in preterm populations, including very low birth weight infants. Future research should also investigate the synergistic effects of sucrose with other non-pharmacological interventions such as non-nutritive sucking, facilitated tucking and positioning (eg, the sitting position, which has been shown to reduce struggling motion and enhance physiological stability in term neonates undergoing LP).23 Fourth, we did not assess longer-term outcomes or the potential for synergistic effects with other non-pharmacological interventions like non-nutritive sucking or facilitated tucking. Fifth, pain assessment was limited to the post-procedural period and was performed by a single assessor; the use of NIPS during the procedure itself was not feasible due to logistical constraints, and we lacked a second assessor to establish inter-rater reliability.

Conclusion

The administration of 2 mL of 25% oral sucrose is a safe, simple and clinically effective non-pharmacological intervention for reducing the pain and physiological distress associated with lumbar puncture in term neonates during the immediate recovery period. Our results provide strong evidence to support its use. However, in line with current clinical quality improvement initiatives, we recommend that sucrose be integrated into a multimodal analgesic protocol, typically combined with a local anaesthetic, to form the standard pain management strategy for this common and necessary procedure. Future multicentre research should investigate optimal dosing strategies, efficacy and safety in preterm populations, and synergistic effects with other comfort measures (eg, non-nutritive sucking, positioning and facilitated tucking).
