Comments on “Impact of Deep Learning-Based Image Conversion on Fully Automated Coronary Artery Calcium Scoring Using Thin-Slice, Sharp-Kernel, Non-Gated, Low-Dose Chest CT Scans: A Multi-Center Study”
Authors: Mukesh Kumar Dharmalingam Jothinathan
Published: 2025

I am writing in response to a recently published paper titled “Impact of Deep Learning-Based Image Conversion on Fully Automated Coronary Artery Calcium Scoring Using Thin-Slice, Sharp-Kernel, Non-Gated, Low-Dose Chest CT Scans: A Multi-Center Study” by Kim et al., 2025 [1]. This multi-retrospective study raises an important and timely question regarding the ability of deep learning (DL)-based image transformation to improve the accuracy of computerized coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring on low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) scans reconstructed with sharp kernels and thin slices, compared to the reference standard calcium scoring computed tomography (CSCT) [2]. The authors made a valuable contribution by investigating whether image conversion methods can reduce the issues of image noise and variability associated with LDCT protocols.

The analysis of 225 paired LDCT and CSCT scans was used in this study, including DL-based kernel conversion and thickness conversion, to represent CSCT conditions. Automated scoring of CAC was performed on the original LDCT, converted LDCT, and subsequently compared with manual scoring of CSCT images. The concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), Bland–Altman analysis, and weighted kappa statistics were used by the authors to establish agreement between datasets [3]. The results are intriguing: LDCT-CONVauto, which had both kernel and thickness conversions, had a significantly smaller bias (-3.45 compared to 206.7 in LDCT-Orgauto) and showed very good agreement with the CSCT manual (CCC = 0.881), in contrast to the poor reliability of unconverted LDCT. Risk stratification was also substantially improved, with LDCT-CONVauto achieving good agreement (κ = 0.792) while the agreement of LDCT-Orgauto remained poor (κ = 0.115) [4].

These results highlight the clinical promise of DL-based image conversion for automated CAC scoring, particularly in an era in which LDCT is increasingly used for lung cancer screening. The underreporting of incidental CAC findings has been a concern in clinical practice, and by improving the reliability of automated scoring, this study offers a path toward the routine incorporation of CAC assessment into LDCT workflows. This implication is significant for leveraging existing LDCT scans, and healthcare systems can provide additional prognostic information regarding cardiovascular risk without requiring additional imaging, radiation, or cost [5].

Despite these strengths, the study population contained a high proportion of individuals with zero CAC scores, which may limit the generalizability to higher-risk cohorts. Subgroup analysis of participants with CAC >0 confirmed improvements, but with somewhat attenuated reliability, suggesting that validation in populations with more extensive calcification is necessary. Although image conversion reduced noise and improved agreement, motion artifacts and attenuation issues remained sources of error. The authors noted that 10.8% of the overestimations were due to motion artifacts, while underestimations occurred when calcifications fell below the 130-Hounsfield unit threshold during reconstruction. This underscores the persistent challenge of non-ECG-gated LDCT for CAC scoring, and suggests that further algorithmic refinement or adaptive thresholds may be required. Broader validation across diverse scanner models and acquisition settings would strengthen confidence in the general applicability of the method [6]. Similarly, the study’s retrospective nature and reliance on a six-month window between LDCT and CSCT acquisitions introduce the potential for biological progression of CAC, although the authors justified the interval based on the literature [2,3]. Prospective validation, ideally using same-day paired imaging, would help eliminate this confounding factor. Although iterative reconstruction (IR) can reduce image noise, previous studies have suggested that it may also influence CAC quantification [4,5]. The interaction between IR and DL-based conversion deserves further exploration as routine clinical adoption must account for diverse reconstruction pipelines across institutions [7].

Future prospective multicenter trials with diverse patient populations, scanner models, and acquisition protocols are required to validate the generalizability of the results. Clinical outcome studies correlating DL-converted LDCT CAC scores with major adverse cardiovascular events would establish prognostic value, moving beyond technical validation to patient-level impact. Cost-effectiveness analyses should be performed to evaluate whether incorporating DL-based CAC scoring into LDCT workflows provides an incremental value to population screening programs [8]. Finally, integrating this technology into automated reporting systems could facilitate its adoption. However, workflow considerations, radiologist acceptance, and medicolegal implications must also be addressed.

In conclusion, Kim et al. [1] presented a carefully executed and clinically meaningful study that demonstrates how DL-based image conversion can substantially improve the accuracy of automated CAC scoring on LDCT scans reconstructed with thin slices and sharp kernels. Further validation, particularly in high-risk populations and prospective designs, will be crucial to fully realize the clinical potential of this approach. Nevertheless, this study represents a significant step forward in the application of artificial intelligence to cardiovascular imaging, with the promise of enhancing both risk stratification and clinical decision-making in preventive cardiology.
