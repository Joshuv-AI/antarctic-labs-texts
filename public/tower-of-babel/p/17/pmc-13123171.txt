Health outcomes of self- and family management programs among community-dwelling older people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Theepapha Jamkrajang, Doungrut Wattanakitkrileart, Virapun Wirojratana, Pratana Satitvipawee, Wanchai Dejsomritrutai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) significantly affects the quality of life of older adults. The growing impact of this chronic condition poses significant challenges for healthcare systems worldwide. This study evaluated an 8-week technology-enhanced self-and family management program targeting health outcomes, including self-management behaviors, exercise tolerance assessed by the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), dyspnea severity, depression, and family functioning as reported by family members.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial included 86 older adults with COPD. Participants were matched by age, sex, and disease severity, and then randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 43) or the control group (n = 43) using a simple randomization procedure based on a lottery method. The experimental group received the self- and family management program supported by the LINE application in addition to standard care, while the control group received standard care alone. Outcomes were assessed using validated instruments at baseline, Week 4, and Week 8.ResultsWithin the intervention group, significant improvements over time were observed in self-management behaviors, exercise tolerance, dyspnea severity, and family functioning (all p < .001). Depression levels did not change significantly within either group, as baseline scores were within the normal range. Between groups, the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than the control group in exercise tolerance (6MWT: Week 4: MD = 56.29 m, p = .003; Week 8: MD = 99.07 m, 95% CI [62.59, 135.55], p < .001), dyspnea severity (Week 4: MD = − 15.24, p < .001; Week 8: MD = − 29.29, 95% CI [− 32.11, − 26.47], p < .001), and family functioning (Week 4: MD = − 9.69, p < .001; Week 8: MD = − 11.69, 95% CI [− 13.61, − 9.77], p < .001). Between-group differences in self-management behaviors and depression were not statistically significant at any time point.ConclusionsThe technology-enhanced self-and family management program demonstrated significant effectiveness in improving health outcomes among older adults with COPD in community settings. This evidence-based nursing intervention, which integrates mobile technology and family support, provides a practical framework for nurses to enhance patient care and achieve positive health outcomes while also improving family functioning.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR) with registration number TCTR20240903003, registered on 03/09/2024 (retrospectively registered). Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common respiratory condition in older adults that contributes significantly to global mortality and leads to high costs for healthcare services. The World Health Organization [1] projects that COPD will become the third leading cause of death globally by 2060. In Thailand, COPD ranks as the fourth leading cause of death among non-communicable diseases. The prevalence of the disease among older adults increased from 151,611 in 2016 to 175,432 in 2020 [2]. Treatment efficacy is often compromised due to poor medication adherence (15%–30%) [3, 4, 5] and low participation in rehabilitation programs (8%–50%), primarily due to transportation challenges, financial constraints, and health-related limitations [6]. As a result, older adults with COPD experience declining fitness, worsened psychological well-being, and reduced social participation, leading to increased dependence on family members and healthcare providers.

According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines [7], older adults with COPD can improve disease management through regular physical activity, medication adherence, early symptom recognition, personalized action plans, participation in rehabilitation programs, and optimized nutrition. Family support plays a crucial role in enhancing these self-management practices by providing emotional encouragement, assisting with medication adherence, and promoting physical activity. Thus, a comprehensive self-management approach that incorporates family involvement is essential for improving health outcomes in older adults with COPD.

Gray et al. [8] proposed the self- and family management framework to explain how individuals and families manage chronic conditions such as COPD, emphasizing vulnerability factors, protective resources, self-management processes, and health outcomes. The model highlights the critical role of family involvement beyond traditional patient-centered care, which is particularly relevant for older adults with COPD who increasingly rely on family support. Consistently, Jónsdóttir et al. [9] reported that partnership-based interventions involving both patients and family members improved dyspnea, exercise capacity, and exacerbation rates. Likewise, Noonan et al. [10] found that family-inclusive care models yielded better clinical outcomes and quality of life compared to individual-focused approaches. The self- and family management framework aligns with the Thai cultural context, which emphasizes patient actions and family involvement in chronic disease management. Studies among Thai patients with COPD have demonstrated that family-inclusive interventions, including home-based multimedia pulmonary rehabilitation [11], structured self-management support programs incorporating family and community involvement [12], and family support-based care models [13], consistently improved clinical symptoms, exercise performance, self-management behaviors, dyspnea severity, and exacerbation rates, underscoring the need for care models aligned with Thailand’s family-centered culture.

Digital health innovations effectively complement family-centered COPD management. Among 422 Thai older adults, smartphone use was high (93.6%), with LINE as the most frequently used application [14]. A 6-week program based on the information, motivation behavioral skills model integrated education, personalized feedback, skills training, and medication reminders, resulting in significant health improvements compared with baseline and control outcomes. Older adults’ familiarity with LINE allows technology based interventions to overcome distance barriers while preserving family connections. This evidence supports the integration of digital tools within the current study’s family-centered framework [15].

Research has shown that effective COPD self-management programs typically adopt a multifaceted approach, integrating education, skill development, and psychological support to address both the physical symptoms and the mental and social factors influencing disease progression and quality of life. Educational components play a critical role, as evidence indicates that health literacy-informed interventions significantly improve patients’ understanding of COPD, including knowledge of the disease, medications, action plans, and physical training [16], providing a foundation for symptom recognition, treatment adherence, and informed health decision-making.

Practical skill development is essential in COPD self-management programs. A randomized controlled trial showed that multidisciplinary team-led self-management training improved quality of life, reduced anxiety and depression, and enhanced physical functioning in patients with COPD [17]. Targeted training in pursed-lip breathing and proper inhaler use significantly increased medication competence, reduced dyspnea severity, and improved overall quality of life [18]. Motivational interview-based programs combining group training with individualized counseling significantly raised self-efficacy compared to standard care, highlighting the role of nurses in delivering effective self-management support [19]. Remote support technologies like telehealth and mobile apps have expanded access to care and symptom monitoring for patients with COPD, though evidence on their impact on clinical outcomes remains uncertain [20].

Synthesizing these research insights, an optimal COPD self-management program for older adults should incorporate person-centered strategies that integrate medical care, educational components, and family engagement across multiple delivery channels. A comprehensive intervention could combine video communication via widely used applications, instructional video content demonstrating practical self-care techniques, and traditional printed materials to accommodate diverse learning preferences and technological literacy levels. This integrative approach aims to enhance patients’ ability to manage their condition while promoting sustained behavioral changes reinforced by family involvement. Although many COPD self-management programs report positive outcomes, variability in intervention components, delivery methods, and evaluation approaches has resulted in inconsistencies across studies. This highlights the need for a standardized framework for designing, implementing, and assessing self-management programs to ensure consistent and comparable results. Such an approach would enhance the reliability of interventions and maximize their effectiveness in improving disease management and quality of life for older adults and their families. Despite theoretical frameworks emphasizing the role of family members in chronic disease management, research on how self-management operates within family systems remains limited. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant given that self-management responsibilities evolve throughout life and often shift between patients and their caregivers.

In this study, the primary outcomes were selected to capture key clinical and psychosocial consequences of COPD and targets of a self- and family management intervention. Self-management behavior is central to COPD care because effective self-management programs improve functional ability, reduce dyspnea and emotional symptoms, and can decrease exacerbations and health-care use [21]. The six-minute walk test (6MWT) is a simple measure of exercise tolerance that reflects older patients’ ability to perform daily activities and is standard in COPD rehabilitation trials [22, 23]. Lower 6MWT distance is consistently associated with greater symptom burden and predicts hospitalization and mortality in COPD [24, 25]. Dyspnea is the most common symptom of COPD and one of the most frequently reported outcome domains in pulmonary rehabilitation, strongly linked to health-related quality of life and physical activity limitation [23]. Depression is common in COPD and is associated with higher symptom burden, worse quality of life, poorer adherence to self-management, and increased mortality; systematic reviews of pulmonary rehabilitation highlight that anxiety and depression improve when baseline levels are high [23]. Finally, family function of the family member is relevant in theThai context, where family caregivers often support day-to-day disease management. Better family functioning can facilitate adherence to self-management plans, encourage physical activity, and buffer psychological distress, thereby indirectly influencing COPD outcomes [21]. Together, these outcomes reflect functional status, symptom severity, emotional well-being, and the family context that may mediate the effect of a self-management–focused intervention in COPD.

This study addressed four gaps in COPD self-management research: (1) the lack of integrated multicomponent programs, (2) limited family caregiver involvement, (3) underutilization of remote technology in community-based care, and (4) insufficient attention to Thailand’s family-centered cultural context. To address these gaps, this study developed a comprehensive program integrating education, skills training, psychological support, family involvement, and LINE-based mobile technology. Family caregivers were actively included as part of the management team, continuous remote guidance was provided via LINE-based video calls and messaging, and Thailand’s cultural context was explicitly incorporated. Building on Grey et al.‘s self- and family management framework [8], this study examined how these integrated components collectively improve self-management behaviors, exercise tolerance, dyspnea severity, depression, and family functioning among older adults with COPD.

Methods

Study design

This study evaluated the effects of self-and family management programs targeting health outcomes, including self-management behaviors, exercise tolerance, dyspnea, depression, and family functioning of family members. The study design was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a control group. The research was conducted in 2022 from February to June. This study was designed according to the principles of a randomized controlled trial (RCT), using random assignment to intervention and control groups and delivering an intervention as planned. The study was retrospectively registered with TCTR20240903003.

Sample size and power analysis

The required sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software based on a two-sided α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an anticipated medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.70), resulting in 36 participants per group. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of 20%, the target sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in 43 participants per group (total N = 86).

The observed attrition rate during the study was 2.3%, with 42 participants per group included in the final analysis. Thus, the final analyzed sample size remained close to the a priori calculated requirement. In accordance with current methodological standards, 95% confidence intervals are presented for all effect estimates to reflect the precision and clinical relevance of the findings.

Participants and setting

Participants

Participants were recruited from the COPD outpatient clinic at Wangchan Hospital, Rayong Province, Thailand. Potentially eligible patients with COPD were identified through medical record review by clinic nurses. After 110 people were first evaluated for eligibility, 86 older patients with COPD who met the inclusion criteria, along with their family caregivers, were selected for the study. Inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: aged ≥ 60 years, having mild or moderate COPD based on the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease [27], diagnosis by a physician, and confirmation by spirometry testing indicating a post-bronchodilator ratio of FEV1 to FVC < 0.7; Mild (GOLD 1) was defined as an FEV₁ ≥ 80% predicted, while Moderate (GOLD 2) was defined as FEV₁ between 50% and 79% predicted, with clinical stability for at least one month, using the LINE application, and having a family member willing to participate in this study. The age criterion of ≥ 60 years was established because the intervention was specifically designed for older adults with COPD, in whom disease burden, multimorbidity, functional decline, and psychosocial issues such as depression and family caregiving needs are more pronounced than in younger patients. COPD is predominantly a disease of later life, with incidence, hospitalization, and disability-adjusted life years highest in adults over 60, who also differ in comorbidity patterns and treatment response compared with younger patients. Inclusion criteria for family members who acted as a primary caregiver were as follows: (1) family members who aged 18 years and over, (2) wife, husband, son, daughter, or grandchildren who were mainly responsible for the care, and (3) having a mobile phone with WiFi, 4G, or 5G internet and being able to use the LINE application with internet access. Exclusion criteria for older patients living with COPD included those diagnosed with lung cancer, pulmonary tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, major cardiac arrhythmias, congestive heart failure, active infectious disease, or musculoskeletal problems causing walking limitations. Additionally, older people diagnosed with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder were excluded. Cognitive function was screened using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [28], with a cutoff score of 25; patients scoring below 25 were excluded to ensure all older patients living with COPD could understand and follow the self-management program instructions. Withdrawal criteria included participants who could not continue to participate in the program for any reason or death during the program period. Dyads who provided verbal consent underwent eligibility screening. Those meeting all inclusion criteria were provided with detailed written information about the study, and written informed consent was obtained from both the patient and their family caregiver prior to enrollment and randomization (Fig. 1).

Randomization and allocation concealment

Following enrollment, participants were first matched by age (± 5 years), sex, and COPD severity: mild or moderate stage according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease [27] to ensure baseline comparability between groups. The random allocation sequence was then generated by the study investigators using a simple lottery method. Each older adult with COPD and their family member was assigned a sequential identification number, and these numbers were written on identical pieces of paper, folded, and placed in an opaque container. A nurse from another department who was not involved in the study drew the papers one by one to assign dyad to either the intervention group (n = 43) or control group (n = 43). In total, 86 participants (43 older adults with COPD - family dyads) were randomized. Most family members were husbands or wives. Allocation concealment was maintained until the completion of baseline assessments.

Blinding

To minimize detection bias, all outcome assessments were conducted by one research assistant (RA) who was (1) independent of the intervention delivery, (2) trained in standardized assessment protocols prior to data collection, and (3) blinded to group allocation. The RA received a training session of the 6MWT according to American Thoracic Society guidelines and the Dyspnea Visual Analogue Scale (DVAS). Inter-rater reliability was established prior to the study. Blinding was maintained by ensuring that RA had no access to randomization lists and did not participate in intervention sessions. Home visits for assessments were scheduled independently of intervention activities. Self-reported outcomes were collected via Google Forms, which participants completed independently.

Participant flow and attrition

After 8 weeks, the completion rate was 97.68%, with 42 participants remaining in each group. Loss to follow-up occurred in both groups, with one participant from each group withdrawing. Loss to follow-up was mostly due to participants refusing to continue in the study (n = 2). No participants were lost due to death or serious adverse events during the study period. All randomized participants were included in the intention-to-treat analysis where applicable.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Nursing, Mahidol University (COA No. IRB-NS2021/613.1904). Written informed consent was obtained first from patients living with COPD to respect their autonomy and privacy, followed by their family members, with the consent process conducted in a quiet and private setting. Patients living with COPD received clear explanations of the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, and were assured of confidentiality and their right to withdraw without consequences. Recruitment and data collection took place in private rooms to minimize interruptions and protect privacy. Family members’ involvement was carefully managed to respect their roles and relationships while protecting confidentiality and reducing potential harm. The researcher used nonconfrontational communication techniques such as simple, neutral language, allowing time for questions, avoiding coercion or judgmental wording, and encouraging open expression to promote comfort and respect for both patients and family members; they also monitored participants for psychological distress and were prepared to provide support if needed. The control group received self-management education materials after program completion.

Intervention

The 8-week of self- and family management program for older adults with COPD was delivered by the researcher, who served as the primary educator, coach, and supervisor throughout the intervention. The program combined face-to-face small-group education, home-based practice, digital support via the LINE@ mobile application, and family involvement. Grey et al. (2015) describe family members as part of the management team. Their roles include shared decision-making, problem-solving together, and providing both practical and emotional support within the family context. This means that family members share responsibility and provide support.

In this study, family members were involved in the management process. Family members shared decision-making with participants and the researcher regarding COPD self-management practices, including proper inhaler use, breathing exercises, walking exercises, and trigger avoidance. They participated in problem solving when symptoms interfered with daily routines or when exacerbations occurred, and they provided resources such as time, finances, and effort to self-management practice. In addition, family members provided emotional and practical social support, for example, by offering encouragement, preparing appropriate meals, assisting with exercises, reminding patients to use inhalers and access the LINE@ application, and accompanying them during practice. They also played a role in maintaining positive family functioning, recognizing that conflict or stress could affect the participant’s ability to manage COPD, and they were encouraged by the researcher to sustain constructive communication and collaboration. Data collection and measures

Primary outcomes were collected at three time points: Week 1 (baseline), Week 4, and Week 8 for both groups. At Week 1, baseline data were obtained during the participants’ initial clinic visit prior to the initiation of any intervention activities. This approach was used to minimize participant burden and reduce the number of clinic visits required for older adults with COPD who may have difficulty traveling frequently to clinical sites. To ensure data integrity, all baseline assessments were completed before any program content was delivered.

To further reduce travel burden, particularly for participants who traveled from rural communities with family members, baseline data collection and the initial intervention session were conducted on the same day. Baseline measures, including the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), dyspnea severity, and questionnaire-based assessments, were administered in the morning. The intervention session was conducted in the afternoon following completion of all baseline assessments.

Self-management behaviors

Self-management behaviors were measured using the Thai version of the COPD self-management questionnaire, adapted from the original 51-item COPD self-management questionnaire developed by Zhang et al. [29] and modified to 32 items in the Thai context. The questionnaire assesses self-management behaviors across five subscales: symptom management, daily life management, emotion management, information management, and self-efficacy. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never to 4 = always), with total scores ranging from 32 to 128. Higher scores indicate better self-management behaviors. The Thai version was developed and validated through a cross-cultural adaptation process following the guidelines of Beaton et al. (2000). In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.94.

Exercise tolerance

Exercise tolerance was evaluated via the 6MWT [30], which measures the distance a participant can walk on a flat, hard surface within 6 min. The participants were instructed to take prescribed bronchodilators an hour before testing, refrain from eating or drinking for at least 2 h prior, and wear appropriate clothing. Before testing, each participant rested for 10 min while their pulse, blood pressure, and dyspnea levels were measured. The participants were instructed to walk back and forth for 6 min at their own pace but could slow down or take breaks if necessary. They were advised to report any dyspnea or chest pain.

Dyspnea severity

The Dyspnea Visual Analog Scale (DVAS) was used to evaluate the dyspnea severity before and after walking for 6MWT. The original DVAS was developed by Gift (1989) [31]. The DVAS is a highly accurate instrument and is widely used among patients with COPD. The DVAS is a straight line 100 mm in length. The participants were asked to place a mark on the line to indicate their dyspnea. The score ranges from 0 to 100. The score of DVAS was interpreted from the scale of the line with no dyspnea or breathlessness. The highest score of DVAS is 100, which reflects severe dyspnea; the middle of the scale, or score of 50, reflects moderate dyspnea; and the lowest score of the scale, 0, reflects no dyspnea or no difficulty breathing. The participants were asked to rate their perceived level of dyspnea after completing the 6MWT. The test-retest reliability in this study was 1.00.

Depression

The Thai Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS) was used to evaluate the depression [32]. The participants were requested to answer yes or no about their felt over the past week. The TGDS consists of 15 items. Each item is scored either as 0 or 1 point; the maximum score being 15. The score range from 0 to 5 indicates no depression, 6–10 indicates suggestive of depression, and 11–15 indicates severe depression. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in this study was 0.83.

Family functions of family members

The family functions were measured with the General Functioning Subscale of the Family Assessment Device (GF-12) [33]. Family members completed this instrument. It includes six items measuring healthy functioning and six assessing unhealthy functioning. The responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree). Negative items (1, 3, 5, 7, and 11) are reverse-scored before all items are summed and divided by 12. Scores ranged from 1 (healthy functioning) to 4 (unhealthy functioning), with scores ≥ 2 indicating problematic family functioning. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in this study was 0.79.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare baseline characteristics between groups. Repeated-measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to examine the effects of the intervention on exercise tolerance (6-Minute Walk Test) and dyspnea severity (Dyspnea Visual Analogue Scale) over time (baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks). MANOVA was selected because these outcomes are conceptually related, measured at the same time points, and this approach accounts for correlations between outcomes while controlling the overall Type I error rate. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used separately for self-management behaviors, depression, and family functioning of family members, as these represent distinct constructs measured using different instruments. Repeated-measures ANOVA used within-subject correlations over time. The sphericity assumption was tested using Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when violations occurred to control Type I error. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment were conducted to identify specific time point differences. Statistical significance was set at p < .05 for all analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants are presented in Table 2. Both groups were comparable at baseline with no statistically significant differences across all characteristics (all p > .05). Participants had a mean age of approximately 69 years, were predominantly male (85.7%), married, had primary education or less, and reported a monthly income between 5,000 and 10,000 Baht, which represents a low-income level typical of older adults in Thailand, given that the minimum wage during the study period ranged from 300 to 370 Baht per day (approximately 9,000–11,100 Baht per month) depending on province [34]. Most had mild-to-moderate COPD with a duration of 5–9 years, and the majority were former smokers. The only characteristic approaching significance was the difference in comorbidities between the intervention and control groups (40.5% vs. 23.8%, p = .055). These findings confirm that both groups were well-matched at baseline, supporting a reliable comparison of outcomes between groups.

Comparison of self-management behavior outcome between intervention and control groups

Self-management scores showed a significant improvement over time with a large group × time interaction effect (F = 52.24, p < .001, η²=0.389). While baseline scores were comparable between groups, participants in the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater and more sustained improvements in self-management behaviors over the 8-week period compared with the control group (see Table 3). These findings indicate a meaningful and robust intervention effect.

Comparison of the 6MWT outcome between intervention and control Groups

To evaluate improvements in exercise tolerance, we analyzed the 6MWT performance of both groups over time. As shown in Table 3, the 6MWT revealed a significant improvement with a substantial group × time interaction effect (F = 83.19, p < .001, η²=0.504), representing the strongest effect size among all measured outcomes. While baseline walking distances were comparable between the groups, participants in the intervention group demonstrated progressive and sustained increases in functional exercise capacity at both Week 4 and Week 8. In contrast, the control group’s performance remained relatively stable throughout the 8-week period. These findings indicate that approximately 50% of the variance in exercise tolerance improvement was attributable to the intervention, underscoring its effectiveness in enhancing physical functional capacity in this population.

Comparison of dyspnea outcome between intervention and control groups

To evaluate changes in dyspnea severity, we analyzed scores across both groups over time. As shown in Table 3, dyspnea scores revealed a significant improvement with a substantial group × time interaction effect (F = 205.07, p < .001, η²=0.714), representing the largest effect size among all measured outcomes. While baseline dyspnea levels were comparable between groups, the intervention group demonstrated a progressive and marked reduction in symptoms at both Week 4 and Week 8. In contrast, dyspnea severity in the control group remained relatively unchanged throughout the study period (see Table 3). The exceptionally large interaction effect size indicates that approximately 71% of the variance in dyspnea improvement was attributable to the intervention. These findings underscore the program’s effectiveness in alleviating breathing difficulties, leading to sustained symptom reduction compared with standard care.

Comparison of depression outcome between intervention and control groups

As shown in Table 3, no significant group × time interaction effect was observed for depression (F = 0.00, p = 1.00, η²=0.000). Depression levels remained relatively stable and comparable between groups throughout the 8-week period, indicating that the intervention did not have a differential effect on depressive symptoms.

Comparison of the family function of family member outcomes between the intervention and control groups

As shown in Table 3, family function scores reported by family members showed a significant group × time interaction effect (F = 54.98, p < .001, η²=0.401), While baseline scores were comparable between groups, the intervention group demonstrated progressive and sustained improvements in family functioning at both Week 4 and Week 8, whereas the control group remained relatively stable throughout the 8-week period. These findings indicate that approximately 40% of the variance in family function improvement was attributable to the intervention.

Discussion

In this study, we implemented an 8-week self-and family management program for older adults with COPD that combined education, skills training, and ongoing motivational support involving family members. The program significantly improved health outcomes, suggesting that multicomponent interventions delivered with family involvement are feasible and beneficial in this population. Our findings are consistent with previous studies showing that structured education focusing on symptom recognition and management enhances daily self-monitoring and adherence to treatment in patients with COPD [35, 36]. In addition, the incorporation of family support aligns with evidence that empowerment and family-focused programs can improve quality of life. Together, these results highlight the importance of integrating education, practical skills training, and continuous support within the family context to optimize self-management among older adults with COPD.

In this study, the intervention and control groups were well matched, with no statistically significant differences in demographic or health-related characteristics, supporting the internal validity of the Results. Most participants were younger older adults, comparable to other COPD self-management cohorts and representing a group that can still engage with new information while facing age-related challenges [37, 38]. Most had mild-to-moderate COPD, similar to many self-management and eHealth interventions [37, 39, 40]. Thus, intervening at these stages is appropriate, as multicomponent self-management programs have been shown to improve health-related quality of life and reduce respiratory-related hospital admissions, and there is a particular need to optimize self-management support for early-stage disease [37, 39].

Most participants were married and lived with a spouse, which is important because social and family support, especially from spouses, is positively associated with better adherence and self-management behaviours [38, 41]. The majority of participants were former smokers (intervention group: 31/42, 73.8%; control group: 37/42, 88.1%), reflecting prior successful behaviour change, which is favourable given that smoking cessation substantially improves lung function, dyspnea, exercise tolerance, and may reduce mortality in COPD [42]. Approximately half of the participants had been living with COPD for 5–9 years, similar to other COPD cohorts [39]. This duration suggests prior exposure to COPD management and experience recognizing symptoms, although motivation and self-management can still vary over time and require reinforcement [38].

The intervention group showed greater improvements in self-management behaviors over time, indicating the effectiveness of the family-based self-management program. Although mean score changes were small, the pattern of improvement in the intervention group, whereas the control group showed a slight decline, suggests a beneficial effect on maintaining and enhancing self-management over time.

Despite the significant overall group × time interaction effect, no significant between-group differences in self-management scores were observed at any individual time point (Baseline: MD = 2.07, 95% CI [− 2.37, 6.51], p = .356; Week 4: MD = 3.26, 95% CI [− 1.10, 7.62], p = .141; Week 8: MD = 3.33, 95% CI [− 1.02, 7.69], p = .132), likely due to the conservative Bonferroni adjustment and small, consistent differences between groups. Similar findings in COPD self-management trials and meta-analyses show significant overall trajectories but small pointwise differences, sometimes below conventional thresholds for statistical or clinical significance [37]. This underscores that in self-management research, the pattern and sustainability of change over time may be as important as the magnitude of change at single time points.

Our results align with evidence that multicomponent self-management programs combining structured education, skills training, and ongoing support improve self-management practices, health-directed behaviors, and related outcomes [37, 39, 43]. The explicit involvement of family members and regular follow-up likely contributed to maintaining behavior change, consistent with literature emphasizing family support and ongoing interactions as key to sustaining self-management behaviour [37, 44].

The self- and family-management program led to improvement in exercise tolerance. Participants in the intervention group showed steady increases in walking distance over time, whereas those in the control group remained stable, suggesting a clear benefit of the intervention on exercise tolerance. The program encouraged patients to exercise by walking short distances, initially for brief durations, and gradually increasing both distance and time. This progressive approach enhances exercise tolerance, leading to improvements in daily functioning capacity. Several key components of our intervention likely contributed to these positive outcomes. First, structured education on proper breathing and aerobic exercise techniques provides patients with essential knowledge on optimizing physical activity despite respiratory limitations. Second, regular practice reinforced by personalized feedback through video recordings enabled older adults with COPD to refine their exercise techniques. Additionally, the role of family involvement in providing motivation and support likely enabled patients to maintain a regular exercise routine, directly contributing to these positive outcomes. This progressive and sustained gain demonstrates a clinically meaningful enhancement in exercise tolerance compared with usual care and is consistent with prior self-management and pulmonary rehabilitation studies reporting significant improvements in 6MWT following structured exercise and education-based programs [39, 45, 46, 47].

The program also reduced dyspnea severity, providing evidence that self-management, breathing exercises, and active chest mobilization can decrease symptoms and improve breathlessness [39, 46]. In this study, participants in the intervention group reported steady decreases in dyspnea, particularly between weeks 4 and 8, suggesting that mastery of the intervention skills required time and practice. These findings suggest that improved self-management behaviors directly influence symptom control. In this program, the use of video-recorded practice and researcher feedback helped COPD patients develop skills to better manage their disease. The results of this study support previous findings, demonstrating that self-management interventions are effective at enhancing health and reducing dyspnea in patients with COPD.

The finding that the intervention had no significant effect on depression scores may be interpreted in several ways. This finding may be explained by the fact that participants in both groups started with low baseline depression scores (5.8 and 5.9, respectively), falling within the “no depression” range. This may have limited the potential for measurable improvement. However, alternative explanations should be considered. It is possible that the intervention was not specifically designed or sufficiently intensive to impact depressive symptoms directly, or that individual variability in responses to the intervention played a role. Family involvement in the intervention likely provided emotional support and fostered resilience, which are important for psychological well-being in chronic illness, even if not reflected in depression scale scores. Research shows a significant inverse relationship between family support and depression severity across various chronic conditions, suggesting that family support can buffer depressive symptoms by enhancing coping and emotional adaptation [48, 49]. Thus, while depression scores remained stable, support from family member may still contribute positively to mental health outcomes beyond what standard depression measures capture [50].

Besides health outcomes, the study examined the effects of the intervention on family members’ functioning. The program provided family members with the knowledge and skills necessary for managing COPD, resulting in significant improvements in family function. Compared with those at baseline, family function scores significantly improved following the intervention. One key factor contributing to this improvement was comprehensive education on COPD pathophysiology, progression, and management, which enabled family members to recognize symptoms early, anticipate needs, and make informed caregiving decisions. Additionally, practical self-management skills increase caregivers’ confidence in providing care. Furthermore, the program also provided family members with support and motivation, which helped them remain engaged in their caregiving roles. No harms, adverse events, or unintended effects were reported in either the intervention or control group.

Strengths

The strengths of this study were its multicomponent, family-managed program, which combines structured education, practical skills training with personalized video feedback, and ongoing motivational support. This holistic approach, including the active involvement of family members, provides a continuous support system that enhances engagement and skill development. The use of technology for continuous engagement and the measurement of many outcomes, from clinical outcomes to psychosocial factors such as family functioning and caregiver confidence, allowed for a complete and thorough evaluation of the program’s effectiveness.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the generalizability of the findings is limited because participants were recruited from a single hospital. Furthermore, the sample consisted primarily of “young-old” married male adults living with their spouses, which may not represent more diverse populations. Second, while most demographic and health characteristics were well-matched, a significant difference in baseline dyspnea scores was observed between groups, which could serve as a potential confounding factor. Third, using per-protocol instead of intention-to-treat reflects ideal conditions and may overestimate the intervention’s real-world effectiveness. Fourth, the relatively short 8-week follow-up period may not be sufficient to determine the long-term sustainability of improvements in self-management behaviors and family functioning. Additionally, the low baseline depression scores in both groups prevented a meaningful evaluation of the intervention’s potential effect on emotional well-being. Finally, the lack of an attention-controlled comparison group is a notable limitation. The control group did not receive matched contact (e.g., weekly video calls, LINE messages, or home visits), making it difficult to distinguish whether the observed improvements resulted from the specific intervention content or from the increased attention and regular interaction with the research team.

Recommendations

These findings support the integration of family-centered COPD management strategies in clinical practice. Healthcare providers should consider combining in-person consultations with digital communication platforms to facilitate ongoing education, skills training, and support. This hybrid approach has the potential to enhance multiple outcomes, including self-management behaviors, physical activity levels, symptom management, emotional well-being, and family dynamics. By bridging the clinical and home environments through technology, this program offers a practical solution to extend professional support while reinforcing family involvement in COPD care.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a multicomponent, self- and family management program is an effective strategy for improving outcomes in older adults with COPD. The 8-week intervention, which combined structured education, practical skills training with personalized feedback, and continuous motivational support, led to significant improvements in participants’ self-management behaviors, exercise tolerance, and dyspnea. Furthermore, the program had a positive effect on family members, significantly improving family functioning scores. While the intervention did not have a statistically significant effect on depression, likely due to low baseline scores, its integrated, family-centered approach offers a valuable model for clinical practice. These findings underscore the importance of involving both patients and their families in a comprehensive management program to achieve positive and holistic health outcomes.
