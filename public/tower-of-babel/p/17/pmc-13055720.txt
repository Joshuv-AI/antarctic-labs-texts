From Insight to Action: A National Patient‐Centered Strategic Framework for Improving Reproductive Health Outcomes in Individuals With Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Mindi Messmer, Tripti Gupta, Rohan D'Souza, Melissa Russo, Jamie L. Jackson, Kashvi Singh, Katherine B. Salciccioli, Katia Bravo‐Jaimes, Nadine A. Kasparian, Ayesha Abusbeitan, Jennifer Cortes, Danielle Hile, Ruth Phillippi, Scott Leezer, Anitha S. John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn increasing number of patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) are pursuing pregnancy with the potential for cardiovascular complications. This study aimed to establish patient‐centered national research and care priorities to improve pregnancy‐related outcomes for individuals with CHD.MethodsThis mixed‐methods study used a modified Stakeholder Engagement in Question Development methodology to systematically identify patient‐centered priorities. Key activities included consortium engagement (phase 1), patient and provider questionnaires (phase 2), and an in‐person facilitated meeting to explore survey results and follow‐up virtual focus groups (phase 3). Reflexive thematic analysis was used to synthesize short‐ and long‐term research priorities (phase 4).ResultsThree overarching goals emerged: (1) improving access to specialized CHD, cardio‐obstetrics, and mental health care; (2) improving quality and consistency in clinical care; and (3) increasing patient and community engagement. These goals were then expanded into 5 central themes: (1) communication within the health care setting; (2) community engagement; (3) ancillary clinical support; (4) integration of patient‐reported outcomes; and (5) implementation, education, and knowledge mobilization. Short‐term (1–5‐year) goals included expanded telemedicine use, best practice recommendations for multidisciplinary care coordination, and targeted educational resources. Long‐term (5–10‐year) goals included expansion of adult CHD and cardio‐obstetrics programs, integration of mental health services, and advocacy to improve insurance coverage.ConclusionsThe consortium provided a national, community‐driven prioritization that highlights actionable pathways to improve pregnancy outcomes for people with CHD. Sustained engagement with patients and community partners is essential to achieving durable improvements across cardio‐obstetric care, education, research, and mental health.

The United States continues to have one of the highest maternal morbidity and mortality rates of any high‐income nation, with cardiovascular disease being one of the leading causes of pregnancy‐related deaths. 1 , 2 , 3 Although recent national surveillance shows some modest improvement in 2023 estimates, maternal mortality rates remain unacceptably high, underscoring the urgency of addressing preventable causes of severe morbidity and death. 3

One contributing factor to the persistently high rate of pregnancy‐related morbidity is the increasing number of individuals with preexisting health conditions, such as congenital heart disease (CHD), becoming pregnant. CHD refers to structural heart defects present at birth and encompasses a diverse group of rare diseases with varying severity and comorbidities. 4 Due to advances in medical and surgical treatments, >1.5 million adults are now living with CHD in the United States, and pregnancies among this group rose by 35% from 1999 to 2007. 4 , 5 Given the rising population, developing a patient‐centered road map to address the unique risks faced by pregnant individuals with CHD is essential to guide targeted research, clinical practices, and policy efforts aimed at reducing maternal morbidity and death.

Although the absolute rate of severe complications in pregnancy in the context of CHD is estimated to range from 2% to 6%, individuals with CHD have a 5‐fold higher likelihood of dying and have an 8‐fold higher risk of cardiac complications during pregnancy compared with individuals without CHD. 5 , 6 , 7 In addition, individuals with CHD are also at greater risk for mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, which are associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10

Despite these well‐documented risks, there is currently no US‐based framework designed to systematically examine or address the concerns, priorities, and lived experiences of pregnant individuals with CHD. As a result, critical gaps remain in understanding the needs of both patients and health care providers (HCPs) who provide care during pregnancy. In 2022, a collaborative effort was initiated to establish a comprehensive road map outlining short‐ and long‐term objectives to mitigate maternal morbidity and to improve reproductive health among individuals with CHD. These efforts align with a national call to action to reduce the concerning rise in US maternal mortality and morbidity rates. 11

This initiative involved the collection of data and insights derived from patient and HCP surveys as well as extensive dialogues with patients, HCPs, researchers, and patient advocates resulting in the formation of a capacity‐building consortium focused on improving reproductive health in individuals with CHD. Recognizing that patient and HCP input is essential for setting research priorities, this capacity‐building consortium developed a strategic road map composed of short‐ and long‐term goals for patient‐centered outcomes research (PCOR) to reduce maternal morbidity in CHD. This road map serves as a foundational guide that highlights patient‐ and provider‐driven priority areas in clinical care and research. While these are not finalized solutions, the recommendations intend to direct future investigation, development, and evaluation of feasible, scalable, and clinically integrated strategies.

Methods

Data Availability

The data underlying this study are not publicly available but are available upon reasonable request. Interested researchers may review the data request process on the congenital heart initiative website (www.congenitalheartinitiative.com). Access will be granted after approval from the patient and scientific advisory board.

Study Overview

The study used a modified Stakeholder Engagement in Question Development methodology for patient and partner engagement. 12 , 13 Stakeholder Engagement in Question Development is a structured framework designed to engage patients and stakeholders in collaboratively generating and refining research questions. This model was selected because it emphasizes colearning, shared decision making, and integration of diverse perspectives. A collaborative, mixed‐methods, multiphase approach was used and is reported in accordance with the Reporting Guideline for Priority Setting of Health Research. 14 The project was completed in 4 phases over 2.5 years (January 2022 through June 2024). The study was deemed institutional review board exempt (category 2).

In phase 1 (identify, engage, and educate), patient and partner engagement focused on building a diverse multidisciplinary consortium and providing education to patient partners about PCOR and patient engagement in research. In phase 2 (consult and conceptualize), 2 electronic needs assessment surveys were developed and disseminated: 1 designed for patients and 1 for HCPs. Phase 3 (conceptualize, generate questions, and prioritize questions) included completion of an in‐person, patient‐powered research meeting followed by 3 directed virtual focus groups to explore survey results. Phase 4 (prioritize questions) was composed of data synthesis to develop key priorities in 4 domains: (1) clinical care and outcomes; (2) patient and provider education; (3) health care advocacy; and (4) PCOR. Prioritization occurred through an iterative consensus process involving review of themes from phase 2 questionnaires, independent ranking by participants, and facilitated discussion to reconcile differences. Each phase built upon data generated from the previous phase, and all data were used to develop short‐ and long‐term priorities for future PCOR that aligned with patient needs and priorities. Patients were partners throughout the consortium, participating in study leadership and guiding the development of study materials.

Phase 1: Identify, Engage, and Educate

Patient and Partner Engagement

Consistent with the Patient‐Centered Outcomes Research Institute's Engagement Rubric, we implemented a structured, iterative approach to engage patients and community partners as active members of the research team. Partners were identified through collaboration with the Adult Congenital Heart Association, a patient advocacy organization for adults with CHD and through the CHI (Congenital Heart Initiative), a patient‐powered registry for adults with CHD. 15 Potential participants completed an intake form with demographic questions and questions about their lived experience with CHD and pregnancy. Recruitment focused on individuals (including family members and partners) with direct experience related to maternal health and CHD. Orientation sessions were conducted to support informed participation in the project as cocollaborators, followed by training on research methods, ethics, and roles within the project. Engaged partners served on the executive board (10 members including 5 patient partners) and other patient partners were integrated into all phases of the research, including study design and research question refinement, survey development, selection of outcomes meaningful to patients, and interpretation and dissemination of results. Engagement activities included regularly scheduled meetings, decision‐making discussions, and coleadership in breakout groups. We used inclusive and transparent communication practices, offering materials in plain language and accommodating preferences for meeting times as well as virtual participation. Partner contributions were used to adapt processes throughout the project. Compensation for time and expertise was provided in alignment with Patient‐Centered Outcomes Research Institute guidelines to ensure equity and respect for partner contributions.

Comparative Effectiveness Informational Webinars

To ensure a consistent foundational understanding across consortium members, 3 webinars were held during summer 2022 covering key components of comparative effectiveness research, including patient‐centered outcomes research principles and methods for patient engagement. 16 , 17

Phase 2: Consult and Conceptualize

Questionnaire Design

Two surveys (1 for patients and 1 for HCPs) were designed to identify gaps and needs across 4 key pillars: access to clinical care, patient and HCP education, mental health, and research opportunities. Project overview and details of survey design have been described previously. 16 , 17

Phase 3: Conceptualize, Generate Questions, and Prioritize Questions

Patient‐Powered Research Conference

A 1‐day, in‐person summit engaged patients, clinicians, researchers, and advocates in shaping research priorities. The summit included patient testimonials and panel discussions and facilitated breakout groups focused on (1) patient and provider education, (2) clinical care, (3) health care advocacy, and (4) PCOR. 18

Virtual Focus Groups

To ensure adequate representation, the in‐person meeting was followed by 3 virtual focus groups (1 HCP, 2 patient‐focused) to explore the prioritized topics with groups not present at the in‐person meeting. A virtual format was chosen to maximize flexibility, efficiency, and inclusion of geographically dispersed participants. 18

Transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following the steps outlined by Braun and Clarke. 19 , 20 NVivo 14 (Lumivero, Denver, CO), a qualitative data analysis software, was used to facilitate data management and coding. The detailed methods, transcript summaries, and qualitative analysis for both the in‐person meeting and the virtual focus groups have been described previously. 18

Phase 4: Prioritize Questions

Road Map Development

Following the distribution of results, 2 consortium meetings were held for consensus building and writing group formation. Participants reviewed all themes and codes generated in phase 3, independently ranked priority areas, and participated in facilitated discussions to reach consensus on the final domain structure. While mental health priorities emerged across all activities and were elevated into a distinct domain, advocacy‐related needs were integrated across all domains due to their cross‐cutting nature.

A detailed description of consortium formation and membership has been published. A total of 22 patient partners, 4 advocacy group representatives, and 20 clinician–scientist partners attended monthly meetings held from July 2022 to December 2023. Needs Assessment Survey (Phase 2)

The patient survey received 828 responses (age range, 18–60 years; 26.8% had not experienced pregnancy) representing 48 US states, and the HCP survey received 218 responses; results have been summarized previously. 15 Common themes between patients and providers were identified across all pillars, including the need for improved access to specialized care, improved care coordination, increased patient and provider education, and resources for mental health care.

Patient‐Powered Research Summit and Virtual Focus Groups (Phase 3)

The in‐person, patient‐powered research summit was held in Washington, DC, on June 20, 2023. In total, 41 participants attended, including 12 patient/family representatives, 12 health care providers (pediatric and adult cardiologists, maternal–fetal medicine specialists, and nursing), 2 health care administrators, 8 clinician–scientists, and 7 patient/professional advocates. The top 3 priorities from each group are illustrated in Figure 2. Virtual focus groups included participants who were underrepresented at the in‐person meeting, including rural‐dwelling patients, individuals from racial and ethnic minority backgrounds, and providers from low‐resource community hospitals. These groups added important nuance, highlighting gaps in communication, health literacy, and education. 18

Road Map Development (Phase 4)

From the data gathered, key goals were generated across all domains (Table 1). There were several cross‐cutting goals, including (1) improving access to specialized CHD, cardio‐obstetrics, and mental health care; (2) improving quality of CHD and cardio‐obstetric care; and (3) increasing patient and community engagement. These goals were incorporated with direct feedback from patients and HCPs from phase 3 (Table 2) to develop a strategic framework with short‐ and long‐term goals for PCOR addressing all domains (Table 3). Five central themes were established: improved communication within the health care setting, increased engagement with communities, improved ancillary clinical support (in addition to the regular clinical care team), incorporation of patient‐reported outcomes into longitudinal data sets, and expanded implementation and educational initiatives.

Short‐term (years 1–3) priorities across all domains focused on reviewing existing resources and practices, identifying gaps, and developing solutions in partnership with patients. Long‐term (years 5–10) priorities shifted toward implementation, evaluation, and sustainable strategies to achieve system‐level transformation. Results by domains are summarized below, and detailed project ideas are presented in Table 3.

Patient and HCP Education

Short‐term priorities included assessing preferred educational formats (eg, videos, social media, and applications) and topics of need among patients and HCPs, evaluating existing educational materials to identify gaps in content for CHD and pregnancy, and engaging multidisciplinary partners to align educational content with patient needs. Long‐term priorities emphasize developing and disseminating reproductive health educational tools that reflect patient preferences and conducting large‐scale evaluations to ensure that these tools are accessible and effective across diverse patient populations. If found effective, the tools will be translated into additional languages and disseminated nationally with support from professional networks to promote standardized provider education.

Clinical Care and Outcomes

In the short term, participants prioritized improving clinical care and outcomes by identifying existing pregnancy‐related CHD care practices, developing tools to support CHD‐specific care coordination (eg, centralized clinical records), and expanding telemedicine to increase access for rural and underserved patients. They also highlighted barriers such as transportation challenges, limited health literacy, inconsistent counseling, and difficulties navigating the health care system. Participants expressed interest in evaluating care coordination models, including the potential role of dedicated clinical care coordinators. In the long term, priorities focused on expanding coordinated regional systems of cardio‐obstetric care, integrating care coordination roles as standard components of cardio‐obstetric teams, improving access through telemedicine and outreach clinics, and incorporating patient‐reported outcomes and digital monitoring tools into care planning.

Patient‐Centered Outcomes Research

Short‐term priorities included a systematic review of communication strategies, a national mixed‐methods study of local care barriers, development of protocols to evaluate clinical care coordinators in cardio‐obstetrics clinics, and a scoping review of CHD‐and‐pregnancy resources, with patient‐centered feedback guiding refinement of care and educational strategies. Long‐term priorities encompassed refining risk stratification tools by incorporating patient‐reported outcomes; developing peer‐led research training models to increase patient participation in research; integrating mental health resources into cardio‐obstetric care; and recognizing nonclinical support roles, such as doulas, to enhance patient experience. 21

Mental Health

In the first 3 years, efforts will focus on embedding mental health screening into clinical care pathways, including prenatal and postpartum assessment of anxiety and depressive symptoms for individuals with CHD and their partners. By year 3, screening prompts will be integrated into electronic health records, and referral systems will be formalized to ensure timely access to perinatal mental health support. Long‐term priorities include integrating mental health professionals and doulas into cardio‐obstetric teams to provide timely perinatal mental health care and comprehensive support, which has been shown to reduce stress, anxiety, and postpartum depression, particularly in high‐risk and underserved populations. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

Discussion

This work provides the first patient‐centered road map to guide research, clinical care, and system‐level improvements for pregnant individuals with CHD. Across all phases, patients and health care providers consistently emphasized gaps in access to specialized cardio‐obstetric care, communication challenges, limited educational resources, and insufficient mental health support. These shared priorities formed the basis of a comprehensive framework detailing short‐ and long‐term goals to improve maternal outcomes and advance patient‐centered research.

This study builds on existing literature by offering a comprehensive, patient‐centered road map to improve reproductive and maternal health outcomes for individuals with CHD. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 In contrast with earlier studies that primarily emphasize descriptive analyses of HCPs or patient input, this project distinguishes itself by integrating patients as equal partners throughout the research and road map development process. The use of both patient‐ and provider‐led facilitation in summit sessions and breakout groups also reflects a strong commitment to coproduction of research and solutions. Including patients in the process was critical to determining solutions that can be implemented effectively while also addressing areas of greatest impact to improving long‐term health outcomes.

In addition to the inclusion of patients as key decision makers, key strengths of this study included the methodological breadth and commitment to equity and inclusion. Through its multiphase approach, which included national surveys, in‐person research summits, virtual focus groups, and iterative consensus‐building, the project captured the lived experiences of patients from diverse backgrounds, including rural populations and those with poor access to care. This allowed for the identification of systemic access barriers (eg, geographic isolation, insurance limitations, and subspecialist shortages) and the development of actionable solutions (eg, hub‐and‐spoke models, telemedicine expansion, improved care coordination strategies, and access to doula services). The findings echo the broader maternal health literature, which point to the role of social drivers of health and health outcomes and highlight the challenges patients face in navigating a complex, often inaccessible health care system. 31 , 32 , 33 While previous studies often emphasize disparities in access and outcomes, this study builds on that foundation by proposing system‐level strategies including policy changes to bridge these gaps and deliver more equitable, coordinated care for high‐risk CHD populations.

Central to these system‐level strategies is an emphasis on communication and education. Road map recommendations include a strong focus on improving communication and developing educational tools for both HCPs and patients navigating pregnancy with CHD. It highlights the need for cross‐disciplinary HCP training, particularly in cardiology, maternal–fetal medicine, and primary care. It also proposes practical solutions such as bilingual patient‐facing resources, infographics, short videos, and mobile applications. Notably, it introduces the electronic health record–integrated “Pregnancy Passport” as a tool to enhance provider–patient communication and care coordination across institutions. In addition, comprehensive contraception counseling and pregnancy risk assessment are essential components of this communication framework. Providing clear, personalized information about reproductive options and associated risks empowers patients to make informed decisions aligned with their health goals and values. 26 While prior studies and statement papers have recognized gaps in education and communication for both patients and HCPs and have suggested general recommendations for HCP training pathways, this study goes farther by offering detailed, implementation‐ready strategies. 34 , 35 These include user testing, codesign with patient and provider partners, and the integration of digital health tools to ensure that educational interventions are accessible, culturally relevant, and scalable within real‐world clinical settings.

In parallel with educational and care coordination strategies, the study also addresses a frequently overlooked dimension of maternal CHD care: mental health. Recent studies exploring patient priorities in adult CHD research have identified maternal mental health as a significant concern, especially given the psychological burden carried by individuals with CHD. 29 , 30 Psychological stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms are associated with multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, and gestational hypertension, a particular concern for individuals with CHD. Our findings also underscore the limited capacity of current health care systems to adequately address the mental health needs of this high‐risk population and support the need for integration of mental health as an independent domain within the proposed road map. Recommendations for routine perinatal mental health screening, embedded care pathways, and inclusion of support structures, such as doulas and peer support networks, illustrate systems‐level recommendations to a frequently overlooked component of care.

To ensure that these recommendations move from insight to action, the study uniquely incorporates implementation science principles. It outlines a long‐term vision that includes future comparative effectiveness research, validation of tools across diverse settings, and cost‐effectiveness analyses of proposed interventions such as care coordination and integrated mental health services. The findings support the call for the development of longitudinal cohorts, standardized data sets incorporating patient‐reported outcomes, and real‐world data to refine existing risk stratification tools. 8 , 36 , 37 This approach reflects an understanding that risk for patients with CHD is dynamic and evolves over time. Data from registries including ROPAC (Registry of Pregnancy and Cardiac Disease), helped inform the 2025 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the Management of Cardiovascular Disease and Pregnancy, further underscoring the importance of standardized longitudinal data sets. 38 , 39 , 40 Expert panel consensus statements have also called for universal cardiovascular disease risk assessment in all pregnant patients. 41 With the recent publication of the Core Outcome Set for Studies on Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy, there is strong alignment between ongoing research efforts and the patient‐prioritized outcomes identified in this study, underscoring the opportunity for synergy and sustained impact in advancing cardio‐obstetric care. 42

Despite growing interest in patient‐centered research in other clinical fields, cardio‐obstetrics has been slow to adopt these approaches. Much of the existing literature relies on retrospective cohort studies or administrative data, with limited patient involvement in shaping research priorities or interpreting findings. This study emphasizes the need for patient‐driven research, including the use of resources such as formal training programs for patients in community‐led research such as Alberta's Patient and Community Engagement Research program. 21 In addition, the findings support using patient‐reported outcomes and real‐world data, representing a novel and meaningful shift toward truly participatory and equitable research in maternal CHD care. This study not only advocates for these approaches but also actively embeds patient and community voices into research design, dissemination, and long‐term planning. The establishment of the CHD‐specific maternal health consortium during phase 1 lays a critical foundation for sustaining these efforts, positioning it as a central driver of future research, advocacy, and policy change in this high‐risk population.

Finally, participants highlighted the importance of understanding and addressing barriers to health care system navigation and access. Short‐term priorities included identifying specific advocacy needs to improve communication and coordination with HCPs. Long‐term goals prioritized advocacy for expanded adult CHD and cardio‐obstetric programs while accelerating the integration of comprehensive telehealth solutions. Current telemedicine use for maternal–fetal medicine care in rural settings is limited, with just 1.7% use in 1 recent cohort, suggesting the need for targeted policy changes, such as expanded reimbursement and infrastructure investment. 43 While initiatives like the Rural Maternal and Obstetric Management Strategies Act have laid the groundwork for telehealth expansion, they fall short in addressing the specific needs of patients with CHD, indicating a gap in federal efforts that this research helps illuminate. 44

While this study offers a robust engagement framework, it has several limitations. First, while it captures input from a broad national cohort, there is potential for response bias, as participants may be more likely to include individuals who are already engaged with advocacy networks or have reliable Internet access. As a result, underrepresented voices, including non–English speakers and those individuals disconnected from care, may not be fully reflected, despite targeted focus groups aimed at improving reach. Second, although the road map outlines detailed and actionable strategies, most of the proposed interventions are still in the conceptual or early development stages. These tools have not yet been evaluated for effectiveness or feasibility. Therefore, the next phase of this initiative will require robust piloting, implementation research, and outcome evaluation to validate and refine these approaches in real‐world clinical settings.

Although this road map includes detailed ideas across clinical care, education, mental health, advocacy, and research, these concepts represent priorities generated through patient and provider engagement rather than fully developed or evaluated interventions. This road map is intended to guide researchers by highlighting the areas of greatest concern to the patient community. As such, the current study is not intended to present implementation plans, operational pathways, or validation strategies. Future work will be needed to assess the feasibility, effectiveness, and scalability of these priorities, including their application in rural settings, integration into existing clinical workflows, incorporation of mental health services, and use of patient‐reported outcomes and educational tools. The road map presented here serves as a foundation to guide subsequent research rather than a finalized set of solutions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study builds on prior work by providing a scalable, patient‐informed road map that goes beyond problem identification to solution development. It complements existing literature by not only centering patient perspectives but also by demonstrating how sustained patient engagement can drive successful implementation. Ultimately, the study calls for a collaborative approach among clinicians, researchers, patients, advocates, and policymakers to build a health care system that is more coordinated, accessible, and responsive to the complex and evolving needs of this growing population.
