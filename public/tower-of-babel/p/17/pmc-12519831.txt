Evaluation of dynamic brain function in patients with different degrees of chronic pain by EEG microstate analysis
Authors: Yi-qi-yuan Zhang, Ya-ru Li, Qi Han, Fei Gao, Yi Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic pain is closely associated with functional remodeling of the central nervous system. The neuroplasticity in the brain can be characterized by the electroencephalography (EEG) microstate analysis of the static topological features of resting-state functional brain networks.MethodsSeventy-four patients undergoing spinal surgery for chronic pain were included. Preoperative resting-state EEG recordings were obtained, and patients were stratified into two groups according to their preoperative Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores. From the EEG data, four specific microstates (labeled A, B, C, and D) were extracted, and their temporal characteristics were analyzed. Statistical analysis was performed to compare the microstate characteristics between the two groups, and to examine the correlation between microstate characteristics and pain intensity.ResultsIn the high-pain (HP) group, microstate D exhibited significantly reduced occurrence frequency, time coverage, and duration compared with the low-pain (LP) group. In addition, differences in microstate transition patterns were observed between the two groups. A significant correlation was also observed between the temporal characteristics of microstate D and the preoperative pain intensity, as measured by the NRS.ConclusionAlterations in microstate dynamics during the preoperative resting state may reflect changes in brain functions among patients with chronic pain. EEG microstate analysis may be recommended for identifying changes in brain dynamics associated with chronic pain.Trial registrationThis study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov prior to patient enrollment (Registration number: NCT03761576; Registration date: November 30, 2018).

Introduction

Chronic pain, occurring in about 10–30% of clinical cases, often arises from physical or psychological trauma [1, 2], adversely impacting patients’ quality of life, psychological well-being, and prognosis. Severe pain often prolongs hospitalization and increases the overall cost of medical treatment [3, 4]. In addition, postoperative pain is closely associated with preoperative chronic pain, highlighting the clinical significance of early pain evaluation in patients receiving surgeries [5, 6].

A growing body of evidence links chronic pain to structural and functional alterations in the brain. A low-frequency spectral activity in resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) may indicate the development of chronic pain. For example, compared with patients experiencing pain relief, those with persistent chronic pain show reduced theta- and low-beta-band oscillations in the EEG. Furthermore, differences are observed in the beta-band node intensities in several brain regions, including the right insula, primary somatosensory cortex (S1), bilateral medial frontal cortex, secondary somatosensory cortex (S2), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), between patients with episodic and chronic migraine [7, 8]. These findings suggest that EEG may serve as a tool to assess pain and manifest the functional changes that pain induces in the brain [8, 9]. Various factors, such as high pain sensitivity, negative emotions, and preoperative pain, can influence functional brain activity. By investigating the alterations in various EEG indicators, we can gain insights into changes in brain function. A deeper understanding of these changes not only enhances our comprehension of the pathophysiology and underlying mechanisms of pain but also holds the potential to promote the development of reliable biomarkers for pain [10, 11].

Among EEG-based techniques, the microstate analysis has been well recognized for investigating the dynamic changes in the brain under high-frequency activities [12]. Resting-state EEG shows that the functional brain network is typically classified into four to six microstates, and their changes may reflect the abnormalities in functions, such as vision, hearing, and attention [13, 14]. Recent studies have revealed differences in microstate patterns between patients with chronic pain and healthy individuals. For instance, May et al. have found that microstate D occurs less frequently in patients with chronic pain, compared to healthy controls [15]. Similarly, another study reports the lower occurrence and time coverage of microstate C in patients with extensive chronic pain [14]. Therefore, a microstate analysis can provide valuable information about the brain activities in patients with chronic pain.

Microstate analysis is one of the mature techniques for studying changes in brain function. Currently, studies on microstate alterations in patients with chronic pain are limited and often report inconsistent findings. There also lacks research focusing on how EEG microstates vary with the intensity of pain. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the dynamic changes in brain function among patients with chronic pain through an EEG microstate analysis.

Materials and methods

This clinical study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov prior to patient enrollment (Registration number: NCT03761576; Registration date: November 30, 2018). Please note that the registry entry was subsequently revised based on our previous study [16], due to similarities in study design and research objectives.

Study participants

This study represented a re-analysis of previous data collected at Peking University People’s Hospital (Beijing, China), which had been used to investigate whether preoperative resting-state electrophysiological signals could predict pain [17]. Between November 2020 and March 2021, 100 patients who consented to undergo lumbar spinal surgery at the hospital were recruited.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) an education level above secondary school; (2) an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I to III; and (3) a preference to postoperative administration of patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA). Exclusion criteria included: (1) cerebrovascular diseases; (2) psychiatric disorders or a family history of psychiatric illness; (3) a history of traumatic brain injury or prior craniotomy; (4) preoperative opioid use; and (5) a planned postoperative transfer to the intensive care unit for further observation and treatment.

Based on these criteria (see Fig. 1), 19 patients were excluded: 10 due to a history of neurological disorders, 4 with psychiatric histories, 2 with opioid dependence, and 3 who declined to provide informed consent. The additional 7 patients were excluded due to suboptimal quality of EEG data. As a result, a total of 74 patients were enrolled in the present study. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort are presented in Table 1. According to the definition revised by the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) in 2020 [18], chronic pain was defined as pain that persists or recurs for more than three months. A score greater than 3 on the NRS was considered clinically significant and warranted therapeutic intervention.

Based on this definition and established epidemiological classification criteria, the patients were stratified into two groups: (1) a high pain (HP) group, with NRS scores ranging from 4 to 10; and (2) a low pain (LP) group, with NRS scores from 0 to 3 [18–20]. On the NRS, a score of 0 indicates no pain, whereas a score of 10 corresponds to the most severe pain imaginable.

Study design

Preoperative pain intensity was assessed using the NRS and recorded prior to the surgical procedure. The study protocol consisted of four sequential phases, as illustrated in Fig. 1. On the day before surgery, the patients signed informed consent forms and underwent preoperative pain evaluation. Two hours before surgery, the participants completed the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) to assess their pre-surgical anxiety and depression levels. Following the HADS assessment, resting-state EEG data were collected prior to any medication administration. EEG acquisition procedures are described in the “EEG Data Recording” section.

Postoperative pain assessments were performed on postoperative day 1 (POD1), day 3 (POD3), and day 7 (POD7), as summarized in Table 1. During each assessment, patients rated the intensity of the most severe postoperative pain experienced in the preceding 24 h using an 11-point NRS, where 0 represents no pain and 10 denotes the worst pain imaginable.

During the final study phase, postoperative pain was assessed via telephone follow-up at postoperative weeks 4, 8, and 12 (denoted as POW4, POW8, and POW12 in Table 1). Patients reported the intensity of the most severe pain experienced over the preceding week using the same 11-point NRS, thereby ensuring a consistency in pain assessment methods. Postoperative pain was defined as pain induced by surgery at the incision site or other anatomically relevant sites.

To minimize variability in pain perception, both preoperative and postoperative NRS assessments were conducted in patients at rest, which largely reduced the potential influence of physical activity. Chronic postoperative pain was defined according to the four-point persistent pain classification proposed by McRae [21]. All data were collected and recorded by the same investigator, and participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time, for any reason.

Anaesthetic management protocol

General anesthesia was induced one hour before surgery in accordance with institutional guidelines to ensure procedural safety. Induction agents comprised propofol (1-2.5 mg/kg), midazolam (0.02–0.04 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.5-1 mg/kg). Anesthesia was maintained through 1-1.5% sevoflurane inhalation, along with continuous intravenous infusions of propofol (0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min) and remifentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg/min).

Additional doses of sufentanil and rocuronium were administered as needed, with a cumulative sufentanil exposure capped at 0.6 µg/kg. For patients without contraindications, a single intravenous dose of flurbiprofen axetil (100 mg) was administered at 30 min before the end of surgery. In addition, a continuous infusion of flurbiprofen axetil (8 mg/h) was maintained postoperatively.

All patients received PCIA with sufentanil. The PCIA device was set with a background rate of 3 µg/h, a bolus dose of 3 µg, a lockout interval of 15 min, and a maximum limit of 15 µg/h.

EEG data recording and preprocessing

EEG was performed in a quiet, comfortable environment using a 32-channel electrode cap positioned according to the international 10–20 system (Compumedics Neuroscan, Australia). Data were acquired using a NuAmps amplifier (Model 7181) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, with a bandpass filter at a frequency between 0.01 and 100 Hz [22]. The ground electrode was placed 10 mm anterior to Fz, and the right mastoid electrode served as the online reference; electrode impedance was maintained below 10 kΩ throughout recordings. The dada were preprocessed in MATLAB using the EEGLAB open-source toolbox. EEG signals were first bandpass-filtered at a frequency between 1 and 80 Hz, and industrial frequency within the 48–52 Hz range was removed [23, 24].

An independent component analysis (ICA) using the runica algorithm was then applied to correct for ocular and myogenic artifacts. Artifact components associated with eye movements and muscle activity were identified based on characteristic temporal and spatial features, and subsequently removed from the bandpass-filtered (1–80 Hz) EEG data. Voltage fluctuations exceeding ± 100 µV were marked for exclusion, including 200 ms before and after each artifact event [25]. All data underwent visual inspection to manually identify and remove residual artifact segments, including those with high-amplitude fluctuations. Damaged electrode channels were repaired via interpolation; however, the datasets with more than three impaired channels were excluded from subsequent analysis. Only continuous, artifact-free EEG epochs over at least 120 s were retained. The pre-processed EEG data were then transformed to average reference [26].

Microstate analysis

Microstate analysis was implemented via two-step k-means clustering. In the first stage, the clustering at the individual level was conducted to segment EEG recordings and derive representative template topographies. In the second stage, individual templates were aggregated to generate microstate templates at the group level, which were then back-fitted to each participant’s EEG data to extract microstate sequences.

The detailed workflow included: Calculation of the global field power (GFP) time series to identify peaks reflecting EEG topographic variability. A threshold of two standard deviations was applied to minimize noise, retaining only valid topographic features. An enhanced k-means clustering algorithm was applied to the GFP peak topographies using non-polarity clustering (k = 4, maximum 100 iterations). Clustering centers were iteratively updated until convergence, thus maximizing global explanatory variance (GEV) and minimizing cross-validation error [27]. All individual topographic maps were aggregated into a unified dataset, followed by a second step of kmeans clustering to establish group-level templates. Previous studies have considered microstates A-D as a classical four-microstate model for microstate analysis. This classification method is closely related to known brain functional systems and better reflects core resting-state networks and neural mechanisms [12, 26, 28]. Accordingly, the clustering results of each individual were integrated twice to create a cross-subject standardized microstate template (MS A/B/C/D), which was then labeled into a classical fourstate model. The resulting group-level templates were back-fitted to individual EEG signals to generate microstate sequences. Microstate features were quantified across four domains: duration, occurrence frequency, time coverage, and transition probabilities between states. A schematic diagram illustrating the complete microstate analysis workflow is presented in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline demographic data and pain scores. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate associations between temporal microstate parameters (occurrence frequency, time coverage, duration), probability of metastasis, and preoperative pain intensity measured by NRS. To determine the differences between the HP and LP groups in microstate metrics and transition probabilities, twosided independentsamples ttests were conducted under both frequentist and Bayesian frameworks using JASP version 0.19.3 [29] and SPSS version 23.0 (IBM). The alpha level for frequentist testing was set at 0.05. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables exhibiting normal distribution were analyzed with ttests; non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Group differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the chisquare test. For p-values of temporal measures, resampling-based the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) across the 4 microstates and the 3 different temporal measures, resulting in a correction for 12 statistical tests per re-run. For the transition matrix of the representative rerun, FDR correction was performed across all 12 transitions. Adjusted p-values are reported throughout and two-tailed p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant [30]. Results were presented in terms of two-sided Bayes factors (BF₁₀), t-values, and adjusted p-values. For BF₁₀ effect size estimation, the median and 95% credible interval of the posterior δ distribution were reported.

Results

Our analysis revealed that the resting-state brain activity varied in patients with varying degrees of chronic pain, suggesting that such variations may reflect alterations in the brain’s functional network.

Demographic and clinical characteristic

All included patients completed the follow-up sessions. Based on preoperative NRS scores, 42 patients with scores greater than 3 were categorized into the HP group, while 32 patients with scores between 1 and 3 were classified as the LP group. Table 1 summarizes general demographic data, preoperative anxiety and depression scores, as well as pre- and postoperative NRS pain ratings. No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in age, ASA physical status classification, or HADS scores. All patients had low preoperative HADS subscale scores (each < 6) for preoperative depression and anxiety. Preoperative NRS scores were significantly higher in the HP group than in the LP group. Following the surgery, the HP group continued to report higher pain scores than the LP group at the same postoperative time points, with statistically significant differences observed on POD 3 (p < 0.001) and POW 8 (p < 0.05).

Temporal characteristics of microstates

Based on previous studies [13, 31, 32], representative microstates in both groups were labeled as microstates A through D, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. The microstate models accounted for 80.05% and 79.08% of interindividual variance in the HP and LP groups, respectively, aligning with prior findings [33, 34].

Independent-samples t-tests revealed that in the HP group, microstate D (MS-D) exhibited significantly lower occurrence frequency, time coverage, and duration, compared to that in the LP group (see Table 2.; Fig. 3B; all Bayes factors [BF10] > 100; all FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.001). Additionally, we calculated transition probabilities between microstates for each group (Table 3.). Compared with the LP group, patients in the HP group showed a reduced probability of transitioning from microstate A to D, and an increased probability of transitioning from D to A (Table 3.: A→D, p = 0.041, BF10 = 1.624; D→A, p = 0.048, BF10 = 1.198; all p-values were FDR-adjusted). Furthermore, the transition probability from microstate C to B was also higher in the HP group (Table 3.: C→B, p = 0.029, BF10 = 1.989). Although these transition probabilities reached statistical significance in the frequentist framework (p < 0.05), Bayesian analysis provided only weak evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis (1 < BF10 < 3).

Relationships between temporal microstate characteristics and clinical characteristics

Having observed the alterations in microstate D, we further investigated the associations between its temporal characteristics and clinical indicators. To this end, we performed correlation analyses between preoperative pain intensity and the temporal parameters of microstate D. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that the duration and occurrence frequency of microstate D exhibited the strongest negative correlations with preoperative pain NRS scores (r = −0.679, p < 0.001; r = −0.639, p < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

This study investigated whether preoperative resting-state EEG microstate characteristics could serve to evaluate preoperative pain in patients undergoing spinal surgery. Through analyzing the EEG data from patients with varying levels of chronic pain, we obtained deeper insights into potential alterations in their functional brain networks. These objective, quantifiable indicators may bring about a new mode of chronic pain assessment in clinical scenarios [12, 13].

Across different levels of preoperative pain, the HP group exhibited modest reductions in occurrence frequency, spatial coverage, and duration of MS-D compared to the LP group. Encouragingly, MS-D duration and occurrence frequency showed the strongest negative correlations with preoperative pain measured by the NRS. Based on synchronous EEG-functional magnetic resonance imaging (EEG-fMRI) recordings, Britz et al. have applied a general linear model to investigate the relationship between microstate activity and fMRI activation. They report that each of the four canonical microstates corresponds to a distinct brain functional network, with MS-D specifically linked to the network of attention and associated cognitive functions [13, 32]. Therefore, the activity of MS-D may partially reflect the function of the attention network. It is related to the reduction of cognitive efficiency or the change of the dynamics of the attention network. The attention network governs the selection, concentration, and maintenance of information and allocates attentional and cognitive resources accordingly. Previous research has demonstrated that patients with chronic pain exhibit cognitive impairments, particularly in attention-shifting processes. MS-D is associated with the activity in multiple neural substrates, including the dorsal attention control network [35], the frontoparietal region [36, 37], and the regions involved in attentional transfer [38–40].

Based on prior findings, we postulate that pain may compete with other environmental stimuli for limited cognitive resources. Consequently, changes in the attention network may predispose patients to excessive focus (or rumination) on their pain. This sustained attention may, in turn, amplify perceived pain intensity. Therefore, the attenuation of MSD may reflect dynamic alterations in the attention network among patients experiencing chronic pain. Recent experimental studies involving persistent pain induction have demonstrated significant correlations between MSD temporal characteristics (duration and time coverage) and perceived pain intensity [41]. For instance, following capsaicin-induced pain, both the occurrence frequency and duration of MSD increase, and duration shows a positive association with perceived pain intensity. Concurrently, persistent pain impairs the global integrity and efficiency of the network associated with MS-D. Additional studies have also reported lower frequencies of MS-D in patients with chronic pain [15, 42]. These findings suggest that MS-D dynamics may shift following pain onset. Upon an acute nociceptive stimulation, the frequency of MS-D increases and its duration prolongs. As widely reported in patients with chronic pain, compared to healthy individuals, persistent nociceptive input competes with other stimuli for limited cognitive resources and impairs higher-order attentional control mechanisms [43, 44]. As the functional integration and efficiency decline within the MS-D network, the signals of MS-D activity decrease on EEG images [15]. In the present study, patients in the HP group exhibited modest reductions in occurrence frequency, time coverage, and duration of MS-D compared to those in the LP group, consistent with prior findings. Importantly, both MS-D duration and occurrence showed the strongest correlations with preoperative pain NRS scores, further supporting the association between MS-D dynamics and pain intensity.

Furthermore, we observed that patients with higher preoperative NRS scores were more likely to experience severer postoperative pain compared to those with lower preoperative scores. This finding is consistent with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis on the relationship between preoperative pain and both acute and chronic postoperative pain. Preoperative pain intensity, sensitivity, and duration have all been reported to influence the severity of postoperative pain [45].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study focusing on a single disease type, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to broader populations or clinical settings. Second, postoperative EEG data were not collected, and the dynamics of EEG signals with the continuation of pain was not evaluated. This limitation hinders a deeper investigation into how pain influences brain function and how functional brain networks adapt during the perioperative period.

Conclusion

Our research has revealed the relationship between EEG MS-D dynamics and pain intensity, and these changes may be related to reduce cognitive efficiency and changes in function of the attention network. These findings add a new understanding about the pathophysiology of chronic pain and suggest that MS-D may be evaluated to catch dynamic changes in the brain functional network, and provided potential markers for identifying the dynamic changes in the brain during chronic pain.
