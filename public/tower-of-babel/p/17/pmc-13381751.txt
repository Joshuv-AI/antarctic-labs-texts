Viral pneumonia detection during the COVID-19 pandemic using deep learning and DCGAN-based data augmentation
Authors: Betelhem Zewdu Wubineh, Andrzej Rusiecki, Krzysztof Halawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning has shown strong potential for automated chest X-ray analysis in the diagnosis of respiratory diseases. However, accurate detection of viral pneumonia remains challenging due to its radiographic similarity with COVID-19 and limited labeled data. In this study, we aim to detect viral pneumonia in chest X-ray images using CNNs, MobileNet, and an ensemble approach, with DCGAN-based data augmentation to address dataset limitations. A total of 317 real chest X-ray images were used, and 1,500 synthetic images were generated using DCGAN to enhance training diversity. Two experimental setups were conducted: (1) training and testing on original data only, and (2) training on combining real and synthetic data with evaluation on real test images. In the second experiment, the improved CNN achieved 92.4% accuracy, MobileNet reached 96.97%, and the ensemble method attained 96.97%. The results demonstrate that DCGAN-based augmentation combined with ensemble learning improves classification performance and robustness. The proposed approach provides an effective tool for supporting automated viral pneumonia detection from chest X-ray images during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)1–3, has had a profound impact on global health systems. While COVID-19 is the primary focus of pandemic-related diagnostics, other respiratory illnesses, particularly viral pneumonia, remain a significant health concern during this period. Viral pneumonia shares several clinical symptoms and radiographic patterns with COVID-19, such as cough, fever, shortness of breath, and diffuse lung infiltrates4–6. These similarities make accurate differentiation critical for patient management and treatment. Chest X-ray (CXR) and Computed Tomography (CT) imaging are widely used to evaluate lung conditions7. While CT scans can offer detailed anatomical views, CXRs are more accessible, faster to acquire, and cost-effective, making them suitable for large-scale screening during a pandemic8,9.

Deep learning (DL) methods have shown remarkable success in automating image classification tasks when sufficient labeled data are available10–12. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are especially effective for extracting high-level features from medical images, and transfer learning (TL)13 with models pre-trained on large datasets like ImageNet have been widely used to improve performance on smaller medical datasets14,15.

Recent advances in machine learning and DL have significantly improved medical image analysis, particularly for disease detection and classification tasks. Various studies have explored hybrid architecture, ensemble learning strategies, and optimized feature extraction methods to enhance diagnostic accuracy in medical imaging. For instance, DL frameworks integrating optimization algorithms and feature selection techniques have demonstrated strong performance in medical classification tasks. Similarly, hybrid and ensemble-based DL models have been applied to improve robustness in medical diagnostics16. Recent research has also focused on improving generalization in medical image analysis using advanced learning strategies and improved network architecture. In addition, explainable and ensemble-based DL approaches have been introduced to enhance interpretability and performance in healthcare applications17. Furthermore, studies on biomedical image classification and decision support systems have demonstrated the effectiveness of DL in clinical applications18 Research has also explored lightweight neural architectures for efficient medical diagnosis in resource-constrained environments19, as well as optimized convolutional neural network frameworks for improved classification accuracy in medical imaging tasks. Recent works such as Rashed and Bahgat20, Gao et al.21, Nauman et al.22 further demonstrate advances in CNN, diffusion models, and TL for COVID-19 detection. However, these studies mainly rely on either large datasets, multimodal inputs, or standalone architectures, whereas our approach focuses on data-scarce, imbalanced viral pneumonia classification using DCGAN-based augmentation combined with a lightweight ensemble model.

Several studies have addressed COVID-19 and pneumonia detection using CXRs. Gopatoti and Vijayalakshmi conducted research on X-ray images for the detection of COVID-19. They utilized deep convolutional neural networks in combination with the grey-wolf optimizer feature selection technique, achieving an accuracy of 97.05% for three classes2. Showkat and Qureshi achieved 95% accuracy using the ResNet model8. George et al., conducted research on the identification of COVID-19 by applying the contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) with a homomorphic transformation filter to extract features from X-ray images. Their method resulted in an accuracy of 96.56% when using VGG for multi-class classification9. Kong and Cheng employed feature fusion techniques, combining DenseNet and VGG16, and achieved an accuracy of 97.3% in a three-category classification23. Kuzinkovas and Clement conducted an ensemble of pre-trained convolutional neural networks (CNNs), including ResNet50, VGG19, and VGG16, and incorporated GLCM textural features, achieving an accuracy of 94.68% in multi-class classification24. Ren et al. conducted research using a ResNet50 backbone combined with a transformer to capture long-distance features, resulting in an accuracy of 97.65% for COVID-19 detection25. Puttagunta et al. worked on DCGAN-based data augmentation for the detection of COVID-19. They utilized VGG-16, InceptionV3, and MobileNet models, achieving accuracy scores of 93.1%, 95.9%, and 91.4%, respectively26. Motamed et al. used IAGAN for COVID-19 detection and achieved an 80% accuracy rate27. Waheed et al., performed data augmentation using ACGAN and CNN techniques, resulting in a 95% accuracy28.

Despite these advancements, most existing studies primarily focus on either COVID-19 detection or general pneumonia classification, with limited attention given to the specific differentiation of viral pneumonia from COVID-19 and normal cases under severely limited and imbalanced datasets. Moreover, existing approaches typically address data augmentation, feature extraction, or model design as separate components rather than as an integrated solution. As a result, challenges related to class imbalance, limited data availability, and model generalization remain insufficiently addressed in a unified framework. In addition, while GAN-based augmentation and TL models such as MobileNet have shown promising results individually, their combined impact in improving classification robustness under constrained medical datasets is still underexplored. To address this gap, this study proposes a unified framework that integrates DCGAN-based data augmentation to mitigate class imbalance with an ensemble of a lightweight CNN and MobileNet to enhance feature extraction and generalization performance. Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop an integrated DCGAN-based augmentation and ensemble learning framework for accurate viral pneumonia detection from chest X-ray images under limited and imbalanced data conditions. Results

The experiments were implemented in the Jupyter Notebook environment using Python scripts with the Keras DL framework. The dataset consisted of 317 real images and 1,500 DCGAN-generated images, categorized into three classes: Viral Pneumonia, COVID-19, and Normal. The primary aim of these experiments was to assess the ability of our models to accurately detect viral pneumonia during the COVID-19 pandemic while maintaining strong performance across all classes. We evaluated three architectures: the improved CNN (convolutional layers replaced with separable convolutions), MobileNet, and an ensemble of both. Two scenarios were considered: original data only, training and testing solely on real images, and augmented data, training on a combination of real training images and DCGAN-generated images, with testing performed exclusively on real images.

The DCGAN used for data augmentation was tuned for high-quality image generation. The Generator contained three convolutional layers for up-sampling, each with batch normalization (momentum = 0.9) and ReLU activations, and a tanh activation at the output. The Discriminator used four convolutional layers for down-sampling, batch normalization, LeakyReLU activations, and a sigmoid output. Training was conducted for 1000 epochs with a dropout rate of 0.25 in the discriminator.

Then, the same models were trained using a combination of real and DCGAN-generated data. In addition to the percentage split evaluation, we also applied 5-fold cross-validation to obtain a more reliable and unbiased estimate of model performance. In this method, the entire dataset is randomly divided into five equal subsets (5-folds). During each iteration, four folds are used for training the model, while the remaining one-fold is used for validation. This process is repeated five times so that each fold is used exactly once as the validation set. The final performance is computed by averaging the results across all five folds. This approach reduces the risk of overfitting to a specific train-test split and provides a more stable evaluation, especially for small datasets such as ours (251 images). The reported results in Table 1 for 5-fold cross-validation are presented as mean ± standard deviation, where the mean indicates the average performance across folds and the standard deviation reflects the model’s consistency. From the results, it can be observed that MobileNet achieved the highest and most stable performance under cross-validation, with an accuracy of 0.9422 ± 0.0264, compared to CNN (0.9009 ± 0.0404). The ensemble model also demonstrated strong generalization capability with competitive performance, confirming the robustness of combining CNN and MobileNet predictions.

To statistically compare the performance of CNN and MobileNet, a paired t-test was conducted on the accuracy values obtained from 5-fold cross-validation. This test was used to determine whether the observed difference in mean accuracy between the two models is statistically significant across the same data splits. The results of the test showed a t-statistic of 1.6963 and a p-value of 0.1651. Since the p-value is greater than the significance level of 0.05, the difference in performance between CNN and MobileNet is not statistically significant. This indicates that although MobileNet achieved a slightly higher average accuracy, the improvement is not statistically reliable across different folds. Therefore, both models demonstrate comparable performance under cross-validation, and no strong evidence exists to conclude that one model significantly outperforms the other in terms of accuracy.

Discussions

The availability of sufficient, balanced data is critical for effective training of CNNs, which rely on learning discriminative visual features from images. However, most publicly available chest X-ray datasets are relatively small and exhibit class imbalance, particularly with respect to cases of viral pneumonia. This imbalance reduces the robustness of DL models, as they tend to overfit the majority classes while underperforming on underrepresented ones. In our study, we addressed these challenges by combining data augmentation via DCGAN with an ensemble of an improved CNN and MobileNet. Unlike training from scratch on a limited dataset, our approach generated realistic synthetic images, especially for the underrepresented viral pneumonia class, thereby balancing the dataset and improving classification accuracy. Due to limited data, a small validation split (5%) was used, which may affect the robustness of hyperparameter tuning. The Grad-CAM results suggest that the model focuses on relevant lung regions rather than background artifacts. However, clinical validation by radiologists is required to confirm the medical reliability of these interpretations.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, cross-validation was applied only to the baseline (real data) experiment, while the DCGAN-based experiment used a fixed data split to ensure strict test set isolation. Second, the absence of explicit qualitative or quantitative evaluation of GAN-generated images, such as visual inspection or FID score, as well as the lack of comparison with alternative augmentation techniques. These aspects will be explored in future work. In addition, an ensemble of weights was selected empirically rather than through systematic optimization, which could be explored in future work. Third, the model was not evaluated on external datasets, which may limit its generalizability across different clinical settings and data distributions. Future work will focus on validating the proposed approach on larger and more diverse datasets.

Materials and methods

Dataset

For this study, we used a publicly available chest X-ray dataset from Kaggle34, containing images across three classes: Viral Pneumonia, COVID-19, and Normal. A total of 317 original images were used, comprising 132 COVID-19 images, 90 normal images, and 90 viral pneumonia images. Images varied in resolution, were in grayscale, and were stored in JPEG format. Since the primary aim of this research is viral pneumonia detection during the COVID-19 pandemic, we retained all three classes in the dataset to ensure the model learns to distinguish viral pneumonia from both COVID-19 and healthy lung images. An example image from each of these three classes is displayed in Fig. 5. Viral pneumonia cases typically present a diffuse interstitial pattern in the CXR, indicating inflammation and fluid accumulation in the lung interstitial spaces. COVID-19 X-rays often display bilateral interstitial infiltrates and hazy opacities, while normal X-rays show clear lung fields without abnormal opacification.

Pre-processing technique

The dataset contained images of varying sizes, so all images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels to standardize input dimensions, reduce computational cost, and ensure uniformity across the dataset. Pixel intensity values were normalized to the range [0, 1], which improves training stability and convergence speed for DL models.

Data augmentation

Deep learning models typically require large amounts of data for robust training35. Our dataset is relatively small, especially for viral pneumonia cases, which can lead to overfitting and poor generalization. To address this, we applied a Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Network (DCGAN)-based data augmentation36,37. DCGAN consists of two main components: the Generator (G) and the Discriminator (D). The Generator’s primary task is to receive random noise (z) and generate images based on this noise, aiming to deceive the Discriminator38. Meanwhile, the Discriminator distinguishes between the fake images generated by the Generator and the real images. When X closely approximates the input data, the discriminator is expected to output a numerical value close to 1, indicating that X is distributing real data and maximizing V (G, D). However, when X is equal to G(Z), two distinct viewpoints emerge: From the discriminator’s perspective, it should recognize that the generated sample is fake and output a numerical result close to 0. This maximizes V (G, D). From the generator’s perspective, its goal is to mislead the discriminator, resulting in a numeric value close to 1 in the output. In other words, the generator is trained to fool the discriminator by minimizing V(G, D)39.

A total of 1500 images were generated using DCGAN, with 500 images for each class for model building. In DCGAN, stride convolutions were employed in the discriminator for down-sampling, and fractional-stride convolutions were used in the generator for up-sampling, eliminating the need for an entire pooling layer40. Both the generator and discriminator networks were batch-normalized. The generator utilized the ReLU activation function in all layers except the output layer, which employed tanh. LeakyReLU was used in all layers of the discriminator. The total number of datasets for real and generated images using DCGAN is summarized in Table 4.

Deep learning architecture

The reason behind using CNN is to utilize a deeper architecture, more complex spatial relationships, and features in the X-ray images. We enhanced the CNN architecture by substituting standard convolutional layers with separable convolutional layers, which reduced the number of parameters and increased the model’s efficiency. Additionally, we incorporated batch normalization to improve training stability and speed. This design improves computational efficiency while maintaining the ability to extract relevant spatial features from chest X-ray images. On the other hand, MobileNet is one of the pre-trained models and is used to solve problems when computational resources are limited. Generally, CNN is used for feature extraction, and MobileNet is for efficiency. Then the ensemble technique mitigates the weakness of the individual model and improves the overall performance to detect COVID-19.

The proposed system consists of three main stages: data augmentation, feature extraction, and classification. In the first stage, a DCGAN model is used to generate synthetic chest X-ray images to address class imbalance in the dataset. In the second stage, an improved CNN and a pre-trained MobileNet are independently trained to extract discriminative features from chest X-ray images. Finally, a decision-level fusion strategy is applied, where the probability outputs of both models are combined using weighted averaging to produce the final classification result.

CNN

CNN is a deep feedforward neural network composed of multiple layers of artificial neurons, designed to excel in processing large-scale images. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on manually extracted features from radiological images, CNNs automatically learn general features41. A typical CNN architecture comprises three layers: a convolution layer, a pooling layer, and a fully connected layer42. The convolutional and pooling layers are used to extract relevant features from the input image, followed by one or more fully connected layers that use these features to make a prediction43.

The architecture of the CNN was intentionally chosen to reduce the risk of overfitting, given the limited size of the dataset. While deeper networks can capture more complex features, they also require significantly larger datasets to generalize effectively. In this study, the shallow CNN was sufficient to extract essential spatial features from chest X-ray images, and its integration with MobileNet in the ensemble further enhanced the model’s ability to learn more complex representations. A simple CNN architecture was used as a complementary model to MobileNet. The goal was not to introduce a novel architecture, but to explore whether combining a basic CNN with a strong pre-trained model could improve performance.

MobileNet

The MobileNet architecture leverages pre-trained weights from ImageNet, loaded from TensorFlow. It utilizes depth-wise separable convolutions and was developed by Andrew G. Howard et al44.. To optimize for low latency and compact models suitable for mobile applications, two key hyperparameters were introduced: depth-wise and point-wise convolutions45. The original MobileNet architecture consists of a total of 28 layers, incorporating depth-separable convolution, batch normalization, ReLU, average pooling, and softmax layers46.

Ensemble

In this case, we have created an ensemble of two DL models: CNN and MobileNet, and combined their predictions using weighted averaging. This ensemble is implemented as a decision-level fusion method using weighted averaging (soft voting), rather than majority voting or feature-level concatenation. The ensemble method leverages the strengths of both models to enhance the overall prediction performance. To implement this method, we first load the models from their respective saved model files and generate predictions using the test data as input for the ensemble model. The combination of CNN and MobileNet is motivated by their complementary characteristics: MobileNet captures lightweight and generalized features, while the CNN focuses on learning task-specific spatial representations. Afterward, we assigned weights to each model to indicate its importance. The ensemble weights were determined empirically through preliminary experiments by testing multiple weight combinations. The configuration (0.6 for MobileNet and 0.4 for CNN) achieved the best performance among the evaluated settings. Since MobileNet demonstrated higher standalone accuracy, it was assigned a higher weight. This strategy allows the ensemble to leverage the stronger predictive capability of MobileNet while incorporating complementary features learned by the CNN. Finally, we combined the predictions by calculating a weighted average. This involved multiplying each model’s prediction by its respective weight and then summing the results of the two models.

The CNN architecture consists of two separable convolutional layers with 24 and 16 filters (3 × 3 kernel), followed by ReLU activation, batch normalization, and max-pooling layers. The extracted features are flattened and passed to a fully connected layer with 128 neurons, followed by a softmax output layer for three-class classification. MobileNet is based on a pre-trained ImageNet model using depthwise separable convolutions, consisting of 28 layers optimized for lightweight feature extraction. The ensemble model combines CNN and MobileNet predictions using weighted averaging (0.4 for CNN and 0.6 for MobileNet), selected empirically based on validation performance.

To improve model interpretability, Grad-CAM was applied to visualize the regions of chest X-ray images that contribute most to the model’s predictions. Grad-CAM uses gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer to generate heatmaps highlighting important areas47, allowing analysis of whether the model focuses on clinically relevant lung regions.

The model development is carried out using the Python programming language within the Jupyter Notebook environment. To ensure robust evaluation, 5-fold cross-validation was performed on the real training dataset (without DCGAN augmentation). This was done to assess baseline model performance under different data splits. For the DCGAN-based experiment, a fixed train-test split was maintained to ensure that the test set remained strictly independent and was not involved in the generation of synthetic images.

The models were trained using the Adam optimizer and a batch size of 32. The input image size was resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Training was performed for 200 epochs. A 5% validation split from the training data was used for hyperparameter tuning. The categorical cross-entropy loss function was used for multi-class classification.

Conclusion

This study highlights the advantages of merging different CNN architectures to improve classification performance, particularly in scenarios with limited chest X-ray data. To address the shortage of training images, we proposed a DCGAN-based data augmentation method to generate 1,500 realistic synthetic images, supplementing the 317 original images obtained from Kaggle and categorized into three classes: COVID-19, normal, and viral pneumonia. The model was trained and evaluated under two scenarios: the first used only the original data, while the second combined real and generated images for training with real images for testing. Three configurations, Improved CNN, MobileNet, and their ensemble, were assessed, with the ensemble achieving the highest performance in the second scenario, attaining accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores of 96.97%, 96.97%, 96.97%, and 96.88%, respectively. Experiments showed that setting the epoch to 200 produced the best results. These findings demonstrate that DCGAN-based data augmentation, combined with ensemble learning, can significantly enhance COVID-19 and viral pneumonia detection accuracy compared to previous studies. This method demonstrates potential for assisting automated analysis of chest X-ray images in research settings. Future work will explore the use of additional DL and pre-trained models to improve detection performance further.
