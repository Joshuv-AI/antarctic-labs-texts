Parental violent discipline and adolescent health: is there a different effect when one or both parents are involved?
Authors: Mariana Amorim, Milton Severo, Sílvia Fraga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren’s exposure to parental violence increases the risk of adverse health outcomes. We aimed to examine how combinations of maternal and paternal violent disciplinary practices at ages 7 and 13 years shape adolescents’ health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms. This cohort study included 4,610 participants from the Portuguese Generation XXI birth cohort, enrolled at birth (2005–2006) and followed up at ages 7 and 13 years. Maternal and paternal disciplinary tactics were reported using the Parent–Child Conflict Tactics Scale, assessing psychological aggression, corporal punishment, and severe physical assault. Outcomes at age 13 years included health-related behaviors (fighting and substance use) and depressive symptoms. Combined effects of maternal and paternal discipline were assessed using multiplicative and additive interaction models. Mothers used corporal punishment and severe physical assault more frequently with boys, whereas fathers used all types of violent discipline more often with boys than with girls. The use of either corporal punishment or psychological aggression by both mothers and fathers was associated with a multiplicative effect on adolescent substance use. Psychological aggression by both parents also showed an additive effect on risky health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms. The use of severe physical violence by both parents was associated with an additive interaction on health-related bahaviors and depressive symptoms. The combined use of violent discipline by fathers and mothers was associated with a multiplicative increase in the likelihood of adolescent negative health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms, suggesting a synergistic effect with significant implications for early interventions and prevention strategies.

Introduction

Parental disciplinary practices are commonly grouped into nonviolent and violent practices. Nonviolent discipline refers to strategies such as reasoning with the child, using time-out, or removing privileges. Violent disciplinary practices include psychological aggression, which encompasses verbal or symbolic behaviors intended to induce emotional distress or fear, and physical punishment, which involves the use of physical force, ranging from corporal punishment, such as shaking, hitting, or slapping, to more severe forms of physical assault, including repeated and forceful beating of a child [1].

Parenting practices play a crucial role in shaping children’s emotional and behavioral development. Despite the well-documented negative consequences of violent disciplinary practices, parents worldwide continue to use such practices to control or punish their children’s misbehavior [2–4]. The experience of violent disciplinary practices is highly prevalent among Portuguese children: at age 7 years, ∼76% experienced psychological aggression, around 74% experienced corporal punishment, and 23% endured severe physical assault [2].

Parental violent disciplinary practices, including corporal punishment and psychological aggression, have consistently been associated with a wide range of negative child outcomes, such as poor school achievement, behavioral problems, delinquency, low self-esteem, emotional difficulties, and mental health conditions [3, 5–9]. As the family environment plays a central role in shaping learning processes and resilience skills, exposure to violence within this context has lasting consequences. Children raised in violent family environments are more likely to engage in substance use during adolescence, often as a strategy to reduce tension and cope with the stress arising from such experiences [10–12]. At the neurobiological level, early life adversity alters neural circuitry involved in cognitive control and emotional processing, particularly in networks regulating threat and reward responses. These alterations are associated with heightened emotional reactivity, blunted reward sensitivity, and poorer emotion regulation [13]. Although these adaptations may be functional in the short term by promoting survival in adverse contexts, they tend to be maladaptive in the long term, placing these children on a risky health trajectory from an early age [13].

While children of all ages are vulnerable to the impacts of violent discipline, experiencing such practices during the first decade of life can be particularly detrimental. Childhood represents a critical period for socioemotional and brain development, during which parenting practices significantly shape behavioral and emotional outcomes. Younger children face a higher risk of physical injury and lack the cognitive capacity to fully comprehend the situation or develop coping strategies to manage distress [2, 14, 15].

Violent disciplinary practices can be understood within the broader framework of child maltreatment, which encompasses various forms of abuse and neglect inflicted by a parent or caregiver and resulting in actual or potential harm to a child’s survival, health, and development [16, 17]. Although not all disciplinary practices meet the threshold of maltreatment, more severe forms, such as harsh physical punishment, clearly fall within this category. These practices predominantly occur within the family context, which is particularly impactful given that it should ideally provide safety and protection for the child [17–22]. In this study, we specifically focus on violence directed at the child through parental disciplinary practices, rather than other forms of family or domestic violence, such as interparental violence.

Despite this evidence, an important limitation in the literature concerns how parental roles are examined. Most studies on parental discipline rely on mothers’ reports, overlooking father’s role and the combined influence of both parents’ behaviors [23]. Our previous research found that while psychological aggression and corporal punishment tend to be used by each parent regardless of the partner’s behavior, severe physical assault often reflects a shared parental decision [24]. Relying on a single parent’s perspective may underestimate children’s exposure, as mothers may be unaware of all instances of paternal violent discipline [23, 25]. Yet, the interaction and cumulative effects of maternal and paternal violent disciplinary practices on adolescent development remain underexplored [24]. To address this gap, the present study examines how combinations of maternal and paternal violent disciplinary practices during childhood (at age 7 years) and adolescence (at age 13 years) shape adolescents’ health outcomes. Thus, we hypothesize that adolescents’ health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms are influenced by the interaction between maternal and paternal violent disciplinary practices, with joint exposure to both parents’ violence expected to have a stronger impact than exposure to either parent alone.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study uses data from Generation XXI (G21), a population-based birth cohort from Portugal [26, 27]. Briefly, mothers who gave birth in a public maternity unit of Porto, Portugal, in 2005–2006 were recruited, and 8,647 infants were included in the study. All participants were invited to attend follow-up assessments at 4, 7, 10, and 13 years (participation: 86%, 80%, 74%, 54%). Trained interviewers collected information about the family and the child.

Measures of parental violence were assessed when participants were aged 7 and 13 years, and therefore, for this study, 4,610 participants were included when at least one exposure or outcome variable was present, using full information maximum likelihood (FIML). This has been shown to produce unbiased parameter estimates and standard errors under missing at random and missing completely at random [28].

Data collection

Exposure measurement: child-reported parental disciplinary practices

Parental discipline was assessed at ages 7 and 13 years using the Portuguese Parent-Child Conflict Tactics Scale-Child version (CTSPC) [1, 29], including nonviolent discipline (four items), psychological aggression (five items), corporal punishment (six items), severe physical assault (four items), and very severe physical assault (three items). Different CTSPC formats were used based on participants’ age [1]. At age 7 years, participants were asked about disciplinary practices used by each parent through a face-to-face interview conducted by a trained interviewer in a private setting. Each item was presented in pictures and interviewer read a description: “This girl’s father shook her when she did something wrong. When you do something wrong, does your father shakes you?”. If the response was affirmative, the interviewer presented a second card showing response categories, using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from never to always [2]. At age 13 years, participants self-reported, in a private setting (without the presence of the parents) and with access to a trained interviewer if assistance was needed, how often each item has occurred in the past year using an eight-point Likert scale ranging from never to >20 times [30]. For current analysis, responses were categorized as “none” if the behavior never happen or it did not happen in last year or “at least once” if a child reported that parents ever used it. Severe and very severe physical assaults were merged for analysis [2]. Dimension scores were calculated by summing items answered “at least once” (range: 0–5 for psychological aggression, 0–6 for corporal punishment, 0–7 for severe/very severe physical assault).

Outcomes’ measurement: health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms

At the 13-year follow-up, participants were asked how many times they were involved in a physical fight in the last 12 months, using an 8-point Likert scale from never to 12 or more times. Participants were considered being involved in fights if they answered “at least once” and not being involved if they answered “never.” Self-reported smoking and alcohol use (current or ever) were used to create a dichotomous variable of alcohol/tobacco use (“yes” if either was reported, “no” if neither).

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Portuguese version of the Beck Depression Inventory-II validated for adolescents aged ≥13 years. Total scores range from 0 to 63, with scores >13 indicating mild to severe symptoms [31].

Measurement of other co-variates

At age 7 years, mothers reported parental age, education, employment, marital status, household income, family structure, and number of siblings. Age was categorized as <30 years, 30–44 years, and ≥45 years; education as 9 or less, 10–12, and more than 12 years, and employment status as employed, unemployed, or other (unpaid family workers, students, retired people, house workers). Marital status was grouped as married/living with partner versus other (including divorced, separated, single, widower). Household monthly income was categorized as ≤1,000€, 1,001€–2,000€, and >2,000€. Family structure was defined as child living with both parents versus living in lone parenthood or others, and number of siblings as none, one, or more than one.

Ethics

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Baseline and follow-up evaluations were approved by Ethics Committees of the University of Porto Medical School/S. João Hospital Centre and ISPUP. Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians (and from participants at age 13 years). Study was approved by the National Data Protection Commission and complied with the EU General Data Protection Regulation under ISPUP’s Data Protection Office supervision (5833/2011). Data collection of violence experiences was performed in a private setting, without the presence of the parents or guardians, by closely supervised fieldwork-trained research assistants, following specific guidelines, as recommended by WHO.

Statistical analysis

Incident rates (IR) of disciplinary practices summary scores and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by Log Poisson. To compare differences in the proportions of health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms by sex of children, a χ2 test of independence was performed. To estimate the multiplicative effect of parental disciplinary practices (psychological aggression, corporal punishment, severe/very severe physical assault) used at ages 7 and 13 years on health-related behaviors (involvement in fights and alcohol/tobacco use) and depressive symptoms at age 13 years, odds ratios (OR) and 95% CIs were calculated using logistic regression models (Model 1). To test for multiplicative and additive interactions between mothers’ and fathers’ disciplinary practices at ages 7 and 13 years on adolescents’ health-related behaviors and depressive symptoms, several models were parameterized. “Model 2” included multiplicative two-way interaction terms, and “Model 3” included multiplicative three-way interaction terms. In this context, an OR significantly different from 1 for an interaction term indicates the presence of a multiplicative interaction (more than multiplicative if OR > 1, less than multiplicative if OR < 1). If the OR is not significantly different from 1, this suggests no evidence of multiplicative interaction, meaning the combined effect is consistent with the product of the individual effects. Models were compared using the Likelihood Ratio Test to identify the model with the best goodness of fit. “Model 4” assessed additive two-way interactions using generalized linear models with binomial distribution and identity link-function, including only the variables associated with the outcomes in Model 2. In this case, β values significantly different from 0 indicate additive interaction (more than additive if β > 0, less than additive if β < 0). Statistical significance was considered when P-value was <.05. The statistical analysis was performed using the software IBM SPSS V.25.0, and R software version 4.3.1.

Additionally, at age 7 years, Model 2 showed a multiplicative interaction between maternal and paternal psychological aggression on adolescents’ substance use, with a less than multiplicative effect (ORMother7y×Father7y: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89; 0.99). Model 4 indicated an additive effect (βMother7y×Father7y: −0.01, 95% CI: −0.01; 0.00). At age 13 years, Model 4 also showed an additive interaction, indicating that the combined effect of maternal and paternal psychological aggression was less than the sum of their individual effects (βMother13y×Father13y: −0.01, 95% CI: −0.02; −0.01).

There was no multiplicative interaction between the use of this discipline by fathers at age 7 years and mothers at age 13 years. Thus, their joint use had a multiplicative effect on adolescents’ use of tobacco and alcohol (ORMother13y×Father7y: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.92; 1.09).

Regarding corporal punishment, Model 2 did not differ significantly from Model 1 across all outcomes (Table S1), revealing no significant multiplicative interactions. Thus, according to Model 1, the use of corporal punishment by fathers at age 7 years and by mothers at age 13 years showed a multiplicative effect on adolescents’ substance use (OR Father7y: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03; 1.19; OR Mother13y: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12; 1.29).

For severe/very severe physical assaults, Model 2 showed a better fit than Model 1, indicating significant multiplicative interactions. The joint effect of maternal and paternal physical violence was smaller than the product of the independent effects (less than multiplicative effects) (ORMother13y×Father13y: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84; 0.94 for fights; ORMother13y×Father13y: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.86; 0.95 for substance use; ORMother13y×Father13y: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.81; 0.91 for depressive symptoms). Model 4 showed additive interactions across all outcomes, indicating that the combined effect of severe/very severe physical violence by both parents was less than the sum of their individual effects (Table 3). Additionally, there was no multiplicative interaction between maternal use of severe/very severe physical violence at age 7 years and paternal use at age 13 years for depressive symptoms, suggesting an additive effect across developmental periods and caregivers (Table 3).

No multiplicative three-way interactions (Model 3) were found across all outcomes (Table S1).

Discussion

This study shows that exposure to violent disciplinary practices by both parents, particularly when consistent across childhood and adolescence, is associated with increased risk of depressive symptoms and risky health-related behaviors in adolescence. These findings indicate that risk is not limited to single-parent exposure but may accumulate across parents, developmental stages, and types of violent discipline.

These results are consistent with the ACEs framework, indicating that exposure to violent disciplinary practices (psychological and physical) within the family environment is a traumatic event associated with increased risk of behavioral and emotional problems during adolescence, placing children on an unhealthy trajectory from early life that can persist and accumulate across the life course. In particular, our findings support a cumulative risk perspective, suggesting that combined exposure to maternal and paternal violent discipline has a stronger impact than exposure to a single parent.

The consistent use of severe physical violence by both parents from childhood to adolescence is often a shared parental decision, as previously demonstrated [24]. Mothers/fathers who tend to discipline their children harshly are more likely to have partners who engage in the same parenting practices [32]. Consequently, the effects of harsh discipline on behavioral problems are not limited to the actions of one parent alone [6]. Such agreement has an amplified multiplicative effect on adolescents’ outcomes. Physical punishment may hinder children’s socialization by limiting their understanding of appropriate behavior, which can negatively affect peer relationships and school adjustment [33]. Rather than improving behavior, it often has the opposite effect, reinforcing unwanted behaviors and impacting multiple areas of children’s lives [33, 34]. Additionally, although corporal punishment may lead to immediate compliance, it can escalate into more severe forms of abuse [16]. Reducing its use may help to prevent more severe forms of maltreatment [33].

Psychological aggression showed similar patterns, with both multiplicative and additive effects when consistently used by both parents. These findings are in line with previous studies reporting associations between psychological aggression (e.g. name-calling) and increased child aggression and distraction [35], reinforcing that psychological aggression can be as harmful as physical aggression, underscoring the importance for parenting programs, researchers, and advocates to advise caregivers against such practices [35].

Adverse childhood experiences, particularly those occurring in the family environment, may disrupt emotion regulation and stress-response systems, leading to increased emotional reactivity and the adoption of maladaptive coping strategies, such as substance use [22, 10–13]. The persistence of these reactive patterns and the compromise of regulatory resources [14] may consolidate a maladaptive way of responding to stress. By shaping internal models of conflict resolution, such trajectories not only impact adolescent health but may also become a pattern of social interaction in the long term, perpetuating the intergenerational cycle of violence [9, 19].

Regarding child sex differences, our results have shown that parents tend to apply different disciplinary practices depending on the child’s sex. However, child sex was not a confounding factor in the interaction models (Table S2). These results are consistent with previous studies showing that fathers use harsher physical discipline with boys than mothers do [36]. On the other hand, they differ from other research that has shown that disciplinary practices by mothers and fathers have distinct impacts on children. Both maternal and paternal physical discipline have been linked to depression and behavioral problems in children, but maternal physical discipline has shown a stronger association with depressive symptoms compared to paternal discipline [37]. Another study found that harsh discipline from both mothers and fathers was related to externalizing behaviors in children, while only fathers’ harsh verbal discipline was linked to internalizing problems [36]. The literature also highlights sex differences in how children are affected by parenting styles. Poor dyadic parenting was associated with hyperactivity and aggression for boys but not for girls at ages 6 and 7 years [38]. In this study, although boys showed more behavioral problems and girls reported more depressive symptoms, experiencing psychological or physical aggression from both parents was significantly associated with adverse outcomes in both sexes. Although these findings highlight the need for research to assess maternal and paternal disciplinary practices and their distinct effects by child’s sex, public health efforts should focus on both parents to reduce harmful discipline and promote healthy child development.

Strengths and limitations

We used data from a population-based cohort, enabling a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of disciplinary practices. Information from both maternal and paternal behaviors, reported directly by children, is an uncommon approach that provides a holistic view of the disciplinary environment while avoiding parental social desirability bias. Children’s reports also captured fathers’ practices—often missing in follow-ups—allowing analysis of both parents’ interaction effects. This approach reduces bias from relying solely on mothers’ reports and offers independent insights into the behavioral consequences of harsh parenting [6].

The 13-year follow-up was interrupted due to the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in a lower-than-expected number of participants (n = 4,640, 54.0%) and a more socioeconomically advantaged sample, as seen in other studies [39]. Compared with nonparticipants, children included in the study (n = 4,610) had older, higher-educated, employed, and married parents, belonged to households with a higher monthly income, and were more likely to live with both parents and less frequently with multiple siblings (Table 1). This selective attrition may have underestimated the prevalence and impact of severe violent discipline, which is often higher in disadvantaged families [2, 40]. Reverse causation may be a concern when parental discipline and outcomes are measured contemporaneously at age 13 years, as adolescents involved in fights may be more likely to experience parental discipline rather than the reverse. However, we also observe significant associations when parental discipline is measured at age 7 years and outcomes are assessed at age 13 years. The inclusion of earlier exposure measures at age 7 years, together with repeated assessments across development, supports a longitudinal interpretation of sustained parental violence and its association with adolescent outcomes. Additionally, variations in CTSPC formats across ages may limit comparability. To address this, following the Straus guidelines, all items were harmonized by recoding responses into a dichotomous variable (“none” vs “at least once”), and dimension scores were calculated by summing the number of items endorsed at least once. This approach ensures comparability across waves despite differences in administration format and response scale [1, 30].

Conclusions

Findings reinforce that both physical and psychological violent discipline are harmful, contributing to behavioral and emotional problems in adolescence and representing an unnecessary and ineffective approach to managing children’s behavior. By showing that exposure to such practices by both parents, particularly when consistently used across development, is associated with additive and multiplicative increases in risk, this study underscores the importance of assessing the disciplinary practices of both parents to better capture children’s exposure to violence within the family context and to provide a more accurate understanding of their impact on child health and development. From a public health perspective, results highlight the need for prevention strategies that engage both parents and address shared disciplinary norms within families, to reduce adolescents’ vulnerability and disrupt intergenerational cycles of violence.
