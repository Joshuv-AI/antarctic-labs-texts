Effects of One Season of Rugby on the Neurological Integrity of Male Adolescent Players
Authors: Stefan Henley, Nicole Spriggs, Natalia Kabaliuk, Danyon Stitt, Annette Swale, Congyu Xu, Kevin Mangan, Mike Hamlin, Rich Masters, Tracy Melzer, Arindam Basu, Tim Anderson, Deborah Snell, Adrian Clark, Keith Alexander, Samantha Holdsworth, Nick Draper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRugby union is a popular contact sport during which high impact collisions frequently occur. There is concern for the overall brain health of those playing the game, as concussion is a potential outcome of high impact collisions. Repeated sub-concussive collisions may compromise rugby players’ neurological integrity, but little is known about the effects on young brains. The brain is still developing during adolescence and may generally be more susceptible to injury, but minimal objective research data are available regarding head acceleration events experienced by junior players.ResultsForty-one adolescent male rugby players underwent pre- and post-season MRI scans and neuro-cognitive assessments. Participants were fitted with instrumented mouthguards to record head acceleration events experienced during the season. Post-season processing of MRI scans focused on within-subject analysis of pre- to post-season changes in white matter as measured by diffusion tensor imaging. Linear mixed models were used to investigate correlations between neurological changes and cumulative head impact loading recorded by the mouthguards. MRI results indicated a non-significant difference between pre- and post-season for data relating to brain structure and function, including white matter microstructure, in response to one season of contact training and match play for under-16 male rugby players, as measured by diffusion tensor imaging. These results held irrespective of level of exposure.ConclusionsOur data suggest that exposure to one season of rugby does not appear to result in neurological compromise. The statistical non-significance reported for the main outcome measure also held when controlling for variables, such as training age and headgear use. Although pre- to post-season differences were statistically non-significant, the long-term effects of high exposure may be of clinical significance going forward. Further research, particularly using longitudinal designs, is needed to further elucidate the potential for microstructural neurological changes in adolescent rugby players.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-00987-1.

Background

The neurological integrity of those playing contact sports is an issue of increasing concern worldwide [1] Research into this issue has for the most part taken place in adult, male cohorts, particularly in American Football [2] A lack of data exists for rugby, despite it being a full contact sport with wide global popularity [3]Data are particularly sparse in regard to junior and female players [2], despite under-18s representing the majority of registered players (74% in New Zealand), and female playing numbers increasing by 20% from 2022 to 2023 [4]. Repeated exposure to sub-concussive collisions is commonplace in rugby [5], which may jeopardize the neurological integrity of young people [6] It is therefore pertinent to measure these collisions and their associated head acceleration events (HAEs). Recent technological advances (namely instrumented mouthguards [iMGs]) provide a means by which to quantify the incidence and relative size of HAEs (peak linear acceleration (PLA) and peak rotational acceleration (PRA)) in rugby [2].

Repeated head impact exposure has been shown to have negative short-term [7, 8] and long-term [9] consequences in multiple collision sports, including rugby [10, 11]The types of impacts experienced by contact sport athletes affect the brain differently. Concussive HAEs typically cause immediate, acute symptoms [12], whereas sub-concussive HAEs typically do not cause immediate symptoms but can potentially lead to long-term neurological compromise if accumulated over time [13, 14] As a high impact collision sport, rugby has higher concussion incidence rates relative to other contact and non-contact sports [15]Within the wider concern surrounding chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) [16], the link between traumatic brain injury (TBI) and dementia [17], and research into negative long-term neurological effects in former rugby players [18], calls to ban contact from youth rugby have been made in academic circles [19], a position that has been challenged by World Rugby (WR) [20] In response to increasing knowledge about the potential harm associated with collisions in contact sports, WR and national governing bodies (NGBs) such as New Zealand Rugby (NZR), have implemented changes designed to improve player safety [21–23] These have included measures such as lowering tackle height to below the sternum, introducing mandatory stand-down periods following a concussion, implementing changes in coach and referee education, and improving recognition of concussion on the field (including withdrawal from play and referral for medical care as needed).

Initial studies into lowered tackle height in youth rugby players have shown changes in tackle behaviour, but without statistically significant reduction in injury or concussion rates [24, 25] Despite a lack of statistically significant findings, Brown et al. [24] suggested the 34% reduction in concussion rate reported in their study may have clinical significance as it was correlated with a reduction in mandated tackle height [17] Guidelines for reporting clinical significance have been reported in reference to adolescent populations [26] Under NZR rules, post-concussion 21-day stand-downs with graduated return-to-play (RTP) protocols are mandated [27] Coaches and referees at under-14 level and above undergo compulsory concussion training every year [28] Changes in concussion initiatives and tackle height could have practical significance for youth players, who have developed their play under these new rules.

There has been little research to date on junior rugby players, despite this being especially warranted in light of the changes in safety-related rules and coach education. WR updated its return-to-play guidelines in 2022 [29], while NZR announced below-the-sternum tackling for the 2023 season [30]. Although research is emerging on repeated HAEs in junior rugby using iMGs [31], presently there is a lack of data on whether repeated HAEs experienced under such playing conditions have a measurable deleterious effect on adolescents’ neurological structure and function. This study is the first of its kind to attempt to quantify potential changes in neurological integrity in a cohort of adolescent rugby players by assessing HAE incidence and magnitude with MRI-based metrics as a primary outcome. As a prospective cohort study, this research differs from predominantly retrospective research that has been conducted in primarily adult players thus far [32–34] This work is essential to more clearly understand the cumulative effect of HAEs on brain integrity. Results from the study could provide critical data to rugby stakeholders (including governing bodies, coaches, and players), so that they can improve the safety, and consequently the brain health, of those playing the game.

Methods

Aim, Design, and Setting of the Study

The aim of the current study was to examine the effect of collision exposure (incidence x magnitude) on potential neurological changes over one season of rugby for adolescent players. Pre- and post-season neuro-cognitive assessments and structural and diffusion-based MRI scans were conducted on all participants. We hypothesised that those participants exposed to a lower incidence and cumulative magnitude of head acceleration events (PLA/PRA) would show fewer changes (pre/post season) in white matter (WM) microstructural integrity, and minimal differences in neurocognitive testing scores and reinvestment scale questionnaires compared to those who experienced higher total exposures.

This study has a prospective cohort design. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) [35] guidelines for cohort studies informed the reporting of the study design for our research and were followed for the reporting of the results. The study was conducted with two junior male (under-16) rugby teams, during the 2022 and 2023 seasons (one in each season). Pre- and post-season testing was undertaken with each participant. The season ran from late April to August. Pre-season testing was conducted in late March and early April; post-season testing was conducted in September. Time between last rugby contact and post-season testing was approximately two weeks.

The study was based at two rugby clubs in Canterbury, New Zealand. The Canterbury Rugby Football Union (CRFU) and Ellesmere Rugby Sub-union (ERSU) were fully supportive of the research and provided letters to indicate their endorsement of the study. Inclusion criteria were defined as: being a member of an U16 male rugby team, able to take full part in training and competitive matches, able to give informed written consent, and willingness to wear HITIQ Nexus A9 mouthguards (HITIQ Ltd, Melbourne, Australia) at all trainings and matches. Compliance with wearing the study mouthguards was 98%. One player declined to wear the study mouthguard partway through the season due to discomfort following fitting of orthodontic braces. Exclusion criteria were defined as: declines to take part in the study.

Participants

In total, 42 (mean age 15.02 ± 0.76) under-16 male rugby players provided informed written assent and signed up to participate in this observational cohort study. All participants’ parents provided written consent. Forty participants completed a full season of rugby with full sets of data associated with their participation. Two participants withdrew, one of whom provided a partial set of data. Participants’ demographics can be seen in Table 1. Five concussions (n = 5, one in the first season, four in the second season) were diagnosed by a medical doctor. All concussed players were compliant in completing post-concussion neuro-cognitive assessments and MRI scans.

Procedures

Head acceleration events were recorded by the iMGs and video verified by trained researchers. Incidence was calculated via the number of HAEs in total, and per player-hour [31, 32] Impact rate was calculated by the sum of verified HAEs ≥ 8 g divided by the sum of player-hours [36] Contact exposure was any period where the player was wearing the mouthguard during contact training or gameplay [37] Exposure was recorded in minutes and divided by a factor of 60 to calculate exposure per player-hour. Magnitude was reported as PLA and PRA. Total cumulative impact loading was calculated per player, in both game-play and training scenarios, by summing PLA and PRA values across the whole season [14] To date, no accepted procedure exists for calculating cumulative impact burden [14].

All training sessions and games were video recorded to provide verification of HAEs recorded by the mouthguards. Cameras were set at a resolution of full high definition (FHD, 1080p) with a primary side-on camera (Canon HFG50 digital video camera, Canon Inc., Japan) at the highest midfield location [36] and a referee-mounted GoPro camera (GoPro Inc., USA) in use [31] The primary camera was used to capture a timestamp to which other cameras, and mouthguard data points, were synchronised [31, 38] The ball was ideally kept at the centre of the frame with a radius of approximately 10 m [39] Video verification of HAEs was conducted by importing iMG data onto the Catapult Focus video analysis platform (Catapult Sports Ltd, Melbourne, Australia). To aid in the verification process, players’ presence in training or game action was confirmed by an expert reviewer and was cross-referenced with the field diary completed at the time of the event.

Prior to the beginning of the season, participants underwent 3D dental scans using a TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) at a local orthodontist trained in the procedure (Donna Batchelor’s, Christchurch, NZ). The dental scans were a non-invasive procedure taking approximately ten minutes. Scans were subsequently digitally sent to HITIQ for manufacture. The mouthguards were 3D-printed using layers of ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) polymer and styrene-butadien-styrene (SBS), materials which comply with biocompatibility standard ISO10993 (approved dental/medical grade materials) [40].

The iMGs used in the study contained three triaxial accelerometers (Analog Devices, USA, ADXL372, range: ± 200 g, 12-bit) and a gyroscope (Bosch, Germany, BMG250, ± 2000 dps range, 16-bit). These accelerometers, embedded in the left, central, and right regions of the iMG, provided an estimate of rotational acceleration independent of the gyroscope [41] The normed signal from the left linear accelerometer was low pass filtered at 300 Hz using a 4th order 300 Hz low-pass Butterworth filter and linearly transformed to the centre of gravity (CG) of the head based on the 50th percentile hybrid III headform [40] The study mouthguards were found to have high accuracy (R2 = 0.996 for PLA and 0.994 for PRA) in an independent validation study [40] A classification algorithm was then used to identify HAEs to be subsequently included in the video verification process [41].

Forty participants underwent an MRI scan protocol pre- and post-season. Neuro-cognitive assessments and motor control questionnaires were completed at the MRI appointment. The NIH Toolbox is a computerized neurocognitive screening battery that can be used as a brief and reliable neurocognitive assessment in clinical practice and research involving those who have sustained mild traumatic brain injuries (mTBI) (ages 3 + years) [42]. The Fluid Composite score encompasses six cognitive subdomains: executive function, episodic memory, language, processing speed, attention, and working memory [43] The Movement Specific Reinvestment Scale (MSRS) and Decision Specific Reinvestment Scale (DSRS) were utilised to assess changes in conscious motor control [44] The scales assess individual differences in the propensity to consciously process movements. A high propensity to consciously process movements or decisions (i.e., high scores on the MSRS/DSRS) is associated with poorer motor control, so we examined the relationship between conscious motor processing and collisions.

The MRI protocol included a T1-weighted image to measure tissue volumes and cortical thickness and high angular resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) to measure WM integrity. This MRI protocol was a non-invasive and safe imaging technique for repeat scans in this cohort. MR imaging was performed on a 3 T Siemens (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) Skyra scanner (Pacific Radiology, St George’s Hospital, Christchurch, New Zealand) using a 64-channel head and neck coil. The primary acquisition was (a) multi-shell HARDI, 2-dimensional diffusion-weighted imaging, with uniformly distributed directions (25, 50, and 75 volumes each with: b = 1000/2000/2700 s/mm2 respectively) and 10 acquisitions without diffusion weighting (b = 0 s/mm2):TE/TR = 92/3600 ms, flip angle = 78°, acquisition matrix = 110 × 110, field of view (FOV) = 220 mm, 72 slices, slice thickness 2 mm, multi-band factor = 3, ungated, phase encoding anterior-posterior (plus flipped phase encoding images to enable distortion correction), voxel = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3. Other parameters were as follows: (b) Structural T1-weighted MPRAGE (TE/TR/TI = 2.85/2000/880 ms, Flip angle = 8°, FOV = 256 mm, bandwidth = 240 Hz/pixel, voxel = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3).

T1-weighted images were processed to calculate estimates of cortical thickness, surface area, and volumes, using Freesurfer (v.7.2.0; http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). Pre- and post-season T1 scans were processed using the longitudinal pipeline. In brief, pre- and post-season T1 scans (T1F0 and T1F1) were used to create an unbiased within-subject template [45]Amalgamation of subject space into midspace, using inverse consistent registration [45], allowed extraction of global metrics for each participant at each timepoint. Images were visually inspected for reasonable representations of grey matter (GM) and WM using spherical surface maps and parcellations [46] The unbiased midspace T1 image was then normalised (warped to fsaverage space and smoothed) to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. The difference in values between the two timepoint scans in MNI space allowed for calculation of within-subject structural change. T1-weighted images are often used in diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) studies to provide anatomic reference [47, 48] The following values for global metrics were then isolated for analysis: cerebral WM volume (mm3), subcortical GM volume (mm3), total GM volume (mm3), cortical surface area (left and right hemispheres, mm2), and cortical mean thickness (left and right hemispheres, mm). Total intracranial volume (ICV, mm3) was used as a co-variate while total cumulative loading for each player (in both PLA and PRA) was the primary variable of interest. The concussion cohort (n = 5) was analysed separately from the non-concussion cohort (n = 34 (structural); n = 28 (diffusion)).

For the diffusion-weighted images, preprocessing in MRTrix3 (version 3.0.3, https://mrtrix.readthedocs.io/en/3.0/3/) included denoising and Gibbs-ringing artefact removal. The images then underwent susceptibility-induced distortion correction (using the reversed phase-encoding volumes) using FSL ‘topup’. Diffusion-weighted volumes then underwent eddy current and motion correction using FSL ‘eddy’[49]. Excessive motion was defined as mean relative motion greater than three times the standard deviation of the group. Brain mask estimations were then improved using bias field correction. Using commands from MRTrix3, shells were extracted using the ‘dwiextract’ command, then underwent diffusion tensor estimation using the ‘dwi2tensor’ command [50] Images for the following DTI metrics in subject space were created: fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD).

All participants’ FA data were used to create an unbiased, within-subject FA median template using Freesurfer (v7.2.0; surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu), as well as registering FA/MD/AD/RD images from the two time-points (F0 and F1) into this within-subject template [45] The template was then used to create a mean FA skeleton which represented the centres of all tracts common to the cohort, using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) via the ENIGMA-DTI skeletonization process (enigma.ini.usc.edu/protocols/dti-protocols/#eDTI). The FA data for each participant were then projected onto the skeleton. The skeleton projection was then applied to non-FA data, therefore creating skeletons for all DTI metrics. For each measure, the two scans (FA F0 and FA F1) were merged into midpoint-space, normalised to MNI space, and projected back onto the skeleton as F0 MNI and F1 MNI. Average DTI metrics along the skeleton and within WM tracts (defined by the Johns Hopkins University [JHU] ICBM-DTI-81 white-matter-labels atlas)[51] were extracted for each participant at each timepoint (F0 and F1). This allowed the difference between the two values (e.g., FA F0 MNI and FA F1 MNI) to be measured for each DTI metric [46].

Data were displayed on spreadsheets covering sixty-three WM tracts. Five regions of interest (ROIs), along with average FA, were selected a priori based on previous research in sports-related concussion (SRC) and/or repeated head impact exposure (RHIE)[52]:

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses for comparing means were performed using the specialist statistical software SPSS (version 29.0 0.0, IBM, New York, USA). The α-level was set at 0.05 for all analyses. Independent samples t-tests, appropriate to use with continuous data, were used to compare means between pre- and post-season neurocognitive assessments (NIH Toolbox fluid composite standard score). For each t-test, means (± SD), t score (df), p-value, and Cohen’s d were reported. Equal variances were not assumed. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA (group: concussed/non-concussed x score: pre-season/post-season) were conducted for the reinvestment scales (MSRS and DSRS). For ANOVA measures, means (± SD), ratio of variance (F-value), p-value, and effect size (ηp2) were reported. For consistency in analysis with the MRI results, the concussion cohort (n = 5) was analysed separately from the non-concussion cohort (n = 35).

For the processed MRI results, spaghetti plots were constructed to visually ascertain the patterns and diversity among individuals. Models for structural analysis were generated by using multivariate modelling, while models for diffusion metrics (FA, MD, AD, RD) were generated by using the linear mixed models analysis in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/), utilizing the 1me4 package described by Bates [53] The linear mixed models treated individual effects as random variables and other variables as fixed effects, enabling partitioning of variance in the outcome variables. We applied a Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. The associated R codes with comments are posted below for further reference. To conduct these analyses, the data were converted from a wide format to a long format and then each of the outcome variables were regressed on the following variables:

The primary metric for testing this hypothesis was the analysis of processed MRI images from both structural and diffusion-based scans. Total cumulative loading (TCL) for PLA and PRA were calculated and organised by quartile. Players were divided into high impact and low impact groups according to the median TCL for PLA (2,342.15 g). T1-Weighted and Diffusion-Weighted Results

T1-weighted images were analysed, focusing on sub-cortical and total GM volume, WM volume, cortical thickness, and cortical surface area. Images for structural and diffusion scans are displayed in Fig. 2. T1-weighted images were investigated for pre- to post-season change in the participants and whether this change correlated with TCL. ICV was the same for both time points and was utilised as a co-variate for the cortical values. Excluding the concussion cohort (n = 5), all remaining participants (n = 34) had usable scans for inclusion. The results for the concussion cohort are included as supplementary data. Means (± SD) were calculated for all participants (Table 4). Although all values decreased from pre to post season, none reached statistical significance. Fixed effects multivariate results are summarised in Table 5. The results indicated no significant change in any of the measured metrics (volume, surface area, cortical thickness), from pre- to post-season, in the non-concussion cohort (n = 34), and non-significant structural change held when controlling for the primary variable of interest (TCL) in both summed PLA and summed PRA. Overall, the results appear to show no change in neurological structure, as measured by T1-weighted scans, in this cohort of non-concussed male adolescent rugby players exposed to one season of play.

Diffusion-Weighted Results

Region-of-interest analysis using TBSS was employed. Pre- to post-season changes for the selected ROIs in FA and MD were quantified for all participants. The primary variable of interest was TCL. Additional co-variates considered in the analysis were: training age, concussion history, body-mass index (BMI), headgear use, time between scans, change in pre- to post-season neuro-cognitive test scores, and average relative motion change between the scans.

Regression modelling was performed for all variables and two diffusion metrics (FA, MD) in the non-concussion cohort (n = 28) (Table 6). Out of the 40 participants who completed pre- and post-season MRI scans, 12 participants were excluded from DWI analysis (n = 2 due to processing failure, n = 5 due to artefact secondary to orthodontic braces, and n = 5 due to concussion during the season). Means (± SD) in FA and MD for the ROIs were calculated. The mean (± SD) relative motion for this cohort was 0.34 mm (± 0.05). No participants were excluded due to excessive relative motion. Pre- to post-season FA values decreased in the CST and IFOF, and increased in the CC, SLF, and SS. No identifiable change was seen in pre- to post-season MD values. No significant relationships were found between any variables.

Discussion

Intersection of Head Acceleration Event Magnitude and Neurological Change

A key methodological innovation embedded in this study was the use of an MRI protocol to assess potential neurological change across one season of playing rugby. The primary MRI scan used to detect potential change was DTI, which has been extensively used in both the SRC[54] and the RHIE populations.[52]This methodology allowed the analysis of diffusion-based WM changes and provided insight into how they may intersect with cumulative HAE burden. This approach has been used with youth American Football players [47, 48], but not with youth rugby players. As such, the study could provide vital baseline data on the effects of one season of rugby exposure on the structure and function of the adolescent brain.

Our analysis of diffusion-based metrics did not reveal any statistically significant changes pre-to-post season. Based on this, it may be prudent to consider the role of safety-based measures implemented at all levels of the game[24, 25] and whether such changes may be contributing to maintaining players’ neurological integrity. Calls to ban tackling in youth rugby[19] and attention on the duty of care in the game[55] have been noted. Changes to the laws of the game and/or increased player safety awareness may have potential to decrease potential neurological harm brought about by HAEs as junior players are playing a different version of the game than previous generations[24, 25]. Although our results do not show negative neurological effects, these data are from a single season of rugby exposure, and must be interpreted with caution. The role of teaching correct technique (for tackles, breakdowns, and falling) from a young age has been emphasised in recent research[56] as necessary preparation for game-play in terms of avoiding injury and increasing enjoyment of the game. This, along with already implemented safety and recovery protocols, may help to keep players safer.

Total Cumulative Loading

The primary variable of interest used in the analysis of the MRI data was TCL. This was a simple metric that allowed clear visualisation of each player’s head impact burden across one season, summing their PLA and PRA separately. Such measures have been used in rugby studies on adult players [57] In diffusion-based studies metrics most often used were total incidence count or measures such as risk weighted exposure (RWE) or head impact telemetry severity profile (HITSP).[52] Combining PLA and PRA into a single metric has been described as methodologically inappropriate, but single measures of impact burden can be descriptively useful [14] Cumulative magnitude quantification allowed for the organisation of participants into low and high magnitude groups, while detailed pre- and during season record-keeping allowed multiple other co-variates, such as training age, BMI, concussion history, and neuro-cognitive testing scores to be considered.

Total cumulative loading was calculated for all players (mean PLA 2539 g; mean PRA 210,497 rads/s2). Compared to rugby studies by King et al. the average cumulative values in our study were lower than comparable values in a men’s amateur team (mean PLA 12,029 g; mean PRA 2,101,028 rads/s2)[57] and in an under-9 team (mean PLA 3,411 g; mean PRA 595,624 rads/s2) [58]. Our results were also lower than those reported in high school American Football (mean PLA 16,746 g; mean PRA 1,090,698 rads/s2)[14]. Although there was some variation within the quartiles in PLA versus PRA, the same players were represented in each quartile, suggesting a linear relationship between increased PLA burden and increased PRA burden. Whether players ended up in the high load or low load group was based more on variables such as contact disposition, exposure time on the field, and time-loss due to injuries and concussion.

While not statistically significant over one season of play, the wide variation in total cumulative loading (see Fig. 1) may have clinical significance for players when considering their brain health and integrity over the longer-term. WR has made a number of changes to the laws of the game designed to improve player safety, but the potential health benefits of these changes for young players have yet to be assessed. Our results suggest that one season of rugby did not appear to affect the brain integrity of this cohort as measured by DTI. Nevertheless there is emerging evidence regarding the development of CTE in former professional players [59, 60] As amateur rugby players have less loading and injury risk than professional players[61], players with high cumulative loading over multiple seasons may be more susceptible to microstructural neurological changes than players with lower loading. Our research highlights the need for longitudinal research to assess the longer-term neurological effects of HAEs in rugby, as young players who continue to play the game may be at risk for neurological damage over multiple seasons [11, 18].

Neuro-Cognitive and Reinvestment Scale Results

Non-significant results were found from analysis of both NIH Toolbox fluid composite standard scores and reinvestment scales. The decision rumination season x group interaction results may suggest concussed participants ruminated less, but these results cannot be considered statistically significant. Further research in this area, preferably with larger cohort groups, is warranted as propensity for decision reinvestment may negatively affect rugby players’ responses to patterns of play in pressure situations [62] These season-based, pre- to post-season non-significant clinical findings reflect other rugby-based studies, conducted at the college and adult level, which similarly showed no significant change in neuro-cognitive functioning based on the tests used (including Immediate Post-Concussion Assessment and Cognitive Testing (ImPACT) and Sport Concussion Assessment Tool [SCAT] assessments) [5, 63, 64] In high school American Football, DTI-based changes (such as decreased FA) have been correlated with decreased verbal and visual memory over one season’s exposure [48, 65] This may be reflective of higher exposure rates in American Football compared to rugby [66] Underpinning the need for longitudinal studies, research into university and adult contact sport athletes has associated higher cumulative load with decreased cognitive scores relative to lower FA [67, 68], whereas other college-level studies found no change [69, 70].

Interpretation of T1-Weighted Results

Results for structural imaging (T1-weighted images) similarly showed non-significant pre- to post-season changes in the non-concussion cohort based on one season of rugby exposure. This is reflective of findings in multiple diffusion-based studies involving the RHIE population, in youth contact sport and/or rugby, which also used T1-weighted structural imaging [5, 47, 48, 63–65] No significant structural differences in athletes, either within- or between-subject, were reported in these studies. Although structural values in our cohort decreased pre to post season, this decrease was not statistically significant. Playing organised sport is generally not associated with global WM and GM changes relative to non-playing.[71] Global metrics such as WM volume have also been cited in the literature, with Brett et al. [72] concluding change in gross WM volumes was not associated with higher cumulative load or concussion history [72] This also reflects our findings.

Localized changes in GM volume may have been detected by focusing on smaller anatomical regions. Although GM changes have been noted after SRC [73], such localized changes have not been reported in studies with similar methodologies to ours.[5]This justifies our hypothesis that any structural GM changes would have most likely been diffuse in nature. Given the heterogeneity in HAEs experienced by rugby players over the course of one season [32], we did not necessarily expect small, localized GM changes. Although we appropriately corrected for the number of comparisons, we limited their number by limiting the number of regions investigated (therefore selecting the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Freesurfer v.7.2.0; http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) over other equally valid options with increased numbers of spatially smaller parcellations).

Interpretation of Diffusion-Weighted Results

Results for DTI in the non-concussion cohort showed non-significant findings in the included metrics (FA and MD) in most WM tracts. Although some borderline statistically significant findings were reported in some ROIs, these did not follow a consistent pattern and may be considered to be outliers. In contrast to our findings, a systematic review on the use of DTI in the RHIE population found the majority of included studies reported change in WM integrity, most often reported as decreased FA [52]. Three rugby-based studies reported DTI-based changes over the course of one or more seasons, but all were conducted in adult players [5, 6, 63] This may be due to adult athletes in these studies having been exposed to a higher incidence and magnitude of HAEs than our younger participants, having had MRI follow-ups at a 3rd time point, or having been compared to non-contact or non-athlete controls.

Other ROI-based studies have found changes in diffusion-based metrics. Schranz et al. [64] reported small FA increases pre- to post-season in university level female rugby players[64], but this was not reported as statistically significant. McAllister et al. [68] reported significant differences in MD (in the CC) and in FA (in the amygdala) of college contact sport athletes [68] Most meaningfully for our study, Bahrami et al. [47] reported correlations between risk-weighted exposure (RWECP) and decreased FA in the right SLF and the left IFOF in youth American Football players (aged 8–13 years) [47] Although statistically non-significant, our findings indicated MD changes in the IFOF related to headgear use, and in the SLF related to time between scans. The most analogous measure to RWE, cumulative loading, was associated with FA changes in the CST.

Overall, our diffusion-based findings for one season of exposure do not correlate well with published research, highlighting the need for further research into adolescent rugby players. Most published studies using DTI in the RHIE population have reported significant changes in FA and/or MD based on one season of play [52, 74, 75] Significant results published by other authors may also reflect multiple other variables, such as the type of sport played, age of participants, sex, concussion history, study design (within-subject versus between-subject), and type of analysis used (ROI-based versus voxel-based). Comparison of rugby-playing cohorts versus non-contact sport controls may further elucidate findings. Ideally, future research should also include the use of blood biomarkers as a further objective measurement of neurological compromise. Cho et al. [76] found while that blood biomarkers could detect concussion, these findings did not correlate with changes in neurological structure or function [76].

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. Research was conducted over one season only, and results must be interpreted specific to this time-frame. Findings are specific to one sex and one age group (male, under-16 rugby players), and to rugby trainings and games filmed under the playing conditions stipulated by NZR. Under NZR rules, the tackle height law was changed during data collection (between the 2022 and 2023 seasons), potentially affecting the behaviour of players in tackle situations. Data are representative and specific to the parameters set by the iMGs used in the study. We acknowledge that iMG faults during data collection may have resulted in some data loss, and iMG re-triggering times for complex tackle events may have contributed to false negative rates. Due to factors such as differences in the way players approach contact situations in the game, a large degree of variability (and high standard deviations) were observed. MRI values may have been compromised during recording, processing, or analysis. Small changes in GM volume may have been obscured by averaging over large regions, but focusing on more localized regions may have increased the chances of identifying false positives due to performing more comparisons. DTI-based metrics may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle microstructural neurological change. Practice effects for testing materials (such as the NIH Toolbox assessment) may have been present. Due to filming conditions at some venues, visuals of some videotaped events had compromised quality. Concussion symptoms may not have been reported by participants, and/or concussions may have been experienced during the season in non-rugby contexts.

Conclusions

Overall, in this cohort of adolescent rugby players, exposure to one season of play did not appear to result in measurable macro- or microstructural neurological change. Results from pre- and post-season diffusion-based MRI scans indicated a statistically non-significant relationship between head acceleration event exposure in one season of rugby and changes in WM microstructure, including for those players exposed to a higher level of impact magnitude. This non-significance held when controlling for variables such as training age, headgear use, and body mass index. It should be emphasised that these results pertain to one season’s exposure, in the absence of concussion, in a cohort of healthy rugby players aged 14–16 years. Longitudinal studies could provide key data on longer term brain health, the ability of any microstructural WM damage to repair itself in the off-season, and how this interacts with cumulative loading. These findings add value to the field and provide a key departure point for researchers to quantify neurological change in young rugby players, and for such data to potentially play a role in making rugby safer.
