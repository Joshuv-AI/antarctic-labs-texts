Grade II obesity is an independent predictor of poor response to ovulation induction with oral letrozole in infertile women with PCOS: A prospective cohort study from North India
Authors: Shanti Tholiya, Archana Kumari, Reeta Mahey, Mani Kalaivani, Hem C. Sati, Mona Sharma, Aarthi K. Jayraj, Neena Malhotra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Letrozole is an effective ovulation-inducing agent for women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), but its efficacy may be influenced by obesity. This study examines obesity’s impact on letrozole-induced ovulation in infertile women with PCOS.MATERIAL AND METHODS:A prospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary medical center in North India with 80 anovulatory women with PCOS. Participants received letrozole (5 mg daily, days 2–6) for up to three cycles. Ovulation was monitored through transvaginal ultrasound, and human chorionic gonadotrophin (HCG) was administered when a dominant follicle reached ≥ 17 mm. The association between obesity and letrozole response was assessed, considering clinical, metabolic, and hormonal parameters. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, and continuous variables were compared via Student’s t-test. Logistic regression assessed the relationship between obesity and ovulation, adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), and baseline antimullerian hormone (AMH).RESULTS:Eighty participants were recruited in the study including 51 (63.75%) responders and 29 (36.25%) nonresponders. Higher BMI, elevated AMH, and increased testosterone levels were linked to a negative response to letrozole. Grade II obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) significantly reduced the likelihood of ovulation (adjusted OR 8.49, 95% CI 1.06-67.56, P = 0.043). Elevated AMH (>16 ng/mL) was also associated with poor response (adjusted OR 5.15, 95% CI 1.52-17.47, P = 0.009).CONCLUSIONS:Grade II obesity is an independent risk factor for poor response to letrozole ovulation induction in PCOS. Personalized treatment based on BMI may enhance success rates.

Introduction

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder in reproductive age women, marked by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovaries.[1] PCOS often coexists with obesity and insulin resistance, which exacerbates its symptoms by increasing androgen production from adipose tissue.[2] This hormonal imbalance disrupts ovulation and fertility, complicating the condition’s management.[3]

Ovulation induction is a cornerstone of infertility treatment for women with PCOS, yet treatment outcomes remain inconsistent. Obesity has been identified as a critical factor that negatively affect the effectiveness of ovulation induction. Legro et al.[4] demonstrated that higher body mass index (BMI) is associated with lower ovulation and pregnancy rates. Similarly, Imani et al.[5] found that women with a BMI over 30 kg/m² had significantly reduced chances of ovulation induction success when using clomiphene citrate compared to women with a normal BMI. These studies suggest there is an association between obesity and ovulation response with oral ovulation agents like clomiphene citrate.

Oral letrozole, an aromatase inhibitor, has emerged as a promising alternative to clomiphene citrate for ovulation induction in women with PCOS.[6] Despite its superior efficacy, responses to letrozole are not uniform, and obesity appears to influence its effectiveness. Recent studies, such as one by Amer et al.,[7] indicate that letrozole efficacy might be affected in women with higher BMI. However, conclusive evidence regarding the strength of this association between grades of obesity and the corresponding odds ratios is lacking.

This study aims to address this gap in the existing literature by investigating the association between obesity and response to ovulation induction with oral letrozole in infertile women with PCOS. By elucidating the relationship between obesity and letrozole efficacy, this research could contribute to more personalized and effective treatment strategies, ultimately improving reproductive outcomes and overall health in women with PCOS.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in New Delhi, India, from March 2022 to November 2023.

Study participants and sampling

Eighty anovulatory women with PCOS seeking infertility treatment were recruited from the gynecology outpatient department. Inclusion criteria encompassed women aged 21–38 years with clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) as per Rotterdam criteria, normal male partner semen parameters (WHO 2021), patent fallopian tubes, euthyroid status, normal prolactin levels, no history of ovarian surgery, and absence of contraception use within the preceding 3 months.[89] Exclusion criteria included other causes of anovulation (thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia), hyperandrogenism (congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing’s syndrome), or previous ovarian surgery.

Data collection tool and technique

After obtaining consent, the patients were recruited in the study and necessary data were taken in the validated proforma. In patients fulfilling the inclusion and exclusion criteria for PCOS, a fasting serum sample of 3 mL was drawn on day 2/3 of the menstrual period for hormonal profile (FSH (Follicle-stimulating hormone), LH (Leutinizing Hormone), PRL (Prolactin), AMH (Antimullerian hormone), TSH (Thyroid-stimulating hormone)), fasting insulin, fasting sugars, and lipid profile (cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), and triglyceride (TG)), and serum testosterone.

Participants underwent up to three cycles of ovulation induction. The ovulation protocol involved Letrozole 5 mg daily on cycle days 3–7. Follicular development was monitored through transvaginal ultrasound, with baseline and subsequent scans as indicated. Ovulation was triggered with HCG when a dominant follicle reached a diameter of ≥17 mm. Cycles resulting in at least one dominant follicle were considered responsive, while those without were classified as nonresponsive and advanced to the subsequent protocol. For nonresponders, the patients were given Human Menopausal Gonadotropin (HMG) as per the hospital protocol.

A cutoff of AMH >16 ng/mL was used to investigate the association between elevated AMH levels and the success of ovulation induction based on previous data, which indicated that pretreatment AMH levels significantly correlated with follicular response. The study found that higher AMH levels were associated with decreased responsiveness to letrozole-based treatment, with an AMH level ≤16.43 ng/mL identified as a significant predictor of ovarian response, achieving an area under curve (AUC) of 0.88.[10]

To mitigate variability in AMH assay results, all assays were performed in a single laboratory by one trained technician under the close supervision of faculty members. Strict adherence to quality control measures was maintained, following the manufacturer’s protocols for standard controls and reagents. Consumables were procured on an annual basis to further minimize variability. Consistency was ensured by applying the same assay protocol across all samples, effectively reducing interassay variation and enhancing the reliability of AMH measurements.

The primary outcome was an association between grades of obesity (as per South Asian classification of obesity) and follicular response to ovulation induction by letrozole-based ovulation therapy.[11] Secondary outcomes included the assessment of follicular response across different PCOS phenotypes, as well as the relationship between follicular response and metabolic parameters such as Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), serum AMH, and triglyceride levels.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines, with approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IECPG-114/24.02.2022, RT 30/24.03.2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Participants were assured of confidentiality, and all data were anonymized before analysis

Statistical analysis method

Participant demographics, infertility history, medical background, physical characteristics, and anthropometric measurements were recorded in the designated proforma. Data analysis encompassed both descriptive and inferential statistical methods. To assess group differences, Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, independent t-tests, and ANOVA tests were used. For categorical data analysis, Fisher’s exact tests were used. Data management and analysis were conducted using Microsoft Excel and SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM SPSS 25 License to Department of Biostatistics, AIIMS, New Delhi), respectively. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05. Logistic regression analysis evaluated the association between obesity and the likelihood of ovulation, controlling for potential confounders such as age, BMI, and baseline AMH.

Association between clinical, metabolic, and hormonal parameters with ovulation response

In terms of body weight categories, as per the WHO Asian-BMI classification, the distribution showed a significant difference between responders and nonresponders. Specifically, a higher percentage of nonresponders were classified as Grade II or more obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m²), highlighting the negative impact of obesity on ovulation induction outcomes.

Table 1 also highlights the comparison of metabolic parameters, including HOMA-IR and serum triglycerides, between responders and nonresponders. Although there was no statistically significant difference in HOMA-IR (P = 0.098) and serum triglycerides (P = 0.449) between the two groups, a trend toward higher insulin resistance and triglyceride levels was observed in nonresponders.

Table 1 also highlights the comparison between hormonal parameters. A significant difference was found in AMH levels, with nonresponders having higher AMH levels (P = 0.001), indicating a possible link between elevated AMH and poor response to ovulation induction. In addition, nonresponders had significantly higher testosterone levels (P = 0.048), suggesting that hyperandrogenism might contribute to the poor response. Other hormonal parameters, including LH, FSH, LH/FSH ratio, and TSH, did not show significant differences between responders and nonresponders.

Table 2 presents the logistic regression analysis for predicting ovulation response based on obesity, AMH levels, and testosterone. The univariate analysis revealed that Grade II or more obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) was significantly associated with nonresponse to ovulation induction (OR = 8.49, P = 0.043). Similarly, AMH values greater than 16.43 were significantly associated with higher odds of nonresponse (OR = 5.15, P = 0.009). Although high testosterone levels approached significance (P = 0.055), they were not a definitive predictor in the multivariate analysis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that Grade II obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) is a significant predictor of poor response to ovulation induction with oral letrozole in women with PCOS, alongside elevated AMH levels. Nonresponders were characterized by higher BMIs, elevated AMH levels, and increased testosterone levels compared to responders. Logistic regression analysis revealed that Grade II obesity was significantly associated with nonresponse to ovulation induction, with an adjusted odds ratio of 8.49 (P = 0.043). This is a considerably higher value than the odds ratio for AMH levels, which was 5.15 (P = 0.009). Although high testosterone levels approached significance (P = 0.055), they did not emerge as definitive predictors in the multivariate analysis. The strong association between higher BMI and poor ovulation response underscores the critical impact of obesity on fertility treatments in women with PCOS. This finding aligns with previous research by Legro et al.[4] and Imani et al.,[5] who reported that obesity adversely affects ovulation and pregnancy rates. Our study extends these findings to letrozole, highlighting the compound effects of obesity on fertility treatments beyond clomiphene citrate.

Previous studies have established that elevated AMH levels are significantly associated with nonresponse to letrozole, suggesting high AMH as a marker for ovarian dysfunction and poor treatment outcomes.[61213] AMH cut-offs of 4.27 ng/ml and 6.25 ng/ml have been reported for clomiphene citrate-based ovulation induction, while a higher threshold of 16.43 ng/ml was identified for letrozole-based therapy as a predictor of ovulation success.[71014] These variations likely stem from differences in study populations, treatment protocols, and laboratory methods. Our study adds to the existing knowledge by providing specific insights into the role of Grade II obesity and elevated AMH levels in predicting response to letrozole, highlighting the complex interplay of metabolic and hormonal factors in PCOS treatment outcomes. While we anticipated obesity to be a significant factor, the magnitude of its impact was more pronounced than expected.

Our study also explored the differences in metabolic and hormonal parameters between responders and nonresponders to letrozole-based ovulation induction. Responders had significantly lower levels of testosterone and a more favorable metabolic profile, including lower insulin resistance, compared to nonresponders. AMH levels were higher in responders, aligning with previous studies that have shown a positive correlation between AMH and ovarian reserve.[15] These findings suggest that AMH and other metabolic parameters, such as testosterone and insulin sensitivity, play a crucial role in predicting ovulation response. The ability to distinguish between responders and nonresponders based on these parameters allows for more personalized treatment plans, which can improve the chances of successful ovulation induction in women with PCOS.

This study’s finding underscores the critical role of personalized infertility management in women with PCOS. Grade II obesity and elevated AMH levels are significant predictors of poor response to letrozole, highlighting the need for targeted treatment strategies. Clinicians should prioritize weight management through lifestyle interventions such as diet and exercise, which have been shown to improve ovulation rates and metabolic profiles. Even modest weight loss can lead to notable hormonal and metabolic benefits, enhancing the likelihood of successful ovulation induction.[16] For women with high BMI and AMH levels, alternative protocols like gonadotropins may be more effective, particularly in cases resistant to oral agents such as letrozole.[17] In addition, metformin, which enhances insulin sensitivity, is especially beneficial for patients with concurrent insulin resistance.[18] By addressing both metabolic and hormonal dysfunctions, clinicians can optimize ovulation induction outcomes in obese women with PCOS, ultimately improving overall treatment success.

Our study has several strengths that enhance its validity and relevance. First, it addresses a critical gap in the literature by focusing specifically on the impact of obesity on the efficacy of letrozole, a drug that has emerged as a highly effective oral agent for ovulation induction. Second, the prospective cohort design allows for a robust assessment of the predictors of ovulation response. In addition, the study’s comprehensive evaluation of metabolic and hormonal parameters provides a holistic understanding of the factors influencing treatment outcomes. Despite these strengths, the study has limitations.

Limitation and recommendation

The sample size, although adequate for initial findings, is relatively small and may limit the generalizability of the results. Although successful ovulation is an important outcome measure, the clinical pregnancy rate would be a more relevant outcome in day-to-day clinical practice. Moreover, since the study participants were recruited from a single hospital, the findings may not fully represent the broader population. This limitation highlights the potential influence of regional, institutional, or demographic factors on the outcomes. Future studies involving multiple centers and diverse populations are needed to enhance the generalizability of these results.

Future studies should include larger, diverse populations to validate these findings and explore obesity’s and AMH’s role in ovulation induction. Investigating the effectiveness of alternative ovulation induction agents or adjunctive treatments in obese women with PCOS could also provide valuable insights. In addition, longitudinal studies tracking pregnancy and live birth rates following ovulation induction would help to better understand the long-term implications of these predictors.

Conclusion

Grade II obesity is a significant predictor of poor response to ovulation induction with oral letrozole in women with PCOS. These findings highlight the need for personalized treatment approaches that consider these factors to enhance reproductive outcomes. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of the complex interplay between obesity, hormonal imbalances, and infertility treatment in PCOS, paving the way for more effective and individualized therapeutic strategies.
