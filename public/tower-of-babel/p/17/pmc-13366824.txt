Global burden of disease and future trends of trichomoniasis in women of reproductive age from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Yuxuan Jin, Yudi Tan, Shuyi Huang, Yong Wang, Shasha Yang, Jinkong Wei, Yuying Wei, Yongcen Zhou, Yi Huang, Caiyi He, Junying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study was conducted to assess the global burden of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age (15–49 years old) from 1990 to 2021, analyze changes in the patterns of this disease, identify regions with a high disease burden and key risk factors of trichomoniasis, provide evidence-based support for the differentiated prevention and control of trichomoniasis, and guide countries to optimize the allocation of health resources to reduce the damage to reproductive health.MethodsUsing the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, we first analyzed the incidence, prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of trichomoniasis among reproductive-age women worldwide and in five sociodemographic index (SDI) regions (low SDI, medium-low SDI, medium SDI, medium-high SDI, and high SDI), and the changes in their trends. Then, we analyzed the effect of the SDI and risk factors of 21 countries and 204 regions on the incidence and prevalence of trichomoniasis among reproductive-age women in different SDI regions. Finally, we constructed an autoregressive moving average (ARIMA) model to predict the global trends of incidence and prevalence among women of reproductive age in different regions of SDI from 2022 to 2030.ResultsThe results of this study revealed that the global incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age showed a yearly increasing trend from 1990 to 2021, and that DALYs were closely associated with regional SDI levels and unsafe sex. The incidence and prevalence of trichomoniasis first decreased and then increased from 2022 to 2030.ConclusionThe global incidence and prevalence rates of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age, measured per 100,000 people, showed a yearly increasing trend from 1990 to 2021.Therefore, preventive and control measures for Trichomonas vaginalis need to be strengthened, health expenditures need to be restructured, and the prevention and control of risk factors need to be focused on.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01262-5.

Introduction

Trichomoniasis is a non-viral sexually transmitted disease caused by Trichomonas vaginalis [1], and infection by this parasite can cause inflammation of the genitourinary system, including vaginitis, urethritis, and cystitis [2]. Although the infection is asymptomatic in some patients [3], Raina N Fichorova found that trichomoniasis is associated with infertility, preterm labor, low birth weight, bacterial vaginosis, and increased risk of cervical cancer, HPV, and HIV infection in women of reproductive age; trichomoniasis also affects the functions of sperm in men, leading to infertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes [4].

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are not only a serious threat to the health of individuals but also a heavy burden on the public health system. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than one million people are estimated to be infected with curable STIs every day. According to the study by Rowley et al. (2019), the number of new infections of T. vaginalis among people aged 15–49 years was approximately 156 million worldwide in 2016; among the affected individuals, approximately 73.7 million were women and 82.6 million were men [1]. In recent years, researchers from many countries and regions have conducted epidemiological studies on T. vaginalis. In the United States, approximately 2–3 million cases of symptomatic trichomoniasis occur annually among sexually active women [5]. The study by Jae-Sook Ryu mentioned that in the Republic of Korea, 10.4% of women with vaginal symptoms were diagnosed with T. vaginalis infection [6].

Trichomonas vaginalis poses a significant health burden at the individual level and socioeconomic burden at the population level. Patients incur high healthcare costs associated with diagnosis, treatment, and possible complications, while the effect of the disease on labor productivity may lead to a decrease in productivity and economic loss [7]. A significant portion of public health resources is wasted due to high morbidity, which further deteriorates the health of the workforce and exacerbates social health inequalities. Low-income and middle-income countries are substantially affected by a lack of public health resources and inadequate social welfare support [8]. Therefore, imparting education on sexual health, optimizing the allocation of public health resources, and providing targeted social support measures are key strategies adopted to reduce the socioeconomic burden of the disease [9].

Although trichomoniasis imposes a significant health burden, researchers have made notable progress in global prevention and control efforts in recent years. The infection rates associated with trichomoniasis have been effectively checked in some high-income countries by promoting sexual health education, expanding screening coverage, and offering free testing services. For example, the 2015 Guidelines for the Treatment of Sexually Transmitted Diseases issued by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the USA provide well-defined recommendations for the diagnosis and treatment of T. vaginalis [9]. The Association of Colleges of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of the United Kingdom (ACOG) has published a practice guideline for managing vaginitis in non-pregnant women. However, the effectiveness of the prevention and control of the disease shows prominent geographical differences, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where the rate of infection continues to be high, which is closely related to the low level of economic development and limited medical resources in this region [10]. Additionally, factors such as socioeconomic status, cultural practices, and education level affect the implementation and efficacy of prevention and control measures to different degrees [11, 12].

As the incidence of trichomoniasis among reproductive-age women (15–49 years old) is high, and this disease strongly affects the socioeconomic system [13], this study was based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD). The system was used to extract and standardize the data on trichomoniasis for both sexes aged 15–49 years globally, in 21 regions and 204 countries, including the incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and risk factors. After stratification based on the quintiles (low, medium-low, moderate, medium-high, and high) of the SDI, a joinpoint regression (JR) model was used to evaluate the temporal patterns and inflection point of disease burden over the past 32 years. The Spearman correlation and generalized additive model (GAM) were used to analyze the non-linear relationship between DALYs and SDI. We also compared and analyzed the differences among the seven age subgroups and gender to determine the high-risk groups. This was the first study to apply the ARIMA time series model to trichomoniasis to predict the incidence and prevalence in different SDI regions from 2022 to 2030. We reported for the first time the incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and associated risk factors for T. vaginalis in the GBD database and modeled the prediction of future epidemiological trends of the disease [14]. The results of this study may provide data support for global health policy makers, public health workers, and researchers to help identify high-risk areas and populations and to provide a scientific basis for developing and optimizing prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and collation

Data acquisition

The data for this study were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Research Database [15] of the IHME. This database uses the DisMod-MR 2.1 system to estimate incidence, prevalence, and DALYs with internal consistency, which is well-suited for analyzing long-term trends [16]. We searched 21 regions and 204 countries (for specific region and country names, please refer to Table 1 and Additional Tables 1 and 2, and 3) around the world from 1990 to 2021. Individuals aged 15 to 49 years (specifically, 15–20 years, 20–24 years, 25–29 years, 30–34 years, 35–39 years, 40–44 years, and 45–49 years) [17] were included in this study. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for trichomoniasis in the GBD list of causes is ICD10 A59.01 [18]. Data with incomplete incidence, prevalence, or DALYs information, or from regions/countries not in the GBD database, were excluded.

The study period from 1990 to 2021 was selected to maximize data availability and consistency, leveraging the comprehensive and reliable data provided by the GBD database. This timeframe is aligned with global health monitoring initiatives, ensuring that our findings are comparable with other long-term health metrics [17]. Additionally, the 30-year span allows for the analysis of long-term trends in the burden of trichomoniasis across different Sociodemographic Index (SDI) regions, thereby capturing both global patterns and regional disparities in disease control efforts.

Data collation

We standardized the crude incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years [25], grouping the total data by SDI quintiles at low, low-moderate, medium, medium-high, and high SDI levels [19].

Using R4.4.0 software, first, we employed Joinpoint Regression software to construct a Joinpoint Regression (JR) model to assess the trend of the burden of T. vaginalis (standardized rate) among women of reproductive age from 1990 to 2021 [20]. Second, we determined the differences and patterns in the burden of T. vaginalis according to age and sex. Third, we examined the relationship between DALYs and the SDI based on the partitioning of the SDI. Fourth, we studied the effects of various risk factors on DALYs. Finally, we used the ARIMA model for the first time to comprehensively study the global epidemiological trends of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age and the factors influencing them. The ARIMA model is a classical model in the time series analysis method that can effectively fit and predict the trend of time series data [21]. For all analyses except ARIMA, we used standardized rates. The initial data were crude rates, which were standardized before any further calculations or graphing. However, to directly describe the overall original trend, we used crude rates in the ARIMA model. The ARIMA model fit the trichomoniasis disease burden (crude rate) among reproductive-age women in 1990–2021 and predicted the global trend of trichomoniasis burden (crude rate) in different SDI regions from 2022 to 2030. Assumptions tested included stationarity and normality of the time series data. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation techniques to ensure robustness of the analysis. Additionally, we conducted diagnostics for the ARIMA model to ensure its appropriateness for the data. This included evaluating model fit and ensuring that the assumptions of the time series analysis were met. These steps helped to validate the model and enhance the transparency of our methodology.

Results

Global overview of the burden of trichomoniasis among reproductive-age women, 1990–2021

Global data profile

In 2021, the global prevalence of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age reached 135 million, with a prevalence rate of 678,810 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 372,460–1,124,770), representing an increase of 11% (95% CI: 9–12.8) since 1990. In the same year, the global prevalence of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age was 110 million, with a prevalence rate of 5,575.5 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 2,912.9–9,356.5), an increase of 9.8% (95% CI: 6.8–12.4) since 1990. Additionally, the global number of DALYs for trichomoniasis among reproductive-age women in 2021 was 208.9 million(95% CI: 86.6–458.3), equivalent to 10.6 DALYs per 1,000 people (95% CI: 3.5–24.4), representing an increase of 9.8% (95% CI: 6.4–12.9) since 1990.

Overview of data for 21 regions

The data were extracted from Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Tables 2, and Supplementary Tables 3, and a comprehensive analysis was conducted on 21 regions. The incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of trichomoniasis were higher in southern sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, and Eastern sub-Saharan Africa than in Western Europe, South Latin America, and Eastern Europe from 1990 to 2021. In southern sub-Saharan Africa, the incidence rate was 21,665.2 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 12,252.2–35,110.2), the prevalence rate was 37,810.6 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 20,106.8–61,288.8), and the DALYs rate was 37.7 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 13.1–85.8). In Oceania, the incidence rate was 16,702.4 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 9,517.7–26,759.6), and the DALYs rate was 30.7 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 10.5–71.1). In Eastern sub-Saharan Africa, the incidence rate was 16,341.5 cases per 100,000 people (95% CI: 9,465.1–31,815.8), and the DALYs rate was 28 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 9.6–63.7). In contrast, the DALYs in Western Europe, South Latin America, and Eastern Europe were the lowest, at 3.1 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 1.0–8.2), 5.1 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 1.7–14.2), and 6.0 per 100,000 people (95% CI: 2.0–17.4), respectively.

The results of a trend analysis showed that the prevalence rates have increased the most in South Western Saharan Africa (by 4.3%, 95% CI: 0.0–8.6), Australasia (by 3.6%, 95% CI: −0.4–7.6), and Central Europe (by 2.9%, 95% CI: −1.1–6.9), while those in South Saharan Africa (by −10.9%, 95% CI: −16.2– −5.3), high-income North America (by −5.9%, 95% CI: −9.6– −1.8), and East Asia (by −5.6%, 95% CI: −9.2– −1.2) have decreased the most. Based on the results of this detailed regional analysis, we can more accurately identify the achievements and challenges of different regions in the control of trichomoniasis. Such information provides a scientific basis for developing more targeted public health strategies and optimizing the allocation of resources.

Overview of data for 205 countries

By comprehensively analyzing the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs of trichomoniasis worldwide from 1990 to 2021, we observed some significant trends and differences at the national level. The incidence rate decreased from 179.8 million cases in 1990 to 134.7 million cases in 2021 (95% CI: 127.0–142.4 million cases). The highest incidence rates in 1990 were recorded in China (incidence rate: 179.8 million cases, 95% CI: 127.0–142.4 million cases), India (incidence rate: 179.8 million cases, 95% CI: 127.0–142.4 million cases), and the United States (incidence rate: 179.8 million cases, 95% CI: 127.0–142.4 million cases). However, in 2021, Nigeria rose to third, surpassing the U.S., with an incidence rate of 134.7 million cases (95% CI: 127.0–142.4 million cases). The highest rates of prevalence in 1990 were recorded in India (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), the United States (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), and Brazil (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), but by 2021, Nigeria had surpassed Brazil to achieve the highest prevalence rate of 110 million cases (95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases). In 2021, the highest prevalence rates were recorded in South Africa (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), Zambia (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), and Zimbabwe (prevalence rate: 110 million cases, 95% CI: 76.5–155.7 million cases), while the lowest prevalence rates were recorded in Belgium (prevalence rate: 1.1 million cases, 95% CI: 0.5–2.4 million cases), the Netherlands (prevalence rate: 1.1 million cases, 95% CI: 0.5–2.4 million cases), and Italy (prevalence rate: 1.1 million cases, 95% CI: 0.5–2.4 million cases). The number of DALYs increased from 1,277,000 in 1990 to 2,089,000 in 2021 (95% CI: 866,000–4,583,000). China, India, and Nigeria had the highest number of DALYs in both years. In 2021, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe had the highest number of DALYs (DALYs: 2,089,000, 95% CI: 866,000–4,583,000), while Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, and San Marino had the lowest number of DALYs (DALYs: 100,000, 95% CI: 30,000–270,000).

These findings are consistent with the results of our previous analysis of 21 regions, particularly for parameters such as the high incidence rates, prevalence rates, and DALYs in southern sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, and eastern sub-Saharan Africa. By combining these data at the national level and global trends, researchers can more accurately identify the achievements and challenges of different countries in the control of trichomoniasis. For example, the prevalence rates have increased the most in Kenya (increase of 4.3%, 95% CI: 0.0–8.6), Iran (increase of 3.6%, 95% CI: −0.4–7.6), and the Taiwan region of China (increase of 2.9%, 95% CI: −1.1–6.9), while those in Burkina Faso (decrease of 10.9%, 95% CI: −16.2– −5.3), Iraq (decrease of 5.9%, 95% CI: −9.6– −1.8), and Mozambique (decrease of 5.6%, 95% CI: −9.2– −1.2) have decreased the most.

Age and sex correlation

We examined the correlation between trichomoniasis and age as well as gender; we found differences in the risk of infection and disease burden among different populations. This information is important for formulating targeted public health strategies.

First, we found that the age group of 35–39 years had the highest incidence rate in 2021. This trend was evident in both genders, but the incidence rate decreased with age. Although the number of cases among men in this age group is quite high, the overall number of cases among women is higher, especially among young women who are 15–29 years old. This finding highlights the high-risk status of young women in trichomoniasis, suggesting that greater attention and more effective intervention are needed for this group (Fig. 3A).

Second, the analysis of the prevalence rate showed that the prevalence rate of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age gradually increases from the age of 15 to 19 years, reaches its peak between the ages of 30 and 34 years, and then remains stable. This indicates that women in this age group are more susceptible to infection, and the risk of infection does not decrease significantly thereafter. In contrast, although the prevalence rate among men reaches a peak value between 35 and 39 years, the overall prevalence rate is still lower than that among women. The prevalence rate is generally high among women who are 25 to 39 years old, which further highlights the high risk of trichomoniasis in adult women (Fig. 3B).

Finally, the analysis of DALY rates revealed that the DALY rate of trichomoniasis was low among men who were 15–49 years old, while for women, the DALY rate reached its peak value after the age of 30–34 years and then gradually decreased. These findings indicate that women suffer more severe health loss due to trichomoniasis, especially during the critical age range of 30–34 years (Fig. 3C).

To summarize, this study provides a scientific basis for formulating public health policies through a detailed analysis of the risk of infection and disease burden of T. vaginalis among different age and gender groups. Our findings emphasize the importance of implementing effective prevention and treatment strategies for young and adult women, especially those who are 30–34 years old. This study also emphasized the importance of strengthening health education and raising public awareness of trichomoniasis for preventing this disease. By adopting these measures, we can more effectively reduce the incidence and prevalence of trichomoniasis and alleviate its burden on the public health system.

DALYs and SDIs

By conducting 21 regional-level analyses, we determined the complex non-linear relationship between DALYs and SDI of trichomoniasis from 1990 to 2021. The ratio of DALYs fluctuated but generally increased as the SDI increased, indicating a close connection between socioeconomic status and the health burden of trichomoniasis (Fig. 4A). This information is extremely important for understanding the epidemiological characteristics of trichomoniasis in different regions and formulating corresponding public health strategies.

The DALYs of the regions that were above the global average, such as Oceania, Central Latin America, Tropical Latin America, high-income North America, the Caribbean, and Central Asia, were higher than the global average. These regions may face specific social, economic, or health challenges, leading to a heavier burden of trichomoniasis. In contrast, the DALYs of the regions that were below the global average, such as South Latin America, Australasia, South Asia, East Asia, Eastern Europe, high-income Asia-Pacific, North Africa and the Middle East, and Southeast Asia, were lower than the global average between 1990 and 2021. The burden of trichomoniasis may be lower in these regions due to the implementation of effective preventive measures, improvement of health services, or enhancement of socioeconomic conditions.

The decrease in DALYs caused by trichomoniasis in 2021, with the increase in the level of social and economic development at the national level, was similar to the pattern observed at the regional level. These findings suggest that improvement in the socioeconomic level can help alleviate the burden of trichomoniasis (Fig. 4B). Countries such as Zambia, South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Mozambique have DALYs far above the global average. Problems such as poverty, inadequate sanitation facilities, and low education levels may affect these countries, making it difficult to implement effective prevention and control measures. Countries with DALYs lower than the global average, such as Belgium, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, Israel, Cyprus, and Spain, may have better health systems, higher education levels, and favorable socioeconomic conditions; therefore, they may effectively control the spread of vaginal Trichomonas infection.

To summarize, the DALYs of trichomoniasis and sociodemographic indicators may have complex associations. At the regional and national levels, significant differences in DALYs were found among different regions, which may be related to local socioeconomic conditions, health facilities, education levels, and the effectiveness of prevention and control measures. To reduce the burden of trichomoniasis, all countries should develop and implement targeted prevention and control strategies based on their circumstances, improve health services and socioeconomic conditions, and strengthen health education and promotion efforts. These measures may reduce the burden of trichomoniasis on the global public health system and improve the quality of life of the affected individuals.

Risk factors associated with dalys

Comprehensive data showed a significant gender disparity in the disease burden caused by T. vaginalis among people of reproductive age, with the burden on men of the same age being significantly lower than that on women. In 2021, the DALY of reproductive-age women worldwide was 208,944 cases, while that of men of the same age was only 7,729 cases, with a male-to-female burden ratio of 27:1 (Fig. 5a). This indicates that women of reproductive age face more severe health consequences after infection. From the perspective of a temporal trend, the DALY of both reproductive-age women and men of the same age worldwide increased by 63% between 1990 and 2021, demonstrating that risk factors continue to have an effect (Supplementary Fig. 3). Regional differences are equally significant. For example, the DALY rate of reproductive-age women in Oceania was 4.3 times that of high-income Asia-Pacific regions, and the burden on men of the same age also shows a gradient distribution of “low-income regions > high-income regions“(Fig. 5b). These findings highlighted the necessity of strengthening targeted interventions in low-income and middle-income countries.

Application of the ARIMA prediction model to predict epidemiological trends in the treatment of trichomoniasis

The results of the trend analysis for the prevalence rate showed that different SDI regions present different change patterns. The prevalence rate in areas with high SDI first decreased and then increased. The prevalence rate globally and in regions with medium-to-high SDI first decreased, then increased, and finally decreased. The prevalence rate in the medium SDI and medium-low SDI regions increased every year. The prevalence rate in the low SDI region first decreased and then increased (Fig. 6A). These diverse trends reflect the different achievements and challenges in the control of trichomoniasis in various SDI regions, suggesting that targeted and differentiated strategies are needed in resource allocation and intervention measures.

Second, except for the trend in the low SDI region, which remained unchanged, the incidence rate in the global and all other SDI regions first decreased, then increased, and finally decreased again. This finding indicates that although the control of trichomoniasis has achieved desirable results globally during certain periods, the incidence rate of trichomoniasis may subsequently increase due to factors such as changes in resource allocation and the implementation of preventive measures, and then decrease again through strengthened control measures (Fig. 6B). This trend highlights that continuous monitoring and assessment of control measures is important, and sustained investment and effort are needed even after achieving initial success.

To summarize, this study revealed the importance of continuously monitoring and evaluating control measures for vaginal trichomoniasis on a global scale. By understanding the trends in different regions, public health decision-makers can precisely identify areas that require additional resources and intervention measures, thereby reducing the incidence and prevalence of trichomoniasis and improving the health of women more effectively worldwide. For example, in regions with medium SDI and medium-low SDI where the prevalence rate is increasing every year, preventive and control measures need to be enhanced, and the coverage and quality of health services need to be improved. This strongly influences improvements in the health of women worldwide and alleviation of the socioeconomic burden caused by trichomoniasis.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the global burden of T. vaginalis among reproductive-age women from 1990 to 2021. Our findings revealed that the incidence, prevalence, and DALYs were significantly and persistently different across regions with varying SDI. This highlighted that trichomoniasis is a condition deeply embedded within a framework of socioeconomic determinants. The divergent future trajectories predicted by the ARIMA model, specifically the “descending-ascending” trend in high and middle-high SDI regions, were different from the persistent increase predicted for middle-low SDI regions, highlighting a critical and potentially widening inequity in the global control of trichomoniasis. These insights can improve health policy planning, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where the burden is the greatest [22, 23].

Our analysis revealed a prominent geographical distribution of the trichomoniasis burden, with the heaviest load detected in sub-Saharan Africa. This pattern agrees with that reported in other studies in which high prevalence was found to be correlated with systemic challenges such as socioeconomic disadvantage, constrained healthcare access, and less robust public health infrastructure. The significantly high DALY values observed in regions like Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, Oceania, and Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, compared to the global average, are a direct consequence of strong socioeconomic inequalities, scarcity in medical resources, and financially constrained health systems. In contrast, the lower DALYs observed in high-SDI regions like Western Europe are probably mediated not only by economic development but also by other factors. They reflect the cumulative benefit of stronger healthcare infrastructure, higher general education levels, and more effective dissemination of knowledge on sexual health [24, 25]. This distinct geographical divide reinforces that trichomoniasis is a poverty-related infectious disease in which economic deprivation directly impedes the ability to seek diagnostics, afford treatment, and access preventive education.

An important finding of our study is the distinct evolutionary trend of trichomoniasis across the SDI spectrum. The predicted “descending-ascending” pattern in high and middle-high SDI regions suggests that even settings with initially strong intervention capabilities are not immune to the resurgence of T. vaginalis. This resurgence could be driven by a complex interplay of factors, including complacency in public health vigilance, the emergence and spread of drug-resistant strains of T. vaginalis, and the evolution of sexual behavior patterns [26–28].In contrast, the persistent upward trend predicted for middle-low SDI regions highlights that the challenge of disease control in resource-limited settings is hard to overcome; the implementation and sustainability of effective prevention and control programs are hampered by structural inequities [29, 30]. This divergence indicates that while developed regions must guard against resurgence through sustained effort, these underlying socioeconomic disparities need to be addressed to achieve sustainable control of trichomoniasis in many parts of the world, necessitating targeted external support and informed policy assistance [31].

In this study, we also defined the disproportionate burden borne by women of reproductive age, a problem that depends on an interplay of biological, social, and behavioral factors. The high gender disparity in DALYs—with a female to male ratio of 27:1—cannot be explained by biological factors alone. The associations between T. vaginalis infection and serious sequelae such as a high risk of HIV acquisition [12, 32], adverse pregnancy outcomes [30, 32, 33], and pelvic inflammatory disease make it a significant threat to reproductive and maternal-child health [34], The peak observed in DALYs among women of reproductive age aged 30–34 years highlights a crucial window of vulnerability that may be associated with patterns of sexual activity and childbirth. To alleviate this burden, a strategic public health response is needed that extends beyond traditional approaches [35]. The direct and evidence-based application of our findings [16] involves highlighting the importance of imparting systematic sexual health education from adolescence, along with providing greater access to free or inexpensive screening and treatment services integrated into broader reproductive healthcare.

Unsafe sexual behaviors are the key drivers shaping the risk landscape for trichomoniasis [29]. However, antimicrobial resistance severely limits the effectiveness of interventions. Some clinical studies have found that the administration of standard doses of metronidazole is now proving inadequate, with an increase in drug resistance [36]. This problem undermines past gains and makes future control efforts a challenging issue, highlighting that researchers need to move beyond one-size-fits-all treatment regimens. They need to prioritize the development and use of personalized dosing strategies [37] and novel therapeutic agents to ensure effective case management.

This study offers valuable insights into the global burden of T. vaginalis; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. The use of aggregated national-level data from the GBD database may obscure subnational heterogeneity, which is essential for localized policy-making. Additionally, while the ARIMA model is effective for trend analysis, it may not fully capture the impact of non-linear events, such as pandemics or rapid socioeconomic changes [9]. Moreover, the limited availability of individual-level data on behavioral and cultural risk factors restricts a more comprehensive understanding of the drivers of trichomoniasis transmission.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature highlighting the high burden of T. vaginalis in sub-Saharan Africa and other low-SDI regions. Similar to previous studies, we found that socioeconomic disadvantage and limited healthcare access significantly contribute to the disease burden. However, our study uniquely applies the ARIMA model to predict future trends, revealing divergent paths for different SDI regions. This approach provides a more granular and dynamic evidence base for targeted interventions.

Future studies must therefore prioritize investigations into the behavioral patterns of individuals in different socioeconomic contexts and rigorously evaluate the real-world efficacy of community-based interventions. Robust and sentinel surveillance for drug resistance, particularly among high-risk groups, is needed. Therefore, novel drug combinations and treatment strategies need to be implemented to counter the threat of resistance. Ultimately, international collaborations need to be strengthened for sharing resources, knowledge, and best practices. For policymakers, our findings highlight that resource allocation and intervention strategies must be decisively differentiated. In middle-low SDI regions, foundational public health measures need to be strengthened, healthcare access needs to be improved, and socioeconomic drivers need to be addressed; for this, constant international support is needed. In high-SDI regions, researchers must focus on preventing resurgence through vigilant surveillance, managing resistance, and ensuring continued coverage of high-risk subpopulations. Such multifaceted and tailored efforts in the global community may help mitigate the escalating and unequal health burden of trichomoniasis on women of reproductive age and advance the broader goal of reproductive health equity worldwide.

Conclusion

To summarize, by conducting a comprehensive analysis of the GBD data from 1990 to 2021, we found a significant and increasing global burden of trichomoniasis among women of reproductive age. We established a prominent socioeconomic gradient and found that the DALYs are closely and inversely associated with the SDI, with the highest burden concentrated in low-SDI and middle-SDI regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and Oceania. A key and innovative finding of this study is the prediction of divergent future trends using the ARIMA model. While high-SDI regions may experience a rebound, middle-SDI and low-SDI regions are projected to experience a persistent increase in incidence and prevalence, which indicates that inequity in global control efforts is ever-increasing.

In this study, we also quantified a strong gender disparity, with the disease burden in women of reproductive age being 27 times greater than that in men, highlighting that gender-specific public health interventions are needed urgently. The primary risk factor identified was unsafe sex, which indicates that behavioral interventions play an important role. The major contribution of this study involves its detailed, SDI-stratified quantification and its pioneering application of predictive modeling to project trichomoniasis trends, providing important evidence for the formulation and implementation of proactive and differentiated health policies. To effectively mitigate this escalating burden, global strategies must prioritize the promotion of sexual health education, expansion of accessible screening and treatment services in high-burden regions, and vigilant monitoring for emerging challenges such as antimicrobial resistance, thereby addressing the strong health disparities driven by socioeconomic determinants.
