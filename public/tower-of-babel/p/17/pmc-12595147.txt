Association between maternal serum levels of Alpha-Fetoprotein and eruption timing of the first central deciduous tooth: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elham Faghihian, Shamin Ghobadi, Rasool Rezaei, Zahra Saberi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current study investigated the relationship between the first central deciduous tooth eruption time and serum levels of maternal alpha-fetoprotein and related multiple of the median (MoM). The cross-sectional study was performed on a convenient sample of children aged 12 to 27 months in Isfahan City, Iran. Data regarding pediatrics’ gender, gestational age, delivery mode, maternal age, serum levels of alpha-fetoprotein at weeks 15–20 of pregnancy, and the age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption were collected using a researcher-made standard checklist. The generalized linear model was used to investigate the link between eruption timing of the first central deciduous tooth and maternal alpha-fetoprotein/MoM. In total, 151 children were included in the study. The mean age of the first central primary tooth eruption was significantly lower in full-term compared to preterm children (8.01 ± 2.06 vs. 9.77 ± 3.15 months; P = 0.02). An inverse linear association was observed between maternal alpha-fetoprotein expressed as MoM and eruption timing in preterm infants (β = –4.27; 95% CI: –7.72 to –0.83; P = 0.01). In full-term infants, the association also approached statistical significance (β = –0.93; 95% CI: –1.86 to 0.01; P = 0.05). For maternal alpha-fetoprotein levels (ng/ml), marginal associations with eruption timing were noted in both full-term (P = 0.08) and preterm infants (P = 0.05). The findings of this study suggest a potential inverse relationship between maternal alpha-fetoprotein levels and the timing of first primary tooth eruption, particularly among preterm infants. Further prospective studies with larger, more diverse populations are required to validate and expand upon these preliminary observations.Keywords: Eruption time, Deciduous tooth, Maternal Alpha-fetoprotein

Introduction

Tooth eruption is a natural physiological process that is defined as the movement of the tooth from its development site in the alveolar bone and gingivae to the occlusal plane in the oral cavity in a sequential manner (Almonaitiene et al. 2010). In most infants, the mandibular central incisor erupts as the first primary tooth, usually between 6 and 10 months of age (Tafti and Clark 2025). The eruption of primary teeth is believed to play an important role in the proper placement, spacing, and occlusion of permanent teeth (Sajjadian et al. 2010). Tooth eruption in a timely manner is also considered as an indicator of normal growth and development in children (Poureslami et al. 2015). Thus, the chronology of tooth eruption and the factors affecting it are of great importance in clinical practice.

Primary tooth eruption is a highly regulated process that is under the robust control of genes (Hughes et al. 2007). However, there is evidence regarding the impact of several other factors on the eruption timing of the primary teeth. For example, it has been found that children from various ethnic backgrounds exhibited differences in the timing of their primary teeth (Holman and Jones 2003; Almonaitiene et al. 2012; Kanagaratnam and Schluter 2012). In addition, several environmental factors like gestational age, exposure to tobacco smoke during pregnancy, birth weight, and socio-economic status are involved in the primary teeth eruption (Oziegbe et al. 2009; Aktoren et al. 2010; Pavičin et al. 2016; Żądzińska et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2019). However, there is no consensus regarding early life factors affecting primary teeth eruption.

The Life Course Health Development (LCHD) framework suggests that exposures during critical periods of prenatal and early postnatal life can have lasting impacts on physiological development. As a key component of the intrauterine environment, maternal biochemical markers are increasingly recognized as early determinants that shape the timing and quality of developmental processes (Hanson and Gluckman 2014; Gomes et al. 2019; Tong et al. 2024). Among them, alpha-fetoprotein, a fetal-derived glycoprotein produced by the yolk sac and fetal liver in early pregnancy, has long been used as a non-invasive screening marker for congenital abnormalities (Adigun et al. 2023). For example, low maternal alpha-fetoprotein is an indicator of Down Syndrome; however, its increased levels are linked with fetal open neural tube defects (Hay et al. 1976; Hu et al. 2020). Additionally, unexplained elevated maternal alpha-fetoprotein levels are associated with increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preeclampsia, fetal death, preterm birth, and low birth weight (Smith et al. 2006; Spencer et al. 2008; Baschat et al. 2014; Cohen et al. 2014; Dinglas et al. 2020).

From a developmental biology perspective, fetal signaling molecules are also involved in morphogenesis and tissue differentiation, including the formation of craniofacial and dental structures. These conceptual frameworks suggest that prenatal biochemical signals may influence the timing of key developmental milestones after birth, such as the eruption of primary teeth (Tucker and Sharpe 2004; Jheon et al. 2013). Therefore, we hypothesized that maternal alpha-fetoprotein levels may be used as a predictor of deciduous teeth eruption timing. Accordingly, the objective of the present study was to investigate the relationship between the timing of primary tooth eruption and maternal serum levels of alpha-fetoprotein and related multiple of the median (MoM) in a subset of Iranian infants.

Methods

The present cross-sectional study was conducted in Isfahan, Iran, on a convenient sample of children, whose mothers had been referred to Arya medical laboratory for pregnancy screening tests between gestational weeks 15 and 20 during the years 2020 to 2024. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Research Number: 340296). In addition, an informed consent form was obtained from each mother after providing an explanation about the objectives of the study. The sample size was estimated using a conventional effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.32, a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%. Based on these parameters, the required minimum sample size was calculated to be approximately 150 participants.

Children aged 12–27 months with complete medical records, whose mothers agreed to participate, were included in the study. Children diagnosed with neurological and metabolic disorders such as Down syndrome, intellectual disabilities, malignancies, genetic abnormalities, polycystic kidney disease, type I diabetes mellitus, thyroid, and liver diseases, and children whose mothers were diagnosed with hypothyroidism were excluded from the study. All clinical examinations and diagnoses were performed by a pediatrician affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences.

The age of first central primary tooth eruption was initially reported by the infant’s primary caregiver, typically the mother, during routine follow-up visits. This information was then confirmed through direct intraoral examination by trained pediatricians during the scheduled pediatric checkups. Only cases with clear documentation of the eruption date and confirmation by clinical observation were included in the analysis to ensure accuracy and consistency. Demographic and perinatal information (child’s gender, gestational age (preterm < 37 weeks and full-term > 37 weeks), delivery mode (cesarean and natural childbirth)) and maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein levels were extracted directly from the medical records of the mothers and infants. These data were extracted by a researcher (R.S) using a structured checklist designed for this study.

Serum alpha-fetoprotein levels (ng/ml) were measured from serum samples of pregnant mothers using a Hitachi Cobas_e411 electrochemiluminescence analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Then, the estimated values of maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein were converted to MoM.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 software and the value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The normality of quantitative variables was examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous and categorical variables were respectively presented as mean ± SD and frequency (percentage). To compare the timing of the first central deciduous tooth eruption based on delivery mode, children’s gender, and gestational age independent sample T-test was used. The correlation between first central deciduous tooth eruption time and maternal age, serum levels of maternal alpha-fetoprotein, and MoM was assessed by the Spearman correlation test. A generalized linear model was used to evaluate the association between the first central milk eruption time and serum levels of alpha-fetoprotein and related MoM both in the total sample and gestational age categories (preterm and full-term).

Results

In total, 151 mother–child pairs were included in the present study. Of these children, 47.02% (n = 71) were boys and 52.98% (n = 80) were girls, and the majority were full-term (n = 130, 86.09%). The mean age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption in the total sample was 8.25 ± 2.38 months. The mean maternal age, serum alpha-fetoprotein levels, and related MoM were 30.51 ± 5.45 years, 35.83 ± 13.19 ng/ml, and 1.05 ± 0.35 respectively. The frequency of mothers with cesarean delivery and natural childbirth was 66.23% (n = 100) and 33.77% (n = 51) respectively.

As presented in Fig. 1A, there was not any significant difference in mean age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption between the children of mothers who experienced cesarean delivery compared to children of those with natural childbirth (8.43 ± 1.95 vs. 8.15 ± 2.48 months; P = 0.49). Furthermore, the mean age of first central deciduous tooth eruption was slightly higher in girls than boys; but the difference was not statistically significant (8.42 ± 2.50 vs. 8.06 ± 2.09 months; P = 0.34) (Fig. 1B). Our findings indicated that the mean age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption was significantly lower in full-term children compared to preterm children (8.01 ± 2.06 vs. 9.77 ± 3.15 months; P = 0.02) (Fig. 1C).

The results of the Spearman correlation test showed an inverse relationship between the mean age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption and both maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein levels (r = –0.24; P = 0.01) and alpha-fetoprotein expressed as MoM (r = –0.29; P < 0.001). However, no significant relationship was found with maternal age (r = –0.05; P = 0.56). Generalized linear adjusted β coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for eruption timing in the total sample and by gestational age categories are presented in Table 1. After adjustment for gestational age, the generalized linear model showed an inverse linear association between eruption timing and both maternal alpha-fetoprotein (β = –0.03, 95% CI: –0.06 to –0.01; P = 0.02) and alpha-fetoprotein expressed as MoM (β = –1.35, 95% CI: –2.30 to –0.40; P = 0.01). Subgroup analysis by gestational age also revealed an inverse association between eruption timing and maternal alpha-fetoprotein expressed as MoM in preterm infants (β = –4.27, 95% CI: –7.72 to –0.83; P = 0.01). In full-term infants, the association with maternal alpha-fetoprotein expressed as MoM approached significance (β = –0.93, 95% CI: –1.86 to 0.01; P = 0.05). Additionally, marginally significant associations with maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein levels were observed in both full-term (β = –0.02, 95% CI: –0.05 to 0.01; P = 0.08) and preterm infants (β = –0.12, 95% CI: –0.23 to 0.01; P = 0.05).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the predictive role of alpha-fetoprotein in the eruption timing of the first central deciduous tooth in infants. The results of the study indicated a significant inverse linear association between the mean age of the first central deciduous tooth eruption and serum levels of maternal alpha-fetoprotein and MoM. Additionally, the results of regression analysis in subgroups of gestational age represented more prominent relationships in preterm infants.

The eruption time of the first tooth in children is an indicator of child’s development. The evaluation of primary teeth eruption time and correlated factors is a key tool in pediatrics’ dental health planning comprising preventive, diagnostic, and therapeutic strategies (Alnemer et al. 2017). Maternal factors during pregnancy are among the potential determinants of tooth eruption timing. Several studies have investigated maternal sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, showing that factors such as maternal smoking, socioeconomic status, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), and poor nutrition can influence the eruption pattern of primary teeth (Rantakallio and Mäkinen 1983; Ntani et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2019). These studies demonstrated that maternal smoking and socioeconomic status affect the eruption time of the first primary tooth. Additionally, insufficient maternal or neonatal vitamin D levels and prenatal exposure to heavy metals have been linked to delayed dental development and eruption (Ntani et al. 2015; Dhamo et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2020). These findings underscore the impact of intrauterine conditions on early oral health outcomes, including dental eruption.

A maternal characteristic that has received attention in previous studies is maternal age, although the evidence remains inconsistent. In contrast to our findings, some studies have reported a significant relationship between maternal age and the time of the first primary tooth eruption (Un Lam et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2019). However, other studies failed to find a significant relationship between maternal age at birth time and primary tooth eruption time (Sajjadian et al. 2010; Alnemer et al. 2017). These inconsistencies may be explained by differences in study design, sample size, and socioeconomic and nutritional characteristics of the studied populations. Moreover, genetic and ethnic variations across populations could also contribute to the controversial results.

To our knowledge, no study to date has specifically investigated the association between maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein levels and the timing of primary tooth eruption. In our study, we observed a significant inverse association between maternal serum levels of alpha-fetoprotein, measure in the second trimester of pregnancy and the timing of the first central deciduous tooth eruption. Although alpha-fetoprotein is routinely measured during the second trimester as part of prenatal screening, its biological relevance may extend beyond this period. Given that alpha-fetoprotein is synthesized from early gestation and may reflect various aspects of fetal development across pregnancy, future studies are encouraged to assess alpha-fetoprotein levels during the first and third trimesters as well. Such broader temporal evaluation may help determine whether gestational timing modifies the relationship between maternal alpha-fetoprotein and postnatal outcomes, including primary tooth eruption.

Additionally, our results indicated that the mean age of eruption was significantly higher in preterm infants compared to full-term infants as has also been reported in previous studies (Ntani et al. 2015; Dhamo et al. 2019). To further investigate the influence of gestational age, we assessed these associations using generalized linear models stratified by term status. Despite the smaller number of preterm infants (n = 21, 13.91%), the association in this group was stronger and statistically more robust, which may reflect a greater biological sensitivity to intrauterine biochemical exposures. However, the unequal distribution between term groups and the lack of adjustment for potential confounders may limit the precision and internal validity of the subgroup analyses. In another group, the association did not reach significance but approached the conventional threshold. Thus, while these trends may indicate a potential relationship, they should be interpreted with caution and validated in future studies involving larger and more diverse populations. Furthermore, in this study, comparisons were conducted based on gestational age (preterm vs. full-term), under the assumption that it correlates with birth weight. However, we acknowledge that direct inclusion of birth weight data would provide more accurate insights, and future studies are encouraged to incorporate this variable explicitly. Therefore, further population-based studies are required to evaluate the relationship between maternal factors, including alpha-fetoprotein, and eruption timing of primary teeth, while also accounting for other potential determinants and confounders.

The current study has several limitations that should be mentioned. First, it was conducted on a relatively small convenience sample of children from a central city in Iran, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric population. Regional differences in socio-demographic, genetic, nutritional, and environmental factors may influence both maternal biochemical profiles and developmental timelines, such as tooth eruption. Therefore, caution is required when extrapolating these results to other populations. Furthermore, due to the limited sample size, it was not possible to perform subgroup analyses based on children’s gender. Although we investigated the role of several important covariates in the analysis, other potentially influential factors, including maternal BMI, smoking status, nutritional status, socio-demographic characteristics such as maternal education, income level, and access to prenatal care, were not captured and remain to be explored in future studies. These factors may independently influence both maternal alpha-fetoprotein levels and the timing of primary tooth eruption, and their omission may introduce residual confounding. Future studies should aim to comprehensively control for these variables to strengthen causal inferences. Nevertheless, the findings of this preliminary study provide a foundation for designing larger, multi-center, population-based research to better understand the determinants of first central deciduous tooth eruption timing in early childhood.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein levels during pregnancy may be associated with the timing of the first central deciduous tooth eruption, particularly among preterm infants. However, given the observational design and the presence of uncontrolled confounding factors, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective, population-based studies with rigorous control of potential biases are warranted to confirm and expand upon these preliminary observations.
