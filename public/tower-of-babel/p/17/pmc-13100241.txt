Problem-solving tools to encourage cancer screening among low health literacy populations in Western New York
Authors: Ajay A Myneni, Yijun Liu, Laurene Tumiel-Berhalter, Jessica Kruger, Armin St George, Darryl Somayaji, Yaron Perry, Katia Noyes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated pre-post changes in readiness to engage in cancer screening among populations with low screening rates after using mobile problem-solving tools. Disseminating strategies for tool scale-up were examined.MethodsThe study was conducted among low-resourced communities in Western New York (2023–2024) across two cohorts; women eligible for breast cancer screening (BCS) and adults eligible for lung cancer screening (LCS). Tools utilized literacy-adjusted, culturally-tailored visual content, guided by focus groups and a community advisory board (CAB). Participants used the tools and completed pre-post (REDCap) surveys. Primary outcome was readiness-to-screen; secondary outcomes included screening-related knowledge, self-efficacy, and concerns. Two dissemination strategies were evaluated – targeted social media ads and printed flyers distributed in CAB-engaged areas.ResultsAfter reviewing the problem-solving tools, low knowledge, low self-efficacy, and high concerns decreased across cohorts, with significant improvements in the LCS group (p < 0.05). Increased readiness-to-screen was associated with current or past employment (OR: 13.95, 95% CI: 1.23, 157.84). Social media outreach generated 770,000 ad views and 1640 website visits but no participation while community engagement with 400 flyers yielded 100 inquiries and 59 participants.ConclusionLiteracy-adjusted, culturally-tailored tools may reduce barriers and enhance cancer screening engagement in low-resource communities, supported by trusted community partnerships.Keywords: Cancer screening, Breast cancer, Lung cancer, Problem-solving tools, Health literacy, Patient-centeredness

Introduction

Cancer screening reduces mortality and morbidity by detecting disease at earlier, more treatable stages (Division of Cancer Control and Population Sciences, 2022; U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, 2021). Although effective tests are widely available and covered by insurance, screening rates remain lowest among groups with the highest cancer burden, including people with low income, limited health literacy, and non-native English speakers (Champion, 1999; Lasser et al., 2011; Lei, 2022; Wittich et al., 2019).

Low uptake in these populations is associated with limited knowledge of screening and treatment availability, misunderstanding symptoms, cultural taboos around disclosing cancer family history or discussing certain body parts, and difficulty navigating healthcare and insurance systems (Lasser et al., 2011; Lei, 2022; Wittich et al., 2019). For the 20–30% of patients without a primary care provider, information often comes from social networks that reinforce misconceptions (Mahajan et al., 2021).

Patient-centered education tailored to appropriate literacy levels (often supplemented with video or a culturally-matching patient navigator), outperforms generic written materials and routine lectures in facilitating prevention behaviors (Ghisi, 2024; Hutchinson et al., 2016). However, such programs are resource-intensive, and difficult to adapt (ACP Decisions, 2019). Small media platforms (newsletters, blogs, niche social media community news outlets) increasingly use mobile video education that enables on-demand viewing and easy cultural and linguistic adaptation (such as multilingual voice-overs and ethnically diverse avatars) (Ciciriello et al., 2013; Cull et al., 1998; Kayler et al., 2021; Reuell, 2017). This builds trust, improves comprehension, and increases sharing, thus enhancing retention and behavior change (Ciciriello et al., 2013; Cull et al., 1998).

The current study primarily evaluated the impact of a culturally and literacy-tailored mobile problem-solving tool on patient understanding of the purpose and process of cancer screening, as well as their motivation to seek age- and risk-appropriate screening. Second, we assessed the feasibility and acceptability of tool dissemination and scale up using social media and active community engagement.

Methods

Study design and population

The study was conducted from 2023 to 2024 in the metro areas in and around Buffalo, New York (Western New York counties) focusing on zip codes with the lowest cancer screening rates, characterized by predominantly communities of color and lowest median income households. Two cohorts were included: (i) women eligible for breast cancer screening (BCS) and (ii) adults eligible for lung cancer screening (LCS) (U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, 2021; U. S. Preventive Services Task Force et al., 2024). For the BCS cohort, we recruited women aged ≥40 years with no prior breast cancer diagnosis. For the LCS cohort, we recruited participants aged between 50 and 80 years, who were current or former smokers (quitting within past 15 years) with a lifetime smoking exposure of at least 20 pack-years and who were not diagnosed with lung cancer (U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, 2021). All study participants were required to be able to read and communicate in English.

Development of problem-solving tools

The problem-solving tools were developed based on validated conceptual models to identify unique barriers to cancer screening not addressed by the current national and local cancer screening protocols (Supplementary Fig. 2) (Von Wagner et al., 2011). The study was designed by a multidisciplinary team of research and clinical faculty, care coordinators, nurse navigators, communication and multimedia experts, and guided by a community advisory board (CAB), comprised of trusted community leaders and residents from the target population. Based on recommendations from the CAB consisting of cancer care providers, patient advocates, cancer survivors and caregivers from the target communities and two focus groups with representatives from the target population (n = 22), the problem-solving tools included an informational motivational video, an interactive map of local health facilities and public transportation, and other resources to facilitate cancer screening (i.e., eligibility criteria, free services provided by county cancer support programs, Medicaid and marketplace health insurance enrollment, primary care clinics accepting new patients, evening and weekend clinic hours and an interactive feature that allows users to ask common questions about cancer screening processes). All the study materials and tools were accessible from the study website through weblinks. The interactive informational video contained web-links to a map tool, websites of reputable non-profit organizations, as well as federal, state and local department of health websites that provide detailed information on breast and lung cancer and available screening tests (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Informational videos on BCS and LCS were developed using a story-telling approach and narration was iteratively adjusted to a sixth-grade level on the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level test (Thomas et al., 1975). For the BCS video, we targeted African American women from underserved communities in the Buffalo metropolitan area and used a culturally-representative interactive avatar character (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the LCS video, narrative audio and recorded testaments from lung cancer patients from target communities were used. Designed to motivate and guide patients through screening logistics, the videos addressed such barriers as insurance, transportation, service eligibility, and fear of unexpected medical bills (Lau et al., 2020). Additionally, all other problem-solving tools mentioned above were embedded within the videos with hyperlinks for ease of use. The videos were revised using a multiple iterative approach based on feedback from two focus group meetings with cancer survivors from target communities and one additional review with the CAB members. Members of these panels were invited to small conference venues and study staff demonstrated the problem-solving tools, survey questionnaires and discussed methods of dissemination. Minutes from these meetings were recorded and the feedback was used to generate the final versions of the study tools.

The map tool, built using Google Maps™, showed directions to clinics and cancer screening centers, including a map of the public transportation routes and highlighting facilities with evening and weekend hours, including co-located facilities and mobile testing centers.

Dissemination strategies

Information about the cancer screening tools and study recruitment was disseminated in the community to assess acceptability, reach, and value of different dissemination strategies to support future scale-up of the tool. Two strategies were evaluated: an online social media campaign and a traditional snowball technique for community engagement (Faugier and Sargeant, 1997).

Online social media campaign

In collaboration with a social marketing company, the team developed a 3-month social media campaign, where images and animated snippets were set up to appear in target communities. Interested individuals were directed to the study website where they were invited to participate in the study. The social media views, visits to study website, participation and video views were tracked to assess the impact of the social media campaign.

Snowball technique for community engagement

In collaboration with community leaders and organizers, the study team developed snowball recruitment strategy (Faugier and Sargeant, 1997) and distributed 400 printed flyers at various sites including community health centers, public libraries, food pantries, and organizations serving medically underserved populations, to encourage use of the tools and study participation. Members of the CAB also distributed flyers and posters about the study through their followers and stakeholders.

Study recruitment

Online participants were redirected from Facebook™ ads or by scanning a QR-code on printed flyers to the study website, which contained embedded links to the informational video and access the survey questionnaires on University at Buffalo's REDCap™ platform. In-person participants used a mobile device (personal or one provided by the study staff) to view the videos and answered the pre- and post-video questionnaires on the mobile device or using paper forms.

A screening questionnaire was used to determine eligibility as described under “Study Setting” in the previous section. Informed consent was obtained from eligible participants before data collection. Participants were directed to complete a pre-video questionnaire, then view the informational video and explore the interactive decision tool. At any point participants were allowed to proceed to completing a post-video questionnaire.

Participants who completed both pre- and post-questionnaires were compensated for their time and efforts with a gift card valued at $25.

Measures (survey instruments and study outcomes)

The study questionnaires were developed based on published validated surveys (Clark et al., 2021; Gursoy et al., 2009; Housten et al., 2018; Lowenstein et al., 2016; Mann et al., 2000; Tanjasiri et al., 2008) to assess the key drivers of cancer screening behaviors as identified by the study conceptual model. Identical pre-video and post-video questionnaires were used to assess four decision making domains: “Knowledge”, “Self-efficacy”, “Concerns” and “Readiness-to-screen” (Supplementary Tables 1–2). To assess “Readiness-to-screen”, participants were asked to select the statement reflecting their readiness: “I am not thinking about getting screened” (0); “I'm beginning to think about getting screened” (1); “I'm seriously considering getting screened” (2); “I am ready to get screened” (3); “I already got screened (this year)” (4); and “I regularly get screened” (5). Improvement in “Readiness to screen” (yes/no) was considered the principal outcome. Participants scoring 3–5 in their pre-video response were excluded from this analysis (n = 27) because of the ceiling effect.

Additionally, the post-video questionnaire assessed ease of access, clarity and usefulness of the informational video and participant's willingness to share the video with family and friends (all binary yes/no). Demographics, access to technology, social media usage, and social and family support were also assessed. Numeric literacy was assessed by asking participants to answer a math problem involving probabilities. The assessments were pre-tested for clarity and duration by two test participants from target communities. Feedback from the test participants and members of the CAB was used to enhance clarity, brevity and formatting, to finalize the questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Differences in the median knowledge, self-efficacy and concerns scores scales from pre- to post-video assessments were evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Differences in participant characteristics were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and Chi-square/Fisher Exact tests for categorical variables.

Association of selected participant characteristics with improved readiness-to-screen was assessed using multivariate logistic regression. Covariates were added to the model as follows: race (Non-White vs. White), social media usage (>1/week vs. ≤1/week), employment status (ever-employed vs. unemployed/disabled), sex (female vs. male) and understanding statistical/qualitative information (correct response to a math problem). Full-time/part-time/self-employed participants were considered “ever-employed”.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and alpha was set at 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC). The study protocol and all materials were reviewed and approved by the University at Buffalo's Institutional Review Board.

Results

Dissemination strategies

Three months of the social media campaign generated 770,000 views and 1640 visits to the study website, 51 video-views (0.001% return) but no study participants. In-person engagement, distributing 400 printed study flyers with help from community collaborators resulted in 100 individuals being screened for participation and 59 (15 from scanning QR code on flyers, 34 using a mobile device at community venues and 10 used paper forms) reviewed the study materials (15% participation).

Study sample

The total sample included 59 participants (including 29 in BCS cohort and 30 in LCS cohort). The average age was 55 years, 42 (71%) were women, and 32% of individuals were white (Table 1). About 27% of participants had high school or lower education, 59% were ever-employed (currently employed or retired) and 49% had an annual household income of $25,000 or less. Most of the participants used a mobile phone to access the internet and send/receive text messages (95%) and watch online videos (85%). About 92% of participants had an email address that they accessed regularly and 76% of the participants used social media at least once a week.

Video effectiveness and outcomes

After reviewing the decision tools, the proportion of individuals with low knowledge and self-efficacy and high concerns declined for the entire cohort; however statistically significant changes were detected in the LCS cohort only, but not in BCS cohort (p < 0.05, Fig. 1B and C). Similarly, in the BCS cohort, the proportion of participants with improvement in readiness-to-screen (80%) was much higher than the LCS cohort (32%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

In a bivariate analysis, participants who reported improvements in their readiness to screen had higher annual household income (p = 0.04), more likely to be ever-employed (80% vs. 34%, p = 0.01), and more likely to have numeric literacy (87% vs. 47%, p = 0.03) compared to those who did not show improvement. Similarly, in a multivariate analysis improvement in readiness-to-screen was positively associated with being ever-employed (OR: 13.95, 95% CI: 1.23–157.84), while no association was found with sex, race, social media usage and literacy level (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that mobile patient-tailored problem-solving tools have potential to improve patient knowledge, motivation and ease of pursuing age- and risk-appropriate cancer screening in low-resourced communities. Assistance and encouragement from trusted community partners is crucial for supporting the use of mobile clinical decision-making platforms among these populations. In these communities, in-person engagement through trusted community organizations resulted in a higher success rate compared to the targeted social media ads campaign, suggesting that social media alone is not sufficient to effectively convert knowledge to action. Our findings demonstrated that strategies to enhance cancer screening must be tailored to each cancer based on screening eligibility and barriers to access. However, the tools had limited effect on individuals with very low SES, literacy or technical skills.

Small media (Reuell, 2017) has become increasingly effective at improving patient skills and adherence to guideline-concordant care across diverse conditions (Ciciriello et al., 2013; Cull et al., 1998; Kayler et al., 2021). However, data on small media's ability to prompt specific patient actions (e.g., increasing cancer screening in low-resource communities) is limited and inconsistent. Only two studies have evaluated small-media interventions for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening among Spanish-speaking patients, and none have examined breast or lung cancer screening. Aragones et al. (2010) found a significant increase in CRC screening among Latino immigrants in NYC (18% vs 55%, p < 0.05) who watched an educational video and were trained to discuss CRC screening with their primary care providers (Aragones et al., 2010). In contrast, Jerant et al. (2014) found no difference in fecal occult blood test uptake in a national cohort (22% vs 23%, p > 0.05) who received socio-psychologically tailored training on CRC screening (Jerant et al., 2014). The current study contributes to evidence that culturally tailored tools can improve participants' knowledge, self-efficacy, and readiness for cancer screenings while reducing concerns. It also underscores small media's potential to address cultural, logistical, and linguistic barriers to cancer screening that disproportionately affect individuals with low health literacy.

In the current study, improved readiness-to-screen was positively associated with ever being employed, a key indicator of socioeconomic status (SES) and adequate literacy (Sundquist and Johansson, 1997). Employment provides a stable income, improving access to resources like food, housing, education, and healthcare. Previous studies show a positive link between higher SES and cancer screening participation, with women in higher SES groups more likely to undergo breast, colorectal, and cervical screenings, even in countries with universal healthcare (Weisband et al., 2021). Kurani et al. (2020) found that individuals in more deprived and rural areas faced greater barriers to screening and had lower participation rates (Kurani et al., 2020). These findings highlight the significant impact of SES on health behaviors and the need for targeted interventions to improve cancer screening in low SES populations.

This study also demonstrated that cancer screening promotion programs must be tailored to specific cancer due to variations in guidelines, screening rates, patient knowledge, and awareness, which can be overwhelming and confusing for patients. This aligns with previous studies showing that media messages can raise awareness and intentions to screen for cancer but have limited impact beyond that (Rimer, 1997; Curry et al., 2003). Future interventions should combine informational campaigns with patient navigation, community support, and healthcare provider reminders to address unique patient risk factors and eligibility questions.

Despite its potential to quickly reach large audiences, our targeted social media campaigns were less effective than in-person engagement. While social media offers wider penetration, it had limited impact on our target population—senior or middle-aged individuals with low health literacy or living in low-resourced communities. Community partnerships are essential for delivering culturally sensitive messages and engaging populations with limited access to healthcare (Wallerstein and Duran, 2006). These partnerships help bridge gaps by offering resources like client referrals and in-person education, ensuring information is accessible, relevant, and trusted (Subramanian et al., 2026).

Our study has several limitations. First, nearly half of the participants reported receiving regular screenings at baseline which led to a smaller sample size for our analysis on improvement in readiness after excluding them. Additionally, successful dissemination requires time and efforts to build strong collaboration with community partners; without their engagement, relying solely on social media may limit participation. Some participants in this study scored worse in knowledge, self-efficacy, or concerns after watching the video. The informational video provided a great deal of information, spanning topics from basic knowledge on breast and lung cancer, to accessible resources for screening and finding coverage options where needed, which may have been overwhelming for some individuals to process in a short period of time. Consequently, participants may have been confused by the new information or felt more concerned after watching the video, contributing to the decline in scores in some areas.

Despite these limitations, this study is innovative in using a culturally tailored, multi-dimensional approach to promote cancer screening in low-health-literacy populations. It offers practical solutions to real-world challenges such as transportation barriers, and uses engaging, culturally appropriate tools. Moreover, participants represented a range of educational levels, income brackets, and employment statuses, and lived in under-resourced neighborhoods, allowing for a broad understanding of their diverse perspectives and opinions.

Conclusion

Mobile problem-solving tools that combine motivational and informational components and are tailored to different cancer types, patient literacy levels, cultural and socio-economic factors, and geographic areas have a potential to improve users' readiness to receive screening, especially if promoted by trusted community partners and organizations. Furthermore, using new health technology capabilities to adapt decision support and motivation tools to unique patient needs does not pose an additional burden on healthcare providers who often do not have resources or expertise in patient-centered culturally tailored education and lack time to go over all the details about screening processes during office visits.

In summary, larger and more studies are needed to assess the long-term effects of educational interventions on cancer screening rates and to explore more effective methods for reducing screening disparities across different socioeconomic and demographic populations.
