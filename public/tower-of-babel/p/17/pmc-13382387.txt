Metabolic and textural changes in whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) during frozen storage: Key quality markers revealed by integrated metabolomics and lipidomics
Authors: Yameng Li, Di Huang, Danhe Wang, Qinxiong Rao, Weiguo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhiteleg shrimp is prone to quality deterioration during frozen storage, yet the underlying metabolic patterns remain unclear. This study integrated metabolomics and lipidomics using a two-step extraction to elucidate the biochemical basis of texture degradation at −18 °C and − 80 °C. A total of 190 metabolites and 698 lipids were identified, with amino acids and glycerophospholipids predominating. Frozen storage induced marked and overlapping metabolic alterations, characterized by downregulation of amino acids (e.g., β-leucine, proline) and upregulation of PUFA-enriched lipids, acetylcarnitines, and ceramides. Network analysis indicated a metabolic shift from amino acid catabolism to fatty acid β-oxidation, with β-leucine and acetylcarnitines as key hubs. KEGG analysis highlighted phenylalanine and proline metabolism in the cold response. Both temperatures activated an antioxidant lipid network involving sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids. β-leucine, L-phenylalanine, and proline were identified as potential targets for preservation strategies, offering insights for metabolically guided shrimp quality control and precision cold-chain management.Keywords: Whiteleg shrimp, Frozen storage, Metabolomics, Lipidomics, Quality markers

Introduction

As the most widely farmed crustacean species globally, whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei, L. vannamei) has become a key component of China's seafood processing and export industries because of its high nutritional value, including rich muscle protein (13.8%) and abundant flavor-enhancing amino acids such as glutamic and aspartic acids (Naser, Sarker, & Hosain, 2022). Despite continuous growth in annual output driven by advances in intensive aquaculture, shrimp—like most seafood—are highly prone to quality deterioration after harvest (Pan, Chen, Hao, & Yang, 2019). This fragility mainly stems from their high moisture content, active endogenous enzymes, and delicate muscle texture, leading to postharvest losses of up to 25–30% (S. Peng et al., 2022). Currently, freezing at low temperatures is the primary method used to preserve the quality of L. vannamei. However, this technique has a clear double-edged effect: while it suppresses microbial activity and enzymatic reactions, ice crystal formation during freezing damages cell membranes, causing osmotic imbalance and protein denaturation (Pan et al., 2022). These structural changes lead to drip loss, texture softening, and lower consumer acceptance after thawing, ultimately resulting in significant economic loss. In this study, “frozen storage” refers to preservation at −18 °C and − 80 °C, representing conventional industrial freezing and ultra-low-temperature storage, respectively. Comparing these two temperatures helps distinguish biochemical responses that are dependent on storage temperature from deterioration associated with freezing in general.

Growing evidence suggests that frozen-storage-induced quality decline is not solely a macroscopic physical process but is accompanied by substantial metabolic perturbations(Cheng, Mei, & Xie, 2023). Shrimp muscle contains not only umami-related metabolites but also a lipid-rich membrane system, including phospholipids (18.5% of total fat) and bioactive lipids, which are crucial for maintaining membrane integrity and muscle quality (Tu et al., 2022). Nevertheless, most existing research has emphasized physicochemical indices and moisture distribution, while the multidimensional metabolic changes underlying texture degradation remains insufficiently understood (Liu et al., 2024). In particular, how central metabolism and lipid remodeling interact under low-temperature stress, and whether key molecular signatures differ between −18 °C and − 80 °C storage, remain open questions that limit the development of mechanistically informed preservation strategies.

Metabolomics has been increasingly used to link storage-related biochemical changes with freshness and stability in crustaceans (Yang et al., 2025). For example, targeted metabolomics has shown that nucleotide degradation and free amino acid metabolism in mud crab are strongly associated with the K-value, a common freshness index, mainly through the breakdown of ATP-related compounds (Lin et al., 2022). However, while targeted metabolomics enables precise quantification of specific compounds such as ATP derivatives and flavor-related amino acids, it remains constrained by its narrow focus on predefined metabolites, limiting a comprehensive understanding of global metabolic regulation. In contrast, untargeted metabolomics provides a broader view of metabolic disturbances during storage and has revealed alterations in pathways such as purine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and amino-acid-related metabolism in shrimp (Solenocera melantho) (Yu et al., 2022). Importantly, most available evidence is derived from a single metabolomics layer and provides limited insight into the coupling between lipid remodeling and central metabolism. Lipid oxidation and remodeling of glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids may exacerbate membrane damage and generate secondary products that interact with amino acid metabolism, thereby amplifying quality deterioration (Guo, Le, Lu, Yang, & He, 2024). Therefore, integrating metabolomics with lipidomics is essential to elucidate coordinated-metabolite lipid networks that underpin texture degradation during frozen storage.

Previous studies on shrimp frozen storage have mainly focused on macroscopic quality indices or single omics profiling. Therefore, coordinated metabolite and lipid remodeling associated with texture deterioration remains insufficiently resolved. In the present study, untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics were integrated to investigate the biochemical basis of texture deterioration in L. vannamei muscle during frozen storage. Paired metabolomic and lipidomic profiles were obtained from the same biological material using a two-step extraction, with methanol–water for polar compounds followed by dichloromethane–methanol for nonpolar lipids. The novelty of this work also lies in comparing two representative conditions, −18 °C (industrial frozen storage) and − 80 °C (ultra-low-temperature storage), to evaluate biochemical alterations associated with frozen storage. It was hypothesized that frozen storage induces coordinated changes in amino acid related metabolism and membrane lipid remodeling, and that these integrated alterations may contribute to tissue deterioration, with distinct patterns between −18 °C and − 80 °C. Accordingly, the objectives were to: (1) establish an integrated annotation profile of metabolites and lipids in L. vannamei muscle; (2) identify key molecular changes and pathways associated with frozen storage and texture deterioration across the two temperatures; and (3) delineate metabolite and lipid interaction networks to screen potential quality markers that can support targeted preservation strategies and precision cold-chain quality management.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The HPLC-MS grade methanol (MeOH), acetonitrile, formic acid (FA), ammonium acetate (CH3COONH4), and isopropanol (IPA) were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, U.S.A.). Dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) of HPLC grade was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.). Pure water (H2O) (18.2 MΩ) for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was purified using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, France).

Preparation of shrimp samples

This study involved three groups of L. vannamei collected from major production areas in Shanghai, China: Chongming District (Group A; n = 36), Fengxian District (Group B; n = 36), and Pudong District (Group C; n = 36), resulting in a total of 108 individuals. Detailed sampling information is provided in Table S1. These three sites were selected because they represent major L. vannamei production areas in Shanghai. Shrimp from the three locations were primarily collected to establish metabolomic and lipidomic fingerprint profiles of L. vannamei muscle.

For the subsequent frozen storage experiment and molecular change analysis, only shrimp from Group A (Chongming District) were used. Therefore, geographical origin was held constant and samples from Groups B and C were not included in comparisons among storage conditions. The shrimp, with an average weight of 16.66 g, were collected in June 2024 and transported to our laboratory on dry ice within 4 h. Upon arrival, muscle tissue from each individual was divided into two portions for texture profile analysis (TPA) and omics analyses, respectively. For TPA, muscle was taken from a standardized anatomical region between the second and third abdominal segments for all individuals. The omics sample was obtained from the remaining untested muscle from the same region (not subjected to compression), which was then finely minced and thoroughly mixed prior to extraction to improve sample homogeneity.

For TPA, within Group A, 30 individuals were randomly selected, and each individual muscle sample was treated as one independent biological replicate. These 30 replicates were randomly assigned to three storage conditions: day 0 (fresh control), −18 °C for 1 month (industrial frozen storage), and − 80 °C for 1 month (ultra-low-temperature storage), resulting in n = 10 independent individuals per condition. For metabolomics and lipidomics, pooled composite biological replicates were prepared within Group A by combining equal masses of muscle tissue (2 g per individual) from six randomly selected individuals to form one pooled replicate (12 g per pool). In total, six pooled replicates were prepared (n = 6). Each pooled replicate was then divided into three equal aliquots and assigned to the three storage conditions (day 0, −18 °C for 1 month, and − 80 °C for 1 month), yielding n = 6 pooled replicates per condition (18 aliquots in total). This aliquoting strategy ensured identical starting material across storage conditions for omics comparisons. After storage, samples were subjected to further analyses.

Texture properties analysis (TPA)

The TPA measurement method for L. vannamei was consistent with that previously described (Xiong et al., 2025). Hardness, springiness, and chewiness were measured using a texture analyzer (TAXT2i, Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, England). A TPA P/50 probe was moved downward onto the region between the second and third abdominal segments of the samples. According to the instrument instructions, the parameters were set as follows: the test speed was 1.0 mm/s, two cycles, and the extrusion cycle interval was 5 s, with compression to 50% deformation. TPA was performed on 10 independent shrimp individuals per storage condition (n = 10), randomly selected from the corresponding condition. For each individual shrimp, TPA measurements were performed in triplicate at the same anatomical location and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Metabolite extraction

A modified two-step extraction strategy was adopted and optimized to sequentially recover polar metabolites and nonpolar lipids from the same muscle sample (methanol–water extraction followed by dichloromethane–methanol extraction of the residual solids), as described in prior studies (Y. M. Li et al., 2023; Want et al., 2013). The extraction methods for metabolites from L. vannamei muscle samples are as follows. Fifty milligrams of muscle tissue from each sample were placed in an Eppendorf tube and dissolved in 1.5 mL of 50% (v/v) methanol solution. The mixtures were then homogenized by ultrasound for 10 min at 4 °C. The samples were allowed to stand at −20 °C for 2 h and purified by centrifugation (15,000 rpm; 10 min) at 4 °C. The supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and used for metabolite analysis, while the residual solids were reserved for lipid extraction.

Lipid extraction

The residual solids were mixed with 1.6 mL of precooled extractant (dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂): methanol = 3:1, v/v). After vortex mixing, the mixture was ultrasonically homogenized at 4 °C for 10 min. The sample was then incubated on ice for 10 min with intermittent vortex mixing. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were carefully transferred to a new centrifuge tube, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at room temperature, and the residue was reconstituted in 120 μL of isopropanol: acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) prior to injection.

Analysis of metabolic profile

Chromatographic separation was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Dionex, Boston, MA, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm × 1.7 μm, Waters) at 40 °C. The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile/water (50:50, v/v) with 5 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile/water (95:5, v/v) with 5 mM ammonium acetate. The gradient conditions were as follows: 98% B for 0–1 min; 98–50% B for 1–17 min; 50% B for 17–21 min; 50–98% B for 21–22 min; and 98% B for 22–25 min. The injection volume was 2 μL, and the sample tray was maintained at 4 °C.

Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on a Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operated in both positive and negative ion modes (Thermo, Boston, MA, USA). The parameters were as follows: spray voltage of 3.00 kV; sheath gas pressure of 30 psi; auxiliary gas pressure of 10 arbitrary units; capillary temperature of 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature of 350 °C; scan mode: full MS (70,000 resolution) - dd/MS2 (17,500 resolution, NCE 30 eV); and scan range: 100–1500 m/z.

Analysis of lipid profiles

A processed tissue extract (4 μL) was injected into an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo, Boston, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an XBridge C18 column (100 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm particle size). Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile/water (40:60, v/v) with 5 mM ammonium acetate, while phase B comprised acetonitrile (10%), isopropanol (90%), and 5 mM ammonium acetate. The gradient elution was as follows: 0 min, 30% B; 0–2 min, 40% B; 2–4 min, 60% B; 4–8 min, 75% B; 8–16 min, 80% B; 16–18 min, 85% B; 18–19 min, 95% B; 19–19.1 min, 30% B; and 19.1–20 min, 30% B. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min.

HRMS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in both positive and negative ESI modes, with full scan acquisition ranging from 100 to 1500 m/z. Electrospray ionization voltages were set to 3.5 kV (positive) and − 4.0 kV (negative). For lipid identification, MS/MS analysis was performed with a collision energy of 30 eV. The ion source gas temperature was set to 300 °C, with a drying gas flow rate of 10 L/min and sheath gas pressure of 50 psi.

Quality control

The quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining 10 μL of each sample. Initially, a set of QC samples was analyzed to assess instrument stability and repeatability. Throughout the analytical run, all samples were injected randomly, with QC samples injected at regular intervals after every six samples. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) of the QC samples were monitored to ensure instrument stability during injections. Before each batch analysis, the mass spectrometer was calibrated using Thermo Scientific Pierce FlexMix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For omics analyses, each sample was extracted once and analyzed with a single injection, while pooled QC injections were used to monitor analytical stability and signal drift throughout the run.

Data processing and statistical analysis

TPA parameters (Fig. 3) and lipid subclass intensities (Fig. 6B) were graphed and statistically analyzed using Origin 8.0 and SPSS 25.0, with results presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine statistical significance (p < 0.05). Before conducting the ANOVA, we assessed data normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and the homogeneity of variances using the Brown-Forsythe test. Dunnett's post-hoc comparisons were applied to data passing normality and homogeneity tests. For data that did not meet these assumptions, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon test in SPSS 25.0 to identify significant differences. In these figures, error bars indicate SD, and asterisks denote statistical significance versus fresh control (pre-storage), with * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, where applicable.

No internal standards were added for metabolomics or lipidomics; thus, quantification was performed in a relative manner based on feature peak areas (intensities). Data from untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics, obtained using the Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer, were uploaded into Compound Discover 3.3 and Lipidsearch 5.1 software for normalization, peak detection, and compound identification. For identified ions, we excluded those with peak areas over 1000 in the solvent, under 1000 in QC samples, and those with RSDs exceeding 30%, to ensure data quality. The filtered compounds were identified based on retention time, mass accuracy, isotope ratio, and MS/MS spectra similarity, matching against commercial and public databases, such as mzCloud, LipidBlast, and MassBank. Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and metabolic pathway analysis were conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), while heatmaps were generated with Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Correlation analyses were performed using OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool. Additionally, |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value <0.05 were used as criteria for identifying differential metabolites and lipids, where fold change indicated relative abundance differences between groups, and the adjusted p-value represented significant differences after Student's t-test.

Results and discussion

Method validation

Metabolites and lipids in L. vannamei muscle were characterized using untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics, respectively. The base peak chromatograms (BPCs) of the six QC samples were overlaid and compared in both positive and negative ion modes to assess the reliability of the metabolite and lipid detection (Fig. S1 A-S1B). As shown in Fig. S1C, the QC samples from L. vannamei muscle clustered together, indicating a high-quality dataset. The consistency observed in the QC samples demonstrated a reliable detection system with excellent reproducibility in terms of compound abundance and diversity. Consequently, the data obtained were deemed suitable for further analysis.

Comprehensive metabolite and lipid profiling of L. vannamei muscle

The metabolite profile in L. vannamei muscle

The comprehensive metabolite profile of L. vannamei muscle was established using untargeted metabolomics on a Q-Exactive Orbitrap system in both positive and negative ionization modes. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) of the mixed QC sample demonstrated excellent complexity and separation of metabolites (Fig. 1A). Rigorous screening based on retention time, accurate mass, MS/MS spectra, and isotope patterns led to the identification of 190 metabolites, with 160 successfully annotated using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). These metabolites were classified into 9 superclasses, spanning 41 classes and 67 subclasses (Fig. 1B). Organic acids and derivatives made up the largest proportion (29.40%), followed by organoheterocyclic compounds (16.88%), lipids and lipid-like molecules (15.63%), benzenoids (12.50%), and nucleosides, nucleotides, and their analogues (11.88%). At the subclass level, the most prevalent subclasses included amino acids, peptides, and analogues, fatty acids and conjugates, carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates, nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues, fatty acid esters, and purines and purine derivatives, together accounting for 48.13% of the total identified metabolites (Fig. 1B). This metabolic diversity highlights the biochemical complexity of L. vannamei muscle. Previous studies have consistently emphasized the importance of amino acids and fatty acids in various crustacean species, such as shrimp and crab (Xing et al., 2023). These compounds not only serve as precursors for protein synthesis and lipid metabolism but also play essential roles in maintaining muscle integrity and enhancing nutritional and sensory qualities of seafood (Bu et al., 2022).

As shown in Fig. 1C, the relative abundance of metabolites was quantified, with arginine identified as the predominant compound, followed by proline, 5-aminovaleric acid, anthranilic acid, 3′-adenosine monophosphate (3’-AMP), hypoxanthine, 5′-adenosine monophosphate (5’-AMP), and β-leucine. These metabolites are closely associated with essential physiological functions. Specifically, arginine plays a crucial role in osmoregulation and immune function through its involvement in the urea cycle and nitric oxide biosynthesis. Proline, identified as an osmoprotectant under high-salinity conditions, supports cellular hydration and enhances postharvest muscle quality (Zhang et al., 2024). Nucleotides, including 3’-AMP and 5’-AMP, are key contributors to seafood flavor, serving as precursors to umami compounds like inosinic acid and hypoxanthine during postmortem degradation (Gao et al., 2021). Additionally, 5-aminovaleric acid and anthranilic acid, intermediates in lysine and tryptophan metabolism, reflect active energy metabolism and antioxidative defense, underscoring their adaptive significance in response to environmental stress (Darlington et al., 2010). Collectively, these findings highlight the integrated metabolic architecture of L. vannamei, emphasizing the crucial roles of amino acids and nucleotides in supporting fundamental physiological processes and enhancing sensory qualities, thereby contributing significantly to L. vannamei quality and consumer preference.

The lipid profile in L. vannamei muscle

A total of 698 lipid species, covering six major categories and 22 subclasses, were identified in L. vannamei muscle using the Q-Exactive Orbitrap system, confirming the robustness and efficiency of the analytical platform (Fig. 2A, B). Among the six categories, glycerophospholipids (GPs) exhibited the greatest subclass diversity (11 subclasses), followed by glycerolipids (GLs, 4 subclasses), sphingolipids (SPs, 3 subclasses), free fatty acids (FAs, 2 subclasses), and both prenol lipids (PRs) and sterol lipids (STs), each with a single subclass (Fig. 2C). In terms of composition, phosphatidylcholine (PC) was the most abundant lipid, representing 21.20%, followed by triglycerides (TG, 19.91%) and diglycerides (DG, 9.03%) (Fig. 2B-2C). These results highlight the structural diversity of lipids in L. vannamei muscle, with PC, TG, and DG being the predominant components.

Notably, PC, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and ceramides (Cer, a subclass of SPs) were the most abundant lipids in L. vannamei, based on intensity levels (Fig. 2D). These findings are consistent with the lipid profiles of other crustaceans, which also show higher abundances of GPs, predominantly PC and PE. PC and PE are key structural components of cellular membranes, performing vital functions such as membrane biogenesis, modulation of lipid bilayer fluidity, and cell signaling, all of which help maintain membrane integrity (Han et al., 2024). In addition, Cer was the most abundant sphingolipid in L. vannamei (Fig. 2D). As a central molecule in sphingolipid metabolism, Cer enhances environmental adaptability and immune defense in marine organisms, regulating biological activities like stress responses, energy storage, and signal transduction (Liu et al., 2024; J. Wang, Yu, Ran, & Wei, 2024). Collectively, these findings provide valuable insights into lipid diversity and functionality in L. vannamei, identifying PC, PE, and Cer as critical components that support physiological homeostasis, enhance stress tolerance, and help preserve nutritional quality.

The textural properties of L. vannamei after frozen storage

Hardness, springiness, and chewiness are key indicators of food texture, reflecting both structural integrity and sensory perception. In this study, frozen storage significantly altered these properties in L. vannamei muscle (Fig. 3A–C). Both −18 °C and − 80 °C freezing treatments reduced hardness, with the −80 °C group showing the most significant decrease (p < 0.01). Similarly, springiness decreased in both frozen groups, with the −18 °C group showing the greatest reduction (p < 0.01). Of the three parameters, chewiness showed the largest decline, with values dropping by 60.16% and 60.02% in the −18 °C and − 80 °C groups, respectively (p < 0.001). These substantial losses indicate a severe disruption in muscle structure following freezing.

The observed texture deterioration is consistent with microstructural disruption reported in previous studies during freeze–thawing, which has been associated with ice crystal growth, dehydration, and protein conformational changes (Zhang et al., 2025). Additionally, ice crystal formation and growth during freezing are associated with muscle fiber disruption and dehydration, which can lead to reduced mechanical resistance after thawing (D. Li, Zhu, & Sun, 2018; Zhang et al., 2025). These textural changes not only degrade sensory quality but may indicate metabolic and structural reprogramming, as shown by untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics in later analyses. The omics data provide molecular context that is consistent with the observed texture loss. Specifically, the decrease in proline and other amino-acid-related metabolites may be consistent with weakened osmolyte-associated protection during frozen storage. Meanwhile, lipidomic changes indicative of membrane lipid remodeling (e.g., decreases in major GPs such as PC and PE) may reflect altered membrane properties, which could facilitate solute leakage and water redistribution during thawing and thus be associated with reduced hardness and springiness. These interpretations are based on associations between texture outcomes and omics patterns and are supported by prior literature; direct measurements of water-holding capacity loss and protein structural changes were not performed in this study, which is a limitation.

Frozen storage-induced metabolomic and lipidomic changes in L. vannamei

Variation pattern of differential metabolites during storage

Frozen storage at −18 °C and − 80 °C was used to simulate conventional industrial freezing and ultra-low temperature storage, respectively. Metabolite changes were more pronounced at −80 °C, likely due to stress responses linked to phase transitions and enzyme inhibition during ultra-low temperature adaptation. To examine these metabolic differences, PCA was performed on samples stored at both temperatures. The results revealed clear metabolic divergence between fresh (pre-storage) and stored samples (Fig. 4A). PC1 explained 60% of the variance, and PC2 accounted for 7.1% (Fig. 4A). A distinct separation was observed between pre-storage and stored samples, indicating marked changes in metabolic profiles during storage. Using strict selection criteria (adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2(fold change) | ≥ 1), we identified 100 differential metabolites in the −18 °C group (23 upregulated, 77 downregulated) and 109 in the −80 °C group (18 upregulated, 91 downregulated) (Fig. 4B). Notably, 94 metabolites were shared between the two storage conditions, with 93 showing consistent trends: 74 downregulated and 19 upregulated (Fig. 4B, Fig. S3A). This overlap suggests that low-temperature storage triggered a global metabolic response in L. vannamei.

K-means clustering of the 93 shared metabolites identified three groups (Fig. 4C). Cluster I (48 metabolites) mainly consisted of amino acids like proline, betaine, and purine derivatives like 2′-deoxyinosine, all of which showed significant decreases (Fig. 4D). Proline has been reported to reduce thawing loss and partially preserve texture attributes in shrimp under freeze–thaw conditions, particularly springiness and chewiness, potentially by alleviating ice-recrystallization-induced structural disruption and the associated water loss (Zhang et al., 2024). As a compatible osmolyte, proline is also discussed as supporting cellular hydration and water–protein interactions; accordingly, reduced proline availability may be consistent with weaker hydration buffering and enhanced thaw-related water migration, potentially coinciding with lower water-holding capacity and texture softening (N. Peng et al., 2025). Consistently, betaine has been described as an osmoregulatory compound that helps maintain cellular water balance and minimize drip loss during thawing, and has been associated with improved water-holding capacity during frozen storage in muscle foods (Bokharaeian & Kaki, 2025). Cluster II (26 metabolites) included β-leucine, L-phenylalanine, and fatty acids like arachidonic acid and oleic acid, all of which were also significantly downregulated (Fig. 4D). The decrease in fatty acids is relevant because unsaturated fatty acids are important precursors of oxidative breakdown products and volatile compounds associated with flavor deterioration. In aquatic products, low-temperature storage has repeatedly been associated with oxidation- and hydrolysis-related depletion of lipid precursors, especially unsaturated species, as shown in shrimp, hairtail, and pacific saury (Fang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Thus, the reduction of arachidonic acid and oleic acid in the present study may reflect ongoing oxidative consumption of flavor-related substrates during frozen storage. In contrast, cluster III (19 metabolites) featured the accumulation of compounds like L-pyroglutamic acid and d-glucose-6-phosphate, which regulate redox homeostasis and energy metabolism (Fig. 4D). Carbohydrates, as cryoprotectants, can accumulate via the pentose phosphate pathway under cold stress to maintain osmotic balance and redox homeostasis. This energy metabolism reprogramming aligns with stress responses induced by low storage temperatures. A similar correlation between carbohydrate accumulation and altitude-related temperature decreases was observed, further supporting this hypothesis (Sun et al., 2024). In summary, these clustering patterns reveal coordinated changes in amino acids, fatty acids, and carbohydrates during storage, suggesting that these shifts regulate energy metabolism and oxidative balance, which influence L. vannamei storage quality.

To identify key metabolites under storage stress, OPLS-DA was performed (Fig. S2A—B). VIP scores >1 and the top 20 biomarkers with |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value <0.05 were selected (Fig. 5A-B). Of these, 15 metabolites were shared between the two storage conditions, spanning 8 subclasses, with amino acids being the predominant group (5 metabolites, Table 1). Notably, L-phenylalanine was significant in both the −18 °C (VIP = 2.35) and − 80 °C (VIP = 3.07) groups (Fig. 5A-B, Table 1). L-Phenylalanine is essential for protein biosynthesis and the synthesis of volatile flavor compounds (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde) and secondary metabolites (e.g., flavonoids) via the phenylpropanoid pathway. This amino acid regulates stress responses and energy metabolism (Ghasemi, Kumleh, Kordrostami, & Rezadoost, 2023). The significant downregulation of L-phenylalanine under both storage conditions may indicate reduced availability of precursors for protein synthesis and aroma-related metabolites, which could be associated with weakened oxidative resilience and quality decline during storage. Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong positive correlations among the 15 shared metabolites (Fig. 5C). For example, β-leucine showed strong correlations with guvacoline (r = 0.91), proline (r = 0.96), 7-methylguanine (r = 0.94), acetylcholine (r = 0.96), and β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (r = 0.91), suggesting β-leucine may act as a metabolic hub and be involved in coordinated metabolic changes during storage. In conclusion, the metabolic adaptation observed in L. vannamei during low-temperature stress, particularly the coordinated regulation of amino acids (e.g., proline, L-phenylalanine, β-leucine), fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid), and energy metabolites (e.g., d-glucose-6-phosphate), suggests systemic stress responses that adjust metabolic networks to mitigate oxidative damage. Collectively, these results indicate that frozen storage induced a systematic reshaping of amino-acid, fatty-acid, and energy metabolism in L. vannamei. Among the altered metabolites, proline, β-leucine, and L-phenylalanine appear to be particularly informative as candidate markers associated with frozen-storage stress and quality deterioration.

Variation pattern of differential lipids during storage

To assess lipid composition changes in L. vannamei during 30 days of storage, PCA was performed on samples collected before and after freezing. The PCA results showed clear differences in lipid profiles at −18 °C, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 39.4% and 24.3% of the total variance, respectively. Samples stored at −80 °C showed smaller compositional changes compared to the fresh group (Fig. 6A). This greater lipid stability may be related to stronger inhibition of residual lipase and phospholipase activities and reduced molecular mobility at ultra-low temperatures. In addition, reduced ice recrystallization and restricted membrane lipid fluidity at −80 °C may limit membrane disruption, enzyme–substrate interactions, and subsequent lipid hydrolysis and oxidation compared with storage at −18 °C. To examine specific lipid subclass responses, changes in lipid intensities were compared between pre- and post-storage samples. Most GP subclasses decreased under both low-temperature conditions (Fig. 6B). At −18 °C, PC, CL, and PG decreased significantly by 22.59%, 28.14%, and 50.74%, respectively, while at −80 °C, PE and PEt decreased by 9.27% and 28.14%. This overall decline in GPs is consistent with previous lipidomic studies on L. vannamei and other aquatic products, in which PC and PE were among the lipid classes most susceptible to low-temperature storage, oxidation, and hydrolysis. Similar decreases in major membrane phospholipids have also been reported in frozen hairtail, pacific saury, and dried shrimp during storage, supporting the view that GP depletion is a common response to cold-induced lipid deterioration (Fang et al., 2022; L. Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). In this context, the present results suggest that frozen storage in L. vannamei involved progressive membrane lipid consumption and remodeling. Interestingly, despite the overall decrease in GPs, PS, LPE, and LPI increased significantly at −18 °C by 26.38%, 78.78%, and 114.81%, respectively (Fig. 6B). This pattern is notable because storage-related increases in lysophospholipids (LPLs), accompanied by decreases in their parent phospholipids, have also been observed in shrimp and hairtail lipidomics studies (Fang et al., 2022; Wang, Yu, et al., 2024; Yan, Jiao, Fang, Benjakul, & Zhang, 2022), and are generally interpreted as evidence of phospholipid hydrolysis and membrane remodeling. Therefore, the concurrent decrease in major phospholipids and increase in selected LPLs in the present study may indicate enhanced phospholipase-driven membrane turnover under frozen storage. Such remodeling may represent an adaptive or damage-associated response, but it may also imply greater membrane fragility and altered permeability during thawing, thereby facilitating solute leakage and water redistribution. In the sphingolipid (SP) subclass, Cer increased by 15.39% at −18 °C (Fig. 6B). Cer is widely recognized as a bioactive stress lipid, and their accumulation is often associated with membrane stress and cellular injury responses (Hu, Zhao, Wang, Sun, & Wang, 2022). Thus, the increase in Cer observed here may reflect activation of stress-associated SP remodeling during frozen storage. In contrast, Hex1Cer and CerPE decreased by 43.93% and 25.89% at −80 °C, possibly due to suppression of their biosynthetic pathways under ultra-low temperatures.

Differential lipids were identified using adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂ (fold change)| ≥ 1. A Venn diagram (Fig. 6C) revealed 125 differential lipids at −18 °C (63 upregulated and 62 downregulated) and 55 at −80 °C (all upregulated). Fifty lipids were shared across both storage conditions and consistently upregulated, suggesting common regulatory mechanisms under subzero stress (Fig. 6C; Fig. S3B). To further explore these shared lipids, their fatty acid compositions and variation patterns were analyzed. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 6D), the 50 upregulated lipids were classified into saturated (SFAs), monounsaturated (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). The most notable changes occurred in PUFAs, particularly those containing EPA (20:5) and DHA (22:6). Lipid species such as AcCa (20:5), PS (19:0_20:5), PA (22:6_22:6), and LPI (20:5) were significantly upregulated under both storage conditions, indicating selective accumulation of EPA/DHA-containing lipids during frozen storage (Fig. 6D). This pattern suggests that PUFA-containing lipids are common targets of frozen-storage stress across aquatic species. In both L. vannamei and pacific saury, highly unsaturated lipids were major targets of low-temperature storage, and the lipid response was more active under conventional frozen storage conditions, particularly at −18 °C (Zhao et al., 2025). However, the direction of lipid remodeling differed between the two species. In L. vannamei, the shared differential lipids were mainly represented by EPA/DHA-containing PA, PS, LPI, and AcCa species, all of which were consistently upregulated under both storage temperatures, whereas pacific saury showed marked downregulation of multiple EPA/DHA-containing TG species (Zhao et al., 2025). This contrast indicates a species-specific lipid adaptation pattern, likely reflecting differences in lipid storage form and remodeling strategy between crustaceans and lipid-rich fish. Notably, comparable phospholipid depletion and LPL enrichment have also been reported in other crustaceans, such as crayfish, supporting membrane-lipid remodeling as a conserved crustacean response to low-temperature storage (Tan, Guan, & Bai, 2025), although the specific EPA/DHA-containing lipid species altered in L. vannamei appear to be more concentrated in PA, PS, LPI, and AcCa.

Integrative analysis of significantly altered metabolites and lipids

Interaction network of significantly altered metabolites and lipids

To explore the dynamic interactions between key metabolites and lipids during L. vannamei storage, we constructed a correlation network containing 15 overlapping metabolites and 17 lipids. Network topology analysis identified β-leucine (centrality = 17) and proline (centrality = 16)—both significantly downregulated—as core metabolic nodes. These amino acids showed strong negative correlations with seven upregulated acylcarnitines (AcCa 11:1–10:0; r = −0.90 to −0.63) (Fig. 7A). From a biological perspective, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) such as leucine are important components of muscle nitrogen balance and can contribute carbon skeletons to energy metabolism; thus, changes in β-leucine may reflect altered amino-acid availability and catabolic utilization during frozen storage. AcCa are widely regarded as indicators of fatty-acid transport and β-oxidation status. Their accumulation is often interpreted as a shift toward enhanced fatty-acid utilization or incomplete β-oxidation under stress conditions (Dambrova et al., 2022). In the present study, the negative association between AcCa and β-leucine (Fig. 7A) may be compatible with a tendency for frozen storage to increase reliance on lipid-based energy utilization relative to amino acid metabolism. Proline showed a similar negative relationship with AcCa (Fig. 7A), which is notable because proline has been implicated in osmotic adjustment and oxidative-stress protection in frozen shrimp. Taken together, these patterns suggest that as storage progressed, metabolites (β-leucine and proline) linked to osmoprotection and amino-acid homeostasis declined, while lipid-derived intermediates (AcCa) accumulated, which may reflect a reorganization of energy and stress-response metabolism.

In contrast to this antagonism, β-d-fructose-6-phosphate showed positive correlations with AcCa (11:0–11:1) and the phospholipid PA (18:2_18:2) (r = 0.82–0.86; Fig. 7A). This pattern may suggest coordinated glycolytic and fatty-acid-associated energy processes that support energy balance. Similar cross-talk has been observed in tumor metabolism, where glycolysis–FAO coupling supports energy homeostasis (Patrick & Tak, 2012). In addition, β-d-fructose-6-phosphate can enter the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to generate NADPH, providing reducing power for phospholipid synthesis enzymes such as glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT) (Boulanger, Sabag-Daigle, Thirugnanasambantham, Gopalan, & Ahmer, 2021). This may explain its positive association with PA (18:2_18:2). Overall, these findings reveal a dual regulatory mode—competition and synergy—governing metabolic adaptation during storage. The competitive relationship between BCAAs and AcCa, together with the synergistic interaction between glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation, is consistent with a metabolic shift from amino acid catabolism to lipid oxidation and membrane lipid synthesis. Notably, these interpretations are based on correlation patterns from omics data; direct evidence for protein structural changes, membrane permeability, or water-holding capacity was not obtained in the present study.

Metabolic pathways involving significantly altered metabolites and lipids

Based on pathway enrichment and network analysis of 15 key metabolites and 17 lipids, this study revealed cascade perturbations in major metabolic pathways and their regulatory influence on the overall network during L. vannamei storage. Pathway enrichment results (Fig. 7B) showed that aromatic amino acid biosynthesis (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan) had the highest impact factor (0.5), followed by phenylalanine metabolism (0.4), highlighting its central role in the global metabolic network. Network mapping further indicated that L-phenylalanine acts as a metabolic hub, linking multiple pathways and regulating downstream reactions (Fig. 7C). As an essential aromatic amino acid, L-phenylalanine is closely tied to muscle protein turnover and nitrogen balance, and it also serves as a precursor for aroma-related metabolites. The pronounced reduction in L-phenylalanine after storage (fold change ≤0.34, Fig. 7C) may be associated with reduced precursor availability for desirable volatiles and a decline in freshness-related sensory characteristics. This interpretation agrees with previous metabolomic studies in oysters, in which storage-induced changes in amino acids and nucleotide-related metabolites were closely linked to freshness deterioration and flavor shifts (Dou, Zhu, Chen, Zou, & Xia, 2025). In addition, arginine and proline metabolism and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism were also significantly enriched (Fig. 7B), accompanied by reduced levels of nicotinamide, nicotinamide D-ribonucleotide, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and proline (Fig. 7C, Table 1). Nicotinamide and its derivative are essential for NAD+ biosynthesis, which supports mitochondrial energy metabolism and oxidative stress defense. Their depletion may indicate compromised NAD+-related metabolism, which could be associated with altered energy and redox status during frozen storage. The decline in GABA, a metabolite involved in osmotic regulation and stress signaling, further indicates weakened stress adaptation. Similarly, the reduction in proline, which has been discussed as an osmoprotectant in frozen aquatic products, may indicate weakened osmotic-stress buffering capacity during cold storage (Choudhary, Wani, Naeem, Khan, & Aftab, 2022; N. Peng et al., 2025). Together, these results indicate a coordinated disruption of energy metabolism and oxidative stress pathways, contributing to metabolic changes and quality deterioration. Regulating NAD+ biosynthesis and proline metabolism may therefore help improve the frozen storage stability of L. vannamei.

Sphingolipid metabolism was also significantly enriched during storage (Fig. 7B), with marked upregulation of ceramide species including Cer (d20:1_5:0), Cer (d18:1_2:0), and Cer (d18:1_4:0) (Fig. 7C, Table 2). As a central component of sphingolipid metabolism, Cer has been widely implicated in lipid remodeling under low-temperature stress (Song et al., 2022). Therefore, the observed increase in Cer may reflect a stress-related shift in membrane lipid composition during frozen storage. Freezing-induced cellular disruption has been associated with membrane rupture, enlarged intercellular spaces, and increased water loss in shrimp muscle after thawing, providing a plausible basis for linking membrane lipid remodeling with texture deterioration (S. Wang et al., 2021). Consistently, low-temperature stress has been reported to induce coordinated remodeling of sphingolipids, including Cer, and GPs, further highlighting the sensitivity of membrane lipids to temperature perturbation. Taken together, these findings suggest that the altered sphingolipid profile may be associated with membrane restructuring and oxidative-response processes during storage.

In parallel, glycerophospholipid metabolism was also enriched (Fig. 7B). Notably, PA (22:6_22:6) and LPI (20:5) were strongly upregulated (Fig. 7C), indicating selective accumulation of glycerophospholipid-derived species containing highly PUFAs. When considered together with the subclass-level decreases in PC and PE and the concurrent increases in LPLs, these results support the view that membrane lipid remodeling is a major feature of frozen-storage stress in L. vannamei. Similar patterns of GP depletion coupled with LPL enrichment have been reported in dried shrimp and crayfish during chilled or frozen storage (Tan et al., 2025; L. Wang et al., 2024), suggesting that this response may be broadly conserved in aquatic muscle tissues. In contrast, the specific lipid species accumulated in the present study, particularly PUFA-containing PA and LPI species, may reflect membrane adaptation that is shaped by both temperature and species.

More broadly, the integrated results illustrate dynamic remodeling of metabolic networks during storage. Aromatic amino acid metabolism may be more closely associated with flavor decline, arginine and proline metabolism may be related to redox balance, and sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid remodeling may reflect structural adaptation or injury of cell membranes during storage. Together, these pathways form an integrated network associated with quality deterioration in L. vannamei during storage. These findings identify β-leucine, L-phenylalanine, and proline as candidate markers for cold-chain monitoring. In commercial settings, these markers could be monitored by targeted LC–MS assays for quality control and trend analysis, and potentially by rapid biosensor-based approaches for on-site screening. Among them, proline may be of particular interest because, in addition to its association with frozen-storage quality changes, previous studies in L. vannamei suggest that it may also act as a cryoprotective compound by reducing thawing loss and limiting structural damage during frozen storage or freeze–thaw fluctuation (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Although −80 °C preserved lipid homeostasis more effectively than −18 °C in the present study, its routine industrial application may be limited by higher energy demand and cost; thus, improving quality retention under conventional −18 °C storage may be more practically relevant.

Conclusion

Frozen storage induced coordinated metabolic adaptation and membrane lipid remodeling in L. vannamei. These changes formed an integrated molecular response associated with quality deterioration during storage. The main affected processes included aromatic amino acid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid remodeling. Together, these pathways may be linked to flavor decline, texture loss, and disruption of membrane homeostasis under frozen-storage stress. From an applied perspective, β-leucine, L-phenylalanine, and proline may have potential utility in cold-chain quality management. The altered pathways identified here also provide a basis for future targeted preservation strategies. Among these markers, proline may be of particular interest because it may serve both as a candidate monitoring marker and as a potential food-grade cryoprotective compound. Although −80 °C preserved lipid homeostasis more effectively than −18 °C, its routine industrial use may be constrained by higher energy demand and cost. Therefore, improving quality retention under conventional −18 °C storage may be more practically relevant for commercial shrimp cold chains. Overall, this study provides a molecular basis for precision quality control and targeted preservation in frozen shrimp. Future studies should define quantitative thresholds for key markers, validate rapid detection approaches in industrial settings, and further link omics-derived indicators with structural, physicochemical, and sensory quality traits.
