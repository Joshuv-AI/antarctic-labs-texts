Challenges and recommendations for embracing digital tools for one health surveillance in LMICs: Viewpoints from a special session of 17th ISVEE
Authors: Thang Nguyen-Tien, Fred Unger, Terdsak Yano, Florence Mutua, Elizabeth A.J. Cook, Hu Suk Lee, Bernard Bett, Hung Nguyen-Viet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The adoption of digital tools presents a critical opportunity to enhance One Health initiatives in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). At the 17th International Symposium of Veterinary Epidemiology and Economics (ISVEE17), a special session explored the role of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) applications in addressing zoonoses, animal diseases, food safety, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Asia and Africa countries. This paper highlights key insights from that session, indicating the barriers and opportunities for ICT implementation and expansion including user engagement and incentives, data quality and standardization, infrastructure, evidence and policy advocacy, capacity building, network and knowledge exchange, sustainability. The necessity of co-design, infrastructure investment, applications integration, interoperability and collaboration among international and national organizations, cross-sectoral and interdisciplinary approaches, continuous training and evidence-based policy support and sustainable funding models are crucial for building effective, inclusive, and scalable ICT-driven solutions.

Introduction

In an increasingly interconnected world, the risks posed by zoonotic diseases, AMR, and foodborne illnesses present growing challenges, particularly in LMICs. ICT provides a framework for interdisciplinary collaborations that are required to operationalize One Health. Disease surveillance is an example where ICT frameworks have been applied for collecting, integrating and reporting health events among diverse actors at local, regional or international levels. In the situation of limited capacity of animal health services in LMICs, ICTs can boost not only livestock productivity, but also facilitate the reduction the risks of one health issues associated with zoonoses, food safety and AMR [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

The ISVEE17 was held from 11 to 15 November 2024 in Sydney, Australia. This symposium brought together a global community of researchers, policymakers, and professionals to discuss a diverse range of topics in veterinary epidemiology, economics, and One Health including disease surveillance, digital transformation, policy development, and the intersection of animal, human, and environmental health. The event provided a critical platform for knowledge exchange, capacity building, and strengthening international networks to improve One Health and animal health systems worldwide.

On November 14, 2024, the International Livestock Research Institute (ILRI) hosted a special session titled “Adoption of digital transformation to deal with One Health issues among the LMICs”. This session aimed to present a snapshot picture about the current situation of several regional ICT works in Asia and Africa and discuss the extension of ICT applications to control and mitigate the burden of animal, zoonotic diseases, food safety, and AMR. This paper synthesizes key insights from that session, emphasizing challenges and recommendations for embracing ICT applications for One Health surveillance in LMICs.

Showcases examples of selected ICT solutions that have been piloted or implemented in different countries

In the starting presentation, Dr. Fred Unger synthesized the ICT applications for animal and zoonotic diseases surveillance and reporting from ASEAN countries. A national system, so called iSIKHNAS, of Indonesia enables local veterinarians and farmers to report disease outbreaks, track vaccination records, and monitor animal movements, which helps in preventing and controlling the spread of Transboundary Animal Diseases. The Philippine Animal Health Information System (Phil-AHIS) focuses on collecting, analyzing, and disseminating animal health data to support disease surveillance, early warning systems, and better coordination between local and national veterinary authorities. The Vietnam Animal Health Information System (VAHIS) was designed to collect data on livestock health, disease outbreaks, and vaccinations, aiming to improve reporting and enhance coordination among government agencies involved in animal health management. Thailand used mobile technology and geospatial mapping to monitor disease outbreaks and animal health status and support rapid response for veterinary authorities. He also mentioned some other ICT applications which were project-based such as Participatory One Health Digital Disease Detection (PODD) of Thailand and Farmvetcare mobile applications of ICT4Health project in Vietnam or from international organization like World Organization of Animal Health (WOAH).

Next, Dr. Bernard Bett introduced a web-based decision-support tool to forecast Rift Valley fever (RVF) risk and suggest interventions based on historical data collected between 1990 and 2024 in Kenya. The tool is designed and hosted on-line using Python web libraries (Fig. 1). Using random Forest model, he and his team identified rainfall, altitude, and soil type and slope variance as the most significant predictors of RVF risk. This tool also provided predictions at individual administrative units, improving the targeted surveillance of RVF, particularly in remote areas that might otherwise be overlooked. By combining advanced machine learning with a structured intervention framework, this web-based decision-support tool enhances the ability to predict outbreaks and implement appropriate control measures at the local level.Fig. 1– Dashboard of web-based decision support tool for RVF prevention and control.Fig. 1

Dr. Terdsak Yano continued the third presentation with his project about PODD surveillance system which was implemented in Thailand since 2015. The cornerstone of the system was a mobile application initiative, also named PODD (Fig. 2), which was developed through extensive community engagement. To facilitate real-time monitoring of health events, a dashboard was created for local government (LG) officials. This dashboard enables LGs to identify and respond to abnormal health patterns within their communities before seeking external support. In 2020, the PODD system successfully detected 11 cases of anomalous backyard chicken mortality in Chiang Mai. In the following phase, the One-Health Toolkits (OHTK) was developed to facilitate the system's implementation in Lao PDR and other countries. A key finding of this study is the potential for engaged LG users to become PODD contributors. By co-creating new features, these individuals can further enhance the system's capacity to protect their communities from health threats. The participatory nature of PODD, particularly the active involvement of LGs, has emerged as a crucial factor in its success. LGs have played a pivotal role in promoting PODD within their networks and inspiring broader participation in the system's development [4,5].Fig. 2– Collaboration flow of PODD.Fig. 2

Then, Dr. Elizabeth Cook presented her project on using mobile syndromic surveillance at slaughter to understand the incidence and distribution of important animal and zoonotic diseases in Uganda. The meat inspectors were trained to use Open Data Kit (ODK) on a mobile phone to report findings from meat inspection at abattoirs (Fig. 3). The preliminary results of this study highlighted the presence of diseases of public health importance in animals at slaughter in Uganda and indicated regional hotspots for the pathogens. The effectiveness of mobile syndromic surveillance during slaughter relies on the motivation and active participation of individuals, alongside successful alignment with governmental policies and priorities.Fig. 3– Data collection by mobile phone of meat inspector (Innocent Obilil).Fig. 3

Dr. Thang Nguyen-Tien's talk emphasized the importance of One Health approach involving stakeholders from the animal, human, and environmental sectors and mobile applications to deal with the Transboundary Animal and Emerging Zoonotic Diseases (TAEZDs) in two northern provinces of Vietnam. In the framework of the project titled “Improving human health through sustainable value chains in human-animal-environmental interactions using ICT in Vietnam” or ICT4Health, three mobile applications, named Farmvetcare, for local department of animal health, veterinarians and farmers (Fig. 4) were recently developed and piloted utilizing in 2023–2024 to improve the timeliness and accuracy of surveillance and response efforts targeting TAEZDs including African swine fever, Foot-and-mouth disease, Leptospirosis, Avian Influenza, and Streptococcus suis.Fig. 4– Farmvetcare mobile applications' principles.Fig. 4

Dr. Florence Mutua showcased the development of an ICT-based system and its use in monitoring veterinary drug use in smallholder poultry systems of Kenya from May 2023 to December 2023. The ICT system was designed and validated in meetings with key stakeholders. The pilot exercise involved veterinary pharmacy outlets (agrovets) and poultry farms. The agrovet module captured data on veterinary drugs that the outlets sold each day. The farmer module focused on diseases reports and medications given. The data were captured using tablets by agrovets and smart phones by farmers. 12 farmers and 14 agrovets participated in the pilot with over 15,000 records being received from agrovets [1]. There are ongoing discussions on how the system can support surveillance activities nationally but also in counties where piloting was done.

While giving their remarks, the panelists expressed ideas and experiences on practical approaches that can be used to enhance the use of ICT applications. To be more specific, the chairman and panelists provided insights and discussed about the challenges of ICT adoption and sustainability strategies to ensure long-term success in LMICs.

Critical challenges in ICT adoption and scalability

The presentations given highlighted several challenges related to ICT implementation. For example, meat inspectors in Uganda faced multiple obstacles, including theft of mobile devices and lack of internet access. The presence of numerous digital tools led to fatigue due to the duplication of reporting activities, as paper-based documentation was still required alongside digital systems.

In LMICs in general, where electricity supply and internet connectivity remain unreliable, implementing and scaling of ICT applications is particularly challenging [6]. Moreover, encouraging individuals to dedicate time to digital reporting or using mobile applications is also difficult. A lack of incentives or benefits discouraged active participation of users. However, if the users of ICT systems receive any incentives or benefits including the in-time information and feedback from the applications such as potential disease outbreaks, farm biosecurity measures etc., they generally appreciate what they were being offered. Responsiveness and flexibility are critical attributes for digital surveillance systems operating in the dynamic and complex environments typical of LMICs. Given the unpredictable nature of zoonotic outbreaks, environmental hazards, and shifting socio-political contexts, digital platforms must be designed to accommodate real-time data integration from diverse sectors such as human health, animal health, and environmental monitoring. Flexibility enables these systems to evolve alongside emerging needs, such as integrating mobile data collection tools in hard-to-reach areas, or incorporating novel data types (e.g., bioaerosol samples or environmental deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or participatory disease reporting by communities). The current ICT projects are facing the challenges on how to make their applications more responsive and flexible in the continuously changing context. As One Health surveillance systems mature, stakeholders increasingly demand digital platforms that support broader and more integrated functionalities; such as modules for animal movement tracking, early warning capabilities, geospatial visualizations, and predictive modeling, climate risk overlays, or offline data entry capabilities that enhance the practical value of digital tools in decision-making. Moreover, frontline users often request customization features, offline functionality, and interoperability with existing national or sectoral databases. Besides, factors such as the predominance of small-scale farming, close interactions between humans, animals, and wildlife, as well as the standardized validation mechanisms for ensuring data quality, were identified as key barriers to ICT adoption. In LMICs, meeting these demands remains a challenge due to limited technical capacity, funding constraints, and misalignment between system designers and end-users [6]. Failure to incorporate user-driven enhancements can lead to system abandonment or parallel data collection efforts, undermining the efficiency of surveillance operations. In terms of a specific example for disease forecasting tools, the major challenge lies with the access to reliable climate variables that are relevant for short to medium-term risk analyses. Local and international partnerships have been established to support further development of tools, knowledge and infrastructure that is required for production of these data.

Strategic recommendations for ICT sustainable implementation and expansion

The panel discussion emphasizes a paradigm shift is urgently needed, moving toward co-design and co-creation of digital tools with the active participation of local stakeholders, particularly those at the frontline of data gathering and disease monitoring. The critical role of LGs and farmers and veterinarians in using mobile applications of PODD or ICT4Health or AMR projects were the clear evidence that ICT applications need to reach to grass-root level as key actors for early detection, reporting and response compliance. As well as, high-level policy commitments and cross-sector collaboration is essential to institutionalize ICT use in national surveillance strategies, promote inter-ministerial collaboration, and allocate dedicated funding. To realize the full potential of ICT in One Health, systems must be interoperable that can be capable of exchanging and analyzing data in the real-time basis, particularly by decision makers and across sectors. Aligning ICT systems across human, animal, and environmental health fosters a holistic approach.

Another strategy that needs attention is to foster continuous training, networks and knowledge exchange. Regional and global networks that facilitate peer learning, technical exchange, and collaborative innovation can accelerate digital transformation. To deal with the challenges from infrastructure conditions of LMICs, blending mobile applications with centralized, web-based dashboards enables both real-time field data capture and response systems. Mobile applications with offline functionality are particularly important in rural and remote areas, ensuring continuous data flow even when internet connectivity is unreliable. Examples such as Nigeria's use of ODK and Form Hub technology during the Ebola outbreak illustrate the transformative potential of such tools in resource-limited settings [7].

Additionally, demonstrating the impact of ICT applications on disease mitigation and policy improvements will encourage further investment. To secure long-term political and financial support, stakeholders must demonstrate how digital solutions concretely improve disease monitoring and system response. Collecting and communicating evidence on the impact of ICT, such as improvement of national health outcomes [8], reductions in outbreak detection time, improved reporting compliance, or better coordination during disease outbreaks [[9], [10], [11]], is essential for advocacy and scaling. An illustrative example of ICT use in outbreak reporting is the Program for Monitoring Emerging Diseases (ProMED) [12], a global digital early warning system for emerging infectious diseases that is widely use in global scale (https://www.promedmail.org/). ProMED operates across 24 countries, providing real-time information on health events and outbreaks to researchers, policymakers, and the public worldwide. Another notable example discussed during our session was Thailand's PODD surveillance system which exemplifies effective advocacy and successful national scale-up of community-based digital surveillance. In recognition of its innovative approach, this system was awarded the grand prize at the Trinity Challenge 2021, receiving a £1.3 million grant. This funding has enabled the development of One-Health Toolkits (OHTK) to facilitate the system's implementation in other countries.

One of the recommendations was to make the digital tools to be used in many countries, it would be good to collaborate with United Nation (UN) agencies or international organizations that offer the use of these tools to member countries. In this way, many countries have access to the tools and possibly to get supports to apply the tools. Additionally, governments, international organizations, and private-sector partners must invest in scalable and long-term ICT infrastructure. Governments must commit national resources while engaging international development partners and private-sector innovators in co-financing arrangements. Also, the donor-based projects should be incorporated or linked to existing government initiatives.

Take-home messages

Our special session exemplified the ICT applications across Africa and Asia and highlighted the importance of embracing the ICTs in dealing with One Health issues such as zoonoses, food safety and AMR.
