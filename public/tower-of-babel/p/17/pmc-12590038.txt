Safety and functional evaluation of a synthetic lamina cover that replaces lost autologous spinal bone after laminectomy, in a sheep and human study
Authors: Cheng-Kuang Chen, Ming-Fu Chiang, Yu-Cheng Yao, Ming-Chau Chang, Axel H Schönthal, Thomas C Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaminectomies involve removal of the lamina, usually to relieve pressure on the spinal cord. We are developing a synthetic lamina cover (SLC) that can replace the resected bone, functioning as an artificial shield, with the objective to minimize common postsurgical risks associated with laminectomies. The SLC is designed to be affixed to a common pedicle screw fixation system.MethodsLumbar laminectomies with posterior fusions were performed in 6 sheep and 30 human patients. Both cohorts were separated into 2 groups, where 1 group received the SLC to replace the lamina, whereas the other group did not. Otherwise, postsurgical care and medical attention were the same in both groups. After 26 weeks, the sheep were euthanized and histopathological and histomorphometrical evaluation of spine sections was performed. Human patients were followed for up to 6 months, and functional recovery was evaluated using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) questionnaire.ResultsIn sheep, the overall host tissue response was minimal, without any signs of irritation, inflammation, or aberrant changes potentially caused by the SLC. Human patients that had received the SLC reported better and faster functional recovery (p<.05) than the group without the SLC. Neither patient group experienced complications from surgery.ConclusionsApplication of the SLC was safe and showed benefit as a shield to replace the autologous spinal lamina postlaminectomy. In its presence, functional recovery not only was faster, but also overall more pronounced.Keywords: Chronic back pain, Dural adhesion, Laminectomy, Spine protection device, Spine surgery, Lamina cover

Introduction

A laminectomy is a surgical procedure that removes a portion of the vertebral bone, ie, the lamina, which is part of the vertebra that covers the spinal canal. Also known as decompression surgery, laminectomy enlarges the spinal canal to relieve pressure on the spinal cord or nerves, which most commonly is caused by bony over-growths within the spinal canal that can occur in people who have arthritis in their spines. Less frequent causes of compression may include trauma, spine tumor growth, or scoliosis. The increased pressure created by spinal stenosis may result in the gradual onset of pain, numbness, weakness in the arms or legs, and in more severe cases loss of bladder or bowel control, and sexual dysfunction. Laminectomies have the potential to greatly reduce or eliminate these symptoms.[1], [2], [3], [4]

Although laminectomies have been performed by surgeons for well over a hundred years, this invasive procedure still may result in detrimental consequences.[5], [6], [7], [8], [9] First, as the laminectomy removes the protective posterior bony element of the spine, the patient is left with only closed paraspinal muscle, fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin closure. Therefore, loss of the lamina results in the possibility of harm to the underlying nervous tissue if there is direct injury to the laminectomized area. In the lumbar spine, there is often enough protection from the muscle and fascia, but in the cervical spine this paraspinal musculature is limited. Second, scar tissue sets in after a laminectomy, as the paraspinal muscles that have closed over the dura form a fibrotic layer over it. Scar tissue surrounding the dura and nerve roots can compress the nerve roots and cauda equina, producing neural complications, such as persistent axial pain, radiculopathy, and neuropathy. Third, revision surgery may prove necessary due to recurrent disk herniation, postoperative spinal stenosis, or because of exuberant epidural fibrosis. As a result, repeat exposure requires going through the previous operative site without the help of normal landmarks and without any protection by a lamina. In such cases, there is a greater chance of the surgeon injuring the dura, resulting in a cerebrospinal (CSF) leak. Fourth, as the wound contracts, the patient may be left with a distinct contracture cavity that often forms over the surgical site. This concavity is cosmetically undesirable, particularly in thin patients and especially over the cervical or thoracic spine.[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]

Considering the above, there is a medical need for a spine protection device that could be applied postsurgery to replace the lost bone and cover the surgery site. Ideally, such a lamina cover would have the following desirable properties. It would: (1) provide protection of the spinal cord and associated nerve roots after laminectomy; (2) prevent postoperative scarring from wound-healing fibroblasts on top of the dura; (3) provide an easy landmark so that the surgeon does not get into the dura, possibly causing a CSF leak during exposure for redo surgery; (4) prevent cosmetic defects after laminectomy; (5) be easily applied after a laminectomy or posterior spinal fusion; and (6) provide long-term durability and facilitate repeated surgeries of the spine, when needed.

In view of this medical need, we pursued the development of a synthetic lamina cover (SLC) that is placed in the setting of posterior fixation. This SLC is designed to be added onto existing spinal fixation systems and is expected to prevent or greatly minimize the occurrence of potential detrimental sequelae after laminectomy. In the following, we introduce the SLC and present results from its assessment both in a large animal model and in human patients after posterior spinal decompression and fixation.

Methods

Component design

The SLC has the principal purpose of replacing the surgically removed endogenous lamina bone after laminectomy. While this present study investigated 1 specific example of an SLC (shown in Fig. 1), its design is flexible with regard to its dimensions, configuration, and elemental composition. This top cover (the synthetic lamina, or shield) is affixed to the 2 rods present in all pedicle screw systems. The specific SLC investigated in this study was made from polyether ether ketone (PEEK), an organic thermoplastic polymer. Its current configuration is designed to be affixed to the pedicle screw fixation system manufactured by Baui Biotech (Taipei, Taiwan).10

Animal model

A sheep (Ovis aries) model was used for this study because of comparable anatomy and physiology of the ovine lumbar spine to that of human, especially in the lumbar region.11 It represents an accepted translational model of a 2-level laminectomy with a posterior lumbar fusion.12 All in vivo experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Colorado State University (CSU; Fort Collins, Colorado), and animal care complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.13 Surgical procedures were performed at the Preclinical Surgical Research Laboratory of the Department of Clinical Sciences at CSU. Six sheep were used for this study.

Ex vivo experimental procedures

Predissection digital images and radiographs of the operated functional spinal units of sheep were taken. The spines were cleaned of extraneous soft tissues, with care to preserve the bony and soft tissue architecture around the fusion mass. Spinal segments were then further dissected to isolate the operated levels, creating 2 functional spinal units from each animal. This was followed by taking postdissection digital images and radiographs of the operated functional spinal units in the anterior/posterior and lateral planes.

Histology of sheep spine sections

Histology sections were prepared from the surgical sites of each sheep. Sections were cut at approximately 5 microns with a minimum of 5 slides per region of interest. Staining was with Hematoxylin & Eosin. Slides were evaluated by the study pathologist for parameters evaluating safety and efficacy of the test materials. The pathologist was initially blinded to the treatment parameters. The bones were analyzed and graded according to cell type and responses following the grading scheme and reference shown in Supplementary Table 1. Synovium and soft tissue organ slides were scored as normal or abnormal, and if abnormal, a semi-quantitative score of inflammation and accompanying qualitative histological description was provided. The histology scoring data included semi-quantitative scoring of each slide. Following completion of the initial grading of all slides, the pathologist sent all slide scores to the ex vivo principal investigator to be locked. The pathologist was then unblinded for data postprocessing allowing comparison between timepoints and to control samples for generation of Figs. and concluding statements. Images were taken using an Olympus BX43 microscope connected to an Olympus DP73 camera and run by Olympus CellSens software (Evident/Olympus Scientific Solutions, Waltham, MA).

Clinical testing in human patients

All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards and principles of the Declaration of Helsinki,14 and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The clinical trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (Taipei, Taiwan). It enrolled 30 patients, all of whom underwent surgical laminectomy and posterior spinal fusion. Half of the patients received the SLC at the conclusion of the surgery, whereas the other half did not. It was a single-blind trial for both groups in a 1-by-1 randomized order, where the operator drew lots before surgery and randomly assigned the subjects to the control group or the SLC treatment group. Patients did not know in advance which group they were assigned to, but were made aware afterwards.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Both study groups were under the same inclusion/exclusion criteria. Eligible patients were females and males at least 20 years old; with degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis in 1 or 2 consecutive segments from lumbar vertebra L1 to sacral vertebra S1; have objective evidence of back pain and numbness radiating the lower limbs; have spinal stenosis, neurogenic claudication or bilateral radiating pain; have received at least 3 months of nonsurgical treatment without effectiveness; received radiographic examination consistent with clinical symptoms of degenerative spondylolisthesis or spinal stenosis; after diagnosis were in need of laminectomy with internal fixation; and were able and willing to sign the consent form.

Patients were excluded if they already had lumbar surgery at the same lumbar vertebra; suffered from malignant tumors or infections; were unable to undergo MRI examination for any reason; or were unwilling to answer the questionnaire.

Analysis of postsurgical patient recovery

The postsurgical care was the same for all patients. The recovery of patients was monitored over several months. The quality of recovery was assessed with a commonly used survey, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). The ODI is a questionnaire that assesses symptoms and severity of low back pain in terms of disablement and how it impacts daily functional activities on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates no disability and 100 indicates bed-bound disability.

Statistical analysis

We used chi-square test to calculate the significance values. A probability value (p) <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The specific SLC design used in this study and its exemplary application to a sheep spine is shown in Fig. 1. The studies in sheep were complemented by a clinical trial in 30 laminectomy patients.

Studies in sheep

Six animals underwent laminectomies at L2-3 and L4-5, followed by pedicle screw fixation (Baui Spine; Taipei, Taiwan). All surgeries were performed by the same veterinarian surgeon. In each case, a standard midline incision and subperiosteal dissection was performed to expose the L2-3 and L4-5 lamina. Pedicle screws were placed into L2, L3, L4, and L5. A laminectomy was performed at both levels. 5.5mm rods were used for fixation. In 3 sheep, the SLC was snapped onto the rods bilaterally and tightened into position. Each sheep was randomized to have the SLC placed into the laminectomy sites either at L2-3 or L4-5, but not at both sites. The surgical wound was closed, and all sheep received appropriate postsurgical care. After reconvalescence, the sheep were allowed to move freely in their pen. Prior to euthanasia at 26 weeks after surgery, all animals were examined. The spine region where the SLC was placed did not show a concavity. Dissection of the dura was very easy with the SLC; the SLC was simply identified, removed, and the dura was directly underneath it. On the other hand, the section of the spine without the SLC had to be carefully dissected to prevent injury to the underlying dura. Histopathological and histomorphometrical evaluation of spine sections was performed.

Gross pathology and ex vivo postdissection results

Pre- and postdissection digital images and radiographs of the operated functional spinal units were prepared from each sheep. Representative examples are presented in Fig. 2. As shown, careful analysis did not reveal any aberrant findings or any noticeable differences between sections with the SLC or those without it, indicating that the SLC did not cause any detectable gross problems.

Histopathological analysis

Tissue sections were prepared from L2-3 and L4-5 from all animals. The overall host tissue response observed within the laminectomy window and underlying spinal canal was minimal in both control and SLC treatment group animals (Supplementary Table 1). The test article reactivity score for SLC-treated animals was categorized as “minimal to no reaction” (Supplementary Table 2), with the average host reactivity score per SLC-treated site being less than that for control animals, consistent with a nonirritant. This result demonstrated an absence of a major rejection response against the implanted foreign object.

Highly representative photomicrographs derived from histopathological analysis are presented in Fig. 3 (A,C,E: control group; B,D,F: SLC treatment group) and show the following: In all animals, the host tissue response was characterized by bilateral production of new bone, which extended from the pedicles and partially filled the laminectomy window. The central portion of the laminectomy window was filled with variably dense fibrovascular tissue, occasionally which contained multifocal rare infiltrates of lymphocytes and macrophages. This fibrous tissue often minimally extended into the underlying spinal canal and was continuous with the dura mater, resulting in minimal thickening of the dorsal portion of the dura. Robust fibrosis or scar tissue was not typically observed in the spinal canal in any treatment group, and this dorsal fibrous thickening of the dura was minimal and not appreciably different between treatment groups. This was reflected in the semi-quantitative scores for neovascular and fibrosis, which were not different between treatment groups. The only minimal histological difference observed between control and SLC-treated animals was a very slight reduction in inflammation observed in SLC-treated animals. This reduction in inflammation was negligible given the overall inflammatory response within these animals; it was very limited and no greater than that expected for this surgical procedure.

Studies in human patients

A total of 30 patients with similar characteristics were scheduled to receive laminectomy. Before surgery, they were randomly assigned to a control group (n=15) or a group receiving the SLC (n=15). The control group consisted of 7 males and 8 females with a mean age of 71.20 years; the SLC group consisted of 8 males and 7 females with a mean age of 69.47 years. The age difference between the 2 groups (1.73±3.54 [SEM] years) was not statistically significant (p=.63) (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 26.74 for the control group and 28.29 for the SLC group, and this difference was not statistically significant (p=.41) (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Both groups presented with similar pathologies (degenerative spondylolisthesis, spinal stenosis) and comorbidities (mild to moderate diabetes and high blood pressure), and each patient was randomly assigned to one of the 2 groups. All 30 patient surgeries were performed by the same 2 senior orthopedic surgeons, and both surgeons operated on both groups of patients. The location of the transpendicular screws and the SLC, respectively, for each patient is shown in Table 1. Installment of the SLC added an average 10 minutes to the overall OR time.

The postsurgical care was the same for all patients, and the recovery of patients was monitored over several months. There were no postsurgical complications in any of the patients, and no patient required revision surgery during the 6-month follow-up period. To determine whether the application of the SLC would make a difference for the recovery process, patients completed the Oswestry Low Back Pain Disability Questionnaire to establish their Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). This test was given before surgery and at 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery.

The results from the ODI are shown in Fig. 4. Before laminectomy, all patients presented with an elevated disability index averaging 52-55. Postsurgery at 1 month, this index did not significantly change, which was not unexpected in view of the short recovery time since surgery. At 3 months postsurgery, however, there was a pronounced functional improvement in the group having received the SLC, as indicated by a 26-point drop in their ODI (from 52 presurgery to 26 postsurgery; ie, 50% lower; p=.0001). In comparison, the control group without the SLC presented with only an 8-point drop (from 55 to 47; ie, 15% lower; p=.109). Similarly, at 6 months the SLC group continued to show overall substantially greater recovery (23 points), whereas the ODI of the control group without the SLC (39 points) was still significantly higher (p=.024). Strikingly, the control group at 6 months (39 points) had still not recovered to the extent that was seen in the SLC group 3 months earlier (26 points at 3 months; p=.045). Thus, these ODI data show a functional recovery that overall was more alleviating and faster in patients with the SLC.

Discussion

The concept of a rigid lamina cover has been proposed before.4,15,16 However, it was not successful because it was used in the setting of the nonfixated spine. In those cases, the lamina cover was fixated onto the facet joint with a screw. Over time, however, it would become loose with continued spinal rotation and movement, creating a number of problems for the patients and requiring revision surgery. To avoid such potential complications, we pursued a different approach and developed a synthetic lamina cover that is placed in the setting of posterior fixation, allowing it to be placed without worry that it may become loose over time. It represents a novel type of accessory that can be used to replace the resected autologous lamina and spinous process. This SLC was not designed to be an enlarged crosslink; its goal is not to provide stability to a construct. Instead, it is designed to provide functional improvement to the laminectomized region from the standpoint of protecting the underlying spinal cord or thecal sac, improving the ease of performing revision surgery when performing the exposure, potentially decreasing the formation of scar tissue, and improving the cosmetic outcome. While the SLC we used in this study was made from PEEK, additional compositions are possible. For example, it may be constructed of biocompatible polymers, metals, or metal alloys, and it can be impregnated or coated with pharmaceutical and therapeutic agents.

We studied the SLC in a comparative study performed in sheep and human patients. Sheep spines are fairly similar in size and structure to human spines, making them a suitable model for spinal surgeries.11,17 As was important for our study, an animal model enabled the histological ex vivo analysis of spine sections. In comparison, the use of human subjects provides real-world variability, direct relevance, and the opportunity to use questionnaires as part of the intervention evaluation process. We took advantage of these complementary aspects to evaluate the application of the SLC after laminectomy in both models. Combined, the results presented in our study show that the SLC is safe and significantly speeds up the functional recovery process after laminectomy. It therefore has the potential to become a widely used accessory, harboring the benefit to accelerate healing, minimize disability, and improve quality of life for the patient.

The investigative sheep model revealed that the SLC was well tolerated by these animals. The tissue surrounding the SLC did not reveal any signs of irritation or rejection responses, and there was no interference with the healing process. In essence, postlaminectomized sheep receiving the SLC showed no discernible difference to their counterparts that did not receive the SLC. In both cases, recovery proceeded as expected at all levels and no detrimental issues could be detected and attributed to the presence of the SLC.

Based on the positive outcome of the sheep study, the investigation was extended to human patients, who were expected to also provide sensory, emotional, and subjective feedback for a further comprehensive evaluation of SLC functioning. In particular, quality of life issues related to typical body movements of daily living and potential disability, were accessible in order to assess the hypothesized benefit of the SLC during postlaminectomy recovery. The results obtained from the comparative patient cohorts with or without SLC application showed that the implantation of the lamina shield produced significant benefits for the patients’ recovery process. In the presence of the SLC, the functional recovery occurred not only faster, but also was overall more pronounced. Particularly striking was our finding that the patients’ average ODI score without the SLC was still higher (39 points) at 6 months than the average score of SLC patients (26 points) at 3 months, although the etiology of this improvement in ODI is not clear. The SLC has no structural benefit. We surmise that the benefit may be secondary to the fact that the fibrosis and scar tissue formed on top of the dura, with potential “tugging” of the dura, which may hinder the recovery process. At this time, however, support for this view has not yet been established.

None of the human or sheep subjects experienced postsurgical complications or had to go back to surgery. While the sheep study demonstrated the absence of negative side effects associated with the SLC at the histological and anatomical level, the human study was able to identify tangible quality of life benefits when this spine-protective device was applied. The combination of these complementary preclinical and clinical studies gratifyingly demonstrated that the device was safe and exerted measurable benefit for the patients.

Our results compare favorably to other studies investigating the implantation of rigid types of artificial laminae with 3-dimensional shapes. For example, a report by Zhao et al.16 presented results from a clinical study that used a lamina cover made of a nano-hydroxyapatite/polyamide66 composite and molded into the hump shape of a lamina. A total of 17 patients received the implant, and they were followed for 4–7 years. It was concluded that this artificial lamina was a reasonable choice for prevention of epidural scar formation after laminectomy, although it wasn’t possible to compare the outcome to other materials and cases, because a control group was lacking in this trial. Studies in laminectomy animal models have presented promising results as well. For instance, Lv et al.18 investigated the implantation of a biodegradable multiamino acid copolymer/nano-hydroxyapatite composite lamina into 12 laminectomized goats and compared the outcomes to a control group of 9 animals. The authors concluded that this artificial lamina could prevent epidural adhesions surrounding the defect. Another intriguing approach was taken by Li et al.,19 who generated a 3D-printed bionic titanium alloy lamina for examination in a pig model. They showed that this artificial lamina could prevent epidural adhesion after laminectomy. Taken together, there is increasing evidence that the implantation of laminar prostheses can provide clinical benefit after laminectomy; our comparative study in sheep and human patients provides further support for this notion and specifically introduces our SLC as a relevant medical device for this purpose.

Limitations

The sample size for the human study at n=15 per group was relatively small. This was further impeded because ODI questionnaires were not obtained from 2 patients in the SLC group and 2 patients in the control group at the post-3 months and post-6 months time points. Nonetheless, the trendlines indicate a potential benefit of the SLC, and statistical significance was reached at these 2 later time points.

Among the postulated benefits of the SLC is its mechanical protection of the dura, which after laminectomy remains relatively exposed and vulnerable without an added shield.4 In our studies, we did not investigate this aspect. Although such a study could be envisioned in a sheep model, where 1 could apply external forces and cause trauma, it cannot be done in humans, due to obvious ethical concerns. Nonetheless, once application of the SLC becomes widespread clinical practice, it would be possible to retrospectively review cases of spinal cord injury or complications after laminectomy with and without inclusion of the SLC, in order to shed light on the aspect of mechanical protection by this new device.

Although it is only suitable for patients that have posterior rods, posterior fixation with pedicle screws do constitute the majority of spinal fusions (up to 70% in lumbar fusions). The laminar cover will be the equivalent of the burr hole cover used to cover the skull defect caused by perforators used in craniotomies. It will protect the laminectomized spine — also in response to patients who frequently inquire as to what will protect their spinal cord after the bone is removed. Moreover, the severe "dimpling" post laminectomy in the cervical and thoracic spine will be alleviated. Although most patients will not undergo repeat surgery, for the patients that do require it, it will enable a safer and more expeditious repeat exposure.

The issue of the postulated decrease in scar tissue was not answered by this study. Although the senior author (TCC) was present during the post exposure to note the difference grossly in the appearance of the sheep dura with and without laminar cover, the histopathologic analysis after 3 months did not support the visual appearance. The possibility of scar tissue-induced spinal cord tethering, leading to pain and neurological symptoms, should not be dismissed. Further studies will determine if this could be a possibility.

The current configuration of the SLC has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) for commercialization, and an application to the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Southern California is ongoing to provide for postregistration studies to be performed. However, at this time approval has only been granted for the lumbar spine in connection with the pedicle screw system from Baui Biotech (Taipei, Taiwan).10 Postregistration studies will need to be performed for other standard commercial instrumentation companies’ 5.5mm rods (eg, Medtronic, Depuy, Globus). The cost of the SLC will be structured similarly to the crosslink, which was widely used previously. The authors envision that a price of $750 per laminar cover will be proposed. The additional time of surgery needed to apply the SLC should be less than 10 minutes, and this added step should not increase the risk of infection during surgery.

Conclusions

Results from a preclinical sheep model, combined with a small-scale clinical trial in human patients, are affirmatively complementary and demonstrate the safety of the synthetic lamina cover, a novel medical device. Follow-up questionnaires in patients indicate that the SLC significantly speeds up the functional recovery process after laminectomy, thereby improving the patients’ quality of life.
