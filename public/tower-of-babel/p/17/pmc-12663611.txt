The Despair of Identity Diffusion: Associations With Psychache and Hopelessness and the Indirect Effect of Childhood Emotional Abuse
Authors: Angela Russolillo, Connor Hawkins, David Kealy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIdentity diffusion, involving a lack of cohesive personal identity, is a vulnerability factor in affective states—psychache and hopelessness—that often precede suicidality. Traumatic experiences, such as childhood emotional abuse, may limit the capacity to form a stable and coherent sense of self, thereby indirectly heightening the vulnerability to psychache and hopelessness through diffuse identity. The present study examined the relationship between identity diffusion and psychache and hopelessness, along with the indirect effect of perceived childhood emotional abuse. The sample (n = 297) comprised UK‐based help‐seeking adults recruited online. Eligible participants completed measures of identity diffusion, unbearable psychache, hopelessness and childhood emotional abuse at baseline, with psychache and hopelessness reassessed two months later. Regression analyses revealed that identity diffusion was significantly associated with both psychache and hopelessness over time, even after controlling for baseline levels. Mediation analysis further indicated that identity diffusion had a significant mediating effect on the relationship between childhood emotional abuse and later experiences of psychache and hopelessness. These findings underscore the importance of clinically targeting identity diffusion to help reduce presuicidal affective states, particularly among individuals who experience childhood emotional maltreatment.

Introduction

States of mind such as unbearable mental pain have been considered potential precursors to suicide. An individual's ability to tolerate and navigate intense feelings related to suicidality, such as mental pain and despair, often hinges on the capacity for emotional regulation and resiliency among other related psychological processes (Troister and Holden 2010). When individuals are overwhelmed by mental pain and believe there is no relief from their suffering, suicidal desire increases (Klonsky and May 2015; Klonsky et al. 2021). These states of intense emotional despair can feel unbearable (Li et al. 2017; Tossani 2013), leading many individuals to seek escape and relief through suicide (Klonsky et al. 2016). It is thus important to understand individual differences and vulnerabilities that may increase susceptibility to unbearable emotional pain and hopelessness, since these may present further targets for clinical efforts aimed at fostering resilience and mitigating risk for suicidality.

Intolerable psychological pain, conceptualized by Shneidman (1993) as unbearable psychache, refers to the severe mental anguish that follows from core psychological needs being unmet (Pachkowski et al. 2021). Hopelessness, a key construct in suicidality, is characterized by a negative outlook on the future with low expectations of achievement or success (Serafini et al. 2020; Hirsch et al. 2012) and a belief that the future holds little or no possibility for positive change (Marchetti et al. 2023; Kwong 2024). Both intense emotional and psychological pain (Shneidman 1993) and hopelessness have been identified as pivotal in contributing to suicidal ideation and in combination can motivate suicide attempts (Klonsky et al. 2016; Troister and Holden 2010). Importantly, although mental pain and hopelessness are conceptually related and may collectively contribute to suicide risk, they are also distinct constructs. Research suggests that they capture different aspects of suicide vulnerability, with mental pain adding unique insights related to emptiness and loss of meaning in life, which are core to the suicidal experience and distinct from hopelessness (Orbach et al. 2003a; Orbach et al. 2003b). Similarly, the Three‐Step Theory (3ST; Klonsky and May 2015) of suicide implicates pain and hopelessness as key components to the desire for suicide, with absence of connectedness and capacity for suicide as additional factors in the pathway from ideation to suicidal action. According to the 3ST, the combination of psychache and hopelessness—a painful sense of despair about the future—invites contemplation of suicide as a potential option for escape from what the individual experiences as unbearable and unmodifiable (Klonsky and May 2015). In this context, mental pain functions as a precursor to suicidality, and hopelessness serves as a moderating variable that facilitates the progression from ideation to attempt (Klonsky and May 2015). Moreover, meta‐analytic reviews of published studies indicate that severe mental pain and hopelessness are clearly linked with elevated suicidal ideation and behaviour (Ribeiro et al. 2018; Verrocchio et al. 2016). Such research suggests that psychache is implicated in suicidality independently from the severity of depression (Verrocchio et al. 2016), and that hopelessness is an antecedent of suicide death (Ribeiro et al. 2018). Beyond the risk of suicide, hopelessness and psychache represent highly distressing mental states in their own right, potentially exerting a negative impact on an individual's overall well‐being and social functioning. Indeed, apart from being a precipitant to suicide attempts, suicidal ideation—along with associated and contributory affective states—is a prevalent mental health problem that remains inadequately understood and deserves further investigation and clinical attention (Jobes et al. 2024).

Vulnerability to psychache and hopelessness may be influenced by difficulties in one's personal identity or self‐concept—the sense of who one is as comprised of relatively stable values, beliefs and roles (Oyserman et al. 2012). Because the capacity for emotional regulation and resilience may emerge from a relatively robust self‐concept, identity diffusion may play a crucial role in the susceptibility to hopelessness and psychache. Defined by a ‘marked and persistent instability in an individual's sense of self’ (American Psychiatric Association 2022), identity diffusion is associated with feelings of insignificance, disconnection and mistrust (Akhtar 1984; Boyer 2022; Kernberg 1985). Individuals with identity diffusion tend to have difficulty defining themselves, often feeling a sense of emptiness, confusion or brokenness when thinking about the self, and experiencing marked shifts in attitudes and behaviours both temporally and contextually (Kernberg 1985; Kernberg 2016). Diffuse identity exists on a continuum and is recognized as a central element in personality pathology, such as borderline personality disorder (Jørgensen and Bøye 2022; Rivnyák et al. 2021). Furthermore, identity disturbance is one of the core features of personality disorder according to Criterion A in the DSM‐5 alternative model of personality disorders (Vizgaitis and Lenzenweger 2022), with identity integration recognized as increasingly important in treatment planning for clinicians working with patients experiencing personality pathology (Caligor 2018). Although identity diffusion is distinct from psychache, the emptiness and confusion regarding one's sense of self may contribute to an individual's vulnerability to intense psychological anguish (Shneidman 1993). Moreover, an unstable or fragmented sense of self might impair one's ability to sustain meaningful relationships, deal with challenges and setbacks and manage emotional distress, thereby intensifying experiences of psychache (Kroger et al. 2010; Ragelienė 2016). Identity diffusion may also interfere with the development of a cohesive life narrative, undermining one's ability to maintain a hopeful outlook for the future (Luyckx et al. 2010; Waters and Fivush 2015). Thus, not only does identity diffusion impede psychological resilience and resourcefulness, but it may also diminish future‐oriented optimism (Johnson et al. 2014; Tugade and Frederikson 2004). Individuals who struggle to maintain a consistent self‐concept across time and throughout different contexts—experiencing confusion and inauthenticity in their sense of self—may find it difficult to maintain a representation of the self as functioning well in the future. Although identity difficulties have been linked with depressive symptoms (Montesano et al. 2017; Potterton et al. 2022), reduced meaning in life (Kealy et al. 2022; Negru‐Subtirica et al. 2016) and suicidality (Sekowski et al. 2022; Sokol and Eisenheim 2016), less is known regarding the specific relations between identity diffusion and psychache and hopelessness. Understanding why some individuals develop a coherent identity, whereas others remain highly uncertain about their self‐concept, is critical for clinicians and requires examination of factors associated with diffuse identity development.

Given that personal identity develops within a social and caregiving context, childhood trauma is thought to negatively impact identity development (Berman and Montgomery 2014; Truskauskaite‐Kuneviciene et al. 2020). Identity formation is a complex yet critical process—involving exploration and commitment to values, beliefs and roles—that unfolds over time as part of normative adolescent development (Branje 2022; Erikson 1968). However, traumatic events that impede this process may prevent the progression towards a relatively continuous sense of self (Kira 2019). The process of establishing a coherent sense of identity may be facilitated by social support and parental nurturance (Hall and Brassard 2008; Kealy et al. 2022) and may be derailed by experiences of childhood trauma (Penner et al. 2019; Hatkevich et al. 2021). Traumatic experiences can often compromise the process of identity formation (Berman et al. 2020), limiting the capacity of resources one can call on to navigate challenges and leading to feelings of self‐doubt, blame and detachment (Browne and Winkelman 2007; Gvion and Fachler 2015). Childhood maltreatment, including persistent emotional abuse from parents (Penner et al. 2019), witnessing domestic abuse (Doroudchi et al. 2023) or experiencing sexual abuse (Hailes et al. 2019), significantly increases an individual's vulnerability to mental pain and despair. Among the different forms of maltreatment, childhood emotional abuse (CEA) is particularly harmful due to its invisible nature and significant impact on self‐perception and the formation of interpersonal relationships (Levy et al. 2015; Lorenzini and Fonagy 2013; Riggs 2010). In contrast to physical abuse, which involves visible external harms, CEA manifests in more silent or subtle ways, fostering negative internal dialogues that can become normalized within families (Bär et al. 2023; Emami et al. 2024; van Harmelen et al. 2010). This normalization can lead to patterns of self‐criticism, low self‐esteem and challenges forming stable trusting relationships (Cao et al. 2022; Jopling et al. 2020; McNeil and Rehman 2024; Pereira et al. 2021). Consequently, CEA has been associated with adverse psychological outcomes including increased rates of depression, anxiety and stress (Carr et al. 2013; Dye 2019). Furthermore, research links experiences of CEA to increased hopelessness and psychache (Hamilton et al. 2013; Holden et al. 2022)—highlighting its long‐term psychological implications. The effect of CEA in contributing to these negative affect states may be partly understood through its influence on identity development. Indeed, empirical research has shown that exposure to interpersonal trauma, such as CEA, is associated with a negative impact on personal identity (Kouvelis and Kangas 2021). CEA may reduce a climate of psychological and social safety within a family, making identity exploration—including imagined future possibilities—seems dangerous or forbidden (Penner et al. 2019; Berman 2016). Moreover, a child who experiences chronic emotional abuse may psychologically comply with the messages conveyed by abusive behaviour, thereby developing beliefs that one is bad or undeserving, or that ostensibly trustworthy others are hostile (Lawson and Quinn 2013; Riggs 2010). Such beliefs may guide the restriction of personal possibilities and commitments, reducing opportunities for personal fulfilment and potentially fuelling a sense of pessimism. Although studies indicate links between CEA and identity difficulties (Berman et al. 2020; Carlson et al. 1997), and between CEA and states of mental pain and hopelessness (Negele et al. 2015; Labonté and Kealy 2024; Wong et al. 2018), limited research has focused on relations with identity diffusion, nor on the pathway to these affect states through identity diffusion as a mediator.

By understanding the roles of identity diffusion and CEA in contributing to psychache and hopelessness, clinicians can be better prepared to identify and address these concerns in their work with patients who experience such despair. The main purpose of the present study was to investigate identity diffusion as a predictor of emotional despair reflected by unbearable psychache and hopelessness. A secondary objective was to examine the role of CEA as an indirect predictor of these despair‐related affective states through identity diffusion as a mediator. First, we hypothesized that identity diffusion would be associated with unbearable psychache and hopelessness. We expected identity diffusion would predict unbearable psychache and hopelessness (i.e., dependent variables) measured at a subsequent time point. Moreover, we investigated these predictive relations after controlling for each dependent variable at baseline, indicating variability in psychache and hopelessness over a 2‐month period. Finally, we hypothesized identity diffusion to function as a mediator in the relation of CEA and both psychache and hopelessness. In other words, we expected that the indirect effects of CEA on psychache and hopelessness would be accounted for by the mediating effect of identity diffusion.

Method

Participants and Procedures

Participants were UK‐based adults who had recently sought mental health care through the National Health Service (NHS). These participants were sourced online through the Prolific Academic (https://www.prolific.com/) recruitment platform, using a posting that called for participants for a study about emotions. All participants endorsed having previously sought NHS mental health care (within 12 months prior to registration). Prospective participants were then provided with information about the study and were invited to consent to participate by initiating questionnaire completion. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Given the nature of the study content, participants were provided with information about mental health resources should they experience distress during participation. Study questionnaires, facilitated through Qualtrics software, were completed online in two phases. All measures in the present study were completed at our first time point (Time 1), with unbearable psychache and hopelessness repeated two months later (Time 2). Participants received modest payments of £5.00 at Time 1 and £2.00 at Time 2. A total of 356 participants provided initial consent and commenced the survey. Following data screening to remove five incomplete and 19 inattentive responders (e.g., failure of at least two out of three attention check items), a final Time 1 sample of 332 was retained. The final analytic sample for the present study was N = 297 and included participants who also provided data for psychache and hopelessness at Time 2, in addition to Time 1 measures.

Participants' average age was 33.78 years (SD = 11.81; range = 18–74). The majority of participants (63.3%) identified as female, 32.3% as male and 4.3% as non‐binary gender identity. Regarding sexual orientation, 65% identified as heterosexual, with 21.2% identifying as bisexual, 4.7% as gay or lesbian and 9% as queer or other identities. The majority (84.2%) identified as Caucasian, and 3.4% identified as Asian, 3% as South Asian and the remainder as other or mixed ethnicities. More than half of the participants (60.1%) were employed; 15.8% were students. A majority (55.2%) were in committed relationships, with the remainder being single or dating casually.

Measures

Identity Diffusion

The Lack of Identity scale from the Self Concept and Identity Measure (SCIM; Kaufman et al. 2015) was used to assess the absence of self‐definition and profound sense of emptiness involved in identity diffusion. Validation studies among non‐clinical and clinical samples have shown good psychometric properties for the SCIM and confirmed the distinctiveness of the Lack of Identity scale (Bogaerts et al. 2018; Kaufman et al. 2015; Kaufman et al. 2019). This scale consists of six items scaled from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); sample items include ‘I feel lost when I think about who I am’ and ‘I feel empty inside, like a person without a soul.’ Higher scores reflect a more severe lack of identity, or identity diffusion. A Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.92 indicated excellent internal consistency of the SCIM Lack of Identity scale in the present sample.

Unbearable Psychache

The three‐item Unbearable Psychache Scale (UP3; Pachkowski et al. 2019) was used to assess intolerable psychological pain conceptualized by Schneidman (1993) as unbearable psychache. The UP3 uses three items from the Psychache Scale (measuring general psychache; Holden et al. 2001) that specifically capture the experience of psychological pain that has become intolerable: ‘I can't take my pain anymore’, ‘Because of my pain, my situation is impossible’ and ‘My pain is making me fall apart’. Items are scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) and summed, where a higher total score indicates greater severity of unbearable psychache. The UP3 demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the present sample, with an alpha coefficient of 0.95.

Hopelessness

Hopelessness was assessed using a two‐item measure, the Brief‐H‐Neg (Fraser et al. 2014). Brief‐H‐Neg items refer to negative expectations, ‘The future seems to me to be hopeless and I can't believe that things are changing for the better’ and ‘I feel that it is impossible to reach the goals I would like to strive for’, and are scored from 1 (absolutely disagree) to 5 (absolutely agree). Higher scores indicate a greater degree of hopelessness. The Brief‐H‐Neg has been found to perform well in terms of internal consistency and test–retest stability and to correlate with a well‐established measure of hopelessness in a large clinical sample (Fraser et al. 2014). In the present sample, the Brief‐H‐Neg showed good internal consistency, indicated by an alpha coefficient of 0.87.

CEA

The emotional abuse subscale of the Child Abuse and Trauma Scale (CATS‐EA; Kent and Waller 1998; Sanders and Becker‐Lausen 1995) was used to assess retrospective perceptions of parents' emotionally abusive behaviour during the respondent's childhood. The CATS‐EA comprises seven items, such as ‘Did your parents insult you or call you names?’ and ‘Did your parents ever lash out at you when you did not expect it?’ scored from 0 (never) to 4 (always). Higher scores indicate recollections of higher frequency. Previous research has shown the CATS‐EA to demonstrate high internal consistency and to correlate strongly with other measures of emotional abuse and psychological aggression (Baker and Festinger 2011). An alpha coefficient of 0.93 indicated excellent internal consistency of the CATS‐EA in the present sample.

Analytic Approach

Analyses were undertaken using SPSS 27 including the PROCESS macro 4.0 (Hayes 2022). Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and zero‐order correlations were computed to examine associations among study variables, including whether psychache and hopelessness were associated with potential covariates, age and gender (dummy coded where male = 1). Next, linear regression analyses were conducted using psychache and hopelessness at Time 2 as separate dependent variables, with their Time 1 equivalents entered as predictor variables, along with relevant covariates, followed by identity diffusion as a predictor variable. Finally, further regression analyses were used to examine our hypothesized mediation models, regarding the indirect effect of CEA. In these models, CEA served as the independent variable, psychache and hopelessness served as separate dependent variables, and identity diffusion was the mediator variable. The indirect effect of CEA through identity diffusion was evaluated conservatively using bootstrapped 99% confidence intervals (CIs), sampled 10,000 times, which are robust to violated assumptions of normality in the products of path coefficients. A significant indirect effect at the level of p < 0.01, indicating mediation, would be determined by the absence of zero within the lower and upper bounds of the 99% CI. Results of regression analyses are reported in standardized coefficients to facilitate interpretation.

Results

Potential covariates were checked using Pearson and point‐biserial correlations regarding associations with dependent variables at Time 2. Age was significantly negatively associated with unbearable psychache, r = −0.15, p = 0.01, but not with hopelessness, r = −0.06, p = 0.30, whereas male gender was associated with hopelessness, r = 0.12, p = 0.04, but not with psychache, r = −0.03, p = 0.66.

Table 1 displays means, standard deviations and zero‐order correlations. Significant associations were observed in expected directions among all study variables. At the level of zero‐order association, CEA accounted for 7% and 2% of the variance in unbearable psychache and hopelessness, respectively (Time 2), whereas identity diffusion accounted for 32% of the variance in each of these domains. Linear regression models, including relevant covariates, were significant regarding downstream unbearable psychache, F(3, 293) = 90.18, p < 0.001, and hopelessness, F(3, 293) = 240.62, p < 0.001. The results of these models are presented in Table 2. Age and gender, although significant at the level of zero‐order correlations, were non‐significant and hence were omitted from further analyses. Consistent with hypotheses, identity diffusion was significantly associated with unbearable psychache after controlling for age and baseline psychache and was significantly associated with hopelessness after controlling for gender and baseline hopelessness. Thus, accounting for 3% of the variance in each model—with the majority of variance accounted for by baseline variables—identity diffusion was a significant factor in the persistence and variability of mental pain and despair over time.

Discussion

Findings from this study indicate that identity diffusion—a relative absence of self‐defining personal continuity—is a vulnerability factor for the kind of emotional despair that is often antecedent to suicidality. With baseline measurement of unbearable psychache and hopelessness controlled for, identity diffusion was significantly linked with the maintenance of and variation in these affective states over a two‐month period. Identity diffusion was also a significant mediator of a more distal vulnerability factor, CEA, in relation to psychache and hopelessness. Thus, individuals who recalled more experiences of CEA tended to have a greater degree of identity diffusion, which accounted for the relationship between such maltreatment and the later experience of intolerable mental pain and despair.

Identity Development and Mental Pain

Our results are consistent with theories of normative identity formation, emphasizing the importance of a stable self‐concept for psychological resilience (Erikson 1968; Feenstra et al. 2014; Kernberg 2006). As conceptualized, a consolidated, stable and flexible identity ‘constitutes an inner resource that is important for self‐regulation and the ability to navigate in a complex social world’ (Jørgensen 2018, 107). Therefore, when individuals lack a stable identity, they are more likely to experience increased psychiatric symptoms and interpersonal problems (Lowyck et al. 2013). This fragmented self‐concept can give rise to painful feelings of worthlessness and despair (Jørgensen and Bøye 2022), potentially manifesting as psychache and hopelessness. Established theories of suicide, particularly those developed by Shneidman (1985, 1993) and Klonsky (Klonsky and May 2015), identify psychological pain (psychache) and hopelessness as primary risk factors for suicidality. Klonsky's 3ST model underscores the central role of unbearable mental pain and a perceived lack of alternatives in the emergence of suicidal ideation and behaviour. Our findings suggest that identity diffusion may be a significant factor in the vulnerability to these affective states. Specifically, the instability and fragmentation of self‐concept involved in identity diffusion could intensify psychological pain and despair, thereby rendering the individual susceptible to the initial components of the progression towards suicidality described in Klonsky's 3ST model. Thus, identity diffusion may represent both a consequence of early trauma and a mechanism through which such experiences continue to influence psychiatric outcomes, positioning it as a critical target of psychological intervention both within and beyond suicidality.

CEA and Identity Formation

Research consistently demonstrates that traumatic experiences in childhood, particularly in the context of caregiving relationships, are associated with identity diffusion (Berman and Montgomery 2014; Waterman 2020; Berman et al. 2020). Chronic exposure to emotional maltreatment during formative years erodes self‐esteem and fosters a pervasive sense of unworthiness and confusion about one's self‐concept (Berman et al. 2020). Such disruption can contribute to identity diffusion, which may carry forward the deleterious effects of maltreatment towards pain and despair. Consistent with our hypothesis, we observed a significant indirect effect of CEA on psychache and hopelessness through identity diffusion. Thus, the finding of a mediating effect of identity diffusion extends previous research establishing the association between childhood maltreatment and adult psychological distress, highlighting the intolerable mental pain and despair that can eventuate from maltreatment and consequent identity difficulties. Although childhood emotional maltreatment has been identified as a distal predictor of depression in adults (Nelson et al. 2017), existing research has focused largely on hopelessness and emotional dysregulation as mediators, without fully considering the foundational disruption in identity formation associated with CEA (Li et al. 2020). One of the implications of identity diffusion in the context of early maltreatment is that it may render one less capable of navigating the social environment, which in turn may give rise to further confusion, interpersonal difficulties and loneliness. Such challenges may contribute to the emergence of psychache and hopelessness. However, the temporal gap between an initial trauma experience and the manifestation of these affective states—combined with the observation that not all individuals exposed to CEA develop these symptoms—indicates that further research is required to clarify these pathways. Nevertheless, our findings point to the importance of addressing identity diffusion in clinical settings when treating individuals with a history of CEA. Integrating therapeutic approaches focused on identity integration may serve an important role in mitigating the escalation of intolerable mental pain in this psychologically vulnerable population.

Clinical Implications and Treatment Planning

Identity impairment is a central component in the conceptualization of personality disorders and our findings highlight the necessity for psychiatric assessment and treatment approaches to target this aspect of personality—not limited to a particular type of personality disorder—as a vulnerability to presuicidal affective states. Given the various clinical expressions of identity diffusion, transdiagnostic and principle‐based approaches that recognize shared psychological mechanisms offer a flexible and comprehensive treatment option for a wide range of patients with complex clinical presentations (Dalgleish et al. 2020; Gazzillo et al. 2021). Alternatively, existing evidence supports the use of specific psychodynamic approaches to addressing identity diffusion. One prominent example is transference‐focused psychotherapy (TFP), an evidence‐based psychotherapy centrally focused on resolving identity diffusion through the integration of polarized representations of self and other (Caligor et al. 2018). Notably, TFP has demonstrated efficacy across clinical and randomized trials showing significant reductions in both suicidality and identity diffusion (Clarkin et al. 2007; Doering et al. 2010; Cain et al. 2025). From a therapeutic process perspective, TFP may be particularly valuable for individuals with CEA histories, as it directly targets the identity fragmentation that our findings suggest mediates the pathway to psychache and hopelessness. The application of TFP to populations beyond those with personality disorders, along with the integration and modification of TFP strategies for different clinical contexts, is an ongoing area of investigation (Hersh and De Panfilis 2024; Hersh et al. 2016). Because of an explicit focus on identity, principles and strategies derived from TFP may therefore be useful in addressing identity diffusion (Clarkin et al. 2024). However, pending further research on identity diffusion as a clinical outcome, other psychotherapy approaches may also be viable options, particularly if aimed at facilitating insight and corrective experience in an individualized manner for the patient (Gazzillo et al. 2021). Perhaps most importantly, our findings indicate that clinicians might develop enhanced understanding—and clinical conceptualization—of their patients' severe states of mental pain and hopelessness by exploring background experiences of CEA and potentially consequent identity problems.

Limitations

The present study has several strengths including the use of a large clinically exposed sample, with dependent variables measured at two timepoints. Indeed, a key strength of our study is in controlling for baseline measurements of psychache and hopelessness, indicating temporal variability across a two‐month period. Moreover, the use of two time points may reduce concerns about potential shared method variance. Despite these strengths, there are limitations to note and opportunities for future research. The study's reliance on self‐report data and an online recruitment platform may introduce bias. As well, the assessment of CEA was retrospective and ultimately based on individuals' perceptions rather than an objective assessment of maltreatment. Future work should replicate findings using a more representative sample of individuals seeking mental health care through different pathways, in order to increase the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the inclusion of other forms of childhood maltreatment (e.g., physical or sexual abuse), and an expansion of temporal sequencing (e.g., more time points), would strengthen future research. Future work could also examine identity diffusion in relation to actual suicidal behaviour, particularly regarding intervention strategies to mitigate risk among individuals who suffer from identity diffusion.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings provide evidence that identity diffusion is not only associated with unbearable psychache and hopelessness but may also contribute to the persistence and variability of these affective states across time. We also demonstrate a significant relationship between CEA and psychache and hopelessness through identity diffusion as a mediator. These findings provide insight into the relationships between psychosocial risk factors (e.g., emotional abuse) and underlying mechanisms, such as lack of identity, in contributing to the affective states that precipitate suicidality. Future research examining the role of identity diffusion in relation to other developmental and contextual factors is needed to further explain the pathways linking emotional abuse to psychological pain and despair, potentially informing more targeted prevention and intervention approaches for individuals who suffer from identity diffusion.
