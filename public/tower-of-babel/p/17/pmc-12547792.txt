Geological and Anthropogenic Factors Jointly Influence Hydrochemical Interactions between Groundwater and Surface Water in the Middle and Lower Yellow River
Authors: Menglei Wang, Jie Fang, Feisheng Feng, Tingting Yao, Yongping Shan, Wanli Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The interaction between groundwater and surface water plays a crucial role in regulating water resource availability and maintaining ecosystem stability, particularly through hydrochemical exchange processes. However, at the regional scale, the driving factors governing hydrochemical interactions between groundwater and surface water remain inadequately understood. This study aims to identify the dominant controlling factors and ion sources influencing hydrochemical interactions in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River. A comprehensive literature review was conducted on the hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater and surface water across China. Ultimately, 150 representative sampling sites located in the middle and lower Yellow River Basin were selected, encompassing 28,351 groundwater samples and 15,487 surface water samples. Sampling was conducted during both the rain and dry seasons, covering different hydrological periods. Hydrochemical characteristics were analyzed by using a combination of hydrogeochemical statistical methods, Piper diagrams, ion ratio relationships, Gibbs plots, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Furthermore, the Absolute Principal Component Scores–Multiple Linear Regression (APCS-MLR) model and PHREEQC geochemical simulations were applied to quantify the contributions of different ion sources and assess mineral dissolution and precipitation processes. The results show that the main ions in groundwater and surface water mainly come from rock weathering, evaporation, and human activities. The dissolution of carbonates and gypsum is the source of Ca2+, Mg2+ and HCO3 – in groundwater and surface water. In addition, the discharge of industrial wastewater and domestic sewage contributed significantly to Cl–, SO4 2– and Na+. The K+ and NO3 – ions might be due to the application of nitrogen and potassium fertilizers, although contributions from other sources (e.g., sewage, natural mineral dissolution) cannot be excluded. Moreover, the PCA and APCS-MLR analyses indicated that the hydrochemical interactions between groundwater and surface water were predominantly controlled by geological conditions (48.979%), followed by agricultural activities (16.773%), industrial influences (12.276%), and unidentified factors (21.972%). Among these, geological factors and mineral dissolution processes emerged as the most significant controls. Unidentified factors may include unmeasured variables such as atmospheric deposition, localized anthropogenic inputs, or complex groundwater–surface water exchange dynamics not fully captured by the model. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving groundwater–surface water hydrochemical interactions and offer valuable insights for regional water resource management, sustainable utilization, and ecological environment conservation in the Yellow River Basin.

Introduction

Groundwater and surface water play a significant role in the Earth’s hydrological cycle, with interactions occurring through the exchange of chemical constituents. , The quality of water resources is dependent upon hydrochemical interactions between groundwater and surface water. Chemicals (potassium and nitrogen) enter different water cycle processes through transport and transformation. Excess nitrogen leads to degradation of water quality and eutrophication of water. , However, the factors that drive groundwater and surface water hydrochemistry interactions remain unclear. It can be better regulated between groundwater and surface water interactions by understanding the transport and transformation patterns of chemical substances under different conditions. , Therefore, identifying the key drivers between groundwater and surface water hydrochemical interactions can help to develop more effective regulatory strategies for water resource management and ecosystem sustainability.

Hydrochemical information can explain the water cycle and evolution characteristics of groundwater and surface water. Hydrochemical analysis had become one of the most important technical means for identifying the interaction between groundwater and surface water. Hydrogeochemical statistics, Piper plots, correlation analysis, ion ratios, Gibbs diagrams, and principal components (PCA) are commonly used to identify the sources and migration of water chemical components. , However, the accuracy of water chemical parameters is mainly influenced by complex geological conditions, water and rock interactions, and human activities, and the characteristics of water chemical interactions between groundwater and surface water are uncertain. In the evolution of groundwater and surface water, the main cations are Na+, Ca2+ and Mg2+; anions are dominated by HCO3 –, Cl– and SO4 2–. Other ions (K+, NO3 –) have lower concentrations. Generally, groundwater and surface water exhibit a similar pH range and are slightly alkaline. Human activities (fertilizers, industrial effluents) can lead to a decrease in pH. The chemical composition and ion concentration of groundwater and surface water are affected by different geological conditions. For example, weathering of rocks in the Loess Plateau will increase the concentrations of Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), Ca2+, Mg2+ and Na+. , Seawater intrusion in coastal areas significantly increase the concentration of groundwater and surface water Cl–, leading to salinization and deterioration of water quality. Ca­(Na)-HCO3 is the predominant water chemistry type in the between groundwater and surface water hydrological cycle. The dissolution of minerals leads to a transition from the Ca­(Na)–HCO3 to the Ca­(Na)–HCO3·Cl·SO4 type. The interactions of groundwater and surface water induce dissolution, precipitation, and redox reaction, along with the exchange of substances (nitrogen, carbonates, heavy metals) and energy (chemical energy). These processes lead to a higher or lower concentration of ions such as Ca2+, Na+, Mg2+, HCO3 –. , This shows that the hydrochemical characteristics and ion sources of groundwater and surface water are closely associated with evolutionary processes and varying geological conditions. Changes in water chemical composition and ion concentrations reflect the interactions of groundwater and surface water, and elucidate the dynamics of these interactions.

Natural processes (rock weathering, dissolution), human activities (pesticides and fertilizers, industrial effluents), climatic conditions (evaporation, precipitation), have a strong influence on groundwater and surface water interactions. The main processes in the hydrochemical cycle of groundwater and surface water interaction are dissolution of carbonate, gypsum, dolomite, calcite and exchange of cations. , Zhang et al. found that groundwater and surface water in the Yarkand River Basin undergo mineral dissolution and precipitation, releasing Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions into the water. Chen et al. found that when dolomite and calcite come into contact with water, the concentrations of Mg2+, HCO3 – and Ca2+ will increase, making the water hardness higher. However, the factors driving groundwater and surface water hydrochemical interactions differ across regions. Hydrological and anthropogenic activities play a crucial role in controlling groundwater and surface water hydrochemical interactions. The dissolution of carbonates and gypsum increase the concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3 – in groundwater and surface water due to the lithology of bedrock in the Loess Plateau region. In the North China Plain region, frequent human activities have led to increased concentrations of K+ and NO3 – in groundwater and surface water. Das et al. and Yin et al. found that agricultural land fertilization and industrial wastewater discharge will significantly increase the concentration of NO3 – and SO4 2–, resulting in a decrease in pH in the water. Gu et al. determined that domestic sewage and septic tank pollution as well as agricultural fertilizers are factors affecting groundwater quality. Understanding the factors driving hydrochemical interactions between groundwater and surface water is essential for the effective management, protection, and sustainable use of these resources in China. Due to the different intensities of natural and human activities in different regions, there are differences in the temporal and spatial changes in water chemical composition. Therefore, in order to accurately identify the change pattern of water chemical composition in rivers, comprehensive investigations and systematic research are needed.

The Yellow River is a vast area; it spans a number of geological units (mountains, hills, plains, and basins) and climatic types (arid, semiarid, humid). As a result, the factors influencing groundwater and surface water hydrochemical interactions differ significantly across different regions of the Yellow River Basin. Groundwater and surface water interactions are primarily influenced by climatic factors (rainfall) in the upper reaches of the Yellow River characterized by high mountains and canyons. The middle reaches flow through the Loess Plateau, where severe soil erosion makes rock weathering the dominant factor affecting these interactions. , In contrast, the lower Yellow River plain, an important agricultural production base in China, is significantly impacted by the excessive use of agricultural fertilizers, which controls groundwater and surface water hydrochemical interactions. Diverse factors influencing groundwater and surface water interactions in the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the Yellow River have resulted in variations in hydrochemistry, negatively affecting the ecological environment and disrupting the balance of water resources. Therefore, this paper examines the hydrochemical composition, evolution, and interactions of groundwater and surface water in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River, focusing on the geological conditions and human activities that drive these processes. This study aims to: (1) analyze the hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater and surface water in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River; (2) explore the main sources of groundwater and surface water; (3) identify the factors that affect the hydrochemical interactions between groundwater and surface water. The results can provide a scientific basis for understanding these interactions and hydrochemical evolution in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

Geographic Location and Sample Points

The Yellow River is the second longest river in China and has profoundly influenced human civilization. The Yellow River Basin extends across China from east to west, primarily situated in the country’s northwest. It spans longitudes 95°53′–119°05′ E and latitudes 32°10″–41°50′ N, with a total length of 5464 km and a watershed area of 795,000 km2, as shown in Figure . This study focuses on the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River Basin, which flow eastward from Tao Hua Yu in Zhengzhou to the Bohai Sea. These sections, characterized by flat terrain, traverse the Loess Plateau and the North China Plain. The basin experiences a warm temperate continental monsoon climate with mild temperatures, distinct seasons, summer-concentrated rainfall, and cold, dry winters. The regional economy is largely agricultural, making it one of China’s key agricultural areas.

Distribution map of sample points in Yellow River’s middle reaches, downstream area.

Land Use/Land Cover

Grassland is mainly distributed in the upper reaches of the Yellow River basin, with the largest land area. The middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River are mainly cultivated land, and the land area of cultivated land gradually decreases with the passage of time. In addition, forested areas comprise the middle and upper reaches of the Yellow River. The main land use types in the Yellow River Basin are grassland (57%), farmland (25%) and forest (10%), as shown in Figure .

Land cover in the Yellow River Basin.

Data Collection

In this paper, the Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and China Wanfang Literature Database were used to conduct a comprehensive literature search on hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater and surface water in China from 1991 to 2024. The keywords used in the search were “interactions,” “groundwater and surface water,” and “water chemistry.” The included studies met the following criteria: (1) Field research was conducted in China; (2) The latitude and longitude coordinates of the sampling points are provided; (3) The processing and analysis methods adopt strict steps; (4) Data consist of maximum value, minimum value, and average value; (5) The sampling year is recorded, and if the sampling year is not mentioned, all data should be based on the date of receipt of the manuscript. In the end, 150 articles from across the country were included in the meta-analysis study, which collected 28,351 groundwater samples and 15,487 surface water samples. The samples covered a range of aquifers, including submersible, confined water and shallow and deep groundwater, as well as surface waters in lakes, rivers, swamps, and glaciers.

Analytical Methods

Descriptive Statistics

This study selected water chemistry data from groundwater (119 samples) and surface water (31 samples) in Yellow River’s middle reaches, downstream area, for the period from 1991 to 2024. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the water chemistry data, strict QA/QC procedures were implemented in laboratory analysis. The water chemical ion parameters of groundwater and surface water are mainly determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) for the contents of Na+, K+, Mg2+ and Ca2+ in the samples. The detection limits of K+, Na+ and Mg2+ are 0.02 mg/L, and the detection limit of Ca2+ is 0.01 mg/L. The contents of SO4 2–, Cl– and NO3 – were determined by an ICS-600 ion chromatography system, and the detection limits were 0.1, 0.06, and 0.02 mg/L respectively. The content of HCO3 – was determined by acid–base titration, with a detection limit of 0.05 mg/L. The charge balance error percentage (%CBE) of all water samples was within ±5%, indicating that the data were reliable. , The pH, oxidation–reduction potential (ORP), dissolved oxygen (DO), conductivity (EC), and water temperature (T) were measured by using the portable multiparameter water quality meter (model: Manta 2.0, Eureka Co., Ltd., Houston, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the concentration characteristics of groundwater and surface water. Piper’s trilinear diagrams were used to analyze the ionic composition and change characteristics of surface water and underground water. Interrelationships between major ions in water were analyzed through the ratio of major ions and to illustrate the origin of hydrogeochemical processes and underground water chemistry. Gibbs plots revealed the main mechanisms of dissolved substances in underground water and surface water: precipitation, rock weathering and evaporation.

During the data collection process, only research that clearly states the sampling methods, analytical techniques, and detection limits in the report was included. All ion concentrations were standardized to consistent units to allow for comparability. When research reports were based on results from different time periods or sampling seasons, these results were retained. Outliers were systematically identified by using statistical methods (interquartile range and standard deviation) and comparing them with the regional background values reported in the literature.

PHREEQC Simulation

PHREEQC for Windows (PHREEQC Interactive version 3.7.3–15968, U.S. Geological Survey) is a computer program for simulating chemical reactions and transport processes in natural or polluted water, in laboratory experiments, or in industrial processes. It is also the official free- and open-source version provided by the United States Geological Survey (USGS), which does not require a license number or institutional access. The mineral saturation index and the dissolution–precipitation of mineral phases were calculated by applying the PHREEQC software to identify the possible mineral phases based on the variation of the sample ion concentration in a representative flow path. The saturation index (SI) was used to indicate the state of mineral dissolution or precipitation. , The SI can be defined by eq . 13 SI=logIAPK where IAP represents the ionic activity product of ions in solution, while K is the equilibrium constant of the mineral. An SI greater than 0 indicates that the mineral is supersaturated; an SI less than 0 means the mineral will dissolve due to an unsaturated state; and an SI equal to 0 signifies that the mineral is in equilibrium with the solution.

In the PHREEQC simulation, the input parameters include the main cations and anions (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, HCO3 –, SO4 2–, and NO3 –) and pH. The calculation was carried out using the phreeqc.dat thermodynamic database. All concentrations were converted to mol/L as required by PHREEQC. For reverse modeling, potential mineral phases (calcite, gypsum, rock salt, dolomite, etc.) include those based on geological background.

Results

Basic Characteristics of Groundwater and Surface Water Hydrochemistry

The pH values of groundwater and surface water in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River were 7.35–9.37 and 7.38–9.1, respectively, with average values of 7.7 and 7.97, showing weak alkalinity. The concentrations of TDS in groundwater and surface water ranged from 118.42–5407 mg/L and 4.73–1430 mg/L, respectively, with average values of 1054.74 and 635.99 mg/L, respectively. According to the average concentration (expressed in mg/L), the cation concentrations of groundwater and surface water in Yellow River’s middle reaches, downstream area were Ca2+ > Na+ > K+ > Mg2+ and Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+, respectively. The concentration magnitudes of anions were SO4 2– > Cl– > HCO3 – > NO3 – and SO4 2– > HCO3 – > Cl– > NO3 –, respectively. The standard deviation (SD) of the main ions in groundwater are all greater than those in surface water. It indicates that the groundwater environment in the mining area is highly heterogeneous, significantly affected by the interaction of karst and fissure water and pollution infiltration. Indicators with large variations are often the main control factors determining the classification of water quality types (such as SO4 2–-Cl–-Na+ type), and are also often sensitive parameters for pollution source tracing analysis, as shown in Table .

Sources of Groundwater and Surface Water Chemistry

In Yellow River’s middle reaches and downstream area, 71.33% of the underground water and surface water sample points fall mainly in the silicate rock end-member region, 10.43% of the sample points were close to the evaporite saline rock end-member; 18.24% of the sample points were close to the carbonate rock end-member, and there is a tendency for the underground water and surface water to move toward carbonate rocks, as shown in Figure . Weathering and hydrolysis of silicate minerals control the hydrogeochemical processes of groundwater and surface water in Yellow River’s middle reaches, downstream area.

Correlation analysis of ions is a commonly used statistical analysis method that is employed to derive the degree of similarity of major ions in groundwater, thereby enabling inferences to be made regarding the chemical composition of water and whether it is homologous or not. The TDS of groundwater in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River was found to be significantly correlated with Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, SO4 2–, Cl– and HCO3 –. Furthermore, Na+ exhibited a significant correlation not only with Cl–, but also with Mg2+, SO4 2–, and HCO3 –. Additionally, SO4 2– and Ca2+ demonstrated a significant correlation. The TDS of surface water in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River was found to be significantly correlated with Na+, Mg2+, SO4 2–, Cl–, HCO3 –, and K+, and it was found to be significantly correlated with Na+. Furthermore, HCO3 – was found to be significantly correlated with Ca2+, Na+, Mg2+, SO4 2–, Cl–, K+ and Mg2+, as shown in Figure .

Reverse Hydrogeochemical Modeling

Mineral Saturation Index

The saturation index (SI) is utilized to evaluate the equilibrium between water and minerals, as well as to identify key geochemical processes. , As shown in Figure a, the SI range of rock salt in groundwater and surface water is −11.5 to −4.5, which is far from saturation, and rock salt will continue to dissolve continuously. In groundwater and surface water samples, 81% SI calcite > 0 indicates saturated calcite, and 19% SI calcite < 0 indicates unsaturated calcite, as shown in Figure b. Similarly, in 80% of the water samples, SI dolomite > 0, and only 20% of the samples were in the unsaturated state, as shown in Figure c. In 98% of the samples, the gypsum SI < 0 indicates that the gypsum is unsaturated and the gypsum is dissolved by groundwater in the general direction of flow. Therefore, Ca2+ and SO4 2– were derived primarily from the dissolution of gypsum in groundwater.

Simulation of Paths and Possible Mineral Reaction Equations

The selection of possible mineral phases in the simulation process is the key to establishing the mass balance model of water–rock interactions. The main mineral components of groundwater and surface water should be given priority, and the main chemical elements contained in the selected possible mineral phases should be consistent with the test results of water samples. Discussion

Groundwater and Surface Water Hydrochemical Characteristics and Ionic Origin Sources

The groundwater geochemical type in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River mainly transitions from the HCO3–Ca·Na type to the SO4·HCO3·Cl–Na·Ca type. This change reflects the combined effects of water–rock interactions, human activities and evaporation processes in the region. Subba Rao pointed out that mineral dissolution and human activities are important driving factors for the evolution of hydrochemical types. Previous studies have found that Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl–, SO4 2– and HCO3 – increase with the increase of TDS concentration, indicating that the dissolution of these ions plays an important role in the increase of TDS in groundwater and surface water. ,

When groundwater flows through different rock layers, it undergoes chemical reactions with minerals in the rocks. When water chloride minerals (such as rock salt) are present, these minerals dissolve to release Na+ and Cl– ions. , As shown in Figure a the Na+/Cl– ratio is greater than 1 indicates excess Na+ derived from both rock salt dissolution and anthropogenic inputs (e.g., alkaline and sodium-rich wastewater). ,,

Factors Influencing the Water Chemical Composition of Groundwater and Surface Water

Water–Rock Interactions

Gibbs plots are commonly used to study the effects of water–rock interactions on hydrochemistry. They provide a macroscopic understanding of the controlling factors of major ions in groundwater and surface water, including water–rock interaction, evaporation, and precipitation. ,

As shown in Figures a and b, the samples are mainly located in the water–rock interaction zone. With the evaporation of surface water, CaCO3 precipitates from the solution, resulting in an increase in the ratio of Na+/(Na+ + Ca2+) to Cl–/(Cl– + HCO3 –), indicating that carbonates precipitate. , A small number of samples are in the transitional zone between the water–rock interaction and evaporation, indicating that the chemical composition of water is mainly controlled by the water–rock interaction, with less influence from evaporation. Only a very small number of samples fell in the precipitation area, indicating that groundwater and surface water are less affected by precipitation. The application of Gaillardet diagrams further distinguishes the types of water–rock interactions. As shown in Figures a and b, the sample points in the study area are mainly located in the action zones of silicates and carbonates, far away from the action zone of evaporites, indicating that the formation of the chemical characteristics of groundwater and surface water is mainly related to the dissolution and filtration of carbonates and silicates. ,

Cation Exchange

Cation exchange is also an important factor affecting the chemical composition of groundwater and surface water. Li et al. showed that the increase in TDS was not significant with the increase in Na+/[(Na+ + Ca2+]), indicating that cation exchange occurred during the water–rock interaction, and the negative ions on the bedrock surface combined with cations (Ca2+ and Mg2+) with strong adsorption capacity, resulting in an increase in the content of Na+ and Ca2+. Jun et al. and Li et al. found that if cation exchange occurs, n­(Ca2+ + Mg2+-HCO3 –-SO4 2–)/(Na+ + K+-Cl–) is approximately equal to −1. As shown in Figure a, the sample points of groundwater and surface water are close to the line with a slope of −1, indicating that the chemistry of surface water and groundwater is affected by cation exchange. Human Factors

The hydrochemical composition Cl–, SO4 2–, and NO3 – in groundwater and surface water are often used as characteristic pollutants indicating anthropogenic input. Gao et al. and Liu et al. found that SO4 2– mainly comes from industrial activities, while Cl– and NO3 – are affected by agricultural activities and domestic sewage. , Wang et al. found that the larger the ratio of NO3 –/Na+ and Cl–, the greater the impact of human activities. In Figure a, both ratios are relatively high, and some sample points fall at the end of agricultural activities, indicating that groundwater and surface water are affected by agricultural activities to a certain extent. Ren et al. found that when SO4 2–/Ca2+ is greater than NO3 –/Ca2+, the impact of industrial and mining activities is greater, and conversely, it indicates that agricultural activities and domestic sewage have a greater impact. As shown in Figure b, SO4 2–/Ca2+ is greater than NO3 –/Ca2+ in most samples, indicating that industrial activities in the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River have a greater impact on water chemistry, but there are also impacts from agricultural activities and domestic sewage. However, K+ is different from NO3 – in that it has some lithological sources. According to the mineral analysis by PHREEQC, potassium salt minerals may exist, and the dissolution of potassium salt minerals will increase the potassium content in groundwater and surface water.

Contribution of Different Control Factors to Water Chemical Composition

In the process of groundwater recharging surface water, minerals and dissolved solids in groundwater enter surface water, which may lead to an increase in the mineralization of surface water. Li et al. found that frequent groundwater extraction accelerated the water–rock effect in the formation of water chemical components. When surface water infiltrates into the ground, the pollutants it contains will also affect the chemical properties of groundwater. Abdulhalim et al. found that this effect is more significant, especially in agricultural and urbanized areas. The groundwater chemical characteristics in the study area are affected by human activity factors as well as hydrogeological conditions, water–rock interactions, and alternating cation adsorption. In order to further determine the extent of the influence of each ion indicator on water chemical characteristics, this study extracted three principal components from both groundwater and surface water. As shown in Table , the principal component results of groundwater and surface water are similar. Guo et al. found that in natural processes, the dissolution of minerals such as silicates and gypsum leads to increased concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, SO4 2–, and HCO3 – ions. PCA shows that PC1 has the highest loading, among which Ca2+, Mg2+, SO4 2–, and HCO3 – account for a high proportion, indicating that PC1 reflects the influence of water–rock interaction on water chemistry. Liu et al. found that potassium fertilizers and nitrogen fertilizers are widely used in agricultural fertilization, and water often contains a large amount of K+ and NO3 –. The significant contributions of K+ and NO3 – to PC2 and the correlation between them indicate that PC2 reflects the impact of agricultural activities on water chemistry. Nasuha et al. and Santucci et al. studied sulfuric acid as an industrial chemical, and sulfate ions are often contained in the byproducts of the production process, which react with water as sulfate ions. , Angelucci et al. found that bleaching agents, disinfectants, and sewage treatment agents are often used in industry, which are usually made with chlorine or its compounds. Therefore, the significant contribution of SO4 2– and Cl– to PC3 indicates that PC3 reflects the impact of industrial activities on water chemistry.

The PCA method is limited to qualitative analysis of ion sources and cannot quantitatively analyze their contributions. In contrast, the APCS-MLR model can quantify the distribution of ion sources. Zhou et al. and Yi et al. found through PCA and APCS-MLR that the hydrochemical composition of groundwater and surface water is jointly affected by rock weathering and human activities. , As shown in the Table , the contribution rates of industrial factors to surface water and groundwater ions are Cl– (43.04%, 44.21%), SO4 2– (46.88%, 47.12%), and Na+ (42.41%, 67.49%), respectively; indicating that the discharge of industrial wastewater is the main source of Cl– and SO4 2– ions. The contribution rates of geological factors to ions are Ca2+ (45.99%, 67.21%), Mg2+ (66.52%, 43.18%), and HCO3 – (42.97%, 30.08%), respectively. This indicates that the dissolution of minerals is the main source of Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3 – ions. The contribution rates of agricultural factors to ions are K+ (69.87%, 67.30%), and NO3 – (58.52%, 59.67%), respectively. This indicates that agricultural activities are the main source of K+ and NO3 – ions.

In this study, the APCS-MLR model indicated that approximately 22% of the variance in hydrochemical composition was attributed to an “unknown factor,” This unexplained fraction highlights the complexity of groundwater–surface water interactions and suggests that some relevant processes or sources were not fully captured by the current set of explanatory variables. Potential contributors to this fraction include atmospheric deposition (e.g., acid rain, dust inputs) localized sewage discharges from rural settlements, and episodic hydrological or anthropogenic events such as storm runoff, irrigation return flow, or accidental wastewater releases. , Moreover, small-scale groundwater–surface water exchange processes that are spatially heterogeneous may also contribute to this unexplained variability. While the present data set primarily focused on major ions, incorporating additional tracers (e.g., isotopes, nutrients, or trace metals) and event-based monitoring would help reduce this “unknown” component in future studies. Thus, the relatively high proportion of the unknown factor underscores both the limitations of the current approach and the need for more integrated monitoring and multiparameter analysis to fully characterize the drivers of hydrochemical dynamics in the Yellow River Basin.

Implications for Groundwater–Surface Water Interaction and Water Resource Management in the Yellow River Basin

Although our data set spans more than three decades, hydrological regimes and anthropogenic pressures in the study area have evolved over time due to climate change, land-use change, and water resource management interventions. Although we subset the data for known sampling years, temporal coverage is not uniform across studies, which may introduce uncertainty into the interpretation. Future meta-analyses should incorporate temporally stratified or continuous monitoring data sets to better resolve temporal dynamics.

The findings of this study have important implications for understanding the hydrochemical evolution of groundwater and surface water in the Yellow River Basin. The transition in water chemistry types as well as the identified dominant ion sources reveal the complexity of water–rock interactions and anthropogenic influences at the basin scale. The high proportion of samples controlled by geological factors, particularly in the Loess Plateau region, suggests that natural weathering processes continue to play a fundamental role in shaping groundwater and surface water chemistry.

The impact of agricultural and industrial inputs on the interaction between groundwater and surface water is growing increasingly significant, especially in the lower reaches of the Yellow River, where irrigation, fertilizer use, and industrial emissions have intensified solute exchange. These processes disrupt the geochemical balance and increase the risk of cross-room pollution. Furthermore, evidence from positive ion exchange and reverse simulations indicates that climate change and intensive water use may further exacerbate spatially heterogeneous flow paths and accelerate the deterioration of the local water quality.

Although our research focuses on historical and current water chemical conditions, future climate change may substantially affect the groundwater–surface water interaction in the basin. Changes in precipitation and temperature may alter the rate of groundwater recharge, surface water flow and solute transport, potentially changing the relative contributions of natural and anthropogenic sources to water chemistry. Therefore, adaptive monitoring and management strategies should take into account potential climate-driven changes in the hydrological and geochemical processes.

To ensure sustainable management of water resources in the Yellow River Basin, it is essential to consider the integrated behavior of groundwater and surface water systems. Monitoring programs should incorporate hydrochemical indicators identified in this study (e.g., SO4 2–, Cl–, NO3 –, and Na+) as early warning signals of contamination. Furthermore, targeted land-use regulation and pollution control policies are urgently needed to mitigate diffuse agricultural and industrial impacts on water chemistry. Future research should focus on coupling hydrochemical data with hydrodynamic models and stable isotope techniques to quantify water fluxes and solute transport mechanisms at the basin scale.

Therefore, in order to effectively prevent further deterioration of groundwater quality in this area, a monitoring framework for the entire basin should be established. This framework should include key water chemistry indicators such as Na+, SO4 2–, NO3 –, and Ca2+, Mg2+ and TDS, and sampling of groundwater (GW) and surface water (SW) should be conducted quarterly. In areas known to be affected by human factors, heavy metals (such As Pb, Cd, As, and Cr) should be monitored monthly. It is also recommended to use automatic sensors to continuously monitor the groundwater level and flow rate to detect rapid changes. The collected data should be analyzed using multivariate statistical methods and geochemical models (such as PCA-APCS-MLR, PHREEQC) to identify trends and potential pollution sources and inform adaptive management strategies. Meanwhile, the government and water resource management departments should force a halt to the discharge of household and industrial sewage that does not meet the standards and promote the optimization of fertilization practices. Differentiated water resource management strategies should be adopted in regions mainly affected by geology (such as the Loess Plateau) and those mainly affected by agricultural activities (such as the North China Plain). In addition, quantitatively identifying potential future pollution sources is necessary for formulating targeted control measures. Overall, these comprehensive monitoring and management strategies provide scientific support for the sustainable management of water resources, ecological protection, and pollution mitigation in the Yellow River Basin and offer a framework for the evaluation of groundwater–surface water interactions in other large river basins with complex hydrogeological conditions.
