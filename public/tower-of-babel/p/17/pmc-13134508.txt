HTLV‐1 Tax Reshapes the DNA‐Binding Pattern of Transcription Factor IRF4 and Disrupts Host Gene Regulation
Authors: Shu Tosaka, Jun Mizuike, Motomu Yano, Kaoru Uchimaru, Makoto Yamagishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman T‐cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV‐1) causes adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) and HTLV‐1–associated myelopathy, and its viral transactivator Tax is central to disrupting host transcriptional control. Although individual interactions between Tax and host factors have been investigated, their global network and functional impact remain unclear. In this study, we performed proteomic analyses of Tax‐associated complexes in HTLV‐1–infected T‐cells and mapped interactions between host factors and Tax. In addition to known nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) regulators, we identified the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) as a novel interactor. Co‐immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed Tax–IRF4 binding, and chromatin profiling revealed that Tax reprograms genome‐wide IRF4 occupancy. In infected T‐cells, IRF4 was enriched in super‐enhancer (SE) regions, where Tax, IRF4, and NF‐κB factors colocalized at ATL‐related loci, and this was accompanied by histone acetylation changes and transcriptional activation. Functionally, Tax and IRF4 co‐expression cooperatively restructured chromatin accessibility and induced aberrant gene expression programs. Moreover, ATL‐associated IRF4 mutants (K59R, L70V, and S114N) partially reproduced Tax‐driven chromatin remodeling and transcriptional reprogramming, suggesting that convergent mechanisms drive leukemogenesis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Tax exploits IRF4 to establish a novel regulatory mechanism. This interaction drives widespread epigenomic reprogramming in HTLV‐1–infected T‐cells. Overall, our study underscores the central role of Tax in IRF4 exploitation to induce infection‐specific epigenomic changes, which offers novel insights into ATL pathogenesis and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: ATL, chromatin, epigenetics, HTLV‐1, interactome

Introduction

Human T‐cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV‐1) causes adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL), a CD4+ T‐cell malignancy, as well as inflammatory disorders such as HTLV‐1–associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Despite being studied for four decades, the molecular mechanisms by which HTLV‐1 reprograms host transcriptional and epigenomic landscapes to drive cellular transformation remain poorly understood. Several viral factors contribute to HTLV‐1 pathogenesis, but the effects of the transactivator protein Tax are notable during early infection. Through interactions with host factors, Tax persistently activates nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) and activator protein 1 (AP‐1) signaling pathways, disrupts cell‐cycle checkpoints, and induces genomic instability to produce pleiotropic effects on host cells, including enhanced proliferation and immortalization [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Tax is believed to drive extensive transcriptional reprogramming in early infection by targeting a broad range of host transcription factors and chromatin regulators.

Defining the proteome‐wide effects of Tax on host proteins is essential for elucidating how HTLV‐1 subverts host‐cell function. Although yeast‐based screening has identified numerous partners of Tax [11], no systematic survey has examined proteins physiologically interacting with endogenous Tax in bona fide HTLV‐1–infected T‐cells. Thus, the complete spectrum of Tax‐associated host factors and their roles in epigenomic remodeling should be defined.

In addition to Tax, the antisense‐encoded viral protein HTLV‐1 basic leucine zipper factor (HBZ) is consistently expressed in ATL cells and sustains leukemic proliferation through AP‐1—and interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4)‐dependent transcriptional networks [12, 13, 14, 15]. IRF4 is a lineage‐defining transcription factor in ATL and peripheral T‐cell lymphoma, with genomic studies revealing recurrent IRF4 mutations and amplifications in ATL [16, 17]. During terminal acute transformation, additional IRF4 mutations accelerate subclonal evolution [18]; hence, IRF4 is considered a key oncogenic driver. IRF4 also cooperates with host transcription factors, such as NF‐κB [19, 20] and AP‐1 [15], as well as HBZ, to regulate enhancer architecture and gene expression; however, its crosstalk with HTLV‐1 Tax and the epigenomic effects of its mutations remain unknown.

Given the importance of transcriptional reprogramming in HTLV‐1–induced oncogenesis, we investigated whether Tax interacts with lineage‐specific host transcription factors to alter epigenetic regulation, including chromatin remodeling and histone modification, to drive aberrant gene expression. Specifically, we identified host factors interacting with Tax in HTLV‐1–infected T‐cells and analyzed their genome‐wide roles in histone modification, chromatin architecture, and gene expression. These analyses provide insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the Tax‐mediated induction of epigenetic dysregulation in early infection and highlight mechanisms potentially linking viral infection to somatic mutagenesis.

Material and Methods

Cell Culture

T‐cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and antibiotics. JPX‐9, a Jurkat‐derived subclone carrying the tax gene inducible by divalent metal ions, was additionally treated with 100 μM ZnSO4 to induce expression of the tax gene [21]. HEK293FT cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 400 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and 1% Triton X‐100) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Nacalai Tesque) and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail 2 (Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck). Lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 19,100 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. The antibodies used for immunoprecipitation were as follows: anti‐Tax (Lt‐4) [22], anti‐IRF4 (Cell Signaling Technology, #15106), anti‐RelA (Cell Signaling Technology, #8242), anti‐RelB (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐226), anti‐HA (Medical & Biological Laboratories, #561), anti‐Mouse IgG (Sigma‐Aldrich, Merck, #I5381), and anti‐Rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, #2729). Antibodies were pre‐bound to Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature. A portion of the lysate was retained as an input control. The remaining lysate was incubated with the antibody‐bead complex for 2 h at 4°C with rotation. The beads were then washed and separated using a magnet. The resulting samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS and immunoblots. For immunoblots, the input and bead‐bound proteins were eluted with SDS sample buffer and denatured by boiling.

Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)

Tax‐associated protein complexes in the HTLV‐1–infected cell line C91/PL (5 × 107 cells) were isolated by co‐immunoprecipitation using an anti‐Tax antibody (Lt‐4). Because Tax localizes to both the nucleus and cytoplasm, extraction conditions were optimized to recover protein complexes from these compartments. The immunoprecipitated proteins were enriched and purified using Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Shotgun proteomic analysis (LC–MS/MS) was performed by the Chemicals Evaluation and Research Institute, Japan (CERI). For nanoLC–MS/MS analysis, an UltiMate 3000 liquid chromatography system coupled with a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. Data acquisition was controlled using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and measurements were performed in duplicate. From the resulting peptide fragment data, intensity‐based absolute quantification values were calculated, and comparative analysis of protein expression profiles across samples was conducted. The identified proteins were further analyzed for protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks using the STRING database (v.12.0).

RNA Isolation and RNA‐Seq

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), treated with Recombinant DNase I, and quantified and qualified by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA (20 ng, RIN > 7) was used for library preparation. The library preparation (rRNA depletion method) and sequencing (30 million paired‐end reads) were processed and analyzed by GENEWIZ. Gene expression data were comparatively analyzed using TPM.

Assay for Transposase‐Accessible Chromatin With Sequencing (ATAC‐Seq)

ATAC‐seq assays were performed using the ATAC‐Seq Kit (Active Motif, #53150) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 1 × 105 viable cells were pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, washed with ice‐cold PBS, and resuspended in ATAC Lysis Buffer. Cells were incubated on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4°C. After removing the supernatant, cells were subjected to tagmentation by adding Tagmentation Master Mix and incubating at 37°C for 30 min with shaking. Tagmented DNA was purified and eluted in Elution Buffer. Libraries were prepared using the ATAC‐Seq Kit workflow. Sequencing quality control metrics are summarized in Table S1.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP‐Seq)

ChIP‐seq was performed using an IRF4 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #15106). For C91/PL cells, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) from 1 × 107 fixed cells and library preparation were performed at Active Motif, and the methods have been described previously [23]. For Jurkat cells overexpressing IRF4, ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (Cell Signaling Technology, #9005) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 4 × 106 cells were cross‐linked with formaldehyde, lysed, and chromatin was fragmented by partial digestion with micrococcal nuclease. Immunoprecipitation was then carried out with ChIP‐Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads, followed by reversal of cross‐links and DNA purification using spin columns. Libraries were prepared using the DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (ChIP‐seq, CUT&RUN) (Cell Signaling Technology, #56795) together with Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (Cell Signaling Technology, #47538). Sequencing quality control metrics are summarized in Table S1. H3K27ac ChIP‐seq data of acute‐type ATL were obtained from a previous study [23].

Cleavage Under Targets & Tagmentation (CUT&Tag)

CUT&Tag assays were performed using the CUT&Tag Assay Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #77552) according to the manufacturer's protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, 1 × 105 cells were first fixed with 0.1% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #28906) for 2 min at room temperature and quenched with 1.25 M glycine for 5 min. Cells were pelleted, washed once with Complete Wash Buffer, including Spermidine and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and resuspended in the same buffer. The cell suspension was mixed with Concanavalin A Magnetic Beads. Following bead binding, the subsequent steps, including permeabilization, antibody incubation, tagmentation, and DNA purification, were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Primary antibodies against H3K27ac (Cell Signaling Technology, #8173) and IRF4 (Cell Signaling Technology, #15106) were used. Libraries were prepared using the CUT&Tag Assay Kit workflow. Sequencing quality control metrics are summarized in Table S1.

Library Preparation, Quality Control, and Sequencing

Libraries from ATAC‐seq, ChIP‐seq, and CUT&Tag experiments were prepared according to the respective manufacturers' protocols, including adaptor ligation, PCR amplification with indexed primers, and magnetic bead purification. All libraries were quantified with a Qubit Fluorometer and assessed for fragment size distribution using an Agilent TapeStation or Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed on Illumina platforms in paired‐end mode (2 × 150 bp), except that ChIP‐seq libraries of C91/PL cells were also sequenced in single‐end mode (75 bp).

Functional Analysis of IRF4

IRF4 cDNA was cloned into a CSII‐EF‐MCS‐IRES2‐Venus lentivirus vector. Jurkat cells were suspended in lentiviral solution and centrifuged at 400 × g, 2 h, at 35°C. After 4 days, Venus‐positive cells were collected by FACSAria II. For IRF4 knockdown, the lentivirus vector CS‐H1‐Venus‐IRES‐Bsd was used with two shRNA. The target sequences of shRNAs are as follows: shIRF4 #1, GCCATTCCTCTATTCAAGAAT; shIRF4 #4, GCCCAAATTCTCCTCTCTAAA.

Immunofluorescence Staining

1 × 105 cells were suspended in 100 μL of PBS and cytocentrifuged onto glass slides using a Cytospin 2 (Shandon) at 800 rpm for 3 min at room temperature. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 10 min, and blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 30 min. Slides were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted 1:200 in PBS containing 3% BSA overnight at 4°C. After washing by immersion in PBS containing 3% BSA, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted 1:500 in PBS containing 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Following washing with PBS containing 3% BSA, excess liquid was removed, and slides were mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon A1Rsi confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon) and analyzed with NIS‐Elements AR software.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Dual‐luciferase reporter assay was performed using HEK293FT cells. pGL4.25[luc2CP/minP] vector (Promega, #E8431) was modified by inserting a 3 × AICE sequence (GAAATGAGTCAGAAATGAGTCAGAAATGAGTCA) upstream of the minimal promoter to generate the reporter plasmid pGL4.25‐AICE‐Fluc. Expression vectors included pC.HA‐IRF4 (WT, K59R, L70V, S114N) and pcDNA3.1 constructs encoding AP‐1 factors (BATF, BATF3, JUN, JUNB). Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 48‐well plates and transfected with the following amounts of plasmids using polyethylenimine and Opti‐MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific): 100 ng of pC.HA‐IRF4 (WT or mutant), 40 ng of a pcDNA3.1‐AP‐1 plasmid mixture, 100 ng of pGL4.25‐AICE‐Fluc, and 5 ng of pRSV‐Rluc. Cells were lysed with Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega, #E1910), and luciferase activities were measured separately with the Luciferase Assay System (Promega, #E1500) and the Renilla Assay System (Promega, #E2820) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Measurements were performed using a Centro LB 960 microplate luminometer (Berthold Technologies). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. Statistical analyses were performed by two‐sided Student's t‐test (mean ± SD, n = 4).

Bioinformatics Analysis

Quality of sequencing data was assessed using FastQC (v.0.12.1). Adapter sequences were removed with Cutadapt (v.4.9), and quality trimming was performed using Trimmomatic (v.0.39) with the parameters LEADING:20, TRAILING:20, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:36. Obtained reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) with Bowtie2 (v.2.5.4) with default settings. Reads with low mapping quality (MAPQ < 30), unmapped reads, secondary alignments, and PCR or optical duplicates were removed using samtools (v.1.21) and Picard (v.3.3.0). PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates with the option REMOVE_DUPLICATES = true. For hg38 alignments, reads overlapping ENCODE blacklist regions were optionally excluded. Normalized signal tracks were generated using the bamCoverage tool from deepTools (v.3.5.5) with a bin size of 10 bp and RPGC normalization, excluding mitochondrial reads (chrM). The effective genome size for hg38 was used for normalization. Peak calling was performed with MACS3 (v.3.0.2). For paired‐end data, BAMPE mode was applied, whereas for single‐end data, fragment sizes were estimated using the predictd function and peaks were called with ‐‐nomodel and the estimated fragment length. Narrow or broad peaks were identified depending on the analysis, with duplicate handling specified as either ‐‐keep‐dup all or ‐‐keep‐dup auto. Input DNA was used as a control when available. Peak annotation and motif enrichment analysis were performed using HOMER (v.5.1). Motif analysis was conducted with findMotifsGenome.pl with a window size of 200 bp centered on peak regions. Genomic annotation of peaks was performed with ChIPseeker (v.1.40.0) in R (v.4.4.1). The optimal number of clusters was determined using elbow and silhouette analyses of k‐means clustering applied to ChIP‐seq occupancy profiles of Tax and associated transcription factors. SE regions were identified based on H3K27ac ChIP‐seq data using the ROSE algorithm [24, 25]. Enhancers within 12.5 kb were stitched together, and regions located within ±3 kb of transcription start sites were excluded from SE calling. Transcription factor footprinting analysis was performed using TOBIAS (v.0.17.1) [26] with built‐in correction for Tn5 insertion bias. Gene Ontology analysis was performed using the DAVID database, and statistical significance was evaluated based on −log10‐transformed p values calculated by DAVID.

Results

Identification of Host Factors Interacting With Tax in HTLV‐1–Infected Cells

Previously, comprehensive analyses of the Tax interactome were often conducted outside the T‐cell context and frequently relied on ectopic, overexpression‐based expression of Tax. Instead, to determine the impact of HTLV‐1 Tax on host cell factors, we purified Tax‐associated protein complexes from the HTLV‐1–infected T‐cell line C91/PL, which stably expresses Tax, using the Tax‐specific monoclonal antibody Lt‐4 [22]. This procedure allowed the identification of Tax‐associated proteins under more physiologically relevant conditions.

We performed two independent shotgun proteomic analyses of the physiologically active complex and identified Tax‐interacting proteins (Figure 1A,B; Table 1). PPI network analysis identified several components of the NF‐κB signaling pathway that were previously reported to interact with Tax, including RelA, RelB, REL, p105/p50, p100/p52, IKKα, IKKβ, and IKKγ [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. These findings highlight the crucial role of NF‐κB–related interactions. Other than these reported interactors, our proteomic analysis also identified several novel host proteins not previously found to associate with Tax, including transcriptional regulators and signaling molecules (Table 1). Among the candidates, the transcription factor IRF4 emerged as a particularly compelling factor for study because of its established functional relevance and frequent genetic alterations in ATL cells [16, 18], even though there was no prior evidence of its direct interaction with Tax. PPI network analysis revealed that IRF4 clustered closely with NF‐κB–related factors within the Tax‐associated network (Figure 1B). This network proximity suggests that IRF4 and NF‐κB components are connected through shared or overlapping interaction partners and may participate in a Tax‐centered regulatory network, suggesting a Tax‐mediated functional association. In context, given that IRF4 and NF‐κB form a feedforward loop in ATL and peripheral T‐cell lymphoma cells, regulating cancer‐related gene expression through super‐enhancers (SEs) [19, 20], this suggests that IRF4 is functionally integrated into an NF‐κB–centered transcriptional network by Tax‐mediated interactions. Hence, we hypothesized that Tax acts as a molecular scaffold to link IRF4 to the NF‐κB signaling network, thereby contributing to transcriptional reprogramming in HTLV‐1–infected cells.

To test this hypothesis, we performed experiments to determine whether Tax directly associates with IRF4. We transiently expressed HA‐tagged IRF4 and Tax in HEK293FT cells and performed co‐immunoprecipitation followed by immunoblotting. Both Tax‐IP and HA‐IP confirmed a physical Tax–IRF4 interaction (Figure 1C, Figure S1A). In the HTLV‐1–infected cell lines C91/PL and HUT102, we also detected endogenous Tax‐containing complexes, including Tax–IRF4 and Tax–NF‐κB (RelA and RelB) interactions (Figure 1D,E). However, we detected no direct, stable interaction between IRF4 and NF‐κB factors (Figure 1F), indicating that their association occurs within a Tax‐associated complex and suggesting that Tax acts as a scaffold to assemble multi‐component transcriptional complexes rather than activating independent transcriptional pathways. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the colocalization of Tax with IRF4, RelA, and RelB within the nucleus (Figure S1B,C). Overall, these findings indicate associations between Tax and multiple host transcriptional regulators, including IRF4.

Tax‐Mediated Reprogramming of IRF4 Chromatin Occupancy

Next, we investigated the impact of the Tax–IRF4 interaction on the chromatin profile of IRF4. Following IRF4 ChIP‐seq in Tax‐expressing C91/PL cells, genome‐wide mapping revealed extensive IRF4 occupancy across chromatin (Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of IRF4‐bound genes revealed enrichment in the GO categories of transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and chromatin remodeling (Figure 2B). Motif analysis using HOMER identified AP‐1–associated motifs as most enriched within IRF4 peaks. Analyzing previous Tax ChIP‐seq data [33], we found enrichment of SRF, AP‐1, and IRF motifs in Tax‐occupied genomic regions, suggesting that Tax is recruited to chromatin through interactions with host transcription factors and other co‐factors, rather than through direct DNA binding (Figure 2C).

To define the active regulatory regions jointly controlled by IRF4 and Tax, we analyzed the active chromatin mark H3K27ac. Using genome‐wide IRF4 ChIP‐seq peaks as anchors, we aligned and quantified ChIP‐seq signals for Tax, RelA, RelB, and H3K27ac, which revealed widespread co‐occupancy at IRF4‐bound regions (Figure 2D). This global overlap is consistent with the cooperative involvement of these factors in transcriptional activation. We then focused on H3K27ac‐high regions representing active regulatory elements and performed k‐means clustering. Elbow and silhouette analyses indicated that k = 3 was the most parsimonious solution, capturing distinct and biologically interpretable Tax occupancy patterns (Figure S2A,B). This analysis stratified active loci into distinct clusters with differential transcription factor occupancy and identified a subset of enhancers characterized by strong, Tax‐associated co‐recruitment of IRF4 and NF‐κB components (Figure 2E).

To further assess the direct effect of Tax on IRF4 chromatin occupancy, we generated Jurkat cell models, either ectopically expressing IRF4 alone or co‐expressing Tax and IRF4, and performed IRF4 ChIP‐seq. Genome‐wide IRF4 peaks were different between Tax co‐expression and IRF4 expression alone. IRF4 occupancy was significantly enriched within promoter‐proximal regions in the presence of Tax (odds ratio = 3.34, 95% CI = 2.84–3.95, p = 2.19 × 10−59), indicating a preferential redistribution of IRF4 toward promoter regions (Figure 2F,G). Genes with increased IRF4 occupancy included RELB, NFKB2, REL, and JUN (Figure 2H). These findings indicate that Tax not only recruits IRF4 to specific loci but also globally reprograms its DNA‐binding pattern. Collectively, these results show that Tax reprograms IRF4 chromatin occupancy, potentially driving aberrant transcriptional programs in HTLV‐1–infected cells.

Widespread IRF4 Enrichment Occurs With Tax‐Mediated Epigenetic Remodeling

To examine the nature of co‐enriched regions marked by H3K27ac and occupied by Tax and IRF4, we performed SE analysis. Based on H3K27ac profiles, we used the ROSE algorithm and identified 779 and 23,986 SEs and typical enhancers (TEs), respectively, in C91/PL cells (Figure 3A). The SEs were enriched for genes linked to transcriptional regulation, NF‐κB signaling, IL‐2 production, chromatin remodeling, and TNF signaling (Figure 3B). Notably, IRF4 was enriched in most SEs (96.1%), and Tax was detected in 7.7% of SEs. SEs in which Tax, IRF4, RelA, and RelB colocalized included key genes known to form SEs in ATL, such as BATF3 and TIAM2 (Figure 3C,D). Importantly, IRF4 enrichment in SEs was rarely observed in the absence of NF‐κB components, indicating that IRF4 does not function as an isolated transcriptional regulator at these loci but instead as a constituent of Tax‐dependent transcriptional complexes.

To assess functional significance, we established IRF4 knockdown models in Tax‐positive C91/PL cells and Tax‐silenced TL‐Om1 cells. Two independent shRNAs inhibited cell proliferation (Figure 3E). In IRF4‐deficient C91/PL cells, expression of SE‐associated genes, including RUNX1 [34], IKZF1 [35], IL2RA [36], and RBPJ [37], was suppressed (Figure 3F). To further investigate downstream mechanisms of IRF4‐mediated proliferation, we knocked down BATF3, a direct target of IRF4. shBATF3 suppressed proliferation in both C91/PL and TL‐Om1 cells, providing evidence for a role of IRF4‐driven transcriptional programs in cancer cell growth (Figure S3A). The direct depletion of individual NF‐κB components also impaired cell proliferation; however, functional redundancy among these factors may provide partial compensation for the loss of any of the individual components.

Subsequently, we tested whether ectopic Tax expression reshapes the H3K27ac landscape and alters SE architecture and IRF4 localization. Tax was ectopically expressed in HTLV‐1/IRF4‐negative Jurkat cells, and epigenetic profiles were analyzed via CUT&Tag. Tax expression induced SE formation, from which 465 associated genes were annotated, including loci linked to the SE‐associated genes BATF3 [15] and DUSP10 [20] (Figure 3G). Furthermore, in IRF4‐positive TL‐Om1 cells, Tax induction remodeled the H3K27ac landscape. Notably, H3K27ac levels and IRF4 occupancy increased coordinately throughout the genome and promoted de novo SE formation (Figure 3H,I), which was consistent with a model in which Tax rewires transcriptional programs through IRF4. To assess the clinical relevance of Tax‐induced SEs, we performed a genome‐wide intersection analysis between de novo SEs established upon ectopic Tax expression in TL‐Om1 cells and SEs identified in a primary acute‐type ATL sample (Figure S3B). Tax‐dependent SEs partially overlapped with SEs in the ATL tumor sample, whereas many SEs were unique to ATL cells, suggesting that additional enhancer reprogramming occurs during later stages of leukemogenesis beyond the Tax‐expressing phase.

We further validated the functional importance of SEs. Treatment of Tax‐expressing C91/PL cells with JQ1, a BRD4 inhibitor, resulted in the downregulation of a broad range of SE‐associated genes, including key factors implicated in infected and malignant T‐cells such as BATF3, IL2RA (CD25), and TNFRSF8 (CD30) (Figure S3C). JQ1 also potently suppressed the proliferation of infected cells, thereby confirming the functional relevance of SE formation (Figure S3D). DHMEQ, which causes broad inhibition of the canonical and non‐canonical NF‐κB pathways that are critical for SE establishment, produced comparable effects, confirming the robustness of these findings (Figure S3E).

Transcriptional Regulation Mediated by Tax–IRF4 Cooperation

To explore transcriptional control via the Tax–IRF4 axis, we used the JPX‐9 Tax‐inducible T‐cell line [21]. Prior to assays, we engineered JPX‐9 cells to stably express IRF4 and profiled the Tax‐driven transcriptomic response. RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) was performed under various conditions, including all combinations of IRF4 expression and Tax induction. Compared with the expression of Tax or IRF4 expression, co‐expression increased the number and magnitude of genes with marked expression changes (Figure 4A,B). GO analysis of genes preferentially regulated by the co‐expression of Tax and IRF4 revealed the enrichment of genes linked to cell motility and invasiveness, which are Tax and IRF4 targets (Figure 4C,D). Furthermore, among genes associated with SEs formed upon Tax induction, gene upregulation was further enhanced under Tax–IRF4 co‐expression (Figure 4E).

To elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms, we examined chromatin structure using the assay for transposase‐accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC‐seq) method. Globally, Tax expression promoted chromatin condensation, consistent with ATL cell features [33]. However, co‐expression with IRF4 attenuated this condensation, suggesting that IRF4 modulates the chromatin remodeling activity of Tax (Figure 4F). Regions of improved chromatin accessibility upon Tax expression were enriched for AP‐1 and IRF motifs, with enrichment enhanced further by IRF4 co‐expression (Figure 4G). Collectively, these results indicate that Tax and IRF4 cooperatively regulate chromatin dynamics as components of a shared transcriptional complex, thereby linking Tax‐mediated AP‐1 activation with IRF4 in shaping transcriptional outcomes.

Convergence of IRF4 Mutation and Tax in Reprogramming IRF4 Chromatin Occupancy and Transcriptional Activity

Next, we investigated the functional importance of IRF4 mutations (K59R, L70V, and S114N) reported in ATL [16, 17, 18]. To evaluate these mutations, we established Jurkat cells expressing the wild‐type IRF4 (WT) or mutant IRF4s (K59R, L70V, and S114N) and used ChIP‐seq to analyze IRF4 occupancy. Although each mutant showed distinct profiles compared with the WT, all three mutants shared a set of newly acquired target genes (Figure 5A,B). The L70V and S114N mutants had more genomic regions with increased rather than decreased IRF4 occupancy relative to the WT. To test function, we performed dual‐luciferase reporter assays using AP‐1–IRF composite elements (AICEs), which are known as IRF4‐binding motifs. Consistent with the profiles shown in Figure 5A, L70V and S114N mutants exhibited significantly enhanced AICE‐dependent transcriptional activity, whereas activity in the K59R mutant was not changed (Figure 5C).

ChIP‐seq data showed that a subset of mutant‐specific IRF4 target genes overlapped with genes bound when IRF4 WT and Tax were co‐expressed (Figure 5D). GO analysis further revealed that mutants primarily influenced fundamental cellular processes, whereas IRF4 WT + Tax selectively enriched stress‐ and immune‐related pathways, with shared effects on transcription and DNA repair. All IRF4 mutants bound preferentially to genomic regions where Tax altered IRF4 occupancy (Figure 5E). At the genome‐wide level, correlation analysis of IRF4 peak intensities revealed that among the three mutants, S114N showed the highest similarity to the Tax‐induced IRF4 cistrome, whereas K59R and L70V showed weaker correlations, indicating mutation‐specific differences in the extent of Tax‐like chromatin reprogramming (Figure S4). Furthermore, RNA‐seq analysis demonstrated that IRF4 mutants enhanced the expression of Tax target genes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of genes induced by Tax and IRF4 demonstrated that each IRF4 mutant recapitulated Tax‐dependent transcriptional signatures (Figure 5F). Collectively, these results indicate that IRF4 mutations partially mimic the Tax‐mediated reorganization of IRF4.

Discussion

Several studies have examined interactions between HTLV‐1 Tax and host factors [38]. However, because Tax‐mediated transcriptional regulation primarily depends on direct interactions with host transcriptional regulators, a comprehensive analysis of the Tax interactome is needed to define its functional role in HTLV‐1–infected cells. In this study, we identified multiple host proteins that exhibit physiological interactions with Tax in HTLV‐1–infected T‐cells, including well‐established NF‐κB–related factors, validating our proteomic approach. Compared with previous studies, we performed our analyses in HTLV‐1‐infected C91/PL T‐cells, allowing us to examine Tax interactions under physiologically relevant conditions, including the transient expression characteristic of early infection. Given that this model is an established cell line, further validation will be required to establish the in vivo relevance and biological significance of the observed interactions and to deepen our understanding of their functional roles.

The discovery of IRF4 as a novel Tax interactor is particularly important given its well‐documented role in ATL. Large‐scale genomic analyses have highlighted IRF4 as a gene that is recurrently altered, often through gain‐of‐function mutations or copy number amplification [16, 17]. Thus, IRF4 is considered a lineage‐defining, oncogenic transcription factor in ATL. Our findings add new insight, showing that Tax directly reshapes IRF4 chromatin occupancy and redirects it to promoter‐ and SE‐associated regions. Our data do not support a model in which IRF4 and NF‐κB operate solely through parallel or independent transcriptional pathways. Instead, we propose that IRF4 functions predominantly as an integral component of Tax‐centered transcriptional complexes, together with NF‐κB and AP‐1–associated factors, to reprogram host gene expression.

The Tax–IRF4 interaction identified in this study was observed in infected cells. Although Tax functions as a potent transactivator in early infection, it is frequently silenced in leukemic clones [39, 40]. Even so, transient Tax re‐expression can reinforce IRF4‐dependent transcriptional programs and support leukemic cell survival [41, 42, 43]. These observations indicate that IRF4 serves as a critical node during both early infection and sporadic Tax reactivation. In contrast, the antisense gene HBZ is constitutively expressed and sustains ATL proliferation by modulating AP‐1 and IRF4‐dependent pathways [13, 14]. Together, our findings support a cooperative model in which Tax initiates IRF4‐mediated epigenetic remodeling while HBZ maintains these programs in established leukemic cells.

Strikingly, we demonstrated that Tax alone can induce de novo SE formation and globally remodel IRF4 occupancy. This finding implies that the Tax–IRF4 axis functions as an epigenetic driver, reorganizing enhancer architecture at loci involved in T‐cell transformation. However, the molecular mechanisms by which Tax reshapes the genome‐wide distribution of IRF4 across different cellular model systems, thereby producing context‐dependent effects, remain to be clarified. AP‐1 motif enrichment within Tax/IRF4‐bound open chromatin further supports this notion. Prior ATAC‐seq studies also revealed widespread chromatin accessibility changes in ATL [33]. Our results expand on these observations, indicating that direct Tax–host interactions are upstream determinants of chromatin reconfiguration.

Finally, the convergence of ATL‐associated IRF4 mutations with Tax‐induced chromatin remodeling highlights a shared molecular mechanism underlying leukemogenesis. In our study, IRF4 mutants partially mimicked Tax‐mediated changes in DNA occupancy and transcriptional activation, indicating that viral and host genetic events converge on common transcriptional and epigenomic programs. Given that IRF4 mutations occur in 14% of ATL cases [16, 17], the functional roles of IRF4 are unlikely to be explained solely by genetic lesions; IRF4 WT cooperates with viral factors, such as Tax and HBZ, to sustain oncogenic programs. Collectively, these results provide insights into how HTLV‐1 manipulates host transcriptional networks and highlight IRF4 as a nodal factor exploited by viral oncoproteins and host oncogenic mutations.

In conclusion, our study establishes IRF4 as a novel, functionally important partner of HTLV‐1 Tax, with the interaction between Tax and IRF4 shown to drive SE assembly, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional reprogramming in infected T‐cells. These results not only advance our understanding of HTLV‐1 pathogenesis but also suggest that disruption of the Tax–IRF4 axis or its downstream transcriptional dependencies may represent a promising therapeutic strategy in ATL.
