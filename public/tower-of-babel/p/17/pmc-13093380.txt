Herpes Zoster Vaccine Uptake Among United States Adults With Rheumatic Disease: A Mixed‐Methods Analysis
Authors: Justin Carrico, Catherine McGuiness, Marie Yasuda, Chi‐Chang Chen, Vasudha Gupta, Rita Campos, Nikita Stempniewicz, Justin Gatwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdults with rheumatic diseases are at increased risk for herpes zoster (HZ). Recommendations for the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) were expanded in 2021 to include those aged ≥19 years at increased risk for HZ due to immunodeficiency or immunosuppression because of disease or therapy, but the impact of these expanded recommendations is unclear. This study assessed the impact of the updated recommendations on RZV uptake among adults with rheumatic disease in the US.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis used open‐source claims data to estimate RZV uptake, series completion, and dosing schedule compliance among unvaccinated adults with rheumatic disease from October 2021 to June 2023. Interviews with rheumatologists were conducted and thematically analyzed to characterize RZV vaccination practices related to managing patients with rheumatic disease.ResultsRZV uptake among adults with rheumatic disease and without prior RZV over the 20‐month period following the updated recommendations was gradual (7.0%–10.4% across conditions) and low, and was particularly less likely among adults <50 years of age. The odds of RZV uptake were higher among those of older age, those with a household income greater than or equal to $50,000, and those of a non‐White race or ethnicity. Financial concerns, lack of formal processes for tracking vaccination records and delivering reminders, and insufficient education and awareness were key barriers to RZV vaccination according to rheumatologists.ConclusionRZV uptake among adults with rheumatic disease has gradually increased, and opportunities to improve HZ prevention exist, some of which may be addressed through targeted educational efforts.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals with rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases (RMDs) and inflammatory diseases are at increased risk of developing herpes zoster (HZ; ie, shingles), with incidence up to 2.9 times greater in patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases, including psoriatic arthritis (PsA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) compared with the general population. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Furthermore, one study demonstrated that individuals with rheumatic diseases have a higher risk of HZ than those without rheumatic diseases regardless of age, including those <50 years. 1 , 2 HZ is also associated with a significant economic burden in those with rheumatic diseases. In the first month after onset of HZ, patients with PsA, RA, and SLE have substantially more outpatient, emergency department, and inpatient visits and associated medical costs than their counterparts without HZ. 4 , 6 , 7

The two‐dose recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) was recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)‘s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) in 2017 for immunocompetent adults aged ≥50 years. 8 In October 2021, the ACIP expanded its recommendations for RZV to include individuals aged ≥19 years who are or will be immunodeficient or immunosuppressed as a result of disease or therapy. 8 The updated recommendations also included a change to the dosing schedule. Specifically, although the second RZV dose should typically be administered two to six months after the first dose, for individuals that are immunodeficient or immunosuppressed who would benefit from a shorter vaccination schedule, the second dose may also be given one to two months after the first. 8

The expanded 2021 ACIP recommendations aimed to address the unmet need for protection against HZ in US adults that are immunodeficient or immunosuppressed aged ≥19 years. 8 In addition to clinical data that supported the expanded indication and ACIP recommendations, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 a post‐hoc analysis of phase 3 clinical trials demonstrated RZV's safety and efficacy in adults with various potential immune‐mediated diseases, including those with RA. 14 Subsequent real‐world evidence studies further supported the safety and effectiveness of RZV in certain populations, which included those with RA, PsA, and SLE. 15 , 16

In addition to the updated ACIP recommendations for RZV use in patients that are immunocompromised, multiple medical societies and specialty organizations have published guidelines on the vaccination of patients with rheumatic diseases. For instance, the American College of Rheumatology strongly recommends administration of RZV in patients aged ≥18 years with RMDs who are taking immunosuppressive medications. 17 Among patients with psoriasis or PsA, the National Psoriasis Foundation recommends that RZV be given to patients aged ≥50 years and patients aged <50 years who are receiving specific therapies that may further increase HZ risk. 18 The Arthritis Foundation similarly provides guidance on HZ vaccination in individuals with arthritis. 19 Medical societies and specialty organizations emphasize the importance of vaccine indications for immunocompromised populations, highlighting the critical role these vaccines play in disease prevention and public health.

However, there is a paucity of data on HZ vaccination uptake and completion among adults aged ≥19 years with rheumatic diseases since the updated ACIP recommendations in 2021. One study evaluated the uptake of several vaccines, including RZV, in patients with SLE, but this study was conducted before the expanded ACIP recommendations. 20 It therefore remains unknown whether the updated recommendations resulted in subsequent increases in HZ vaccination in the immunocompromised population.

This study aimed to quantitatively evaluate RZV uptake and series completion among adults with rheumatic diseases since the 2021 ACIP recommendations, including identifying patient characteristics associated with receipt of RZV. Additionally, this study qualitatively analyzed rheumatologists’ insights related to RZV vaccination among patients with rheumatic disease.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Overall study design

This was a mixed‐methods study to evaluate RZV uptake among adults with rheumatic diseases and gather insights on HZ vaccination from rheumatologists. A retrospective cohort analysis estimated RZV uptake, series completion, and dosing schedule compliance; qualitative interviews of rheumatologists described vaccination practices and perceived barriers to RZV vaccination.

Quantitative analyses

Design

A cross‐sectional analysis described RZV uptake using IQVIA's open‐source medical and prescription claims data linked to OneKey health care provider (HCP) profile data and Experian ConsumerView data on social determinants of health (SDOH). Additional information about each of these data sources is available in Supplementary Table 1.

The study period was April 20, 2021 to June 30, 2023 (Figure 1). This timeframe was selected to provide a six‐month period before the expanded ACIP recommendations vote (index date: October 20, 2021) for the evaluation of baseline characteristics and to capture all RZV use in the first 20 months following the expanded recommendations.

Patient selection

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥19 years as of October 20, 2021; had evidence of a rheumatic disease of interest (AS, RA, SLE, or PsA per the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases codes) in the six months before or on October 20, 2021; had at least one claim from a pharmacy with stability throughout the study period; and had at least one claim from a provider with stability throughout the study period. AS, RA, SLE, and PsA were selected as the rheumatic diseases of interest as they, along with their associated therapies, are known risk factors for HZ; the risk of HZ has been well explored in these populations. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 21 , 22 For all rheumatic diseases other than SLE, a claim for an immunocompromising medication during the six‐month baseline period was also required for inclusion; this inclusion criterion was not required for SLE as patients with SLE may experience intrinsic immune dysfunction and be at increased risk of infections independent of medication use. 23 , 24 , 25 Additional details on codes for rheumatic diseases and immunosuppressive medications are found in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. As this study used open‐source claims data that lack enrollment information, stability of the reporting source and claim activity were used as proxies for continuous enrollment. 26 , 27 , 28 Pharmacy stability in prescription claims is a pharmacy‐level monthly indicator of consistent provision of data to IQVIA for that month; provider stability in medical claims signifies consistent reporting of claims from the provider each month.

Patients were excluded from analysis if they had data quality issues (eg, invalid year of birth, sex, region, or payer type) or received the first RZV dose before the index date (October 20, 2021). Patients were observed from the index date until receiving a second RZV dose or until the end of the study period, whichever came first. RZV receipt was assessed using National Drug Codes (NDCs) or Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes.

Outcomes

Outcomes of interest were RZV uptake (receipt of at least one dose) among those without prior RZV, series completion (receipt of two doses of RZV) among those who received a first dose before January 1, 2023 (six months before conclusion of the study period, to ensure each patient had at least six months to receive the second RZV dose), time to RZV series completion, and RZV dosing schedule compliance (second RZV dose received one to six months after first dose among those who received two doses).

Analyses

A cohort analysis was used to describe RZV series completion among patients with at least one RZV dose. Among patients with two RZV doses, time to second dose and dosing schedule compliance were also assessed. RZV series completion, time to second dose, and dosing schedule compliance were reported by rheumatic condition.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics as well as provider characteristics were presented descriptively. RZV uptake among those without prior RZV (by individual rheumatic condition) was calculated as the proportion of patients who received at least one dose of RZV among the total number of eligible patients. Cumulative increased uptake and monthly rates were estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis.

A generalized estimation equation model, which used binomial distributions and logit link functions, evaluated factors associated with RZV uptake among patients with linkable SDOH data from the Experian ConsumerView data set. The model adjusted for patient age, sex, payer type, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, pharmacy density, Area Deprivation Index (ADI) percentile, and SDOH (eg, household income, education, and race or ethnicity). The primary predictors were presence (yes or no) of individual rheumatic disease types.

Qualitative analysis

Target population and recruitment

Potential interview participants were rheumatologists identified through the IQVIA Research Network, which maintains a US‐only, double opt‐in HCP panel. Invitations were sent via email, with a link to consent forms and a questionnaire. HCPs completed the screener questionnaire after electronic consent to participate was provided.

Eligible HCPs included those licensed to practice in the US and specializing in rheumatology, working primarily in an outpatient setting, agreeing to respond to a brief online questionnaire about personal and practice characteristics, and agreeing to be recorded while participating in an interview and responding to questions related to vaccination practices. HCPs indicating acute care as their primary practice setting and those unable to communicate in English were excluded.

Interview

An interview guide was developed based on a targeted literature review to identify processes of and common barriers to vaccination. The guide was reviewed by HCPs and health services researchers who did not participate in the study. Each rheumatologist participated in a semistructured, in‐depth interview led by an experienced moderator that lasted approximately one hour. Interviews were conducted during April 12 to 13 and June 5 to 12, 2024. All interviews were recorded and transcribed, and HCPs were compensated for their participation.

Analysis

Participants’ characteristics were presented descriptively. Transcribed interview data were thematically analyzed using inductive and deductive coding to ensure both known and new concepts were captured, from which representative quotes were selected. Responses were coded and analyzed in a quasi‐quantitative format, with qualitative interpretation of open‐ended responses. Results were summarized to identify key themes and address study objectives.

Ethics statement

As the quantitative portion of this study was conducted using limited de‐identified data sets that did not constitute human participants research as defined by the 45 CFR 46 Category 4 Common Rule, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. The qualitative portion of the study was approved by WCG Institutional Review Board (approval number 20241209).

Data availability statement

The data sets generated during and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available because they contain proprietary information.

RESULTS

RZV uptake and series completion

Patient population

Overall, 548,426 patients with AS (n = 18,278), RA (n = 346,010), PsA (n = 53,240), or SLE (n = 130,898) were included in the study. The mean age was 58.7 years (SD 15.3 years); 27.2% were 19 to 49 years of age, and 72.8% were ≥50 years of age. The majority (79.1%) were female, and 41.2% had a CCI score of ≥2. The most prevalent comorbidities were kidney conditions (chronic kidney disease, renal failure, and/or dialysis; 27.6%), diabetes (type 1 or 2; 23.3%), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (22.5%; Table 1). The most prevalent immunocompromising medications identified by NDCs in the overall population were disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs, including hydroxychloroquine sulfate (31.1%) and methotrexate sodium (28.7%), whereas the most prevalent by procedure code were glucocorticoids (18.5%). Overall RZV uptake among those without prior RZV

In the 20 months following the 2021 updated ACIP recommendations, 8.7%, 10.4%, 9.9%, and 7.0% of patients with AS, RA, PsA, or SLE received at least one dose of RZV, respectively (Figure 2).

Cumulative uptake of first RZV dose following October 20, 2021, by rheumatic disease. RZV, recombinant zoster vaccine.

Among those who received at least one dose of RZV (n = 52,063), 24.6% were also co‐administered (received on the same day as RZV) an influenza, pneumococcal, or tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis vaccine. Most patients (85.2%) with at least one dose of RZV received their first dose at a pharmacy; the mean copay for this subgroup of patients was $62 (SD $66). Of those who received the first dose at a physician's office (14.8%), most received the dose from a family medicine (42.5%) or internal medicine (25.1%) provider; only 7.6% received the first dose of RZV from a rheumatology provider.

Series completion and dosing schedule compliance

RZV series completions at 6 months were 61.9% (AS), 63.0% (RA), 62.2% (PsA), and 61.4% (SLE); 12‐month series completions were 70.6% (AS), 71.1% (RA), 70.9% (PsA), and 68.6% (SLE) (Figure 3). The median time to series completion was similar across conditions, ranging from 4.0 to 4.2 months.

RZV series completion at 6 and 12 months and dosing schedule compliance by rheumatic disease. RZV, recombinant zoster vaccine.

Predictors of uptake among those without prior RZV

Patients with AS had significantly higher odds of uptake than patients with other rheumatic conditions (odds ratio [OR] 1.13 [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.05–1.21]), and patients with SLE had significantly lower odds of uptake (OR 0.86 [CI 0.82–0.89]). Patient characteristics associated with significantly higher odds of RZV uptake included being ≥60 years of age (OR for 60–64 years vs ≥65 years: 1.13 [CI 1.10–1.17]; OR for 19–29, 30–39, and 40–49 years vs ≥65 years: 0.10 [CI 0.08–0.12], 0.11 [CI 0.09–0.12], 0.27 [CI 0.25–0.28]), being female (vs male; OR 1.04 [CI 1.01–1.07]), having commercial insurance (OR for Medicare vs commercial insurance: 0.94 [CI 0.92–0.97]), having a household income greater than or equal to $50,000 (vs <$25,000; all income groups, OR ranges 1.06–1.27), having completed college or graduate school (vs graduating from high school; OR 1.06 [CI 1.04–1.09] and 1.24 [CI 1.19–1.29], respectively), being African American, Hispanic, or Asian/other (vs White; OR 1.11 [CI 1.06–1.16], 1.25 [CI 1.20–1.30], and 1.30 [CI 1.20–1.40], respectively), being from areas of lower social deprivation (ORs vs ADI score of 1–25 [lowest level of social deprivation]: 0.93 [CI 0.90–0.97], 0.88 [CI 0.85–0.91], and 0.76 [CI 0.72–0.80] for ADI scores of 26–50, 51–75, and 76–100 [highest level of social deprivation], respectively), and having a CCI score of 1 (vs 0; OR 1.03 [CI 1.00–1.07]; Figure 4; Supplementary Table 5).

Rheumatologists’ insights

The qualitative analysis included 15 rheumatologists, among whom the mean age was 52 (SD 10.7) years, and a majority were White (67.0%) and male (60.0%). Most (73.0%) had been practicing medicine for ≥20 years and reported seeing 20 patients per day, on average. Most HCPs operated in health care organizations (HCOs) that were nonacademic (73.0%), not for profit (47.0%), and in an urban location (93.0%). In addition, 53.0% of HCPs operated in small (1–13 providers) HCOs. The HCPs’ patients were well‐represented across races and ethnicities and insurance types (as reported by the participants; Supplementary Table 6).

Overall, the HCPs had a good understanding of RZV eligibility and the importance of administering the vaccine before starting immunosuppressive treatments. However, although the participants advocated for HZ vaccination, some viewed their role as primarily advisory, often referring patients to primary care providers or pharmacists for vaccination as RZV was rarely offered in‐clinic (only 2 out of 15 participants reported that their practice site stocked RZV).

Thematic analysis revealed the following three themes specific to barriers to RZV vaccination among adults with rheumatic disease: financial concerns, lack of formal processes for tracking vaccination records and delivering vaccination reminders to patients, and insufficient education and awareness. HCPs noted the complexities of the reimbursement process, emphasizing cost, copays, and insurance issues as primary barriers to vaccination that were particularly relevant to younger patients. The cost of stocking and storing the vaccine was also noted as a barrier among HCPs, particularly for practices in which preventive care was not a focus and, thus, the overhead cost and storage needs associated with stocking RZV would not align with financial goals.

The HCPs also highlighted a lack of formal processes for tracking vaccination records, for exchanging information among specialists, primary providers, and pharmacies, and for delivering vaccination reminders to patients. Only three HCPs were aware of or used state vaccination registries to determine patient vaccination history, and many noted an inability to access records of vaccinations administered by an out‐of‐network primary care physician. Implementation and use of technology such as electronic medical records systems, patient portals, and mobile apps varied widely across practices. HCPs also highlighted that they viewed their primary role as addressing the disease states for which the patients were referred to the specialist. Often, there was inadequate time for vaccine discussions built into appointments, which could be an exacerbated barrier for vaccine‐hesitant patients who may require more time to address concerns and ask questions about vaccination. Additional time, knowledge about patients’ vaccination status, and a specified process for facilitating vaccine discussions were stressed as important factors for improving HZ vaccination uptake.

Finally, the HCPs underscored a need for educational interventions for both patients and HCPs to promote knowledge and awareness regarding RZV and navigation of the health care system. Some HCPs noted that limited technological literacy among older patients presented a barrier to communicating with their patients through digital channels (eg, electronic medical records, patient portals, mobile applications), acknowledging that younger patients were more likely to use technology to access their health records. In addition, insufficient patient education may lead to vaccine hesitancy due to misconceptions about the vaccine. Some HCPs also expressed concerns regarding the risk of disease flares following RZV vaccination, which could result in provider hesitancy to encourage patients to receive the vaccine. DISCUSSION

This study evaluated RZV uptake, series completion, and dosing schedule compliance among RZV‐naive adult patients with rheumatic disease (AS, RA, SLE, and PsA) following the updated ACIP recommendations in 2021 and gathered insight on RZV vaccination practices from US rheumatologists. Over the 20‐month period after the updated recommendations, RZV uptake among adults with rheumatic disease (AS, RA, SLE, PsA) ranged from 7.0% to 10.4% and was particularly less likely among adults ≤50 years of age. Among patients with rheumatic disease who had received the first dose of RZV, the majority (61.4%–63.0%) completed the vaccine series within six months. This finding aligns with results from the general population of adults ≥50 years of age who received an initial dose of RZV, with 70% and 80% completing the series within 6 and 12 months of the initial dose, respectively. 29

There was some variation in increased RZV uptake and series completion by type of rheumatic disease and across certain demographics. Completion was similar among patients with AS, RA, SLE, and PsA but numerically lowest among those with SLE. This study observed increased odds of RZV uptake among Black and Hispanic adults versus those of White adults. This finding should be considered within the context of the varied prevalence of certain diseases by race and ethnicity. Indeed, Black and Hispanic adults have higher rates of lupus, consistent across all age groups and sexes, and Hispanic and non‐White women older than 60 years have higher rates of RA than women of other races and ethnicities. 30 Significant differences in HZ vaccination by race and ethnicity have also previously been observed in the general population, with HZ vaccination uptake as of 2022 estimated at 24.8%, 23.1%, and 40.0% for Black, Hispanic, and White adults ≥50 years of age, respectively. 31 The discrepancy in vaccination patterns by race and ethnicity in both the general population and patients with rheumatic disease warrants future investigation to better understand factors influencing vaccine uptake.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to estimate RZV uptake among unvaccinated adults with rheumatic disease after the ACIP recommendations update in 2021. A previous claims registry study estimated a cumulative RZV uptake rate of 27.3% in adults 50 years and older with SLE in southeast Minnesota and southwest Wisconsin (N = 440) in the two years following RZV introduction (January 2018–February 2020). 20 This reported uptake is higher than estimated in the present study (larger, more recent, and more geographically diverse), although the current study only evaluated increased uptake in the 20‐month period following the updated ACIP recommendations vote date. A previous literature review highlighted variable HZ vaccine uptake (2.0%–37.4%) among US patients with RA and PsA. 32 However, these estimates were collected during a period when RZV was not yet available; the only HZ vaccine available at that time was the one‐dose live zoster vaccine, which is contraindicated in immunocompromised populations and is no longer available in the US. A recent study on RZV uptake among US veterans receiving immunosuppressive medications found that 36.2% of veterans aged ≥50 years received at least one dose of RZV before the expanded ACIP recommendation (January 2018 to February 2022) and 49.8% had received at least one dose of RZV by mid‐2023 (January 2018 to June 2023). The study also found significantly lower odds of vaccination among adults aged <50 years, consistent with the present study's findings. 33

This study also expanded the current understanding of HCPs’ perspectives on RZV uptake by providing qualitative analysis of rheumatologists’ insights. The interviewed rheumatologists understood the eligibility criteria for RZV vaccination well but highlighted several barriers to vaccination. Key challenges identified via thematic analysis included financial concerns, providers’ preference to play an advisory rather than a vaccine administration role, a lack of formal processes for accessing patient vaccination records and communicating vaccination reminders, insufficient education directed at HCPs about the indications and appropriate use of RZV, and inadequate education and awareness among patients regarding the value and importance of HZ prevention. Although some rheumatologists were concerned about the risk of disease flares after RZV vaccination, current real‐world evidence suggests that RZV does not increase risk of disease flares in patients with immune‐mediated diseases. 34 , 35 The rheumatologists suggested the development of informational materials for patients, including pamphlets, posters, and television advertisements, to increase patient awareness and understanding of RZV. This insight may inform future intervention strategies for improving prevention of HZ and other vaccine‐preventable diseases in adults with rheumatic disease.

Previous studies highlighted additional factors impacting vaccination uptake in patients with rheumatic diseases. A 2022 scoping review evaluating barriers to vaccination (primarily influenza and pneumococcal) identified fear of adverse reactions and lack of recommendation by a rheumatologist or primary care physician as prominent obstacles among patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases, concluding that a physician recommendation for a specific vaccination is an important factor in patients’ decision to receive that vaccine. 36 Furthermore, a greater emphasis on the role of rheumatologists specifically, as a strategy for improving vaccination uptake, is supported by recent literature. A 2023 editorial underscored the importance of the role of rheumatology specialists in vaccinating patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic disease, noting that more time spent per patient and use of electronic support systems may improve vaccine uptake. 37 Using reminder systems may also be effective for ensuring timely vaccination initiation and series completion, as a previous systematic literature review showed that vaccine uptake among patients with RA may be improved through vaccination reminders for providers. 38 By encouraging HCPs, including specialists such as rheumatologists, to actively recommend vaccination and implementing strategies such as electronic reminder systems, vaccination uptake may be improved among patients with rheumatic diseases.

This study had several limitations. First, increased uptake was only evaluated in the 20‐month period following the updated ACIP recommendations vote date; consequently, adults ≥50 years of age who received RZV before this date were excluded. Therefore, uptake estimates from this study do not represent cumulative uptake among adults with rheumatic disease since RZV became available. Open‐source data from a network of pharmacies and providers were used for this study, collected primarily for billing and reimbursement purposes. Therefore, information about patients’ uptake and series completion may not have been available, and misclassified or missing data were possible. For example, patients may have been vaccinated at locations outside of the open‐claims providers, resulting in a potential underestimation of series completion.

Multivariable analyses to evaluate predictors of RZV uptake were performed only among patients with linkable SDOH data; these individuals tended to be older than the overall cohort and, therefore, may not be representative of the larger cohort for characteristics such as age, insurance status, and race and ethnicity. Additionally, patients with rheumatic disease were identified using baseline data; as such, it is possible that the patients may not have continued to be immunosuppressed during the study period. This study did not evaluate differences in RZV uptake by medication class, which may impact the likelihood of vaccination due to the heightened risk of HZ among those with certain therapies such as Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors. 39 Indeed, an evaluation of RZV uptake among veterans receiving immunosuppressive medications reported that adults treated with targeted synthetic disease‐modifying agents had higher odds of RZV uptake. 33 This study did not analyze RZV uptake in all rheumatic disease, and findings may not be generalizable to adults with other conditions such as vasculitis, scleroderma, or polymyalgia rheumatica. Finally, limitations associated with the qualitative analysis included the small sample size of rheumatologists, recruitment from an HCP panel, the possibility of recall bias, and difficulty assessing possible factors behind differences in rheumatologists’ insights.

RZV uptake among previously unvaccinated adults with rheumatic disease has been low since the ACIP recommendations update, especially among younger adults. Addressing barriers related to education and awareness, as well as improving workflow processes and information exchange, could support future improvement in HZ prevention among patients with rheumatic disease.
