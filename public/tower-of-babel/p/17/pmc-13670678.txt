Breaking barriers: nano-boosted drug delivery systems in overcoming the defenses of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Yujia Wang, Yuxin Zhang, Xueyan Wang, Wei Zhao, Qiuyi Li, Minglu Zhou, Ying Liu, Xiaoli Yi, Jinhan He, Yanping Li, Jie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains a highly lethal malignancy presenting formidable therapeutic challenges, primarily attributable to the substantial barriers posed by its dense desmoplastic stroma, profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment, and poor drug bioavailability. Nanotechnology offers an effective strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy through improved targeting, optimized pharmacokinetics, enhanced tissue penetration, increased biosafety, and high clinical translational potential. Consequently, this nanotechnology-based platform has been extensively investigated over the past decade for treating PC. This review examines a comprehensive overview of nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems for targeted PC therapy, critically evaluating recent developments and ongoing hurdles along the bench-to-bedside pathway. We discuss applications across chemotherapy, immunotherapy, gene therapy, and other modalities employed as monotherapies or combination regimens.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a malignancy with a profoundly poor prognosis worldwide, posing a significant and growing threat to global public health. Based on Global Cancer Statistics 2022, PC was responsible for nearly 467,000 deaths out of 511,000 newly diagnosed cases in a single year [1]. Its dismal prognosis ranks PC as the third- and fourth-leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States and worldwide. Projections indicate that PC is poised to emerge as the second leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States in the next two to three decades [2]. Histologically, PC encompasses several subtypes, including adenocarcinomas, neuroendocrine tumors, and acinar carcinomas, among which pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) predominates over 90% of cases [3]. The most recent SEER data indicate that PC remains associated with a poor prognosis, with a 5-year relative survival rate of 13.7% for cases diagnosed during 2016–2022.

Multiple factors contribute to the difficulty in accurately diagnosing and treating PC effectively. Early-stage disease often presents with nonspecific symptoms, and the absence of reliable biomarkers hinders early detection. Advanced symptoms are frequently misdiagnosed, and only a minority of patients (15–20%) are considered candidates for surgical resection [4]. Current treatment modalities include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, although the efficacy of both radiotherapy and resection is limited. Thus, chemotherapy, most commonly gemcitabine (GEM) or the combination regimen FOLFIRINOX (oxaliplatin, irinotecan, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and leucovorin), serves as the primary systemic treatment for advanced stages and as neoadjuvant therapy in borderline-resectable cases. Emerging therapies such as immunotherapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), and gene therapy, particularly the messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccination applied alone or in combination, are also under investigation [5–7]. However, the outcomes of both conventional and novel therapies remain unsatisfactory, largely due to PC’s complex tumor microenvironment (TME). This includes a dense desmoplastic stroma, hypovascular architecture, impaired drug perfusion, and acquired drug resistance, all of which compromise therapeutic delivery and efficacy.

Anatomically, the pancreas is a significant organ involved in digestion and metabolism. It contains exocrine tissue responsible for secreting digestive enzymes, and endocrine tissue, largely comprised of the islets of Langerhans, which participate in blood glucose regulation [8]. Pancreatic carcinogenesis typically arises from noninvasive precursor lesions, evident through morphological progression [9]. As the main subtype of PC, the PDAC’s two main precursor pathways are categorized into pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanINs) and intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) [10, 11].

A defining feature of PDAC is a highly complex TME, dominated by dense fibrosis and a profoundly immunosuppressive landscape. The stromal compartment represents a complicated network of diverse cell types, accompanied by non-cellular matrix. Cellular participants, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), stellate cells, and various immune cells, are embedded within an acellular environment rich in hyaluronic acid (HA), collagen, fibronectin, cytokines, and others. This dense fibrous network orchestrates a multifaceted barrier, physically restricting drug perfusion and biochemically programming the local microenvironment to drive immune evasion and therapeutic resistance [12, 13]. Furthermore, the pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), a vital part of pancreatic stroma among TME, are very important in guaranteeing the structural integrity and exocrine function of the pancreas under normal physiological conditions. Once in the pathological conditions, these quiescent PSCs would be activated, therefore inducing excessive collagen and tumor growth factors secretion, and thus tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance (Fig. 1). Moreover, the pathogenesis of PDAC is also driven by the concerted accumulation of mutations in core driver genes, predominantly featuring constitutive KRAS activation alongside the loss of tumor suppressor functions in TP53, SMAD4, and CDKN2A.

Nanomedicines, a combination of nanotechnology and medicines, improve pharmacokinetics, reduce off-target toxicity, and promote tissue penetration [14, 15], thus inducing a remarkable contribution in the treatment efficacy and an enhancement of both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) among PC patients [16, 17]. Several nanomedicines have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating PDAC, including Nab-paclitaxel (Nab-PTX) and MM-398 (liposomal irinotecan), both of which demonstrate improved efficacy over their free drug counterparts [18, 19]. Other nanoplatforms, such as polymeric micelles, exosomes, and lipid-based nanoparticles (NPs), are being actively explored for PC treatment in both preclinical and clinical studies [17, 20].

This article presents a comprehensive review of the pathophysiological barriers, the rational design, and translational application of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (DDSs) for PC. It highlights emerging strategies spanning chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy, and discusses the clinical translation potential of each strategy, along with ongoing clinical challenges and future prospects in nanomedicine for managing this devastating disease.

Pathophysiological barriers and therapeutic strategies in PC

PC is protected by interconnected pathophysiological barriers, including stromal desmoplasia, vascular insufficiency, elevated interstitial pressure, hypoxia, immune exclusion, and KRAS mutations [21, 22]. These barriers not only restrict therapeutic access and efficacy but also define targetable vulnerabilities within the TME [23]. Therefore, the pathophysiological barriers in PC represent both key causes of therapeutic failure and targetable vulnerabilities within the TME, providing a biological rationale for subsequent discussion of drug delivery systems and treatment strategies.

Dense desmoplastic stroma

Desmoplastic stroma is one of the most distinctive pathological features of PDAC and represents a major determinant of therapeutic resistance [24]. Rather than acting as a passive scaffold, the stromal compartment functions as an active barrier system composed of non-cellular matrix components and stromal cell populations. The non-cellular compartment is dominated by extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and polysaccharides, including collagen, HA, fibronectin, laminin, and proteoglycans. Excessive ECM deposition and crosslinking generate a dense and mechanically rigid tumor architecture, which increases tissue stiffness, compresses intratumoral vessels, elevates interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), and restricts both convective and diffusive transport [25]. Different ECM components contribute to this barrier through distinct mechanisms: HA-rich matrices promote water retention and interstitial hypertension, whereas collagen-rich fibrillar networks and lysyl oxidase-mediated crosslinking reinforce matrix rigidity and limit the penetration of macromolecules, therapeutic agents, and immune cells [26]. Thus, the ECM barrier not only reduces therapeutic access but also contributes to heterogeneous drug distribution and immune-cell exclusion within PDAC tissues.

The cellular compartment further reinforces the stromal barrier. Activated CAFs and PSCs continuously remodel the ECM and secrete cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that regulate tumor growth, angiogenesis, inflammation, and immune suppression [27]. CAFs are highly heterogeneous rather than uniformly tumor-promoting [28]. Myofibroblastic CAFs are closely associated with ECM deposition, tissue stiffening, and stromal contraction, whereas inflammatory CAFs mainly contribute to soluble factor-mediated immune suppression and tumor-supportive signaling [29, 30]. Through these cellular-matrix interactions, the desmoplastic stroma forms a dynamic pathological niche that simultaneously limits drug delivery, excludes effector immune cells, and supports therapy-resistant tumor phenotypes.

Therapeutic strategies targeting this barrier have therefore shifted from broad stromal depletion toward controlled ECM remodeling and CAF/PSC reprogramming. Matrix-directed approaches, such as hyaluronan depletion, collagen remodeling, lysyl oxidase-mediated crosslinking inhibition, angiotensin pathway modulation, and stromal stiffness reduction, have been investigated to relieve vascular compression, lower interstitial pressure, and improve tissue penetration [26]. In parallel, CAF/PSC-directed interventions targeting transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), Hedgehog (Hh), FAK, CXCL12/CXCR4, IL-6/JAK/STAT, and vitamin D receptor signaling may suppress fibroblast activation, attenuate immunosuppressive signaling, or restore a more quiescent stromal state [31, 32]. However, it is noteworthy that the stroma in PC is a double-edged sword, and non-selective clearance of the stroma may actually promote tumor invasion, metastasis, or immune evasion. Therefore, a more reasonable approach is to remodel the stroma or reprogram functional CAFs/PSCs for overcoming the desmoplastic barrier [33, 34].

Hypoperfused tumor vasculature

Vascular insufficiency is a major determinant of poor therapeutic access in PC. Functional vessels are sparse and often embedded within a dense fibrotic matrix, while elevated solid stress further compresses intratumoral vessels and compromises vascular patency [35]. These features restrict the movement of systemically administered agents from the circulation into the tumor parenchyma and contribute to the weak and spatially heterogeneous enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in PC. Thus, small-molecule drugs, macromolecules, and drug delivery systems often show insufficient tumor distribution. Although vascular dysfunction may indirectly aggravate hypoxia and therapeutic resistance, its most immediate consequence is the limited accessibility of pancreatic tumor tissues to systemic therapy.

Strategies targeting this barrier have focused on restoring functional perfusion and improving tumor accessibility. Tumor vasculature normalization could improve tumor perfusion by blocking the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-VEGFR2 axis; however, excessive vessel pruning further compromises blood supply [36, 37]. Stromal decompression represents another important approach, as reducing matrix stiffness and solid stress may reopen compressed vessels and restore blood flow. In addition, tumor-penetrating peptides, active targeting ligands, endothelial transport modulation, and transcytosis-enhancing strategies have been explored to promote transvascular transport into poorly perfused regions [38]. Thus, overcoming the hypoperfused vasculature of PC requires not merely increasing systemic drug exposure, but also restoring effective tumor access and improving the spatial distribution of therapeutics within the tumor parenchyma.

Increased interstitial fluid pressure

IFP is a hallmark of tumors with leaky vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage. In PC, fluid retention, compressed vasculature, dense matrix architecture, and lymphatic dysfunction collectively elevate IFP and diminish the pressure gradient required for convective transport, thereby limiting both intratumoral drug dispersion and immune-cell infiltration [39]. As a result, therapeutic agents tend to accumulate in perivascular or peripheral regions rather than spread uniformly throughout the tumor parenchyma [35]. This transport limitation is particularly relevant for antibodies, macromolecules, particulate systems, and immune cells, whose movement is strongly influenced by interstitial pressure, tissue porosity, and diffusion distance. Therefore, increased IFP should be distinguished from the stromal barrier itself: while the latter refers mainly to the structural and cellular composition of the desmoplastic matrix, the former describes a transport-level barrier that restricts tissue penetration and spatial drug distribution.

Strategies to overcome this barrier aim to restore pressure gradients, correct abnormal fluid transport, and improve intratumoral dispersion. Vascular normalization may reduce vessel leakage, improve perfusion, and limit the transmission of intravascular pressure to the interstitial space, thereby contributing to IFP reduction [40]. In parallel, alleviation of solid stress can decompress blood and lymphatic vessels, improve fluid drainage, and help restore more physiological pressure levels [41]. Matrix-directed approaches, such as reducing hyaluronan-associated fluid retention or remodeling dense ECM, may further relieve interstitial hypertension and facilitate deeper therapeutic penetration [42]. Thus, IFP reduction should be integrated with vascular normalization and stromal remodeling to achieve more homogeneous therapeutic distribution within PC tissues.

Hypoxic tumor niche

Hypoxia is a characteristic biochemical barrier in PC, arising largely from poor perfusion, vessel compression, and the dense stromal architecture [43]. Oxygen deprivation stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factors, particularly hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), and activates transcriptional programs involved in angiogenic remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, stemness maintenance, DNA damage repair, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [44, 45]. Hypoxia also directly compromises oxygen-dependent therapeutic modalities, including radiotherapy, PDT, and SDT, in which molecular oxygen is required for the generation of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) [46, 47]. Although hypoxia-associated metabolic adaptation and lactate accumulation may further reinforce immune suppression, the central role of the hypoxic niche lies in protecting tumor cells from oxygen-dependent cytotoxicity and promoting therapy-resistant phenotypes.

Strategies targeting the hypoxic barrier can be organized into hypoxia alleviation, inhibition of hypoxia-driven signaling, and hypoxia exploitation [48]. Hypoxia-alleviating approaches, including oxygen supplementation, oxygen-generating materials, catalase-like systems, perfluorocarbon-based oxygen carriers, and vascular normalization, aim to increase local oxygen availability and sensitize PC to oxygen-dependent therapies such as radiotherapy, PDT, and SDT [49]. Inhibition of HIF-1α stabilization or downstream pathways may attenuate hypoxia-driven adaptation and treatment resistance [50]. Conversely, hypoxia-activated prodrugs and hypoxia-responsive systems exploit oxygen-deficient conditions through reducible motifs such as nitroimidazole, azobenzene, quinone, and N-oxide groups, enabling selective drug activation, release, or carrier disassembly in hypoxic tumor regions [51]. Given the spatial heterogeneity and dynamic regulation of hypoxia in PC, these strategies may need to be integrated with perfusion improvement, stromal remodeling, or local therapeutic activation to achieve durable therapeutic benefit.

Immune-excluded tumor microenvironment

PC is typically characterized by an immune-excluded TME, which largely accounts for its limited responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [52, 53]. This immune barrier arises from defects at multiple stages of the antitumor immune response. First, insufficient antigen presentation and impaired dendritic cell (DC) activation limit effective T-cell priming, resulting in weak endogenous antitumor immunity [54–56]. Second, even when cytotoxic T lymphocytes are generated, they are often scarce, dysfunctional, or retained within stromal regions rather than effectively infiltrating tumor nests [57]. This spatial exclusion is reinforced by dense ECM, activated CAFs, abnormal vasculature, and suppressive chemokine gradients, which collectively restrict immune-cell trafficking and prevent productive tumor-immune cell interactions [58]. Third, the pancreatic TME is enriched in immunosuppressive cell populations, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), tolerogenic DCs, and immunosuppressive CAF subsets. These cells suppress antigen presentation, inhibit T-cell cytotoxicity, promote T-cell exhaustion, and maintain a non-inflamed immune contexture [59]. Thus, the immune barrier in PC reflects a coordinated failure of immune priming, effector-cell infiltration, and local antitumor function.

Therapeutic strategies targeting this barrier should move beyond checkpoint blockade alone and address the sequential defects that prevent effective antitumor immunity in PC. First, immune-priming strategies are needed to increase tumor antigen availability and activate antigen-presenting cells. Cancer vaccines, oncolytic viruses, STING or toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, CD40 agonists, and cytokine-based therapies have been explored to enhance DC maturation, promote T-cell priming, and initiate de novo antitumor immune responses [60–62]. Second, because effector immune cells often fail to physically access tumor nests, immune activation may need to be combined with strategies that improve T-cell trafficking and infiltration, such as stromal remodeling, vascular normalization, or chemokine modulation [63, 64]. Finally, immunosuppressive cellular networks must be restrained to restore effector function within the TME. Targeting TAMs, MDSCs, Tregs, or CAF-derived suppressive signals may relieve local immune inhibition and reduce T-cell dysfunction [65]. Thus, effective immune intervention in PC is likely to require an integrated strategy that sequentially promotes immune priming, enables effector-cell access to tumor nests, and relieves local immunosuppression to restore antitumor function, rather than relying solely on immune checkpoint inhibition.

KRAS mutations

Oncogenic alterations constitute a tumor-cell-intrinsic barrier that sustains PC progression and therapeutic resistance, with KRAS mutations serving as the central molecular driver rather than the sole determinant of resistance [66]. Activating KRAS mutations occur in the vast majority of PDAC and continuously engage RAF-MEK-ERK, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, and Ral-GEF signaling cascades, thereby promoting tumor-cell proliferation, survival, invasion, metabolic adaptation, and resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy [67]. However, KRAS-centered resistance is further shaped by co-occurring molecular alterations. Frequent inactivation of TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 contributes to genomic instability, cell-cycle dysregulation, invasive progression, and apoptotic resistance [68], while defects in DNA damage repair define clinically relevant subsets with potential sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy or poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibition [69].

Rare but actionable molecular phenotypes, such as MSI-H/dMMR or TMB-high disease, may also produce responsiveness to ICB. However, the majority of patients do not exhibit this responsiveness, a condition often induced by the elevated expression of mutant KRAS (mKRAS) [70]. Oncogenic KRAS, expressed in tumor cells, facilitates these effects by remodeling the surrounding stromal cells through the induction of various molecules, including cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Furthermore, oncogenic KRAS collaborates with mutations in other oncogenes or tumor-suppressor genes to promote a pro-inflammatory and/or immunosuppressive stroma [71, 72]. Specifically, mKRAS signaling contributes to immune evasion through multiple mechanisms: it downregulates major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) expression, impairs DC recruitment, and upregulates immune checkpoints such as PD-L1 and CD47. It also promotes MDSC infiltration, excludes cytotoxic T cells, and stimulates the production of a fibroinflammatory stroma. Thus, the oncogenic barrier in PC should be viewed as a multilayered molecular resistance network centered on KRAS, but modified by tumor suppressor loss, DNA repair status, and immune-relevant genomic features.

Therapeutic strategies targeting this barrier are moving from conventional cytotoxic regimens toward biomarker-informed and combination-based approaches. Direct KRAS inhibition has provided important proof of concept, particularly in KRASG12C-mutant tumors, whereas more common PDAC variants such as KRASG12D and KRASG12V remain major targets for allele-specific inhibitors, pan-RAS inhibitors, RNA-based strategies, and combination regimens [73, 74]. Inhibition of downstream MEK/ERK, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, or adaptive survival pathways may suppress KRAS-driven signaling, but feedback activation, pathway redundancy, and intratumoral heterogeneity often limit durable responses [75, 76]. For homologous recombination-deficient tumors, platinum agents and PARP inhibitors provide a molecularly guided therapeutic opportunity, while immunotherapy is currently most relevant to rare MSI-H/dMMR or TMB-high subsets [77]. Given the close crosstalk between oncogenic signaling, stromal remodeling, immune suppression, hypoxic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance, effective targeting of KRAS-centered molecular resistance will likely require rational combinations that integrate tumor-cell-intrinsic pathway inhibition with DNA repair-directed therapy, immune modulation, and microenvironmental remodeling.

Together, stromal desmoplasia, vascular insufficiency, interstitial hypertension, hypoxia, immune exclusion, and KRAS mutations form an interconnected defense network that limits therapeutic access and responsiveness in PC. These barriers also define actionable vulnerabilities within the TME. Therefore, effective treatment strategies should move beyond direct tumor-cell killing and incorporate stromal remodeling, perfusion improvement, pressure relief, hypoxia modulation, immune activation, and molecular resistance targeting. This barrier-based perspective provides the biological foundation for subsequent discussion of DDSs and therapeutic strategies aimed at overcoming the defenses of PC.

Nano drug delivery systems applied in the treatment of PC

As we demonstrated above, the leverage of nanomaterials could magnify tumor treatment efficacy via reducing off-target effects, improving biodistribution and bioavailability of these free drugs. As with other cancers, the DDSs of antitumor agents to pancreatic tumors primarily employ two strategies: passive and active targeting. Passive targeting is primarily mediated by the EPR effect, with nanomedicine size being a key factor modulating this process. Besides, active targeting utilizes ligands that specifically bind to receptors overexpressed on PC cells, such as integrin receptor [78], neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) receptor [79], CD44 receptor [80], insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor [81], transferrin receptors [82], urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor [83], CXCR4 [84], and so on. Although the EPR effect remains an important theoretical foundation for tumor accumulation of nanomedicines, the pronounced heterogeneity of human solid tumors and the highly complex TME including dense ECM and high IFP levels substantially limit the clinical translation of nanomedicines that rely solely on EPR-mediated passive targeting in PC tumor management [85, 86]. Therefore, future nanomedicine design for the treatment of PC should move beyond conventional passive targeting strategies and incorporate active targeting, active transport and retention mechanisms, TME modulation, and biomarker- or imaging-guided patient stratification to improve tumor delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcomes [87].

As we all know, the particle size, surface charge, hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity, and functional groups of NPs collectively influence their stability, tissue distribution, cellular uptake, and toxicity. Through careful selection of materials and fabrication techniques, nanocarriers can be engineered with tailored size, surface charge, and hydrophilicity to optimize their performance.

Particle size and surface charge must be optimized to keep a balance between the stability in the bloodstream and the capacity to penetrate deeply into the tumor ECM and facilitate cellular uptake. In stroma-rich PDAC models, only NPs with a size of approximately 30 nm have demonstrated effective stromal penetration and antitumor efficacy [88]. Then, a charge reversal mechanism may be implemented, wherein a near-neutral or mildly negatively charged “stealth” surface is used for extending blood circulation while positive charge is triggered by the high levels of stimuli within the TME for enhanced cellular uptake [15, 89]. In addition, controlled release should be considered by leveraging stimuli specific to the microenvironment of PC, such as pH, ROS, enzymes, hypoxia, and glutathione (GSH), to mitigate systemic toxicity. Furthermore, strategies aimed at stromal remodeling and functional CAF/PSC reprogramming must be integrated, including the co-delivery of matrix modulators, drugs targeting CAFs, collagen or hyaluronic acid degraders, and the application of ultrasound or other related techniques to remodel the microenvironment [90, 91]. Overall, the design concept of nanomedicines for PC must fulfill five fundamental requirements: retention within the circulation, evasion from immune clearance, deep penetration into the ECM, specific targeting of tumor tissues, and effective release at the site of action. These criteria ensure that therapeutic payloads are successfully delivered to and activated within the target cells by leveraging unique physiological or molecular features of the TME.

Current nanocarriers are broadly categorized into lipid-based NPs, polymeric NPs, inorganic and organic-inorganic hybrid NPs, as well as biomimetic and extravesicular systems in the treatment of PC. Each class possesses distinct properties that suit specific therapeutic applications, and they could serve as carriers to achieve chemotherapy, gene therapy, targeted therapy, PDT, SDT, and immunotherapy (Fig. 2) [92].

Lipid-based NPs

Lipid-based NPs are a versatile and widely investigated class of drug carriers. Prominent examples include liposomes, solid lipid NPs (SLNs), niosomes, and nanoemulsions, and others. Among these, liposomes, first described in the 1960s by Dr. Alec D. Bangham and colleagues, are synthesized through the self-assembly of amphiphilic lipids in an aqueous solution, resulting in nanostructures characterized by the aqueous core enclosed within lipid bilayers. This unique architecture has the capacity for dual encapsulation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic therapeutic agents [93]. Liposomes offer several advantages, including high biocompatibility, biodegradability, and high surface customizability for attaching targeting ligands. These properties allow for reduced systemic toxicity and enhanced therapeutic efficacy of encapsulated drugs. Consequently, liposomes have been extensively employed to deliver chemotherapeutic agents such as irinotecan, demonstrating improved efficacy and safety profiles in the treatment of PDAC [18, 94]. Despite these benefits, several challenges limit their broader clinical translation. These include low drug encapsulation efficiency, a tendency for premature drug leakage, inadequate storage stability, and circulation issues that continue to drive research toward more robust lipid-based formulations [95].

Polymeric NPs

Polymeric NPs are colloidal systems typically ranging from 10 to 1000 nm in size, which consist of polymeric materials [96]. Compared to liposomal carriers, they offer distinct advantages such as tunable biodegradability, superior drug stability, and controlled release profiles. Moreover, their structural and chemical properties, including molecular weight, morphology, and surface functionality, can be precisely engineered to achieve active tumor targeting and combination therapy strategies [97]. Polymeric NPs are commonly categorized into micelles, dendrimers, nanogels, and other architectures.

The polymers used in the fabrication of polymeric NPs include synthetic varieties such as poly(lactic acid) (PLA), poly (DL-glycolide) (PLG), poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and poly(cyanoacrylate) (PCA), and natural polymers like chitosan, dextran, and pectin. Hybrid polymers, which combine synthetic and natural components, are also increasingly employed to integrate multiple functional properties into a single delivery system [98]. Among these polymers, PEG, an hydrophilic polymer approved by the FDA for human use, is widely employed in biomedical applications owing to its high solubility and excellent biocompatibility. Researchers have used PEG as a building block to conjugate hydrophobic drugs to improve their aqueous solubility and blood circulation profiles, thus extending their half-life for better PC treatment efficacy [99]. Moreover, PEG facilitates the straightforward functionalization of NPs with targeting ligands, enabling active targeting without complex synthetic procedures. This technique has been utilized to optimize the delivery and antitumor capacity of GEM and camptothecin in PDAC models [100].

Owing to the low toxicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, natural polysaccharide-based polymers like chitosan have attracted considerable interest. For example, Thakkar et al. developed chitosan-coated SLNs co-loaded with aspirin, curcumin, and free sulforaphane for PDAC treatment. Compared to uncoated particles, the chitosan-modified formulation significantly reduced pancreatic weight, underscoring the role of chitosan in suppressing tumor growth [101]. In another approach, the natural compound celastrol was conjugated with chitosan to form a polysaccharide-drug conjugate. This system not only restrained tumor growth and metastasis in BxPC-3 human PC cells but also reduced celastrol-induced cytotoxicity in hepatic cells, underscoring its potential for strengthened antitumor efficacy and diminished biotoxicity [102].

Inorganic and hybrid NPs

In contrast to polymeric NPs, inorganic NPs offer distinct advantages, such as a high surface-to-volume ratio enabling a high drug-loading capacity, tunable optical and magnetic properties, well-defined morphologies, narrow size distributions, and versatile surface chemistry that facilitates functionalization with fluorescent probes, targeting ligands, therapeutic agents, or nucleic acids, as well as polymeric coatings [103, 104]. However, their inherent insolubility in aqueous environments has limited clinical translation, prompting the development of organic-inorganic hybrid systems for improved solubility and colloidal stability. For example, Ischyropoulou et al. constructed inorganic-organic hybrid NPs (IOH-NPs) using ZrOCl₂ and phosphorylated GEM monophosphate (GMP). These were further coated by glucose (yielding GMP-GLU-IOH-NPs) or functionalized with cetuximab (GMP-CTX-IOH-NPs). Compared to free GEM, the GMP-IOH-NPs exhibited enhanced accumulation in both primary tumors and metastatic lesions, resulting in superior antitumor efficacy, reduced side effects, and overcoming chemoresistance. And they combined cetuximab-functionalized GMP-CTX-IOH-NPs to further enhance the efficacy of PC treatment [100]. In another approach, Pittella et al. engineered calcium phosphate (CaP)-based NPs coated with a copolymer composed of PEG and an endosome-disrupting polyanion, poly(N’’-[N’-[(N-cis-aconityl)-2-aminoethyl]-2-aminoethyl]aspartamide) (PAsp(DET-Aco)). This design addressed endo-lysosomal entrapment of small interfering RNA (siRNA) and improved colloidal dispersion, resulting in enhanced gene silencing efficiency in PC cells [105].

Biomimetic and extravesicular NPs

All the above NPs have been widely explored to treat PC, attributed to their ability to reduce systemic toxicity and enhance tumor-specific accumulation [106]. However, their targeting efficiency faces a dual dependency: first, on passive mechanisms like the EPR effect, which is notoriously heterogeneous across patients; and second, on active targeting, the success of which hinges on both the density of target receptors and the efficacy of the ligand on the nanoparticle surface. To overcome these limitations, biomimetic and extravesicular NPs have emerged as a promising strategy that synergizes passive and active targeting mechanisms.

By leveraging natural biological components, these platforms enhance tumor accumulation and tissue penetration while mitigating immune recognition [107]. Commonly used biological sources for membrane coating include cancer cells [108], stem cells [109], viruses, bacteria [110], leucocytes [111], erythrocytes [112], and platelets [113]. Such membranes can camouflage synthetic NPs to improve their bio-interfacial properties and antitumor effectiveness. For example, Wang et al. constructed a biomimetic nanoplatform using an A-D-A-type conjugated TPC, a small molecule, with dual photodynamic (type I and II) and photothermal functionality, along with bright emission of second near-infrared window (NIR-II) upon excitation. Coating these NPs with PC cell membranes resulted in a system that combined high tumor-suppressive efficacy with excellent safety profiles and additional capabilities for tumor and vascular imaging [114]. In another study, membranes derived from CAFs were used to coat liposomes modified with cell-penetrating peptides and co-loaded with pirfenidone and the BRD4 inhibitor JQ-1. This biomimetic design facilitated homologous targeting, leading to reduced ECM production and suppressed glycolysis, while improving drug delivery capacity and sensitivity in PDAC models [115].

Furthermore, extracellular vesicles (EVs) give rise to a heterogeneous class of membrane-delimited NPs. These structures, collectively known as extravesicular NPs, comprise apoptotic bodies, ectosomes, exosomes, large dense-core vesicles, membrane blebs, microparticles, microvesicles, oncosomes, outer membrane vesicles, and prostasomes [116]. Among these, exosomes isolated from PC cells have been engineered to deliver therapeutic agents such as siRNA or hydrophobic drugs. These natural vesicular systems exhibit enhanced tumor-targeting capability, improved antitumor efficacy, prolonged survival in preclinical models, and reduced systemic toxicity [117].

Strategies for the therapy of PC

Monotherapy

To date, monotherapy remains a cornerstone of current PC treatment. Modalities such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy continue to form the mainstay of PC clinical management [118]. Advances in nanotechnology have substantially enhanced the efficacy of these conventional approaches, enabling improved drug delivery, targeting efficiency, and therapeutic outcomes [119]. The following sections will discuss each of these treatment strategies, examining their mechanisms of action, targeting barriers, clinical applications, and the transformative improvements facilitated by nanotechnology.

Chemotherapy

The majority of PDAC patients are diagnosed at their advanced stages, when surgical resection is rarely feasible. In such cases, systemic chemotherapy represents the standard of care to eradicate or control tumor progression by disrupting the cytoskeleton or DNA synthesis of tumor cells, as opposed to directly breaching the pathological barriers associated with PC. First-line regimens in clinical practice primarily consist of GEM plus Nab-PTX (GEM/Nab-PTX) or FOLFIRINOX [120]. While these therapies can extend median survival time (MST), their efficacy is limited by a lack of tumor specificity, leading to severe off-target toxicities such as diarrhea, vomiting, and hematological complications [121]. Drug encapsulation within tailored nanoplatforms allows for precise delivery, enhancing tumor-specific accumulation while simultaneously minimizing systemic toxicity [122]. The following sections will detail the mechanisms and applications of nanocarrier-based chemotherapeutics, organized according to drug class and formulation design.

Gemcitabine (GEM)

GEM, an inhibitor drug for DNA synthesis, is a long-standing first-line chemotherapeutic agent of PC management [123]. When it comes to the mechanism of GEM, it inhibits the synthesis of DNA. After entering cells, it is phosphorylated to its monophosphate derivative (dFdCMP), which will be continuously turned into its active triphosphate state (dFdCTP), and then incorporated into DNA. After this incorporation, it restrains the DNA elongation and makes DNA lose attraction to DNA repair enzymes, leading to cell apoptosis [124]. It seems that GEM is capable of inducing tumor cell apoptosis, but its effectiveness is restricted by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, which influences the efficiency of GEM [125]. Apart from cytidine deaminase (CDA), an enzyme participating in DNA synthesis that affects metabolic pathways and therapeutic efficacy, treatment-related adverse events, like hypocytosis, rash, diarrhea, and vomiting, also impair the therapeutic efficacy and induced cytotoxicity against normal tissues [126].

The advent of nanotechnology hugely improves this phenomenon. A carbon nano-onion (CNO)-based DDS, designated as HA-PEG-CNOs, was developed to target PC cells. In this system, PEG served as a linker to conjugate HA with a molecular weight of 200 kDa to the surface of CNOs, which were synthesized through the thermal annealing of detonation nanodiamonds. The design enhanced the interaction with CD44 receptors on the cell membrane, thereby improving the efficacy of GEM and mitigating its side effects [127]. In another study, linoleic acid was conjugated with GEM to fabricate a GEM prodrug, which can present hydrophilic and lipophilic characteristics at the same time. With the assistance of DSPE-PEG2k and plectin-1 peptide (PTP), which has an affinity for plectin-1 receptors expressed in PDAC cells, modified DSPE-PEG2k (DSPE-PEG2k-PTP) and GEM prodrug could self-assemble into PDAC-targeting NPs, thus obtaining longer drug plasma half-life, increasing drug stability, and yielding marked inhibition of tumor progression in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [128].

The clinical efficacy of GEM alone in PC treatment is unsatisfactory due to many factors. Nanomaterial-based co-delivery systems that combine GEM with other cytotoxic agents have emerged as a promising approach to enhance the antitumor activity of GEM. Several compounds, including cisplatin, erlotinib, oxaliplatin, black phosphorus, and PTX, have been co-delivered with GEM using nanocarriers such as liposomes, PLGA NPs, and nanoscale coordination polymers. These platforms aim to improve pharmacokinetics, reduce off-target effects, and enhance tumor treatment efficacy in PC [129–131]. For example, a study reported by Yao et al. constructed a nanoprodrug (Nut@FSSG), which contains ferrocene (Fc), a Fe catalyst, GEM, and nutlin-3a, which serves as a sensitizer of tumor cell apoptosis and ferroptosis. The GEM and Fc were conjugated with a disulfide linkage, a GSH-responsive trigger, which was named FSSG. Additionally, the targeted formulation, Nut@FSSG-PTP, was fabricated by encapsulating the prodrug Nut@FSSG into plectin-1-directed, DSPE-PEG-PTP-based phospholipid micelles. This nanodrug responded to the overexpressed GSH and had a disassembly to release the encapsulated Nut, which inhibited the cystine uptake for enhanced ferroptosis induced by Fc and reinforced the apoptosis resulting from GEM. As for the in vivo assay, this nanodrug could improve the tumor accumulation, antitumor efficacy, and biosafety in different types of PDAC mouse models, particularly in the PDX model [132].

Even if the NPs loaded with GEM have shown good performance in killing pancreatic tumor cells, most of the drugs are blocked in the experimental stage due to the complicated TME and high tumor heterogeneity [133]. Future therapeutic strategies should move beyond solely eliminating tumor cells to reprogramming the complex TME, offering a more comprehensive approach to control tumor growth.

Nab-paclitaxel (Nab-PTX)

PTX has played a significant role in anti-tumor paradigms in different types of tumors, including PDAC, which not only binds to microtubules to interrupt the mitosis of cancer cells and trigger apoptosis but also induces chromosomal disaggregation for the prevention of cell mitosis [134]. However, PTX is almost completely insoluble in water but dissolved in Cremophor EL, which brings various side effects in human body, like hypersensitivity and neurotoxicity [135]. The appearance of Nab-PTX, a type of soluble nanoparticle, relieved this struggle as it is soluble in water. However, Nab-PTX had limited drug delivery efficacy in PC. An experiment was launched by using multistage nanovectors to co-deliver GEM and Nab-PTX. This innovation exhibited superior efficacy over free drugs or single-drug NPs, as evidenced by suppressed proliferation, enhanced apoptosis, and greater intra-tumoral drug accumulation, accompanied by a marked increase in caveolin-1 expression [136]. Moreover, a retrospective multi-center study analyzed 1065 metastatic PDAC patients who received different chemotherapeutic regimens. Compared with GEM alone, the nano-based albumin-bound PTX in combination with GEM presented a significant survival benefit [137]. But unavoidably, this therapy also brings some side effects, like sarcopenia [138].

Moreover, the albumin in Nab-PTX may change metabolic pathways of PTX, thus inducing a stronger effect on inhibiting the growth of tumor cells. Recently, researchers have applied nanocarriers to co-deliver GEM and Nab-PTX. Compared with monotherapy of GEM or Nab-PTX, the Nab-PTX + GEM combination strategy performed better in the reduction of tumor volumes and extension of MST in the mouse model, owing to the elevated expression of Nab-PTX transporters like caveolin-1 [139, 140]. Based on these benefits, GEM combined with Nab-PTX could be one of the first-line paradigm for PC treatment.

FOLFIRINOX

The FOLFIRINOX regimen, which combines oxaliplatin, irinotecan, and 5-FU with folinic acid (a vitamin B derivative that enhances 5-FU toxicity), has been applied in PC treatment. Its modified version (mFOLFIRINOX) with drug dose adjustment is increasingly used due to its efficacy in prolonging MST. The following part will introduce these drugs respectively and the application of nanotechnology to this therapy, then discuss their advantages and disadvantages.

5-FU has been used as an effective chemotherapy drug for various types of cancer. 5-FU could exhibit anti-tumor effects by inhibiting the action of thymidylate synthase and interrupting the synthesis of DNA and RNA by the metabolic incorporation. However, the side effects of 5-FU are unacceptable, like cardiotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, myelosuppression, and adverse reactions of the gastrointestinal tract. NPs (MPT-NCs), which were composed of pectin A, pectin C, or modified pectin and tannic acid at different concentrations, were prepared and tested for reliable safety and used to load 5-FU. MPT-NCs showed higher efficacy in decreasing tumor cell viability [141]. Chitosan-based NPs were fabricated using the ionic gelation method and used as the carrier for 5-FU together with quercetin for faster drug release and higher cytotoxicity toward tumor cells in vitro cultured in 2D or 3D milieu [142].

Leucovorin, also known as 5-formyltetrahydrofolic acid, is a special form of reduced folic acid, and it transforms into folic acid, which stays in the central place of the carbon metabolic network. Thus, it can participate in various intracellular metabolisms, especially in cancer cells. Mostly, leucovorin is applied in combination chemotherapy, and it has been proven that this combination of leucovorin, 5-FU, and oxaliplatin, which has been applied in colorectal cancer and PC, has increased the MST and decreased the tumor metastasis of patients [143]. Clinically, based on a single cancer center evaluation using liposomal irinotecan and 5-FU/LV (N + F) for the treatment, it revealed the good efficiency and safety of N + F [144].

Irinotecan hydrochloride (CPT-11) is a semisynthetic derivative chemically derived from camptothecin. Its active form, SN-38, could bind to topoisomerase I and induce irreversible single-strand breakage of single DNA and unwind double DNA strand structures, thus causing tumor cell death. Even though this drug is involved in antitumor activity efficiently, the cytotoxicity brought by the free drug cannot be neglected. Based on a recent meta-analysis, the efficiency and safety of nano-liposomal irinotecan (nal-IRI) were evaluated. Within 3,044 advanced PDAC patients who received nal-IRI therapy, this study discovered that nal-IRI contributed significantly to prolonged progression-free survival and OS, along with a better overall response rate [145]. In addition, the clinical effect of nal-IRI has also been applied in a clinical trial, which elucidated that irinotecan, combined with 5-FU and folinic acid that was encapsulated in nano-liposomes, extended the MST and performed a safe profile at the same time [18]. Apart from liposomes, other nanocarriers were also applied. A novel nanomedicine (LB-MSNP), in which mesoporous silica nanoparticle (MSNP) was encapsulated with double lipid layers, was developed for the delivery of irinotecan to PDAC cells. The LB-MSNP had stronger loading capacity, stability, targeting ability, tumor cell-killing efficiency, and increased safety, compared with liposomal NPs [94].

Oxaliplatin, the platinum metal drug after cisplatin and carboplatin, has been widely used in antitumor therapy, especially for patients who have resistance to cisplatin or carboplatin, as its potency in causing DNA structural damage and inhibition of DNA synthesis. To address the clinical dilemma of oxaliplatin in PC, such as dose-limiting toxic effects and drug resistance, nanotechnology has been utilized. The amorphous iron oxide (AIO), which was prepared by a fast reversed microemulsion method, was modified with PEG to prolong its circulation time and then was used to load oxaliplatin. The resultant NPs (AIOoxp NPs) elevated the content of Pt in pancreatic tumor cells, decreased cytotoxicity induced by oxaliplatin, and suppressed the expression of CA19-9. Moreover, this DDS also loaded Fe2+/Fe3+ ions, which successfully induced ferroptosis and amplified oxaliplatin efficacy [146]. With the use of nanocarriers, oxaliplatin encapsulated by IND (indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase)-MSNP was demonstrated for inducing immunogenic cell death and reversing immunosuppression in PDAC as well [147].

Oxaliplatin-based FOLFIRINOX regimen has been proven to be more efficacious than GEM. However, the high-grade toxicity of FOLFIRINOX in accompany with the high efficacy further impedes its clinical application. The nano-based combination regimens seem as a promising means to realize the balance of therapeutic efficacy and adverse effects. Recently, nanoscale coordination polymer-1 (NCP-1) based NPs were prepared by an initial crosslinking of (dach)Pt(BP), a Pt4+ prodrug, and GEM monophosphate (GMP) with Zn2+ ions using the reverse microemulsion method and further coating with a DSPE-PEG layer. These NPs loaded with oxaliplatin and GEM were developed with the ability to prevent the endocytosis by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), leading to longer blood half-life, and this combination strategy also presented better therapeutic effect and fewer side effects [148]. Another study published by Li et al. reported targeted lipid-polymer hybrid NPs (T-LPNPs) that were composed of a diblock copolymer mPEG-PLA core and a bilayer of PEGylated lipid shell consisting of DSPE-PEG2000 and DSPE-PEG3400-RGD. The hydrophilic drugs 5-FU and oxaliplatin could be loaded into the inner aqueous section by the double emulsion method, while the hydrophobic one, irinotecan, was inserted into the hydrophobic layer. Compared with the free FOLFIRINOX regimen, these novel drug-loaded NPs exhibited enhanced tumor uptake efficacy, higher antitumor efficacy, and minimal side effects. Additionally, these targeted lipid-polymer hybrid NPs possessed extended blood circulation time and greater stability even in the presence of serum [149].

Overall, more and more studies focus on overcoming their adverse effects, and several clinical trials are being launched currently using monotherapy and combination therapy to verify the safety and efficiency. Apparently, the application of nanotechnology to PC chemotherapy has shown huge potential, which makes it possible to take the treatment of PC one step further (Fig. 3A).

PDT

PDT is a therapy that utilizes light of a specific wavelength to activate photosensitizers within tumor tissues, generating ROS. This process results in oxidative damage to cellular components, ultimately inducing cell death (Fig. 3B). The clinical potential of PDT for selective tumor eradication, particularly in deep-seated sites such as the pancreas, has been impeded by suboptimal light penetration and the poor pharmacologic properties of conventional photosensitizers, including low water solubility and inadequate physiological stability [150].

Fortunately, with the improvement of nanotechnology, the efficacy of PDT has been enhanced. For example, Li et al. designed and prepared a creative aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-featured photosensitizers (DCTBT) to treat PC. With the assistance of an EGFR-targeting-peptide-modified amphiphilic DSPE-PEG2000-GE11, DCTBT could form DCTBT-loaded liposomes, and this system exhibited enhanced AIE effect, type-Ⅰ ROS generation, PDT efficiency, and photothermal therapy (PTT) functions in accompany with excellent NIR-II fluorescence imaging ability. In vivo assay reported that this system highly accumulated in the deep-seated pancreatic tumor and imaged it, thus resulting in good antitumor efficiency in PC mouse models [151]. In addition, eutectic gallium indium (EGaIn) spheres were coated with HA and benzoporphyrin derivative (a photosensitizer) to obtain EGaPs for inhibiting tumor growth in murine PC xenograft via PDT [152]. Furthermore, a new nanomedicine composed of polyphosphoester was also fabricated for the encapsulation of hydrophobic photosensitizer, chlorin e6 (Ce6). Then, this nanomedicine (NP-PPE/Ce6) was applied for the PDT of PC. In vitro assay revealed that NP-PPE/Ce6 improved the cellular internalization and phototoxicity in BxPC-3 tumor cells in comparison with free Ce6. Furthermore, enhanced tumor accumulation and superior anti-tumor efficacy were observed in human BxPC-3 xenografts, collectively demonstrating the strong potential of nanoparticle-based photosensitizer delivery for treating PC [153].

Apart from directly killing tumor cells, PDT has also been utilized in modulating the dense tumor ECM. A study used targeted PDT (tPDT) to eliminate CAFs, which express the fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP), to further induce tumor cell death. By conjugating a FAP-targeting monoclonal antibody to the phthalocyanine photosensitizer IRDye700DX, the resulting conjugate showed significant tumor accumulation in a PDAC mouse model, which contributed to a remarkable upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein cleaved caspase-3 [154].

Additionally, PDT is also able to combine with other types of therapy modalities, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, gene therapy, and immunotherapy, along with nanotechnology, which enhances its superiority compared with monotherapy. These combinations will be discussed in later paragraphs.

SDT

SDT, a non-invasive therapy that leverages ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers, induces tumor cell death through ROS generation (Fig. 3C). This approach thus represents a hopeful strategy to overcome the hurdle of PDT for PC treatment, owing to the superior tissue penetration of ultrasound. SDT often utilizes microbubbles or nanobubbles to deliver and concentrate therapeutic agents at target sites, with precise drug release through ultrasound triggering at optimal time points [155]. The integration of nanotechnology further enhances the capability of SDT by strengthening the stability, targeting, and bioavailability of sonosensitizers, thereby augmenting therapeutic outcomes.

Recently, a nanocomposite (TiO2/C) was prepared based on a sonosensitizer made from metal-organic framework (MOF) self-sacrificial templates, TiO2, which was then coated with a carbon shielding. This nanocomposite exhibited excellent stability, thus giving rise to enhanced ROS generation and repeated type I SDT even under hypoxic tumor conditions. It revealed that the nanocomposite demonstrated an attractive biosafety profile and antitumor capability, supporting its promise as a therapeutic agent in the PC model [156]. Moreover, an oxygen-self-produced SDT nanoparticle (IR780@O2-FHMON) was successfully synthesized to reverse the hypoxia situation in the pancreatic TME and to induce PANC-1 tumor cell apoptosis. These fluorocarbon (FC)-chain-functionalized hollow mesoporous organosilica NPs (FHMONs) were synthesized utilizing a solid silica core and a hybrid FC-chain-containing organosilica shell, formed by co-condensing tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) with 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane. Then, this nanoplatform was used to load IR780 and oxygen. It revealed that this nanoplatform indeed broke the hypoxia-specific transportation barriers, supplied sufficient oxygen to hypoxic PANC-1 cells with the help of ultrasound irradiation, and relieved hypoxia, thus conquering the hypoxia-induced resistance to SDT, inducing sufficient ROS, and thus killing PANC-1 cells and restraining the growth of hypoxic PANC-1 tumor [157]. Overall, as an emerging therapy, SDT has already shown its superiority in PC treatments.

Targeted therapy

In contrast to chemotherapy, which directly induces tumor cell death, targeted therapy has gained prominence in PC treatment by focusing on the regulation of cellular metabolism, including glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and protein metabolism [158, 159]. The highly heterogeneous and hypoxic nature of PC, coupled with nutrient scarcity, drives metabolic reprogramming among cancer cells, creating vulnerabilities that are therapeutically exploited. Disrupting metabolic processes is primarily realized by interfering with signal transduction pathways either through blocking protein activity or reducing expression levels of critical molecules such as Hh, KRAS, and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), all of which play crucial roles in tumor metabolism and progression (Fig. 3D). Nanotechnology offers powerful tools to overcome the delivery challenges associated with these targeted agents. The following section discusses recent advances in nano-based delivery systems designed to optimize metabolic targeting in PC.

Hedgehog signaling pathway inhibition

Hh signaling pathway is observed in various types of cancer, including PC, and is closely associated with the development of dense desmoplastic stroma and hypovascularity in PC. It has been proven that the delivery efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs can be increased after inhibiting this pathway.

The canonical route of the Hh signaling pathway is activated by three secretory Hh ligands, including Sonic Hedgehog (Shh), Indian Hedgehog (Ihh), and Desert Hedgehog (Dhh). Differently, the Hh signaling cascades are strongly suppressed when these ligands are absent. However, these three secretory Hh ligands can be activated by receptors and pathway repressor Patched (Ptch1), a transmembrane receptor as well as a tumor-inhibiting gene. The cascades initiate from Shh, a peptide that attaches to Ptch1. This connection between Shh and Ptch1 inhibits the repression of G-protein coupled receptor-like protein, like Smoothened (SMO), which is suppressed by the Ptch1 receptor via stopping its accumulation at the primary cilium when the Hh ligand is absent. After the SMO is released, it will translocate into the cytoplasm and bind to costal-2 (COS2), and then inactivate the Suppressor of fused (Sufu). Sufu can prevent the transportation of Gli to the nucleus, which is divided into 3 types: Gli1, Gli2, and Gli3. They together maintain like a quadruple body. The Gli1 is necessary for the survival and KRAS-mediated cell transformation in human PDAC cells [160].

Based on this signaling pathway, various inhibitors have been applied to suppress tumor growth, such as LDE225 and GDC-449. JK184, a molecule specifically inhibiting Gli in the Hh pathway, was encapsulated into the MPEG-PCL micelles using a one-step dispersion method. Compared with free JK184, the prepared nanomedicine exhibited enhanced cellular uptake, improved cytotoxicity, and proliferation inhibition ability among tumor cells. Moreover, JK184 micelles suppressed tumor growth more strongly in PC tumor models and also exhibited greater capacity in repressing the Gli expression in the Hh signaling pathway [161]. Additionally, anthothecol, a limonoid compound isolated from Khaya anthotheca, disrupts the connection of Gli to DNA, thereby suppressing the transcription of Gli and the expression of its target genes. The compound was successfully encapsulated in PLGA NPs (Antho-NPs), which subsequently inhibited cell proliferation and colony formation, induced cell apoptosis, and markedly attenuated cell motility, migration, and invasion in PC stem cells [162].

The inhibition of Hh signaling pathway could effectively reduce the dense desmoplastic stroma, improve vascular permeability and hypovascularity, and enhance drug delivery efficacy [163]. However, inhibiting the Hh signaling pathway is not merely a straightforward approach to dismantling the stromal barrier to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Recent mechanistic and clinical investigations have revealed that certain stromal components do obstruct the infiltration of drugs and immune cells, while some Hh-dependent stromal elements may also constrain tumor invasion and preserve vascular and immune equilibrium [164]. Consequently, indiscriminate or isolated inhibition of Hh may result in alterations in CAF composition, increased immunosuppression, or the abrogation of the stroma’s tumor-suppressive properties [165]. A more promising strategy involves the short-term, windowed inhibition of Hh/SMO to enhance nano-based drug delivery efficacy, in conjunction with imaging-based stratification, CAF/immune phenotyping, or integrative approaches such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy [165–168].

Luckily, nanotechnology is emerging in loading these inhibitors and other agents for ideal therapeutic effects. However, the clinical translation of these nanodrugs is hindered by a lack of compelling clinical evidence. Future explorations should not only focus on discovering novel inhibiting targets but also on designing a reliable clinical DDS of Hh inhibitor for PC patients [169].

KRAS signaling pathway inhibition

PDAC is commonly initiated by the mutation of KRAS, which has been proven to promote the formation of pancreatic neoplasia. RAS genes encode three different proteins in the human body, including KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS, among which KRAS is highly expressed in the pancreas, and 90% of it is mutated in PDAC patients. The KRAS protein is a small GTPase, which is a transducer protein with a size of almost 21 kDa, and it has two variants, KRAS4A and KRAS4B, in which the KRAS4B plays a dominant role in humans. KRAS protein is transformed between the active and inactive states. When it binds to guanosine diphosphate (GDP), it performs its inactive state; however, it shows its active state and activates downstream signaling pathways once binding to guanosine triphosphate (GTP). In most cases, the KRAS gene is in an inactive state, however, when it is in an active state, it stimulates the initiation of intracellular signaling by PI3K/PTEN/AKT, MEK/ERK pathways, and Ral guanine nucleotide exchange factor, which make great contributions in promoting tumor cell survival, proliferation, and cytokine release and thus tumor growth [170]. An experiment was conducted by the CRISPR/Cas mediated genome editing technique to inhibit KRAS expression and exhibited that the KRAS gene is dispensable for a subtype of PDAC [171].

Based on the unique role KRAS played in PC, using KRAS as a target has been explored extensively. KRAS-targeted inhibiors have been classified into five types according to the mutated positions including KRASG12C (off) inhibitors (Sotorasib, LY3537982, and so on), KRASG12D inhibitors (MRTX1133, ASP3082, iExosomes), pan-RAS (on) inhibitors (RMC-6236), SHP2 inhibitors (RMC-4630, GDC-1971, and so on), and SOS inhibitors (BI1701963), among which SHP2 and SOS inhibitors indirectly inhibit the KRAS pathway [172]. A recent study by Hu et al. put forward a new strategy to sequentially deliver pirfenidone and KRAS-specific inhibitor (AMG-510) and GEM. AMG-510 or GEM was loaded into the liposomes by the ultrasonic precipitation encapsulation method. The resultant AMG-510-loaded NPs (AMG NPs) exhibited excellent stability. According to an antitumor assay, the combination of pirfenidone and AMG-510, as well as GEM, effectively downregulated ECM deposition and improved tumor blood perfusion, thus inducing the greatest antitumor effect when compared with others. And the molecular mechanism assay revealed the AMG NPs could decrease the expression of ERK and AKT, the phosphorylation of ERK (p-ERK), and p-AKT in vivo [173].

Apart from KRAS inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies targeting KRAS have also been used for targeted therapy. A recent study reported that nanoassemblies based on HA have been used to deliver an anti-KRAS monoclonal antibody (anti-KRASG12V mAb). HA was conjugated with palmitoyl acid via the esterification reaction. Then, the obtained palmitoyl hyaluronate was modified with a targeting peptide-tLyp-1 via the thiol-maleimide reaction to get HAC16-tLyP polymers. Then the nanocomplexes (aKRASG12VtLyP1-NAs) were prepared with HAC16-tLyP, Lipoid S100, and anti-KRASG12V mAb, and they exhibited good capacity in inhibiting the cell growth and colony formation of different KRASG12V mutant cell lines. Moreover, aKRASG12VtLyP1-NAs could specifically bind to the KRASG12V oncoprotein and arrest the tumor growth of the PC mouse model [174].

Given the critical role of KRAS in the development of an immunosuppressive TME and a pro-inflammatory stroma, direct inhibition of mutated KRAS or its downstream RAS-MAPK/FAK/ROCK signaling can remodel stromal and immune barriers, increase cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, and improve responses to chemotherapy or immunotherapy [175–177]. However, the stromal barrier of PDAC is highly adaptable and can restore its function through mechanisms such as canonical epithelial state, macropinocytosis, and drug resistance following stromal depletion. Consequently, the inhibition of the KRAS signaling pathway is more appropriately utilized as a means to reprogram the TME and should be integrated with immunotherapy, stromal modulation, and metabolic inhibition strategies. In summary, targeting and suppressing the KRAS pathway holds significant promise for the treatment of PC. However, the effectiveness of KRAS inhibition in PC treatment requires further enhancement due to the intricate nature of the TME.

The regulation of tumor stroma

As we demonstrated above, the unsatisfactory effect of therapy in PC is largely caused by the rich tumor stroma; there is a need to regulate the formation and progression of tumor stroma and develop some methods to overcome this hurdle. There has been elaborate literature summarizing the detailed strategies to overcome stromal obstacles and enhance tumor penetration of drugs [178]. Chen et al. have designed and synthesized nanogels by crosslinking branched polyethyleneimine with dithiobis (succinimidyl propionate), which were then decorated with Mal-PEG-SCM to obtain Nanogel-MPEGs. Then the remaining-Mal on the surface of resultant Nanogel-MPEGS was conjugated with sulfhydryl groups of modified hyaluronidases and GEM was absorbed into the nanogel to obtain GEM@Nanogel-MPEGS-hyaluronidases (GEM@NGH). In vitro and in vivo 3D model verified that tremendous GEM@NGH infiltrated in 3D model via depleting HA obstacles and increasing their endocytosis by tumor cells when compared with the simple mixture of hyaluronidase and GEM. In vivo biodistribution and antitumor assays revealed that NGH achieved much greater accumulation at the tumor sites via the eradication of ECM and superior antitumor efficacy [179].

PARP signaling pathway inhibition

PARP is a significant DNA damage receptor protein, which is associated with the mutation of the BRCA1/2 cancer suppress genes in tumor cells. When the DNA chain is damaged, the PARP enzymes are responsible for the repair. However, with the presence of PARP inhibitors, cancer cells with mutational BRCA cannot repair, resulting in cell death with the accumulation of damaged DNA. But the normal cells still have an unmutated BRCA gene that can repair the DNA chain and keep cells alive. Thus, this strategy provides a chance to selectively kill the cells with BRCA mutation [180].

As for clinical treatment, Olaparib has been authorized as a PARP inhibitor, and using Olaparib alone or in combination gives more perspective on PC treatment. One clinical trial used Olaparib monotherapy to treat PDAC patients whose BRCA mutated mostly, and the result indicated that this drug increased the tumor response rate with good tolerance [181]. With the deeper clinical trials launched, 72% of advanced PC patients treated by Olaparib presented stable disease, and the MST was 9.9 months without any unexpected toxic effects, which reveals the safety and therapeutic effects in pancreatic patients [182].

The application of nanotechnology to deliver PARP inhibitors is a valuable means to maximize its efficacy. For example, ROS-responsive micelles were designed and prepared to deliver Olaparib and AZD0156, an ataxia telangiectasia mutated inhibitor, for dual sensitization of DNA damage repair inhibition induced by irreversible electroporation treatment. This resultant nanoformulation (M-TK-OA) was fabricated by conjugating hydroxyl-terminated PEG to a ROS-sensitive thioketal linker, 2,2′-(propane-2,2-diylbis(sulfanediyl))bis(ethan-1-amine) (PDSE), followed by a ring-opening polymerization of ε-caprolactone (CL) to produce MPEG113-TK-PCL88 and encapsulation of Olaparib and AZD0156. M-TK-OA could effectively regress the growth of PDAC tumor in PC murine models and prolong the survival time, meanwhile resulting in potent CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor response with a long-term immunological memory against tumors [183]. Therefore, when combined with nano-coatings, these PARP inhibitors demonstrated enhanced potential to suppress tumor growth.

Moreover, it is noteworthy that the use of PARP inhibitors could upregulate the expression level of PD-L1 on the surface of PC cells, and this mechanism is at least partially mediated via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. The combination of PARP inhibition and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade can enhance the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and reduce the infiltration of MDSCs, thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive TME in PC [184].

Gene therapy

Gene therapy represents a latent therapeutic strategy for PC, offering potential alternatives for patients with limited treatment options. This approach delivers nucleic acids into tumor cells using viral or non-viral vectors to induce cell death. Gene therapies can be broadly categorized based on the vector system employed: viral or non-viral. TNFerade, an adenovirus-based vector, was the first gene therapy evaluated in patients with advanced PC. While viral vectors generally offer high transfection efficiency, their clinical application is constrained by immunogenicity, potential mutagenic risks, and challenges in large-scale manufacturing. In contrast, non-viral vectors exhibit favorable biocompatibility, stability, tunability for large genetic payloads, and ease of synthesis. The design of effective gene delivery vectors with high therapeutic efficacy and minimal toxicity has thus become a major focus in PC research [185].

An ideal gene vector should be an integration of capacity to deliver large or multiple genetic payloads, high transduction efficiency across both dividing and non-dividing cells, targeting ability toward specific cells, high stability, sustained expression, low immunogenicity and pathogenicity, as well as scalability for clinical-grade production.

There has been substantial focus in recent years on developing non-viral nanocarriers including lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, and hybrid NPs for delivering diverse nucleic acid therapeutics, encompassing siRNA, miRNA, plasmid DNA, and gene-editing systems (Fig. 3E). However, multiple biological barriers impede efficient gene delivery, such as degradation by nucleases, rapid clearance by the RES, the dense desmoplastic stroma, elevated IFP, poor cellular internalization due to the negative charge of nucleic acids, and inefficient endo-lysosomal escape. Achievements in nanotechnology are driving innovative solutions to these challenges, providing more possibilities for targeted gene delivery.

The following sections discuss key modalities in gene-based therapy for PC, including siRNA, microRNA (miRNA), plasmid DNA, and gene-editing approaches, highlighting their mechanisms and translational progress.

miRNA-based therapy

miRNAs are small endogenous non-coding RNAs, which consist of 20 to 22 nucleotides, and they have a strong power in regulating gene expression in the downstream gene translation process. The expression level of miRNA differs between normal and tumor tissues. Thus, theoretically, every type of cancer has its specific miRNA, which is beneficial for clinical diagnosis and targeted treatment [186]. Some of them are identified as cellular regulators for carcinogenesis, which are used for diagnosing the tumor types, and researchers desire to manipulate these regulators to devastate tumor cells.

Recent surveys report that some miRNAs, like miR-34a, are helpful to increase the expression level of TP53 and regulate stem cells of PC to control tumor progression [187]. Conversely, some miRNAs, for instance, miR-10b, miR-100, and miR-155, have been found overexpressed in PC tissues compared with normal pancreatic tissues, which participate in cell invasion, proliferation, and drug resistance of PC. For instance, miR-21 has been reported to be correlated with PC cell invasion by increasing the expression level of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9. Therefore, researchers have applied nanomedicines (TPN-21), including “tandem” peptides that featured a bipartite structure with the cell-penetrating peptide transportan and iRGD, to complex anti-miR-21 via charge and hydrophobic interactions. This nanomedicine specifically interacted with integrins/NRP-1 receptors overexpressed on the PDAC tumor models, thus achieving a great potency in inhibiting miR-21 expression levels, which was comparable to the conventional anti-miR-21 transfection. Furthermore, TPN-21 was capable of restoring the expression level of PTEN and PDCD4, which were suppressors of tumors [188].

Additionally, the miRNAs of exosomes are also considered as a landmark for the treatment and drug resistance in PC [189]. A study transferred miRNA from macrophage exosomes into peritoneal macrophages and delivered them into the PDAC mouse model. It was discovered that the drug resistance of GEM was elevated owing to the mediation and up-regulation of miR-365 level, and that the drug sensitivity was recovered with the addition of miR-365 antagonist [190]. There is no doubt that miRNAs could be used as signatures for the therapeutic target for the treatments and reversal of drug resistance. Unluckily, there is still a lack of enough evidence to verify that the use of the miRNA inhibitors could strongly suppress the drug resistance and tumor progression.

siRNA-based therapy

siRNA, a double-stranded RNA molecule typically 21-23 nucleotides in length with 3’ two-nucleotide overhangs, is not naturally present within organisms but is instead synthesized artificially and could be used to transfect cells. Owing to its capacity for precise RNA interference (RNAi) without associated genotoxic risks, siRNA has emerged as a potent therapy with significant potential in the treatment of PC.

As we demonstrated above, the KRAS oncogene mutation is very critical in the initialization of PC, and it is very difficult to directly inhibit KRAS owing to its limited druggability. So, RNAi could be applied as a useful strategy to regulate the level of KRAS. Yang et al. proposed a safe and new gene delivery carrier (BCPV) using a biodegradable charged polyester as a building block, and it could efficiently deliver siRNA targeting mKRAS in pancreatic tumor MiaPaCa-2 cells, thus bringing about the knockdown of KRAS and resultant regulation of KRAS-associated signaling pathways like cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis [191].

Moreover, a recent study published by Cao et al. synthesized a triblock cationic polymer (PDNM) composed of DMAEMA, NIPAM, and MPC (PDMAEMA-co-PNIPAM-co-PMPC, PDNM) through photoinitiated free radical polymerization. This particle was able to condense the siKRAS efficiently by taking advantage of its positive charges to form nanocomplexes (PDNM/siKRAS) with a uniform particle size. And the resultant PDNM/siKRAS nanocomplex showed robust cellular internalization and high biocompatibility, therefore achieving high transfection efficiency and resultant antitumor effects, marked by promoted apoptosis, S phase cell cycle arrest, and mitotic inhibition, and diminished cell migration. All of these effects were realized by the inhibition of KRAS and caspase 3 activation [192]. Additionally, owing to the critical driving role of KRASG12D mutation in most PC patients, some scientists designed and prepared cRGD-modified bioresponsive chimeric polymersomes, exosomes from normal human foreskin fibroblasts, or t-Lyp1-functionalized lipid NPs to encapsulate siKRASG12D, and these nano delivery systems efficiently condensed siKRASG12D and realized potent gene silencing, good antitumor effects against PC, and improved survival benefits without inducing obvious toxicity [193–195]. Overall, siRNA-based therapy holds great potential in the management of PC. However, unsatisfactory therapeutic efficacy is obtained due to the complex TME.

Gene editing-based therapy

Gene editing is a strategy that starts the breakage of the DNA double-strand at specific gene spots, which is fulfilled by programmable nucleases. The four primary nuclease types, including meganucleases, ZFNs, TALENs, and CRISPR-Cas, have been developed for gene editing, with CRISPR-associated systems being notably the most extensively leveraged because of their flexibility, considerable efficiency, and low cost [196]. The frequently applied nucleases in gene editing include Cas9, Cas12, Cas13, Cascade-Cas3, and Cas7-11. In 2023, an unprecedented submission of the first CRISPR-based cell therapy exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel) has been approved by the FDA to treat various diseases, which was proposed by Vertex and CRISPR Therapeutics [197]. Although it has obtained great progress in a short period, there are still some challenges for its full potential in clinical applications, such as the inability to enter cells, toxicity to cells, side effects owing to off-target gene effects, and easy degradation in biological media as a result of its composition of nucleic acids and proteins.

The advancement of nanotechnology has shown significant potential in preparing tailor-made gene vectors for gene editing due to its unique characteristics, thus making achievements in PC. R8-dGR-modified liposomes were designed and prepared by post-insertion of R8-dGR-PEG to the liposomes formulated by the film hydration method, and these liposomes co-delivered Cas9 and HIF-1α-targeting sgRNA plasmids into PC cells, leveraging the ability of R8-dGR to bind to integrin αvβ3 and neuropilin-1. In the BxPC-3 model, this approach effectively downregulated HIF-1α, validating its notable antitumor and antimetastatic effects [198].

Apart from that, non-autologous exosomes were applied to load the CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid DNA to target the mKRASG12D gene in PC cells, and they could induce targeted gene loss and suppress tumor growth [199]. In a recent study, an MMP-degradable star-PEG-heparin hydrogel was developed for investigating the role of the tumor-associated protease kallikrein-related peptidase 6 (KLK6) by recapitulating the pathophysiological PC microenvironment. By combining this platform with CRISPR/Cas9 screening, KLK6 was identified as a potential target, as it promoted immunosuppression, impaired the recruitment of neutrophils and the cellular responses to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy in PC [200]. Additionally, CRISPR-based systems have also been applied for screening of drug targets. Genetic knockout (KO) screenings carried out by CRISPR/Cas9 have been performed in 4,000 genes in vivo and in vitro to verify potent drug targets, which could amplify the effectiveness of GEM in oncogenic KRAS mutations of PDAC patients. And they found protein arginine methyltransferase gene 5 (PRMT5) is a hopeful druggable candidate, and its downregulation markedly enhances the synergistic susceptibility of PDAC cells to GEM [201].

Over recent years, significant advancements have been made in the field, encompassing the development of various non-viral gene vectors and the initiation of numerous genetic clinical trials aimed at assessing safety and efficacy. These developments have undeniably instilled hope in patients with advanced-stage tumors, particularly those exhibiting drug resistance. Nonetheless, the practical application of these therapies is constrained by limited transfection efficiency. The incorporation of nanosystems offers a promising solution, as they enhance the stability of nucleic acids, preserving their integrity at target sites and facilitating deeper tissue penetration.

Immunotherapy

With the advancement in the comprehension of intricate mechanisms and therapeutic approaches in oncology, immunotherapy has achieved significant progress in recent years. It has demonstrated efficacy across various tumor types and is increasingly regarded as a promising therapeutic strategy for PDAC [202]. Unlike cancer types that exhibit marked antitumor responses to immunotherapy, PC has generally failed in early-stage testing. This poor outcome can be attributed to a highly complex and immunosuppressive TME, marked by inadequate T cell infiltration, dominant myeloid-driven inflammation, and a low tumor mutational burden that restricts immunogenic antigen availability. Therefore, PC has been considered as a “cold” tumor [203]. The major immunotherapies for PC include adoptive T cell therapies, such as chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy and T cell receptor-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapy, stroma-modulating immunotherapy, myeloid-based immunotherapy, agonistic immunotherapy, ICB containing primarily PD-1, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), as well as therapeutic vaccines (Fig. 4).

Among the therapeutic modality, mRNA vaccine is the one applied extensively. This emerging strategy, which leverages immune-adjuvant nanocarriers in combination with mRNA to reprogram the immunosuppressive TME, has progressed to preclinical and clinical trials in an effort to validate its therapeutic efficacy [7, 204]. Most nanoplatforms are fabricated based on various lipids to obtain different lipid NPs (LNPs) for the co-delivery of KRAS mRNA and/or cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), a potent STING pathway agonist, to synergistically enhance antigen-specific immunity and therapeutic outcomes [205]. Given its high prevalence, the KRASG12D mutation is a prime target for such mRNA vaccine therapy. Supporting this, one study demonstrated by Xu et al. has prepared liver-targeting ionizable LNPs to co-deliver mRNA encoding a KRASG12D epitope and cGAMP, for the suppression of the liver metastasis and prolonged survival in PC models. This resultant nanoformulation was fabricated based on cGAMP, mKRAS, and various lipids containing MC3, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 [206]. In parallel, interleukin-12 (IL-12) has also been encapsulated in LNPs to activate myeloid/lymphoid cells and concurrently reprogram the immunosuppressive TME [207].

Apart from the above therapeutic strategies, some other paradigms are also applied in the treatment of PC, such as PTT, metabolic reprogramming therapy, and so on. Admittedly, the applications of monotherapy have treated a tremendous number of PC patients and prolonged their MST. However, because of the heterogeneity of PC, most of them are confronted with drug resistance in their late stages. Moreover, the extensive toxicity from these therapies causes patients a lot, and it is difficult to sustain drug concentrations within the narrow therapeutic window that ensures efficacy and minimizes toxicity. The application of nanomaterials to these therapies brings hope to both clinicians and patients, as they are equipped with the targeting ability, decreased immunogenicity, and stable blood concentration for better effects. However, their efficacy in treating PC is far from the expectations of clinicians. And with the development of the combination therapy, this efficacy could be further enhanced, and the detailed combination regimens and their achievements will be introduced below.

Combination therapy

While monotherapeutic approaches, particularly chemotherapy, continue to be the cornerstone of current clinical management for PC, increasing preclinical and clinical evidence supports the adoption of combination regimens to mitigate toxicity and enhance antitumor efficacy. As treatment strategies advance, it has become evident that integrating two or more therapeutic modalities can extend MST in PC patients, resulting in a growing number of combination therapies being assessed in clinical trials. Common combination paradigms include chemotherapy-based, immunotherapy-based, and multi-modal therapies. Chemotherapy is frequently combined with radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, PDT, PTT, or gene therapy, while immunotherapy is often paired with radiotherapy, PDT, or gene therapy. Moreover, advanced drug delivery platforms have the capability to co-deliver multiple therapeutic agents within a single vector, thereby facilitating direct tumor cell killing, TME remodeling, and immune activation concurrently in the management of PC. This following section will detail various nanotechnology-based combination therapies currently under investigation for PC.

Chemotherapy-based combination therapy

Unsatisfactory therapeutic responses have been obtained in chemotherapy owing to the drug resistance, complex and immunosuppressive TME, abnormal tumor metabolism, and serious adverse effects induced by high-dose applications. These findings underscore the need for treatment strategies that are both more effective and better tolerated. In response, chemotherapy-based combination approaches incorporating immunotherapy, PDT, or PTT have been explored in clinical settings, demonstrating improved therapeutic outcomes [208, 209]. Furthermore, the addition of nanotechnology has substantially enhanced the effectiveness of these regimens, especially in addressing drug resistance, a crucial hurdle in PC management. The following sections detail recent advances in chemotherapy-based combination therapies for PC, evaluating their respective benefits and limitations.

Chemotherapy/radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is a cancer treatment that utilizes high-energy ionizing radiation to kill tumor cells, primarily by destroying their DNA, which aims at eliminating tumor cells mostly but minimizing harm to normal cells and tissues. In terms of PC, several types of research revealed that radiotherapy contributed to locoregional control but cannot distinctly prolong the survival time for the resectable or localized advanced PC [210]. And sometimes PC is radioresistant. Therefore, the only application of radiotherapy is not adequate for PC treatment. Recently, Dong et al. have designed and prepared a novel conjugate complex (AuNP-PTP), which was composed of gold NPs with strong X-ray absorption capacity, and a peptide targeting plectin-1 (PTP) that was overexpressed on the membrane of PDAC cells. Both in vitro and in vivo assays, AuNP-PTP combined with relevant radiotherapy (RF: 6 MV X-ray, 2 Gy) enhanced targeting and induced tumor cell death in PANC-1 models more effectively than unmodified AuNPs. Furthermore, pathological analysis confirmed that a six-week regimen of AuNP-PTP, even with concomitant X-ray exposure, demonstrated no significant treatment-related toxicity in mice. This compelling safety profile supports its potential to improve the therapeutic index of radiotherapy in PDAC [211].

Moreover, radiotherapy has also been used for the reinforcement of chemotherapy. A recent study conjugated methotrexate (MTX) to the surface of LiYF4:Ce3+ NPs (NP-MTX) through a photocleavable linker molecule for addressing the issue of drug resistance of MTX in pancreatic cells. Once the X-ray irradiation occurs, the LiYF4:Ce3+ NPs emit light, thus inducing the release of MTX. The dual radiosensitization effect, achieved through the nanocarrier and the chemotherapeutic drug, enhanced the therapeutic window of MTX by enabling a 40% increase in its effective dose, along with an increased generation of ROS and strengthened S phase arrest in the cell life cycle, thus achieving remarkable in vivo efficacy against pancreatic tumors. More importantly, an ultralow radiation dose of 0.1 Gy indeed led to the photocleavage of MTX for enhanced radio-sensitizing chemotherapy [212]. Overall, this combination could amplify the drug efficacy of chemotherapy, thus effectively inhibiting the pancreatic tumor growth.

Chemotherapy/gene therapy

Given that gene mutations are involved with the development of PC and that some genes hugely contribute to drug resistance, insufficient infiltration of immune cells, and abnormal tumor metabolism, there is an urgent need to perform a combination of chemotherapy and gene therapy. Chemical drugs often target and destroy the DNA in tumor cells for mitigating tumor growth, while their efficacy is impaired by drug resistance. The Ataxia-telangiectasia and Rad3-related (ATR) protein kinase is very critical in protecting cells from the death via detecting the DNA damage, activating or inhibiting the downstream signaling pathways, such as the checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) and activity of CDC25C phosphatase. Therefore, a novel nano-regulator (MOFDOX&siATR) was developed by Wang et al., which was composed of MOF. This system is designed to encapsulate the DNA-damaging agent doxorubicin (DOX) and siRNA targeting ATR (siATR), aiming to inhibit DNA damage repair and thereby enhance antitumor efficacy. The results revealed that MOFDOX&siATR had a diameter of 122–142 nm, and they were uniformly spherical. This formulation induced DNA damage through the action of DOX and interfered with DNA damage repair via siATR, thereby overcoming DOX resistance. Furthermore, it facilitated the generation of neoantigens from Atp8b1, which enhanced the immune response and immunogenicity in the treatment of PC [213].

Additionally, the decreased levels of human equilibrative nucleoside transporter-1 (hENT1), a GEM cell membrane transporter, and elevated levels of ribonucleotide reductase subunit 2 (RRM2) are associated with the low chemosensitivity of GEM [214]. Based on these targets, a cationic liposome nanomedicine based on N-[1-(2,3-dioleoyloxy)propyl]-N,N,N-trimethylammonium methylsulfate (DOTAP) was synthesized via the lipid film method. This synthesis employed DOTAP, dioleoyl-phosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE), cholesterol, and distearoyl-phosphatidylethanolamine-methyl-polyethyleneglycol conjugate-2000 (DSPE-mPEG2000) in a molar ratio of 8:3:8:1. And this resulting liposome was applied to deliver GEM in conjunction with siRNA targeting RRM2 and elevate its chemosensitivity both in vivo and in vitro [215]. Despite killing tumor cells directly, this nano system also advocates in the TME.

Moreover, apart from genes involved with drug resistance, some genes are also involved with abnormal tumor metabolism. For example, decreasing the expression level of ATG5, the autophagy protein 5, could modulate tumor metabolism by reducing autophagy and enhancing macropinocytosis. A study developed a biomimetic liposomal nanomedicine, termed Clip@siATG5, which incorporated siRNA targeting ATG5 (siATG5), aiming to improve the uptake and therapeutic efficacy of Nab-PTX. The nanomedicine was developed by initially complexing siATG5 with the calcium phosphate (CaP) core, followed by liposomal coating and fusion with cancer cell membranes. This nanodrug camouflaged by the tumor cell membrane contributed to evading uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system, to infiltrating into tumor tissues, and to upregulating macropinocytosis. And compared with a double doses of Nab-PTX, the combination (CLip@siATG5 and Nab-PTX) effectively regulated the tumor metabolism, performed better effects in anti-tumor and lowered side effects in the mouse model [216].

Additionally, CD39, the key enzyme in the adenosine metabolic axis, is a metabolic immune checkpoint and crucial in regulating extracellular adenosine and immune escape of pancreatic tumor. Fan et al. designed and prepared a nano system (siCD39@BPDEA-pep-Alb) to deliver GEM and siRNA regulating CD39 (siCD39) to achieve chemotherapy sensitization, metabolic regulation, and immune activation of pancreatic tumors. ROS-responsive charge-reversible polymer B-PDEA, which was synthesized via reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization, was complexed with siCD39 to obtain polyplexes via electrostatic interactions, followed by the conjugation with GEM-loaded albumin through MMP-responsive peptides for improved circulation stability and intratumoral accumulation ability. These NPs achieved good accumulation owing to the endogenous demand from the tumor, and they realized a size reversal from large to small under the action of MMP-2 in the TME to realize deeper tumor penetration and enhanced GEM uptake. Once suffering from the high level of ROS, siCD39-loaded polyplexes changed from negative to positive, thus inducing a separation of polymer from siCD39 and therefore an effective inhibition of the expression of CD39. It implied that this nano-system realized effective antitumor efficacy, longest survival time, and favorable biocompatibility in accompany with the reversal of the immunosuppressive TME and reactivation of antitumor immunity in vitro and in vivo [217].

In addition, some genes are also associated with the poor infiltration of immune cells. Recently, Yuan et al. synthesized PI/JGC/L-A NPs containing an IL-12 plasmid through a stepwise process. These NPs were able to express the immunostimulant IL-12 through gene delivery to activate infiltrated immune cells and enhance immunotherapy. Initially, a cationic inner core (PI) was developed by complexing an IL-12 plasmid with polyethylenimine (PEI). Subsequently, an anionic liposomal shell (JGC/L-A) was constructed, incorporating JQ1, GEM elaidate, and C18 ceramide. This shell was further modified with DSPE-PEG-AAEA to target CAFs. Ultimately, the core and shell were combined through electrostatic interactions to form the complete NPs. Additionally, JGC/L-A stimulated CAFs to produce GEM-loaded exosomes, facilitating deep tumor penetration and promoting IL-12 secretion, which resulted in cytotoxic effects. In this DDS, tested in a PDAC mouse model, JQ1 modulated and normalized CAFs within the TME, reducing ECM deposition and enhancing the infiltration and viability of immune cells [218].

Chemotherapy still plays the dominant role in the treatment of PC; however, its efficacy is often limited by the intricate TME, and some patients are confronted with drug resistance in the late stage. The integration of nanotechnology and gene therapy has markedly enhanced the therapeutic outcomes by disrupting the TME and tumor metabolism, as well as by regulating the dense ECM to facilitate drug penetration. Additionally, these approaches mitigate adverse effects through enhanced targeting capabilities. This synergistic combination holds substantial promise for the treatment of PC, offering significant hope for patients.

Chemotherapy/targeted therapy

Targeted therapy mediated by small-molecule inhibitors, such as metformin, PTX, TGF-β inhibitors like LY364947, and so on, aims at interfering with cell metabolism to reprogram the TME, thus improving the efficacy of cytotoxic chemo-drugs [219, 220]. Furthermore, the combination of chemotherapy and targeted therapy could strengthen tumor cell killing ability, inhibit tumor progression, and prevent tumor metastasis via disrupting the metabolism homeostasis. Several preclinical and clinical experiments are being conducted widely. A recent clinical trial used a WNT inhibitor, Ipafricept, with GEM and Nab-PTX to launch a phase Ib study in 26 previously untreated stage IV PDAC patients. The results demonstrated the majority of patients reflected the decreased tumor burden after this therapy, implying the feasibility and tolerability of this combination therapy [221]. When this combination therapy regimen is integrated with nanotechnology, the antitumor efficacy against PC is obviously improved.

Autophagy is critically involved in the initiation and progression of PDAC, with its upregulated level observed in both tumor and stromal cells. This process contributes to tumor metastasis through the degradation of paxillin and enhances immune evasion by PDAC tumors. A recent study designed a pH-responsive nanodrug (PDGL-GEM), which was composed of GEM and 6-phosphonohexanoic acid (6PA)-modified dendrigraft poly-L-lysine (DGL) (PDGL), for enhancing the tumor penetration of GEM to inhibit the metastasis of PDAC. Subsequently, PDGL-GEM was synthesized with an autophagy inhibitor, chloroquine phosphate (CQ), accompanied by calcium phosphate (CAP) to form PDGL-GEM@CAP/CQ. At pH 6.5, it suffered from the dissolution of CAP and induced the release of CQ, thereby achieving an enhanced penetration of PDGL-GEM. As a result, the combination of the deep penetration of PDGL-GEM, pH-responsive intracellular release of GEM, and the downregulated autophagy level among tumor cells and fibroblasts induced by CQ all effectively inhibited tumor cell growth. Moreover, the PDGL-GEM@CAP/CQ presented better inhibiting ability in tumor growth, in accompany with decreased levels of MMP-2, paxillin, and fibrosis, when compared with PDGL-GEM [222].

Owing to the fatal role of desmoplastic stroma in pancreatic tumor therapy, the combination of chemotherapy with the regulation of ECM has a promising potential in PC therapy by enhancing drug penetration or addressing drug resistance. Zhao et al. have engineered a size-switchable nanosystem, designated as APTEDB-(NS-TAX@Lipo-VAC). This system incorporated paclitaxel (TAX)-encapsulated PEG-PLGA NPs (NS-TAX) alongside the TGF-β1 receptor kinase inhibitor, vactosertib (VAC). These components are co-encapsulated within the hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains of APTEDB-modified liposomes, respectively, which were designed to target the fibronectin extra domain B (EDB). The size of APTEDB-(NS-TAX@Lipo-VAC) was approximately 200 nm, and it could accumulate into the ECM of PC via the specific interactions between the APTEDB peptide and tumor-associated fibronectin in the matrix. Moreover, VAC deactivated PSCs and reduced the production of matrix. After arriving at the ECM, the released NS-TAX with a size of 40 nm approximately was conducive to penetrating into tumor cells through the degraded dense matrix, contributing to the reduction of tumor volume [223]. Similarly, Yu et al. designed and fabricated two sub-20-nm nanomedicines based on methoxy poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(caprolactone) (mPEG-PCL), whose hydrophobic core was loaded with a stearyl-decorated prodrug that was composed of antifibrotic halofuginone (HF) and PTX via the ketal linkage. The HF nanomedicine pretreatment disrupted stromal homeostasis to potentiate PTX delivery and tumor penetration in two PC models. The resulting remodeling of the TME boosted cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and achieved marked tumor regression [224].

Interestingly, in addition reducing the density of the matrix in PDAC progression, a recent study focused on interfering with the specific structure of dense matrix in the PDAC microenvironment. It co-delivered two inhibitors of lysyl oxidase-like 2 (LOXL2) and discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1), to inhibit the secretion of crosslinked collagen and MMP-1 and remodel the structure of matrix. These inhibitors were encapsulated by methoxy poly (ethylene glycol)-poly (DL-lactide-co-glycolide) (mPEG-PLGA) at first, followed by Nab-PTX treatment for enhanced efficiency in treating PDAC [225].

Moreover, interactions between tumor and stromal cells play vital roles in drug resistance. Recently, a superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticle (SPION) was fabricated as a delivery system for curcumin (CUR), a nontoxic, bioactive compound possessing both anti-inflammatory and anticancer activities to form SP-CUR. And nanoparticle-mediated system enabled efficient CUR delivery to pancreatic tumor tissues while concurrently enhancing the uptake and therapeutic efficacy of GEM. Mechanistic results revealed that the SHH pathway was inhibited via targeting an oncogenic CXCR4/CXCL12 signaling axis using CUR, thus effectively inhibiting bidirectional tumor-stromal cell interactions, decreasing the content of tumor stroma and matrix stiffness. Furthermore, SP-CUR potentiated GEM efficacy by modulating key regulatory genes: it upregulated transporters (DCK, hCNT) to enhance GEM uptake while downregulating resistance markers (RRM1/RRM2) to increase sensitivity. In vivo results revealed that SP-CUR could achieve an enhanced accumulation of CUR in the pancreas, implying its great potential in the management of PC [226].

Based on the different pathological mechanisms of PC, targeted therapy and chemotherapy are combined in the management of PC. The utilization of nanotechnology enables this combination regimen to move a huge step forward. But there is still a lack of evidence to strongly prove its efficiency and biosafety. The future work should focus on both exploring novel compounds encapsulated within nanocarriers and launching related biological experiments, even clinical trials, to prove their accessibility.

Chemotherapy/PDT

Even with the assistance of nanotechnology, PDT still presents unsatisfactory effect in treating PC, particularly due to insufficient tissue penetration, accompanied by moderate accumulation of photosensitizers at the tumor areas. The co-delivering of photosensitizers and chemo-drugs precisely seems to be a potential strategy. In the last decades, several experiments have been launched to assess their anti-tumor ability, ranging from increasing drug penetration to decreasing drug resistance.

There was an exploration of porous coordination network (PCN), which was complexed with Fe(III) and then encapsulated with PTX, thus obtaining (PCN-Fe(III)-PTX) NPs. In the tumor sites, the light and low pH released PTX after their disassembly. And Fe(III)-based Fenton-like reactions further converted H2O2 in the tumor site to O2, thus mitigating the anaerobic TME and improving the efficacy of PDT. A good antitumor efficacy was obtained in mice bearing PANC-1 tumors with the assistance of PTX-mediated chemotherapy and Fe(III)-improved PDT [227].

In addition, some supramolecular nanomaterials have been applied as carriers in the therapy, as their unique merits like high stability, high capability, and ingenious modification of superior structure. A recent study published by Chen et al. reported an aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-based supramolecular nanodots (α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp(+)), which were fabricated by two parts via host-guest coassembly: one is a MMP-2-sensitive PEG-peptide (PEG2000-RRRRRRRR (R8)-PLGLAG-EKEKEKEKEKEK (EK6)) that was a zwitterionic stealth peptide, and the other is a GEM modified far-red/near-infrared fluorescent AIE luminogen decorated α-cyclodextrins (α-CD). This study demonstrated that the EK6 peptide facilitated increased tumor accumulation and penetration. Furthermore, upon deshielding of EK6 and subsequent release of the cell-penetrating peptide R8, triggered by elevated levels of MMP-2 and GSH, there was a notable enhancement in cellular endocytosis and GEM release. These processes collectively contributed to tumor cell death, resulting in significant antitumor efficacy in PC mice [228]. Moreover, GEM was also combined with a two-photon-excited photosensitizer, emodin, to be loaded into GSH-responsive biopolymer core and followed by the sheath of guanidine group functionalized HA for enhancing antitumor activity via the synergistic action of chemotherapy and PDT [229]. Generally, this emerging combination nano-therapy is an effective plan in treating PC, as it not only shows its superiority in anti-tumor activities but also in decreasing side effects. But there is still inadequate evidence to illustrate its safety and efficiency in clinical trials, and the penetration depth of light is not enough.

Chemotherapy/PTT

PTT is a promising strategy that employs photothermal agents to convert NIR light into hyperthermia, inducing localized thermal ablation of tumor cells. This modality offers notable advantages, including non-invasiveness, high spatiotemporal selectivity, controllability, and minimal systemic toxicity. PTT could not only upregulate the inflammatory factors among tumor tissues but also break the ECM, thereby enhancing the drug penetration ability and therapeutic efficiency of chemotherapy [230]. Tthe agents, which could absorb the NIR light and transform into heat, often include some inorganic materials and dyes.

As for the inorganic materials, there have been numerous literatures reported. Recently, a biomimetic nanodrug was prepared based on gold (Au) nanocages, which co-loaded the antitumor agent, GEM, and a nitrogen oxide (NO) donor, L-arginine, and was further coated by PDAC cell membranes. The high level of GSH and ROS in tumor tissues specifically and effectively broke the disulfide bonds to release GEM and activate the L-Arg to produce NO, respectively, thereby promoting vasodilation and vascular normalization, and enhancing the tumoral penetration of GEM. The chemotherapy mediated by the released GEM acted in combination with PTT resulted from Au nanocages, showing a good potential in both orthotopic and PDX models [231]. Apart from GEM, DOX was also applied with Au to regulate the ECM for depressing tumor growth, demonstrating an outstanding performance in degrading the tumor matrix and promoting the depth of drug diffusion [232]. Besides this, polydopamine (PDA) has also been used for PTT. Liu et al. prepared PDA-based nanospheres (Apt-PDA@PTX/AV3) to co-deliver PTX, a chemotherapy drug, and peptidomimetic AV3, an integrin α5 (ITGA5) antagonist, which can specifically bind to ITGA5 receptor on human PSCs (hPSCs) to successfully silence them. It was further modified by the CD71-specific targeting aptamers for the combination of chemotherapy and PTT against PC. When Apt-PDA@PTX/AV3 was endocytosed by the tumor cells, the released AV3 silenced hPSCs and decreased the desmoplastic stroma, which further improved the tumor accumulation and tumor cell-killing efficacy of PTX. Meanwhile, the PDA core further resulted in hyperthermia with the assistance of an NIR laser, thus achieving an effective chemotherapy/PTT combination towards the tumor growth of PC [233].

Additionally, some photothermal agents are also applied. A recent study developed and characterized a nanoplatform (GEM-POSS-SQ) based on polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane (POSS), where the GEM and photothermal agent squaraine (SQ) were conjugated into the POSS cores by click chemistry, for the tumor therapy of PC. In vitro assays showed that GEM-POSS-SQ NPs were effectively endocytosed by chemoresistant PANC-1 cells, thereby leading to the disruption of cell membranes mediated by the NIR laser and nuclear damage resulting from GEM. And the released SQ was further aggregated to improve the efficacy of photothermal performance, which acted together with chemotherapy to boost the effects of cytotoxicity and cell apoptosis. Moreover, a considerable level of tumor cell apoptosis and a lower level of proliferation were found in the mice treated with GEM-POSS-SQ NPs under the irradiation of NIR, without obvious adverse effects [234]. Moreover, a croconaine dye, 2,3,3-trimethyl-5-(thiophen-2-yl)-3H-indole, together with GEM, was encapsulated into the synthesized lipid particles for inhibiting PANC-1 cells and inducing the death of PSCs both in vitro and in vivo [235].

All in all, PTT has shown its advantages in treating tumors, especially in PC with dense stroma and complicated TME [236, 237]. Through combining with chemotherapy, it achieves an enhanced synergistic therapeutic effect on PC. The mainstream research spots on designing nano-systems with the clinical application potential to co-load chemo-drugs and photothermal agents to acquire better therapeutic effects and fewer side effects [238].

Immunotherapy-based combination therapy

As we said above, the efficacy of immunotherapy only has been proven to be poor in PC due to immune escape mechanisms, immunosuppressive TME, and complex crosstalk between the TME and the immune system. There have been several challenges in the immunotherapy treatment of PC including low tumor antigenicity (low mutational burden and MHC-I expression), immunosuppressive TME (myeloid cell infiltration, low activation marker expression of T cells), and mKRAS-driven immunosuppression (Fig. 5A). Current research endeavors are focused on improving the efficacy of immunotherapy through combination strategies, such as the combination of immunotherapy with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, PDT, PTT, or gene therapy, to effectively induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) or enhance the antitumor immunity to reinforce the antitumor responses of immunotherapy (Fig. 5B). These strategies aim to: (1) enhance the function of endogenous T cells, (2) prime or adoptively transfer tumor-specific T cell immunity, and (3) remodel the TME from an immunologically “cold” to a “hot” state.

Immunotherapy/radiotherapy

Fundamentally, immune priming depends on antigen presentation; without it, tumor cell killing fails to initiate. A crucial advantage of radiotherapy is to potentiate antigen presentation. The integration of stereotactic body radiation therapy with immunotherapy holds considerable promise in the evolving treatment landscape of PC, primarily through its dual mechanism of inducing ICD and mitigating immunosuppression to sensitize tumors to immunotherapy [239]. Besides, radiotherapy-mediated tumor cell death contributes to the exposure of tumor neoantigens and immunostimulatory molecules, which are recognized as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by the immune system to initiate a cytotoxic response. And in the clinic setting, radiotherapy combined with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) has demonstrated distinguished therapeutic effects [240]. Another phase II combination trial includes radiation, PD-1 inhibitor, nivolumab, and CTLA-4 inhibitor, ipilimumab, to successfully stimulate the immune system and restrain the tumor mutation in PDAC patients, proving the great potential of this combination paradigm [241]. With the addition of DDS, as a therapeutic enhancer, it provides more opportunities for regulating immune responses.

A study synthesized a 10B-containing copolymer composed of cRGD-terminated poly (ethylene glycol) segment (cRGD-PEG), the middle segment with thiol pendant (PME), and the 10B-containing segment with benzoborazole pendant (PBOB). NPs were synthesized through the self-assembly of this copolymer with PD-L1 siRNA. This process involved the formation of phenylboronic ester bonds between the cis-diols of the ribose groups at both ends of the siRNA and the benzoborazole groups in PBOB. Subsequently, disulfide cross-linking was facilitated in the presence of oxygen to yield 10B/siPD-L1. And in the cytosol of tumor cells, the 10B/siPD-L1 could disassemble into 10B polymer and PD-L1 siRNA. And the 10B precisely killed tumor cells under the neutron irradiation while preserving T cells within the tumor simultaneously. This combination demonstrated significant therapeutic effects both in vitro and in vivo, enhancing DNA damage through boron neutron capture therapy while stimulating a robust immune response [242].

Collectively, recent preclinical and clinical studies of radiotherapy/immunotherapy combinations aim to amplify antitumor immune responses. Nevertheless, treatment-related toxicities have prompted trial discontinuation in some patients. Subsequent investigations must balance immune potentiation against acceptable safety profiles.

Immunotherapy/chemotherapy

Apart from radiotherapy, the combination of immunotherapy with chemotherapy seems to be a feasible strategy. According to research involving almost 60,000 PDAC patients, it compared the OS of patients receiving chemotherapy only and patients receiving both chemotherapy and immunotherapy, and found that the combination was related to a remarkable improvement in survival, which was almost 1.6 months longer [243]. Furthermore, some clinical trials are also launched to verify its effectiveness and biosafety as well. A randomized phase II study used cabiralizumab with nivolumab, combining or without chemotherapy (GEM or 5-FU) in PDAC patients to evaluate the combination therapy efficiency. Compared with immunotherapy alone, the combination with chemotherapy resulted in prolonged PFS [244]. The following paragraphs will introduce the current advances of this combination strategy using nanocarriers.

Recently, a study reported that the combination of atezolizumab (an inhibitor for PD-L1), adjuvant autogenous cevumeran (uridine mRNA lipid NPs of novel individual antigen), and mFOLFIRINOX could elevate the contents of T cells in PDAC, greatly exhibiting the potential of chemo/immunotherapy [245]. Liu et al. reported IRI encapsulated by silicasome nanocarrier with anti-PD-1 could induce the innate and adaptive immune responses more efficiently in comparison with anti-PD-1 plus free IRI. This nanoparticle demonstrated its superiority in increasing the drug stability, prolonging the blood half-life, and enhancing the targeting ability [246]. Similarly, lipid bilayer (LB)-coated mesoporous silica NPs were prepared to co-deliver TLR agonists (3M-052) and irinotecan for chemo-immunotherapy of PDAC. With the addition of 3M-052, which takes responsibility for mediating the activation of DCs in tumor tissues, the ICD induced by irinotecan was apparently strengthened, along with increasing CD8+ T cell infiltration and shrunken tumor tissues [247].

Apart from the inorganic NPs, some polymeric NPs are also extensively used in the management of PC. Recently, a programmed nanoparticle (DAS@P/H/pp) has been fabricated based on three motifs: the core DAS@P, which integrated DOX, SDF-1α, and Au: Ag2Te quantum dots within the biodegradable block copolymer PEI-(PLGA)2 (P); hyaluronidase, which induced HA degradation in the TME; a pH-responsive shell [p(OEGMA)-p(AMA)-DMMA (pp)] to protect the drugs and facilitate the response of DAS@P/H/pp to TME. The nanoparticle could respond to the extracellular pH to de-shield the shell, resulting in the release of hyaluronidase, degradation of the ECM, and thus the enhanced delivery efficiency of SDF-1α and DOX. Moreover, an enhancement of treatment efficacy was observed in mouse models of PC with the assistance of enhanced recruitment level of NK92 cells mediated by SDF-1α and chemotherapy induced by DOX [248]. Similarly, some researchers also applied liposomes [249] or polyphenol-based nanocarriers [250] with high biocompatibility, to co-load cytotoxic drugs and NLG919 (an inhibitor of indolamine 2,3 dioxygenase 1), which could restrain the production of immunosuppressive kynurenine, increase CD8+ T cell infiltration and decrease Tregs recruitment, thus significantly enhancing the treatment efficacy.

Additionally, bacteria have also been used for tumor therapy. Zhang et al. used probiotic Escherichia coli 1917 (EcN), known for its preferential colonization of the hypoxic core of the tumor, as a delivery vehicle for hyaluronidase-hybridized albumin NPs (HD@HH). These HD@HH co-delivered hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), an intelligent autophagy inhibitor, and DOX to form a formulation termed “bulldozer” (HD@HH/EcN). Experimental assays revealed that HD@HH/EcN abundantly accumulated in the tumor hypoxic core, and the release of DOX was mediated by the abundant GSH in the TME, significantly inducing ICD effect. And then the released HCQ further amplified ICD effect by inhibiting tumor autophagy and remodeling the immunosuppressive TME, thus achieving a good antitumor efficacy [251].

Moreover, to address the inadequate infiltration of T and natural killer (NK) cells induced by the dense ECM, some scientists applied a size reversal strategy. In this approach, the nanomedicine remains a large size during circulation in the bloodstream, while it transitions into smaller NPs within the ECM upon encountering the highly expressed stimuli. This strategy could effectively improve the therapeutic effect of immunotherapy. For example, Tong et al. recently fabricated a novel nanomedicine named SPN@Pro-Gem. This nanomedicine was composed of a pH-sensitive polymer that was synthesized by conjugating N, N-dipentylethyl moieties (DnPEA) and monomethoxylpoly (ethylene glycol) (mPEG) onto PAMAM dendrimer, followed by an encapsulation of a GEM prodrug (Pro-Gem). This nanomedicine, with a size of 120 nm, became ultra-small-sized particles of 8 nm once exposed to the acidic pH at the tumor sites for the enhanced deep penetration of GEM. Moreover, SPN@Pro-Gem effectively remodeled TME via downregulating TAMs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, while simultaneously increasing the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells. This dual transformation of size enabled the combination therapy of ICB with SPN@Pro-Gem, thereby achieving an enhanced therapeutic efficacy on PDAC [252]. Similarly, a GEM-conjugated polymer (PGEM) was used for loading a CCR2 antagonist to address the tumor immune resistance resulted from the increased levels of CCL2 and CCL7, obtaining an improved overall therapeutic effect [253].

Overall, the integration of chemotherapy with immunotherapy has the potential to address the immune barriers and significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy through several mechanisms. Firstly, it facilitates the breakdown of dense ECM, thereby improving the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells. Secondly, it reduces the levels of immunosuppressive cells, such as Tregs, MDSCs, and TAMs, thereby alleviating immunosuppression. Thirdly, it inhibits the production of immunosuppressive metabolites, which enhances the infiltration and function of immune cells while downregulating immunosuppressive cells. Finally, chemotherapy-induced ICD in tumor cells results in the release of tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, promoting the maturation of DCs and enhancing cross-presentation, which in turn activates CD8⁺ T cells. Based on these surprising discoveries, they indeed bring hope to PC patients. However, more clinical trials should be launched to verify the validity and biosafety as soon as possible.

Immunotherapy/gene therapy

Gene therapy is often combined with immunotherapy to realize the treatment of pancreatic tumors. Interleukin 10 (IL-10) contributes to forming immunosuppressive TME by inhibiting DCs from producing IL-12, and CXCL12 is a crucial factor in inhibiting T-cell tumor infiltration. A study published by Shen et al. designed and engineered lipid-protamine-DNA NPs to co-deliver two types of trap genes (IL-10 trap and CXCL12 trap) for regulating immunosuppressive TME and thus enhanced antitumor immunity. This co-therapy reversed key immunosuppressive mechanisms by reducing M2 macrophages, MDSCs, and PD-L1+ cells, and simultaneously reinvigorated the immune landscape by activating tolerogenic DCs, NK cells, and macrophages. Synergistically, it re-educated the tumor architecture via collagen reduction, ultimately translating into suppressed tumor growth and extended survival in mice bearing pancreatic and triple-negative breast cancers [254].

Furthermore, Zhu et al. designed and prepared mRNA lipid-polymer hybrid NPs (LPNPs), which were fabricated by PLGA, DSPE-PEG, C14-modified PAMAM, and LNPs composed of SM102, cholesterol, DSPC, DMG-PEG2000 to deliver anti-PD-1 mRNA and neoantigen-mRNA vaccines (NVs mRNA), respectively. Through the subcutaneous injection of NVs mRNA@LNPs, an enhanced level of CD8+ T cells and interferon-γ was found in orthotopic PDAC mice. After treatment of anti-PD-1 mRNA@LPNPs, the vaccine therapy effectiveness of NVs mRNA@LNPs was further reinforced via the in-situ production of anti-PD-1 antibody (aPD-1) among tumor areas, which improved their binding affinity toward T cells, thus inducing superior therapeutic outcomes in orthotopic and metastatic PDAC models [255].

Immunotherapy/PDT

The combination of immunotherapy and PDT has been investigated clinically for years, and recent studies reveal the capacity of PDT to trigger ICD and remodel the TME, thereby promoting infiltration of immune cells and augmenting systemic immune responses simultaneously [256]. PDT-induced tumor destruction could release DAMPs that lead to the activation of complement cascades, resulting in the initiation of acute inflammation and recruitment of innate immune cells for efficient debris clearance. The addition of nanovesicles could co-modulate the pharmacokinetics and regulate the TME to improve the therapeutic effects via combining immunotherapy and PDT [257].

A cetuximab-conjugated photo-immunonanoconjugate nano-lipid (Cet-PINs) was engineered for PC therapy. Evaluated across in vitro and in vivo models, this therapy presented highly spatiotemporal precision, tumor-targeting capability, and potent antitumor efficacy, while maintaining favorable in vivo safety [258]. Another nanoplatform named MDK Nb-PCP NPs integrated three key components: Midkine nanobodies (MDK Nbs), a semiconducting polymer (poly[2,6-(4,4-bis(2-ethylhexyl)-4H-cyclopenta-[2,1-b:3,4-b] dithiophene-alt-4,7-bis(thiophen-2-yl) benzo-2,1,3-thiadiazole] (PCP), and DSPE-PEG2000-MAL. After light irradiation, the MDK Nb-PCP NPs generated considerable ROS at tumor sites, induced robust apoptosis and ICD, therefore facilitating T cell infiltration and DC maturation for TME regulation and thus an enhanced antitumor efficacy [259].

Apart from polymeric NPs, some biological vesicles are also applied. For instance, Xu et al. designed nitric oxide-producing multiple functional NPs named M1@PAP. These NPs were constructed using poly-L-arginine (Arg9)-modified pyropheophorbide-a (PPA) as a type I photosensitizer and anti-PD-L1 siRNA (siPD-L1), which were subsequently encapsulated within M1 macrophage membrane vesicles. M1@PAP effectively killed the tumor cells under the laser irradiation. Additionally, M1@PAP not only provided nitric oxide to improve the penetration ability of the nano-photosensitizer via the inhibition of CAF activation and decomposition of the ECM, but also actively targeted and remodeled immunosuppressed M2 macrophages, thus re-educating the immunosuppressive microenvironment, reinforcing the immunotherapy mediated by anti-PD-L1 siRNA and achieving good efficacy against hypoxic pancreatic tumor via the combination of PDT and immunotherapy [260]. In addition to cell membranes, some exosomes were also utilized. Jang et al. utilized tumor-derived reassembled exosomes to load a photodynamic agent, chlorin e6, for PDT. This system generated ROS to directly kill tumor cells and concurrently stimulated immune cells to release cytokines, thereby enabling a combined PDT and immunotherapy against PC [261].

With regard to the combination between PTT and immunotherapy, Wang et al. utilized a TME-responsive AIO nanosystem to co-deliver indocyanine green (ICG), a photothermal agent, and imiquimod, a TLR-7 agonist. The amplified tumor-specific immune responses resulted from the released imiquimod and tumor antigens from PTT mediated by ICG under the laser irradiation, thereby leading to systemic antitumor immunity towards tumor metastases and prolonging the survival time [262].

Conclusively, the immunotherapy-based combination therapy with the assistance of nanomaterials has benefited from shrinking the tumor volume, enhancing immune responses, and regulating TME to achieve better therapeutic effects and mitigate side effects [263, 264]. However, the efficacy is far from expectations due to many factors. Therefore, future exploration in the immunotherapy-based combination therapy should consider the penetration efficacy of nanomedicines and the interactions between TME and nanomedicines, which aims to better serve PC patients clinically.

Multi-modal therapy

Nanomedicines with multi-modal therapy ability have also been engineered to facilitate the therapeutic efficacy of PC. Zhang et al. synthesized a black phosphorus nanoparticle capsulated with mPEG-NH₂ and low-molecular-weight HA (HA@BP NPs) for photo-immunotherapy. It demonstrated that HA@BP NPs exhibited exceptional biocompatibility, stability, and therapeutic efficacy after laser irradiation. Furthermore, this system reprogramed TAMs from immunosuppressive M2 macrophages to antitumor M1 phenotype, thus bringing about the combination therapy involved PDT, PTT, and immunotherapy and enhancing the immunotherapeutic outcomes [265]. Additionally, innovative research applied a triple therapy-integrated nanosystem (GPS) to address the resistance of primary ICB and the recurrence after surgery, which was composed of polylactide (PLA) monoconjugated GEM prodrug (PLA-GEM), poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(D,L-lactic acid) (PEG5k-PLA8k), and anti-PD-L1 antibody (αPD-L1) functionalized PEG-PLA (αPD-L1-PEG5k-PLA8k), followed by an encapsulation of a STING agonist, diABZI-3. The application of GEM had enhanced the expression level of PD-L1, which induced the tumor-specific accumulation and ICB-based synergistic antitumor efficacy via the binding of αPD-L1 and PD-L1. Furthermore, by reprogramming the TME and potently converting “cold” tumors to “hot”, the addition of a STING agonist potently inhibited the growth of established tumors and reduced metastatic burden in multiple mouse models, including orthotopic PC. This approach establishes a novel multi-modal therapy strategy [266].

Moreover, given the positive role of PSCs in immunological “cold” TME of PC, Wang et al. designed and prepared an amphipathic triblock polymer. This polymer was composed of mPEG, polylysine (PLLys), and poly(asparagyl(diisopropylethylenediamine) cophenylalanine) (P(Asp(DIP)-co-Phe)) and it was used to co-deliver vitamin D receptor (VDR) agonist, calcipotriol, and siRNA targeting CXCL12, a chemokine mostly secreted from activated PSCs and responsible for immunosuppressive TME. This system effectively inactivated the PSCs and downregulated CXCL12 for reduced ECM and immunosuppressive T cells, therefore realizing the remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME and consistently facilitating the immunotherapy efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody in PDAC [267].

Taken together, the studies summarized in Table 1 reveal that the therapeutic performance of nanomedicines in PC is strongly influenced by their ability to address pathophysiological barriers. Emerging nanoplatforms are increasingly engineered not only to deliver therapeutic agents, but also to remodel the fibrotic stroma, restore intratumoral transport, modulate hypoxia and immune suppression, and target tumor-cell-intrinsic molecular resistance. By integrating barrier modulation with chemotherapy, gene therapy, phototherapy, or immunotherapy, these systems have improved intratumoral drug distribution and antitumor efficacy in preclinical models. These advances provide a strong preclinical rationale for the further clinical translation of nanomedicines in PC, as discussed in the following section.

Clinical translation of nanomedicines in PC

Approval and clinical trials of nanomedicines for PC

Ultimately, nanomedicine has profoundly expanded the therapeutic landscape for PC. By enabling the multifaceted modulation of the complex TME, systemic immunity, cell-cycle dynamics, and the characteristic desmoplastic stroma, nanotherapeutics offer distinct functional advantages. Furthermore, advancing insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of PC have expedited the identification of novel therapeutic vulnerabilities and driven the rational design of targeted nanodrugs. Crucially, compared with conventional monotherapies, these sophisticated delivery platforms have demonstrated substantial efficacy in overcoming established spatiotemporal barriers, thereby providing a robust framework for precision oncology [17, 276].

Currently, the landscape of marketed nanotherapeutics for PC is spearheaded by two prominent formulations: albumin-bound PTX (nab-PTX or Abraxane®) and PEGylated liposomal irinotecan (Onivyde®) [277–280]. In a phase 3 clinical trial involving 861 patients with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma, the combination of nab-PTX and GEM demonstrated significant improvements in OS, PFS, and response rate [19]. Consequently, this combination regimen received FDA approval in 2013, for the first-line treatment of metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Similarly, in a phase 3 trial with 417 patients with PDAC, PEGylated liposomal irinotecan, in combination with fluorouracil and folinic acid, was shown to extend median OS with a manageable safety profile compared to treatment with fluorouracil and folinic acid alone [18]. This regimen was approved by the FDA in 2015 for use in patients with metastatic PC previously treated with GEM. The success of these formulations underscores the clinical feasibility and safety of albumin- and liposome-based nano delivery systems.

Furthermore, some other nanocarriers are also included in clinical trials. For example, iExoKrasG12D, engineered from exosomes/extracellular vesicles derived from mesenchymal stromal cells, was designed for the delivery of siRNA targeting KrasG12D mutations. The iEXPLORE Phase I trial, a single-arm dose-escalation study, enrolled patients with advanced or metastatic PDAC harboring the KRASG12D mutation (NCT03608631). The study demonstrated that iExoKrasG12D was generally well tolerated, as no dose-limiting toxicities were observed and the maximum tolerated dose was not reached even at the highest dose tested. Additionally, several patients achieved stable disease. Correlative analyses further showed a reduction in KRASG12D DNA, inhibition of p-ERK signaling, and increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in tumor sites [281]. Moreover, nanocells derived from bacteria (EDV) were also utilized as a type of delivery system. In PDAC patients, EDV was used for the encapsulation of α-galactosyl ceramide or an EGFR-targeted, super-cytotoxic drug, PNU-159,682. This trial was a first-in-man, single-arm, open-label, phase I/IIa, dose-escalation clinical trial, and enrolled 25 patients. Results demonstrated that the median OS was 4.4 months, while the median OS of 17 patients completing one treatment cycle was 6.9 months. This paradigm enabled advanced PDAC with enhanced OS, minimal adverse effects and weight stabilization [282]. Several clinical trials (Table 2) and preclinical efforts are also launched to evaluate the efficacy and biosafety of these nanomedicines.

In addition to clinically evaluated nanomedicines, granted patents further illustrate barrier-oriented nano delivery strategies for PC (Table 3). For example, CN107362370B describes positively charged gold nanoclusters that electrostatically adsorb nerve growth factor (NGF) siRNA to target NGF-associated tumor-nerve interactions in PC. Compared with free siRNA, the resulting nanocomplex showed improved serum stability, increased uptake in PANC-1 cells, enhanced suppression of NGF mRNA and protein expression, prolonged circulation, and higher accumulation in pancreatic tumor tissue. In an orthotopic PANC-1-Luc model, the gold nanocluster-NGF siRNA complex significantly inhibited primary tumor growth and mesenteric metastasis without obvious body-weight changes. Beyond target-specific gene silencing, multifunctional nanoplatforms have also been designed to simultaneously address intratumoral penetration and microenvironmental barriers in PDAC. CN114272247B describes a hypoxia/ROS-responsive polymeric prodrug nanoparticle designed for PDAC, comprising a polyethylene glycol-azo-polylysine-polyornithine copolymer bearing iRGD-associated NRP-1-targeting and carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX)-targeting functions, together with hypoxia-responsive larotrectinib and ROS-responsive camptothecin conjugates. The approximately 90-nm spherical nanoparticles were designed to undergo hypoxia-triggered dePEGylation and size reduction, thereby facilitating further penetration into hypoxic tumor regions. Larotrectinib release is intended to interfere with tumor innervation, whereas ROS-responsive camptothecin release provides cytotoxic activity. Thus, these patents represent an experimentally evaluated NGF-silencing strategy and a multifunctional responsive design aimed at neural and hypoxic barriers in PDAC.

Overall, the evidence from currently approved nanomedicines and ongoing clinical trials indicates that the successful translation of nanotherapeutics for PC is not predominantly dependent on the development of increasingly complex material functions. Instead, the key to clinical translation of nano delivery systems hinges on the integration of established clinical treatment paradigms, the effective surmounting of well-characterized delivery barriers, and the assurance of reproducible manufacturing processes, controllable quality, acceptable safety profiles, and demonstrable therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, an evaluation of the clinical translational priority of various nanotherapeutic paradigms is justified.

Evaluation of the translational potential of different strategies

In light of the prevailing standard-of-care treatment regimens for PC and the translational potential of nanotechnology-based delivery systems, the most promising nanomedicines for clinical translation are sophisticated nano delivery systems integrated with one or more drugs in the standardized chemotherapy protocols for monotherapy or combination therapy. Notably, Nab-PTX and nal-IRI have received approval for the treatment of PC, thereby substantiating the clinical feasibility and safety of nanomedicines. Given the demonstrated efficacy of the FOLFIRINOX regimen in treating PC, the utilization of advanced DDSs to enhance the pharmacokinetic profiles of relevant drugs, mitigate systemic toxicity, or augment antitumor efficacy represents the most feasible direction. Consequently, the development of nano delivery systems for monotherapy, co-delivery or sequential release systems for combination therapy holds a great promise in the clinical translation, particularly when utilizing widely employed clinical drugs such as GEM, PTX, 5-FU, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin.

In contrast, nanomedicines based on PARP and KRAS signaling pathway inhibition, immunotherapy, and stromal remodeling strategies show a potential but mostly remain in the early stages. And their applications are strictly dependent on molecular subtyping, immune status, and combination therapy regimens. Among these, PARP pathway inhibition represents a precision therapy approach with a relatively well-defined patient population [295]. Currently, olaparib has been approved by the FDA for maintenance therapy in metastatic PC. However, PARP-targeted therapy is not suitable for all PC patients; its clinical application must rely on patient screening using biomarkers such as BRCA1/2 and PALB2. Therefore, the translational value of PARP inhibitor-related nano delivery systems is primarily reflected in enhanced drug bioavailability, reduced toxicity, and synergistic effects in combination therapy for patients with specific molecular subtypes.

Inhibition of the KRAS signaling pathway represents a promising direction in PC treatment. Small-molecule inhibitors to KRAS/RAS are rapidly approaching clinical translation. In April 2026, Revolution Medicines announced interim results from its randomized, controlled phase III clinical trial (RASolute 302), showing that pan-RAS inhibitor, Daraxonrasib, significantly prolonged PFS and OS compared to the chemotherapy group. In the overall study population, the median OS was 13.2 months in the Daraxonrasib group and 6.7 months in the chemotherapy group. Furthermore, Daraxonrasib demonstrated good tolerability and manageable safety, and no new safety signals were observed [296]. Regarding gene silencing strategies for KRAS, a 2026 phase II clinical study targeting KRASG12D/G12V showed that extended-release siRNA implants could improve OS and objective response rates (ORR) in patients with locally advanced PC [297]. However, systemically delivering relevant nucleic acids to deep tissues in PC, avoiding RES clearance, and achieving effective and sustained gene silencing effect remain a key challenge for clinical translation in this area. Therefore, although KRAS-related nano delivery systems hold great promise, they are still in the exploratory stages.

Regarding immunotherapy, PC typically presents as an immunologically “cold” tumor, and ICB alone have limited efficacy in unscreened patient populations. Therefore, reshaping the immunosuppressive TME via nanomedicines and combining this with ICB represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Among these, mRNA or neoantigen vaccines are of particular interest. Results from a 2026 phase I clinical trial of a personalized mRNA PC vaccine showed that 8 out of 16 patients developed tumor-specific T-cell responses, with 7 of these patients still alive 4–6 years post-surgery. However, given the small sample size of this study, larger-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate its efficacy and safety. From the perspective of nano delivery systems, LNP-based mRNA vaccines, nanoadjuvant vaccines, and KRAS-mutation based vaccines demonstrate high translational potential; however, they are more suitable for patients involving the adjuvant therapy, or immunotherapy-based combination paradigms.

Stromal modulation is another important area for PC treatment. Using PEGylated recombinant human PH20 hyaluronidase (PEGPH20) to degrade HA was highly anticipated, but the HALO-301 phase III trial was unsuccessful [26]. Subsequent studies have also suggested that PEGPH20, as a radiosensitizer, may be suitable for specific patient subgroups with a high level of hyaluronan within the tumor [298]. Therefore, stroma-targeted therapy should shift from “stromal clearance” to “stroma remodeling”, vascular normalization, and biomarker-based patient screen.

In contrast, nanomedicines based on Hedgehog signaling pathway inhibition, PDT, SDT, and gene therapy, complex multifunctional nanomedicines, and some types of inorganic nanomaterials may face significant barriers to clinical translation in the short term and are unlikely to become mainstream treatment options for PC therapy. Although modulation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway is mechanistically linked to the PC stroma and tumor progression, results from previous clinical trials have been disappointing [299]. Therefore, Hedgehog signaling pathway is not suitable as a core translational target for current nanomedicines. PDT is limited by the penetration depth of light, making its applications in widely metastatic pancreatic cancer challenging. Although SDT possesses tissue penetration ability, current clinical evidence remains insufficient, and its value as a standalone treatment requires further validation. Gene therapy is highly attractive from a mechanistic perspective, but its clinical translation is constrained by several factors, including nucleic acid delivery barriers, immunogenicity, off-target effects, long-term safety, good manufacturing practice, and batch-to-batch consistency, making translation particularly challenging.

Overall, future clinical translation of nanomedicines for PC should prioritize strategies with a well-established clinical therapeutic foundation, mature delivery systems, feasible quality control, and the potential to tangibly improve efficacy or reduce toxicity.

Challenges and future prospectives

PC exhibits multiple pathophysiological barriers, including dense stroma, hypoperfused vasculature, increased IFP, hypoxic tumor niche, immune-excluded TME, and KRAS mutations, which collectively hinder drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 6A). To overcome these barriers, various nano-DDSs have been developed to deliver chemotherapeutics, nucleic acids, and inhibitors (Fig. 6B). By improving tumor penetration and remodeling the stromal and immune microenvironment, these systems may convert a “cold” TME into a more treatment-responsive “hot” state (Fig. 6C). However, biological, technological, and regulatory challenges continue to limit their clinical translation in PC.

Challenges

Despite the clinical milestones achieved by albumin-bound and liposomal formulations, the broad clinical translation of nanomedicine in PC remains constrained by three major challenges: TME- and EPR effect-related heterogeneity, uncertainties in distribution and biosafety, and manufacturing and regulatory barriers (Fig. 6D). A primary obstacle is the distinctively dense, desmoplastic stroma and the resultant elevation of IFP, which together create a robust physical blockade that severely restricts nanoparticle extravasation and deep parenchymal penetration [300]. Furthermore, the historical reliance on the EPR effect, often pronounced in homogeneous murine models, frequently fails to mirror the marked inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity observed in human PDAC, leading to suboptimal intratumoral drug accumulation. Collectively, the stromal barrier, hypovascular architecture, and heterogeneous EPR effect substantially compromise the therapeutic efficacy in PC.

Besides, the inherent nanotoxicity of nanomedicine restricts safe translation. Due to miniature dimensions and high surface areas, NPs readily cross biological barriers and accumulate in vivo. Their toxicity is primarily driven by intracellular ROS overproduction and toxic metal ion release, causing severe oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [301]. Plus, NPs adsorb circulating macromolecules to form a protein corona, which impairs normal biomolecular functions and biodistribution of drugs, thus triggering apoptosis, systemic inflammation, and cross-organ toxicity [302, 303]. Crucially, these toxicological effects are highly dependent on particle size, shape, and surface charge. Therefore, thoroughly elucidating nanotoxicity mechanisms and executing optimal targeted surface modifications are vital for the future safe clinical application of nanotherapeutics.

Moreover, the widespread clinical translation of nanomedicine in PC remains impeded by scalable manufacturing and regulatory compliance. In contrast to traditional small-molecule formulations, nanomedicines demonstrate biological behavior that is influenced by several factors such as particle size, surface properties, and structural morphology. Consequently, the assessment of their critical quality attributes is imperative. These critical features encompass particle size, polydispersity index, morphology, surface charge, surface chemistry, drug loading efficiency, encapsulation efficiency, in vitro drug release behavior, colloidal stability, sterility, endotoxin levels, residual solvents, and batch-to-batch consistency. For nanomedicines targeting PC, regulatory evaluation must consider whether the nanotechnology platform improves the in vivo distribution, tumor penetration, systemic exposure, or toxicity profiles in comparison to the free drug [304, 305].

Regulatory authorities such as the FDA, European Medicines Agency (EMA), and the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA)/Center for Drug Evaluation (CDE) advocate for a case-by-case evaluation based on the specific characteristics and risks associated with the formulations. In 2021, the NMPA and CDE in China promulgated technical guidelines pertaining to the quality control, nonclinical pharmacokinetic assessment, and nonclinical safety evaluation of nanomedicines [306]. Furthermore, for gene vaccines or carriers derived from biological sources, such as extracellular vesicles and exosome-like carriers, the safety of the source materials, immunogenicity, potency, in vivo distribution, ethical considerations, and long-term safety of these nanomedicines may be necessitated [307].

Future prospectives

To bridge the gap between preclinical promise and clinical reality, a paradigm shift is required across multiple developmental stages. Future breakthroughs in PC nanomedicine will depend on a multi-faceted approach addressing disease barriers, standardized reporting, and clinical strategy (Fig. 6E). First, overcoming the complex pathophysiological barriers of PC necessitates a transition toward biologically relevant testing platforms. Historically, simple subcutaneous xenografts have failed to recapitulate the anatomical context and severe desmoplasia of human PC [308]. Future research must prioritize the usage of orthotopic metastasis models, PDX models, and advanced 3D bioprinting technology with organ-on-a-chip to better recapitulate the clinical reality of drug resistance, high IFP, and complex tumor-stroma interactions [309–312]. By more accurately mirroring the native tissue architecture and pathological barriers of PC, these platforms may provide more predictive assessments of nanoparticle penetration, retention, efficacy, and safety.

Simultaneously, the profound influence of the TME cannot be overlooked. PC is characterized by a dense, immunosuppressive stroma that physically obstructs drug penetration and promotes tumor survival [313–315]. Therefore, comprehensively studying immune and stromal effects is imperative. Nanomedicines should no longer be viewed merely as vehicles for enhancing the cytotoxicity against tumor cells. The future strategies must transcend the limitations of passive accumulation by prioritizing stroma-modulating nanocarriers that utilize active transport mechanisms such as transcytosis, or enzymatic remodeling of the ECM to overcome high IFP and enhance deep-tissue penetration ability. A critical frontier lies in reprogramming the exquisitely immunosuppressive “cold” TME into an immunologically “hot” state; this will be achieved through biomimetic nanovaccines, engineered biological nanoparticles and personalized mRNA LNPs targeting neoantigens, reflecting recent landmark clinical advances [316–318]. Advanced nanomedicine incorporates multi-stage and stimuli-responsive triggers such as local pH, redox gradients, or specific enzyme concentrations to achieve spatiotemporal-specific release while mitigating off-target toxicity [319, 320].

Furthermore, to ensure reproducibility, the field must adopt rigorous frameworks for reporting standardized physicochemical characterization. The biological fate of nanomaterials is highly dependent on their physical properties. Detailed, standardized reporting of nanoparticle size, morphology, surface charge, encapsulation efficiency, and dynamic stability (e.g., protein corona formation in physiological fluids) is mandatory. Establishing consensus on minimal characterization standards will facilitate inter-laboratory comparability and accelerate regulatory evaluation [321–323]. Equally critical is the challenge of improving manufacturability. The broad clinical translation of nanomedicine in PC is often hampered by formidable obstacles related to scalable manufacturing. Developing robust, continuous-flow synthesis techniques is vital to ensure precise control over nanoparticle properties and minimize batch-to-batch variability. Streamlining this manufacturing pipeline is a crucial prerequisite for commercial viability and meeting stringent regulatory compliance [324, 325].

Finally, the integration of biomarker-based patient stratification is crucial to circumvent high attrition rates in clinical trials [326]. Given the vast inter-patient heterogeneity, marrying high-throughput screening and AI-driven precision oncology with clear predictive biomarkers will enable clinicians to identify which patient subpopulations will benefit most from a specific nanoplatform [327–329]. Ultimately, by improving large-scale manufacturability, streamlining complex regulatory paths, and embracing precision tailoring, nano-boosted DDSs are poised to transform PC from a highly lethal malignancy into a manageable, multi-targetable condition.

Conclusions

Despite significant advances in conventional therapies, PC remains one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by its dense desmoplastic stroma and highly immunosuppressive microenvironment. This review has summarized how nano-boosted DDSs offer a transformative toolkit to circumvent these formidable biological barriers. By leveraging rational design ranging from biomimetic “nano-ghosts” and camouflaged nanoparticles to responsive platforms, nanomedicines have demonstrated remarkable preclinical efficacy by enhancing deep tumor penetration and synergistic combinations involving chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy [108, 109, 330].

However, the path to clinical translation remains fraught with challenges. The inherent heterogeneity of the TME necessitates a shift from “one-size-fits-all” nanocarriers to sophisticated, multifunctional systems [52, 331]. Future research must prioritize the development of bio-inspired delivery vehicles capable of actively navigating high interstitial pressures and the rigid extracellular matrix. Furthermore, the integration of nanotechnology with emerging modalities such as mRNA vaccines, PROTACs, and synthetic lethal therapies, is poised to unlock new therapeutic dimensions [332–335].

Looking ahead, the successful clinical integration of PC nanomedicines will depend on improving the reproducibility and scalability of nano-formulations. Transitioning toward more predictive, patient-derived models and organ-on-a-chip technologies will be essential to better simulate human TME dynamics. Overall, by bridging the gap between innovative molecular engineering and clinical oncology, nano-boosted DDSs are well-positioned to redefine the treatment landscape, offering a precision medicine approach to overcome this devastating disease.
