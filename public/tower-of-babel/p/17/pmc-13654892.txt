Early catheter-related thrombosis in oncology patients with central venous access devices: a narrative review of incidence, mechanisms and clinical implications
Authors: J Kania, J Zawadzki, B Kudliński, W Ziółkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral venous access devices (CVADs) are widely used in oncology patients to facilitate administration of chemotherapy and supportive treatments. However, catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) remains one of the most important complications associated with their use. Although overall CRT incidence has been extensively studied, early CRT occurring shortly after catheter implantation remains insufficiently characterized. A narrative review of the literature was performed focusing on studies evaluating CRT in oncology patients with central venous access devices. Relevant studies were identified through searches of the PubMed database and reference lists of selected articles. A total of thirteen studies published between 2005 and 2025 were included in this review. The studies consisted of randomized controlled trials, observational studies, non-randomized studies and retrospective cohort studies, with sample sizes ranging from 48 to 1331 participants. Most studies focused on peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), while fewer evaluated centrally inserted central catheters (CICC) or implantable ports. Reported CRT incidence varied widely depending on catheter type, ranging from 3.3% to 61.3% for PICCs, 6.5% to 49% for CICCs and 1% to 7.1% for implantable ports. Early CRT (defined as ≤ 7 days after catheter implantation) was rarely evaluated and was reported in only three studies, with incidence ranging from 2.4% to 48.39%. Across studies assessing thromboprophylaxis, overall CRT incidence ranged from 3.1% to 16.1% in control groups and from 0.4% to 14.1% in intervention groups. CRT remains a clinically relevant complication in oncology patients requiring central venous access. Available evidence suggests substantial variability in thrombosis incidence across catheter types, with generally higher rates observed in PICCs compared with implantable ports. Data on early CRT remain limited and heterogeneous, underscoring the need for prospective studies specifically addressing early thrombotic events following catheter implantation. Introduction

Central venous access devices (CVADs) are widely used in oncology patients to facilitate the administration of chemotherapy, parenteral nutrition and supportive treatments. Reliable venous access is essential for modern cancer therapy, particularly in patients requiring repeated or prolonged intravenous treatment [1, 2].

Despite their clinical benefits, the use of central venous catheters is associated with several complications, among which catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) represents one of the most clinically relevant. CRT may lead to catheter dysfunction, interruption of oncological treatment, increased risk of infection and, in some cases, serious thromboembolic complications such as pulmonary embolism [3, 4].

The risk of thrombosis in oncology patients is particularly elevated due to cancer-associated hypercoagulability, systemic inflammation and the effects of anticancer therapies. The presence of an intravascular catheter may further contribute to thrombosis development by causing endothelial injury, altered blood flow and local activation of coagulation pathways [5, 6].

Previous studies have demonstrated considerable variability in the incidence of CRT depending on catheter type, patient population and study design. Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) have been associated with a higher risk of thrombosis in several studies, whereas implantable ports may demonstrate lower thrombotic rates [7–9]. Importantly, thrombotic events may occur shortly after catheter insertion. However, early catheter-related thrombosis remains insufficiently investigated, and its true incidence and clinical significance remain poorly defined [10–13].

The aim of this narrative review was to summarize current evidence regarding the incidence of CRT in oncology patients and to examine available data on early CRT occurring shortly after catheter implantation. Understanding early thrombotic complications may be particularly important for optimizing catheter selection, implantation techniques and early patient management. Despite growing interest in catheter-related thrombosis, early thrombotic events remain insufficiently characterized, particularly in the context of heterogeneous study designs and diagnostic approaches.

Overview of included studies

Search strategy and study characteristics

A structured narrative review was conducted to summarize current evidence on catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) in oncology patients, with a particular focus on early events occurring within 7 days of catheter implantation.

A literature search was performed in the PubMed database from January 2005 to March 2025 using combinations of relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), including “catheter-related thrombosis”, “CRT”, “early”, “asymptomatic”, “screening”, “catheter thrombosis”, “cancer”, “oncology”, “central venous catheter”, “PICC”, and “implantable port”. Filters applied included clinical studies, randomized controlled trials, and observational studies. Reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened to identify additional studies.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) involved adult oncology patients requiring central venous access devices; (2) evaluated the incidence, risk factors, mechanisms, or prevention of CRT; and (3) were published in English. Studies focusing on paediatric populations or non-oncological settings were excluded.

Study selection was performed through initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles. Due to the limited number of studies specifically addressing early CRT and substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and outcome definitions, a formal systematic review or meta-analysis was not feasible.

A total of 13 studies were included in the final analysis, comprising randomized, non-randomized, and retrospective cohort studies, with sample sizes ranging from 48 to 1331 participants. Catheter types and study focus

The most frequently analysed catheter type was the peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC), which constituted the primary focus of the majority of included studies. Fewer studies evaluated centrally inserted central catheters (CICC) or implantable ports. Five studies directly compared different catheter types in relation to CRT incidence.

Incidence of catheter-related thrombosis

The reported incidence of CRT varied widely across studies. In studies evaluating thromboprophylaxis, CRT incidence ranged from 3.1% to 16.1% in control groups and from 0.4% to 14.1% in intervention groups.

When stratified by catheter type, CRT incidence ranged from 3.3% to 61.3% for PICCs, 6.5% to 49% for CICCs, and 1% to 7.1% for implantable ports.

Early catheter-related thrombosis: incidence and limitations of current evidence

Early CRT (defined as ≤ 7 days from catheter implantation) was rarely specifically analysed. Only three studies reported early CRT incidence, with estimates ranging from 2.4% to 48.39%.

This wide variability likely reflects substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and methods of thrombosis detection. Notably, studies employing systematic screening strategies tended to report higher incidence rates compared to those based on clinically overt events.

Overall, the available evidence on early CRT remains limited and heterogeneous, precluding quantitative synthesis and highlighting the need for more standardized study designs.

Discussion

Main findings

This narrative review summarizes the current evidence regarding catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) in oncology patients requiring central venous access devices. The available literature demonstrates considerable variability in reported CRT incidence, which appears to depend on catheter type, patient population and study design. Among the included studies, PICCs were the most frequently analysed devices, while implantable ports and centrally inserted central catheters (CICCs) were evaluated less often.

Across studies, CRT incidence varied widely. The highest rates were generally reported in studies evaluating PICCs, whereas implantable ports were associated with lower thrombotic risk. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that catheter characteristics, insertion site and interaction between the catheter and vessel wall may influence thrombosis development.

Several mechanisms may explain the higher risk of thrombosis observed in PICCs. Although PICCs often have a smaller diameter compared with other devices, they typically have a longer intravascular course and are externalized catheters. Even tunneled PICCs may exhibit minor movement within the vessel during arm motion, which may lead to repetitive mechanical irritation of the vascular endothelium. In addition, due to their length, PICCs may be transiently compressed in the axillary region during certain arm positions, potentially contributing to venous stasis. Catheter maintenance may also play a role. PICCs require regular flushing to maintain patency, which may influence the risk of thrombotic or occlusive complications, even in catheters equipped with a Groshong valve [23, 24].

Early catheter-related thrombosis incidence and heterogeneity

Early CRT remains insufficiently investigated in the available literature. Only a limited number of studies specifically reported the incidence of thrombosis occurring shortly after catheter implantation. The reported incidence of catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) in oncology patients varies widely across studies, ranging from 2.4% to as high as 48%.

This substantial heterogeneity is likely multifactorial. First, differences in study design play a significant role, with prospective studies employing systematic screening (e.g., ultrasound-based detection) reporting higher incidence rates compared to retrospective studies relying on clinically symptomatic events.

Second, variability in patient populations including cancer type, stage of disease, and use of thrombogenic treatments such as chemotherapy, contributes to different thrombotic risk.

Third, the type of central venous access device is an important determinant, as peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are generally associated with a higher risk of catheter-related thrombosis compared to implanted ports. Among included studies, there were no studies investigating early catheter-related thrombosis associated with implanted port devices, but overall incidence of CRT remains higher in PICC group.

Importantly, the wide variability in reported early CRT incidence appears to be largely driven by differences in detection strategies, with studies employing routine ultrasound screening reporting substantially higher rates compared with those relying on clinically symptomatic events. Heterogeneity in patient populations further contributes to the observed discrepancies.

Additionally, the definition of CRT and the timing of assessment differ considerably between studies. In particular, early CRT occurring within the first 7 days after catheter placement is often underreported due to the lack of routine early imaging and inconsistent diagnostic criteria. Finally, procedural factors, including insertion technique, catheter-to-vein ratio, and use of ultrasound guidance, may further influence thrombosis rates.

Taken together, these factors highlight the complexity of comparing CRT incidence across studies and underscore the need for standardized definitions and prospective data focusing specifically on early thrombotic events.

Pathophysiological mechanisms of early CRT

The occurrence of early CRT is biologically plausible in the context of catheter implantation. Mechanical disruption of the vessel wall during catheter insertion results in endothelial injury and initiation of a local inflammatory response. Tissue damage leads to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate platelets and endothelial cells and initiate processes collectively described as immunothrombosis. DAMP release may also stimulate tissue factor expression and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, further promoting thrombus development.

Thrombus formation itself may amplify the inflammatory response through activation of complement pathways and additional platelet activation. This bidirectional interaction between thrombosis and inflammation is increasingly referred to as thromboinflammation. Myeloid cells appear to play a particularly important role in this process due to their capacity for strong tissue factor expression and interaction with activated platelets and neutrophils [25–28]. In addition to local vascular injury, the hypercoagulable state associated with malignancy further contributes to thrombosis risk. Cancer cells may express procoagulant factors such as tissue factor or podoplanin, which promote systemic activation of coagulation. Moreover, anticancer therapies may exacerbate this prothrombotic state. Reduced mobility during chemotherapy, dehydration associated with treatment-related adverse effects, and systemic inflammatory responses may all contribute to an increased risk of early catheter-related thrombosis [29, 30].

Finally, venous stasis represents the third component of Virchow’s triad contributing to CRT. Venous stasis may occur when the catheter occupies a substantial proportion of the vessel lumen, particularly when the catheter-to-vein ratio is high. This combination of endothelial injury, hypercoagulability and altered blood flow provides a pathophysiological framework for early thrombus formation following catheter implantation [31, 32].

Risk factors and catheter characteristics

Several catheter-related and patient-related factors may influence the risk of CRT in oncology patients. Catheter-related factors include catheter diameter, tip position, catheter material and insertion technique, including the number of venipunctures required for successful placement [33]. Patient-related factors include malignancy-associated hypercoagulability, chemotherapy, systemic inflammation and reduced mobility during treatment.

PICCs in particular have been associated with an increased risk of thrombosis, which may be related to their longer intravascular course and placement in relatively small upper-extremity veins. High CRT incidence observed with CICCs may also be influenced by catheter material and mechanical irritation of the vessel wall.

Implantable ports appear to demonstrate lower rates of CRT in several studies. This may be related to their structural characteristics, including smaller catheter diameter and the use of flexible silicone catheters. In addition, chest ports are implanted within a subcutaneous pocket where the device becomes stabilized by surrounding connective tissue. This stabilization may limit catheter movement within the vessel and reduce endothelial irritation. Furthermore, ports generally require less frequent flushing compared with PICCs. Although flushing practices vary between institutions, recent studies suggest that normal saline may be as effective as heparin for maintaining catheter patency [34].

In addition to traditional central venous access devices, hybrid systems such as PICC-PORT have recently been introduced, combining characteristics of peripherally inserted catheters and implanted ports. While these devices may offer potential advantages in selected patient populations, evidence regarding their association with thrombosis risk remains limited [35, 36]. PICC- port systems may represent a particularly attractive option in patients requiring long-term venous access but presenting with difficult central venous anatomy or contraindications to conventional chest ports. By combining peripheral insertion with a totally implantable design, these devices may reduce some disadvantages associated with externalized PICCs while maintaining procedural feasibility.

Theoretically, improved catheter stabilization and reduced external manipulation decrease endothelial irritation and infection risk. However, comparative data specifically evaluating early catheter-related thrombosis in PICC-ports devices versus traditional PICCs or implantable ports remain scarce and determine their potential impact on early thrombotic complications.

Clinical implications and prevention strategies

The role of pharmacological thromboprophylaxis in preventing CRT remains controversial. Although some studies have evaluated antiplatelet agents and anticoagulation strategies, the results remain inconsistent and inconclusive [37, 38]. Recent international guidelines, including those from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), do not recommend routine thromboprophylaxis solely due to the presence of a central venous catheter in oncology patients [39–44].

Procedural factors also play a key role in thrombosis prevention. The use of ultrasound-guided insertion, optimization of the catheter-to-vein ratio, and adherence to standardized insertion protocols, such as those proposed by the GAVeCeLT group, have been associated with a reduced risk of catheter-related complications [45–47].

However, thromboprophylaxis may be considered in selected high-risk patients, particularly those with additional prothrombotic risk factors, such as active malignancy, previous venous thromboembolism, or specific systemic therapies.

These findings suggest that a universal prophylactic approach is unlikely to be appropriate and emphasize the need for individualized risk assessment and targeted preventive strategies.

Clinical management of early catheter-related thrombosis

Early catheter-related thrombosis (CRT), particularly when detected within the first days after catheter insertion, presents a clinical challenge due to the limited evidence guiding its management. In most cases, treatment strategies are extrapolated from general recommendations for catheter-related thrombosis.

In patients with confirmed CRT, anticoagulation therapy is generally recommended, most commonly with low-molecular-weight heparin or direct oral anticoagulants, provided there are no contraindications. Treatment is typically continued for at least 3 months or as long as the catheter remains in situ.

Catheter removal in the setting of CRT remains a clinically relevant decision point. Current evidence suggests that routine catheter removal is not required in all cases of CRT, particularly when the catheter remains functional and is still clinically necessary [48]. However, removal should be considered in cases of catheter dysfunction, persistent symptoms despite anticoagulation, infection, or thrombus progression.

In oncology patients, the decision should be individualized, balancing the risks of thrombosis progression against the need for ongoing vascular access. When feasible, continuation of anticoagulation with catheter retention is often preferred.

In the context of early, asymptomatic CRT detected through screening, the optimal management strategy remains uncertain. Management of asymptomatic early CRT remains particularly challenging and should be individualized according to thrombus burden, anatomical location, bleeding risk and the ongoing need for central venous access. Small distal thrombi (< 5 cm) without evidence of progression may, in selected cases, be managed conservatively with close clinical and ultrasonographic surveillance. In contrast, larger thrombi (≥ 5 cm), central vein involvement, catheter dysfunction or extension toward major vessels generally favour initiation of anticoagulation therapy. Importantly, decision-making should also consider the patient’s oncological prognosis, thrombocytopenia risk and the feasibility of managing catheter function during treatment. A case-by-case approach is warranted.

Given the limited data on early CRT, clinical decision-making should be individualized, and further prospective studies are needed to establish evidence-based management algorithms. Future direction

Despite growing awareness of catheter-related thrombosis (CRT), significant gaps remain in understanding the incidence, mechanisms, and optimal management of early thrombotic events occurring shortly after catheter implantation. Future research should focus on several key areas.

First, multicentre prospective cohort studies are needed to better define the natural history, timing, and long-term clinical consequences of early CRT. Standardized definitions of early thrombosis and routine imaging protocols may improve comparability across studies and reduce current heterogeneity.

Second, further development and external validation of early CRT-specific risk prediction models are warranted. Integrating clinical variables, catheter-related factors, imaging findings, and biomarkers may improve individualized risk stratification and facilitate targeted preventive strategies. Several preliminary prediction models have already demonstrated promising results [49]. Lin et al. developed a model integrating ultrasonographic findings and clinical variables, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 for CRT prediction [19]. More recently, Hu et al. reported a prospective prediction model for PICC-related thrombosis in oncology patients with an AUC of 0.79 [50]. Machine learning-based approaches have also emerged, including the model proposed by Ma et al., integrating clinical and procedural factors to improve individualized thrombosis risk assessment [51–55]. Despite these promising results, most currently available models remain limited by relatively small sample sizes, lack of external validation, and insufficient focus specifically on early CRT occurring shortly after catheter implantation.

Third, randomized controlled trials evaluating pharmacological thromboprophylaxis specifically in high-risk patients are needed to determine whether selected oncology populations may benefit from individualized preventive anticoagulation approaches during the early post-implantation period.

Finally, comparative effectiveness studies evaluating different central venous access devices, including PICCs, implantable ports, CICCs, and hybrid PICC-port systems, may help optimize catheter selection and reduce thrombotic complications in oncology patients.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of study design, catheter type and patient population. Second, definitions of CRT varied between studies, which may influence reported incidence rates. Finally, early catheter-related thrombosis was rarely specifically analysed, limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding its true incidence. The narrative design of this review and the lack of standardized definitions across studies further limit the comparability of results.

Conclusion

Catheter-related thrombosis remains a clinically relevant complication in oncology patients requiring central venous access devices. Available evidence suggests substantial variability in CRT incidence depending on catheter type, with generally higher rates observed in PICCs compared with implantable ports.

Early catheter-related thrombosis remains insufficiently characterized despite biologically plausible mechanisms linking catheter implantation, endothelial injury, and thromboinflammatory responses.

Future research should focus on prospective studies with standardized definitions and early imaging strategies, as well as on the development of risk stratification tools to support individualized prevention and management approaches.
