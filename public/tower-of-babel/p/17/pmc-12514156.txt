Economic and nutritional value of insect pollination services in Ethiopia
Authors: Haftom Gebremedhn, Gebre Hadgu, Tesfay Atsbha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The contribution of insect pollination to crop production, food and nutrition security, and ecosystem stability is significant. Nevertheless, their significance in Africa, particularly in an agriculture-dependent nation like Ethiopia, remains largely overlooked. This study was designed to evaluate the economic and nutritional value of insect pollination in Ethiopia and assess regional vulnerabilities to pollinator loss. Using publicly available data on agricultural production, commodity prices, and nutritional composition of crops, the economic and nutritional value of insect pollination was evaluated using the dependency ratio method. Our results show that between 1993 and 2023 the cultivated area of pollinator-dependent (PD) crops has increased three times, while their production volume has grown twofold. During the same period, the economic value of insect pollination has increased more than fivefold, rising from around US$100 million to approximately US$543.8 million. In 2023, the economic value of insect pollination for crop production in Ethiopia was estimated at approximately US$ 543.8 million, representing 5% of the total economic value of crop production. PD crops contributed 20.9% of the total economic value of crop production in 2023, generating approximately US$ 2.3 billion. On average, PD crops have higher nutrient densities in calcium, iron, zinc, protein, fat, vitamin E and vitamin B12, both per hectare and per 100 g, compared to PI crops. Notably, 71% of plant-based vitamin E, 12.5% of dietary fat, and 6.2% of calcium are directly attributable to insect pollination. In this study, Southern (5.8%), Benshangul (5.3%), Oromiya (4.3%), Amhara (4%) and Tigray (3.44%) regions showed the highest economic vulnerability to pollinator loss. Oil crops had the highest vulnerability to pollinator decline (26.3%), followed by crops such as coffee (21.3%), pulses (12.2%), and fruits (11.4%). Therefore, to ensure food and nutrition security in Ethiopia, it is imperative to protect pollinators and promote sustainable agricultural practices that support pollinator conservation. Future policies should incorporate pollinator protection into agricultural and nutrition strategies, while conservation efforts should be strategic and well-targeted, taking into account the crop- and region-specific vulnerabilities to pollinator decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19426-4.

Introduction

Pollination plays a vital role in enhancing agricultural crop production, ensuring food and nutrition security, conserving biodiversity, protecting the environment and stabilizing the ecosystem1–4. Globally, more than 75% of the leading food crops including fruits and vegetables, rely, at least in part, on pollination for their reproduction5–7. The pollination service, mainly due to insect pollination, is estimated globally to contribute more than US$800 billion in gross economic value8, highlighting its contribution to supporting global economic development.

Pollinator-dependent crops (PD) contribute approximately 35% of total crop production by volume7 and provide up to 40% of the global dietary supply of essential nutrients9. With increasing consumer demand for fruits, vegetables, and other PD products, the volume of agricultural production reliant on insect pollination has increased by 300 percent10. As the proportion of land cultivated with PD crops has increased, the global demand for and reliance on pollination service has also grown substantially11,12. In 2019, the global demand for honeybee pollination rose by approximately 2.3 times more than the number of honeybee colonies stocked13. However, pollinator populations (20–40%) and diversity are rapidly declining due to anthropogenic stressors, including habitat loss, pesticide exposure, climate change, and disease12,14,15. This has created a mismatch between pollination supply and demand in agricultural fields16–18. In response to the decline in pollinator populations, action is urgently needed to protect and promote pollinator populations to ensure food and nutrition security and sustainable crop production.

Approximately, 70–80% of the global agricultural population depends on smallholder farming systems19. Many of the crops cultivated on these smallholder farms are highly dependent on pollinators, making the families that manage them heavily reliant on insect pollinators for their livelihoods and food security. In this context, it is particularly crucial for smallholders to understand the contribution of insect pollinators to their crop production. However, evidence suggests that farmer knowledge or awareness about the economic and nutritive benefits of pollinators remains limited, especially in many African countries20–22. Ethiopia, where more than 85% of the population relies heavily on agriculture for its livelihood23,24 could be extremely vulnerable to a reduction in pollinator populations. The increasing use and application rates of pesticides, intensive agricultural practices, habitat loss, decline in floral diversity, spread of diseases and pests, change in land use and landscape structure and climate change are major factors contributing to pollinator decline, including honeybees25–27, and have contributed to a concerning loss of pollinator diversity28. This threatens the future of pollinators and their vital role in agricultural productivity and quality. There is a notable lack of awareness regarding insect pollinators’ benefits, and no comprehensive studies have been conducted on the economic and nutritional values of insect pollination. Here, we address this knowledge gap by highlighting the economic and nutritional implications of insect pollination, promotes sustainable agricultural practices, and advocates for their conservation. By providing insights into the benefits of insect pollinators for PD crops, the study aims to inform agricultural policies and promote practices that enhance food security, nutrition, and pollinator protection in Ethiopia. Thus, the objectives of the present study were to: (I) evaluate the economic and nutritional value of insect pollination services in the context of human nutrition needs and quantifies historic change in pollinator reliance in Ethiopia, (II) assess the nutritional contributions of pollinator dependent crops (PD) as compared to pollinator independent crops (PI) in the context of human nutrition needs, and (III) analyze regional variations in the vulnerability of essential nutrients and the economic losses resulting from reduced crop production due to pollinator loss.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was carried out in Ethiopia, a country located in the Horn of Africa, covering approximately 1.13 million km2 and situated between 30−150N latitudes and 330- 480E longitudes. It shares borders with Eritrea, Djibouti, Somalia, Sudan, South Sudan, and Kenya. The country is characterized by its diverse topography, with elevations ranging from 124 m below sea level to 4325 m above sea level. The selected crops were subsequently categorized as PD or PI (Table 1) based on the classification system developed by Klein et al., (2007)32. Pollinator dependency refers to the degree to which a crop relies on animal pollinators for successful fruit and seed production. This dependency is typically assessed by measuring the reduction in crop yield when pollinators are excluded or absent. A greater yield reduction in the absence of pollinators indicates a higher level of dependency. According to Klein et al., (2007)32, crops classified into five classes based on yield reduction in the absence of pollinators: no impact (pollinator-independent), little (> 0 to 10% reduction in production), modest (10–40% reduction in production), great (40–90% reduction in production), and essential (90–100% reduction in production). In this present study, the midpoint of each categorical ranges was used to set level of dependency for each selected crops which is similar with the previous finding of dependency of each selected crop9,33,34.

As the FAOSTAT database only provides data exclusively at the national level29, it makes difficult to analyze regional dynamics within Ethiopia. To address this limitation, we utilized data from the Ethiopian Central Statistical Agency (CSA)30, which provides the region-specific data. However, list of crops in FAOSTAT and CSA did not fully match. Out of the 52 crops listed in FAOSTAT, only 38 crops (including both PD and PI crops) were also found in the CSA’s regional records. The discrepancy between FAOSTAT and CSA crop listings arises because CSA reports broader categories based on major crops, while FAOSTAT provides a more detailed breakdown, including minor crops not separately listed by CSA. As a result, only these overlapping crops were used for the regional-level analysis in Ethiopia. However, the CSA has not yet published crop production data for 2023, nor has it provided market values of crops (US$/ton) for each cropping season. For this purpose, we used CSA data from the 2019 cropping season on crop production and farmgate prices from, FAOSTAT for the same year.

Data analysis

Trends in pollinator-dependent crops

To quantify historic changes in pollinator reliance in Ethiopia, a trend analysis (1993–2023) was performed for the PD crops. For this purpose, selected crops were categorized into two groups: PD, which rely to some extent on pollinators, and PI, which do not depend on pollinators. For each year, the production volume, cultivated area and economic gain due to insect pollination of each crop were aggregated based on these classifications. Finally, we analyzed the trend in production volume and cultivated area allocated to both categories as well as economic gain due to insect pollination services.

Economic value of crop pollination service

To assess the economic contribution of insect pollinators to agricultural production in Ethiopia, this study applied the pollinator dependence ratio method, which is a widely adopted approach33,35,36 that quantifies the economic loss resulting from pollinator loss37. Pollinators enhance crop production by improving both the quantity and quality of yields, thereby increasing the overall economic value of agricultural output—a value that is also influenced by prevailing market prices38. This study, employs a bio-economic approach based on four key data components: (1) identification of the crop of interest (Ci), (2) the crop’s dependence ratio on insect pollinators (Di), (3) the quantity of crop production (Qi), and (4) the market price per unit of the crop (Pi). The production quantity (Qi) was determined by multiplying the crop’s productivity (in tons per hectare) by the cultivated area. Economic vulnerability

To assess the economic vulnerability of agricultural product due to the loss of insect pollination at national and regional levels of Ethiopia, the ratio of the economic value of insect pollination to the total economic value of the crop (EV) was used37. This ratio provides an estimate of the potential relative production loss attributable solely to the absence of insect pollination. Nutritional contribution of pollinator dependent crops

Nutrient composition data—including total energy, macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats), and micronutrients such as calcium, iron, zinc, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, vitamin C, vitamin E, vitamin A, and total folate per 100 g of each selected crop—were primarily obtained from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) National Nutrient Database and the McCance and Widdowson’s The Composition of Foods Integrated Dataset (CoFID). This approach is consistent with previous studies, such as Devkota et al., (2024)36. For crops not listed or less commonly reported in these databases, such as Teff (Eragrostis tef), additional nutrient data were obtained from peer-reviewed journals41–44.

Total crop production (in tons), obtained at both national and regional levels, was first converted into grams. Subsequently, the total production (in grams) of each crop was multiplied by its corresponding nutrient composition to calculate the total amount of each nutrient (in grams) provided by that crop at both regional and national levels. To estimate the contribution of insect pollinators to the nutrient supply, the total nutrient values derived from each crop were then multiplied by the crop’s pollinator dependence ratio. This allowed for the quantification of the proportion of each nutrient attributable to insect pollination across regions and nationally. This analysis enables to identification of specific nutrients and geographic areas that are particularly vulnerable to pollinator loss.

For the regional-level assessment, it was assumed that the nutritional composition of each crop remains constant across regions, irrespective of variations in agricultural practices, environmental conditions, or crop varieties. While this assumption may introduce some margin of error in the estimated nutrient values, it is not likely to have substantial impact on the relative importance of pollination services to nutrient production across the country, as supported by previous studies36.

Results

This study also analyzed the trends in production volume and economic value of PD and PI crops over the last three decades. Between 1993 to 2023, both volume of production and cultivated land for crops increased from approximately 7.4 million tons to over 33.3 million tons, and from about 3.9 million ha to more than 10.5 million ha, respectively. During this period, the area cultivated with PD crops expanded threefold, increasing from 775,844 ha in 1993 to 2,274,817 ha in 2023, whereas the area under PI crops increased by 2.7 times, from 3,113,666 ha to 8,270,483 ha. The production volume of PD crops also showed a considerable increase, from 718,701.25 tons in 1993 to 3,279,734.46 tons in 2023. The production volume of pollination-independent (PI) crops also rose substantially, from 6,702,376.92 tons to 30,085,036.14 tons over the same period.

Economic vulnerability due to pollinator loss

This study revealed that economic vulnerability, defined as the potential economic loss in the absence of insect pollinators, varies by crop type and region (Fig. 2 and Fig. 4). It is estimated that Ethiopia would experience an economic loss of $543.8 million USD, representing 4.95% of the total crop economic value, in the absence of insect pollinators (Table 2). In terms of crop type, Oil crops show the highest vulnerability (26.3%), followed by coffee (21.3%), pulses (12.2%), and fruits (11.4%). At the regional level, economic vulnerability due to pollinator loss is highest in Southern region (5.8%), followed by Benshangul (5.3%), Oromiya (4.3%), Amhara (4%) and Tigray (3.44%). Discussion

Agriculture remains a backbone of Africa’s economy, providing employment opportunities for many smallholder farmers45, many of whom depend heavily on PD crops for their food production, and nutritional security46. Despite this, many people are unaware of the benefits and significance of insect pollinators47. This study shows high economic and nutrition values of insect pollinators, estimated at US $544.8 million, representing approximately 9% of the total agricultural revenue and/or nearly one-tenth of the sector’s income. This underscores the critical role that pollinators play in Ethiopia’s agricultural economy. The findings are consistent with findings from Rwanda48, highlighting the broader significance of pollination services across Africa’s agrarian economy.

This study revealed that PD crops contributed about 20.9% (US$2.3 billion) of Ethiopia’s crop production value in 2023, representing 3.9% of the total agricultural revenue49. Between 1993 and 2023, the economic value of PD crops increased more than five-fold, while the area allotted to these crops nearly tripled. Similar trends are observed globally, with increasing land allocated to PD crops50. The increasing economic value of pollination services is primarily attributed to the expansion of land cultivated with PD crops and the rising market value of these crops15,36. The expansion of PD crop cultivation, along with the substantial rise in the economic value of insect pollination, highlight Ethiopia’s growing reliance on pollinators and increasing vulnerability to their decline36. The substantial economic reliance on insect pollination and the expansion of PD crop cultivation underscores the urgent need to integrate pollinator conservation into national agricultural and environmental policies, adopt pollinator-friendly practices, and monitoring pollinator populations to protect agricultural productivity and ensure long-term food security amid environmental change.

This study also found that PD crops such as oil crops, coffee, pulses, and fruits are highly vulnerable to pollinators decline, consistent with findings from other locations, including Rwanda48 and Nepal36. These results highlight the need for crop-specific interventions that address the unique pollination requirements and vulnerabilities of each crop to sustain their productivity more effectively. Furthermore, the study identified significant regional variation in vulnerability to pollinator decline within Ethiopia. The Southern region faces the greatest economic risk, followed by Benshangul, Oromiya, and Amhara. This indicates that farmers in these regions are likely to face substantial income losses if pollinator decline continues36. These findings highlight the need for region-specific pollinator conservation strategies and targeted education efforts., with high-risk areas within prioritized in national agricultural planning and environmental policies to protect both productivity and livelihoods.

This study revealed notable differences in micro- and macronutrient concentrations between PD and PI crops, both per unit area and per 100 g. PI crops provide higher amounts of carbohydrates, energy, and certain vitamins such as vitamin C compared to PD crops. Since many fruits and vegetables, which are major sources of vitamin C, are highly PD crops, one might expect higher vitamin C yields from PD crops than from PI crops. However, our study found that vitamin C levels were actually higher in PI crops. This may be attributed to the low production volume of PD fruits such as oranges and lemons, which are known sources of vitamin C in Ethiopia. Additionally, this study did not include certain fruits like apples and almonds, which are both rich in vitamin C and highly pollinator-dependent. The dominance of PI fruits and vegetables such as potatoes, bananas, sweet potatoes, and taro—with their relatively high production volumes—may also contribute to the greater overall vitamin C production from PI crops31. On the other hand, the present study found that, on average, PD crops contain higher concentrations of key micronutrients including vitamin E, calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), and folate, both per hectare and per 100 g, consistent with previous observations9. These nutrients are often deficient in the diets of populations in developing countries51. In Ethiopia, for example, approximately 72% of the population is estimated to suffer from zinc deficiency52, while iron deficiency anemia (IDA) accounts for 35% of anemia cases, with anemia affecting 57% of preschool children53–55. This makes micronutrient deficiencies a significant public health challenge. Given these alarming statistics, promoting the cultivation of nutrient-dense PD crops and safeguarding the pollinators they depend on could be a strategic intervention to enhance the nutritional quality of the national food supply and improve public health outcomes in Ethiopia and other developing countries. Therefore, integrating pollinator conservation into both agricultural and nutrition policies offers a cost-effective and sustainable pathway to strengthen nutrition security.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, where food and nutrition insecurity remain major challenges, expanding the cultivation and land allocation of nutrient-rich PD crops is an effective approach to reduce micro- and macronutrient deficiencies without compromising income46. According to Mulungu et al., (2023)46, increasing the cultivation of PD crops relative to PI crops reduces the prevalence of nutrient deficiencies and enhances crop income without compromising macronutrient production. This suggests that expanding the cultivation of PD crops could contribute to the goals of Ethiopia’s National Nutrition Program (NNP II), which seeks to reduce anemia prevalence among adolescents from 30 to 15%56. However, the feasibility of expanding PD crops depends on effectively addressing pollinator declines driven by pesticide use, habitat loss, and land degradation, challenges that are increasingly evident across many regions of Ethiopia57–59. Approximately 50% of Ethiopia’s agricultural land—particularly in the highlands—is affected by land degradation, which has contributed to the decline of forest cover60. Extensive deforestation—driven by rapid population growth, large-scale forest clearing for cultivation, overgrazing, and the extraction of fuelwood and construction materials—has significantly reduced natural habitats, further limiting the availability of nesting and foraging resources essential for sustaining pollinator diversity and abundance61–63. To counter these threats, adopting pollinator-friendly practices—such as agroforestry, intercropping with flowering plants, minimizing pesticide use, forest protection, home gardening, and targeted conservation efforts—can enhance pollinator richness and abundance while supporting crop yields. Integrating beekeeping with crops such as coffee in agroforestry systems could also serve as a promising strategy to alleviate pollinator scarcity. Beyond enhancing pollination services, beekeeping can provide alternative sources of income and nutrition through honey and related products, thereby benefiting rural livelihoods and helping to sustain pollinator populations61.

Globally, including in Ethiopia, there has been a significant decline in insect pollinators abundance and diversity, including honeybees58,59,64,65, which is mainly associated with improper pesticide application26, habitat loss and loss of floral diversity and degradation, diseases and pests, and climate change27. Between 1990 to 2022, the global total use and intensity of pesticide application increased at an alarming rate, with pesticide use per unit of cropland rising by 94%66. During the same period, pesticide use and application rate in Ethiopia increased significantly, rising eightfold and fourfold, respectively, reflecting intensified agricultural practices67. This trend may further exacerbate the ongoing loss of pollinators in the country58,59. It has been found that the loss of pollinators could lead to a significant increase in micronutrient deficiencies, with up to 56% of the population becoming newly vulnerable to micronutrient deficiencies68. Consequently, supporting farmers to adopt pollinator-friendly farming practices could be an effective means of addressing nutrient deficiencies and increasing farmers’ incomes. Hence, future policies should integrate pollinator protection into agricultural and nutritional strategies, with attention to the region-specific vulnerabilities highlighted in this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that insect pollination significantly enhances the nutritional content and economic value of major crops in Ethiopia. The growing economic and nutritional importance of pollination-dependent crops, combined with their vulnerability to pollinator loss, underscores the need for integrated policies and conservation efforts that promote pollinator health through habitat protection, reduced pesticide use, and adoption of sustainable, pollinator-friendly agricultural practices to safeguard food security and public health. Conservation efforts should be strategic and well-targeted, taking into account the crop- and region-specific vulnerabilities to pollinator decline. Failure to do so could threaten both economic gains from agriculture and the availability of nutrient-rich foods, particularly in regions heavily dependent on PD crops.
