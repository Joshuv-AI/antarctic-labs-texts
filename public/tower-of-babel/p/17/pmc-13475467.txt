Depression and Anxiety in Pulmonary Hypertension Associated With Congenital Heart Disease: Pathophysiology and Clinical Implications of the Heart–Brain Axis
Authors: Nicodemus N Triatmojo, Valerinna YS Putri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary hypertension associated with congenital heart disease or PH-CHD is an increasingly prevalent disorder in modern cardiology. It is characterized by progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, chronic right ventricular (RV) pressure overload, and abnormal cardiopulmonary hemodynamic. Due to advances in congenital cardiology and treatments for pulmonary hypertension, survival rates have increased. As a result, the focus has switched to long-term functional status and quality of life. Depression and anxiety are currently recognized as prevalent and clinically significant manifestations in PH-CHD; nonetheless, they remain underdiagnosed and undertreated within cardiovascular care frameworks.More study shows the disease's severity or symptom count cannot explain PH-CHD patients' psychological distress. Instead, it shows a chronic heart disease-induced pathogenic process. The heart-brain axis underlines this idea. Chronic pulmonary vascular disease and RV failure induce neurohormonal activation, autonomic instability, systemic inflammation, hypoxia, and inadequate cerebral perfusion, which affect brain structure and emotion regulation.This brief review gathers evidence of objective cardiovascular dysfunction linked to depression and anxiety in PH-CHD, and investigates into the molecular pathways that connect the heart and brain. The ideas of psychological distress as a factor associated with chronic cardiovascular disease requires a more extensive interdisciplinary approach, which may ultimately affect favorable patient outcomes and long-term prognosis.Keywords: Heart brain axis, Pulmonary hypertension, Congenital heart disease, Depression, Anxiety, Quality of life

1. Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) associated with congenital heart disease (CHD) is a complex phenotype within the pulmonary hypertension spectrum, caused by prolonged exposure to abnormal cardiopulmonary physiology that develops over decades [1–6]. Advancements in the field of congenital cardiology, surgical repair, and targeted PH therapies have led to significant improvements in survival and an increasing population of adult patients with PH-CHD, thereby changing clinical emphasis from short-term outcome to long-term functional status and quality of life [2–7].

Depression and anxiety are common comorbidities in patients with pulmonary hypertension, with a prevalence of approximately 25–40% among adults, and contribute significantly to symptom burden and disability [7–9,43–45]. Historically, PH was viewed anxiety and depression through a process of negative emotional reactions to breathlessness and decreased exercise ability. However, psychological distress is only weakly associated with pulmonary hemodynamics and often does not subside even after stabilization of the disease, indicating factors beyond disease severity alone [46,48,49]. Furthermore, depression and anxiety are both independent predictors of poor outcomes such as decreased treatment adherence, increased health care utilization, and mortality even after cardiopulmonary parameters were controlled [50–53,67–69].

Emerging evidence suggests a biologically mediated connection between cardiovascular illness and mental-based pathways, elicited by neurohormonal activation, systemic inflammation, autonomic dysregulation, chronic hypoxaemia, and cerebral structural aberrations in function [20–22,29–31]. This series of events meets in a bidirectional heart–brain axis, where cardiovascular derangement affects the central nervous regulation of mood and cognition, closely related to psychological stress, which aggravates disease status reciprocally [22,63–65].

This narrative review was conducted through a targeted literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases, focusing on studies published in English over the past two decades. Keywords included ‘pulmonary hypertension’, ‘congenital heart disease’, ‘depression’, ‘anxiety’, ‘heart–brain axis’. Priority was given to clinical studies, meta-analyses, and translational research relevant to PH-CHD and related cardiovascular conditions. Given the limited PH-CHD–specific data, evidence from pulmonary arterial hypertension and heart failure populations was also incorporated to provide mechanistic insightsc. This review uniquely integrates cardiovascular, neurohormonal, inflammatory, and neurostructural mechanisms to provide a unified heart–brain axis framework specific to PH-CHD.

2. Pulmonary vascular and cardiovascular pathophysiology in PH-CHD

PH-CHD results from chronic exposure of the pulmonary circulation to abnormal flow, pressure, or both, and leads to progressive injury and remodelling of the pulmonary vasculature [4,10–12]. Left-to-right shunt defects, especially when left untreated or repaired late, expose the pulmonary vascular bed to chronic shear stress, abnormal endothelial function, and inflammation that initiate a destructive sequence of events leading to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance [10–12].

At the molecular level, PVR in PH-CHD is characterized by dysregulation of the nitric oxide signaling pathway with decreased bioavailability of prostacyclin and overexpression of vasoconstrictive and smooth muscle proliferative agents such as endothelin-1 [11–14]. ROS leads to the dysregulation of bone morphogenetic protein receptor type-2 signaling, activation of transforming growth factor-β pathways, and stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α, which advocates for intimal fibrosis, medial hypertrophy, and anti-apoptosis as well [11,12,19]. These processes are increased by inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine generation, maintaining vascular stiffening and reduced compliance [12,19].

With elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, the right ventricle becomes chronically overloaded. The initial adaptive hypertrophy is intended to maintain cardiac output, but persistent afterload eventually leads to maladaptive remodelling, myocardial ischemia, fibrosis, and RV failure [16–18]. Progressive right ventricular failure has systemic effects that results in impaired perfusion of the peripheral organs, such as the kidneys, skeletal muscle, and brain [20–22,39,40]. Viscera are also impeded by venous congestion, leading to neurohormonal activation. These haemodynamic changes serve as a central connection between the pulmonary vasculature disease and stimulation of the heart–brain axis.

In the setting of advanced PH-CHD, and specifically with Eisenmenger physiology, chronic hypoxemia aggravates cardiovascular dysfunction [15,33–35,78]. Continuous arterial desaturation causes secondary erythrocytosis, raising blood viscosity, and thus, may be at risk for cerebrovascular events such as ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke [33–35]. Cerebral metabolism and vascular regulation are also directly influenced by chronic hypoxia, thus establishing early brain susceptibility [39,40].

3. Neurohormonal activation and autonomic dysregulation: the core of the heart–brain axis

The chronic cardiovascular stress in pulmonary hypertension due to congenital heart disease (PH-CHD) results in continuous stimulation of the neurohormonal and autonomic pathways, which form an integral part of the two-tiered heart–brain axis [22,23,29–31]. Although initially adaptive, sustained neurohormonal activation is maladaptive, giving rise to mechanisms involved in disease progression as well as certain central nervous system changes that predispose patients to depression and anxiety.

3.1. Sympathetic nervous system overactivation

Chronic sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activation is an early and critical hallmark of advanced pulmonary hypertension. Plasma norepinephrine concentrations and muscle sympathetic nerve activity are consistently augmented in patients with PAH, and these increases associate with disease severity and poor outcome [28,34,79]. In patients with PH-CHD, sympathetic overactivity may be even more prevalent given the presence of RH and chronic RV pressure overload, reduced baroreflex sensitivity, and persisting hypoxia [9,15,23,79].

Chronic activation of the SNS has direct effects on the brain, particularly limbic centers responsible for emotional control. The amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex are modulated by the adrenergic neurotransmitter norepinephrine, which in turn influences processing of fear, reactivity to stress, and regulation of mood [18,63–66]. Receptor downregulation, synaptic remodeling, and neural connectivity are altered with the chronic effects of catecholaminergic stimulation, which have been proposed to play a role in both anxiety and depressive syndromes [66–68]. Moreover, increased sympathetic tone also strengthens somatic symptom awareness, such as dyspnea and palpitations, that in turn may raise anxiety through interoceptive routes [41,42].

3.2. Parasympathetic withdrawal and heart rate variability

Autonomic dysfunction in PH-CHD is defined by sympathetic overactivity and withdrawal of parasympathetic tone. Decreased vagal tone and impaired HRV have also been reported in patients with pulmonary hypertension, and are related to poor clinical status and prognosis [18–20]. Low HRV is consistently linked to depression and anxiety among cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular populations [18–20,27,66]. Attenuated vagal input to the brainstem and limbic system lessens inhibitory influence on stress response, and fosters emotional dyscontrol and excess anxiety [66,67]. In PH-CHD, chronic autonomic imbalance may thus serve as a mechanistic bridge connecting cardiovascular dysfunction to enduring mood disorders beyond the level of symptom expression.

3.3. Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) dysregulation: a neurocardiac modulator

Activation of the RAAS is a hallmark of advanced pulmonary hypertension and serves as a critical mechanistic mediator in the heart–brain axis in pulmonary hypertension [24,25,77]. Inadequate effective circulating volume from decreased cardiac output combined with reduced renal perfusion and renal venous congestion, stimulates renin release; such unrestrained activation of the rennin/angiotensin system is associated with chronically elevated circulating angiotensin II and aldosterone. Although RAAS activation in the short term is hemodynamically beneficial for maintaining blood pressure and organ blood flow, its sustained overactivity ultimately becomes maladaptive with harmful consequences on both cardiovascular function and the central nervous system.

Besides its peripheral vasoconstrictor and profibrotic effects, angiotensin II is a powerful neuromodulator. Angiotensin II is readily able to cross the blood–brain barrier and is also produced in the brain, where it acts on angiotensin type 1 receptors (AT1R), which are expressed abundantly within the hypothalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, and autonomic nuclei of the brainstem. When central AT1R is activated, it facilitates the activation of the sympathetic system, inhibits parasympathetic tone, and increases stress response in a positive feed-forward manner, thus driving autonomic imbalance further [18–21,26,27].

Mineralocorticoid receptors have higher expression in brain structures such as the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex, which serve mood regulation and cognitive control. Clinical and experimental findings indicate that chronic surplus aldosterone is associated with depressive symptoms, stress maladaptation, and enhanced anxiety. Aldosterone is particularly important as a stimulant of neuroinflammation and microvasculature endothelial dysfunction within the cerebral parenchyma, which is respectively involved in the pathogenesis of blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and also creates suitable conditions for supporting neuroimmune activation [19–21].

3.4. Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation: chronic stress signalling and brain vulnerability

The HPA axis is a major stress response system-that integrates physiological and psychological stimuli. In PH-CHD, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, hypoxia, and neurohormonal activation with consequent repeated hospitalizations constitute a continuously active setting for HPA axis activation [19–23]. This results in, over time, dysregulation evident as changes in patterns of cortisol secretion, abnormalities in negative feedback, and altered ability to cope with stress.

Prolonged activation of the HPA axis leads to chronic exposure to high levels of glucocorticoids and has profound long-term effects on brain structure and function. The hippocampus, which has in excess of abundant glucocorticoid receptors, is the primary target of cortisol-mediated neurotoxicity [30,31]. Long-term hypercortisolemia induces synaptic plasticity deficits, neurogenesis inhibition, and dendritic atrophy in the hippocampus, alterations strongly associated with depression and cognitive impairment [30–32].

In PH-CHD, HPA axis dysregulation synergizes with hypoxia and inflammation to further accelerate neural injury. Hypoxia enhances glucocorticoid-induced neurotoxicity by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [36–38]. Simultaneously, inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) disturb glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, causing additional impairment in negative feedback inhibition of the HPA axis and enhancing the cycle to higher cortisol levels [31,37,38]. This destructive loop sets up a neurobiological climate amenable to mood disorders. Elevated cortisol promotes insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and sympathetic activation, all of which may exacerbate pulmonary vascular remodelling and right ventricular dysfunction [29–31]. Thus, HPA axis abnormalities contribute to a bidirectional amplification loop within the heart–brain axis, linking psychological distress to worsening cardiopulmonary disease.

To summarise: chronic neurohormonal activation and autonomic dysregulation represent the fundamental biological connection between pulmonary vascular disease and mood disorders in PH-CHD. Chronic sympathetic overactivity, withdrawal of parasympathetic activity, activation of RAAS, and HPA axis dysfunction have an impact on brain structure and function, resulting in a decrease in stress resilience and vulnerability to depression and anxiety. These mechanisms are essential for understanding the ensuing inflammatory, hypoxic, and neurostructural changes within the heart–brain axis.

4. Inflammation, hypoxia, and neuroimmune crosstalk in the heart–brain axis

Moreover, in addition to the neurohormonal and autonomic imbalance, chronic inflammation and hypoxia accentuate heart–brain axis dysfunction [11–14,19,29,36,70]. These processes not only contribute to pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular failure but also have profound direct and indirect impacts on brain architecture, neurochemical signaling systems, and emotional regulation, leading to depression and anxiety.

4.1. Systemic and pulmonary vascular inflammation

Inflammation has been increasingly understood as a central pathomechanism of pulmonary vascular diseases in all subtypes of PH, including PH-CHD [12–14,19]. Increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the circulation (eg, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP) have been consistently described in patients with PAH and closely correlate with severity of disease and prognosis [12,19,36]. In PH-CHD, chronic endothelial injury in the setting of abnormal shear stress from flow disturbances and neurohormonal activation results in hypoxia, which maintains a pro-inflammatory vascular environment over many years.

These cytokines are not restricted to the pulmonary circulation. A systemic over-spill of these products causes chronic low-grade inflammation, which targets a range of organs, including the brain. Moreover, IL-6 and TNF-α act as neuromodulators in the brain that can influence neurotransmitter metabolism, synaptic plasticity, and neuroendocrine control [36–38]. Increased levels of inflammatory markers have been independently correlated with depressive symptoms, in cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular populations, which further confirms the mechanistic association between inflammation and mood disorders [37,38,67].

4.2. Blood–brain barrier dysfunction and neuroimmune activation

Chronic systemic inflammation and neurohormonal activation in PH-CHD may compromise blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, facilitating the entry of peripheral cytokines, immune cells, and neurotoxic mediators into the central nervous system [36–39]. Experimental studies demonstrate that angiotensin II, aldosterone, and pro-inflammatory cytokines disrupt tight junction proteins and increase BBB permeability, particularly in limbic regions involved in emotional processing [38,39,72–75]. After entering the brain, inflammatory mediators activate microglia, which increases cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and nitric oxide, causing neuroinflammation [37–40]. Chronic microglial activation reduces synaptic plasticity, neurogenesis, and neurotransmitter balance, all of which are hallmarks of depression and anxiety [37,38].

4.3. Hypoxia as a central amplifier

Hypoxia is important in PH-CHD, notably in Eisenmenger physiology or residual shunting [15,33–35]. At the pulmonary vascular level, hypoxia promotes vasoconstriction, smooth muscle proliferation, and inflammation via hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-dependent pathways [11–14,19]. Systemically, hypoxia contributes to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming.

Within the brain, chronic hypoxia impairs cerebral oxygen delivery and disrupts regional blood flow, particularly in watershed areas and metabolically active regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [39,40]. Hypoxia-induced oxidative stress damages neuronal membranes and synapses, while HIF-1α activation alters neurotransmitter systems, including serotonin and dopamine pathways implicated in mood regulation [36–40]. Inflammation and neurohormonal excess are neurotoxically enhanced by hypoxia. Experimental studies show that hypoxia increases cytokine-induced microglial activation and glucocorticoid-mediated neuronal damage [36–38]. Lifelong intermittent or chronic hypoxemia may aggravate heart–brain axis dysfunction in PH-CHD.

4.4. Cytokine–neurotransmitter interactions

Pro-inflammatory cytokines directly influence central neurotransmitter systems relevant to depression and anxiety. IL-6 and TNF-α alter tryptophan metabolism by activating the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase pathway, diverting tryptophan away from serotonin synthesis toward kynurenine production [37,38]. Accumulation of neurotoxic kynurenine metabolites has been linked to glutamatergic excitotoxicity, cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms [38].

Additionally, inflammatory signaling interferes with dopamine synthesis and reuptake, contributing to anhedonia, fatigue, and motivational deficits frequently reported by patients with PH-CHD [36–38]. These neurochemical changes provide a biological explanation for why depression in PH is often characterized by prominent somatic and motivational symptoms rather than purely affective complaints.

4.5. Clinical implications

The recognition of inflammation and hypoxia as central mediators of heart–brain axis dysfunction reframes depression and anxiety in PH-CHD as biologically embedded components of disease pathology. These mechanisms underscore the limitations of purely psychosocial explanations and highlight the need for integrated therapeutic strategies targeting both cardiovascular and neuroimmune pathways.

5. Structural and functional brain changes in PH-CHD

Emerging evidence indicates that pulmonary hypertension is linked to changes in brain structure and function that may contribute to depression and anxiety, which offers neurobiological support for the heart–brain axis model [39–42,63–66]. Neuroimaging studies in PAH and heart failure populations have demonstrated reduced gray matter volume in the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex, regions critically involved in emotional regulation. Functional MRI studies further reveal altered connectivity within the default mode and salience networks, which are associated with impaired stress processing and affective regulation. Additionally, reduced cerebral blood flow and impaired cerebrovascular reactivity have been observed, supporting the role of chronic hypoperfusion in neurocognitive and emotional dysfunction. Although direct neuroimaging data in PH-CHD remain limited, these findings provide a plausible structural and functional substrate for the observed psychological burden [35,38–41]. The neuroimaging results do not demonstrate causality, but they offer physiologic support for the increased incidences of anxiety and depression in PH-CHD.

6. Epidemiology and clinical phenotypes of depression and anxiety in PH-CHD

Depression and anxiety are common in PH and two of the top listed non-cardiopulmonary related comorbidities seen in clinical practice [7–9,43–45]. The proportion of patients with clinically relevant depressive or anxiety symptoms among mixed PH populations ranges from 25 to 40%, far exceeding that observed in the general population [7–9].

Distinct subtypes of ill-being phenotypes might be identified in the PH-CHD population. Increased prevalence of anxiety in patients with Eisenmenger physiology might be better understood by chronic hypoxaemia, cyanosis, and heightened perception of symptoms [33–35,45]. On the other hand, depressive symptoms may be more prevalent in people with progressive RV failure and declining functional status and/or greater treatment burden [46,50]. Sex female, younger in age, and of low socio-economic class have also been associated with susceptibility to mood disorders in adults with CHD or PH [9,47–49,71].

Depression and anxiety often occur together and may not be directly related to cardiopulmonary function. Medical therapy and the psychological burden. Long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated that PAH patients maintain an impaired psychosocial status after clinical stabilization or improvement with PAH-specific treatment [48–52]. This durability is consistent with a concept of biologically mediated vulnerability that relies on chronic neurohormonal activation, inflammation, and brain dysfunction rather than continuing psychological responses alone. Failure to acknowledge the presence of such states is an important issue, as psychological suffering in itself predicts lower quality of life, adherence rates, and poor prognosis [50–53].

7. Impact of depression and anxiety on quality of life, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes

Traditional objective hemodynamic measurements alone may not often adequately explain the poor quality of life (QoL) [22,48]. Perceived health is more strongly impacted by depression and anxiety than by hemodynamic severity or functional class, according to patient reports in individuals with PH-CHD [48–50]. A large body of research using both disease-specific and QoL measures (e.g., CAMPHOR, SF-36, and the Cambridge Pulmonary Hypertension Outcome Review) shows that mental health issues, such as depression and anxiety, are significantly associated with poorer physical, emotional, and social QoL [48–51,55–57]. In a manner that is independent of right ventricular function or six minutes of walking distance, the level of tiredness and dyspnea perception, along with social involvement, deteriorates as the scores on PHQ-9 and GAD-7 rise [49–51,55]. These findings suggest that, in addition to cardiopulmonary impairment, there are psychological and neurological components that amplify the experience of symptoms and functional limitations.

Depression and anxiety in PH-CHD affect therapeutic adherence. Multiple oral medication regimens inflict enormous mental and emotional strains on patients [56–59]. Depression's impact on motivation, executive function, and adverse illness beliefs may explain non-adherence to medicine and lifestyle recommendations [56–60]. Anxiety may prevent people from performing things that hurt or have side consequences, which can lead to not taking their medication or stopping completely. Non-adherence is immediately significant in pulmonary hypertension. Lack of PAH-targeted therapies increases symptomatic burden, hospitalizations, and mortality [58–61].

8. Multidisciplinary management targeting the heart–brain axis: cardiologist and psychiatrist perspectives

8.1. Optimization of pulmonary hemodynamics and right ventricular function therapy

The quintessential therapy in treating PAH remains the hallmark of PH-CHD therapy and indirectly stems a much larger influence on mental health through alteration of the upstream hemodynamic strain over the heart [12–16]. Endothelin, NO, and prostacyclin pathways inhibition has been shown to reduce PVR and increase cardiac output [12–14], while endothelin-receptor antagonists (ERAs), phosphodiesterase type-5 inhibitors (PDE5i), soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators, and prostacyclin pathway agents mitigate pulmonary artery constriction [15].

Improved pulmonary hemodynamics decrease RV afterload and could halt the chronic sympathetic and RAAS activation noted earlier [20–24,36]. Echocardiographic indices, such as TAPSE, right atrial size and RV longitudinal strain, and the RV fractional area change can provide a measure of RV adaptation or failure [16–18]. Following treatment for PAH, these parameters improve, and as the latter are predictive of exercise capacity and independence from symptoms. Additional indirect benefits on mood and mentation may result from diminished right atrial pressure and systemic venous congestion, leading to lowered neurohormonal activation and cerebral hypoperfusion.

8.2. Autonomic and neurohormonal modulation: beyond hemodynamics

Empirical imbalance of the autonomic nervous system is a reversible component of heart–brain axis injury. PAH treatment efficacy is associated with partial restoration of autonomic tone, as characterized by a lowered resting HR combined with favorable HRV patterns [24–27]. HRV is an integrative measure of autonomic–central nervous system regulation, and has been found to be independently associated with depression and anxiety severity [26,27,66].

Beyond conventional PAH-targeted therapies, emerging strategies aimed at modulating neurohormonal and inflammatory pathways may offer additional benefit within the heart–brain axis framework. Experimental and early clinical data suggest that selective modulation of RAAS signaling, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism, and anti-inflammatory approaches may attenuate both pulmonary vascular remodeling and neuropsychological burden. Furthermore, interventions targeting cytokine signaling and oxidative stress pathways are being explored as potential adjunctive therapies to reduce neuroinflammation and improve mood-related outcomes. Although these approaches remain investigational in PH-CHD, they represent promising avenues for future translational research [24,25,29,36,77].

Exercise training and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation are one of the best non-pharmacological interventions to restore autonomic balance. Other studies reported that controlled low- and moderate-intensity aerobic exercises and resistance training could improve peak VO2, VE/VCO2 slope, endothelial function, and HRV in patients with PAH [60–62]. Importantly, these physiological improvements are accompanied by increasing reductions in depressive and anxious symptoms, likely attributable to increased tonus of the vagal nerve and enhanced self-efficacy complemented with-attenuated inflammatory signaling [60–62,80].

8.3. Psychiatric interventions: non-pharmacological and pharmacological considerations

Psychologically based non-pharmacological treatments should be the first approach in mild/moderate depression and anxiety in the PH-CHD population. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) improved coping techniques, illness perception, and adherence behavior, making it one of the most effective therapies for chronic cardiovascular disease. [61,62]. Mindfulness-based stress reduction, acceptance-commitment therapy, and supportive psychotherapy could also diminish stress-mediated HPA axis activation and autonomic dysregulation.

Pharmacological interventions should be kept for patients with mild-to-mixed mood disorders. Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most benign drug, appropriate as a first-line choice for PH [62,76]. However, interactions with PAH-specific medications may need to be exercised cautiously. SSRIs have an inhibitory effect on platelet aggregation, and the concurrent use with prostacyclin analogues cannot be supported. This is also the case for CYP450 interactions with ERAs, such as bosentan, which has demonstrated enzyme-inducing properties and potentially reduces plasma concentrations of SSRIs [76].

Tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors also require caution because of the possibility of heart rate, blood pressure, or arrhythmogenic effects. Benzodiazepines, which are effective for acute anxiety, can exacerbate hypoventilation and should be used only in the setting of advanced disease.

In addition to individualized psychiatric care, patient-centered peer support initiatives play an important role in addressing psychological distress. Structured group support, including in-person meetings, patient communities, and digital platforms (e.g., online forums or messaging groups), allows individuals with PH-CHD to share experiences, reduce social isolation, and enhance coping strategies. Such peer-based interventions have been associated with improved emotional well-being and may represent a low-cost, scalable adjunct to multidisciplinary care, particularly in patients with rare or orphan diseases such as PAH [54,56,60].

8.4. Integrated care models and monitoring

Close coordination of cardiologists and pulmonologists with psychiatrists and psychologists, as well as rehabilitation specialists, is required for successful therapy of the heart–brain axis dysfunction in PH-CHD. By addressing the interplay of pulmonary hemodynamics, RV function, autonomic balance, and psychological well-being together in multidisciplinary care, maladaptive heart–brain axis signaling can potentially be interrupted and quality of life and long-term outcomes improved in adults with PH-CHD.

From a practical perspective, routine screening for depression and anxiety should be incorporated into standard PH clinic workflows. Validated tools such as PHQ-9 and GAD-7 can be administered at baseline and during follow-up visits, particularly in patients with worsening functional status or frequent hospitalizations. A stepwise management approach is recommended, beginning with psychoeducation and non-pharmacological interventions, followed by referral to mental health professionals when moderate-to-severe symptoms are identified. Integration of mental health assessment into routine cardiovascular care may facilitate early detection, improve adherence, and ultimately enhance overall clinical outcomes [43–45,48–50,56].

9. Limitations

Despite growing recognition of the heart–brain axis in PH-CHD, several limitations in current evidence should be acknowledged. Most available data are derived from cross-sectional studies or extrapolated from PAH and heart failure populations, limiting causal inference. Longitudinal studies evaluating the temporal relationship between cardiovascular dysfunction and neuropsychological outcomes remain scarce. In addition, neuroimaging data specific to PH-CHD are limited, and standardized biomarkers reflecting neurohormonal or neuroinflammatory activity are not yet established in clinical practice. Future research should focus on prospective, multimodal studies integrating hemodynamic, neuroimaging, and psychological assessments to better characterize this complex interaction [40–42,48,52].

10. Conclusions

Depression and anxiety are highly prevalent comorbidities in adult patients with PAH-CHD. These are now perceived as biological consequences of chronic cardiovascular dysfunction, rather than reactions to psychological experiences. Through neurohormonal activation, inflammation, hypoxia, and autonomic dysregulation, pulmonary vascular disease and right ventricular failure have a direct effect on brain structure and function as components of the heart–brain axis. Optimization of pulmonary hemodynamics, right ventricular function, and autonomic balance, combined with treatment for mental health, is necessary to improve quality of life, medication compliance, and long-term outcomes in PH-CHD.
