Evaluation of peripheral blood stem cell collection in the Japan Marrow Donor Program (JMDP)
Authors: Rie Yamazaki, Kimikazu Yakushijin, Satoshi Yoshihara, Kazuhiko Ikeda, Akaru Ishida, Michiko Kajiwara, Atsushi Kikuta, Naoe Takagi, Tokiko Nagamura‐Inoue, Noriko Namba, Kyoko Haraguchi, Yoshiaki Fujimori, Shin‐ichiro Fujiwara, Minami Yamada‐Fujiwara, Yoshiki Okuyama, Ryuji Tanosaki, Yasunori Ueda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) collection of unrelated donors, the Japan Marrow Donor Program (JMDP) sets the target CD34+ cell dose as the minimum of 2 × 106 cells/kg of recipient body weight (RBW) and recommends a processing blood volume (PBV) as high as 200 mL/kg of donor body weight (DBW) with ≤250 mL/kg per day within two consecutive days. Here, we retrospectively reviewed this guideline.Study Design and MethodsWe analyzed 1656 unrelated donor PBSC collections between January 2012 and November 2022 from the JMDP database.ResultsThe target CD34+ cell dose was achieved within 2 days in all but 40 cases (2.4%), suggesting that the current JMDP guideline largely meets its objectives. The distribution of PBV per DBW showed three peaks. The most prominent peak, consisting of 834 (50.4%) cases, occurred around the recommended PBV (180–220 mL/kg of DBW), whereas 659 (39.8%) cases were in the lower PBV range (<180 mL/kg), and, notably, there was no significant difference between these groups in the collected CD34+ cells/kg RBW. On the other hand, among the 163 (9.8%) cases in the higher PBV group (>220 mL/kg), the number of collected CD34+ cells/kg of RBW was significantly lower than in the other two groups.DiscussionThese results suggest that the target PBV in the lower and higher PBV groups was determined using CD34+ enumeration before or during apheresis procedure. CD34+ enumeration may help reduce the burden on donors, although further investigations are needed to confirm.

INTRODUCTION

Since the Japan Marrow Donor Program (JMDP) started performing peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) transplantation from unrelated donors in 2010, more than 1700 PBSC collections have been conducted at 134 accredited facilities in Japan. JMDP adopted 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of recipient body weight (RBW) as the minimal number of PBSCs for successful engraftment regardless of the underlying disease and recommended that more than that number of CD34+ cells should be collected by apheresis procedure within two consecutive days starting on the fourth or the fifth day of granulocyte colony stimulation factor （G‐CSF） administration. Based on the accumulated data of the related PBSC collections, JMDP further recommended that the target processing blood volume (PBV) should be 200 mL/kg of donor body weight (DBW) in a single PBSC apheresis session, with an upper limit of 250 mL/kg to secure the target CD34+ cell dose. It was necessary to use PBV as a surrogate guideline for the target CD34+ cell dose because it was not possible to determine CD34+ cell counts by flow cytometry during the procedure, especially in apheresis facilities where the flow cytometry assay could not be performed at any time. JMDP restricted the venipuncture sites and included the possibility of obtaining access lines with 18‐gauge needles in the veins of the cubital fossa among the donor eligibility criteria because there had been several reports of deaths associated with CV catheter insertion via the subclavian or internal jugular veins. 1 , 2 , 3 On the day of apheresis procedure, if blood access cannot be secured from the cubital veins despite all attempts, the JMDP guidelines permit temporary CV catheter insertion via the femoral vein. Because the maximum blood flow will be limited via peripheral veins, it is speculated that it will take too much time to complete apheresis procedure—especially in cases of low blood flow or heavy donors—without using CD34+ cell enumeration. Since the JMDP guidelines did not specify a maximum processing time, certain donors inevitably experienced prolonged apheresis procedure. The main purpose of this study was to elucidate whether the target setting of PBV is appropriate by investigating the real‐world data of unrelated donor PBSC collections performed in Japan.

METHODS

Donors

Among the 1704 cases in which PBSC collection was performed between January 2012 and November 2022 through the JMDP, PBSC collections from 1656 unrelated healthy donors were included in the analysis after excluding cases with missing data, procedure interruptions due to equipment failure, or donor‐related issues. Demographic, procedural, and product information for all eligible donors was collected from the JMDP database, which was provided by the JMDP Data Center. This study was conducted with the approval of the JMDP Ethics Committee.

Peripheral blood stem cell collection according to JMDP guidelines

PBSC collection was conducted in accordance with the JMDP guidelines. Eligible unrelated donors were 20–55 years of age. Stem cell mobilization was achieved with daily subcutaneous injections of filgrastim (400 μg/m2) or lenograstim (10 μg/kg); dose modification was required if the leukocyte or platelet counts exceeded thresholds.

The first apheresis session was performed on Day 4 or 5 after G‐CSF initiation according to institutional discretion, with a maximum of two consecutive sessions. The decision for a second collection was based on the CD34+ yield from the first‐day apheresis product. Each session targeted a processed blood volume of 200 mL/kg (maximum 250 mL/kg), using anticoagulant citrate dextrose solution ‐A (ACD‐A) as anticoagulant.

No specific apheresis device was mandated, but institutions were required to use their own cell separators under written standard operating phrocedures (SOPs) with continuous medical supervision. Peripheral venous access at the antecubital fossae was preferred; if inadequate, femoral vein catheterization was permitted with donor consent, while subclavian and internal jugular access was prohibited.

CD34 measurement

In Japan, some institutions are unable to measure CD34+ counts on site and therefore outsource the testing to external laboratories. The JMDP does not mandate in‐house capability for CD34 measurement. If CD34+ cell enumeration cannot be performed on site, a sample obtained from the collection bag is sent to an external laboratory. A preliminary report is usually available by the evening of the first apheresis day, and the result is used to determine whether a subsequent collection will be performed on the following day.

Statistical analysis

Differences between two groups were compared using Fisher's exact test or the Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. A Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess the overall differences among three groups due to the non‐parametric nature of the data. Following a significant result from the Kruskal–Wallis test, post‐hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Steel–Dwass test to identify specific intergroup differences while controlling for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value <.05. All statistical analyses were performed with EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). More precisely, it is a modified version of R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics. 4

RESULTS

Overview of PBSC collection from unrelated donors on the first apheresis day

The median age of donors was 39 years (range: 20–55 years), with 71.6% male (Table 1, Figures 1 and 2, n = 1656). The median procedure time was 245 min (range: 47–522 min), with 54 cases (3.3%) requiring more than 6 h. The median PBV was 11,500 mL (range: 3406–22,578 mL), and the median PBV per DBW was 195 mL/kg (range: 47–294 mL/kg). In 276 cases (16.7%), fewer than 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of RBW were collected in the first apheresis procedure, and 240 cases (14.5%) required the second apheresis the next day. More than 5 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of RBW were collected in 609 (36.8%) cases.

Distribution of PBV per donor body weight on the first apheresis day (n = 1656)

PBV per DBW showed a prominent peak of around 200 mL/kg, along with more gradual peaks at around 120–140 mL/kg and around 250 mL/kg (Figure 3). Therefore, we categorized the cases into three groups: a lower (<180 mL/kg), a recommended (180–220 mL/kg), and a higher (>220 mL/kg) PBV group. The processing time differed significantly among the three groups (203 vs. 265 vs. 295 min, p < .001) (Figure 4A). The collected CD34+ cell dose per RBW was significantly lower in the higher PBV group (3.96 × 106 vs. 4.30 × 106 vs. 2.70 × 106 cells/kg, p < .001), but there was no significant difference between the lower and recommended PBV groups (p = .66) (Figure 4B).

In 276 (16.7%) cases, the CD34+ cell doses were below the target of 2 × 106 CD34+ cells, and 240 of these cases (14.5%) proceeded to the second‐day apheresis. The reasons for not performing a second apheresis despite a yield below the target were varied, but the primary ones were that the stem cell count was nevertheless considered sufficient or that adverse events such as liver dysfunction, difficulty in securing venous access, fever, or thrombocytopenia prevented a second collection. In the 276 cases that did not reach the CD34+ cell target, PBV was distributed in the same manner as for cases overall: 87 (31.5%), 124 (44.9%), and 65 (23.6%) cases in the lower, recommended, and higher PBV groups, respectively.

Collection schedule and CD34+ cell counts

PBSC collections were initiated on Day 4 of G‐CSF administration in 1186 cases (71%), while 469 cases (29%) were started on Day 5 (Figure 5). Among those who began PBSC collection on Day 4 of G‐CSF administration, 18.2% (216 cases) required 2‐day apheresis, compared to only 5.1% (24 cases) among those who started on Day 5 of G‐CSF administration. Furthermore, cases where PBSC collection started on Day 5 of G‐CSF administration had significantly higher CD34+ cell counts compared to those that started on Day 4 (3.55 × 106 vs. 4.88 × 106 cells/kg, p < .001). When comparing the CD34+ cell doses in the second PBSC collection on Day 5 (n = 216) versus Day 6 (n = 24) of G‐CSF administration, median CD34+ cell doses were significantly lower in the cases on Day 6 (1.80 × 106 vs. 0.90 × 106 cells/kg, p < .001).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the current practice of PBSC collection from unrelated donors in Japan and evaluated the guideline for unrelated PBSC collection by JMDP.

The required number of CD34+ cells/kg of DBW for allogeneic peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (PBSCT) is inconsistent worldwide. Some studies suggested a correlation between higher stem cell doses and prolonged survival and reported that lower CD34+ cells were associated with poor outcome. 5 , 6 However, other research indicated an increased risk of acute and chronic graft‐versus‐host disease in patients receiving higher stem cell doses. 7 , 8 In Japan, a dose of 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of DBW is commonly used, and some studies suggest that this number is adequate. 9 , 10 In our present analysis, only 40 cases (2.4%) did not achieve the goal of 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of RBW within 2 days, suggesting that the current JMDP guideline largely meets its objectives. PBSC collection from unrelated donors adhered to the guidelines by JMDP, 11 sometimes resulting in prolonged collection times. While PBV was in the JMDP‐recommended range of 180–220 mL/kg of DBW in 50.4% of all the apheresis procedures, 39.8% of PBSC collections were completed with PBV below 180 mL/kg.

A survey conducted by the Cytapheresis Working Group of the Japanese Society of Transfusion Medicine and Cell Therapy revealed that 47 out of 140 (34%) facilities measured CD34+ cell counts or hematopoietic progenitor cell（HPC）in intermediate products during collection, and 39 (83%) facilities adjusted PBV based on these measurements. 12 It was also revealed that 41 out of 136 (30%) facilities determined the total PBV from the pre‐apheresis PB‐CD34+ cell concentration. 12 The fact that some collections ended before reaching the recommended PBV with appropriate CD34+ cell doses suggests that those apheresis facilities probably adjusted PBV by CD34+ cell enumeration, though there may have been some cases in which the donor's physical condition made it difficult to continue.

The National Marrow Donor Program reported that the product yield of donors was highly correlated with the pre‐apheresis CD34+ cell count in their peripheral blood. 13 PBV may be adjusted based on pre‐apheresis CD34+ cell counts and the collection efficiency of each facility. 14 , 15 If an adequate number of stem cells could be reliably collected while reducing the collection time, it would be possible to reduce not only the donor burden but also the cost and manpower for PBSC collection. In addition, physicians could determine to increase PBV up to the maximum allowance from the obtained CD34+ counts. For donors showing poor mobilization on Day 4 of G‐CSF administration, a 2‐day collection with a short apheresis session on Day 4 could be an effective strategy to ensure adequate CD34+ cell collection. Starting apheresis procedure on Day 4 rather than Day 5 may be preferable to reduce G‐CSF exposure and optimize yield, even if multi‐day collection is required. 16 However, Hsu et al. concluded that a 2‐day collection is associated with a higher risk of donor toxic events compared to a 1‐day collection. 17

Although Halter et al. reported that the incidence of severe adverse events and mortality is higher in PBSC collection than in marrow harvesting, unrelated PBSC collection is generally safe. In their study, all four reported deaths following PBSC collection occurred in related donors, suggesting that the eligibility criteria may differ for unrelated donors. 18 Previous reports from Japan indicate that the incidence of apheresis‐related severe adverse events ranges from 0.06% to 0.58%. 19 , 20 The restriction of the venipuncture sites is suitable from the standpoint of safety, and the recommendation to start collection on Day 4 is reasonable. Although it is sometimes difficult to increase the flow rate in apheresis with peripheral blood access, a slow but stable flow has been shown to contribute to stable collection efficiency. 21 Nonetheless, it is problematic that the donor weight is used to decide the PBV, and the processing time tends to be too long.

There are three main limitations in this study. First, while the data were collected from the national registry, detailed background information for the individual cases was not available. Therefore, there is no direct evidence that the facilities measuring CD34+ cells achieved higher CD34+ cell yields in a shorter processing time. Second, although the external quality assessment of CD34+ cell counts has been conducted at many apheresis‐site laboratories in Japan, the data are not yet sufficiently standardized and there may be inter‐facility variability. 22 Third, while a long collection time is clearly a burden for donors, it remains unclear whether it is correlated with an increase in adverse events. The JMDP has established a sensitive monitoring system for any adverse events that may develop in association with PBSC collection, and all adverse events have been published on the JMDP website, but there have been no complications directly attributable to prolonged apheresis procedure.

Based on our present analysis, we propose the following approach regarding the target PBV for PBSC collection from unrelated donors. First, while the guidelines state that the goal is to collect 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of RBW, it is important to explicitly clarify that the collection can be terminated once the CD34+ cell count reaches the target dose irrespective of PBV. It must be definitively proven that 2 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg of RBW is sufficient. Some reports indicated that the infusion of higher doses of CD34+ cells may improve survival and decrease morbidity in recipients, 6 , 23 while Yamamoto et al. reported that the allogeneic PBSC transplantation with low CD34+ cell doses of 1–2 × 106 cells/kg of RBW resulted in acceptable transplantation results. 24 For that purpose, we also recommend the measurement of CD34+ cell counts in intermediate products or in peripheral blood before apheresis procedure. Adjusting the PBV based on the CD34+ cell counts could be beneficial. However, this approach requires that CD34+ cell counts be measured at the appropriate time points using appropriate methods at each hospital, and thus it is essential that the CD34+ measurement procedures be standardized across institutions.

Second, we suggest that the required PBV should be calculated based on the RBW rather than DBW. This approach is also adopted in the national marrow donor program (NMDP) protocol. 25 We propose that 200 mL/kg of RBW is an appropriate target. Using the present sample dataset, we examined whether redefining the standard processed volume as 200 mL/kg of RBW instead of DBW would affect the rate of insufficient collections. We found that the proportion changed only slightly from 15.5% to 14.7%, suggesting that this adjustment would be unlikely to compromise collection success. Nonetheless, to ensure donor safety, it is crucial to adhere strictly to the current guideline, which limits the maximum PBV to 250 mL/kg of DBW.

Third, the maximum permitted processing time should be decided for donor safety. Apheresis procedure should not continue for over 6 h. In our analysis, there were 54 cases whose processing times were more than 6 h. In 18 of these cases (33.3%), total CD34+ cells of over 5 × 106/kg of RBW were collected (Figure 2), while in 15 cases (27.7%) the CD34+ cells did not reach 2 × 106/kg of RBW, and 2‐day apheresis was required.

In unrelated donor PBSC collections, adverse events may result not only from apheresis procedure but also from G‐CSF administration. Due to the recent application of long‐acting G‐CSF agents to PBSC mobilization, the collection conditions are likely to be diversified further. In conclusion, based on the results of the present study, we propose a revision of the JMDP guidelines for PBSC collection to ensure donor safety.
