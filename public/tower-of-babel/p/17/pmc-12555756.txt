Primary herpes Simplex Virus type 1 pneumonitis in a patient after bilateral lung transplantation
Authors: Genichiro Fujioka, Julia Hwang, Uriel Sandkovsky, Haiying Zhang, Tiana Endicott, Katherine Vandervest, Todd Grazia, Chetan Naik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHerpes Simplex Virus type 1 (HSV-1) can cause disseminated, systemic infections in immunocompromised patients. HSV-1 pneumonitis is a rare pulmonary infection that is associated with high morbidity and mortality. There is a dearth of published literature on clinical characteristics and outcomes of primary HSV-1 pneumonitis in lung transplant recipients.Clinical caseWe describe case of primary HSV-1 infection in an immunocompromised patient immediately after lung transplantation. Introduction

Herpes Simplex Virus type-1 (HSV-1) is a neurotropic dsDNA virus primarily associated with mucocutaneous lesions in the oral and genital areas, as well as meningitis and encephalitis in more severe cases [1]. Although most primary infections are limited to the upper respiratory tract, HSV-1 can spread to the lower respiratory tract. The prevalence rates of HSV-1 DNA in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples vary from 4.3 % to 62 % and the rates are higher in ICU patients, immunocompromised or patients dependent on mechanical ventilation [2], [3], [4]. HSV-1 detected in BAL may be an innocent bystander or could be a true pathogen, especially among immunocompromised patients causing severe pneumonitis associated with high mortality [5]. There is scant information available on the clinical course and outcomes of HSV-1 pneumonitis among lung transplant recipients as a result of universal prophylaxis. We present a case of donor- derived HSV-1 pneumonitis in the immediate post-lung transplant period that was treated successfully. Despite successful therapy, patient subsequently developed early-onset chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD).

Clinical case

Patient is a 60-year-old female who underwent bilateral lung transplantation for cellular nonspecific interstitial pneumonia. Serological testing 5 months prior to transplant were positive for CMV IgG, HSV-2 IgG positive (IgM negative) but negative for HSV-1 IgG (Liaison-XL assay, Diasorin). She was transplanted on central veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenator (VA-ECMO) via bilateral anterolateral sterno- thoracotomy incisions. Induction immunosuppression included Basiliximab, methylprednisolone 1000 mg, and mycophenolate mofetil 1000 mg. A standard triple immunosuppression regimen consisting of tacrolimus (goal 11–14 ng/ml), mycophenolate mofetil 1000 mg twice daily, and steroids was initiated post-transplantation. Intravenous vancomycin and cefepime were initiated as prophylaxis for post-operative infections. The patient had a mild, grade 1 primary graft dysfunction (PGD) on post-op day 1. After a routine surveillance bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage, and she was successfully extubated and required oxygen at 5 L/min. On post-op day 2, she developed worsening hypoxemia and progressive infiltrates bilaterally that did not improve with diuresis (Fig. 1). She continued to deteriorate and required re-intubation on post-op day 4. Due to a concern for acute cellular rejection, she underwent bedside bronchoscopy with transbronchial biopsy and broncho-alveolar lavage. Cytomegalovirus and HSV-1 were detected by PCR in the BAL sample from post-op day 1 but routine cultures for other pathogens namely bacterial, fungal, acid-fast bacilli, nocardia and pneumocystis remained negative. Intravenous ganciclovir was promptly initiated to treat HSV-1 and CMV pneumonitis. Repeat BAL PCR on Post-op day 3 was negative for CMV but remained positive for HSV-1. Qualitative blood PCR testing was negative for CMV but was positive for HSV-1. Serum HSV-specific glycoprotein antibodies were positive for HSV-2 but not HSV-1. The transbronchial biopsies revealed acute necrotizing pneumonitis with diffuse alveolar damage (acute lung injury pattern, seen in clinical ARDS) and herpes viral inclusions. The HSV immunoperoxidase was positive and CMV immunostain was negative. (Fig. 2) There were no evidence of HSV-1 infection outside the lungs. The dose of mycophenolate mofetil was lowered to 500 mg, IV ganciclovir was changed to IV acyclovir and oral valganciclovir was also initiated for CMV prophylaxis. Blood HSV-1 PCR became negative twelve days after initiation of antiviral therapy, however, HSV-1 continued to be detected in BAL samples for 6 more weeks. Patient was continued on IV acyclovir for 2 more weeks after HSV-1 was undetectable in the BAL fluid after which, valganciclovir was initiated for CMV prophylaxis. Patient was decannulated from VV-ECMO at 9 days and weaned off mechanical ventilation at day 39 post-transplantation. She was eventually discharged on day 63 post-transplant breathing room air. She developed de novo donor specific antibodies (DSA) 2 months after transplant that persisted despite multiple treatments for antibody mediated rejection. She developed chronic lung allograft dysfunction – bronchiolitis obliterans (CLAD-BOS) 18 months after her lung transplant. Patient is now 5 years post-lung transplant and is living with stage 3 CLAD-BOS with persistent DSAs. Patient did not develop recurrence of HSV-1 infection after the initial episode.

Discussion

We describe a rare case of primary HSV-1 infection post-lung transplantation. Our patient’s serum HSV-1 glycoprotein antibodies were negative pre-transplant indicating that this was a primary HSV-1 infection. Primary HSV-1 infection usually results in a self- limiting upper respiratory tract infection among immunocompetent patients, usually followed by latent infection of the sensory ganglia, resulting in a life-long carrier state. Reactivation of HSV-1 infection can occur at any time and is usually precipitated by stress, trauma, critical illness, or immunosuppression [6]. HSV-1 can cause severe pneumonitis in immunocompromised patients associated with a high mortality rate of up to 60 % [8]. Our patient developed severe necrotizing pneumonitis with HSV-1 along with HSV-1 viremia, likely as a result of critical illness and induction immunosuppression. Concomitant infection with CMV has been described in immunocompromised patients and is associated with a worse prognosis [7], This is attributed to the depression of cell-mediated immunity in the setting of acute CMV infection that may contribute to a severe HSV infection [9], [10]. However, our patient’s CMV PCR detection in the BAL likely reflected viral shedding rather than a true infection.

Real-time PCR assays are very sensitive facilitating prompt diagnosis but carry low specificity in BAL samples due to the high prevalence rates of HSV-1 in BAL, or carried from the oropharynx or upper airways particularly among severely ill patients. Detection of HSV DNA in blood indicates disseminated systemic infection. Detection of viral inclusions from tissue samples confirms a visceral infection. In our patient, HSV DNA was detected in the BAL and serum by PCR, and viral inclusions were noted on transbronchial biopsy specimens, indicating pneumonitis [11]. Although disseminated infection leading to pneumonitis is possible, our patient more likely developed pneumonitis and subsequently viremia.

Acyclovir at 10 mg/kg three times a day is the drug of choice used to treat systemic infections [12]. Our patient was initially treated with intravenous ganciclovir to cover both HSV-1 and CMV detected in the BAL but antiviral therapy was transitioned to IV acyclovir once CMV PCR was no longer detected and HSV-1 pneumonitis was confirmed. In cases with suspected acyclovir resistance, foscarnet or cidofovir can be alternatives. ARDS is a known complication of HSV pneumonitis requiring supportive care with mechanical ventilation and in some cases, VV-ECMO support. VV-ECMO support allows for low-tidal volume mechanical ventilation, reducing the risks of barotrauma, atelectrauma, ergotrauma, and volutrauma [13]. Our patient developed ARDS and required VV-ECMO support for 9 days to allow for lung protective mechanical ventilation.

Community-acquired viral (CARV) pneumonia in lung transplant recipients is associated with wide spectrum of immune mediated lung injuries including acute cellular rejection, de novo donor-specific antibodies (DSA) resulting in antibody mediated rejection and chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), resulting in graft loss [14], [15]. CARV infections can trigger production of cytokines that orchestrate the immune mediated lung allograft injury [16].

Conclusion

HSV-1 pneumonitis is a rare infection with a fulminant course associated with high morbidity and mortality. Prompt diagnosis and treatment are necessary for successful outcomes. In lung transplant recipients, HSV-1 pneumonitis may potentially be associated with de novo DSAs and early graft loss.
