Gut microbiota dysbiosis and neurologic diseases: New Horizon with potential diagnostic and therapeutic impact
Authors: Ali Keshavarzian, Sangram S Sisodia
Published: 2024

As our society ages, there is a growing appreciation of an increase in the incidence and prevalence of many neurological disorders that include neurodegenerative diseases. Indeed, two major neurodegenerative disorders, Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) are now the leading causes of morbidity and health care costs in the USA [1,2]. Unfortunately, despite large investments, there have been no breakthroughs in early detection, or therapeutic approaches for these devastating chronic disorders. There are several potential reasons for this failure including: (1) the vast majority of research that has focused on identifying diagnostic and therapeutic approaches has been brain-centric. By the time patients with these disorders develop brain-related symptoms such as cognitive deficits in AD, motor deficits in PD, muscle atrophy and weakness in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), there is already major neuronal loss (up to 80 ​%) in brain regions that are critical for function, thus making successful early diagnosis or disease modifying interventions highly suspect; (2) immune-directed investigations have not been the focus of neurological disease research, although recent studies suggest that aberrant immunoinflammatory biological processes are the initial pathological events in AD, PD and ALS, diseases that were not traditionally considered as immune-mediated disorders; (3) suboptimal attention to peripheral organs where these early pathological events appear to start outside the brain; and (4) the underappreciated role of environment-host interactions. In this regard, many epidemiological studies have documented that the incidence and prevalence of many neurological disorders varies significantly among different communities that cannot simply be accounted for by genetics alone, suggesting the importance of environmental factors and response of the host to these factors that initiate, or promote disease pathogenesis. Indeed, recent studies suggest that gut and/or oro-nasal cavity might be the port of entry for the host to encounter environmental factors and that microbiota could serve as the “middleman” in modifying the impact of these environmental aspects on the host [3].

The notion that the oro-nasal cavity and the gastrointestinal tract are the primary port of entry for environmental factors to impact host physiology relevant to neurological disorders, and that the microbiota modify risk is not surprising. First, environmental factors, including pesticides and other water or air-borne toxins, are known to be associated with increased risk of neurological disorders [4], and these have easy access through the oro-nasal cavity. In this regard, there is an extensive neuronal communication between the oro-nasal cavity and the brain; the oro-nasal cavity is highly innervated, and the deep nasal cavity, which is termed “olfactory mucosa”, is close to the olfactory bulb. Second, the gastrointestinal tract is the largest interface (32 square meters) between us and our environment [5], and ingested and inhaled environmental factors can have access to the host through the GI tract and initiate multiple biological processes. Third, both the oro-nasal cavity and GI tract are host to the richest and most diverse microbial communities. Indeed, humans carry trillions of bacteria, viruses and fungi in the GI tract with estimates of bacteria/host gene ratio of 100/1 [6]. Furthermore, environmental factors and metabolic disorders that are known to be risk factors for neurological disorders such as stress [7], diet [8], sleep, circadian disruption/shift work [9,10] and metabolic syndrome [11] are also associated with the dysbiotic microbiota community [12,13].

Indeed, multiple cross-sectional studies have documented alterations in oro-nasal and intestinal microbiota (termed “dysbiosis”) in several neurological disorders including AD [14,15], PD [[16], [17], [18]], ALS [19] and multiple sclerosis (MS) [20]. Several experimental studies have also shown that intestinal microbiota contribute to the pathogenesis of neurological diseases including AD, PD and multiple system atrophy [12,21]. However, the identification of “druggable” targets that fully capitalize on what has been learned about the microbiota-gut-brain axis has remained elusive. This is, in part, due to less than optimal understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanism(s) by which the microbiota contribute to the pathogenesis of these neurological disorders. In this special issue of Neurotherapeutics on the “Microbiome in Neurologic Diseases”, the invited authors provide comprehensive and up to date reviews of the role of microbiota in neurological diseases and discuss how this information can be leveraged to develop early diagnostic tools, biomarkers for disease stratification and risk assessment, and disease-modifying therapeutic strategies.

Merchak et al[22] provide evidence for the central role of myeloid cells, including peripheral macrophages and microglia for brain health and neurologic diseases including AD, PD and MS and discuss how intestinal microbiota and neurological disorder-relevant environmental factors influence their functions to promote these neurological disorders. Kaul et al. [23] provide a comprehensive review of the current state of microbiome research in ALS, including interactions with different ALS subtypes, evidence in animal models and human studies, key immunologic and metabolomic mediators, and a path toward microbiome-based therapies for ALS. Sun and Zhang [24] provide further evidence for the importance of the gut-microbiome-neuron axis in ALS pathogenesis and a discussion of recent progress in understanding the impact of the microbiome, that includes bacteria, fungi, and viruses, in ALS research. These authors propose that the mechanistic and translational studies should allow for the development of new diagnostic and treatment strategies for ALS. Zhu et al. [25] summarize studies in the literature regarding the role of microbiota in the pathogenesis of tauopathies, a group of neurodegenerative diseases characterized by abnormal aggregates of the protein tau leading to progressive neuronal and synaptic loss, and eventual cognitive and motor impairment. In this review, the authors highlight the latest efforts investigating the intricate interplay between the gut microbiome and tauopathies and discuss the physiological interactions between the microbiome and the brain, as well as clinical and experimental evidence suggesting that the presence of tauopathy alters the composition of gut microbiota. These authors also explore both animal and human studies that define causative relationships between the gut microbiome and tauopathy by directly manipulating or transferring gut microbiota. Their review highlights future directions into identifying and mechanistically elucidating microbial species causally linked to tauopathies, with the ultimate goal of identifying therapeutic targets against the gut microbiome to treat tauopathies. Kawakami and Wekerle [26] summarize data supporting a role of brain antigen-specific autoreactive T cells in inducing inflammation in the central nervous system (CNS) in MS and describe how microbiota influence autoreactive T cells during their lifespan, starting in the thymus, migrating through the periphery and inducing inflammation in their target organ, the CNS. The data suggest potential “druggable” targets for developing disease modifying strategies for MS. Four reviews in this issue discuss different aspects of the impact of microbiota in Alzheimer disease (AD. Shanmugam et al. [27] review the concept of pathobiomes and how microbes may be involved in AD. Examining the gut-brain axis, microbes with known associations with AD, and existing therapeutic tools that may be utilized, the evidence for and the challenges to the microbial hypothesis are reviewed. The authors posit that the rise in evidence for microbial involvement in AD presents an important hypothesis that could open novel methods of treatment and diagnosis with a more complete elucidation of the microbes and pathways involved. Kang et al. [28] review evidence for the role of gut microbiota metabolites in the pathogenesis of AD and AD-related dementias (ADRD). As discussed by Shanmugam et al. [27], it is now well established that patients with AD and ADRD have disrupted microbiota communities (dysbiosis); how this dysbiosis contributes to the pathogenesis and disease course of AD and ADRD is the focus of this review. The authors posit that these alterations in organismal composition of microbiota result in changes in the abundance of functions encoded by the microbial community, including metabolic capabilities, which likely impact host disease mechanisms. Examples of microbial metabolites that impact brain function and AD relevant processes are short chain fatty acids, secondary bile acids, tryptophan derivatives (such as kynurenine, serotonin, tryptamine, and indoles), and trimethylamine/trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) that can cross the blood-brain-barrier (BBB) and can have various effects on the brain, including modulating the release of neurotransmitters and neuronal function, inducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and impacting synaptic function. Microbial metabolites can also impact the CNS through immune, enteroendocrine, and enteric nervous system pathways. In turn, these perturbations can impact gut barrier function and peripheral immune responses, as well as BBB integrity, neuronal homeostasis and neurogenesis, and glial cell maturation and activation. The authors examine the evidence supporting the notion that ADRD is influenced by gut microbiota and its metabolites. The potential therapeutic advantages of microbial metabolites for preventing and treating ADRD are also discussed, highlighting their potential role in developing new treatments. Chandra and Vassar [29] review the potential importance of the microbiome in the regulation of both reactive astrocytosis and microgliosis in the context of both amyloidosis and tauopathy. The authors summarize evidence regarding the gut microbiome in the control of reactive astrocytosis in AD - a potential therapeutic target to prevent and/or treat early stage AD. Saha and Sisodia [30] delve into three critical areas of sex differences in AD: First, they explore how genetic predisposition and hormonal changes, particularly those involving sex-specific modifications, influence susceptibility and progression of the disease. Second, they examine neuroimmune dynamics in AD, emphasizing variations in microglial activity between sexes during crucial developmental stages and the effects of hormonal interventions on disease outcomes. Crucially, this review highlights the significant role of gut microbiome perturbations in shaping AD pathophysiology in a sex-specific manner, suggesting that these alterations can further influence microglial activity and overall disease trajectories. Third, the authors provide a viewpoint that advocates for personalized therapeutic strategies that integrate the understanding of hormonal fluctuations and microbiome dynamics into treatment plans in order to optimize patient outcomes. Finally, Sampson et al. [31] discuss the role of microbiota in PD. The authors summarize recent experimental and clinical data that demonstrate a significant dysregulation of the gut microbiome in individuals with PD. With a substantial influence on all aspects of physiology, this dysregulation has potential to directly, or indirectly, contribute to disease pathology. Experimental models have bridged these associations toward defined contributions, identifying various microbiome-dependent impacts to PD pathophysiology. These studies have laid the foundation for human translation, examining whether certain members of the microbiome and/or whole restoration of the gut microbiome communities can provide therapeutic benefits for people living with PD. In this review, the authors summarize recent and ongoing clinically-focused studies that use microbiome-targeted therapies to limit the severity and progression of PD. Fecal microbiome transplants, prebiotic interventions, and probiotic supplementation are each emerging as viable methodologies to augment the gut microbiome and potentially limit PD symptoms. While still early, the data in the field to date support continued cross-talk between experimental systems and human studies to identify key microbial factors that contribute to PD pathologies.

In summary, compelling data indicate that the bacteria in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract (i.e., the microbiota) impact neuroinflammation and neurological disease [12,13,32]. There are many mechanisms by which the intestinal microbiota can influence the brain, but bacterial metabolites are increasingly recognized as significant contributors. Among the thousands of bacterial metabolites, short chain fatty acids (SCFA) [33,34], secondary bile acids [35,36], indoxyl-sulfate [37] and indole-3-proprionic acid [38] can impact neuroinflammation, brain function, and brain disease. These metabolites are an exciting topic of research by many groups as potential biomarkers for early diagnosis, risk stratification and therapeutic target(s) for neurologic diseases. The future is promising for a paradigm shift in the management of patients with neurologic disorders, and this special issue of Neurotherapeutics provides roadmaps for this exciting journey.
