Are we ready to predict progressive disease in juvenile systemic sclerosis?
Authors: Ivan Foeldvari, Jens Klotsche, Kathryn S Torok
Published: 2026

Background

Currently, there are no well-validated, prospective disease trajectory models to predict progressive disease in juvenile systemic sclerosis (jSSc). The rarity1 and clinical heterogeneity of jSSc2 pose significant challenges to developing robust predictive tools. However, retrospective cohort studies and clinical observations suggest that specific features present at diagnosis—such as early internal organ involvement (particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD) or cardiac manifestations), rapid progression of skin thickening and younger age at disease onset—may be associated with a more aggressive disease course3 4

To begin addressing this unmet need, our group has leveraged prospectively collected clinical data from the international Juvenile Scleroderma Inception Cohort (jSScIC)2 5 to identify variables that may serve as early indicators of disease trajectory.6 Drawing parallels to the predictive models developed in adult systemic sclerosis (aSSc)712 we propose that similar structured approaches could be applied to jSSc. These efforts aim to support clinicians in making early, informed decisions regarding the need for intensified therapy or monitoring, especially in the current era where autologous stem cell transplant and emerging cellular therapies13 are increasingly considered for severe or refractory disease. The ability to identify patients at greatest risk early in their disease course may be critical for optimising long-term outcomes and minimising irreversible organ damage.

There are currently around 300 patients enrolled in the jSScIC dataset. It is an international observational registry of jSSc which provides standardised measures on disease activity and disease burden including patient-related outcomes in order to identify possible disease trajectories in jSSc. We reviewed clinical data available in the jSScIC dataset, collected between 2008 and 2024. Most data presented is derived from the recent data pull from the cohort, though some organ manifestations or comparisons performed prior for abstract or poster presentations for international rheumatology meetings are also included here.

The Juvenile Scleroderma Inceptions Cohort

The jSSclC cohort data consisted of 268 patients at April 2024. At that time the median time from first non-Raynaud symptom to cohort entry was 2.5 years (IQR 1–4.8), with predominant diffuse cutaneous subtype (70%), female-to-male ratio was 3.4:1 in diffuse and 5:1 in limited subtypes, which is lower than ratios observed in aSSc. Overlap features are present in approximately 20% of patients, primarily with dermatomyositis followed by juvenile idiopathic arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. The median age of onset for Raynaud’s phenomenon is 10.4 years (IQR 7.3–13.0), and the first non-Raynaud symptom appears at a median age of 10.9 years (IQR 7.4–13.4). Although the distinction between diffuse and limited subtypes in jSSc is not as strong as in adults in regard to the frequency of organ involvement, patients with diffuse disease exhibit more severe disease manifestations of certain organ involvement and also based on patient and physician global assessments (table 1).

Candidates for trajectories of rapidly progressive disease in jSSc

The diffuse cutaneous subtype of jSSc stands out as a strong candidate. Despite some overlap in organ presentation patterns between the diffuse and limited subtypes, patients with the diffuse form (n=188 of 268 total) exhibit significantly higher modified Rodnan Skin Scores (mRSS) (p=0.001), increased rates of sclerodactyly (p=0.001), more frequent history of ulcerations (p=0.015), Gottron papules (p=0.032), telangiectasia (p=0.011) and decreased body mass index (BMI) <2 z score (p<0.004) (table 1). Patient-reported outcomes such as global disease activity, disease damage, Raynaud’s phenomenon activity and ulceration activity, measured via Visual Analogue Scale, are significantly higher in the diffuse subtype (p values ranging from 0.028 to 0.001). Physician-assessed outcomes show a similar trend (table 1). As such, both diffuse subtype and increasing skin score, particularly a ≥25% increase in mRSS or ≥2-point increase over 12 months, are strong candidates for modelling disease trajectories.

ILD also emerges as an important candidate. In a prior publication based on the jSScIC, we reported ILD 44% in the overall cohort based on high-resolution CT (HRCT) imaging.14 In our recent cohort analysis, 209 of the 268 patients had HRCT performed for ILD assessment, of those 43% (66/152) of diffuse subtype and 46% (26/57) of the limited subtype demonstrated ILD findings (table 1). Among the 101 patients with jSSc with 24-month follow-up data, 5% (n=5) developed new HRCT findings, though this is likely an underestimate due to limited repeat imaging. Given the potential for lung function deterioration and the known impact on morbidity, ILD progression is a compelling feature of rapidly progressive disease.

The correlation of the gastro-oesophageal involvement and ILD is currently evaluated for a presentation in the upcoming Pediatric Rheumatology European Sociaty (PRES) and American College of Rheumatology (ACR) meetings as for publication. In 279 patients gastro-oesophageal reflux clearly correlates with the occurrence of ILD, with significantly more number of patients with decreased functional vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) <80 and higher number of patients with positive HRCT findings for ILD.

Cardiac involvement, the leading cause of mortality in systemic sclerosis in children, was observed in 3% (5/188) of diffuse and 10% (8/80) of limited subtype patients (p>0.05). Despite its low prevalence, the severe clinical consequences of cardiac involvement render it a critical feature for consideration in progressive disease trajectories. New cardiac manifestations developed in 2 of the 101 patients with jSSc during the 24-month follow-up.

Renal involvement, characterised primarily by proteinuria, occurred in 3% of patients in both subtypes (table 1), with no cases of scleroderma renal crisis and only one case of hypertension reported among 268 patients. Given the low frequency and mild presentation, renal involvement appears to be a less important factor in the rapid progression in jSSc.

Male sex has emerged as a notable risk factor for severe disease.15 In our earlier cohort publication of 175 patients with jSSc (142 females and 33 males), males were more likely to present with active digital ulcers, significantly lower BMI and tendon friction rubs. Physician global assessments of disease severity and digital ulcer activity were significantly higher in males. Although composite pulmonary involvement (based on FVC, DLCO and HRCT) was more common in males, it did not reach statistical significance.15 Still, the trend suggests male sex may be a predictor of more aggressive disease and should be included in trajectory models.

Nailfold capillaroscopy (NFC) findings also hold potential. In a previous analysis of the cohort including 237 patients16 (figure 1) (185 female, 126 with diffuse subtype), 77% were classified as having pathological nailfold changes from the treating physician (NF+).17 Nailfold changes could be only categorised based on the available current data in the cohort as simple normal/abnormal dichotomy, as not all centres were using the same magnification to describe the findings. NF+ patients had significantly more frequent Raynaud’s phenomenon (96% vs 78%, p<0.001), digital ulcers (59% vs 27%, p<0.001), abnormal HRCT findings (49% vs 30%, p=0.034), gastrointestinal involvement (49% vs 20%, p<0.001), oesophageal involvement (47% vs 19%, p<0.001), musculoskeletal involvement (71% vs 41%, p=0.003), decreased joint range of motion (63% vs 45%, p=0.022) and muscle weakness (25% vs 3%, p=0.002). These associations highlight NFC as a promising early biomarker of disease severity and trajectory.

BMI is another potential predictor. At EULAR (European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology) 202318 (n=232), we showed that patients with BMI z-scores < −2 reported significantly higher patient-perceived disease damage (p=0.004), and had more frequent puffy fingers, sclerodactyly and Gottron papules (all p<0.001). Although we did not observe increased organ involvement, the strong correlation with cutaneous severity and perceived disease damage suggests that decreased BMI may signal aggressive disease.

We also investigated the role of anti-Scl-70. In a poster presented at the 2024 World Scleroderma Congress (n=225), we found no major differences in organ involvement between anti-Scl-70-positive and anti-Scl-70-negative patients.19 However, anti-Scl-70-positive patients reported significantly higher Raynaud’s activity (p<0.05) and disease damage (p<0.005), suggesting limited utility of SSc-specific autoantibodies as predictors in disease trajectory models for jSSc.

Recognising the limitations of adult-derived disease activity indices in paediatrics, we evaluated the European Scleroderma Study Group Index,20 its modified version, and the revised European Scleroderma Trials and Research Index21 in jSSc.6 None of these scores demonstrated clear superiority or reliability in our paediatric cohort. Thus, the development of paediatric-specific disease activity measures is essential.

To further explore predictors of disease progression, we applied the Composite Response Index in Systemic Sclerosis (CRISS)22, revised CRISS (rCRISS)22 and the RCID23 (responder composite index for diffuse cutaneous SSc) in 95 patients with jSSc with diffuse subtype and 12-month follow-up (table 2). While the ACR CRISS demonstrated ceiling and floor effects, both the rCRISS and RCID discriminated between patients who reported clinical improvement versus worsening.6 Skin score and FVC were the most influential variables in CRISS scoring, highlighting them as potential core predictors of disease trajectory. The RCID showed promise for evaluating treatment response and could serve as a meaningful outcome measure in future jSSc clinical trials. Importantly, future research should aim to determine paediatric-specific weights for CRISS and RCID components rather than relying on adult extrapolations.

Conclusion

In summary, multiple clinical features—including diffuse subtype, ILD progression, cardiac involvement, male sex, decreased BMI, nailfold capillary abnormalities and skin score progression—stand out as strong candidates for identifying trajectories of rapidly progressive disease in jSSc. Meanwhile, traditional autoantibody status and renal involvement appear to play a lesser role. The development and validation of paediatric-specific trajectory models are essential to guide timely and targeted interventions, particularly as more aggressive treatments such as cellular therapies become available. These therapies offer promise but also carry risks, underscoring the need to identify the right patients at the right time for optimal outcomes.
