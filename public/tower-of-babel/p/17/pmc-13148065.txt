Structural and functional properties of gluten proteins in air-classified sprouted wheat flour and their impact on steamed gluten quality
Authors: Jun Pan, Shaoying Li, Fengcheng Wang, Jie Zheng, Zhiyi Liu, Ke Bian, Zhenxiang Guo, Jiangtao Lin, Jiping Wen, Xiaoxiao Qi, Jia Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sprouting markedly alters the properties of wheat gluten proteins, thereby influencing their functional behavior during steaming. In this study, sprouted wheat flour was separated by air classification into three particle-size fractions: F1 (>45 μm), F2 (20–45 μm), and F3 (<20 μm). The intermolecular interactions, molecular weight distribution, functional properties, and dynamic rheological behavior of gluten proteins in each fraction were systematically analyzed, and steamed gluten was prepared to evaluate structural and textural properties. Sprouting destroys the molecular integrity of partial gluten proteins (sprouting-induced damaged proteins, SDP), alters non-covalent interactions, and promotes disordered aggregation. Air classification achieves differential enrichment of SDP. F2, enriched in partial SDP, results in steamed gluten with uniform pore structure and optimal textural properties. F3, enriched in extensive SDP, also exhibits good steaming performance. F1 is unsuitable for steamed gluten production due to its excessive elasticity and inadequate extensibility.

Introduction

Wheat is prone to sprouting when exposed to prolonged rainfall during harvest or when insufficiently dried during storage. During sprouting, endogenous hydrolytic enzymes are activated, among which proteases induce partial hydrolysis of gluten proteins, disrupting their molecular integrity and altering molecular weight distribution. Accompanied by protein conformational loosening, the stability of non-covalent interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds) that maintain the ordered assembly of gluten proteins is weakened, leading to increased exposure of hydrophobic groups (Chen, Zuo, Li, Men, & Lian, 2024). These changes are unfavorable for the formation of stable viscoelastic structures and ultimately lead to a decline in gluten network functionality, thereby limiting the application of sprouted wheat flour in the food industry (Dai & An, 2024). Air classification is a mature dry physical fractionation technology that has been widely applied to separate cereal and legume powders into fractions differing in particle size and composition. Previous studies have demonstrated that, by optimizing air classification parameters, effective enrichment of protein and starch into different particle-size fractions can be achieved without the use of chemical reagents or thermal treatment. For example, in pulse flours, fine fractions are typically enriched in protein, whereas coarse fractions are mainly starch-rich (Wetterauw et al., 2025). It has been reported that regulating particle-size flour distribution can improve the structure and quality of various wheat-based products. For instance, in quick-frozen dumpling wrappers, smaller particle-size flours have been reported to enhance elasticity and mitigate quality deterioration during freeze–thaw cycles (Yin et al., 2024). Previous research indicates that air classification significantly improved the fermentation tolerance and gas-holding capacity of gluten proteins in coarse wheat flour fractions, thereby increasing bread volume. However, the structural characteristics of gluten proteins in medium and fine particle-size fractions and their performance in specific food processing applications remain insufficiently understood (Guo et al., 2023; Guo, Feng, & Bian, 2023).

Currently, the primary method for improving the quality of sprouted wheat involves inhibiting the activity of hydrolytic enzymes (including traditional heating and microwave treatment) to control further deterioration of wheat flour. However, these methods often lead to an imbalance in the functional components of gluten proteins, altering their water absorption, viscoelasticity, and extensibility. (Liu et al., 2019; Wang, Zhao, Shang, Liu, & Zhao, 2024). As a mild non-thermal dry separation technology, air classification does not exert additional interference on the molecular structure of gluten proteins, but rather achieves physical separation via particle size classification, thereby resulting in different contents of sprouting-induced damaged proteins (SDP) in different particle size fractions.

Generally speaking, the endosperm structure subjected to sprout-induced hydrolysis becomes looser, thus being more prone to form fine powder during the milling process. Therefore, from the perspective of physical separation, the application of air classification to distinguish particle-size fractions provides a promising approach to elucidate the particle-size-dependent distribution of sprouting-induced gluten protein damage and its potential utilization pathways. Nevertheless, systematic investigation of the effects of air classification on the intermolecular interactions, physicochemical properties, and final product quality of gluten proteins in sprouted wheat remains unclear.

Gluten is a high-protein, low-fat food material with substantial market demand, with the market size in China projected to exceed 18 billion CNY by 2025. The quality of steamed gluten products is highly dependent on the balance between the structural and functional characteristics of gluten proteins. A relatively weaker gluten structure may contribute to improving the textural quality of steamed gluten.

Therefore, this study applied air classification to sprouted wheat flour to obtain three particle-size fractions: F1 (>45 μm), F2 (20–45 μm), and F3 (<20 μm). Unsprouted wheat flour (WF) and sprouted whole flour (SF) served as control samples. The aim was to systematically elucidate the enrichment of sprout-induced gluten protein damage in different particle size fractions. By characterizing the intermolecular interactions, molecular weight distribution, functional properties, and dynamic rheological properties of gluten proteins, this study further investigates the relationship between these properties and the cellular structure and textural properties of steamed gluten. The present research aims to provide a theoretical basis for the graded utilization of sprouted wheat flour and its rational application in the steamed gluten system.

Materials and methods

Materials

The wheat seeds of Bainong 307 (Water content: 10.7%, protein content: 14.2%, starch content: 68.6%) were purchased from Henan Qiule Seed Industry Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). All chemicals and biochemical reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and Tianjin Kermel Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China), respectively.

Sample preparation

Preparation of sprouted wheat

Based on preliminary trials, sprouting conditions were selected as follows. After impurities were removed, wheat grains were soaked at 25 °C for 4 h, drained, and spread on trays. The seeds were then placed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (LRH-550-S, Shaoguan Taihong Medical Device Co., Ltd., Shaoguan, Guangdong, China) and cultured at 20 °C with a relative humidity of 85% for 12 h. Subsequently, they were dried at 45 °C for 12 h to reduce the moisture content to below 13% and stored at room temperature (RT).

Preparation of wheat flour

Wheat moisture conditioning was performed at RT for 24 h, with a target moisture content of 15%. Then, the wheat was ground using a mill (MLU-202, Buhler Group, Switzerland), and the flour from each system was collected and mixed. A portion of the straight-grade flour (SF) was further fractionated using an air classifier (AF, Shandong Weifang Elite Flour Engineering Co., Ltd., Weifang, Shandong, China) to obtain three particle-size fractions: F1 (>45 μm), F2 (20–45 μm), and F3 (<20 μm).

Preparation of gluten protein

Gluten protein was extracted from wheat flour using the water-washing method. The gluten dough was vacuum freeze-dried (Freezone6 plus, Labconco, USA) for 48 h, pulverized, sieved through an 80-mesh sieve, and sealed in a refrigerator at 4 °C for future use.

Size exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-HPLC)

Samples containing 1 mg of protein were mixed with 1.0 mL extraction buffer (0.05 M sodium phosphate, pH 6.8, containing 1.5% SDS) and extracted by shaking for 2 h. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic filter into a sample vial. Non-reducing conditions used a buffer containing 1.5% SDS. Chromatographic analysis was performed using a Waters high-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters Technologies, USA) equipped with a BioSep-SEC-S 4000 New column (Phenomenex, USA). The mobile phase was acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, column temperature of 30 °C, injection volume of 20 μL, and detection wavelength of 214 nm. The lowest absorbance reading between peaks was used as the cutoff point. The total extractability was determined by calculating the percentage of peak areas under non-reducing and reducing conditions (Yang, Yang, Guan, & Bian, 2025).

The contents of SDS-soluble polymers (SDS-P), SDS-soluble monomers (SDS-M), and SDS-insoluble proteins (SDS-I) were calculated from the peak areas and expressed as Eqs. (1), (2), (3):(1)SDS-P (%) = (Ap/Ar) × 100%(2)SDS-M (%) = (Am/Ar) × 100%(3)SDS-I (%) =100%-(SDS-P + SDS­M)where Ap and Am correspond to the peak areas of soluble polymers and monomers, respectively, and Ar is the total peak area of reduced proteins extracted with SDS buffer in the presence of 1.0% dithiothreitol (DTT).

Chemical interaction analysis

The method described by Liu et al. (2019) was used with some modifications. This method is based on the principle that selective reagents can disrupt specific types of bonds (ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, or hydrophobic interactions), reflecting the corresponding contributions of different chemical interactions.

The following four solutions were prepared: 0.05 mol/L NaCl (SA), 0.6 mol/L NaCl (SB), 0.6 mol/L NaCl +1.5 mol/L urea (SC), and 0.6 mol/L NaCl +8 mol/L urea (SD). Gluten samples (0.2 g) were mixed with 5.0 mL of the above solutions, kept at 4 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatants were collected, and protein contents were determined using the biuret method as described in Section 2.5.

Ionic bonds were represented by the difference in protein dissolved between SB and SA, hydrogen bonds by the difference between SC and SB, and hydrophobic interactions by the difference between SD and SC.

Solubility of gluten protein

The solubility of gluten protein was determined using the biuret method (Feyzi, Varidi, Zare, & Varidi, 2017). 1% gluten protein suspension was prepared, magnetically stirred for 30 min, centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 20 min, and 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of biuret reagent. After standing at RT for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 540 nm.

Protein content was determined using the biuret method (Feyzi et al., 2017). A standard curve was plotted with casein concentration as the abscissa and absorbance measured at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV2150, Unico Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) as the ordinate. The standard curve equation was y = 4.2542× + 0.0001, where y represents the absorbance at 540 nm and x represents the casein concentration, with a correlation coefficient R2 = 0.9997.

Four milliliters of biuret reagent were added to 1 mL of protein solution, mixed at RT for 30 min, and the absorbance at 540 nm (A540) was measured. The protein solubility value was calculated by inserting the absorbance into the standard curve.

Water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil-holding capacity (OHC)

Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined according to the method of (Vioque, Sánchez-Vioque, Clemente, Pedroche, & Millán, 2000) with minor modifications. Briefly, 1.0 g (m1) of protein sample was placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and mixed with 10 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min and then centrifuged at 2200 ×g for 30 min. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the tube was weighed again (m3). The weight of the empty tube with rotor was recorded as m2. WHC was expressed as grams of water retained per 100 g of protein, calculated using Eq. (4).

Oil-holding capacity (OHC) was measured following the same procedure, except that 10 mL of corn oil was used instead of distilled water.(4)WHC or OHC (%) = (m3 - m2) / m1 × 100%

Foaming capacity (FC) and foam stability (FS)

The foaming properties were analyzed according to the method of (Zhang et al., 2021). 2% (w/v) protein suspension (100 mL) was stirred at 10,000 rpm for 1 min and quickly transferred to a 500 mL graduated cylinder. The initial volume (V0) and the volume after 30 min (V30) were measured. The calculation formulas are as Eqs. (5), (6):(5)FC (%) = (V0/100) × 100%(6)FS (%) = (V30 / V0) × 100%

Emulsifying capacity (EC) and emulsion stability (ES)

The method described by (L. Zhang, Wang, Jiang, & Qian, 2017) was used with some modifications. 2% (w/v) protein suspension (100 mL) was mixed with 100 mL of soybean oil and stirred at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The emulsion was divided into four portions, each containing 40 mL of emulsion. Two samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the heights of the emulsion layer (H1) and the total height of the mixture (H2) were recorded. The other two samples were kept in a 50 °C water bath for 30 min, cooled to RT, and centrifuged again at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The heights of the emulsion layer (H3) and the total height of the mixture (H4) were measured again. The calculation formulas are as Eqs. (7), (8):(7)EC (%) = (H1/H2) × 100%(8)ES (%) = (H3/H4) × 100%

Dynamic rheological tests

The method described was used with some modifications. The rheological properties of gluten protein were evaluated using a dynamic rheometer (MARS60, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Wet gluten (2 g) was kneaded into a small ball, placed at the center of a 35 mm diameter disk (1 mm gap), and the excess sample was scraped off. To prevent dehydration during testing, a thin layer of silicone oil was applied around the dough. Before frequency sweep measurements, strain sweep tests were conducted to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) for all samples. Based on the strain sweep results, a strain value of 0.1% was selected, which was within the LVR of all tested samples. Frequency sweep measurements were then performed at a constant strain of 0.1% and 30 °C over a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz. The changes in elastic modulus (G′), viscous modulus (G″), and loss tangent (tan δ = G″/G′) with frequency were measured.

Product preparation

Twenty grams of gluten protein were weighed and mixed with 50 g of water, manually kneaded into a dough to ensure full hydration of the gluten. After pre-boiling water, the kneaded gluten dough was placed on a steamer rack, covered with a cylindrical breathable mold (8 cm diameter, 9 cm height), and steamed over high heat for 25 min. After steaming, the lid was slightly opened to balance the internal and external pressure, and the gluten dough was removed and cooled before measurement.

Product quality

The method of He et al. (2020) was used with slight modifications. The cooled steamed gluten was sliced into 15 mm-thick samples for texture analysis. A Texture Analyzer (TA.XT.plus, Stable Microsystems, London, England), equipped with a P/36R cylindrical probe, was used to measure its texture characteristics. The parameters were set as follows: trigger point load of 10 g, test speed of 1 mm/s, compression degree of 40%, pre-test speed of 2 mm/s, and post-test speed of 3 mm/s.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27 (IBM, USA). Data normality was assessed before correlation analysis. Because the data did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution, correlations between gluten protein structural and functional parameters and steamed gluten textural properties were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Chemical interactions analysis

As shown in Fig. 1, compared with gluten proteins isolated from non-sprouted wheat flour (WF), sprouted whole flour (SF) exhibited an approximately 6.74% decrease in hydrogen bond content and a 36.0% increase in ionic bond content, while no significant change was observed in hydrophobic interactions. After air classification of SF, the hydrogen bond content in the large particle-size fraction (F1) recovered to the level before sprouting. No significant difference in ionic bond content was observed between F1 and F2; however, the ionic bond content in F3 was 21.60% higher than that in F1 and F2. These results indicate that during sprouting, endogenous protease hydrolysis disrupted the peptide chain structure of gluten proteins, leading to dissociation of their ordered higher-order structures. Hydrogen bonds, which originally maintained protein spatial conformation and network stability of gluten proteins, were markedly reduced due to disordered spatial arrangement (Feng et al., 2024; He et al., 2026).

After air classification, the large particle-size fraction (F1) was mainly enriched in gluten proteins that were hardly affected by sprouting. These proteins retained their intrinsic native structures, allowing the hydrogen bond content to recover to pre-sprouting levels. In contrast, the smallest particle-size fraction (F3), enriched in SDP, exhibited a sharp increase in hydrophobic interactions and a moderate rise in ionic bonds. Essentially, protein unfolding exposed a large number of hydrophobic amino acid residues that were originally buried within the molecular interior to the aqueous environment. Thermodynamically, these exposed hydrophobic groups were driven to aggregate through strong hydrophobic interactions, which became the dominant force governing the re-association of protein fragments. Although proteolysis also generated new charged termini and side chains (Li et al., 2024), theoretically increasing the potential sites for ionic bond formation, the disordered spatial distribution of peptides, pH constraints of the system, and the competitive dominance of hydrophobic aggregation hindered the formation of stable and spatially matched ionic pairs. Consequently, ionic bonds exhibited only a limited increase and failed to alter the aggregation pattern dominated by hydrophobic interactions.

Molecular weight distribution

Size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-HPLC) combined with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) extraction is an indirect yet effective method for analyzing the molecular size distribution of wheat gluten proteins. SDS buffer extraction maintains proteins in a soluble state and prevents aggregation (Larroque, Gianibelli, Sanchez, & MacRitchie, 2000). SDS disrupts hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions to dissolve protein aggregates; however, aggregates stabilized by disulfide bonds (such as glutenin macropolymers, GMP) remain insoluble and require reduction with 1.0% dithiothreitol (DTT) to break disulfide bonds.

Under non-reducing conditions, the SE-HPLC elution profiles can typically be separated into three major fractions. The first fraction is high-molecular-weight SDS-soluble polymers (SDS-P, N1), with molecular weights ranging from approximately 91 to 688 kDa, which are composed of loosely cross-linked polymeric proteins. The second fraction consists of SDS-soluble monomers (SDS-M, N2), with molecular weights of approximately 16–91 kDa, including monomeric glutenin, gliadin, and some low-degree oligomers. The third fraction corresponds to low-molecular-weight components (N3) with molecular weights below 16 kDa, comprising amino acids, peptides, and small molecular degradation products. The SDS-insoluble fraction (SDS-I) is generally considered to represent highly cross-linked glutenin macropolymers (GMP) formed through disulfide bonds, and its content is often used as an indicator of protein polymerization (Ma et al., 2022).

After air classification, no significant differences in SDS-M or SDS-P contents were observed between F1 and F2. These results indicate that the sprouting process primarily damages SDS-M, represented by gliadins and monomeric glutenin. Notably, the increase in SDS-I in SF and F3 did not originate from an increase in functional GMP but rather from non-functional aggregation formed after protein structural damage. In SF and F3, sprouting-induced protein denaturation exposed numerous hydrophobic and charged groups, promoting non-functional aggregation of degraded protein fragments that could not be fully solubilized in SDS buffer. This conclusion is also supported by the findings in Section 3.1. Therefore, the increase in SDS-I essentially reflects protein damage and quality deterioration rather than recovery of functional structures.

Functional properties of gluten

The solubility, hydrophobicity, structural integrity, and aggregation state of gluten proteins collectively determine their functional properties. These molecular attributes sequentially regulate protein hydration behavior, water- and oil-holding capacities, and further regulate protein diffusion, adsorption, and film formation at interfaces (Grossmann & McClements, 2023; L. Zhang et al., 2017). Consequently, solubility and structural integrity not only affect protein hydration but also modulate interfacial behavior, ultimately manifesting as differences in foaming and emulsifying properties.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the solubility of gluten proteins in SF did not differ significantly from that of WF. However, after air classification, the solubility of the large particle size fraction F1 was higher than that of F2 and F3. As shown in Fig. 3B, sprouting-induced protein degradation and conformational loosening promoted the exposure of hydrophilic groups (such as amino, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups) as well as nonpolar amino acid side chains on the molecular surface, resulting in increases of 9.94% and 5.03% in the water-holding capacity (WHC) and oil-holding capacity (OHC) of SF compared with WF, respectively. After air classification, the WHC values of F1 and F2 were both lower than that of F3, which is attributed to their higher contents of intact high-molecular-weight gluten proteins with more compact structures and reduced pore spaces, thereby restricting the penetration of water and oil into the protein network (Yang, Guan, Zhang, Li, & Bian, 2022); In F3, both hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups were extensively exposed on the molecular surface. The exposure of hydrophilic groups enhanced interactions with water molecules, leading to a 9.85% increase in WHC; meanwhile, the increased exposure of hydrophobic groups provided more active binding sites for oil molecules, resulting in a 15.28% increase in OHC (Shao et al., 2025).

Foaming and emulsifying behaviors are highly dependent on protein diffusion, adsorption, and subsequent interfacial film formation, particularly in complex interfacial systems where structural stability requirements are higher. Small protein monomers or oligomers can rapidly diffuse to interfaces and complete adsorption, whereas denatured protein aggregates exhibit hindered diffusion and adsorption due to aggregation and low interfacial affinity (Shen, Peng, Sagis, & Landman, 2024). As shown in Fig. 3C, sprouting reduced the content of soluble monomers responsible for rapid interfacial adsorption while increasing insoluble aggregates, resulting in a 34.6% decrease in foaming capacity (FC) of SF compared with WF, whereas foaming stability (FS) showed no significant change. After classification, F1, enriched in more soluble and structurally intact proteins, rapidly migrated to the gas–liquid interface and formed dense, elastic interfacial films, leading to the greatest increase in FC and an improvement in FS (Embaby, Swailam, & Rayan, 2017). F2 contained partially enriched SDP, whereas F3 was enriched in more SDP with the lowest soluble protein content, resulting in lower FC and FS.

As shown in Fig. 3D, SF formed non-functional aggregates due to increased severely degraded components, leading to reduced diffusion and adsorption efficiency and consequently lower emulsifying capacity (EC) and emulsifying stability (ES). After air classification, F1 exhibited high solubility and structural integrity, enabling rapid adsorption and formation of dense interfacial films, resulting in higher EC and ES than those of F2 and F3. Although F2 had lower solubility, it retained good interfacial activity, and its emulsifying performance was superior to that of F3. In contrast, F3 contained a large number of protein aggregates with low diffusion rates and weak interfacial adsorption ability due to extensive non-functional aggregation; moreover, hydrophobic groups had already associated internally, preventing effective unfolding and film formation at the interface, leading to the lowest EC and ES.

Analysis of dynamic rheological properties

Dynamic rheological analysis is a core approach for evaluating the structure–function relationship of gluten proteins. By applying oscillatory stress or strain and recording the corresponding responses, the viscoelastic properties of gluten proteins can be obtained, thereby providing insights into their internal structure and molecular interactions, which are crucial for food processing applications (Song & Zheng, 2007). Among the key parameters, the storage modulus (G′, reflecting elasticity) represents the ability of the system to store deformation energy, while the loss modulus (G′′, reflecting viscosity) describes energy dissipation. When the loss tangent (tan δ = G′′/G′) is lower than 1, the system is considered to be elasticity-dominated, and deformation within the linear viscoelastic region is largely recoverable (M. Zhang, Zhang, Li, & Sun, 2021).

During oscillatory frequency sweep tests conducted at a constant strain (frequency range: 0.1–10 Hz), both G′ and G′′ of all gluten samples increased with increasing frequency, exhibiting clear frequency dependence. Throughout the entire frequency range, G′ remained consistently higher than G′′ (Fig. 4A, B), indicating that all gluten systems were dominated by elastic behavior. At the same frequency, compared with gluten prepared from non-sprouted wheat flour (WF), gluten from sprouted whole flour (SF) showed a decrease in G′ accompanied by an increase in G′′, resulting in a higher tan δ value (Fig. 4C). This indicates that WF exhibited the strongest elastic dominance and the highest structural stability, whereas in the SF gluten system, the relative contribution of elasticity decreased while viscous contributions increased, leading to enhanced flowability.Fig. 4Dynamic rheological properties of gluten proteins. After air classification, the gluten corresponding to F1 showed a partial recovery of G′, a decrease in G′′, and a concomitant reduction in tan δ. In contrast, the gluten corresponding to the fine fraction (F3) exhibited no pronounced differences in G′, G′′, or tan δ compared with SF, indicating that its viscoelastic behavior was dominated by severely damaged and aggregated protein structures.

Quality analysis of steamed gluten

As shown in Fig. 5, the steamed gluten formed from WF exhibited thin pore walls and heterogeneous pore sizes, with a relatively large number of medium-to-large through-pores, indicating insufficient extensibility during expansion, which led to localized rupture and coalescence of gas cells; however, the overall structure remained relatively stable. The steamed gluten formed from SF also showed thin pore walls and was dominated almost entirely by large pores, but with fewer through-pores, and severe collapse occurred at the top. After air classification, the steamed gluten formed from F1 exhibited thin pore walls with adhesion at the top. This was attributed to the rapid expansion of F1 gluten during steaming, followed by rapid contraction after steaming; under elasticity-dominated conditions with limited stress-buffering capacity, the pore structure became unstable during the post-steaming stage and collapsed into each other, ultimately leading to top collapse and adhesion.

By comparison, F2 exhibited moderate elasticity and a higher viscous contribution, such that excessive enlargement of large pores did not occur during expansion, nor did insufficient flowability lead to fragile pore walls. Its uniform and moderately sized small pores, together with thicker pore walls, were able to maintain stronger structural support, thereby avoiding collapse and forming a dense and intact structure. In F3, the elastic contribution of the gluten system decreased while the viscous fraction increased, resulting in slightly weaker structural support during steaming; however, the smaller pore size led to lower internal stress, which was balanced with the available support, and therefore, collapse was also unlikely to occur.

The texture profile analysis results (Table 2) further confirmed the effects of the above structural differences on the functional properties of steamed gluten. Compared with WF, SF exhibited significantly lower hardness, adhesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness due to its loose gluten network, reflecting markedly insufficient overall structural support. In contrast, WF possessed relatively intact pore walls and a more compact three-dimensional network, thereby showing better structural stability. The steamed gluten prepared from F1 was prone to pore wall collapse during steaming and the post-steaming stage, resulting in the highest hardness (569.60 ± 32.98). Meanwhile, F1 also showed the highest adhesiveness (−5.99 ± 1.61), as indicated by the maximum negative work required to detach the probe. However, the lowest springiness (1.56 ± 0.15) and weaker structural recovery indicate that it was difficult to maintain a stable structure. By comparison, the steamed gluten formed from F2 exhibited intact pore walls, uniform pore distribution, and a stable structure. Its hardness and gumminess were comparable to those of WF, while it showed the highest springiness (1.85 ± 0.01), together with relatively higher cohesiveness, chewiness, and resilience, suggesting the formation of the most compact and stable three-dimensional network. Correlation analysis

Fig. 6 illustrates correlations between gluten functional properties and steamed gluten texture. Differences among gluten fractions and their functional properties were associated with variations in network compactness, continuity, and structural stability, which were in turn related to hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. SDS-M content showed significant positive correlations with hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. In contrast, SDS-I content was significantly negatively correlated with hardness (r = −0.745*, p < 0.05), cohesiveness (r = −0.697*, p < 0.05), gumminess (r = −0.830**, p < 0.01), and chewiness (r = −0.673*, p < 0.05). Hydrophobic interactions were negatively correlated with hardness (r = −0.745*, p < 0.05), cohesiveness (r = −0.661*, p < 0.05), gumminess (r = −0.806** p < 0.01), chewiness (r = −0.806** p < 0.01), and resilience (r = −0.661*, p < 0.05). Strong hydrophobic interactions, which were more pronounced in fractions enriched with SDP, promote non-functional aggregation of SDS-soluble monomers (SDS-M) into SDS-insoluble aggregates (SDS-I), forming loose hydrophobic aggregates that reduce network continuity and increase structural heterogeneity, thereby weakening gluten system stability and mechanical strength. The storage modulus (G′) showed a significant negative correlation with adhesiveness (r = −0.702**, p < 0.01), indicating that when the gluten protein system is dominated by elastic behavior, the resulting steamed gluten products generally exhibit lower surface adhesiveness. Meanwhile, the loss tangent (tan δ), which reflects the relative contributions of viscous and elastic components, was negatively correlated with hardness (r = −0.781**, p < 0.01), adhesiveness (r = −0.657**, p < 0.01), and chewiness (r = −0.557*, p < 0.05). These results suggest that neither excessively high elasticity nor excessive viscosity alone is favorable for steamed gluten texture. Instead, an appropriate balance between elastic energy storage and viscous energy dissipation is critical for maintaining network integrity during steaming, buffering internal stress, and achieving desirable textural properties.

Emulsifying capacity (EC) and emulsifying stability (ES) were also significantly positively correlated with hardness, gumminess, and chewiness. Stronger emulsifying ability indicates that proteins can more readily form stable interfacial films and provide sustained support, helping maintain pore structure stability during steaming and enhancing overall network compactness (Cai et al., 2023). Protein solubility and hydrogen bonding interactions further enhance effective protein–protein association and improve network uniformity, thereby increasing hardness and gumminess (Calle, Benavent-Gil, & Rosell, 2020). In contrast, water-holding capacity was significantly negatively correlated with hardness (r = −0.891** p < 0.01), gumminess (r = −0.879** p < 0.01), and chewiness (r = −0.721* p < 0.05), indicating that excessively high water retention loosens the network and reduces structural support. Additionally, the positive correlations between foaming capacity and hardness (r = 0.837** p < 0.01) and gumminess (r = 0.751*, p < 0.05) suggest that moderately stable foam structures enhance volume retention during steaming and improve product structural stability.

Conclusion

In summary, wheat sprouting disrupted the structural integrity and intermolecular interactions of gluten proteins, leading to reduced protein solubility, impaired interfacial functionality, and weakened viscoelastic performance-all of which ultimately deteriorated the structural stability and textural quality of steamed gluten. Air classification enabled the separation and enrichment of sprouting-induced damaged proteins (SDP), resulting in distinct structural and functional characteristics among different particle-size fractions. Among these fractions, the F2 fraction exhibited the most balanced protein functionality (including optimized solubility, interfacial activity, and viscoelasticity), which contributed to the formation of a uniform pore structure in steamed gluten and rendered it the most suitable fraction for steamed gluten production. The F3 fraction was enriched in SDP and non-functional aggregates; although its gluten network continuity was significantly weakened, the smaller and more uniform pore size distribution partially mitigated structural collapse during the steaming process. In contrast, SF and F1 exhibited the most severe pore instability and the poorest textural properties. Correlation analysis further confirmed that protein composition, intermolecular interaction strength, interfacial behavior, and viscoelastic characteristics synergistically determined the key textural attributes of steamed gluten. Overall, air classification provides a feasible technical strategy for the high-value utilization of sprouted wheat, enabling fraction-specific quality control and targeted application in steamed gluten production.
