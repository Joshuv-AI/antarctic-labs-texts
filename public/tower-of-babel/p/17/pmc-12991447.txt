Association between systemic immune-inflammatory index and circadian syndrome: Data from NHANES 2005 to 2018
Authors: Yunyan Lu, Jiaer Gao, Tian Lan, Li Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The relationship between the systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) and circadian syndrome (CircS) is unclear. This study aims to explore this association. We extracted 23,148 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys 2005 to 2018. We used 3 multivariate logistic regression models to investigate the associations between SII and CircS, along with restricted cubic splines and subgroup analyses. The participants with CircS tended to be older and female. They had higher body mass index, poorer liver function, lower levels of education, and economic status compared with non-CircS participants. The SII level in the CircS group was higher than in the non-CircS group (odds ratio = 1.25, 95% confidence interval = 1.09–1.44, P = .002). There was no significant nonlinear relationship between SII and CircS (P overall < .001; P nonlinearity = .337). However, we did not observe any significant interactions between SII and other clinicopathologic factors (all P values for interaction > .05). We demonstrated a positive association between SII and CircS, particularly among people with low body mass index, low physical activity, cardiovascular disease, or cancer. For these populations, we may identify and intervene in CircS at an earlier stage by monitoring SII.

1. Introduction

Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), a novel and stable measure of systemic inflammation, consists of 4 hematological lineages: lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, and platelets. SII was originally computed and used by Hu.[1] Higher SII was generally associated with a poor prognosis in some malignancies.[1–3] Recently, more research demonstrated that SII was associated with various diseases, including diabetic kidney disease,[4] psoriasis,[5] anemia,[6] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[7]

Circadian syndrome (CircS) is a chronic condition characterized by the disruption of the biological clock, which leads to multiple metabolic and physiological disorders.[8] The diagnosis of CircS requires the presence of at least 4 of the following chronic symptoms, including hypertension, lipid abnormalities, obesity, diabetes, depression, and sleep disturbances.[9] CircS was also implicated in diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD).[10] CircS was associated not only with the fundamental etiology contributing to metabolic syndrome (MetS) but also with its related comorbidities.[11]

Previous studies have demonstrated that a higher SII was associated with the onset of MetS, suggesting its potential as an independent risk factor for predicting MetS onset.[12] However, research exploring the relationship between SII and CircS is still insufficient. In recent years, individuals have become more vulnerable to CircS due to nighttime lighting, social jet lag, low physical activity, and night shift work.[13–15] Considering the potential capability of SII and the practical urgency, we conducted a population-based study to evaluate the relationship between SII and CircS, as well as to explore the potential role of SII.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This study was conducted using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a program designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. NHANES utilized a sophisticated multistage probability sampling design and collected data in every 2-year cycle (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). There were 70,190 participants in the NHANES database from 2005 to 2018. The study procedure is illustrated in Figure 1. The exclusion criteria were outlined as follows: age < 20 years or pregnant; missing data on CircS; and missing information on SII and covariates. In total, 23,148 participants were included in the analysis.

Flowchart of participant selection. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, SII = systemic immune-inflammation.

2.2. CircS definition

CircS was assessed based on 7 components, with a cutoff diagnosed at ≥4 components.[16] The 7 components were as follows: elevated waist circumference (≥102 cm in men, ≥88 cm in women); high blood pressure (systolic ≥ 130 mm Hg and/or diastolic ≥ 85 mm Hg) or current drug treatment for hypertension; low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (<40 mg/dL in men and <50 mg/dL in women) or current drug treatment for dyslipidemia; high triglycerides (TG, ≥150 mg/dL) or current drug treatment for dyslipidemia; elevated fasting glucose (≥100 mg/dL), with drug treatment for elevated glucose as an alternative indicator; self-reported short sleep duration (<6 hours per day); depression, measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire, where depressive symptoms were indicated by a score of ≥5.

2.3. SII definition

SII is a novel scoring index for evaluating the immune system. This index was computed based on lymphocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts using the Uni Cel DxH 800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter). According to previous research reports, SII = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count.[1]

2.4. Assessment of covariates

In this study, age was classified into 3 groups: ≤39, 40 to 59, and ≥60 years. Race was categorized as Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and other racial groups. Education levels were divided into 3 groups: lower than high school, high school, and college graduate or above. We divided the population into 2 groups based on the family income-to-poverty ratio: the not poor (≥1) and poor (<1) groups. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current smoker, while alcohol consumption status was classified as yes or no. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized as follows: underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obese (≥30 kg/m2). Physical activity was classified into 4 levels: inactive, moderate, vigorous, and both moderate and vigorous.

In addition, the following continuous variables were measured when participants provided blood samples at baseline: lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, platelet count, red blood cells, hemoglobin, mean platelet volume, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, uric acid, serum potassium, serum sodium, serum creatinine, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Blood pressure and waist circumference were obtained during the participants’ physical examination.

The participants were asked, “Have you ever been informed by a doctor or other health professional that you have congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, or stroke?” Participants who answered “yes” to these questions were classified as having CVD. Tumor history, medication use, and sleep duration were also obtained from self-reported questionnaires.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard error (weighted), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and weighted percentages. The differences in general characteristics were estimated using the t test or chi-square test.

We used weighted logistic regression models to determine the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Given its right-skewed distribution, the continuous SII values were log-transformed. The continuous and categorical SII values were separately incorporated into the logistic regression models.

We used the first quartile of lnSII (Q1) as the reference group and assigned a median value to each category to evaluate the linear trend. We constructed 3 models for analysis. Model 1 was the crude model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, and race. Model 3 was adjusted for the same variables as Model 2, along with education level, family income-to-poverty ratio, alcohol use, smoking status, BMI, physical activity, congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cancer. To investigate the dose-response association between SII and CircS, restricted cubic spline regression with the multivariable adjustments mentioned in Model 3 was employed. The likelihood ratio test was used to evaluate nonlinearity.

Subgroup analysis was conducted using several stratified factors, including age, gender, race, BMI, physical activity, CVD, and cancer. We evaluated the P values for interaction in these subgroups. The statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.1; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A 2-tailed P value < .05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

The CircS patients were older than the non-CircS participants. The percentage of females, smokers, and drinkers in the CircS group was greater than in the non-CircS group. The CircS patients also demonstrated significantly higher levels of BMI, mean platelet volume, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, uric acid, serum potassium, serum creatinine, and SII compared with the non-CircS participants. Furthermore, we revealed a higher incidence of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cancer in the CircS group. There were lower levels of education, physical activity, and family income-to-poverty ratio in the CircS group.

3.2. Relationship between SII and CircS

The relationship between SII and CircS was analyzed using multiple logistic regression models (Table 2). Adults with higher lnSII were more likely to suffer from CircS in Model 1 (OR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.21–1.43) and Model 2 (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.20–1.42). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), a positive association between SII and CircS was also observed (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.07–1.29), indicating that for every unit increase in lnSII score, the risk of developing CircS increased by 18%. To further validate the positive correlation between SII and CircS, we converted the continuous SII into categorical SII. Compared with the lowest SII quartile, the CircS risk in the highest SII quartile increased in the unadjusted Model 1 (OR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.28–1.64, P < .001), Model 2 (OR = 1.44, 95% CI = 1.27–1.64, P < .001) and Model 3 (OR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.09–1.44, P = .002).

3.3. Subgroup analysis

To investigate the roles of potential confounders in the associations between SII levels and CircS, we divided the participants into several subgroups stratified by age, gender, race, physical activity, BMI, CVD, and cancer (Fig. 3). In the subgroup analysis, a significant association was identified exclusively in individuals with low physical activity, low BMI, CVD, and cancer. However, we did not observe any significant interactions between SII and all these potential clinicopathologic factors (all P values for interaction > .05).

4. Discussion

In this study, we explored the association between CircS and SII by employing a complex weighted logistic regression model, based on NHANES 2005 to 2018. We found that SII was significantly higher in patients with CircS, and there was a positive correlation between SII levels and CircS risk. This correlation remained significant even after controlling for several confounding variables. Furthermore, this association was more pronounced among individuals with low physical activity and low BMI. One plausible explanation is that weight loss influences neutrophil function, potentially attenuating abnormal inflammatory responses and resulting in lower baseline SII levels. Consequently, even slight fluctuations in SII may precipitate significant alterations in CircS.[12]

Several studies have been published on the association between SII and some diseases, such as sleep disorders and depression.[17,18] SII could also serve as an independent risk factor for predicting the onset of MetS.[19] There is a potential connection between MetS and CircS. Disruption of circadian rhythms is likely linked to MetS and may constitute a fundamental factor in the etiology of CircS. CircS is a relatively novel concept, including the components of MetS, depression, and short sleep duration.[20] Therefore, CircS is a better substitute for the MetS.

There are several potential molecular mechanisms based on the association between SII and CircS. First, some inflammatory indices are involved in this relationship. Disruption of circadian rhythms was reported to be linked to 2 inflammatory cytokines, C-reactive protein, and interleukin-6.[21] Second, sleep disturbance results in increased inflammation through the activation of nuclear factor-κB.[22] In animal experiments, prostaglandin D2 played a key role in circadian-disruption-induced inflammation.[23] Third, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system are regulated dynamically by normal sleep, which are potential regulatory pathways.[24] Future research should continue to explore these mechanisms to formulate enhanced management strategies for patients with CircS.

Many individuals experience circadian disruption, which is a primary cause of CircS. Approximately 35% of the US workforce is involved in constant sleep deprivation, 70% live in a state of social jet lag, and 20% experience shift work.[25–27] Hence, chronomedicine, including light, exercise, and food, has been proposed as a therapeutic approach to treat and prevent CircS.[28] Nighttime exposure to light disrupts temporal adaptations and alters the natural light-dark cycle.[29] We should promote strategies to mitigate the effects of nighttime light exposure on behavior and physiology. Physical activity can serve as a zeitgeber to recalibrate the circadian biological clock in patients with CircS and enhance their endogenous circadian cycles.[30,31] In terms of diet, tea can prevent and mitigate circadian rhythm disorder by regulating the gut–liver–brain axis.[32] Exogenous melatonin agonists may be another treatment to bolster and reset circadian rhythms, although further clinical trials are necessary.[33] The aforementioned lifestyle interventions are relatively cost-effective and simple to implement. Meanwhile, SII may be an effective tool for the early detection and diagnosis of CircS.

In this study, subgroup analyses indicated that the positive correlation between CircS and SII significantly varied by age, race, physical activity, BMI, and certain diseases. Among individuals with low physical activity, low BMI, CVD, and cancer, SII was significantly related to CircS, suggesting that SII may serve as a valuable instrument for the early identification of CircS in these populations. Compared to older individuals, younger individuals were more sensitive to light and other zeitgebers, which may partially elucidate the significant relationship between SII and CircS in those under the age of 40.[34] SII is widely accessible, employs a nonintrusive approach, and is cost-effective. Its potential for screening and therapeutic applications is indeed promising in these particular populations. The positive correlation between SII and CircS enables precise identification of individuals at high risk for CircS and facilitates the development of personalized treatment strategies in clinical practice.

Our findings are reliable due to the large sample size and the stratified sampling survey of the US population. However, there are some limitations in the study. First, our study had a cross-sectional design, which did not allow us to explore the causality between SII and CircS. Second, sleep duration was assessed through self-reporting rather than through objective measurement. Sleep quality was not included in this study. Future studies should obtain more objective data through polysomnography.[35] Third, we were unable to account for all potential confounding factors that could impact the results. Finally, some covariates (such as heart failure, cancer, stroke, etc) were collected via recall questionnaires in NHANES, which may be susceptible to recall bias.

In conclusion, we demonstrated a positive association between SII and CircS. Additionally, the association was more significant among individuals with low physical activity, low BMI, CVD, and cancer.
