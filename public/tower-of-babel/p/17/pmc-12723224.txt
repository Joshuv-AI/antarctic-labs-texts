Potent and durable gene modulation in heart and muscle with chemically defined lipophilic siRNAs
Authors: Hassan H Fakih, Clemens Lochmann, Rosemary Gagnon, Ashley Summers, Jillian Caiazzi, Julianna E Buchwald, Qi Tang, Bruktawit Maru, Samuel R Hildebrand, Mohammad Zain U I Abideen, Raymond C Furgal, Katherine Y Gross, Yeon-Suk Yang, David Cooper, Kathryn R Monopoli, Dimas Echeverria, Jae-Hyuck Shim, Ken Yamada, Julia F Alterman, Anastasia Khvorova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall interfering RNAs (siRNAs) hold promise for treating cardiac and muscular diseases, but robust and scalable delivery remains a hurdle. While biologic–siRNA conjugates (e.g. antibodies) are in clinical development, their manufacturing is complex. Lipophilic siRNAs are readily chemically synthesized at scale and support effective heart and muscle delivery. Here, we refine siRNA chemical design for enhanced potency and durability to support clinically relevant silencing. Targeting myostatin (MSTN), a key gene in muscle-wasting, a single subcutaneous dose in mice achieved potent silencing (80% inhibition up to 6 weeks, 30% up to 14 weeks). Biweekly dosing led to over 95% MSTN reduction for half-a-year with no observed toxicity. This resulted in muscle growth, increased lean mass, and improved grip strength. Phenotypical benefits extended beyond direct target silencing, suggesting prolonged effects. The siRNA scaffold was effective across multiple muscle groups, with its modularity confirmed by three additional targets. Optimized dosing extended durability to 20 weeks without compromising phenotypic outcomes. As a proof of concept, MSTN inhibition with siRNAs successfully combated muscle wasting in an inflammatory myopathy model (cardiotoxin). These findings pave the way for long-lasting gene modulation in heart and muscle, offering new therapeutic strategies for muscular diseases.

Introduction

Oligonucleotide therapeutics [e.g. small interfering RNA (siRNA), antisense oligonucleotides, splice-switching oligonucleotides] are now regarded as the third class of drugs after small molecules and biologics, as they revolutionize medicine by enabling potent and efficient modulation of gene expression to previously “undruggable” targets with months-long durability [1, 2]. Currently, there are >18 approved oligonucleotide therapies, of which 7 are siRNA-based [2]. The clinical success of oligonucleotide therapeutics hinges on their stability and efficient delivery to the tissue of interest [2, 3]. There are now several approved siRNA drugs with the majority being based on conjugation of the chemically defined trivalent N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) molecule, which enables robust hepatic delivery [1]. Current efforts are focused on enabling delivery to other organs of interest such as the central nervous system, heart, and muscle [1, 2].

The ability to robustly deliver and modulate gene expression in muscle tissues is crucial for developing effective therapeutic intervention for cardiac and skeletal muscle diseases [1, 3]. Local injections of oligonucleotide therapeutics directly into muscle can silence specific genes, but this approach is limited to the area treated, hindering its overall therapeutic impact [4, 5]. Recent efforts have focused on achieving systemic delivery of therapeutics to muscles throughout the body, which is more clinically relevant for treating widespread muscle diseases [5]. Delivery via biologic conjugates—e.g. antibody/antibody fragment oligonucleotide conjugation (AOC) and peptide-conjugated oligonucleotides—is at the forefront of clinical development [3, 6–9]. Similarly, engineered Centyrin proteins that bind specific antigens are showing exciting preclinical data for muscle delivery [10, 11]. Strategies for targeted delivery of biologic–siRNA conjugates hold promise, but challenges include complex administration (e.g. hours-long intravenous infusion), developing efficient conjugation methods, ensuring an antibody retains its target recognition ability (epitope sensitivity), cross-species compatibility challenges for preclinical testing, preserving siRNA activity, and overcoming large-scale manufacturing difficulties [12, 13]. These complexities limit the broad applicability and scalability of these approaches. Some of these challenges are being addressed with recent development of bicyclic peptides, which are structurally constrained peptides around a chemical scaffold [14, 15]. By being smaller in size, less immunogenic, and retaining high affinity and selectivity to their target of interest, bicyclic peptides greatly advances biologic-based delivery, including delivery of siRNAs to muscle tissues [16].

Meanwhile, lipophilic siRNAs are readily chemically synthesized at scale and have been shown to support extrahepatic delivery, including to heart and muscle [3, 17, 18]. Cholesterol, palmitic, and tocopherol-conjugated siRNAs deliver their payload to muscle following systemic injection and efficiently silence multiple targets [18, 19]. However, meaningful silencing required high dose (>50 mg/kg) with repeated administration and resulted in observed toxicity, limiting tolerability and clinical translatability [19].

Recently, our lab has shown that conjugating the fatty acid docosanoic acid (DCA) to siRNAs (DCA–siRNA) supports effective heart and muscle delivery [20] with improved safety profile compared to other lipid conjugates. However, even with repetitive doses the maximum observed silencing was <50%, which is insufficient for many clinical indications. We hypothesize that optimization of the DCA–siRNA scaffold may boost efficacy, decreasing the required dosage for potent target inhibition.

In this work, we explore whether systematic chemical engineering of the muscle-delivering scaffold (DCA–siRNA) can achieve robust and durable silencing in cardiac and skeletal muscle. We demonstrate that optimization of the drug components, including hyper-functional siRNA sequence identification, chemical modifications’ patterns engineering, and backbone stabilization, results in a scaffold that enables potent and durable gene silencing in the heart and muscle at clinically relevant levels. Applying this strategy to target myostatin (Mstn), a key gene that promotes muscle-wasting, we demonstrate that a single subcutaneous injection in mice achieves robust and durable silencing (~80% inhibition up to 6 weeks, ~30% at 14 weeks), with repetitive dosing resulting in >95% reduction of circulating MSTN protein for half a year. The potent MSTN suppression led to desirable phenotypical outcomes (increased muscle mass and strength), extending beyond silencing at the molecular level, suggesting long-lasting clinical benefit. This work paves the way for effective and long-lasting gene modulation in heart and muscle using chemically defined, lipid-conjugated siRNAs, presenting a promising new approach for treating muscular diseases that is expandable to other targets of interest.

Materials and methods

Designing siRNAs against myostatin

siRNAs were designed based on an algorithm developed by Shmushkovich et al. [21]. Briefly, siRNAs were selected based on a 20-nucleotide target sequence from human (accession number: NM_005259) and mouse (NM_010834.3) MSTN transcripts. siRNA sequences were excluded if they had: (i) >56% of GC content, (ii) single-nucleotide stretches of four or more, or (iii) perfect homology to human miRNA seeds at position 2–7 of the antisense strand [22]. To minimize off-target effects, siRNAs were excluded if position 2–17 of the antisense strand had full complementarity to nontarget messenger RNAs (mRNAs). Targeting sequences (including +10/−15 flanking nucleotides) were scored using a weight matrix and top-scoring sequences in each species were identified. Cross-species targeting was determined based on perfect homology of the 16-nucleotide region within the target sequence (positions 2–17) to the target sequence of the other species. siRNAs were named based on the position in the target transcript from which they were extracted (e.g. human siRNA 2155 was extracted from positions 2155–2174 of NM_005259; mouse siRNA 1928 was extracted from positions 1928–1947). In vitro screening oligonucleotides sequences and modifications are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Synthesis and purification of oligonucleotides

Compounds for in vitro screening were synthesized using standard solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry on a Dr. Oligo 48 high-throughput RNA synthesizer (Biolytic) [23]. Two sets of 96 fully chemically modified antisense and sense single strands were synthesized, one set for human and the other for mouse, at 1 µmol scale. Compounds for in vivo injections were made using a MerMade 12 (BioAutomation) synthesizer. Standard RNA 2′-O-methyl, 2′-fluoro modifications were applied to improve siRNA stability (ChemGenes and Hongene). Extended nucleic acid (exNA) was incorporated at the 3′-termini antisense strand of in vivo compounds (in-house and Hongene). Sense strands of in vitro compounds were synthesized on a cholesterol-conjugated solid support (ChemGenes), and the sense strands of in vivo compounds were synthesized at 10–40 μmol scale on a DCA-functionalized controlled pore glass (CPG) (Hongene) [17, 20, 23]. Antisense strands were synthesized on CPG functionalized with a Unylinker (ChemGenes), bis-cyanoethyl-N, N-diisopropyl CED phosphoramidite (ChemGenes) was used to introduce a 5′-mono-phosphate for in vitro compounds, and custom 5′-(E)-vinylphosphonate 2′-OMe-uridine CED phosphoramidite (Hongene) was applied to introduce 5′-(E)-vinylphosphonate for in vivo studies.

All sense strands have a dT2 spacer between the oligonucleotide and the conjugate. All strands were cleaved and deprotected using 28% aqueous ammonium hydroxide solution for 20 h at 55°C, followed by drying under vacuum at 40°C, and resuspension in Millipore H2O. Oligonucleotides were purified using an Agilent Prostar System (Agilent Technologies) on a C18 column for lipid-conjugated sense strands and an ion-exchange column for antisense strands. Purified oligonucleotides were desalted by size-exclusion chromatography and characterized by liquid chromatography/mass spectroscopy (LC–MS) analysis on an Agilent 6530 accurate-mass quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) LC-MS (Agilent Technologies).

The sequences and modifications of in vivo oligonucleotides are shown in Supplementary Table S2. JAK1 sequences were acquired from the work of Tang et. al. (for JAK1-3033) and [23] Tang et. al. (for JAK1-860) [24]. MECP2 targeting sequences were acquired from the work by Hariharan et. al. [25]. HTT targeting sequences were the same as previously reported [26].

Duplex formation

Equimolar amounts of antisense and sense strands were prepared in 1× PBS and incubated at 95°C for 5 min, cooled to room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C. To validate efficient duplex formation, 20 pmol of duplex was loaded onto a nondenaturing 20% tris-borate-EDTA gel (Invitrogen, #EC63155BOX) and run at constant 180V for 1 h. The gel was then washed with deionized water for 10 min, stained with 1X SYBR Gold Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Invitrogen, #S11494) for 10 min and washed again. Bands were visualized on the ChemiDoc MP Imaging system (Bio-Rad, #17001402).

Cell culture

Human SJCRh30 cells (ATCC, #CRL-2061) were cultured in T75 tissue-culture treated flasks (Corning, #353107) containing RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. Murine C2C12 cells (ATCC, #CRL-1772) were propagated in DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum under the same conditions. All cells were maintained at 10%–40% confluency and split every 3–5 days until passage number reached 20 or 10, respectively. The supernatant medium was discarded, the cells were washed with DPBS (Gibco) and then detached by incubation with TrypLE Express (Gibco) for 5 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. Trypsinization was stopped by adding fresh medium. Counting of cells was performed by trypan blue staining using a Cellometer Auto 1000 instrument (Nexcelom).

Primary screening

Screening for MSTN and Mstn targeting siRNAs was conducted in SJCRh30 cells for human-targeting siRNAs, and in C2C12 cells for mouse-targeting siRNAs. siRNA uptake was via passive cellular uptake. Samples were diluted in OptiMEM (Gibco) to two-fold final concentration. Cells were detached and counted and subsequently, resuspended in media containing 6% fetal bovine serum. Fifty microliters of cell suspension (7500 cells for SJCRh30, 7500 cells for C2C12) were plated with 50 µl of siRNA in a 96-well tissue-culture plate to obtain a final serum concentration of 3% and an siRNA concentration of 1.5 µM (screening) or the corresponding target concentration (concentration-response). For each compound three technical replicates were prepared and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 72 h for SJCRh30 or 144 h for C2C12, mRNA was quantified as described below.

mRNA quantification

mRNA expression was quantified with the QuantiGene Singleplex Assay Kit (Invitrogen, #QS0016), a hybridization-based assay, as previously described [20, 23]. Cultured cells were lysed in a total volume of 250 µl diluted lysis mixture consisting of one part lysis mixture, two parts deionized H2O and 0.167 mg/ml proteinase K (Invitrogen, #AM2548). Cell lysates were thoroughly mixed by pipetting up and down 15–20 times. Probe set validation was performed for each cell line and tissue to determine an appropriate lysate volume that would allow unambiguous and linear mRNA detection. The corresponding volumes were transferred to the capture plate as indicated in the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen, #MAN0018628), and the protocol was followed further. Luminescence detection was performed using the SpectroMax M5 Microplate Reader (Avantor).

For mouse tissue lysate preparation and mRNA quantification, tissues were collected at the indicated time points and stored in RNA later solution (Sigma–Aldrich, #R0901) at 4°C overnight. Punch biopsies from the specified tissues were collected (1 × 4 mm punches) and added to 400 μl of homogenizing solution (Invitrogen; QS0517) containing 0.2 mg/ml proteinase K (Invitrogen, #AM3546), in a QIAGEN collection microtube holding a 3-mm tungsten bead. The tissues were then homogenized for 10 min under 30 Hz of frequency using a QIAGEN TissueLyser II, followed by incubating at 55°C for 30 min. Lysate was then used as described above for mRNA quantification, or for siRNA accumulation (described below) or stored at −20°C.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were conducted according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocols of the University of Massachusetts Chan Medical School (IACUC protocols 202000010; 202200036) and in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. FVB female mice (7–9 weeks old) were used for all studies. The colonies were maintained and housed at pathogen-free animal facilities at UMass Chan Medical School with 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at controlled temperature (23°C ± 1°C) and humidity (50% ± 20%) with free access to food and water. When indicated, mice body weight was measured using a standard balance. All mice were ear-tagged prior to any study initiation in order to track each mice over the course of the study.

In vivo efficacy

siRNAs formulated in 1× PBS as described above, were always prepared at 200 µM concentration. The siRNAs were then administered (based on the indicated quantities in each experiment) subcutaneously between the shoulder blades of mice. In the multidosed groups, the dosage was maintained constant relative to the original weight (e.g. 20 mg/kg was fixed based on the weight on day 0). Animals were then euthanized at the indicated timepoints, and tissues were harvested and processed for mRNA quantification and siRNA accumulation as described above.

Quantification of myostatin, Troponin, and CK-MB proteins in plasma

Blood samples were collected from control or siRNA injected animals via cheek bleeding, where 20–50 µl was collected on the indicated times (weekly or biweekly). Blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) coated tubes, and then spun at 10 000 rpm for 10 mins at 4°C to separate plasma. Plasma was collected and stored at −80°C until ready to use for quantification. MSTN protein was quantified using ELISA kits (Bio-Techne/R&D systems, #DGDF80) as previously described [20], where 3 µl of plasma was used per sample and following standard provided protocol. For Troponin and creatine-kinase myocardial band (CK-MB) quantification (NBP3-00456, NBP2-75312), 2 µl of plasma was used per sample and then followed by standard provided protocol for each ELISA kit.

siRNA tissue accumulation quantification

To quantify siRNA accumulated in tissues following systemic administration, Peptide nucleic acid (PNA) hybridization assay was used on the lysate prepared for mRNA quantification, as previously described with slight modification [20, 23, 27]. All samples that were quantified were weighed to calculate siRNA accumulation per milligram of tissue. Briefly, the accumulation quantified using a custom Alexa488-labeled fluorescent PNA oligonucleotide probe (Alexa488-OO-AAUAAGGAAAGAAGAAUCUUA, PNABio) that is fully complementary to the antisense strand. Prepared lysate was used, and sodium dodecyl sulphate was precipitated from the lysate by adding 50 μl of 3 M potassium chloride followed by centrifugation for 15 min at 5000 × g. siRNAs in the clear supernatant were hybridized to the PNA probe under 95°C followed by slow cool down. Anion-exchanged chromatography was used to analyze the sample mixtures on an Agilent 1260 Infinity quad-pump HPLC with a 1260 FLD fluorescent detector, and quantification was based on compared to standard curves of spike-in siRNA in tissue lysate as previously described [3].

MRI imaging and analysis

At indicated times for MRI imaging (e.g. predosing, 6, 10, 19 weeks), mice were weighed and then put under anesthesia (2% isoflurane) followed by supine placement in MRI imaging system (Bruker Biospin MRI 7T BioSpec). Mice remained under anesthesia for the duration of imaging, where the left leg (quad and calf) was captured over 3 min of imaging for an acceptable resolution. All images were then analyzed using ImageJ to quantify volume, where the groups/mice were blinded. Briefly, quad and calf areas were highlighted as regions of interest (ROIs) and measured over 7 Z-axis slices; volume in mm3 was then calculated by summing the areas and multiplying by Z-slice thickness of 0.4 mm.

Lean mass measurement

Lean mass was measured at indicated time points using 1H-MRS (Echo Medical System) imaging system by UMass Chan Medical School Metabolic Disease Center. Mice are awake (no anesthesia) when measurements are done to avoid anesthesia-associated complications.

Grip strength measurement

Grip strength was measured using Grip Strength Meters (harvardapparatus.com) at week 10. Briefly, grip strength using all limbs for each mouse was measured once if the number does not fluctuate, and twice if there was fluctuation followed by averaging. Data were then normalized and plotted as a percentage of the control NTC–DCA–siRNA treated group.

Blood diagnostics

Blood sampling for blood diagnostics was performed when mice were terminated via cheek bleed. In total, 200 μl of blood was collected in a lithium heparin-coated BD Microtainer tube (BD, #365965) for blood chemistry test. One-hundred microliters of blood was collected in a K2 EDTA-coated bd Microtainer tube (BD, #365974) for a complete blood count (CBC) test. Blood chemistry and CBC diagnostics were conducted by the Diagnostics Laboratory in the Department of Animal Medicine at UMass Chan Medical School. ALT, BUN, platelet count, and immune cell population percentage is shown; remainder of data are available upon request.

Cardiotoxin muscle injury model

Cardiotoxin-induced muscle injury was setup as previously described [28]. Briefly, C57BL6/J 8-week old female mice (~20–22 g body weight) were injected with 40 mg/kg Mstn1928 or NTC–DCA–siRNA. Three days after siRNA dose, mice were intramuscularly injected with cardiotoxin (Naja mossambica—Sigma, C9759) in the gastrocnemius on each leg with two injection spots—top and bottom of the muscle (50 µl of 10 µM cardiotoxin per injection). Body weight and MSTN in plasma were measured throughout the study at the indicated timepoints. Animals were euthanized for 7 days post cardiotoxin injection, and tissues were collected and stored in RNALater for downstream mRNA quantification.

Graphs and statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software for Windows (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). For each independent mouse experiment, the levels of human or mouse mRNA silencing was normalized to the mean untreated group (for in vitro screening) or NTC group (for in vivo studies). In vivo data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA with a post hoc Dunnett multiple comparisons when comparing to control group only, or Tukey multiple comparisons when comparing all groups to each other. Asterisks (*) denote gene expression significance relative to NTC or indicated group with bracket (*P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001, ****P < .0001), nonsignificant was not indicated. Graphs are plotted as mean ± standard deviation (except body weight plot, which is plotted as mean ± standard error of mean).

Results

Identification of hyper-functional lead siRNAs targeting murine and human myostatin mRNAs

To develop siRNAs that enable potent gene silencing of myostatin, we used a custom siRNA design algorithm [22] to identify a panel of 184 siRNAs that target the 5′-untranslated region (5′-UTR), open reading frame, or 3′-UTR of the mouse Mstn (88 siRNAs) or human MSTN (96 siRNAs) mRNAs. The siRNAs were synthesized for in vitro screening with full chemical modification, including a 3′-cholesterol moiety on the antisense strand for efficient cellular internalization via passive uptake and rapid testing in vitro. Sequences and modifications used for these compounds are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Primary screening in differentiated murine C2C12 and human SJCRh30 cells identified several siRNA sequences that achieve 80%–95% silencing (Fig. 1). The IC50 values for lead compounds were derived from seven-point dose-response studies (Supplementary Fig. S1). Based on the IC50 values, the newly identified siRNAs Mstn1928 (IC50 = 26 nM) and MSTN2155 (IC50 = 75 nM) were the most potent murine and human siRNA leads, respectively. The previously reported sequence (Mstn1192 / MSTN1194 IC50 = 29 and142 nM, respectively) was used as a benchmark [18, 20].

Optimizing siRNA sequence and antisense strand stability improves potency in muscle and heart in vivo

To compare the in vivo activity of the lead Mstn1928 siRNA sequence to that of the previously reported Mstn1192 sequence, we synthesized Mstn1928 in the muscle-delivering DCA-conjugated siRNA scaffold (Mstn–DCA–siRNA; Fig. 1A). All antisense strands were modified with 5′-(E)-vinylphosphonate (5′-VP) for enhanced stability and loading into Ago2 as previously reported [29, 30]. We also included an exNA modification near the 3′ end of the antisense strand (Fig. 1A), which we previously reported to significantly increase exonuclease resistance and thereby enhances siRNA accumulation and activity in vivo, including in muscle and heart tissues [31]. We compared the activities of Mstn1192–DCA–siRNA with or without exNA and Mstn1928–DCA–siRNA with exNA (Fig. 1B) when injected subcutaneously at 20 mg/kg on days 0 and 3 [20]. On day 7, muscle (quadriceps and gastrocnemius) and heart tissues were harvested and processed for Mstn mRNA quantification. exNA modification enhanced the silencing activity of Mstn1192 by ~50% in all tissues tested compared to without exNA (Fig. 1B). exNA modification also improved silencing of a Jak1 targeting siRNA in quadriceps, consistent with the general ability of exNA to stabilize siRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Notably, exNA-modified Mstn1928–DCA–siRNA with the optimized sequence and stability, showed better potency achieving 80%–90% silencing in skeletal muscle and 75% silencing in the heart, highlighting the importance of optimizing every possible component of the siRNA that can lead to enhanced potency.

Chemical engineering of modification patterns greatly enhances the durability of siRNA silencing in muscle and heart tissues

The modification pattern (e.g. 2′OMe-rich versus 2′OMe-2′F balanced) of an siRNA can greatly influence distribution, efficacy, and stability [32, 33]. We therefore tested different modification patterns to improve the potency and durable activity in muscle and heart. We assessed three different patterns (Fig. 2): Pattern 1 has a 2′OMe:2′F ratio of ~55:45 with a 2′OMe-rich asymmetric tail for increased protection from degradation. This pattern was used in the screen to identify the Mstn siRNA lead sequence. Pattern 2 has a 50:50 2′OMe:2′F content, in an alternating pattern, as originally reported by Allerson et. al.[33] and adopted in the previous work for asymmetric siRNAs by Biscans et. al. [20]. Pattern 3 has ~75% 2′OMe content, which greatly boosts nuclease resistance and stability in vivo [1, 34] (Fig. 2A). Within each pattern, we generated a version with seven PS (7PS, denoted by hollow circles) modifications and one with four PS (4PS, denoted by filled circles) modifications in the tail of the antisense strand. Varying the PS content will determine whether more PS groups improve stability and silencing in muscle and heart tissues, even in the presence of exNA modifications [32].

To compare the activities of each siRNA pattern, mice were injected subcutaneously with two 20-mg/kg doses of each Mstn1928–DCA–siRNA on days 0 and 3. Plasma was sampled weekly for 4 weeks to evaluate kinetics and durability of MSTN silencing (Fig. 2B). Patterns 2 and 3 both outperformed Pattern 1 overall. Patterns 2 and 3, in both PS tail versions, achieved slightly better silencing at week 1 than Pattern 1. Whereas Patterns 2 and 3 maintained silencing throughout the 4-week analysis, Pattern 1 gradually lost silencing activity after week 1.

Interestingly, Pattern 1 with 4PS was slightly more active than with 7PS at weeks 2 and 3, before eventually converging on to the same level (Fig. 2B, left panel). We observed no significant durability difference between 7PS and 4PS in Patterns 2 and 3, except for a slight improvement observed in Pattern 2 4PS versus Pattern 2 7PS.

At week 4, animals were euthanized, and muscle/heart tissues were collected to measure siRNA accumulation and Mstn mRNA knockdown (Fig. 2C and D). All three patterns accumulated to similar levels, suggesting that differences in silencing durability are unrelated to changes in distribution (Fig. 2C). mRNA knockdown data correlated with MSTN protein levels, with Patterns 2 and 3 outperforming Pattern 1 (regardless of PS version) in all tissues (Fig. 2D). Additionally, we observed a significant improvement in silencing activity in Patterns 2 and 3 compared to Pattern 1, specifically in the 4PS version (statistics for 7PS versions not shown; Fig. 2D). We also confirmed that MSTN protein levels in quadriceps and gastrocnemius correlate with the mRNA silencing and circulating protein silencing, by achieving 60% protein silencing (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Overall, these data show that optimizing the modification pattern—2′OMe:2′F ratio and PS content—improves the potency and durability of the lead Mstn siRNA in muscle and heart.

Accumulation of lipophilic DCA–siRNA in muscle and heart has a nonlinear relationship with ascending dose

Focusing on exNA-modified Mstn1928–DCA–siRNA as a lead, we sought to optimize the dosing regimen and compare the impact of dosing strategy on delivery (pharmacokinetics, PK) and efficacy (pharmacodynamics, PD). For that, two regimens were tested (i) single injection with ascending doses (total of 1, 5, 10, 20, or 40 mg/kg), and (ii) split injection with ascending doses (total of 2, 10, 20, 40, or 80 mg/kg, injected on days 0 and 3; Fig. 3A). At 2 weeks post injection, we observed a dose-dependent reduction of MSTN protein (plasma) and mRNA (quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and heart) in both regimens (Fig. 3B and C). Interestingly, the single injection 40-mg/kg dose performed slightly better than split injection (e.g. ~60% versus 50% reduction in protein, P = 0.9; and 75% versus 50% reduction of mRNA in quadriceps, respectively, P = 0.2), suggesting that a single bolus injection, as long as it is tolerated, is a preferred administration regimen with slight improvement. Repetitive injections of the 40 mg/kg produce similar level of mRNA silencing locally in the quadriceps and gastrocnemius (~80% reduction Mstn mRNA for both 1 × 40 and 2 × 40 mg/kg, P = 0.2) with some level of improvement on MSTN reduction in circulation (~80% versus 65% reduction in MSTN for 2 × 40 mg/kg versus 1 × 40 mg/kg, respectively, P = 0.9).

Dose escalation resulted in a nonlinear increase in siRNA accumulation across all examined tissues (Fig. 3D). Notably, doubling the dose led to an approximately four-fold surge in siRNA levels within both quadriceps and gastrocnemius muscles. In contrast, the heart exhibited a more modest, ~2.2-fold increase. Furthermore, a single 40-mg/kg bolus injection significantly enhanced both siRNA accumulation in the quadriceps (1399 versus 658 fmol/mg tissue, ***P < .001) and heart (4888 versus 2779 fmol/mg tissue, *P < .05) compared to a split injection. A similar trend was observed for the percentage of injected dose (% I.D.), where single injection consistently demonstrated higher % I.D. compared to split injection (Supplementary Fig. S4). Conversely, liver accumulation was higher with split injection (Supplementary Fig. S4). Notably, % I.D. for liver did not increase when dose was increased from 20 to 40 mg/kg, suggesting more siRNA is distributed extrahepatically. These findings further support the superiority of a single bolus injection for maximizing muscle tissue exposure.

Interestingly, the heart consistently displayed higher siRNA accumulation than the quadriceps, while delivery to the quadriceps and gastrocnemius was comparable, indicating consistent siRNA distribution to similar skeletal muscle types regardless of location. This observation was further supported by tissue accumulation ratios (Supplementary Fig. S4): the gastrocnemius-to-quadriceps ratio remained consistently around 1 across all doses, whereas the heart-to-quadriceps ratio was greater than 1 at low doses, which then rapidly decreased with increasing dose.

Finally, a strong correlation was observed between siRNA accumulation and silencing activity across all three muscle tissues (Fig. 3E). The estimated 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) for quadriceps and gastrocnemius fell within the range of 100–250 fmol siRNA/mg tissue, while the heart exhibited a slightly higher IC50 of ~400 fmol/mg. Collectively, these data demonstrate that a single bolus injection of DCA–siRNA yields superior overall accumulation and potency in both skeletal muscle and heart compared to a split injection of the same total dose. These findings underscore the critical impact of dose regimen optimization on tissue-specific pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, especially for extrahepatic delivery using lipid conjugated siRNAs.

Optimized siRNA scaffold shows enhanced silencing for other targets

To evaluate whether the muscle-optimal scaffold supports potent silencing of genes other than Mstn, we designed muscle-optimal siRNAs (Pattern 2 with 4PS and exNA) targeting Janus kinase 1 (Jak1; two different sequences), methyl CpG binding protein 2 (Mecp2), and huntingtin (Htt), for which we have previously identified potent lead sequences [23, 24, 35, 36]. Four weeks post single injection of 40 mg/kg dose, we observed ~80% silencing of Jak1 (both sequences), 75% silencing of Mecp2, and 50% silencing of Htt in the quadriceps, and ~60% silencing of all targets in the heart (Supplementary Fig. S5). This supports that the muscle-optimized scaffold can be easily reprogrammed to silence any gene of interest in muscle and heart as potently as Mstn targeting siRNA.

For over two decades, myostatin’s role as a major inhibitor of muscle growth has fueled efforts to target it for muscle-wasting diseases such as cachexia and aging [37, 38]. Lowering of myostatin levels induces muscle generation and growth providing for an opportunity to phenotypically characterize the functional consequence of the siRNA-based target modulation in muscle.

To assess the impact of lowering myostatin on phenotypic changes, we treated wild-type mice with Mstn1928–DCA–siRNA in the optimized scaffold (modification Pattern 2 with 4PS and 2 exNA). We administered the siRNA subcutaneously once at 10, 20, and 40 mg/kg. Dosage amount and frequency impacts myostatin silencing’s durability and consequent phenotypic changes

Although a 40 mg/kg dose administered biweekly effectively and safely achieves near-complete myostatin (MSTN) inhibition (>95%), this high dose and frequency might be unnecessary if a single 40 mg/kg dose sustains maximal silencing for approximately 8 weeks. To investigate this, we assessed the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) profile of our muscle-optimized, myostatin-targeting siRNA by evaluating different dose amounts and frequencies. Animals were divided into groups receiving either 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks, 20 mg/kg every 4 weeks, or 40 mg/kg every 8 weeks—regimens designed to deliver an equivalent total siRNA dose over 8 weeks (Fig. 7A). We then monitored plasma MSTN inhibition for three consecutive 8-week cycles (24 weeks) under these equivalent dosing schedules, followed by a recovery phase where treatment was discontinued. The results showed that while the 10 mg/kg biweekly regimen delivered a comparable total dose by week 8, it never exceeded 75% MSTN inhibition and reached a steady state of 75% inhibition from weeks 4 to 24 (Fig. 7B). In contrast, the 20 mg/kg/4wks regimen achieved approximately 80% inhibition at week 8, followed by a steady-state inhibition of around 85% (Fig. 7B). Notably, the 40 mg/kg/8wks regimen reached ~85% MSTN inhibition most rapidly (immediately after first dose). As MSTN levels began to recover around week 8, the subsequent dose further increased inhibition to ~90%, which slowed the recovery at week 16 (~75% inhibition) compared to week 8 (~60% inhibition), suggesting an additive effect (Fig. 7B). Examining the recovery from the final dose across all groups, the 40 mg/kg/8wks, as expected, exhibited the longest recovery period of 20 weeks (an extension from the 14 weeks previously observed; Fig. 3), followed by 16 weeks for the 20 mg/kg/4wks and 14 weeks for the 10 mg/kg/2wks.

Discussion

Multiple technologies are currently being explored to enable the delivery of siRNAs that mediate robust, potent, and durable silencing in muscle tissues [3], including biologic conjugates—e.g. antibodies or peptides—that target specific receptors [3, 6–8, 41] or lipophilic conjugates that extend circulation time and enable extrahepatic delivery [3, 17]. Here, we have systematically optimized a simple and readily available lipophilic DCA-conjugated siRNA scaffold and dosing to enhance delivery and activity in muscle and heart tissues. The optimized siRNA targeting the clinically relevant muscle growth inhibitor MSTN exhibits superior potency, durability, and desirable phenotypic changes via subcutaneous administration at clinically relevant doses, significantly lower than other modalities. Our findings pave the way for potent and long-lasting gene modulation in muscle using chemically defined, lipid-conjugated siRNAs, offering a new avenue for treating muscular diseases.

Comparing biologic and lipophilic siRNA conjugation strategies for muscular delivery

Biologic and lipophilic siRNA conjugates offer distinct advantages and disadvantages for muscle delivery [1, 13, 42]. Biologic conjugates often require complex and expensive manufacturing processes due to the separate production of the biologic and oligonucleotide components [43]. Lipophilic conjugates—such as the DCA scaffold—are chemically well defined and can be synthesized in a single step, streamlining production [42]. Another major consideration is their functional behavior across species, which is critical for preclinical development. For lipophilic conjugates, serum protein binding largely drives distribution; while the exact serum composition varies among species, the overall components and binding principles are likely conserved. This consistency holds for some, but not all, peptide or antibody conjugates, which may require species-specific optimization, transferrin being a key example [16, 44].

Stability, dosing, and administration are also crucial factors to consider. Biologic conjugates typically have low drug substance content (10%–30%) [45] and are labile at elevated temperature [43]. Lipophilic conjugates, however, offer high drug substance content (95%) and superior stability, which simplifies storage and dissemination. But the hydrophobicity of lipophilic conjugates may lead to aggregation and dosing challenges, and long-term safety profile of repetitive administration is not established yet. In terms of dosing, the low solubility and high mass of biologic-conjugated siRNAs require hundreds of milliliter formulation and hours-long intravenous infusions in clinic [44, 45], whereas lipophilic siRNAs are predicted to require a much smaller clinical volume (~1–5 ml), which can be administered both subcutaneously (divided dose) or intravenously (within minutes).

Antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates (AOCs) have shown enhanced delivery and potency in preclinical and clinical studies [7, 45, 41], including better muscle-to-liver distribution compared to DCA–siRNA. For instance, a Transferrin-AOC achieved a 1:5 muscle-to-liver ratio and 1:2 heart-to-liver ratio [7], whereas DCA–siRNA distribution resulted in ~1:10 muscle-to-liver ratio. Transferrin-AOC also achieved maximum silencing at lower doses than DCA–siRNA [7]. The optimized DCA–siRNA in this work still achieves robust and durable silencing in the muscle at different dosing regimens, including 10 mg/kg biweekly, 20 mg/kg monthly, or 40 mg/kg bimonthly. Although these doses remain relatively high compared with AOCs (3 mg/kg intravenous injection for 1 week [44]), they are comparable to those reported for bicyclic peptide conjugates (10 mg/kg intravenous injection weekly, assessed 1 week after the last injection [16]). While further technical innovation is likely to reduce the required dose, the data presented here enable use of this type of compound for functional evaluation of potent gene modulation in muscle and heart, with approaching a dose range compatible with clinical translation.

Ultimately, the choice between biologic and lipophilic conjugates for muscle delivery will depend on specific clinical needs and considerations.

Systematic optimization of the siRNA scaffold components and dosing led to a significant enhancement in potency, durability, and accumulation in heart and muscle

Systematic optimization of siRNA scaffold components and dosing led to marked improvements in potency, durability, and accumulation in heart and muscle. Although previous studies demonstrated effective DCA–siRNA delivery to these tissues improving potency and durability is of essence for potential clinical translation. Notably, combinatorial optimization of sequence and modification patterns—including 5′ and 3′ end modifications, 2′OMe/2′F distribution, and phosphorothioate (PS) content—was required to improve pharmacokinetic behavior. While each factor alone had only a modest effect, their cumulative impact produced measurable gains in potency and durability. A key finding from chemical stabilization studies was that, although a balanced 2′OMe/2′F pattern (Pattern 1) had been effective previously, an alternating 2′OMe/2′F scaffold with four PS linkages (Pattern 2–4PS) yielded more durable silencing in muscle for the sequence tested. This underscores the need for iterative optimization of sequence and chemical modifications and likely explains why most approved siRNA therapeutics use distinct modification patterns. Interestingly, increasing PS content further to seven linkages (7PS), expected to enhance durability, had the opposite effect in Pattern 1 compared with 4PS, which maintained stronger silencing at 4 weeks. Similar findings have been reported for liver-targeting siRNAs [1] emphasizing the importance of tissue- and sequence-specific evaluation of modification patterns to optimize therapeutic performance.

One interesting observation was a nonlinear relationship between lipophilic DCA–siRNA dose and muscle tissue accumulation. A single bolus injection unexpectedly enhanced delivery to skeletal muscle more than to cardiac muscle. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, it is likely influenced by the maximum siRNA concentration (Cmax) in circulation, which depends on multiple biological factors such as blood flow, tissue penetration, and cell-type-specific interactions [46, 47]. Another potential explanation is that a higher bolus dose may saturate uptake in the liver (the “first sink”) and subsequently in the heart (the “second sink”), allowing more siRNA to reach skeletal muscle. This tissue-specific accumulation also correlates with LDL receptor (LDLR) expression, as DCA–siRNA associates with LDL in circulation[17]. The liver expresses the highest LDLR levels (≈58 TPM at the RNA level), followed by the heart (~19 TPM) and skeletal muscle (~4 TPM), a pattern mirrored at the protein level. Further studies are needed to dissect this effect and clarify the underlying mechanisms

Silencing the therapeutically relevant target myostatin leads to positive phenotypic observations with no adverse events

Silencing myostatin is known to induce muscle growth and improved functionality (e.g. strength), which is a desired phenotype to combat muscle wasting observed in multiple conditions (e.g. muscular dystrophies, ALS, sarcopenia, pancreatic cancer). Clinically evaluated approaches were limited to biologics, which block myostatin’s extracellular signaling by targeting the circulating protein. While demonstrating potent decrease in circulating myostatin, they often faced limitations such as off-target effects and insufficient efficacy due to complex signaling interactions governing muscle growth [38]. Only recently, positive clinical data have been reported for apitegromab, a myostatin fully human monoclonal antibody, in a 2025 clinical trial for patients suffering from spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)[48]. Developing siRNA therapy for myostatin inhibition at the intracellular level has not been extensively explored, which might result in stronger clinical efficacy resulting from a more potent and durable inhibition of MSTN.

The muscle-optimized siRNA design (Pattern 2 4PS with exNA) supported significant and durable Mstn silencing in mice with a single subcutaneous dose (>80% suppression for 6 weeks, >30% suppression for 14 weeks). This potent and durable Mstn suppression is achieved via a subcutaneous administration at substantially lower doses than previously reported for muscle-targeted lipid-conjugated siRNA applications [18, 19]. Maximal silencing of Mstn in muscle was enhanced to >95% and maintained using a repeat-dosing supratherapeutic regimen (40 mg/kg/2wks). Notably, silencing Mstn with either dosing regimen led to similar increases in muscle mass and grip strength. While we only quantified the muscle growth in the lower limbs, our systemic siRNA delivery approach was shown to achieve potent Mstn mRNA suppression across various muscle tissues following subcutaneous administration. This is particularly advantageous for muscle-wasting diseases, where muscle loss often occurs throughout the body. Similarly, our potent scaffold confers improved activity to other targets as demonstrated by three other genes. These findings suggest the broad potential of this approach for therapeutic interventions in muscle and heart diseases requiring potent gene silencing.

Phenotypic changes of myostatin silencing extend beyond gene modulation levels

We were surprised that a single, 40 mg/kg dose and repeated 40 mg/kg/2wks dosing had similar phenotypic effects. By 19 weeks, although MSTN protein levels had recovered in the single-dose cohort but remained suppressed (>95%) in the repeat-dosing cohort. Despite this difference, both cohorts showed nearly identical increases in quadricep and calf muscle volumes. Moreover, both groups showed similar increases in total lean mass by 26 weeks—near the study endpoint. These observations suggest that muscle growth induced by a single treatment of siRNA therapy may offer long-term clinical benefit. This possibility might be an improvement over the weekly or monthly administration of MSTN-neutralizing antibody therapies, which have majorly failed in clinical trials [38, 49, 50] except for apitegromab recently showing positive clinical data for SMA [48]. Future work is needed to determine a long-term dosing schedule that provides the greatest clinical benefit for siRNA therapies. However, this observation raises the possibility of a single dose with a much lower frequency (months for siRNAs compared to weeks for antibodies) to be sufficient for inducing a therapeutic effect in a clinical setting.

Furthermore, lean mass gain was observed (although not statistically significant) even at the lower 20 mg/kg dose at 26 weeks, compared to the control group, suggesting that even modest modulation of Mstn mRNA in tissues and circulation can lead to positive phenotypic outcomes.

DCA siRNA is well tolerated with no toxicity observed from neither on-target effects nor general siRNA chemistry at high doses

Although a biweekly 40 mg/kg dose exceeds anticipated therapeutic need, it allowed us to evaluate two key safety aspects: complete target inhibition and potential toxicity associated with the DCA–siRNA chemistry. Although mice received a large cumulative dose of siRNA (~520 mg/kg), we found no evidence of liver or kidney damage, changes in immune cell populations, nor cardiac hypertrophy or injury. The lack of observable gross phenotypes was encouraging and supports the safety profile of an siRNA-based therapeutic for muscle diseases, including those necessitating high and repeat dosages. Further detailed toxicology studies would be necessary for a complete assessment.

PK/PD modulation via dosage amount and frequency reveals necessary MSTN inhibition threshold for phenotypic benefits

To elucidate the ideal DCA–siRNA dosing regimen for achieving maximal muscle silencing and its broader impact, specifically on MSTN inhibition and muscle growth, we manipulated dose amount and frequency and measured the resulting effects on silencing and phenotypic outcomes. The data revealed that a sustained maximal inhibition level of approximately 80% or greater is necessary to elicit significant phenotypic alterations, including sustained gains in body weight and lean mass. Importantly, the attainment of this silencing threshold, whether through a higher dose administered at longer intervals (40 mg/kg every 8 weeks) or a slightly lower dose given at shorter intervals (20 mg/kg every 4 weeks), does not appear to be a critical factor, allowing for adaptable dosing strategies in clinical translation. It’s critical to highlight that such strong levels of silencing were only achievable following optimization of siRNA chemical modifications (component and pattern) as well as dosing strategy.

DCA–siRNA strongly inhibits MSTN and mitigates weight loss in a model of muscle wasting

We investigated whether DCA–siRNA targeting Mstn could offer therapeutic benefits in a disease model. Using a cardiotoxin-induced muscle injury model, we aimed to combat muscle and weight wasting associated with inflammatory myopathies. DCA–siRNAs effectively inhibited MSTN in the disease model, matching their potency in healthy mice. This led to significant muscle growth, preventing the characteristic muscle and body weight loss in this model. These results underscore the therapeutic potential of the DCA–siRNA platform in treating disease.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this work establishes chemically defined, lipid-conjugated siRNAs as a potent, safe, and long-lasting platform for gene modulation in skeletal and cardiac muscle, both in healthy and diseased tissue. This provides an alternate, non-biologic based avenue for oligonucleotide delivery to the muscle, further expanding the therapeutic landscape for muscular and cardiac diseases. Critically, our results underscore the paramount importance of rigorous chemical optimization—driving enhanced stability, potency, and durability—as the key to unlocking the full potential of next-generation oligonucleotide therapeutics.
