Towards a Standard Protocol for Fecal Microbiota Transplantation in Irritable Bowel Syndrome
Authors: Magdy El-Salhy, Maura Corsetti, Odd Helge Gilja, Sutep Gonlachanvit, Jan Gunnar Hatlebakk, Dag Arne Lihaug Hoff, Perttu Lahtinen, Johan Axel Lunding, Tarek Mazzawi, Tanya M Monaghan, Bodil Ohlsson, Tanisa Patcharatrakul, Yong Sung Kim, Cheol Min Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) have produced outcomes varying from no effect at all to high efficacy and durable effects over time. This review analyzed differences in the protocols used in FMT RCTs for IBS in the recently published literature with the aim of identifying the factors responsible for the success or failure of these RCTs. The results of this analysis might be useful in formulating an effective standard protocol for FMT in IBS. A systematic search was conducted in the PubMed database of the literature published in English from January 2015 to December 2023 using several search phrases comprising MeSH expressions. Those RCTs that carefully selected donors based on environmental factors that are known to affect the gut microbiota positively and ensured bacterial diversity before and during FMT produced successful outcomes. Furthermore, direct freezing of the donor’s fecal transplant, storing it at −80°C until the FMT is performed, and then thawing it at 4°C and mixing it manually appear to be factors associated with the success of FMT in IBS. Administering the donor’s fecal transplant into the small intestine results in durable effects of FMT and long-term colonization of beneficial bacteria. A standard protocol for FMT with large and durable effects should include (1) careful donor selection, (2) handling the donor’s fecal transplant in a way that preserves its microbiota contents, and (3) administering the transplant into the small intestine.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a common disorder of gut–brain interactions with an overall prevalence of 10% in the global population.1 However, the reported global prevalence of IBS has varied markedly depending on whether the Rome III criteria or the more-restrictive Rome IV criteria were used, at 9.2% and 3.8%, respectively.2 The etiology of IBS is not known, and in the absence of biochemical, radiological and endoscopic tests for its diagnosis, symptom assessment using the Rome criteria is used for diagnosis.1 IBS markedly reduces the quality of life of affected patients and is an economic burden on society.1

The bacterial profile of patients with IBS differs from that of healthy subjects in that microbial disruption characterized by a low bacterial diversity (dysbiosis) is present.3,4 IBS patients have been found to have a lower abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria such as Erysipelotrichaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Erysipelotrichaceae spp. than healthy subjects.5,6 However, IBS patients have higher abundances of Veillonella, Lactobacillus, and some Ruminococcus spp. than healthy controls.5,6 Moreover, IBS patients who do not respond to dietary regimes such as low-fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols (FODMAPs) and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)-modified diets suffer from severe dysbiosis.7 These observations have led to the belief that the gut microbiota in general and intestinal bacterial diversity in particular play major roles in the pathophysiology of IBS.

There are several reports on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that used fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) as a therapeutic intervention for IBS.8-16 The outcomes of these trials have differed markedly, from no effect at all to high efficacy and durable effects over time.8-16 Several meta-analyses performed within the last 2 years have evaluated the use of FMT as a treatment for IBS.17-26 All of the authors of these meta-analyses agreed that further investigations are needed before FMT can be used as a routine treatment for IBS in the clinic. However, the authors of some of these meta-analyses concluded that there is insufficient evidence that FMT is an effective treatment to relieve IBS symptoms,19,24 while others concluded that IBS patients may benefit from FMT only when well-defined donors and appropriate fecal administration routes are used.17,18,20-23,25,26

Most of the RCTs for treating IBS have adapted protocols designed for the treatment of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) (Table 1).8,9,11-13,15,16 The guidelines for FMT are based on expert opinions and aim at treating CDI.27-29 The variations in the protocols used for this indication seem to have little impact on the efficacy of FMT.27,30-33 Most of these protocols involve the fecal transplant being transported and/or prepared in ambient air, and delivered either to the large intestine via a colonoscope or retention enema or to the upper intestine via a nasogastric tube, nasoduodenal tube, duodenoscope, or oral enteric-coated or lyophilized capsules containing the fecal-transplant samples.34,35 The known mechanisms underlying the effect of FMT in CDI have been described elsewhere,36 and they may differ from those underlying the effects of FMT in IBS. It therefore seems reasonable to assume that the protocols that work well in CDI will not necessarily be suitable for treating IBS.

This review analyzed the different factors that could affect the outcome of FMT RCTs performed in IBS with the aim of identifying the factors responsible for the success or failure of these RCTs. Knowledge of these factors might be useful in formulating a standard FMT protocol for use in IBS with high efficacy and durable effects.

Data Search Strategy and the Articles Included

The PubMed database was searched using the following search phrase comprising MeSH expressions: “fecal microbiota transplantation and irritable bowel syndrome” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and donor selection” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and sampling and storage of the fecal samples” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and sampling and storage methods on fecal gut microbiome” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and route of administration” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and capsules” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and large intestine” OR “fecal microbiota transplantation and small intestine.” This database search based on MeSH expressions identified 1007 original articles, narrative reviews and systematic reviews, of which 47 were duplicates. Of the remaining 960 articles, 587 were excluded since they were not relevant to the topic of this study. The remaining 373 articles were accessed for eligibility, of which 102 were finally included in the study; and another 271 were excluded due to them including too-few patients, not describing randomized double-blinded placebo-controlled trials or being based on experimental animals (Fig. 1).

Donor Selection

The healthy volunteer donors used in FMT for CDI were spouses, relatives, or intimate others of the patients. The selection of the donor does not affect the clinical efficacy of FMT in patients with CDI, whereas the success of FMT in inflammatory bowel disease and other disorders is donor-dependent.30,37-40 A new definition of superdonor has been coined to describe a donor who is normobiotic and has a positive microbial signature.30,37-40 A previous attempt to use fecal-transplant pooling as a superdonor surrogate was not successful, and what constitutes a positive microbial signature in a superdonor remains unclear.38 However, using the available data on factors that might positively or negatively affect intestinal microbiota could help in predicting the characteristics required of a superdonor.

The composition of the gut microbiota in healthy subjects is determined by both genetics and environmental factors.38,41 Genetics can explain only 5-10% of the microbiota variability between individuals, which indicates the importance of the environmental factors.38 These environmental factors include diet, the frequency of antibiotic treatment, treatment with certain non-antibiotic drugs, geographical location, surgery status, smoking and depression.38,41,42 Among the environmental factors, diet appears to play a significant role in shaping the gut microbiota composition. Plant-based diets give more diverse and stable gut microbiota than diets rich in animal protein and saturated fat.43,44 Taking in consideration the differences in regional food cultures, a good donor in a regional food culture area, would probably not be suitable in another regional food culture area. The bacterial diversity is reduced due to the negative effects on the gut microbiota of aging, smoking/smoking cessation, having been born by cesarean section, having been formula-fed, receiving frequent treatment with antibiotics or having a regular intake of non-antibiotic drugs.42,45-54 On the other hand, regular exercise and consuming a sport-specific diet are known to be associated with favorable gut microbiota.55-57

In addition to considering the above-mentioned factors when selecting the donor, bacterial diversity of the fecal transplant should be tested before and during the donation period. Stability of the bacterial profile of the donor’s fecal transplant has been reported to be one of the factors associated with a favorable response to FMT.14 In an RCT of FMT in IBS with high response rates and durable effects, the bacterial profiles of the donors’ fecal transplants were stable during a 2-year follow-up (Fig. 2).14,58

Another way to select a superdonor is to test fecal transplants for bacterial diversity and profile to ensure normobiosis and to test their efficacy in a pilot study.14 However, this approach is both tedious and time-consuming.

Patient Selection

The patients selected for inclusion in the RCTs of FMT in IBS have differed in several aspects (Table 2). The number of included patients has differed markedly between studies, and statistical type-II errors should be kept in mind in underpowered studies. While the included IBS subtypes have also differed between studies, these differences seem to have had no impact on the outcomes since there were no differences in the response to FMT between IBS subtypes.10,58-60 In contrast, sex and symptom severity seem to affect the responses to FMT, with the responses being less in male patients and those with moderate IBS symptoms than in female patients and those with severe IBS symptoms (Fig. 3A and 3B).14,61,62 It was particularly interesting that studies with positive outcomes seem to have selected patients naïve to second-line treatments such as neuromodulators.

Donor–recipient compatibility is another factor that has been considered when choosing the patients and donors in FMT.63,64 Genetics regulates the expression levels of immune and regulatory proteins that affect the intestinal microbiota profile.63-66 According to this concept, donor–recipient incompatibilities could at least partially contribute to FMT failure.63 Further investigations are needed considering the minor role that genetics plays in shaping the composition of the gut microbiota compared with the environmental factors.38,41,42 45-54 Another factor that needs to be investigated is the donor–recipient gender compatibility. Sex hormones affect the gut microbiota composition, and gut microbiota interact with sex hormone.67-69 Should the donor and recipient have the same sex?

The difference in the response to FMT between females and males could be caused in part by the small intestine being longer and the large intestine being shorter in females than in males.70 The longer small intestine in females may explain why Alistipes spp. are more abundant in females than in males in both healthy subjects and IBS patients.10,71 Alistipes spp. are bile-resistant bacteria that are abundant in the terminal ileum.72-75 Alistipes spp. levels seem to be important for the initial response to FMT and for maintaining that response.59,76 Low fecal Alistipes spp. levels have been suggested to be a marker for predicting IBS patients who are not likely to respond to FMT.76 The gut microbiota composition differs between females and males and sex hormones shape the profile of gut microbiota.67-69 This could explain the difference in response to FMT between females and males.

Transplant Dose and Single Versus Repeated Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

Fecal transplant doses of ≤ 50 g resulted in a lower efficacy in FMT for CDI compared with higher doses.30,64 Increasing the dose of the fecal transplant up to 60 g was found to increase the overall response rates of IBS patients to FMT.10 While increasing the dose from 60 g to 90 g did not increase the overall response rates further,58 this did increase the response rates of male IBS patients and IBS patients with moderate IBS symptoms.77

In CDI, repeated FMT is more effective in curing patients than is single FMT.30,64,78-81 Repeating FMT does not increase the response rate but it does significantly reduce the IBS symptoms relative to single FMT during the first year after transplantation (Fig. 3B and 3C).58 However, at 2 years after transplantation the IBS symptoms did not differ between repeated and single FMT.82

Fecal-transplant Handling

It is well known that the anal sphincter and urinary sphincter relax at the same time during defecation, which allows urine and faeces to pass simultaneously.83 Therefore, to avoid mixing the fecal transplant with urine during collection, a smart toilet or an easy-to-use sampling kit should be used.84

Both fresh and frozen fecal transplants have been used in FMT for CDI.39 The time from collecting fecal samples to their administration have varied between studies, with a median of 6 hours for fresh samples and between 1 hour to 8 weeks for frozen samples.39 The fecal samples were mixed with either water or saline.39

Most fecal bacteria are anaerobic bacteria that may die when exposed to ambient air during the collection, transportation and preparation of fecal transplants.85-88 In addition, delayed processing of fecal transplants at room temperature and in ambient air could result in substantial increases in the abundance of oxygen-tolerant species, including those that are opportunistic pathogens.35 Thus, exposing the donor’s fecal transplant to ambient air at room temperature can result in its healthy microbiota profile changing to one resembling those of patients with inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes or colorectal cancer.35,89-92

Different anaerobic systems have been proposed for collecting the donor’s fecal transplant under anaerobic conditions to avoid damaging anaerobic bacteria.84,93-95 However, collecting the donor’s fecal transplant under anaerobic conditions could damage the aerobic bacteria and other microorganisms in the fecal transplant, resulting in the transplantation of only anaerobic bacteria. Processing the fecal transplant directly under strict anaerobic conditions results in non-viability of about 50% of the faecal-transplant bacterial contents.35 Direct freezing of the donor’s fecal transplant appears to be the best alternative for preserving the microbiota viability since the freeze-thaw process does not significantly alter the composition of viable microbiota.35,96 The donor’s fecal transplant should not be exposed to room temperature for more than 4 hours or kept at 4°C for no more than 24 hours.84,97 However, fecal transplants can be stored at −20°C for up to a few months or at −80°C for up to 2 years.84,97

Using 10-20% (volume/volume) glycerol as a cryoprotectant to preserve the bacterial viability upon freeze-thawing has been recommended.98,99 However, glycerol changes the fecal transplant into an insufficiently dry and sticky product, could penetrate cell membranes and change certain characteristics of the fecal transplant, and upon thawing causes medium enrichment, which may increase the levels of certain bacteria and thereby alter the original microbiota composition.100,101 Rapid deep-freezing to −80°C without using glycerol or stabilizers such as RNAlater is strongly recommended, and is now considered to be crucial factor for preserving the microbiota composition of the donor’s fecal transplant and its viability.96,102-108

While preparing a donor’s fecal transplant, the obtained sample is mixed with physiological saline solution using a handheld or laboratory blender. High-speed blending increases the exposure of the fecal transplant to oxygen and hence damages oxygen-sensitive bacterial species more than does manual homogenization.35

It is noteworthy that in the studies with the best outcomes of FMT in IBS, the donors’ fecal transplants were frozen directly at −20°C, transported frozen to the hospital and stored at −80°C for a few months before transplantation.10,58,60 Furthermore, the fecal transplants were thawed at 4°C, mixed with physiological solution at 4°C and kept at 4°C before being administered to the patients at that temperature.10,58,60 However, further validation of these procedures is required across diverse geographic regions and ethnicities in future studies.

Route of Transplantation

Administering the donor’s fecal transplant to the small or large intestine in FMT to treat CDI does not appear to impact the outcome.34,35,39,64 However, administering the fecal transplant via an enema had a lower efficacy than other administration routes severe or fulminant cases, and according to AGA guidelines there is insufficient evidence for the efficacy of FMT for CDI via enema or capsules.30,109

In FMT for IBS the administration route appears to be an important factor for the efficacy. Oral administration of the fecal transplant in the form of capsules had no effect at all, while administering into the large intestine resulted in short-lasting effects.8,9,11,12 On the other hand, delivering the donor’s fecal transplant to the small intestine resulted in positive and durable effects.10,14,60,82

Comparisons between administering the donor’s fecal transplant into the small or large intestine showed no differences in the response rates to FMT or in the reduction of IBS symptoms at 3 months after FMT.58 However, at 6, 12, and 24 months after FMT, the response rates to FMT were higher in IBS patients who received the transplant in the small intestine than in those who received it in the large intestine (Fig. 3E and 3F).58,82 Moreover, at 2 years after FMT the IBS symptoms were more severe in IBS patients who received the transplant in the large intestine than in those who received it in the small intestine.58,82 The abundance of several intestinal beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus spp. and Holdemanella biformis at 3 months after FMT increased in IBS patients who received the transplant in either the small or large intestine.58 Whereas the abundance of such bacteria decreased to the baseline levels in IBS patients who received the transplant in the large intestine, the levels of these bacteria remained high up to 2 years after FMT in IBS patients who received the transplant in the small intestine (Fig. 4).58,82 These observations indicate that delivering the donor’s fecal transplant to the small intestine results in the long-term colonization of beneficial bacteria, whereas delivering it to the large intestine does not.58,82

It was particularly interesting that administering the donor’s fecal transplants into either the small or large intestine reduced fatigue in IBS patients to the same degree over both the short and long term.58 This observation supports the assumption that different conditions need different FMT protocols.

Conclusion

Using FMT protocols designed for the treatment of CDI does not seem be an appropriate approach for treating IBS. A successful FMT protocol for IBS must consider the following factors: (1) careful selection of the donors and IBS patients, (2) directly freezing and storing the fecal material at −80°C as well as manual mixing of the fecal material, and (3) delivering the transplant to the optimal location in the gut (Table 3).

The environmental factors that contribute to beneficial microbiota in the donor’s fecal transplant should be considered when selecting the donor. Their fecal transplant should be examined before and during the donation period for bacterial diversity (normobiosis) and the stability of the bacterial profile. Regarding patient selection, IBS should be present in accordance with the Rome criteria. Patients with either severe or moderate IBS symptoms as well as male and female patients benefit the most from FMT if the transplant dose is at least 90 g.

The donor’s fecal transplant should be frozen directly without using a cryoprotectant or RNAlater, transported frozen, and kept frozen at −80°C for no longer than 2 years. The fecal transplant should be thawed at 4°C, mixed with physiological solution at 4°C and kept at 4°C for a few hours before administration. The fecal transplant should be administered at 4°C into the small intestine.
