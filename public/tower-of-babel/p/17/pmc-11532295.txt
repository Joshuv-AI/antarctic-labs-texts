The effect of unintended porosity on the first compressive stress maximum of alumina hollow sphere-filled bimodal composite metal foams
Authors: Alexandra Kemény, Tamás Bubonyi, Imre Norbert Orbulov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Bimodal composite metal foams made from Al99.5 aluminium and quasi-eutectic Sr-modified AlSi12 matrix were investigated, where the bimodality was introduced by two alumina hollow sphere sets with nominal diameters of Ø7.0 and Ø2.4 mm in various volume concentrations. The research aimed to determine the effect of the cell structure (especially the unintended porosity) on the first compressive stress maximum, depending on the applied matrix material. The various features of the cell structure were quantitatively measured in composite metal foams by filtering raw computed tomography analysis data. The casting porosity, the wetting porosity, the porosity from the various-sized hollow spheres, and the partially matrix-filled hollow spheres have been successfully distinguished based on volumetric and geometrical conditions, such as sphericity and compactness. The unintended porosity (as the sum of the casting porosity and the wetting porosity) influences the first compressive stress maximum of the Al99.5 matrix composite metal foams. In contrast, for the AlSi12Sr bimodal foams, the results correlated with the total porosity values. The first compressive stress maximum could be estimated well from the compressive yield strength of the matrix and the total porosity of the foam.

Introduction

Closed-cell foams are generally considered to have high mechanical specific energy absorption capacity due to the long plastic deformation regime of their load-deformation curve (plateau). These materials can be advantageous for lightweight construction as the weight-specific bending stiffness of engineering components can be optimised using aluminium foams. Metal foams can also be used as non-flammable vibration-damping and sound absorption elements. Furthermore, they can be used at high temperatures [1] and have a low thermal conductivity compared to dense metals. If not just one but two or even more qualities (e.g. energy absorption, sound absorption, vibration damping or biocompatibility) are taken advantage of, a metal foam is more likely to be widespread. True multi-functionality, for instance, would imply that a lightweight construction dampens noise and absorbs impact energy [[2], [3], [4]]. Composite metal foams (CMFs) offer higher mechanical strength and energy absorption capacity than conventional closed-cell foams, making them a competitive choice for the abovementioned applications.

To make the metal foams’ properties tailorable, investigating the relationship between the mechanical and the macro- and microstructural properties is crucial [5,6]. The easiest 2D method to investigate the porosity is to cut the specimens and make low-magnification optical images of them without deformation and, if needed, in a deformed state, too. With image analysis, some features (Fig. 1) can be determined in the given section, such as the total cell area (porosity), average cell size, cell geometry, and, for CMFs, the volume fraction of matrix and filler particles [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Additionally, defects in the foam (e.g. wetting voids and casting voids) or even (partially) matrix-filled filler particles can be found [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].Fig. 1Schematic figure of the characteristics and possible defects of CMFs with hollow filler particles.Fig. 1

Schematic figure of the characteristics and possible defects of CMFs with hollow filler particles.

The casting voids are caused by absorbed gases in the melt, creating inclusions during matrix solidification, whereas the wetting voids are caused by insufficient wetting conditions between the filler and the matrix material during liquid-state foam-fabricating processes. However, numerous (large enough) sections from a single specimen should be examined to obtain a representative calculation, and the results may differ from the actual 3D characteristics (e.g., due to image resolution).

Information from a selected volume can be obtained via computed tomography (CT) analysis. Because CT software tools can reconstruct 3D geometries, substantially more accurate data is available for analysis [17,18]. Some studies deal with CT results on metal foams, usually defining the total volume of cells compared to the total volume of the foam, but CT scans at different deformation values [19] or describing the cell morphology and distribution [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]] are also possible.

Kádár et al. [27] characterised composite metal foams with acoustic emission analysis during their compression. They have found that the plastic deformation of the matrix materials can occur earlier than the fracture of the ceramic hollow spheres (CHSs). In a recent study [5], they created a finite element model that predicts the strain localisation characteristics of CMFs and the early plastic yield of the matrix.

Palmer et al. [12] investigated the effect of casting temperature on the unintended porosity and mechanical properties of aluminium alloy CMFs. Aluminium vapour-coated mixed-oxide CHSs, close to liquidus casting temperature, 450 kPa pressure and the use of directional solidification have been found to be optimal for the pressure infiltration of the applied matrix and filler materials, resulting in low-level unintended porosity. The defects of the samples were investigated with scanning electron microscopy images; therefore, no quantitative results were disclosed.

Mukherjee et al. [14] investigated the effect of cooling rate on the density and mechanical properties of closed-cell AlSi6Cu4 foams. A medium cooling rate (2.5 K/s) resulted in the largest density and the highest first compressive stress maximum. The cooling rate also influenced the microstructural properties of the foams.

Kader et al. [22] investigated the structural properties of closed-cell aluminium foams by computed tomography throughout 21 interrupted drop-weight impacts. They found that the cellular structure of the material is heterogeneous, and the deformation occurs by the formation of multiple irregular collapse bands, which are concentrated in narrow bands.

Only a few articles on bimodal composite metal foams (BCMFs) can be found in the literature. Pandey et al. [28] produced hybrid composite foams with added unimodal, bimodal and trimodal size distribution microspheres by stir-casting. Tao et al. [9] fabricated BCMFs from mixed ceramic microspheres of dual size ranges and AlSi1MgMn aluminium alloy via pressure infiltration. Su et al. [29] used stir casting to produce BCMFs with different-sized Al2O3 CHSs as filler. AlSi8Cu3 alloy was applied as a matrix, and 2 wt % Ca particles were introduced to increase the viscosity and improve the wettability between the filler and the matrix. Bolat et al. [30] investigated the effect of particle size, volume fraction and heat treatment on BCMFs fabricated by pressure-assisted automated cold chamber die casting. As fillers, low-cost pumice particles with a porous inner structure were used in two size ranges. Bolat et al. [31] conducted a similar study using low-cost expanded glass particles that were more spherical than the previously used pumice particles. Májlinger et al. [32,33] fabricated BCMFs using a pressure infiltration technique from AlSi12 aluminium alloy used as matrix material and mixed oxide CHSs with similar compositions in two different diameters. These publications mainly dealt with the production and mechanical properties of the BCMFs, considering them almost perfect in the production and quality aspects (no unintended porosity) and, therefore, neglected these structural features and their effects on the properties.

The strontium modification of the AlSi12 is based on the eutectic nucleation, where Sr is absorbed by the Si grains [34], resulting in a finer grain structure. The structural changes can be confirmed by optical metallography and tensile tests [35,36]. The critical minimum Sr amount for eutectic modification is around 70−120 ppm to achieve the desired microstructure without rapid cooling during solidification. Above this range, the size reduction of Si needles is not significant [36,37].

The authors of this research previously used CT analysis to investigate the overall porosity and failure rate of the CHSs during compression of CMFs [38,39]. It has already been found that depending on the ductility of the surrounding matrix material, the plateau region shows different behaviour. The ductile Al99.5 has a monotonous plateau with high plastic deformation, and the brittle AlSi12Sr has a non-monotonous plateau with stress fluctuation, resulting in the disintegration of the foam. However, to the authors' best knowledge, there is no research yet defining the effect of the unintended porosity amount in CMFs on their mechanical properties, depending on the applied matrix material. Moreover, BCMFs have not yet been investigated in this aspect. Therefore, the current paper aims to determine the connection between the unintended porosity and the measured first compressive stress maximum by investigating a quasi-discrete geometrical packing in various filler particle volume concentrations and matrix materials. The aim has been realised by conducting 3D CT analysis to quantitatively identify the various features of the cell structure (including production imperfections) in CMFs and BCMFs before their compression. From an industrial perspective, BCMFs can provide optimised energy absorption with tailored mechanical properties and lower density than CMFs with uniform filler particles, which is advantageous in the automotive sector.

Materials and methods

BCMFs were produced in the bulk density (ρb) range of 1.48–1.74 g/cm3 via the low-pressure infiltration method from Al99.5 aluminium or 0.08–0.10 wt% Sr modified AlSi12 matrix with two sized (Ø7.0 and Ø2.4 mm) Al2O3 CHS sets as filler, obtained from Hollomet GmbH (Dresden, Germany). The production method has been detailed in previous papers by the authors [38,39]. The infiltration parameters were the following: 550 °C preheat temperature of the mould, held for 45 min, followed by the infiltration of molten Al99.5 at 720 °C, with 3 bar argon pressure for 5 s. The preheat temperature for producing AlSi12Sr BCMFs was 500 °C, the melt temperature was 690 °C, and the argon gas pressure was 3.5 bar. The preheat and infiltration times were the same as for the Al99.5 blocks. From the BCMF blocks, 56 × 56 × 50 mm specimens were cut with a diamond cutting wheel for the examinations.

In the case of BCMFs, the theoretical large sphere concentration (cL) is the most important parameter used to describe bimodality. Based on Brouwers [40], it can be calculated as (eq. (1)):(eq. 1)cL=VLVL+Vs,where VL and Vs are the bulk volume of the large and small spheres, respectively. Since the system is bimodal, it is evident that cL+cS=1, where cS is the small sphere concentration. In the following, CMFs with two unimodal (cL=0.00and1.00) and three bimodal random packings (cL=0.50,0.67and0.80) will be evaluated. The theoretical random packing mode for unimodal spheres can be defined between random close packed (RCP) and random loose packed (RLP). There is an agreement between scientists on the upper limit of unimodal RCP volume fraction as η1rcp=0.64 [41,42], while the limit of the unimodal RLP volume fraction is contested, as it is calculated in the interval of 0.525≤η1rlp≤0.577 [[42], [43], [44]]. Increasing the difference in the filler size results in an increased packing volume fraction; however, mixing spheres with large size differences can cause problems in the even distribution of the spheres. The increase of the large hollow sphere concentration is advantageous up to a particular value, which depends on the size ratio (u=dL/dS), where dL and dS are the large and small hollow sphere outer diameters, respectively. If the compressive strength (σLmax and σSmax) of the hollow spheres and the compressive yield strength (σy,Al) of the matrix materials are known, the theoretical first compressive stress maximum (σc,th) of the foams can be calculated as (eq. (4)). The first compressive stress maximum is the value where the brittle CHS failure occurs during compression, the elastic region ends, and the plateau region starts.(eq. 4)σc,th=ηLrp∙σLmax+ηSrp∙σSmax+(1−ηrp)∙σy,Al,where σLmax=F‾Lmax/(d‾L2∙π) and σSmax=F‾Smax/(d‾S2∙π). F‾Lmax and F‾Smax are the fractures forces and d‾L and d‾S are the average outer diameters of the large and small CHSs, respectively. These data have been obtained from the measurements of the characterisation of the applied CHSs, which have been carried out and published in a previous work of the research group [30]. Numerically: F‾Lmax=288±41N, F‾Smax=42±12N, d‾L=6.88±0.22mm, and d‾S=2.37±0.13mm.

The 3D properties were characterised by CT analysis in the 3D Lab at the University of Miskolc. CMFs and BCMFs with more than 50 % filler particle content and a minimum of the average particle diameter to the specimen side length of 8 were used. The samples were scanned in a YXLON FF35 μCT equipment (YXLON International X-Ray GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) with a tube voltage of 140 kV and a tube current of 95 μA. For each measurement, 1080 projections were recorded with a focus-object distance of 140 mm and a detector distance of 1050 mm, resulting in a voxel size of 18.5 μm. A 0.1 mm thick pure copper sheet was used as a filter, the detector had a frame rate of 3 Hz, and the integration time was 0.33 s. The scanned data was evaluated by the VGSTUDIO MAX 3.4.4 software. First, a 3D model was reconstructed from the scanned slices and then prepared for analysis. Foam analysis was performed after smoothing with a 2.00 value, edge thresholding with a 0.040 value, and adaptive Gauss filtering with the ideal 35 % overlap. To obtain the ideal values for the tested foam samples, overlapping between 25 % and 65 % was evaluated in 10 % steps (Fig. 3). 0 % separates volumes inside a single partially filled hollow sphere, while 65 % overlap results in combining multiple neighbouring voids. Since foam analysis disintegrates larger wetting voids into smaller ones, the volume of a larger void network cannot be detected; only the overall volume of the wetting porosity can be quantified.Fig. 3The effect of different adaptive Gauss overlap values on 3D foam analysis.Fig. 3

Furthermore, to compare the calculations with experimental results, the CMFs and BCMFs have been quasi-statically compressed after the CT scanning to measure the first compressive stress maximum (σc,m) of each specimen. The tests were executed between two flat platens, without lubrication, on an Instron 5989 universal electromechanical material testing machine (Instron, Norwood, MA, USA) with 600 kN load cell at 5 mm min−1 cross-head speed, following the ISO 13314:2011 standard [45]. Wetting voids were primarily observed between CHSs in the middle of the CMF and BCMF blocks (Fig. 5b–d), with a considerable amount of 5.9–16.3 % in the investigated specimens. These voids might be reduced with the coating of the CHSs, therefore creating a better wetting of the surfaces. Adjusting the infiltration pressure without changing the surface properties can also have beneficial effects. The casting voids occurred because of the aluminium's gas-absorbing capacity and shrinkage during cooling throughout the whole volume of the specimens, with a much lower amount of 0.3–1.4 %. The higher the melt temperature, the more gas will be absorbed in the molten aluminium, as the process occurs in atmospheric conditions. Therefore, the casting voids can be reduced by either using a vacuum for the whole process or by lowering the melt temperature, but the latter can result in insufficient infiltration quality. A measurable amount of partially filled CHSs in 3D analysis (0.5–1.8 %) indicates the presence of entirely filled CHSs too. This phenomenon results from the CHS wall porosity [46] and potential cracks on their surface combined with the low viscosity of the melt. Lowering the melt temperature and infiltration pressure can reduce the melt infiltration into the CHSs, risking proper infiltration throughout the mould. The total porosity (P) values were measured lower than the theoretical volume fractions, resulting from the effects of (i) partially or completely matrix-filled CHSs, (ii) the fact that the CHSs have a wall thickness, (iii) the non-perfectly spherical geometry of the CHSs, (iv) the imperfections of mixing, (v) the CHSs cut by the edges, which were excluded from the analysis, and (vi) the possibility of CHS movement under the steel net during infiltration [38]; therefore, the loosening of the packing.

For the theoretical stress calculations, the compressive yield strength at 0.2 % plastic deformation of the used matrix materials has been measured as: σy,Al99.5=47±1MPa, and σy,AlSi12,Sr=177±3MPa (Fig. 6a). The compression curves up to the beginning of the plateau region of the CMFs and BCMFs are shown in Fig. 6b and c.Fig. 6Compression curves of the (a) matrix materials, the (b) Al99.5 matrix CMFs and BCMFs and the (c) AlSi12Sr matrix CMFs and BCMFs.Fig. 6

The compression curves of the matrix materials show a monotonous rise with a small deviation of the compressive yield stress, which is expected from solid bulk materials. On the other hand, the first compressive stress maximum values of the foams are followed by a stress drop, as the ceramic filler particles crush under the load with a brittle failure.

The stress drop of the AlSi12Sr matrix foams is more significant than the drop of the Al99.5 matrix foams, using the same quality and amount of filler particles. This can be explained by the higher strength of the AlSi12Sr (see Fig. 6a) and the brittleness of the AlSi12Sr, which was also found previously in Refs. [38,39]. To further understand the behaviour of these cellular materials, the effect of the unintended porosity (consisting of the casting voids and the wetting voids) was examined.

The first compressive stress maximum, calculated from the bimodal sphere fraction of the CT analysis (σc,CT) results in the highest values, as it only considers the measured bimodal concentration (similarly to eq. (4)) – which is lower than the theoretical concentrations – and neglects the other measured voids. The deviation of these values from the measured ones for the AlSi12Sr matrix BCMFs (marked with blue squares in Fig. 7b) was low (0 − 3 %); however, the CMFs with the same matrix had higher absolute differences (21 − 36 %). This shows that the unintended voids have little effect on BCMFs with brittle matrix materials. On the other hand, for the ductile Al99.5 matrix samples (marked with blue squares in Fig. 7a), no significant difference was observed between the deviation of the CMFs and the BCMFs; they were rather high (46 − 56 %), similar to the theoretical values.

The first compressive stress maximum considering the unintended porosity measured in the CT analysis (σc,ui) results in reduced stress values correlated with the unintended porosity amount (5.9–16.3 %). For AlSi12Sr CMFs, this method estimates the measured value within 5 % absolute error, while for the BCMFs, an 18–22 % deviation was observed (red circles is Fig. 7b), which latter is significantly higher than for the σc,CT values. For Al99.5 matrix foams (marked with red circles in Fig. 7a), the difference was significant; however, within a narrow interval (58–62 %), suggesting that there are mechanisms other than just the interaction of the filler and the matrix that were not considered during the calculations, but the trend correlates well.

The first compressive stress maximum from the CT measured total porosity (σc,tp), as the easiest obtainable CT result shows a somewhat different trend in the function of the large sphere concentration than the previous ones (marked with yellow triangles in Fig. 7), as it neglects the presence of the hollow sphere walls and only considers the strength of the matrix material. The deviation of these values from the measured ones for the AlSi12Sr matrix BCMFs was slightly higher (3 − 9 %) than the σc,CT values; however, it is a good estimation without the need to analyse the various void types that require high computing capacity.

Comparing the results of the different calculation methods to the measured first compressive stress maximum (σc,m), a significant difference outlined based on the applied matrix material. The compression test results of the ductile Al99.5 matrix CMFs and BCMFs (marked with black triangles in Fig. 7a) correlate with the trend of the calculated values considering the unintended porosity; therefore, the CT analysis and the introduced filtering parameters are essential but not enough to estimate the properties of these foams and potentially other ductile matrix foams. The interaction effect between the filler particles and the matrix material results in an elevated stiffness and higher first compressive stress maximum compared to the compressive yield strength of the solid Al99.5. The results also show that the ductile Al99.5 aluminium matrix material reaches its plastic deformation state before the failure of the CHSs. Kádár et al. observed this effect in composite metal foams during compression by acoustic emission measurements [27].

On the other hand, for the brittle AlSi12Sr matrix CMFs and BCMFs, the exact opposite effect is shown compared to the measured values (marked with black triangles in Fig. 7b). The compressive properties of the material are dominated by the total porosity, as after the filler particles are fractured (at the first compressive stress maximum), the foam disintegrates due to the brittle fracture of the matrix material. The various void types have little effect on the behaviour of these foams; therefore, if a brittle matrix material is used, the filtering of the CT analysis is not necessary to gain information about the compressive properties of the material. With the combination of the materials used, the first compressive stress maximum of the BCMFs could be estimated with 9 % precision from only the compressive yield strength of the matrix and the total porosity of the foam. The unimodal CMFs show a larger difference, especially the only large CHS-filled foams (cL=1.00), which can be derived from the CT measurement method, considering that the excluded CHSs cut by the edges potentially occupy a larger volume, resulting in a lower measured total porosity.

These results also confirm the previous findings [38,47] about the connection between the interface layer thickness of the filler and matrix and the compressive behaviour of the CMFs. Furthermore, it adds to the current knowledge of the effects of the macrostructural properties during compression.

Conclusions

A filtering method has been created based on 3D CT analysed bimodal composite metal foams with two sized (Ø7.0 mm and Ø2.4 mm) spherical hollow filler particles, more than 50 % filler particle content, and a minimum of the average particle diameter to the specimen side length of 8, to differentiate and quantitatively determine the following types of voids from volumetric and geometrical conditions (sphericity and compactness): (i) casting porosity, (ii) wetting porosity, (iii) large hollow sphere porosity, (iv) small hollow sphere porosity, (v) partially matrix-filled large hollow sphere porosity and (vi) partially matrix-filled small hollow sphere porosity.

The unintended porosity (as the sum of the casting porosity and the wetting porosity) influences the first compressive stress maximum of the Al99.5 matrix composite metal foams, suggesting that it is favourable to filter the results of CT analysis to define the correlation between the compressive properties and the structural properties of various ductile matrix foams. The compressive yield strength of such ductile materials is insufficient for estimating the compressive properties, as the matrix reaches the plastic deformation state before the failure of the filler particles. For the AlSi12Sr bimodal foams, the results correlated with the total porosity values, and the first compressive stress maximum could be estimated well from the compressive yield strength of the matrix and the total porosity of the foam; therefore, brittle materials are less influenced by the production imperfections, such as unintended porosity.

As the unintended porosity significantly affects CMFs with ductile matrix (such as Al99.5), the production parameters can be further optimised to reduce these voids in the structure. A future plan of the authors is to define the parameters of the low-pressure infiltration technique, resulting in the lowest possible amount of unintended porosity.

The introduced methodologies of the present paper can be used for various other composite metal foams in the future, and they can be combined with machine learning to follow the deformation behaviour of such materials and potentially learn yet unknown mechanisms, which can help facilitate widespread application in the industry.
