Clinical outcomes and prognostic factors in lupus nephritis: impact of remission on renal and overall survival in a single-center study
Authors: Ula Qasem, Rama Rayyan, Laith Bdier, Taima Bader, Mohammad Alshqeirat, Heba Al-lala, Ahmad Al-Tanjy, Abdulraheem Jaber, Abdel Rahman Jaber, Izzat AlAwwa, Nisreen Abu Shahin, Marwan Adwan, Randa I. Farah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLupus nephritis (LN) is a severe complication of systemic lupus erythematosus. This study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes in patients with LN, including progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and mortality. MethodsThis retrospective study included all patients diagnosed with LN via kidney biopsy between January 2010 and March 2022. Rates of cumulative renal survival and overall survival, as well as cumulative survival based on the development of ESKD, were compared according to treatment response. ResultsA total of 125 patients with LN were included in the analysis, with a mean follow-up period of 59.9 ± 32.4 months. Overall, 58 patients (46.4%) achieved complete remission, 35 (28%) partial remission, and 32 (25.6%) did not respond to treatment. Baseline clinical characteristics were similar across groups. Patients in complete remission experienced significantly higher rates of cumulative renal and overall survival compared to those with partial or no treatment response (log-rank: P <0.001). Progression to ESKD occurred in 20 patients (16.0%), and 18 (14.4%) died. After multivariable adjustment, low C3 at diagnosis was independently associated with higher odds of treatment non-response (adjusted OR 5.29, 95% CI 1.52–21.3). Achieving complete remission was strongly protective against renal events (doubling of creatinine) (adjusted HR 0.11, 95% CI 0.03–0.39, p < 0.001). Additionally, non-responders were at higher risk of CKD, ESKD, and mortality, with P values of <0.001, 0.005, and 0.014, respectively.ConclusionsThe findings of this study emphasize the importance of early detection, prompt treatment, and vigilant monitoring to optimize patient outcomes. Achieving complete remission is critical for improved renal and overall survival in patients with LN. In particular, low C3 levels at diagnosis was associated with poor treatment response. Future multicenter prospective research is needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05203-6.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by multi-organ involvement and considerable morbidity [1]. Among its various manifestations, lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most common and severe, affecting 30–60% of adults and up to 70% of children with SLE [2]. Clinical presentations vary widely, ranging from asymptomatic urinary abnormalities, such as proteinuria, to evident nephrotic syndrome and rapid progressive renal insufficiency [3, 4]. Poorly managed, LN may progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in approximately 4.3–10.1% of cases [2]. While advancements in treatment have reduced LN-related five-year mortality rates since 1975, the rate of progression to ESKD has remained largely unchanged [3].

The prevalence and clinical outcomes of LN are influenced by ethnicity and geographic location, with the incidence and severity of LN differing across populations, with higher prevalence observed among individuals of African, Asian, and Hispanic descent compared to Caucasians [5]. LN is histologically classified into six classes (I–VI) based on renal involvement severity and prognosis. Classes III, IV, and V are associated with a higher risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, whereas class VI represents irreversible kidney atrophy in ESKD [6]. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment are critical to mitigating nephron loss, yet these efforts are often undermined by delayed intervention, nonadherence to therapy, or recurrent LN flares [7]. Prognosis also varies by ethnicity, with more aggressive or poorer long-term outcomes reported in East Asian compared to Caucasian populations [8, 9].

Despite its global significance, there is limited research on LN in the Middle East [8, 10]. Most studies from Arab populations focus on SLE rather than LN specifically, often including mixed ethnic cohorts [9]. However, LN appears to be a common and severe manifestation of SLE in the region [11]. Studies report its prevalence in 37–69% of SLE patients in Arab-dominated groups and up to 50% in exclusively Arab populations [9, 12]. In Jordan, LN is the most common form of biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis, accounting for 56% of all biopsies [13]. Moreover, historical data suggest an increasing prevalence, with demographic and pathological characteristics correlating with specific histological classes [14]. Nonetheless, data on LN outcomes in Arab populations, particularly in Jordan, remain scarce. We conducted this study to fill this gap in the literature by evaluating the clinical outcomes of Jordanian patients with LN, including CKD progression, ESKD development, and mortality rates. Additionally, it sought to identify prognostic factors associated with these outcomes.

Methods

This retrospective study, which has been approved by the institutional review board of the Jordan University Hospital, (26/2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample and patients

The study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with SLE at Jordan University Hospital between January 2010 and March 2022. SLE was diagnosed according to the applicable classification criteria based on the year of diagnosis: the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR), 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC), or 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology/ACR classification criteria [15]. Patients with class VI LN, those lost to follow-up, or those with incomplete clinical data were excluded.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from patients’ medical records and reflect baseline characteristics at the time of kidney biopsy. These included demographic and clinical variables such as age, sex, lupus duration, the presence of renal failure at presentation, and hypertension (defined as blood pressure > 140/90 mmHg). Concurrent laboratory evaluations included urinalysis, proteinuria assessment (measured via 24-hour urine collection or spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio), serum creatinine, serum albumin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and serum hemoglobin. Anemia was defined as a hemoglobin level of < 12.0 g/dL in females and < 13.5 g/dL in males. Immunological parameters were also recorded at the time of biopsy. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were considered positive at a dilution of ≥ 1:40, while anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies were considered positive at a dilution of > 1:20 as detected by immunofluorescence assay. Serum complement levels were defined as low if C3 was < 0.9 g/L or C4 was < 0.1 g/L. Indications for kidney biopsy included nephrotic (defined as proteinuria ≥ 3.5 g/day or urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR) > 3.5 with serum albumin < 2.5 g/dL). Nephritic syndrome (defined as active urinary sediment (dysmorphic haematuria > 5 RBC/hpf with or without RBC casts) plus a rise in serum creatinine). Asymptomatic was defined as hematuria (defined as > 5 red blood cells per field) with Subnephrotic proteinuria (0.5–3.5 g/day) at time of biopsy. Histological patterns of LN were categorized as classes I to VI as per the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society or as lupus glomerulonephritis [16]. Patients with class III or class IV were considered to have proliferative LN.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as an increase in serum creatinine of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, an increase to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or reduced urine output. CKD was defined as evidence of kidney damage or estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for more than 3 months.

Treatment, management, and response rate

The Institutional protocol cyclophosphamide (CYC): Euro lupus protocol IV pulse, 0.5 g every 2 weeks for 6 doses; mycophenolate mofetil (MMF): target 2–3 g/day; oral prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day with protocolized taper) and hydroxychloroquine used to all SLE patients per The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidance unless contraindicated. Azathioprine induction was used predominantly during the earlier study period (2010–2014) or in patients with relative contraindications to MMF or CYC, including planned pregnancy, prior cytopaenia, or limited formulary access.

Treatment options were categorized into induction and maintenance therapies. Induction treatments included MMF, corticosteroid, hydroxychloroquine, CYC in addition to azathioprine (AZA) and ciclosporin if MMF or cyclophosphamide are contraindications. Maintenance therapies included MMF, AZA, and ciclosporin. Response to treatment was defined as either partial remission, complete remission, or no response to treatment. Partial remission was defined as a 50% reduction in baseline proteinuria to ≤ 3 g/day, uPCR) or protein in 24 h urine collection and stabilization or improvement of kidney function (+ 10–15% of baseline) within 6–12 months. Complete remission was considered as proteinuria of ≤ 0.5 g/day (as measured by uPCR or protein in 24-hour urine collection) and stabilization or improvement of kidney function (+ 10–15% of baseline) within 6–12 months. No response was defined as failure to achieve partial or complete remission within 6–12 months of therapy initiation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and were compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as means ± standard deviations and were compared using Student’s t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Log-transformation was applied if/when parametric testing was required.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of complete remission and treatment non-response. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine independent predictors of renal survival, overall survival, and the combined endpoint of ESKD or death. All multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, ISN/RPS class, induction therapy, baseline serum creatinine, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR), low C3, hypertension, AKI at biopsy, late-onset LN, and treatment era.

Patients were stratified into early-onset LN (≤ 2 years) and late-onset LN (> 2 years) based on the interval between SLE diagnosis and biopsy-proven LN, consistent with prior literature [15, 17]. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were employed to evaluate overall survival and cumulative renal survival based on clinical response and ESKD progression. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.Missing data were handled using available-case analysis; patients were excluded only from analyses involving variables for which their data were missing.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 125 patients with biopsy-confirmed lupus nephritis (LN) were included, with a mean follow-up period of 59.9 ± 32.4 months. The majority of patients were female (84.8%). The mean age at LN diagnosis was 30.6 ± 12.2 years, with a mean interval between systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) diagnosis and LN development of 3.6 ± 4.4 years. At diagnosis, 72.8% of patients were hypertensive, 77.6% were anemic, and 29.6% presented with AKI. The most common indications for kidney biopsy were nephritic syndrome (40.8%) and nephrotic syndrome (20.8%). Additionally, 24.8% of patients developed LN more than two years after the diagnosis of SLE.

The most common histological class was ISN/RPS Class IV LN (48.0%), followed by Class V (20.0%). One patient (0.8%) with Class VI LN was excluded from the analysis. For induction therapy, most patients received MMF (68.0%), while 15.9% received intravenous CYC. Maintenance therapy consisted predominantly of MMF (56.8%), followed by AZA (13.5%) (Table 1).

Overall treatment outcomes demonstrated that 58 patients (46.4%) achieved complete remission (CR), 35 patients (28.0%) achieved partial remission (PR), and 32 patients (25.6%) had no response (NR). During follow-up, 20 patients (16.0%) progressed to ESKD and 18 patients (14.4%) died (Table 2). Response rates differed according to LN histological class. Patients with Class III LN demonstrated the highest rate of complete remission (66.7%), whereas Class V LN was more frequently associated with partial remission (44.0%). Non-response rates were highest among patients with Class IV LN (32.8%). (Supplementary Table 2)

Predictors of treatment response

Univariable analysis demonstrated a significant association between low C3 levels at diagnosis and lack of treatment response (P = 0.006). Moreover, non-responders had significantly higher risks of developing CKD, ESKD, and mortality (P < 0.001, P = 0.005, and P = 0.014, respectively) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis evaluating predictors of complete remission (CR), male sex remained significantly associated with higher odds of achieving CR (adjusted OR 3.47, 95% CI 1.03–12.12, P = 0.044). No significant associations were observed for LN class, induction regimen, serum creatinine, proteinuria, low C3 levels, hypertension, AKI, or delayed-onset LN (Supplementary Table 3).

In contrast, older age at biopsy, lower urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR) at biopsy, low C3 levels at diagnosis, LN class, and treatment era were significantly associated with non-response in the multivariable model (Table 3). Older age at biopsy was associated with higher odds of non-response (adjusted OR 1.05 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.10, P = 0.047), while lower uPCR at biopsy was also associated with non-response (adjusted OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.57–0.89, P = 0.006). Low C3 levels at diagnosis significantly increased the odds of non-response (adjusted OR 5.29, 95% CI 1.52–21.26, P = 0.01). Patients with Class V LN had significantly lower odds of non-response compared with those with Class IV LN (adjusted OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.03–0.72, P = 0.023). Additionally, patients treated during the 2017–2022 era had significantly higher odds of non-response compared with those treated during 2010–2016 (adjusted OR 4.13, 95% CI 1.43–13.11, P = 0.01).

Renal and overall survival

Survival analysis demonstrated that patients who achieved partial remission had lower renal survival compared with those who achieved complete remission (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). However, patients in partial remission demonstrated similar overall survival to patients in complete remission, whereas non-responders experienced significantly poorer long-term survival outcomes (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, patients who developed ESKD had a significantly increased risk of mortality (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

In a multivariable Cox regression analysis evaluating renal survival, CR and serum creatinine at biopsy emerged as the only independent predictors of renal outcomes. Achieving CR was associated with a markedly reduced risk of renal events (doubling of creatinine) compared with non-response (adjusted HR 0.03, 95% CI 0.004–0.28, P < 0.001), whereas higher serum creatinine at biopsy was independently associated with poorer renal survival (adjusted HR 3.1, 95% CI 1.82–5.30, P < 0.001). Partial remission and low C3 levels were not independently associated with renal survival outcomes (Table 4).

Comparison of MMF and cyclophosphamide induction therapy

In subgroup analyses comparing MMF and CYC induction therapies, no statistically significant difference was observed between treatment regimens regarding achievement of complete remission or renal survival. In the logistic regression model, CYC induction was not significantly associated with complete remission compared with MMF (adjusted OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.09–1.41, P = 0.142) (Supplementary Table 5). Similarly, in Cox regression analysis for renal survival, induction with CYC was not associated with significantly different renal outcomes compared with MMF (adjusted HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.12–1.47, P = 0.192) (Supplementary Table 6).

Finally, in the univariate analysis, patients diagnosed with LN more than two years after SLE diagnosis showed a trend toward a higher rate of progression to ESKD compared with those diagnosed within two years of SLE onset; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4 and 5; P = 0.27). Furthermore, late-onset LN was not an independent predictor of treatment non-response after multivariable adjustment (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights by filling the gap in the literature on the clinical outcomes of LN in a cohort of Jordanian patients with SLE, particularly regarding the progression to CKD, ESKD, and mortality. Our findings highlight the significant role of treatment response in determining renal and overall survival, with complete remission associated with the best outcomes, as is to be expected.

A literature review indicates that 31–48% of patients develop LN following a diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), with this rate rising to 50.4% in Arab countries [18]. The interval between SLE diagnosis and the development of LN is an important factor in understanding disease progression, with crucial implications for patient management [7]. In our study, the mean time from SLE diagnosis to LN development was 3.6 ± 4.4 years. This finding aligns with previous studies, although variability in the timing of LN onset is evident across different cohorts. For instance, in one study, 700 SLE patients (38%) were diagnosed with LN, of which (81%) were diagnosed at study enrollment and (19%) during the follow-up period (mean follow-up period: 4.6 years) [2]. Galindo-Izquierdo et al. found that (50%) of 1,092 patients developed histologically confirmed LN within the first 12 months of SLE diagnosis, and (74%) within five years, highlighting an increased risk of LN development in the early years following SLE diagnosis, as the majority of the patients develop LN within the first year of diagnosis with SLE [19]. Another study involving 180 patients with renal involvement out of 436 SLE patients found that (36%) presented with renal involvement soon after SLE diagnosis, while (31%) developed renal abnormalities more than five years later [16].

Our study found that (24.8%) of patients developed LN more than two years after their SLE diagnosis. Survival analysis revealed a significant difference in ESKD development between patients who developed LN within the first two years versus those who developed it later. These findings partially align with previous reports indicating that patients with later-onset LN may experience better kidney survival [16, 17]. In contrast to previous studies reporting poorer overall survival in patients with late-onset disease, our study found no significant difference in overall survival between the two groups (Fig. 5). This suggests that, in our cohort, the timing of LN onset may influence renal trajectory more than all-cause mortality.

Our patient population exhibited a renal survival curve similar to that described by Chen et al., where patients in partial remission had a lower renal survival rate compared to those in complete remission [17]. Furthermore, patients who did not respond to treatment showed the lowest renal survival rate, consistent with our findings [17]. In our study, patients in complete remission and those in partial remission demonstrated similar long-term survival rates, aligning with Chen et al.’s results, which reported a 10-year survival rate of 95% and 76% for the complete remission and partial remission groups, respectively [17]. Additionally, patients who showed no response to treatment in our study experienced significantly poorer outcomes, a trend consistently observed in other research [17, 20, 21].

Beyond the long-term associations between remission status and survival, our 6-month landmark analysis underscores the time-sensitive nature of treatment response. Patients who achieved complete remission within the first six months had a significantly lower risk of subsequent renal events compared with non-responders (adjusted HR 0.05, 95% CI 0.01–0.37), whereas partial remission at this landmark did not confer significant protection. This reinforces the concept that early, complete suppression of disease activity is critical, and it supports the use of stringent remission targets within the first 6 months of therapy. Failure to achieve CR by this window may identify patients who require treatment escalation or alternative immunosuppressive strategies [22].

Childhood-onset lupus nephritis is generally characterized by more aggressive disease and worse long-term renal outcomes compared to adult-onset disease [23]. In contrast, our adult Jordanian cohort demonstrated a relatively favorable ESKD rate (16%) despite a high prevalence of Class IV LN (48.0%). This difference may be attributed to variations in age at onset, genetic background, and treatment practices.

In addition to treatment response, higher serum creatinine at biopsy emerged as a robust independent predictor of renal failure and the combined endpoint of ESKD or death (adjusted HR 2.43). This finding highlights the prognostic weight of baseline renal function and underscores the importance of early detection and prompt renal biopsy before significant irreversible nephron loss occurs. Together with our landmark analysis, these results suggest that both the ‘starting point’ (baseline creatinine) and the ‘early trajectory’ (6-month remission status) are the two dominant drivers of long-term renal prognosis in LN [24].

When placed in the regional context, our CR rate (46%) and ESKD rate (16%) fall between those reported in Pakistani cohorts [25] and a recent Saudi study [8], while our proliferative-class predominance (62.4%) aligns with Egyptian data [26]. These comparisons underscore that Jordanian LN patients share the high proliferative burden seen across Arab populations, yet prospective, multi-center Arab registries are needed to disentangle genetic, environmental, and practice-pattern drivers of these outcomes.

The role of serum C3 levels as a prognostic factor in LN remains controversial. Recent studies suggest that persistently low C3 levels (even after treatment) are an even stronger predictor of ESKD [27]. Some studies have found no significant relationship between C3 levels and outcomes in LN patients [30–32], while others have identified a significant association [28]. In our study, we found that lower C3 levels at the time of diagnosis were significantly linked to a lack of response to treatment, as opposed to patients in complete remission. This might be due to the fact that C3 serves as an indicator of complement system activation, which is often heightened in LN and correlates with increased disease activity [29]. Persistent low C3 levels may reflect ongoing inflammation and immune dysregulation, contributing to more severe renal involvement and a poorer response to treatment. Alternatively, reduced C3 could suggest a more aggressive or refractory form of the disease, which may not respond well to standard immunosuppressive therapies [30]. Notably, although low C3 predicted non-response in our logistic model, it was not independently associated with renal survival in the multivariable Cox analysis (Table 4), suggesting that its adverse renal impact is mediated primarily through treatment resistance rather than direct, ongoing complement-driven nephron destruction.

Unexpectedly, multivariable analysis identified the 2017–2022 treatment era as an independent predictor of non-response compared with 2010–2016. Whether this reflects temporal changes in referral patterns (e.g., more severe or refractory cases being referred in recent years), evolving biopsy thresholds, or differences in undocumented treatment protocols remains unclear. Given the risk of residual confounding by indication, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and warrants validation in prospective cohorts.

Our findings also suggest that patients with LN who progress to ESKD have an increased risk of mortality. This is consistent with previous studies from Australia, New Zealand, China, and Saudi Arabia, which have demonstrated that patients with ESKD, particularly those requiring dialysis, have poorer overall survival than patients with malignancies or cardiovascular disease [12, 31, 32].

The increased risk of CKD, ESKD, and mortality in non-responders to treatment in our study aligns with findings from other studies from Egypt, India, and Greece, highlighting the need for personalized treatment strategies and close monitoring to optimize outcomes and minimize renal complications [33].

Taken together, our findings support a risk-stratified approach to LN management in Arab populations. Patients presenting with low serum C3 at diagnosis should be flagged as having a higher likelihood of treatment resistance and may benefit from more intensive initial therapy or earlier transition to alternative agents if response is suboptimal at 3–6 months. The strong protective effect of complete remission—particularly when achieved early—reinforces the need for stringent treatment targets and frequent monitoring during the induction phase. Finally, given that baseline serum creatinine and early remission status were the dominant independent predictors of renal outcomes, efforts to reduce diagnostic delays and initiate prompt, goal-directed therapy may be the most effective strategy to reduce ESKD incidence in this population.

While this study provides valuable insights into the clinical course of LN in Jordan, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, as one of the largest referral centers in the region, the cohort likely captures a broad cross-section of the Jordanian population. Second, the retrospective nature of the study introduces potential selection bias, missing data, and treatment heterogeneity. Third, important histopathological details, including NIH Activity and Chronicity Indices, were not systematically available in the pathology records. Fourth, exact dates of treatment response and renal flares were not captured, precluding time-to-response and flare analyses. Finally, ESKD and mortality represent competing risks that were not formally modeled, which should be considered when interpreting the survival estimates. Despite these limitations, this study possesses notable strengths. Crucially, it contributes robust data on an Arab cohort, a demographic that remains underrepresented in global LN literature. Within this specific population, our analysis emphasizes key clinical dynamics, particularly the prognostic role of complement levels and the critical timing of remission. Coupled with a relatively large sample size and an extended follow-up period that surpass previous regional reports, these insights provide a strong foundation for future research. Furthermore, the comprehensive detailing of specific induction and maintenance therapies offers a clear understanding of contemporary LN management strategies, which can help inform and improve clinical practice globally.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical role of achieving complete remission—particularly within the first six months—in the management of patients with LN. Baseline serum creatinine and early treatment response emerged as the dominant independent predictors of renal survival. Moreover, low C3 levels at diagnosis were independently associated with a higher likelihood of treatment non-response, identifying a subgroup that may require intensified or alternative initial therapy. Patients who failed to respond to treatment were at an elevated risk of developing CKD, progressing to ESKD, and experiencing mortality. These findings suggest that late-onset lupus nephritis is associated with poorer kidney survival but not reduced overall survival, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis, timely initiation of appropriate therapy, and close long-term monitoring to optimize renal outcomes. Future prospective research is needed to validate these findings and evaluate early risk-stratified treatment protocols in Arab populations.
