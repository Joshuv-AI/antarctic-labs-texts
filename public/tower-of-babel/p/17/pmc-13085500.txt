Evaluation of antiviral activity indicators in selected medicinal plants used traditionally for treatment of respiratory viral infections
Authors: Sithembiso Lethukuthula Ndwandwe, Vuyisile Samuel Thibane, Sechene Stanley Gololo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe continued usage of plants for treatment of diseases and healthcare purposes dates back from time immemorial, including for treatment of respiratory viral infections. The current study was aimed at the evaluation of the antioxidant, anti-neuraminidase and anti-inflammatory properties of selected medicinal plants (Artemisia afra, Lippia javanica, Tetradenia riparia and Tulbaghia violacea) used traditionally for treatment of respiratory viral infections.MethodsThe plants were extracted using water and 80% ethanol. Phytochemical screening of the plant extracts was evaluated using standard protocols. The quantitative antioxidant activity evaluation was performed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay and the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) assay. Anti-viral potential was evaluated using anti-neuraminidase inhibition assay. The anti-inflammatory effect of the plant extracts was determined by their activity against cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) using colorimetric COX-2 inhibitor screening assay kit.ResultsThe phytochemical screening results of extracts tested showed the presence of different classes of secondary metabolites that included coumarins, flavonoids, terpenoids, saponins, steroids, glycosides, and phenolic compounds. The results showed possession of antioxidant activity by the leaf extracts of the selected medicinal plants through dose dependent inhibition of DPPH, H2O2 and FRAP free radicals. The possessed antioxidant activity was shown at varying strengths amongst different plants as demonstrated by differences in determined IC50s. In addition, the plant extracts showed notable inhibitory activity on the enzymatic action of viral neuraminidase with the lowest IC50 of 50 µg/ml recorded. Furthermore, the plant extracts showed possession of anti-inflammation activity demonstrated by C0X-2 inhibition activity.ConclusionsThe results of the current study showed possession of antioxidant, anti-neuraminidase, and anti-inflammatory properties by the extracts of Artemisia afra, Lippia javanica, Tetradenia riparia and Tulbaghia violacea.Keywords: Phytochemical screening, antioxidant; Anti-neuraminidase; Anti-inflammation; Cyclooxygenase; Respiratory viral infections

Background

The continued usage of plants for treatment of diseases and healthcare purposes dates back from time immemorial [1]. Despite the progress made in the treatment and therapeutic strategies by modern medicine, the incidence, morbidity, and mortality caused by respiratory viral infections remains a major global challenge [2, 3]. Several plants are reported to be ethnobotanically used for treatment of respiratory viral infections and such medicinal plants included A. afra, L. javanica, T. riparia and T. violacea. However, the efficacy or potential efficacy of many such medicinal plants traditionally used for treatment of respiratory complications emanating from viral infections remains not evaluated. In addition, the possible anti-viral mechanisms of many of these plants extracts or their phytochemical compounds remains not yet characterized, which informed their current selection for investigation in the current study [4–6]. Plants efficacy against respiratory viral infections is usually evaluated through assays that involve evaluation of activity based on the two anti-viral mechanisms of action, namely the viral infection inhibition and the post-infection anti-inflammatory properties. Some plants components or bioactive ingredients may possess or exhibit either of the two mechanisms or both. As such, plants that possess inhibitory effects against viral infection aiding proteins/enzymes like neuraminidase and/ or anti-inflammatory properties are regarded to have efficacy against respiratory viral infections [7, 8].

There are different types of diseases caused by respiratory viral infections, and several traditional medicines have been tested on these viruses for their presumed antiviral activity. Several phytochemicals from medicinal plants extracts, including the coumarins, flavonoids, organosulfur compounds, thiophenes, terpenoids, polyphenolics, saponins, peptides and proteins have been found to possess antiviral activity via different mechanisms [9, 10]. Therefore, the phytochemical composition screening of medicinal plants provides invaluable information in the justification for the continued investigation of their potential anti-viral properties. The antiviral mechanism of these phytochemicals found in medicinal plants may follow various pathways, including their antioxidant activities, scavenging capacities, inhibition of the virus penetration into the host cell, or inhibiting the viral reproduction and by competing for pathways that activate intracellular signals such as anti-inflammatory activity [9, 11]. Therefore, the pathways that can be investigated when determining potential efficacy of medicinal plants against respiratory viral infections as is the case for influenzae virus, is through evaluation of the medicinal plants extracts or phytoconstituents’ antioxidant potential, ability to inhibit neuraminidase activity and exhibition or possession of anti-inflammatory properties.

Respiratory viral infections can, therefore, be targeted directly or indirectly. One of the indirect viral reduction targeting looks to address the host response rather than solely focusing on the virus, this is done by the plant’s antioxidants restoring the redox imbalance that act against amplifying the spread of the virus. Oxidative stress occurs due to increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production during viral (e.g., influenza virus) infection. This viral infection-induced oxidative stress may contribute to several aspects of pathogenesis of respiratory viral infection, including tissue damage, inflammatory response, and apoptosis. Antioxidants that can block oxidative damage may alleviate respiratory viral infection [12, 13]. Therefore, evaluation of possession of antioxidant properties by medicinal plants extracts or phytoconstituents investigated for anti-viral potential becomes of importance.

The influenzae virus is one of the most implicated respiratory complications causing virus upon human infection. Influenzae virus infection can affect the respiratory tract directly or through the damage that results from the response of the immune system [14]. The enzyme, neuraminidase, has been reported to play a deciding role during the influenzae virus host infection. The inhibition of neuraminidase is an attractive direct target for anti-influenza drug design as it halts viral propagation by interfering with effective shedding. Virus neuraminidase influences the release of newly synthesised progeny virion from the infected host cells. It also encourages virus replications and spread within the infected cells [15]. The evaluation of medicinal plants’ possession of anti-neuraminidase activity is thus informative on the phytoconstituents from such plants’ exhibition of anti-viral activity and potential inhibition of influenzae virus replications. Such bioassay, the anti-neuraminidase activity assay, could therefore, be applicable universally for indication of potential anti-viral activity against respiratory viral infections.

One other anti-viral activity mechanism or pathway is the targeting of the host post-infection inflammatory response in the search for new therapeutics against intense viral respiratory infections [14]. Most of the antiviral herbs have built-in immune-stimulating and inflammation-modulating effects, meaning they can help prevent or regulate immune overreaction (“cytokine storm”) resulting from respiratory viral infections while simultaneously helping fight infections [16]. Prostaglandins are important mediators of the body’s response to pain and inflammation and are formed from essential fatty acids found in cell membranes. This reaction is catalysed by cyclooxygenase, a membrane-associated enzyme occurring in two isoforms, cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) that is constitutively expressed and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) that gets induced in the inflamed tissue [17]. Therefore, inhibition of cyclooxygenase presents a therapeutic target for post-infection cytokine storm induced hyperinflammation and an indication of anti-inflammation properties.

The search for new therapeutic agents against respiratory infections is needed to date, more especially with viral respiratory related pandemics such as the swine influenza and the recent COVID-19, which include evaluation of several medicinal plants for that purpose [16, 18–20]. Also, there is a need to fill the gap regarding the elucidation of the anti-viral mechanisms for therapeutic agents that possess antiviral potency. Such mechanisms could include the viral interaction mode through inhibition of viral infection and/or post infection mode through anti-inflammatory properties. The elucidation of the antiviral activity mechanisms would be important in informing antiviral product development and application strategies. The current study was therefore, aimed at the evaluation of the antioxidant, anti-neuraminidase and anti-inflammatory properties in selected medicinal plants used traditionally for treatment of respiratory complications. The findings of the current study contribute to knowledge regarding the anti-viral mode of action exhibited by phytoconstituents within the extracts of the selected medicinal plants different parts or tissues.

Methods

Plant material preparation and extraction

The study was approved by the Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University Research Ethics Committee (SMUREC/S/498/2023 PG). Plants materials were collected from wild habitations in different communal land non-protected areas of KwaZulu Natal, South Africa; A. afra and L. javanica were collected in Ladysmith (28°28’47.3"S 29°45’19.0"E), T. riparia in Durban (29°47’47.0"S 30°53’49.6"E) and T. violacea in Ladysmith (28°35’40.5"S 29°47’47.4"E). Collection of the plant material was done in compliance with the Guidelines for integrating the conservation and sustainable use of biodiversity in access and benefit-sharing approaches in South Africa [21]. The plants were authenticated by plant taxonomists based at the South African National Biodiversity Institution (SANBI) Pretoria herbarium and voucher specimen were prepared and deposited: T. violacea Harv. (Confirmed by Mothogoane MS; 5/2024; Voucher specimen number: PRE2024-0003), A. afra Jacq. ex Willd. (Confirmed by Makhoba TC; 8/2024; Voucher Specimen number: PRE2024-0001), L. javanica (Burm.f.) Spreng. (Confirmed by Simali S; 8/2024; Voucher Specimen number: PRE2024-0002), T. riparia (Hochst.) Codd (Confirmed by Simali S; 10/2024; Voucher Specimen number: PRE2024-0004). The plant material was then dried at room temperature and ground to fine powder. The ground plant material was then extracted using water and 80% ethanolic (v/v) as solvents applying serial cold maceration for 24 h on an orbital shaker. Resultant extracts were filtered using a Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtered ethanolic extracts were evaporated using a rotatory evaporator and dried under a stream of air while aqueous extracts were concentrated using a freeze dryer. Concentrated extracts were then stored in airtight containers and kept in the dark until further usage.

Phytochemical analysis

The plants extracts were subjected to qualitative phytochemical analysis using relevant standard chemical screening tests for determination of the presence of coumarins [22], flavonoids [23], glycosides [24], quinones [22], steroids [25], terpenoids [24] and saponins [26]. The results were recorded in a tabular form indicating presence or absence based on a positive or negative test outcome, respectively. Quantitative phytochemical analysis was also done for the determination of total phenolic content of the plants’ extracts using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [27].

Evaluation of antioxidant activity

The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay

The ability of plant extracts to donate hydrogen atoms for radical scavenging activity was tested by decolorizing a purple methanol solution of 2,2-diphenyl-1- picrylhydrazyl (DPPH). In the presence of antioxidants, DPPH generates a purple colour in methanol solution and diminishes to shades of yellow. DPPH assay evaluation of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts was done using a method described by Baliyan et al., [28]. The aqueous and ethanolic extracts, including the positive control standards (ascorbic acid and quercetin) were dissolved in ethanol then serially diluted to different concentrations. A volume of 150 µL (250, 125, 62.5, 31.3, 15.6, 7,8 (µg/ml)) for all the plant extracts of different concentrations was then put in the 96 well plate. Then, 50 µL ethanol solution of DPPH (0,2 mg/ml) was added into all the wells and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Absorbance was then measured at 517 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microtiter plate reader (Molecular devices, USA). The percentage inhibition of the extracts was calculated using the formula:

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) assay

The scavenging activity of natural antioxidants found in plant extracts against hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) has been widely tested by detecting the decrement of H2O2 in an incubation system containing H2O2 and the scavenger using the classical UV absorbance. H2O2 assay evaluation of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts was determined using the method described by Ngonda [29]. with minor modifications. A solution of H2O2 (20 mM) was prepared in phosphate buffer saline (PBS), pH 7.4. The aqueous and ethanolic extracts and the positive control standards, ascorbic acid and quercetin were dissolved in ethanol then serially diluted to different concentrations. An amount of 100 µL for all the diluted samples of different concentrations (1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.3, 15.6, 7,8 (µg/ml)) was then put in the 96 well plate. Then, 100 µL of PBS and H2O2 solution of was added into all the wells and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 560 nm against a blank solution containing phosphate buffer without hydrogen peroxide using a SpectraMax iD3 microtiter plate reader (Molecular devices, USA). Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay

At low pH, reduction of Fe3+/ferricyanide complex to the ferrous (Fe2+) form (which has an intense blue colour) can be monitored by measuring the change in absorption at 593 nm. The change in absorbance is therefore, directly related to the combined or total reducing power of the electron donating antioxidants of the plant extracts present in the reaction mixture. FRAP assay evaluation of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts was done using a method by Lim et al. [30], . with minor modifications. The extracts and the positive control standards, ascorbic acid and quercetin were dissolved in ethanol then serially diluted to different concentrations. About 30 µl of the plant extract solution were added to a microtiter plate (1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.3, 15.6, 7,8 (µg/ml)). Then, 40 µl potassium phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 7.2) and 40 µl potassium ferricyanide (1% in phosphate buffer, w/v) was added to each well of the microtiter plate. The microtiter plate was then covered with foil and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. After the incubation period, 40 µl trichloroacetic acid (10% in phosphate buffer, w/v), 100 µl dH2O and 100 µl FeCl3 (0.1% in phosphate buffer, w/v) was added to the microtiter plate and the plate was recovered with foil and further incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The antioxidant activity was monitored by measuring the change in absorption at 593 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microtiter plate reader (Molecular devices, USA).

Anti-viral neuraminidase inhibition activity assay

The Amplex Red Neuraminidase (Sialidase) assay kit utilizes Amplex Red to detect H2O2 generated by galactose oxidase action, the end result of neuraminidase action. The H2O2 then, in the presence of horseradish peroxidase (HRP), reacts with a 1:1 stoichiometry with Amplex Red reagent to generate the red-fluorescent oxidation product, resorufin. Resorufin has absorption and fluorescence emission maxima of approximately 571 nm and 585 nm. Samples of different concentrations of the extracts were prepared using a DMSO and Deionized water solvent mixture (10:90). A volume of 50 µL (1000, 500, 250, 125 (µg/ml)) of each extract was pipetted into separate wells of a 96-well microplate. Then added 50 µL of the working solution containing 50 µM Amplex Red reagent, 0.1 U/mL HRP, 2 U/mL galactose oxidase, 250 µg/mL fetuin and a 1X reaction buffer to each microplate well containing the samples and controls. Incubated the reaction for 30 min at 37 °C, protected from light. Oseltamivir was used as the control or anti-neuraminidase standard compound. The absorbance was measured in a microplate at 560 nm. Anti-inflammatory COX-2 Inhibition activity assay

The anti-inflammatory effect of the selected plant extracts and the formulated concoction against cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibition was determined using colorimetric COX-2 inhibitor screening assay kit (Item No. 701080, Cayman Chemical).

The COX reaction Procedure

The Cox-2 enzyme was prepared by mixing 80 µl of enzyme with 320 µl of reaction buffer. Heme solution was prepared by mixing 40 µl of heme with 960 µl of reaction buffer. Arachidonic acid solution with a concentration of 200 µM was prepared by combining 50 µl of arachidonic acid with 50 µl of potassium hydroxide. Stannous chloride solution was prepared by dissolving 125 mg of stannous chloride in 2.5 ml of hydrochloric acid. COX reactions were performed by boiling 10 µl of COX-2 enzyme for 3 min to inactivate it. Then Mixing 160 µl reaction buffer, 10 µL Heme, and 10 µL inactivated enzyme for the background wells. Combined 160 µL reaction buffer, 10 µL Heme, and 10 µL active COX-2 enzyme for the initial activity wells. Added 10 µL of prepared extract/inhibitor (50 µg/ml) to the above mixture. For controls, added 10 µL of inhibitor vehicle instead for the inhibitor wells. All wells were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Added 10 µL arachidonic acid to each well to start the reaction, which was then mixed and incubate for 2 min at 37 °C. To stop the reaction 30 µL of stannous chloride solution was added to each well. Incubate for 15 min at 37 °C. The ELISA Procedure was then followed and after incubating the plate in the dark on an orbital shaker the absorbance was read at 412 nm. Statistical analysis

Experiments were carried out in triplicates with the results presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was done using Microsoft (2024) Copilot program. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the differences in means of the antioxidant activity, anti-neuraminidase activity and percentage inhibition of COX by the extracts, with the differences considered significant if the p ≤ 0.05. The quantitative data analysed was assigned groupings (A, B, C, D) using the K-means cluster analysis. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess correlation of extracts antioxidant activity with anti-neuraminidase activity and cox-2 inhibition.

Discussion

Phytochemical screening

The medicinal plants selected for investigation in the current study, namely A. afra, L. javanica, T. riparia and T. violacea, are amongst plants used traditionally for treatment of respiratory infections. However, their efficacy or potential efficacy to alleviate complications associated with respiratory viral infections is not adequately explored. Therefore, the current study was aimed at the evaluation of antiviral activity indicators possessed or exhibited by phytochemical compounds within the extracts from the four selected plant species, which contribute information to establish their potential efficacy against respiratory viral infections. The phytochemical screening of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts from the different plants’ parts (leaves, stem, roots) of the selected medicinal plants showed the presence of different classes of secondary metabolites that included coumarins, flavonoids, terpenoids, saponins, steroids, glycosides and phenolic compounds. The results also showed that the presence of the detected plant secondary metabolites/phytochemicals were recorded at different intensities amongst the different selected medicinal plants and, at times, among the different plants parts of the same plant species. For example, quinones were not detected in the leaf extracts of A. afra, whereas in was detected to be present in both the stem and roots extracts. It is not uncommon for different plant parts of the same plant species to show a certain level of disparity in the possessed secondary metabolites during phytochemical screening as it was also reported in several previous studies for different plants species than those selected for the current study [31–34]. It should also be factored in that the phytochemical compositions of extracts from different sources are depended on the properties of solvents used for extraction and the method or procedure employed for extraction [35]. Therefore, the reported phytochemicals detected in the plant materials from the samples of the selected medicinal plants is limited to those that are extractable through the used extraction solvents and the extraction method in the current study.

Antioxidant activity

Evaluation of free radical scavenging properties of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts of the different plants’ parts (leaves, stem, roots) showed exhibition of inhibitory activity against free radicals’ test reagents (DPPH, H2O2 and FRAP) in all plants. The results of the current study agree with the findings of previous studies that reported antioxidant properties in A. afra, L. javanica, T. riparia and T. violacea [36–39]. The results of the current study thus confirm the antioxidant properties of the selected medicinal plants reported in previous studies. Phytochemical screening of the extracts from the selected medicinal plants materials showed the presence of the different phytochemicals that included coumarins, flavonoids, terpenoids, saponins, steroids, glycosides and phenolic compounds. All these phytochemicals detected in presence have been reported to possess strong antioxidant properties [37, 40]. Therefore, the exhibited antioxidant properties of the different plant parts of the selected medicinal plants in the current study could be attributed to the phytochemicals that were detected to be in possession thereof.

Neuraminidase activity inhibition

Evaluation of anti-neuraminidase activity of the aqueous and ethanolic extracts showed possession of some certain levels of neuraminidase inhibition properties in all plants under investigation. All aqueous extracts showed higher inhibition of neuraminidase activity when compared to the ethanolic extracts. The results therefore suggest that the anti-neuraminidase activity exhibited by the extracts of the selected medicinal plants are contributed for mainly by the phytoconstituents of relative higher polarity. The results of the current study confirm the findings of a study done by Monto [41] on the anti-neuraminidase activity of A. afra. However, the anti-neuraminidase activity of the three other selected medicinal plants, namely, L. javanica, T. riparia and T. violacea is reported for the first time through this study. Possession or exhibition of anti-neuraminidase activity by medicinal plants is an indication of potential anti-viral properties and it is an attractive target for anti-influenza related drug design. Neuraminidase (NA) is found in all wild-type influenza viruses, and its inhibition halts viral propagation by interfering with effective shedding [42]. The observed anti-neuraminidase activity in this study could be attributed to the nature of phytochemicals possessed by the selected medicinal plants. For example, saponins were amongst the detected phytochemicals in the medicinal plants selected for the current study and compounds of saponin nature have been reported to possess antiviral activity [40]. The results of current thus places anti-neuraminidase activity as one possible antiviral mechanism of action exhibited by saponin compounds. Therefore, the findings indicate that the selected medicinal plants possess phytoconstituents with anti-neuraminidase activity, which demonstrates potential anti-viral activity through the inhibition of viral propagation against influenzae virus and other respiratory infectious microorganisms.

Cox-2 activity inhibition

Two plant parts extracts of each of the selected medicinal plants that showed higher anti-neuraminidase activity in the current study were evaluated for Cox-2 inhibition activity. The results showed possession of some Cox-2 inhibition activity by all the plant parts extracts evaluated, which was more similar in strength to the positive control, Indomethacin. Phytochemicals such as flavonoids, terpenoids, saponins and coumarins are well known for anti-inflammatory properties [43], as such their presence in the selected medicinal plants extracts that was revealed through phytochemical analysis suggest their contribution to the observed anti-inflammatory activity of the plant’s extracts observed in this study. The exhibition of Cox-2 inhibition activity by the extracts from various plant parts of the selected medicinal plants, namely A. afra and L. javanica, is reported in this study for the first time. Inhibition of the activity of Cox enzyme is one of the ways for demonstration of anti-inflammatory properties. Anti-inflammatory agents mainly inhibit the cyclooxygenase (Cox) enzyme responsible for synthesizing inflammatory mediators by converting arachidonic acid to prostaglandins. Prostaglandin G2 (PGG2) is converted into prostaglandin H2 (PGH2) together by the enzyme peroxidase, which creates long membrane channels [44, 45]. Due to the release of chemical mediators, membrane channel opening causes the release of arachidonic acid from the membrane and is then converted to prostaglandin. This also plays part in acute and chronic inflammation [46]. Therefore, the results suggest that the different plant parts of selected medicinal plants for the current study possess phytoconstituents with Cox-2 inhibition activity and thus anti-inflammation properties.

Evaluation of correlation relationship between pharmacological properties

A correlation relationship was tested between antioxidant activity versus anti-neuraminidase activity, antioxidant activity versus Cox-2 inhibition activity and between anti-neuraminidase versus Cox-2 inhibition activities using the Spearman’ correlation rank test. The Spearman’s correlation rank test demonstrated a negative correlation outcome between antioxidant activity and anti-neuraminidase activity for the selected medicinal plants, with ρ (rho) ≈ -0.45 for DPPH antioxidant assay activity; -0.50 for H2O2 assay activity and − 0.07 for FRAP assay activity. The outcome, therefore, suggest that plant extracts with strong antioxidant activity are likely to also have strong anti-neuraminidase activity, since as the EC50 antioxidant concentration gets lower, the higher the % neuraminidase inhibition. When spearman’s rank correlation was done between DPPH, H2O2 and FRAP reduction activities versus COX-2 inhibition, a positive correlation of ρ (rho) ≈ + 0.36 was found between DPPH and COX-2 inhibition activity. The outcome, therefore, suggest that plant extracts with strong antioxidant activity are likely to also have strong COX-2 inhibition. The outcome of the testing of the correlation relationship between anti-neuraminidase and COX-2 inhibition resulted in the correlation ρ (rho) ≈ − 0.10, which indicated a very weak to negative correlation between anti-neuraminidase and COX-2 inhibition or anti-inflammatory properties. It is important to note that correlation does not equate to causation. More tests would need to be done to account for causation. The correlation outcome between anti-neuraminidase activity versus Cox-2 inhibition activity could be arising from the distinction in the viral infection stage of the two invoked anti-viral mechanisms. For example, anti-neuraminidase action is invoked during the viral infection stage to thwart the propagation of the virus to mount effective infection. On the other hand, Cox-2 inhibition is linked to anti-inflammation activity following the post infection cytokine immune-response storm leading to high inflammation state [47]. The findings of the study, therefore, suggest that the phytoconstituents within the selected medicinal plants have inherent anti-viral properties that arise from two distinct mechanisms that occur at the different viral infection stages. The findings further suggest strong correlation between antioxidant activity and anti-neuraminidase activity inherent in the plants’ phytoconstituents. Antioxidant activity reverses oxidative stress and immune response cytokine storm in living systems and therefore alleviates post viral infection hyperinflammation. In contrast, anti-neuraminidase activity stops viral infection and propagation, which prevents the elevation of the immune cytokine storm and thus prevents hyperinflammation.

Ultimately, the Spearman rank correlation was determined between the TPC and the antioxidant, anti-neuraminidase and anti-COX activities. The resultant correlation between TPC and DPPH, H2O2 and FRAP inhibition EC50s dataset was generally weak with ρ ≈ − 0.05; − 0.12 and – 0.60, respectively. The Spearman rank correlation between TPC and anti-neuraminidase IC50s dataset was moderately weak (ρ ≈ − 0.67). Similarly, the Spearman rank correlation between TPC and % COX-2 inhibition dataset was also moderately negative (ρ ≈ − 0.52). the outcome, therefore, suggest that there is no correlation between the phenolic content and the antioxidant, anti-neuraminidase and anti-COX activities exhibited by the phytoconstituents of the different plant parts extracts of A. afra, T. riparia, L. javanica and T. violacea.

Conclusion

Phytochemical screening confirmed the presence of important phytochemical group compounds within the extracts from medicinal plants selected for the current study. The different plant parts of the various selected medicinal plants exhibited antioxidant activity, which is a validation of their health beneficial properties. The extracts showed exhibition of good anti-neuraminidase and Cox-2 inhibition activities, although no correlation between the two mechanisms was demonstrated. Therefore, the results of the current study suggest that the investigated medicinal plants, namely, A. afra, L. javanica, T. riparia and T. violacea, possess properties to mitigate against respiratory viral infections through the different mechanisms, including the viral propagation inhibition properties demonstrated by anti-neuraminidase activity and post-infection anti-inflammatory properties evidenced by Cox-2 inhibition activity. Higher anti-neuraminidase activity was seen with aqueous plants extracts, which suggest that the active components against neuraminidase activity could be of extremely high polar in nature. Compound-level identification, biosafety and direct antiviral testing such as cell-based assays are recommended in future studies to strengthen the current findings. In conclusion, the study has demonstrated that the phytoconstituents possessed by the selected medicinal plants have the potential to be developed into products for usage against respiratory viral infections.
