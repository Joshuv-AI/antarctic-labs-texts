DLK1 is a GATA1s-driven dependency and therapeutic target in Down syndrome–associated myeloid leukemia∗
Authors: Lonneke J Verboon, Sonali P Barwe, Meredith Tavenner, Joshua R Faust, Hasan Issa, José Gonçalves-Dias, Konstantin Schuschel, Raj Bhayadia, Patrick H van Berkel, Aimy Sebastian, Rhonda E Ries, Sophie Paczesny, Soheil Meshinchi, Johann Hitzler, Yana Pikman, E Anders Kolb, Dirk Heckl, Jan-Henning Klusmann, Anilkumar Gopalakrishnapillai

ABSTRACT
Key Points•DLK1 is a GATA1s-driven leukemia dependency in ML-DS, linking fetal hematopoietic programs to leukemic stemness and refractory disease.•Targeting of DLK1 with an antibody-drug conjugate eradicates leukemia cells and prolongs survival in ML-DS patient-derived xenograft models.

Introduction

Children with Down syndrome (DS) have a striking predisposition to myeloid malignancies, with an ∼500-fold increased risk compared to the general population.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Most cases arise in early childhood and are preceded by a preleukemic condition termed transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM), which is detected in up to 30% of neonates with DS.6 Although TAM often resolves spontaneously, in 20% to 30% of children, it progresses to myeloid leukemia of Down syndrome (ML-DS). Treatment of ML-DS with reduced-intensity chemotherapy has transformed outcomes, with current event-free survival rates approaching 90%.7, 8, 9 Nevertheless, DS patients particularly suffer from treatment-related toxicity and mortality. Moreover, 10% to 20% of patients present with refractory disease or relapse early after therapy, and their survival drops below 25%.10 Effective therapeutic options for this group are lacking, highlighting the urgent need to identify new vulnerabilities that can be exploited in a targeted manner.11

The biology of ML-DS is intimately linked to fetal hematopoiesis. Somatic mutations in GATA1, which generate the truncated transcription factor GATA1 short (GATA1s), arise exclusively in fetal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs).12 These mutations alter transcriptional output leading to TAM.13 A defining feature of this fetal origin is the persistence of developmental gene expression programs that are normally extinguished after birth. Mounting evidence suggests that these fetal-restricted programs are central to leukemogenesis and may also represent selective therapeutic entry points, as they are absent from postnatal hematopoiesis.14,15

One such program involves Delta-like noncanonical Notch ligand 1 (DLK1). DLK1 is a paternally imprinted16 transmembrane protein–containing epidermal growth factor-like repeats and is normally expressed at high levels in the fetal liver, kidney, and other developing tissues.17, 18, 19 In hematopoiesis, DLK1 marks immature HSPCs and contributes to the regulation of self-renewal and differentiation.20 Postnatally, DLK1 expression is strongly downregulated21 and is essentially absent from normal adult CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors.22 Its reexpression has been documented in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), acute myeloid leukemia (AML),22,23 and several solid tumors,24 where it is associated with stemness, impaired differentiation, and inferior prognosis.25,26 The restricted developmental expression pattern, together with negligible levels in normal adult hematopoiesis, makes DLK1 a highly attractive candidate for immunotherapeutic intervention. Preclinical strategies for DLK1 targeting have demonstrated antitumor activity, including antibody-drug conjugates (ADC),27,28 chimeric antigen receptor T cells in hepatocellular carcinoma models,29 and radioimmunotherapy with 125I-labeled anti-DLK1 antibodies in lung cancer.30

Mechanistically, DLK1 acts as a noncanonical Notch ligand, capable of either suppressing or activating Notch receptor signaling in a context-dependent manner.25,31 It can also signal independently through intracellular cleavage product that influences transcriptional programs.32 In MDS, elevated DLK1 expression has been linked to increased clonogenic growth and impaired differentiation of CD34+ progenitors, but its role and regulation in AML and ML-DS have remained largely undefined.22,33

In the present study, we identify DLK1 as a direct transcriptional target of GATA1s in ML-DS. Chromatin profiling revealed GATA1s occupancy at a distal enhancer within the DLK1-DIO3 locus, linking GATA1s mutations (a necessary initiating lesion in ML-DS) to aberrant DLK1 upregulation. This establishes a mechanistic connection between fetal transcriptional programs and leukemic dependency. Building on this, we demonstrate that DLK1 can be therapeutically targeted using an ADC (DLK1-ADC), which selectively eradicates DLK1-expressing cells and prolongs survival in refractory ML-DS xenograft models. These findings position DLK1 as a leukemia-specific vulnerability and provide preclinical proof-of-concept for DLK1-directed therapy in ML-DS and other leukemias with fetal-like expression programs.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293T and CMK (ACC 392) cells were obtained from DSMZ (DSMZ - German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH) and cultured as described previously.14,34 For culturing patient-derived xenograft cells, StemSpan SFEM media (StemCell Technologies, Ontario, Canada) supplemented with TPO 25 ng/mL, IL3 10 ng/mL, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor 10 ng/mL (from R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) was used. Human CD34+ HSPCs were isolated from mobilized peripheral blood from anonymous healthy donors and enriched using CD34+ immunomagnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). HSPC expansion and cytokine-driven differentiation conditions are provided in the supplemental Methods.

RNA-seq

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on ML-DS PDX samples, CMK cells, DLK1 knockout CMK cells and human bone marrow–derived CD34+ cells. Sequencing and differential expression analysis were performed using established pipelines as described previously.35 External RNA-seq data sets from bone marrow specimens of normal individuals, and patients with TAM or ML-DS on Children’s Oncology Group trial AAML1531 and AAML08B1 were obtained from the TARGET AML database (dbGaP study ID phs0002187.v2.p1; described in Figure 1A). UniProt list of membrane-associated proteins based on subcellular locations was used for filtering cell surface proteins. Detailed procedures and processing steps are described in the supplemental Methods.

Genetic perturbation of DLK1

DLK1 was genetically perturbed in CMK cells using lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 single guide (sgRNA) constructs, RNP-based genome editing and short hairpin (shRNA)-mediated knockdown approaches. Lentiviral sgRNAs targeting DLK1 were generated as described previously.36 Detailed procedures for lentiviral production, RNP-based editing, and shRNA knockdown are provided in the supplemental Methods. sgRNA and shRNA sequences are listed in supplemental Table 3.0)

CRISPR editing efficiency (TIDE)

Genomic DNA was isolated with using a MicroDNA kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) or a Quick-DNA miniprep kit (Zymo Research Europe, Freiburg, Germany). The target locus was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–amplified with Q5 High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, Frankfurt am Main, Germany) using primers flanking the expected Cas9 cut site region surrounding the CRISPR cut site in the genomic DNA. PCR products were Sanger sequenced and analyzed using a web-based tool https://ice.synthego.com/ (https://ice.synthego.com or https://apps.datacurators.nl/tide/). PCR primers used for TIDE/ICE genotyping are listed in supplemental Table 4.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing was performed as previously described with minor modifications.37 Human HSPCs, as well as in vitro differentiated megakaryocytes and monocytes were used. Detailed processing steps are described in the supplemental Methods.

CUT&RUN/CUT&Tag

Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) and Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) was performed on sorted patient leukemic blasts and CMK cells expressing doxycycline-inducible HA-GATA1s as previously described.34,38,39 Histone modifications (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, and H3K27me3) and HA-GATA1s occupancy were profiled. Detailed procedures and processing steps are described in the supplemental Methods.

Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequence

For each library, 50 000 leukemic blasts were fluorescence-activated cell–sorted as described earlier. Nuclei were prepared and tagmented according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Tagment DNA enzyme and buffer kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Transposed DNA was purified with MinElute PCR purification kit (Qiagen). Libraries were generated using NEBNext High-Fidelity 2× PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) with barcoded i5/i7 primers (Integrated DNA Technologies). Detailed processing steps are described in the supplemental Methods.

In vivo studies

NSG-SGM3 mice (Stock no. 013062) purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME) were transplanted with leukemic cells (1.0 × 106 to 2.5 × 106) by tail vein injections for efficacy studies. For competitive homing assay, Violet proliferation dye–stained CMK cells were coinjected with CFSE stained DLK1 knockout (KO) cells (106 each) via the tail vein and mice were euthanized 72 hours after. The percentage of CMK and DLK1 KO cells in the bone marrow was determined after performing euthanasia, by flow cytometry as described previously.40 Mice were maintained in the Nemours Life Science Center following the guidelines established by the Nemours Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Following engraftment confirmation, mice were either left untreated or treated with indicated doses of DLK1-ADC or isotype ADC via the tail vein. The leukemic burden was measured by flow cytometry of mouse peripheral blood drawn by submandibular bleeds as described previously.41,42 Mice were monitored daily and once mice reached experimental end points they were euthanized. Immunohistochemistry was performed as described previously.40

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations of experimental data were carried out using 2-way analysis of variance or unpaired t test was used for comparisons between the 2 groups. To calculate the survival of mice the Kaplan-Meier survival estimate was used. A value of P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as standard error of the mean. Calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (STATCON). Statistical analyses of gene expression data (RNA-seq) were carried out in R using edgeR or via msigdbr.

Ethics statement

All PDX samples, patient samples, and primary human CD34+ cells were obtained with informed consent under protocols approved by the institutional review boards of the contributing institutions (Nemours Children’s Health, Goethe University Frankfurt and collaborating centers).

Results

Aberrant DLK1 expression characterizes ML-DS

We first assessed DLK1 expression in ML-DS by comparing transcriptomes of ML-DS cell/PDX lines (n = 5) with normal bone marrow-derived CD34+ cells (n = 4). DLK1 emerged as one of the top upregulated cell surface proteins (supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Table 1). Analysis of pediatric AML data sets (from patients enrolled on Children’s Oncology Group trials AAML1531 and AAML08B1) confirmed significantly higher DLK1 expression in ML-DS bone marrow samples (257.2 ± 73.8 transcripts per million [TPM], (n = 77) compared with normal controls (2.2 ± 0.2 TPM, n = 68) (Figure 1A). In an independent cohort, DLK1 expression was also enriched in ML-DS compared with normal adult peripheral blood stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) and other cytogenetic subtypes of pediatric AML, including non-DS acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL; Figure 1B).43 Fetal-liver CD34+ HSPCs showed high DLK1 levels comparable to, and in some samples exceeding, those in TAM and ML-DS.43 No significant difference was observed between TAM and ML-DS samples in both data sets. DLK1 expression greater than normal samples (log10TPM > 1) was seen in 68 of 101 (66.6%) of ML-DS patient samples when the 2 cohorts were combined.

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR confirmed higher DLK1 transcript levels in PDX lines derived from refractory ML-DS compared with those from newly diagnosed patients (Figure 1C). Flow cytometry and immunoblotting corroborated surface and protein expression in refractory PDXs, whereas DLK1 was undetectable in bone marrow from an ML-DS patient who went on to achieve remission (remission bone marrow, Figure 1D-E; supplemental Figure 2). Importantly, unlike CMK cells, DLK1 was absent on CD34+ progenitors from individuals with or without DS (Figure 1F; supplemental Figure 2B), underscoring its disease-specific expression. Normal-tissue profiles from the Human Protein Atlas44 show medium-high DLK1 protein in selected tissues (eg, adrenal, bronchus, placenta, ovary, and testis), with little or no detection in most other tissues including bone marrow (supplemental Figure 4A), consistent with selective expression.

Together, these data establish DLK1 as a robustly expressed and selective marker of ML-DS.

GATA1s drives aberrant DLK1 expression through distal enhancer activation

To investigate why DLK1 is aberrantly expressed in ML-DS, we characterized the epigenetic landscape of the imprinted DLK1-DIO3 locus, which also harbors the noncoding RNA MEG3. Chromatin profiling demonstrated that the DLK1 promoter is predominantly marked by the repressive histone modification H3K27me3, with only low-level H3K4me3, consistent with a poised or permissive state in CD34+ cells and megakaryocytes. In contrast, the immediately upstream regulatory region displayed clear enrichment for active chromatin marks, including H3K4me3, most prominently in megakaryocytes, indicating the presence of an active regulatory element (Figure 2A). These epigenetic features are consistent with active DLK1 transcription (supplemental Figure 4B) and the previously described activity of the DLK1-DIO3 locus with higher DLK1 expression in the megakaryocytic lineage.22,45 In monocytes, the DLK1 promoter is dominated by repressive H3K27me3, concordant with low DLK1 expression (supplemental Figure 4B). Notably, in the ML-DS cell line CMK, the DLK1 promoter is marked by strong H3K4me3 enrichment, indicative of active transcription and consistent with the high DLK1 expression.

CUT&RUN with exogenous HA-tagged GATA1s identified GATA1s occupancy at a distal enhancer upstream of DLK1, coinciding with H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, marks of active transcriptional programs. To confirm that GATA1s also binds to this enhancer in vivo, we performed CUT&TAG for H3K27ac, CUT&RUN for GATA1s, and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequence on 1 TAM and 1 sample from a patient with ML-DS, which showed a similar GATA1s occupancy pattern with active transcription mark and open chromatin (Figure 2A). Targeted CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis of 2 enhancer peaks (A and B) resulting in the deletion of GATA consensus motifs (GATAA) (supplemental Figure 4A) significantly reduced expression of both DLK1 and MEG3 in CMK cells (Figure 2B), and reduced DLK1 surface expression (Figure 2C, left panel) establishing GATA motif dependence and enhancer–promoter cooperation within this domain. Additionally, chromatin conformation analysis using 4C-seq (Circular Chromosome Conformation Capture sequence) in CMK cells demonstrated topological contact between the DLK1 promoter and the GATA1s-bound distal enhancer (Figure 2A).

CMK cells exclusively express the truncated GATA1s isoform and lack full-length GATA1 protein (supplemental Figure 4B). To directly assess the functional contribution of GATA1s to DLK1 regulation, we performed CRISPR/Cas9–mediated knockout of GATA1s using 2 independent sgRNAs. One sgRNA targeting GATA1s (G1s#1), achieving 32.8% editing efficiency (supplemental Figure 5) and ∼50% reduction in GATA1s protein levels (supplemental Figure 4C), resulted in a significant decrease in DLK1 surface expression as measured by flow cytometry (Figure 2C, right panel). A second sgRNA (G1s#2), despite lower editing efficiency, yielded a consistent reduction in DLK1 expression, supporting the robustness of this effect. To further interrogate isoform-specific regulation, we ectopically expressed HA-tagged full-length GATA1 or GATA1s in CMK cells, which display high baseline DLK1 expression (supplemental Figure 4D). RNA-seq analysis revealed only modest changes in DLK1 transcript levels following ectopic expression of either GATA1 isoform (supplemental Figure 4E). Notably, ectopic expression of full-length GATA1 in CMK cells did not repress DLK1, arguing against a dominant repressive role of full-length GATA1 or a strictly activating role of GATA1s at this locus. Consistent with this, flow cytometric analysis detected a subtle but reproducible increase in DLK1 surface expression upon expression of either GATA1 isoform (supplemental Figure 4F).

These observations extend our previous finding that both GATA1 and GATA1s occupy regulatory elements upstream of the DLK1-DIO3 locus46 and suggest that GATA1 activity is permissive, rather than repressive, for DLK1 expression in this cellular context. Together with the enhancer perturbation and chromatin interaction data, these results support a model in which GATA1s contributes to aberrant DLK1 expression through activation of a distal regulatory element within the DLK1-DIO3 locus.

DLK1 maintains leukemic proliferation and engraftment

To assess whether DLK1 contributes functionally to leukemic growth, we performed genetic perturbation in the ML-DS cell line CMK. Perturbing DLK1 and MEG3 expression using CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis of GATA1 consensus motifs in the 2 enhancer peaks led to a significant growth disadvantage (Figure 2D) and induction of apoptosis (Figure 2E). Similarly, CRISPR/Cas9–mediated KO of DLK1 using 2 independent sgRNAs efficiently reduced DLK1 surface expression (Figure 3A) and resulted in progressive loss of DLK1-deficient cells in competitive proliferation assays (Figure 3B). This depletion was accompanied by increased apoptosis (Figure 3C). To dissect the contribution of noncoding RNA MEG3 within the DLK1-DIO3 locus, we next perturbed its expression alone or in combination with DLK1. Notably, loss of MEG3 alone resulted in a proliferative defect comparable to that observed upon combined DLK1/MEG3 perturbation (supplemental Figure 6), indicating that the growth disadvantage induced by enhancer disruption cannot be attributed solely to loss of DLK1. Importantly, independent single-cell DLK1 KO clones (supplemental Figure 7A) further confirmed a strong proliferative defect, with a >60% reduction in EdU incorporation compared to parental CMK cells (Figure 3D; supplemental Figure 7B). Partial suppression by shRNA knockdown also impaired cell growth, demonstrating a graded dependency on DLK1 expression.

To explore the underlying mechanism, we performed bulk RNA-seq of sgRNA-transduced CMK cells. Loss of DLK1 resulted in broad transcriptional changes, with 424 genes differentially expressed (supplemental Table 6). Gene set enrichment analysis highlighted significant activation of the Notch pathway (Figure 3E-F) with a more comprehensive analysis provided in supplemental Figure 8. Specifically, in single-cell DLK1 KO clones NOTCH1 and its downstream targets HES1 and HES6 were upregulated, whereas canonical Notch ligand JAG1, as well as NOTCH2 and NOTCH3, were downregulated (Figure 3G). Consistent with these findings, flow cytometry confirmed increased NOTCH1 protein expression (Figure 3H; supplemental Figure 9A; P < .0001) and reduced levels of active β-catenin in DLK1 KO (Figure 3I; supplemental Figure 9B; P < .05), suggesting that DLK1 sustains leukemic proliferation by suppressing NOTCH1 activity and maintaining β-catenin signaling.

We next evaluated the role of DLK1 in leukemic propagation in vivo. Immunodeficient recipient mice transplanted with single-cell-derived DLK1 KO CMK cells displayed dramatically reduced engraftment: human blasts were virtually absent in bone marrow and spleen compared to mice receiving wild-type CMK cells, which developed extensive infiltration and tissue destruction (Figure 4A-B; supplemental Figure 10A). Median survival was significantly prolonged in recipients of both sexes, with DLK1 KO cohorts living >60 days longer than controls (Figure 4C). Competitive homing assays further revealed impaired early localization of DLK1-deficient cells to bone marrow niches (Figure 4D; supplemental Figure 10B).

Together, these results demonstrate that DLK1 is not merely a marker but a functional dependency of ML-DS, required for proliferation, survival, homing, and long-term engraftment. By sustaining leukemic stemness programs through modulation of Notch and β-catenin pathways, DLK1 supports disease maintenance in vivo.

Therapeutic targeting of DLK1-ADC

Given the essential role of DLK1 in sustaining leukemic growth, we next evaluated its therapeutic potential using a DLK1-directed ADC (DLK1-ADC; ADCT-701), consisting of an anti-DLK1 antibody conjugated to the pyrrolobenzodiazepine dimer payload PL1601.28,47,48 In vitro, DLK1-ADC–induced potent and selective cytotoxicity in CMK cells, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.027nM, compared to 0.172nM in DLK1 knockdown cells and complete resistance in DLK1 KO cells (Figure 5A). These results demonstrate strict dependence of ADC activity on DLK1 surface expression. Isotype control ADCs had no effect, confirming target specificity.

We extended these findings to primary ML-DS PDX samples. In refractory PDX7, which expressed high levels of DLK1, DLK1-ADC showed nanomolar potency (IC50 0.234nM) and completely abrogated colony formation in methylcellulose assays (Figure 5A-B).

In vivo, DLK1-ADC significantly reduced leukemia burden and prolonged survival in PDX-engrafted recipient mice. A single dose of 0.25 to 1 mg/kg led to dose-dependent improvements in median survival, extending life expectancy by up to 24 days compared to isotype controls (Figure 5C-D). Across 3 independent refractory ML-DS PDX models, repeated dosing at 1 mg/kg consistently prolonged survival, with the strongest effect observed in high-DLK1 expressers (Figure 6A-B). Remarkably, a single 3 mg/kg dose in PDX4-induced durable remissions in 2 of 3 mice, with no evidence of disease recurrence (Figure 6C-D). Notably, administration of the same single 3 mg/kg dose also achieved remission in 3 of 4 mice when treatment was initiated at an advanced stage of disease, with leukemic burden exceeding 25% in peripheral blood (Figure 6C-D).

These data demonstrate that DLK1-ADC selectively eradicates DLK1-positive leukemic cells and achieves meaningful survival benefit in vivo. The correlation between surface expression and therapeutic efficacy further highlights DLK1 as a tractable immunotherapeutic target in ML-DS.

Discussion

Despite excellent survival for most children with ML-DS treated with reduced-intensity chemotherapy, relapsed or refractory disease remains a major challenge, with survival rates below 25%.10 Our study identifies DLK1 as a tractable vulnerability in ML-DS. By tying aberrant DLK1 expression to GATA1s-driven enhancer activation within the DLK1-DIO3 locus, our study provides a rationale and preclinical proof-of-concept for therapeutically targeting this fetal stemness regulator that is normally silenced in adult hematopoiesis.

Expression analyses revealed that DLK1 is strongly upregulated in ML-DS compared to normal hematopoietic progenitors, whereas it is absent from CD34+ progenitors of healthy individuals. This finding is consistent with earlier observations that DLK1 expression is largely restricted to fetal hematopoiesis and development, but reemerges in a range of malignancies including MDS, AML,22,23,47 neuroblastoma,27 and hepatocellular carcinoma.25,48 In mouse models, DLK1 is expressed in the aorta-gonad-mesonephros region where it regulates emerging HSPCs by restricting their expansion through membrane-bound DLK1 rather than soluble isoforms.49 Moreover, in mice the Dlk1-Gtl2 imprinted locus, which also harbors Meg3, is required for long-term hematopoietic stem cell maintenance by modulating mitochondrial metabolism and limiting oxidative stress.50 These developmental roles illustrate how DLK1 sustains stemness in fetal contexts, explaining why its dysregulation in ML-DS enforces a fetal-like leukemic state and is directly coupled to disease pathogenesis.

Mechanistically, we show that DLK1 is a transcriptional target of GATA1s, product of the mandatory initiating mutation in ML-DS. Chromatin profiling revealed GATA1s occupancy at a distal enhancer within the DLK1-DIO3 locus. We note that this region also harbors annotated noncoding transcripts and that the H3K4me3 signal may therefore reflect promoter activity in addition to enhancer-like features. CRISPR/Cas9–mediated deletion of GATA consensus motifs within the enhancer as well as perturbation of GATA1s expression reduced DLK1 surface expression, establishing a direct connection between GATA1s-driven transcriptional control and aberrant DLK1 upregulation. Notably, full-length GATA1 activity is permissive, rather than repressive, for DLK1 expression in this cellular context. We acknowledge that although GATA1s is the major contributor in regulating DLK1, additional contributions from other GATA family members cannot be excluded. This enhancer-promoter cooperation also affected expression of MEG3, a noncoding RNA implicated in hematopoietic regulation,51 consistent with reports that the DLK1-DIO3 imprinted domain acts as a hub of developmental gene regulation and stem cell maintenance.50 In ML-DS, GATA1s mutations hijack this fetal circuitry to enforce DLK1 expression and leukemic dependency. These findings underscore how developmental programs normally extinguished after birth are exploited in ML-DS to sustain leukemic growth.

Functional studies confirmed that DLK1 is not merely a marker but a functional dependency. Genetic ablation reduced proliferation, induced apoptosis, and profoundly impaired in vivo engraftment. Transcriptomic profiling of DLK1-deficient cells revealed activation of NOTCH1 and suppression of β-catenin signaling, indicating that DLK1 sustains leukemic stemness by balancing developmental pathways. DLK1’s dual role as a noncanonical Notch ligand31,52, 53, 54, 55 and regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling has been described in other contexts, including hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma, where it supports tumor initiation and progression.56 In normal hematopoiesis, DLK1 expression in fetal liver HSPCs has also been linked to enhanced self-renewal capacity. Together, these data highlight DLK1 as a nexus of fetal transcriptional programs and leukemic stemness.

DLK1 thus represents a leukemia-specific dependency directly linked to the ontogeny of ML-DS and to the obligate GATA1s mutation. The therapeutic implications of these findings are considerable. Unlike broadly expressed AML antigens such as CD123, CD33, or CLL-1, DLK1 is absent from adult hematopoiesis, making it a promising target with reduced risk of myelosuppression. Its surface localization and higher expression in 66.6% of ML-DS patient samples further enhance its suitability for antibody-based strategies in DLK1 positive ML-DS patients by exploiting DLK1 as a surface-accessible entry point, rather than relying on DLK1 signaling function. Our preclinical testing of a DLK1-directed ADC (ADCT-701) demonstrated potent, expression-dependent activity: nanomolar cytotoxicity in cell lines and PDXs, elimination of colony-forming capacity, and significant survival benefits in vivo. Notably, durable remissions were achieved at higher doses in a refractory PDX model, even when dosed at leukemia cell counts >25% in blood, providing evidence that DLK1 can be therapeutically exploited. These data align with growing evidence that DLK1 is a tractable immunotherapeutic target across cancers with activated or reactivated oncofetal expression programs. In hepatocellular carcinoma, DLK1-directed chimeric antigen receptor T cells achieved strong antitumor activity in preclinical models.29 In lung cancer, 125I-labeled anti-DLK1 antibodies demonstrated efficacy in radioimmunotherapy studies.30 A DLK1-ADC has also been tested in neuroblastoma and adrenocortical carcinoma, showing selective killing of DLK1-expressing tumors driven by antigen-dependent internalization and intracellular delivery of the pyrrolobenzodiazepine payload resulting in potent cytotoxicity.57,58 Importantly, treatment with the corresponding naked humanized anti-DLK1 antibody (HuBA-1-3D), lacking the cytotoxic payload, was ineffective in neuroblastoma xenograft models, indicating that ADCT-701 does not act through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.57 Consistent with these findings, our in vivo efficacy studies were performed in ML-DS PDX models engrafted in NSG-SGM3 mice, which exhibit profound immunodeficiency including absence of functional natural killer cells, arguing against a major contribution of natural killer cell–mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity to the observed antileukemic activity. Moreover, antigen downregulation is unlikely to represent a mechanism of resistance, as DLK1 surface expression remained unchanged in ML-DS cells recovered from mice treated with DLK1-ADC compared with isotype control (supplemental Figure 10C). This observation mirrors prior reports in neuroblastoma and adrenocortical carcinoma PDX models, where DLK1 expression was retained following ADCT-701 treatment and retreatment led to renewed tumor control.58 Together, these studies highlight the translational feasibility of DLK1-directed therapies.

Our findings further highlight the broader concept that developmental dependencies can be leveraged as therapeutic vulnerabilities in pediatric leukemia. In recent years, fetal-restricted programs have been implicated in infant AML subtypes such as NUP98- or KMT2A-rearranged leukemia, where persistence of embryonic transcriptional networks enforces stemness and therapy resistance.59,60 Targeting such fetal programs, whether through epigenetic modulators or surface antigens like DLK1, offers a rational strategy to improve outcomes in diseases where conventional cytotoxic intensification is not feasible or inefficient. The demonstration that GATA1s, the founding lesion of ML-DS, directly drives DLK1 expression places DLK1 within this conceptual framework and strengthens its appeal as a rational therapeutic target.

Some limitations must be considered. Our PDX analyses, although robust, were limited in number; larger cohorts will be required to confirm its clinical utility. Because the ML-DS PDXs do not reliably engraft in mouse models with preserved innate immunity (such as NOD-SCID), we were unable to perform a matched NOD-SCID vs NSG-SGM3 comparison to dissect the contribution of immune cells in mediating DLK1-ADC efficacy. Potential toxicities remain to be evaluated, especially in infants or regenerative contexts such as adrenal gland where residual DLK1 expression might persist, thereby necessitating careful toxicity monitoring in future clinical translation. DLK1-targeted therapies have thus far shown a favorable safety profile and have been well tolerated in patients,61 with other phase 1 trials of DLK1-ADCs underway: NCT0600574062 and NCT06041516.63 Mechanistically, the relative contributions of Notch-dependent vs Notch-independent pathways downstream of DLK1, as well as potential functions of soluble DLK1 isoforms, require further investigation.

Nevertheless, our results indicate that DLK1 as a leukemia-specific dependency in ML-DS, directly tied to its fetal origin and GATA1s-driven enhancer activation. By integrating developmental biology with therapeutic targeting, we provide both mechanistic insight and translational promise. DLK1’s restricted expression pattern, strong disease association, and amenability to multiple therapeutic modalities underscore its appeal as a target. These findings support clinical development of DLK1-directed therapies to improve outcomes for children with refractory ML-DS and potentially for other pediatric leukemias with fetal-like expression programs.
