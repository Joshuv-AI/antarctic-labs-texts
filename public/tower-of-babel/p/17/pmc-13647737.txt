Social determinants of early childhood development in The Gambia: a multi-level analysis of household, maternal, and healthcare factors
Authors: Ousman Bajinka, Amadou Barrow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly childhood development is a critical determinant of lifelong health and well-being, yet evidence on its social and maternal determinants from West Africa remains limited. We examined the independent contributions of household, maternal, and healthcare factors to maternally reported suspected developmental concerns in children under two years in The Gambia.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 504 mother-child dyads recruited through multi-stage probability sampling from urban and rural settlements in The Gambia (August–October 2021). The primary outcome was maternally reported suspected developmental concerns, assessed by a single binary question combining developmental and growth-related concerns. Cumulative maternal stress was measured using a 32-item binary checklist adapted from the Revised Prenatal Distress Questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.79). Hierarchical multivariable logistic regression examined factors at household (settlement type, education, income), maternal (age, stress, polygamy, intimate partner violence, anemia, hypertension), and healthcare (adequate antenatal care, ≥ 8 visits) levels with child age and sex retained as covariates. We formally tested for effect modification by settlement type using a product term.ResultsSuspected developmental concerns were reported for 69 children (13.7%). High cumulative maternal stress (≥ 6 stressors) was independently associated with the outcome (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.80, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.38–5.70, p = 0.004). Intimate partner violence during pregnancy showed a positive association of borderline significance (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 0.99–4.28, p = 0.054). Adequate antenatal care was not associated with the outcome (aOR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.46–1.41, p = 0.452). The interaction between high stress and settlement type was not statistically significant (interaction p = 0.59); stratified estimates were of similar magnitude in urban (OR = 3.48) and rural (OR = 4.62) settings. Maternal-level factors substantially improved model fit (Δ pseudo-R² = 0.060) compared with household (R² = 0.027) or healthcare factors (ΔR² = 0.002).ConclusionCumulative maternal psychosocial stress was independently associated with maternally reported suspected developmental concerns in young Gambian children, with a similar association observed in urban and rural settings. Intimate partner violence during pregnancy showed a positive association of borderline statistical significance. The number of antenatal care visits alone was not associated with the outcome, suggesting that visit quantity may be insufficient without quality improvements that address maternal mental health. These findings, while cross-sectional and based on a single-item maternal-report outcome, support further investment in integrated maternal mental health services within antenatal care. Longitudinal studies using validated developmental assessments are needed to establish temporal relationships and causality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28373-2.

Introduction

Early childhood development; encompassing physical growth, cognitive advancement, and socio-emotional maturation, forms the foundation for lifelong health, educational achievement, and economic productivity [1]. In sub-Saharan Africa, an estimated 43% of children under five years are at risk of not reaching their developmental potential, representing approximately 95 million children [2, 3]. The consequences extend beyond childhood: early developmental difficulties are associated with lower educational attainment, reduced adult earnings, and the perpetuation of intergenerational poverty [4, 5].

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework posits that environmental exposures during critical periods of early development; including the prenatal period, can have lasting effects on health trajectories across the life course [6, 7]. Maternal psychosocial stress during pregnancy represents a key exposure within this framework, with biological plausibility supported by evidence of stress-induced alterations in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis functioning, inflammatory processes, and epigenetic modifications that can affect fetal neurodevelopment [8, 9]. Prospective studies in high-income countries have documented associations between prenatal maternal stress and child cognitive, behavioural, and emotional outcomes [10, 11]. Evidence from sub-Saharan African contexts remains limited, despite the region bearing a high burden of maternal stress related to poverty, food insecurity, conflict, and limited healthcare access [12, 13].

Child development is influenced by multiple, interacting determinants operating at different ecological levels, from distal socioeconomic and environmental factors to proximal caregiver–child interactions [14, 15]. Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model provides a theoretical framework for understanding how household characteristics (e.g., income, parental education, settlement type), maternal individual factors (e.g., age, health status, psychosocial well-being), and healthcare system factors (e.g., antenatal care quality and utilization) collectively shape developmental trajectories [16]. In West African contexts, additional structural factors including polygamous marriage systems and intimate partner violence, may further influence maternal stress and child development, yet these relationships remain inadequately characterised [17, 18].

The Gambia, a small West African nation with a population of 2.5 million, faces substantial maternal and child health challenges. Under-five mortality stands at 56 per 1,000 live births, and only 46% of pregnant women achieve the World Health Organization’s recommended eight or more antenatal care contacts [19, 20]. Rapid urbanisation is transforming the country’s demographic landscape: 44% of the population now resides in urban areas, potentially exposing women to new stressors while disrupting traditional social support systems [21]. Despite these challenges, comprehensive data on early childhood developmental outcomes and their determinants in The Gambia remain scarce, limiting evidence-based policy development.

Previous studies on early childhood development in sub-Saharan Africa have predominantly focused on nutritional determinants or examined single-level risk factors in isolation [22, 23]. Few have employed hierarchical analytical approaches to quantify the relative contributions of factors at different ecological levels, and even fewer have examined maternal psychosocial stress comprehensively. The majority of existing research has been conducted in Eastern or Southern African contexts, with West Africa underrepresented despite its distinct sociocultural and healthcare landscapes [24, 25].

This study addresses these gaps by examining social determinants of suspected developmental concerns in young children in The Gambia using a multi-level analytical framework. We hypothesised that (i) maternal psychosocial factors, particularly cumulative stress burden, would be independently associated with maternally reported suspected developmental concerns after controlling for household and healthcare factors; (ii) factors at different ecological levels would contribute differentially to the outcome, with maternal-level factors showing the strongest associations; and (iii) associations of key exposures might vary by settlement type (urban vs. rural), which we examined formally as effect modification.

Throughout this paper, the outcome is defined as maternally reported suspected developmental concerns, acknowledging the distinction between developmental delay (motor, language, cognitive, or socio-emotional milestones) and growth retardation (physical growth). Because routine developmental screening is not available in most Gambian health facilities, we used a combined maternal-report measure. We interpret findings with appropriate caution, using associative language throughout and emphasising the cross-sectional, exploratory nature of the population attributable fraction analyses (presented in the supplementary materials).

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in The Gambia, West Africa, between August and October 2021. The Gambia has a population of approximately 2.5 million, with high maternal and child health burdens and limited healthcare infrastructure. The study employed a socioecological framework [16] to examine factors at three hierarchical levels: distal (household/socioeconomic), intermediate (maternal/individual), and proximal (healthcare).

Study population and sampling

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were mothers who: (1) had been resident in The Gambia for at least one year prior to enrolment, (2) had given birth within the previous 24 months, (3) were able to provide informed consent, and (4) were willing to participate. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe mental illness preventing informed consent, (2) inability to communicate in any of the study languages (English, Mandinka, Wolof, or Fula), and (3) multiple births from the index pregnancy.

Sampling procedure

A multi-stage probability sampling approach was used: Stage 1 (settlement selection): Eight settlements were randomly selected from The Gambia’s 42 districts, stratified by urban (Municipal Regulatory Areas; n = 3) and rural (Non-municipal Regulatory Areas; n = 5) status. Stage 2 (enumeration area selection): Within each settlement, enumeration areas were randomly selected proportional to population size. Stage 3 (household selection): Households were randomly selected using a random-walk procedure with a random starting point. Within each selected household, all eligible mother–child dyads were invited to participate.

Response rate

Of 587 eligible mother–child dyads approached, 504 enrolled (response rate = 85.9%). Non-participants did not differ significantly from participants by maternal age (p = 0.32) or settlement type (p = 0.45).

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated to detect an odds ratio of 2.0 for the association between high maternal stress and the primary outcome, assuming a 15% prevalence of the outcome in the unexposed group, 80% power, two-sided α = 0.05, and accounting for 10% non-response. The target sample size was 500 mother–child dyads.

Data collection

Trained research assistants fluent in local languages conducted face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires. Interviews were performed in participants’ homes or at health facilities based on participant preference and lasted approximately 45–60 min. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The study protocol was approved by the the Research and Publication Committee (RePubliC), School of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences, University of The Gambia (approval #: R021 008v2).

Outcome measure

Primary outcome: maternally reported suspected developmental concerns

The primary outcome was defined as maternally reported suspected developmental concerns in the index child. We acknowledge that developmental delay (referring to motor, language, cognitive, or socio-emotional milestones) and growth retardation (referring to physical growth) are distinct constructs. In this resource-limited setting where routine developmental screening is unavailable, we assessed a combined indicator using the single question: “Has your child been diagnosed with or shown signs of developmental delay or growth retardation?” Responses were dichotomised as Yes/No.

While this combined measure has limitations, maternal report has shown acceptable agreement with clinical developmental assessment in similar sub-Saharan African settings [24, 25]. Throughout the manuscript, we use the term “suspected developmental concerns” to reflect this measurement approach and avoid over-interpretation.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary child health outcomes included: (1) malnutrition (maternal report of clinician diagnosis or visible signs), (2) low birth weight (< 2500 g, from health records or maternal report), (3) preterm birth (< 37 weeks gestation), and (4) child anaemia (clinician-diagnosed or symptomatic). These were assessed by maternal report with reference to available health records when possible.

Exposure variables

Maternal stress assessment

Maternal stress was measured using a 32-item binary checklist covering stressors experienced before pregnancy (16 items) and during pregnancy (16 items). The checklist was adapted from the Revised Prenatal Distress Questionnaire (NuPDQ) and culturally adapted through: (1) translation into local languages (Mandinka, Wolof, Fula) with back-translation; (2) cognitive interviewing with 10 Gambian mothers; and (3) pilot testing with 30 mothers (Cronbach’s α = 0.84, indicating acceptable internal consistency). Domains included: Economic stressors (e.g., financial worries, unemployment), Social stressors (e.g., family conflicts, lack of support), Reproductive stressors (e.g., pregnancy complications, previous miscarriages), Psychosomatic symptoms (e.g., sleep difficulties, headaches), and Work-related stressors (e.g., heavy workloads, domestic responsibilities). Each item was coded as present (1) or absent (0). A total stress score was calculated by summing all 32 items (range: 0–32; sample mean = 7.7, SD = 4.3, median = 7). For primary analyses, stress was categorised as Low (0–2 stressors), Moderate (3–5 stressors), or High (≥ 6 stressors). The threshold of ≥ 6 stressors was selected based on visual inspection of the stress–outcome relationship, which showed an inflection point between five and six stressors. For multivariable analyses, stress was dichotomised as High (≥ 6) versus Low/Moderate (< 6).

Sensitivity analyses for stress

To assess robustness of the cut-point, we conducted sensitivity analyses using (1) stress as a continuous score, (2) quartiles, and (3) an alternative cut-point at the sample median (≥ 7). Results were consistent across specifications and are reported in Supplementary Section S1 and Table S1. We also separately examined pre-pregnancy (16 items, MS1–MS16) and during-pregnancy (16 items, MS17–MS32) stress sub-scores both individually and with mutual adjustment; results are reported in Supplementary Section S2 and Table S2.

Intimate Partner Violence (IPV)

IPV during pregnancy was assessed by a single item asking mothers whether they had experienced violence from their partner during the index pregnancy (Yes / No). While a single-item measure likely underestimates prevalence, it provides a conservative estimate and has been used in similar resource-constrained settings [40]. Polygamous marriage: Polygamous marriage status was assessed by asking mothers whether their husband had another wife (co-wife) during the index pregnancy (Yes/No). This structural characteristic is common in The Gambia and may influence household resource allocation and maternal stress. Antenatal care (ANC) utilization: ANC utilisation was assessed by maternal report of the total number of antenatal visits attended during the index pregnancy. Based on the WHO 2016 recommendations, adequate ANC was defined as ≥ 8 visits and inadequate ANC as < 8 visits [20]. Maternal health conditions: Maternal health conditions assessed included: anaemia (clinician-diagnosed or symptomatic), chronic hypertension (pre-existing or diagnosed during pregnancy), diabetes mellitus, sickle cell disease, and sexually transmitted infections. For primary analyses, anaemia and chronic hypertension were included as covariates given their prevalence and known associations with child outcomes.

Covariates

Household-level (distal) factors: Settlement type: Urban (Municipal Regulatory Areas) versus Rural (Non-municipal Regulatory Areas). Maternal education: None/Primary, Secondary, or Tertiary. Household income: Monthly family income in Gambian Dalasi (GMD), categorised as Low (< 3000 GMD), Middle (3000–6000 GMD), or High (> 6000 GMD) based on approximate tertiles. Maternal-level (intermediate) factors: Maternal age (continuous, years), Parity (total number of children, continuous), Maternal anaemia (Yes/No), Chronic hypertension (Yes/No), High maternal stress (≥ 6 stressors), Polygamous marriage (Yes/No), IPV during pregnancy (Yes/No) Child-level factors: Child age in months (continuous) and child sex (male / female) were available for all 504 participants and were retained as covariates in all adjusted models because developmental expectations vary substantially by age and modest sex differences have been reported in developmental outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using means and standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, medians with interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed variables (household income), and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using histograms and Q-Q plots. Associations between potential risk factors and the primary outcome were examined using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts < 5) for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.12 (pandas, scipy, statsmodels), R 4.3.0 (base, stats, lme4), and SPSS 26 for initial data management. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided) for all tests.

Hierarchical multivariable logistic regression

Variables were entered in three sequential models based on the socioecological framework: Model 1 (Household/distal factors): Settlement type, maternal education (secondary, tertiary vs. none/primary), household income (middle, high vs. low). Model 2 (Model 1 + maternal/intermediate factors): Model 1 plus maternal age, maternal anaemia, chronic hypertension, high maternal stress, polygamous marriage, IPV during pregnancy, child age, and child sex. Model 3 (Full model): Model 2 plus adequate ANC utilisation (≥ 8 visits). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model fit was assessed using McFadden’s pseudo R² and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The incremental contribution of each level was quantified by the change in pseudo R² (ΔR²). We interpreted pseudo R² as a comparative model fit statistic, not as a direct measure of variance explained in the ordinary least squares sense.

Given that participants were nested within settlements, we estimated cluster-robust standard errors with settlement as the clustering variable (24 clusters). We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (all VIF < 2.5). With 69 outcome events and 9 predictors in the final model (events-per-variable ratio = 7.7:1), we acknowledge that the model may be close to overfitting; results should be interpreted cautiously.

Effect modification by settlement type

To formally test whether the association between high maternal stress and the outcome differed by urbanicity, we added a product term (stress × settlement type) to Model 3 and report the interaction p-value. Stratified analyses (urban vs rural) are presented in Table 3.

Population attributable fractions (exploratory)

We calculated population attributable fractions (PAF) for key exposures as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating exercise. PAF represents the theoretical proportion of cases that could be prevented if the exposure were eliminated, assuming causality, no residual confounding, and a temporal relationship assumptions that are unlikely to be fully met in cross-sectional data. PAF estimates are presented in the Results section (Table 4) with explicit limitations and are not emphasised in the Conclusion. The exploratory nature of these estimates and the underlying causal assumptions are discussed in detail in the relevant Results subsection.

Missing data

Missing data were minimal (< 2% for most variables). Complete-case analysis was used for multivariable regression models. Child age and child sex were complete for all 504 participants. The final models therefore included 504 participants with complete data on all variables.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research and Publication Committee (RePubliC), School of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences, University of The Gambia (approval #: R021 008v2). All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. For participants with limited literacy, the consent form was read aloud in their preferred language, and thumbprints were accepted instead of signatures. Participants could withdraw at any time without consequences. Given the sensitive nature of IPV and stress questions, research assistants were trained in trauma-informed interviewing and provided referral information for support services. Confidentiality was maintained: identifiers were stored separately from study data and accessible only to authorised personnel. Participants received health education materials and service referrals when health concerns were identified.

Results

Study population characteristics

The study included 504 mother-child dyads recruited from across The Gambia between August and October 2021. The mean maternal age was 29.7 ± 6.8 years, with a mean parity of 3.1 ± 2.6 children. Fathers were on average 9.1 years older than mothers (mean age: 38.8 years). Approximately 44% (222/504) of participants resided in urban settlements (Municipal Regulatory Areas), while 56% (282/504) lived in rural areas (Non-municipal Regulatory Areas). The primary outcome, developmental delay, was identified in 69 children (13.7%). Secondary child health outcomes included malnutrition (90 cases, 17.9%), low birth weight (93 cases, 18.5%), preterm birth (43 cases, 8.5%), and child anemia (28 cases, 5.6%). Table 1 presents the complete baseline characteristics stratified by developmental delay status.

Maternal risk factors and exposures

The mean total maternal stress score was 7.7 ± 4.3 stressors (range 0–32; median 7), with children whose mothers had suspected developmental concerns showing significantly higher mean stress scores compared with those without (9.5 ± 4.2 vs. 7.4 ± 4.2, p < 0.001). When categorised, 61.5% of mothers experienced high stress (≥ 6 stressors), 33.9% experienced moderate stress (3–5 stressors), and 4.6% experienced low stress (0–2 stressors). Polygamous marriage was reported by 90 mothers (17.9%) and intimate partner violence (IPV) during pregnancy by 47 mothers (9.3%). Maternal health conditions were common, with 147 mothers (29.2%) reporting anaemia and 69 (13.7%) reporting chronic hypertension. Mothers attended an average of 6.7 ± 2.1 ANC visits, with only 232 (46.0%) achieving adequate ANC as defined by the WHO 2016 guidelines (≥ 8 visits).

Bivariate associations

In bivariate analyses, high maternal stress was associated with a significantly elevated prevalence of suspected developmental concerns (18.7% vs. 5.7%, p < 0.001). Urban residence showed borderline significance (17.1% vs. 11.0%, p = 0.050). IPV during pregnancy was strongly associated in bivariate analysis (34.0% vs. 11.6%, p < 0.001). Maternal health conditions and other social factors showed elevated but non-significant associations. See Fig. 1 for details.

Hierarchical multivariable analysis

Hierarchical regression revealed that maternal-level factors contributed most to the model fit (incremental R² = 0.060) compared with household-level factors (R² = 0.027) or healthcare-level factors (incremental R² = 0.002). High maternal stress was the strongest independent predictor (aOR = 2.80, 95% CI 1.38–5.70, p = 0.004) in the fully adjusted model. IPV during pregnancy showed a positive association of borderline significance (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI 0.99–4.28, p = 0.054). Adequate ANC utilisation was not associated with the outcome (aOR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.46–1.41, p = 0.452). Table 2 presents the complete regression results.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the association between high maternal stress and suspected developmental concerns was robust across alternative parameterisations of the stress exposure: as a continuous score (aOR 1.07 per stressor, 95% CI 1.00–1.15), in quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1 aOR 5.89, 95% CI 2.01–17.26), and at an alternative ≥ 7 cut-point (aOR 1.96, 95% CI 1.07–3.56); see Supplementary Section S1 and Table S1. Decomposing the 32-item checklist into the two reference periods, the during-pregnancy 16-item sub-score was associated with the outcome (aOR 1.17 per stressor, 95% CI 1.02–1.33, p = 0.022) while the pre-pregnancy sub-score was not (aOR 1.10, 95% CI 0.97–1.26, p = 0.144); see Supplementary Section S2 and Table S2.

The full distribution of cumulative maternal stress scores by outcome status is shown in Fig. 2. Scores were right-skewed in both groups, with a visible shift toward higher scores in mothers of children with suspected developmental concerns (median 8, IQR 6–12) compared with those whose children had no concerns (median 6, IQR 4–9; Welch t-test p < 0.001). The cut-point used to define high stress (≥ 6 stressors) coincided with the median of the no-concerns group; 84.1% of mothers of children with suspected concerns scored at or above this threshold compared with 57.9% of mothers in the no-concerns group (Table 1). This pattern is consistent with the use of ≥ 6 stressors as the dichotomisation threshold for multivariable analyses.

Test of effect modification by settlement type

We added a product term for high maternal stress × settlement type to the fully adjusted model. The interaction was not statistically significant (interaction p = 0.59), indicating no detectable effect modification of the stress-outcome association by urbanicity in this sample. For descriptive purposes, stratified bivariate odds ratios were of similar magnitude in the two settings (urban OR = 3.48, 95% CI 1.46–8.32; rural OR = 4.62, 95% CI 1.57–13.61); the rural confidence interval was wide because of the smaller number of rural cases (Table 3). Given the non-significant interaction, we do not interpret these stratum-specific estimates as evidence of differential vulnerability and have removed the corresponding interpretation from the Discussion.

Population attributable fractions (exploratory)

We computed exploratory population attributable fractions (PAFs) for the principal binary exposures examined in this study to provide a rough order-of-magnitude estimate of the proportion of suspected developmental concerns cases that might be associated with each exposure at the population level. PAFs were calculated using Levin’s formula: PAF = Pₑ(OR – 1) / [1 + Pₑ(OR – 1)], where Pₑ is the population prevalence of the exposure. Both crude PAFs (using bivariate odds ratios) and adjusted PAFs (using adjusted odds ratios from the full Model 3 specification, which includes all household-, maternal-, and healthcare-level covariates plus child age and child sex) were computed. Confidence intervals were estimated by non-parametric bootstrap with 1,500 resamples of the analytic dataset, refitting the multivariable model in each replicate. PAFs are reported only for exposures with adjusted OR > 1; adequate antenatal care utilisation is excluded because its adjusted OR (0.81) is suggestive of an apparently protective association for which the standard PAF formulation does not apply.

Adjusted PAF point estimates ranged from 1.5% (maternal anaemia) to 52.6% (high maternal stress) (Table 4). Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were wide for all exposures, reflecting the modest number of outcome events (n = 69) and cumulative uncertainty in odds-ratio estimates. The 95% CI for high maternal stress (19.9–78.8%) was the only adjusted PAF whose interval excluded zero. Confidence intervals for IPV during pregnancy, chronic hypertension, maternal anaemia, and polygamous marriage all included zero, indicating that the observational data do not support precise estimation of attributable burden for these exposures. Adjusted PAFs were uniformly smaller than crude PAFs, as expected when other measured exposures share variance with the focal exposure.

We interpret these estimates as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Interpretation of a PAF as a “preventable fraction” requires (i) a causal effect of the exposure on the outcome, (ii) no residual or unmeasured confounding, (iii) a temporal relationship in which exposure precedes outcome, and (iv) reversibility of the exposure-outcome relationship assumptions that cannot be fully verified in our cross-sectional design with a single-item maternal-report outcome. Reverse causation (caring for a child with developmental concerns may itself increase maternal stress) and residual confounding by unmeasured factors (paternal mental health, household crowding, child nutritional intake, genetic factors) further constrain causal interpretation. The wide bootstrap confidence intervals reinforce this caution. We therefore do not present these PAFs as causal effect sizes or as quantitative targets for intervention; they are reported to indicate the order of magnitude that future longitudinal studies using validated developmental assessments could plausibly investigate.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 504 Gambian mother–child dyads, suspected developmental concerns were reported for 13.7% of children, and cumulative maternal psychosocial stress was the predictor most strongly and consistently associated with the outcome in multivariable analysis. High maternal stress (≥ 6 stressors) was independently associated with the outcome (aOR = 2.80, 95% CI 1.38–5.70). Hierarchical modelling demonstrated that maternal-level factors contributed substantially more to model fit (ΔR² = 0.060) than household-level (R² = 0.027) or healthcare-level factors (ΔR² = 0.002), supporting the central importance of maternal psychosocial well-being in this setting. We did not find statistical evidence of effect modification by settlement type (interaction p = 0.59); the magnitude of association was broadly similar in urban and rural mothers, although confidence intervals were wide.

The 13.7% prevalence of suspected developmental concerns falls within the range reported in sub-Saharan African studies (10–35%) [26, 27]. The independent association between maternal stress and child developmental outcomes aligns with evidence from both high-income and low- and middle-income countries. A meta-analysis of 35 studies found that prenatal maternal stress was associated with a 1.5-fold increase in child behavioural problems, similar to our findings [28]. A South African study reported that maternal depression and anxiety were associated with a 2.2-fold increased risk of developmental delay in children under two years, comparable to our effect size for cumulative stress [29, 30]. Our findings extend this literature into a West African context using a comprehensive 32-item assessment of cumulative stress burden across multiple domains, suggesting that the full spectrum of maternal stressors rather than any single condition may be the more useful target for population-level intervention. Exploratory period-specific analyses (Supplementary Section S2) indicated that the during-pregnancy 16-item sub-score carried most of the cumulative-stress association, consistent with the DOHaD framework outlined in the Introduction; we interpret this pattern cautiously given the cross-sectional design and modest case count.

IPV during pregnancy showed a positive association with suspected developmental concerns of borderline statistical significance (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI 0.99–4.28). The point estimate is consistent with the published literature linking IPV to adverse maternal and child outcomes [31–33], but the lower confidence bound just touches one and we therefore interpret this association cautiously. The association of IPV with the outcome was attenuated after adjustment for cumulative stress, which is compatible with stress acting as part of the pathway by which IPV affects child development; this remains hypothesis-generating in cross-sectional data and would benefit from formal mediation analyses in longitudinal samples.

Contrary to our pre-specified third hypothesis, we did not find statistical evidence that the association between high stress and the outcome differed between urban and rural settings (interaction p = 0.59). Stratified estimates were broadly similar in magnitude (urban OR = 3.48; rural OR = 4.62), with wide rural confidence intervals reflecting the smaller number of rural cases. We therefore caution against interpreting urban–rural differences in the descriptive estimates as evidence of differential vulnerability; this finding from our earlier analyses has been corrected in the present version. The substantive implication is that cumulative maternal stress appears to be a meaningful exposure across both settings in The Gambia, rather than a primarily urban phenomenon.

We found no association between adequate ANC utilisation (≥ 8 visits) and the outcome (aOR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.46–1.41). This is consistent with research suggesting that, in low-resource settings, the content and quality of antenatal care matter at least as much as the number of contacts [34, 35]. The WHO 2016 recommendations emphasise that increased contacts should provide psychosocial support and mental-health screening [36]. Our results are compatible with the interpretation that current antenatal care delivery in The Gambia may not adequately address maternal mental-health needs, although our cross-sectional design cannot establish this directly.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a multi-level analytical framework to quantify the contribution of factors at different ecological levels, an approach rarely used in sub-Saharan Africa and a comprehensive 32-item maternal stress assessment covering five domains. The population-based, multi-stage probability sampling and high response rate (85.9%) support the generalisability of findings to mothers of children under two in The Gambia. The study addresses an evidence gap in West African early childhood development research.

Limitations are important. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, including the possibility of reverse causation in which caring for a child with developmental concerns increases maternal stress. Our outcome is based on a single binary maternal-report item rather than a validated developmental screening instrument; while maternal report has shown acceptable agreement with clinical assessment in similar settings [24, 25], this measurement approach combines suspected developmental delay and growth retardation and may misclassify both directions. Our stress assessment is self-reported and dichotomised, which does not fully capture severity. The single-item IPV measure likely underestimates prevalence [37]. We lack data on important potential confounders including paternal mental health, household crowding, breastfeeding patterns, child nutritional intake, and genetic factors. Our single-country sample may limit generalisability to other African contexts. With 69 outcome events and an events-per-variable ratio of approximately 6.3:1, the multivariable model is close to recommended limits for overfitting and statistical power was modest for detecting smaller associations or higher-order interactions. Cluster-robust standard errors were estimated with a small number of clusters (eight settlements), which may reduce precision.

Implications for policy and practice

Our findings reinforce calls to integrate maternal mental-health support into routine antenatal and postnatal care in low-resource settings. Evidence-based interventions including problem-solving therapy and peer-support groups have been shown to be effective in sub-Saharan Africa and could be embedded within existing maternal health platforms [38, 39]. The lack of association between the number of ANC visits and the outcome suggests that scaling up ANC contacts in isolation may not be sufficient; quality improvements that incorporate routine assessment of psychosocial stressors and mental-health symptoms are likely to be needed. Addressing structural drivers of maternal stress including IPV and economic precarity will require multisectoral approaches beyond the health system. Implementing brief, validated developmental screening at child health clinics could help identify children who may benefit from earlier referral [40–42].

Future research directions

Several research priorities emerge from these findings. First, prospective cohort studies that follow women from early pregnancy through early childhood are needed to establish temporal relationships and to identify sensitive periods during which stress exposures have the greatest impact. Second, studies that pair standardised developmental assessments (e.g., the Malawi Developmental Assessment Tool, the Kilifi Developmental Inventory, or the Bayley Scales) with biomarkers of maternal stress would strengthen causal inference and elucidate biological mechanisms. Third, formal mediation analyses examining whether maternal stress mediates the associations between structural factors (polygamy, IPV, poverty) and child outcomes would inform intervention targeting. Fourth, intervention research testing integrated maternal mental-health and child-development programmes in West African settings is urgently needed. Finally, economic evaluations of maternal mental-health interventions in terms of child developmental outcomes and longer-term productivity would provide critical information for resource allocation and advocacy.

Conclusion

In this multi-level analysis of social determinants of suspected developmental concerns in young children in The Gambia, cumulative maternal psychosocial stress was the strongest independent correlate of the outcome, with a similar magnitude of association observed in urban and rural settings. IPV during pregnancy showed a positive association of borderline statistical significance, while the number of antenatal care visits alone showed no association. Maternal-level factors contributed substantially more to model fit than household- or healthcare-level factors. These cross-sectional findings support the integration of maternal mental-health services into antenatal and postnatal care, alongside multisectoral action on structural determinants of maternal well-being. Longitudinal studies using validated developmental assessments are needed to confirm temporal relationships and to test the effects of integrated interventions.
