Three-Dimensional Bioprinting as a Tool for Tissue Engineering: A Review
Authors: Sneha C. Dare, Pavan S. Bajaj, Anand N. Wankhede, Shubham U. Tawade, Khyati N. Manik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe field of reconstructive and regenerative therapy has shown an increased amount of interest in three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting techniques in recent years. This technique applies 3D printing methods to tissue engineering, utilizing additive manufacturing techniques and bio-inks containing biomaterials and living cells. 3D bioprinting has the potential to create the lost tissue precisely. It provides control over the bio-ink component and printing structure, enabling the creation of spatially diverse constructs for the treatment, regeneration, and restoration of various maxillofacial abnormalities, and appears to be a promising alternative. As a result, this review aims to discuss the advancements in 3D bioprinting, including its multiple applications in regenerative and reconstructive dentistry, as well as future perspectives, such as the evolution of 4D bioprinting.

INTRODUCTION

Regeneration in dentistry poses a big challenge in restoring lost tissue using available techniques. Only histologic analysis, which lacks ethical approval, can confirm the success of various regenerative techniques. Thus, three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting was introduced in reconstructive and regenerative medicine to introduce the required type and amount of cells using customized bio-inks. Tissue regeneration faces enormous problems in trying to mimic such 3D complexity and the multicellular interconnections found naturally in oral tissues. By stimulating certain cellular functions, biomaterials, and growth factors, the regeneration strategies of the tooth and its surrounding tissues aim to replicate dental developmental processes or organizational structures.[1] The process known as 3D printing deposits materials such as plastic polymers, metal, ceramics, or even living cells to create 3D objects. 3D bioprinting is a novel technology that includes the process of printing living cells in a predetermined pattern, and its development is still in its early phases.[2] In tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, it appears to be a promising method that combines cells, growth factors, and biomaterials using additive manufacturing principles.[3] Bioprinting significantly mitigates the homogeneity issues associated with post-fabrication cell seeding by incorporating cell placement into the fabrication process.[4] This article presents an overview of the materials, techniques, and applications of 3D bioprinting in regenerative medicine.

Components of 3D bioprinting

The two major components of 3D bioprinting are bio-inks and scaffolds. Bio-inks are the key material used for printing purposes. Scaffolds are the structural elements that shape the final form of the 3D-bioprinted model.

Bio-inks

Biomaterials compatible with bioprinting are referred to as bio-inks. They are made up of both living cells and biomaterials.[6] In general, we can categorize them into two groups: natural and synthetic bioinks. To promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation following printing, they are comprised living cells and biomaterials that replicate the extracellular matrix environment. Bioinks should exhibit biocompatibility, printability, mechanical stability, tunable viscosity, and functionality modifications to support live cell viability, proper extrusion, structural integrity, printing adaptability, and enhanced cell attachment.[7]

Scaffolds

It is a transient framework, typically composed of biomaterials, employed in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine to facilitate cell proliferation and direct tissue development. Bioprinting strategies involve co-printing biomaterials and cells to form encapsulated cell constructs, and printing biomaterial scaffolds to seed cells either in vitro or in vivo. This is crucial for tissue engineering because it enables scaffolding materials to actively stimulate the cells inside of them without the help of an external mechanism.[8] The geometry of a scaffold, including its curvature, pore geometry, pore size, and porosity, influences the support, stimulation, and guidance of bone development. There are different types of scaffolds based on the compartments present, such as; monophasic, biphasic, and triphasic scaffolds.

3D bioprinting techniques

Inkjet-based bioprinting

Desktop inkjet printing serves as the foundation for 3D inkjet-based bioprinting techniques. Biomanufacturing creates 3D-printed items by placing ink droplets on multiple layers without any physical interaction. Inkjet printing helps create well-structured 3D-printed structures by precisely placing small-volume droplets containing cells or proteins. It accurately localizes cells and biological components in 2D and 3D architectures for tissue engineering and other applications.[10]

Extrusion-based bioprinting

The bio-ink is extruded from the nozzle using a piston or screw under pneumatic pressure or mechanical force to create customized scaffolds with specific mechanical strength. Using a steady bio-ink flow and rapid deposition rates makes this system easy to extend. Shear stress, which depends on nozzle diameter, can affect cell survival. Thus, maintaining cellular viability and printing velocity while reducing pressure or nozzle size is critical.[11]

Laser-assisted bioprinting

Laser-assisted printing produces moderately viscous biological materials without touching the bio-ink. This reduces cell mechanical stress and provides over 95% cell viability. Laser-induced forward transfer (LIFT), stereolithography appearance (SLA), and digital light processing (DLP) are among the numerous processes that are frequently implemented in light-assisted bioprinting.

Laser-induced forward transfer (LIFT)

Laser-absorbing layer transfers a small amount of bioink from a bioink-coated layer to another surface.[12] On the lower side of the absorbent layer, heat vaporizes the bio-ink, forming a high-pressure bubble. This bubble is then received by the receiving substrate, forming the desired pattern.

Stereolithography-based bioprinting

SLA uses laser scanning to polymerize photopolymers and create intricate 3D structures from a bath. When the biomaterial is exposed to light, it polymerizes at the top of the bioink container. The platform carrying the structure will be gradually lowered into the container to build a new layer on top by photopolymerization after each layer hardens.[13]

Direct light processing (DLP) technique

Digital micromirror devices (DMD) control DLP bioprinting lasers. DMD uses several hundred thousand minute mirrors to display images onto the photopolymer bath. The main difference is that SLA moves the light beam, while DLP fixes it. When cured by light, liquid photosensitive resins construct the 3D structure in layers in DLP.[14]

Applications of 3D bioprinting in dentistry

Drug delivery systems

Customized scaffold designs allow precision drug targeting at localized regions like periodontal pockets or tissue damage, improving drug bioavailability by enclosing them in biocompatible materials. Combining different drugs or therapeutic agents on a scaffold with multiple compartments allows customized combination therapy to meet patient needs. This can improve therapeutic outcomes over regular drug administration.[15]

For root coverage

Supporting tissue and bone loss from gingival recession can cause tooth loss. Rasperini et al.[16] utilized a 3D-bio printed scaffold made of a bioresorbable polymer and growth hormones to treat a substantial periodontal osseous defect. The scaffold remained concealed for 12 months, exhibiting a 3 mm clinical attachment gain and partial root coverage.

Socket preservation

Goh et al.[17] tested a 3D-printed bioresorbable scaffold for socket preservation in a pilot randomized controlled clinical trial. After 6 months, scaffolds improved alveolar ridge preservation and bone repair compared to extraction sockets without scaffolds.

Maxillofacial prosthodontics

Maxillofacial prosthodontics is thought to have a bright future due to 3D bioprinting. Recent technological advances have allowed engineers to create a “cyborg ear.” The cyborg ear uses tissue engineering to integrate organic and electrical components. In this, a CAD design of the ear was used to arrange the anatomic geometry and functional materials, and then chondrocyte cells, silicone, and silver nanoparticles were used for 3D bioprinting on an extrusion-based 3D bioprinter. For 10 weeks, the bioelectronic hybrid ear construct was cultured in vitro to generate cartilage tissue. A receiving antenna, an inductive coil, detects signals from audible to radio frequencies in this ultimate bioelectronic hybrid ear. In vitro, the results of this cyborg ear are promising, and human trials are needed.[18]

Endodontics

3D bioprinting is being used to regenerate dental pulp by creating patient-specific shaped 3D dentin-pulp complexes using a combination of bioinks and scaffolding materials. The bio-inks utilized in this procedure often comprise fibrin-based substances that can induce localized differentiation of human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs) into odontogenic cells. The bio-inks are formulated to induce differentiation in precise areas of the printed structure, enabling the fabrication of a dentin-pulp complex with a morphology personalized for the individual patient. After printing, the construct is crosslinked with thrombin and cultured with an odontogenic differentiation medium to promote differentiation and mineralization. This method has demonstrated encouraging outcomes in the regeneration of patient-specific 3D dentin-pulp complexes with localized mineralization.[19]

4D bioprinting

4D bioprinting has led to “smart scaffolds” that may change shape or function in response to certain stimuli at specific times. While there are few 4D bio-printed grafts in the literature, the focus is on smart polymers that can respond to pH, temperature, and magnetic fields to change shape and function, enabling the creation of next-generation smart or programmable tissue constructs. Understanding the kinetics of smart polymers used in 4D printing, as well as the dynamics of human fluids and tissues, could facilitate tissue remodeling monitoring for organ regeneration.[20]

CONCLUSION

3D bioprinting creates scaffolds with uniform cell dispersion. The use of 3D-bioprinted materials shows significant promise because it allows for customization to the desired dimensions, configuration, and organization of specific issues. Due to its architectural control, versatility, and reproducibility, this may surpass conventional fabrication methods. Despite delayed progress, 3D bioprinting in dentistry has huge potential. Nevertheless, it is possible to manufacture anatomical parts of the human body using this technology. The practical use of 3D bioprinting advances personalized medicine.
