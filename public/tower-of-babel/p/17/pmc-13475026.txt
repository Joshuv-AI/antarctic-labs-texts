Recurrent Severe Iron Deficiency Anemia Caused by a Small Bowel Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor in a 90-Year-Old Woman: A Case Report
Authors: Yuna Oshima, Sayaka Kawamoto, Taichi Fujimori, Ryuichi Ohta, Chiaki Sano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is common in older adults, and gastrointestinal bleeding is an important cause that should be investigated. However, identifying the bleeding source can be challenging when upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy findings are negative. We report the case of a 90-year-old woman who presented with anorexia, general fatigue, and dizziness and was found to have severe IDA with a hemoglobin level of 3.9 g/dL. Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy failed to identify the bleeding source. Although her anemia initially improved after blood transfusion and iron supplementation, it recurred, requiring readmission. Double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) revealed a submucosal tumor with ulceration in the ileum, and surgical resection confirmed an ileal gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). Retrospective review of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) images also identified the corresponding small bowel lesion. This case highlights the importance of considering small bowel lesions in patients with recurrent IDA despite negative findings on upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. It also highlights the value of retrospectively reviewing existing CT images, which may reveal overlooked lesions. Our findings suggest that additional evaluation of the small bowel should be considered in patients with recurrent IDA regardless of advanced age.

Introduction

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is frequently encountered in older adults, and evaluation for gastrointestinal bleeding is an important component of identifying its underlying cause. Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy are standard diagnostic procedures; however, in a substantial proportion of patients, the bleeding source cannot be identified. Current guidelines do not recommend routine small bowel evaluation in patients with negative findings on upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. However, additional investigation for small bowel lesions should be considered in patients with recurrent anemia, inadequate response to iron replacement therapy, or anemia requiring blood transfusion [1]. Small bowel gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are relatively uncommon neoplasms and may be detected following investigation of occult gastrointestinal bleeding or IDA [2]. In addition, small bowel bleeding is often difficult to diagnose using conventional upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy alone and may require further evaluation with capsule endoscopy or device-assisted enteroscopy [3,4]. We report the case of a 90-year-old woman who was diagnosed with a small bowel GIST after presenting with recurrent severe IDA. No bleeding source was identified on the initial upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopic examinations. Although blood transfusion and iron supplementation initially improved her anemia, the anemia subsequently recurred, prompting further evaluation of the small bowel. This led to the diagnosis of a small bowel GIST and subsequent surgical treatment. We report this case because it highlights the importance of considering small bowel lesions in patients with recurrent IDA, even among the very elderly, and demonstrates the diagnostic value of retrospective review of existing imaging studies.

Case presentation

A 90-year-old woman presented to our hospital with anorexia, general fatigue, difficulty with physical activity, and dizziness. On Day 1, she developed anorexia and general fatigue. On Day 2, abdominal distension and difficulty with physical activity appeared. On the morning of Day 3, she developed dizziness and hypotension, with a systolic blood pressure below 100 mmHg. On the same day, she visited a local clinic because of abdominal pain. Blood tests and intravenous fluid therapy were performed, and she was instructed to discontinue her antihypertensive medications before returning home. On Day 5, severe anemia was identified on laboratory testing, and she was referred to our hospital and admitted. Her relevant medical history included hypertension and cerebral infarction with residual right-sided hemiparesis. Her medications included aspirin 100 mg once daily and rabeprazole sodium 15 mg once daily.

Physical examination

At admission on Day 5, her blood pressure was 148/82 mmHg, pulse rate was 76 beats/min, respiratory rate was 20 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. Pale palpebral conjunctivae and skin pallor were noted. No cardiac murmur or lower extremity edema was present. Digital rectal examination revealed black stool.

Investigations and diagnosis

Laboratory testing demonstrated a hemoglobin level of 3.9 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume of 81.1 fL, serum iron level of 5 μg/dL, transferrin saturation (TSAT) of 1.3%, ferritin level of 3.0 ng/mL, and an elevated reticulocyte count of 4.3%. These findings indicated profound iron deficiency, and the elevated reticulocyte count was consistent with ongoing blood loss. She was diagnosed with IDA (Table 1). Although the MCV remained within the normal range, the markedly reduced ferritin and TSAT supported the diagnosis of iron deficiency despite the absence of overt microcytosis. Black stool was observed on digital rectal examination, and a fecal occult blood test was positive. To identify the bleeding source, esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy were performed during the initial hospitalization; however, no structural lesion responsible for bleeding was identified. Because persistent gastrointestinal bleeding was strongly suspected despite negative findings on upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy and the subsequent recurrence of IDA, a small bowel lesion was suspected, and further investigation of the small bowel was pursued. Small bowel enterography was performed before double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) to help localize the suspected lesion and facilitate subsequent endoscopic evaluation. It demonstrated a well-defined filling defect in the ileum (Figure 1). Further evaluation with DBE revealed an approximately 25-mm submucosal tumor in the proximal ileum with ulceration at its apex, raising suspicion for a small bowel GIST (Figure 2). Vitamin B12 and folate levels were within the normal range. After identification of the lesion, retrospective review of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) images demonstrated a well-defined small bowel mass corresponding to the same location (Figure 3).

Treatment and clinical course

Because severe IDA with a hemoglobin level of 3.9 g/dL was identified on Day 5, treatment with red blood cell transfusion and oral iron supplementation was initiated. Aspirin was temporarily discontinued and was subsequently resumed. Hemoglobin improved to approximately 8 g/dL following transfusion and iron replacement therapy, and the patient was discharged on Day 21. However, her hemoglobin level decreased again to 7.6 g/dL on Day 40, necessitating readmission. Because the response to oral iron supplementation was insufficient, treatment was changed to intravenous iron replacement. Following the diagnostic evaluation described above, the lesion was considered suitable for surgical treatment, and laparoscopic partial small bowel resection was performed on Day 68. The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged on Day 83. Histopathological examination demonstrated fascicular proliferation of spindle-shaped cells. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for c-kit and partial positivity for CD34, whereas α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and S100 were negative, establishing the diagnosis of an ileal GIST (Figure 4). The resected tumor measured 4 cm in greatest dimension. At the outpatient follow-up visit on Day 96, her hemoglobin level had improved to 10.9 g/dL, and no recurrence of anemia has been observed thereafter (Table 2). Discussion

Routine small bowel evaluation is generally not recommended in patients with IDA and a negative bidirectional endoscopy. However, further evaluation should be considered in patients with recurrent anemia, inadequate response to iron replacement therapy, transfusion dependence, or ongoing overt bleeding, as well as in selected patients receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, in whom identification of a bleeding source may influence subsequent management [1]. In the present case, the patient experienced recurrent severe IDA requiring readmission and blood transfusion. Consequently, further evaluation of the small bowel was performed, leading to the diagnosis of a small bowel GIST with ulceration. Small bowel GISTs are relatively uncommon tumors and may be detected during evaluation for occult gastrointestinal bleeding or IDA [2]. In addition, small bowel bleeding is often difficult to diagnose using conventional upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy alone and may require further small bowel evaluation [3,4]. A previous report described a 64-year-old patient in whom a small bowel GIST was diagnosed following recurrent melena [5]. In contrast, our patient had no clinically evident massive gastrointestinal bleeding and was 90 years old. Although careful consideration was required regarding the indication for invasive diagnostic procedures in this very elderly patient, further small bowel evaluation was pursued because of the clinical course of recurrent severe IDA. This ultimately led to the diagnosis and surgical treatment of the tumor. While diagnostic evaluation may sometimes be limited in older patients because of concerns regarding invasiveness, this case suggests that additional investigation should not be withheld solely on the basis of advanced age.

In patients with recurrent IDA of unknown origin, it is important not only to proceed with additional diagnostic testing but also to re-evaluate existing imaging studies from the perspective of possible small bowel lesions. In addition to capsule endoscopy and device-assisted enteroscopy, contrast-enhanced CT has been reported to be useful in the evaluation of small bowel bleeding [3,4]. In the present case, retrospective review of a previously obtained contrast-enhanced CT scan after lesion identification by DBE revealed findings suggestive of a small bowel mass at the corresponding site. Similarly, Choi et al. reported a case in which a small bowel GIST had been visible on CT but was not initially recognized, resulting in a delay of approximately three years before the final diagnosis was established [6]. These observations suggest that small bowel lesions may not be absent on imaging studies but rather may be overlooked when they are not specifically considered during image interpretation. Therefore, in patients with recurrent IDA and negative findings on upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, re-examination of existing CT images from the perspective of a potential small bowel bleeding source may provide an important diagnostic clue in addition to pursuing further investigations.

Conclusions

We report the case of a 90-year-old woman who was diagnosed with a small bowel GIST following recurrent severe IDA. In patients with recurrent IDA and negative findings on upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, small bowel lesions should be included in the differential diagnosis, and additional small bowel evaluation should be considered. Furthermore, retrospective review of existing imaging studies may provide important diagnostic clues. Even in older patients, evaluation of the underlying cause should not be withheld solely because of advanced age, and the possibility of small bowel lesions should be considered in cases of recurrent IDA.
