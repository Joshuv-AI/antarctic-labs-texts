Malignant Brenner tumor of the ovary: radiologic and histopathologic correlation demonstrating benign-to-malignant transition
Authors: Nozomi Futaki, Takashi Kamiishi, Fumi Kato, Takashi Mitamura, Hiromi Okada, Takeru Kuwabara, Satonori Tsuneta, Masato Yoshikawa, Rina Kimura, Noriko Nishioka, Keita Sakamoto, Manami Sakurai, Hiroko Matsumiya, Hiroyuki Kurosu, Hiroyuki Yamazaki, Kei Ihira, Daisuke Endo, Yosuke Konno, Hidemichi Watari, Kohsuke Kudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant Brenner tumors of the ovary are extremely rare, and imaging features remain poorly characterized. We report a rare case of a malignant Brenner tumor of the ovary with both extra-cystic and intra-cystic solid components. Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography revealed a left ovarian cystic mass with two distinct solid components: an extra-cystic solid component showing low T2 signal, minimal enhancement, no diffusion restriction on magnetic resonance imaging and dense and amorphous calcifications on computed tomography, and an intra-cystic solid component with heterogeneous T2 signals, strong enhancement, diffusion restriction on magnetic resonance imaging and no calcifications on computed tomography. These findings suggested a mixed tumor comprising benign and malignant elements. Surgical resection confirmed that the extra-cystic solid component was consistent with a benign Brenner tumor. The intra-cystic solid component exhibited decreased fibrous stroma and increased cellular density along with focal stromal invasion. These findings led to the diagnosis of a malignant Brenner tumor. Retrospective review identified a continuous band-like low T2 signal connecting the two solid components, corresponding to histologic transition from benign to malignant tissue. Recognition of this transition zone may aid in the diagnosis and differentiation of malignant Brenner tumors from other malignant ovarian neoplasms.Keywords: Benign Brenner tumor, Malignant Brenner tumor, Ovary, Magnetic resonance imaging, Computed tomography, Histopathology

Introduction

Brenner tumors are rare epithelial ovarian neoplasms, representing approximately 2-3% of all ovarian tumors [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, they are categorized as benign, borderline or malignant, based on proliferative activity and the presence or absence of stromal invasion [2]. While benign Brenner tumors are common, malignant variants are extremely rare, comprising only about 1% of cases [3]. Imaging features of benign Brenner tumors typically include markedly low T2 signal intensity on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) due to abundant fibrous stroma and punctate or amorphous calcifications on computed tomography (CT) [[3], [4], [5]]. In contrast, malignant components tend to show higher T2 signal intensity, diffusion restriction, stronger contrast enhancement, and few calcifications [3,[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. We report a rare case of a malignant Brenner tumor, the diagnosis of which was challenging due to the presence of both extra-cystic and intra-cystic solid components. Notably, the extra-cystic solid component was histologically benign, while the intra-cystic solid component was malignant.

Case report

A woman in her 70s presented to a local urology clinic with urinary retention. During evaluation, a large pelvic mass was incidentally identified on CT. She was referred to our gynecology department for further evaluation and treatment. At the time of her visit to our institution, she was asymptomatic. Blood tests showed elevated tumor markers, with CEA at 6.8 ng/mL, CA19-9 at 202.9 U/mL, and CA125 at 121.1 U/mL. MRI was performed on a 3.0-T scanner and included axial and sagittal T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (b = 0 and 1500 s/mm²), and post-contrast fat-suppressed T1WI. Slice thickness ranged from 4 to 6 mm. Contrast-enhanced multidetector CT from the chest to the pelvis was performed in the portal venous phase with a slice thickness of 5 mm. Imaging revealed a large cystic mass measuring 149 × 134 × 175 mm was detected in the pelvis, likely originating from the left ovary (Fig. 1). The tumor had two distinct solid components: an extra-cystic solid component and intra-cystic solid component. The extra-cystic solid component exhibited markedly low signal intensity on T2WI, mild enhancement lower than that of the myometrium on post-contrast fat-suppressed T1WI, no diffusion restriction on DWI, and dense and amorphous calcifications on CT (Fig. 2). The intra-cystic solid component which projected papillary-like into the lumen displayed heterogeneous signal intensities on T2WI ranging from low to high signals, enhancement comparable to that of the myometrium on post-contrast fat-suppressed T1WI, high signal intensity on DWI with low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (0.755 × 10−3 mm2/s), and no calcification on CT (Fig. 3). CT demonstrated a small amount of ascites but no evidence of peritoneal dissemination, omental caking, lymphadenopathy, or distant metastasis. These imaging findings raised suspicion of a collision tumor composed of a benign Brenner tumor and a malignant ovarian neoplasm. Bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and partial omentectomy were performed. Because the patient had impaired cardiac function, comprehensive surgical staging was not performed in order to minimize surgical invasiveness. The tumor was confirmed to originate from the left ovary. Histopathologically, the extra-cystic solid component was characterized by abundant fibrous stroma with calcifications. Small nests of urothelial-like atypical cells were observed (Fig. 4). These features were consistent with a benign Brenner tumor. The intra-cystic solid component exhibited decreased fibrous stroma and increased cellular density, along with nuclear atypia and mitotic figures. Focal stromal invasion was observed (Fig. 5). These findings led to the diagnosis of a malignant Brenner tumor. The tumor capsule was intact, the surgical margins were negative, peritoneal washing cytology was negative, and no omental metastasis was identified. The final pathological stage was FIGO stage IA. No adjuvant therapy was administered after surgery. The patient has been followed every 3 months, with no evidence of recurrence during 2 years of follow-up.

These findings suggest malignancy.

Discussion

Brenner tumors are rare ovarian neoplasms, accounting for approximately 2-3% of all ovarian tumors [1]. According to the WHO classification, they are categorized as benign, borderline, or malignant, based on proliferative activity and the presence or absence of stromal invasion [2]. The vast majority are benign, while borderline tumors account for about 5%, and malignant tumors are extremely rare, comprising only around 1% [3].

Benign Brenner tumors typically exhibit markedly low signal intensity on T2WI due to their abundant fibrous stroma and often demonstrate punctate or amorphous calcifications on CT [[3], [4], [5]]. They are also frequently associated with other epithelial ovarian tumors, particularly mucinous tumors [[3], [4], [5]].

Reports on imaging findings of borderline and malignant Brenner tumors are limited. Some cases have presented as solid masses [3,6], while others appear as large cystic masses with mural solid components [[7], [8], [9]]. In both types, the solid components may include regions characteristic of benign Brenner tumors as well as regions reflecting malignant transformation. Malignant areas are known to exhibit higher signal intensity on T2WI due to reduced fibrous stroma, relatively strong enhancement and restricted diffusion [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Takeuchi et al. reported that the coexistence of calcified components typical of benign Brenner tumors and enhancing components suggestive of malignancy in close proximity may reflect the transition from benign to malignant tissue, and it is characteristic of malignant Brenner tumors [8].

In our case, the tumor exhibited both extra-cystic and intra-cystic solid components, a presentation not previously reported. In the preoperative evaluation, the extra-cystic solid component demonstrated features characteristic of benign Brenner tumors, while the intra-cystic solid component showed findings typical of malignant tumors, raising suspicion of a mixed lesion comprising two distinct neoplasms. However, retrospective evaluation revealed that a band-like structure of low signal intensity on T2WI connecting the extra-cystic and the intra-cystic solid components. This structure exhibited histological features consistent with a benign Brenner tumor and was located adjacent to the intra-cystic solid components (Fig. 6). This spatial relationship was considered to represent the radiologic manifestation of histologic transition from benign to malignant tissue. This finding may serve as a valuable radiologic clue to differentiate malignant Brenner tumors from other malignant ovarian neoplasms.

Correlation of imaging and histopathologic findings. A band-like structure of low signal intensity connecting the extra-cystic and the intra-cystic solid components is identified on T2WI (A, arrow). H&E stain (1.25 ×) of the interface between the band-like structure (white stars) and intra-cystic component (black stars) reveals that the structure exhibited histological features consistent with a benign Brenner tumor and was located adjacent to the intra-cystic solid component showing malignant features. This spatial relationship suggests that the structure may represent histologic transition from benign to malignant tissue.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of a malignant Brenner tumor of the ovary exhibiting a large cystic mass with both extra-cystic and intra-cystic solid components. The presence of a continuous band-like structure extending between these components as seen on T2WI, may represent a transition from benign to malignant histology and serve as an important imaging finding in the differentiation of malignant Brenner tumors from other malignant ovarian neoplasms.
