Oritavancin in Complicated Bloodstream Infections and Endocarditis in Spain and Italy (ORIBAC Study): A Retrospective Multicenter Observational Study

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe management of complicated Gram-positive bloodstream infections (cBSI) and infective endocarditis (IE) often requires prolonged intravenous therapy. Transition to oral therapy is frequently unfeasible because of resistance, drug interactions, or adherence concerns, increasing the risk of intravenous-related complications and healthcare burden. The pharmacokinetic profile and spectrum of oritavancin may enable effective treatment and early discharge. This study evaluated its effectiveness, safety, and impact on hospital resource use in patients with cBSI or IE.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, retrospective, observational study across eight hospitals in Italy and Spain (April 2023–February 2025). Adult patients with Gram-positive cBSI (persistent/refractory BSI or prosthetic material) or IE (2023 modified Duke criteria) treated with ≥ 1 dose of oritavancin were included. Dosing was determined by the treating physicians. Data on outcomes, adverse events (AEs) and healthcare use were collected from medical records. Patients were followed for at least 6 months.ResultsTwenty-seven patients were included: 13 (48.1%) cBSI and 14 (51.9%) IE. Median age was 73 years (IQR 65–81); 18 out of 27 (66.7%) were male; Charlson Comorbidity Index 6 (IQR 4–7). In cBSI, oritavancin was first-line in 6/13 (46.2%) and given in multiple doses in 8/13 (61.6%), median of 2 doses (IQR 2–2.5). Clinical success was 100%, with no recurrence or BSI-related mortality. In IE, Staphylococcus aureus was identified in 5 of 14 cases (35.7%), of which 40% were methicillin-resistant. Native valves were involved in 6/14 (42.8%), prosthetic valves in 6/14 (42.8%), cardiac devices in 2/14 (14.3%). Clinical success was achieved in 85.7% of patients with IE (12/14). Two AEs occurred: nausea and a non-fatal acute coronary syndrome in a patient with pre-existing ischemic heart disease. Oritavancin use reduced 369 hospital days with potential savings of approximately €4235 per patient.ConclusionsOritavancin may represent a safe, effective treatment option for selected patients with cBSI or IE, with advantages in outcomes, reduced hospital stay, and cost containment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01314-7.

Introduction

The incidence of complicated bloodstream infections (cBSI) and infective endocarditis (IE) has risen steadily across Europe over the past decade, driven by population aging, increasing numbers of immunocompromised individuals, and the widespread use of intravascular devices, including prosthetic valves and cardiac implantable electronic devices [1–3]. These infections are predominantly caused by Gram-positive cocci, with Staphylococcus aureus remaining the most frequently isolated pathogen, although Enterococcus spp., including multiresistant strains, have emerged as a significant cause in recent years [4–6].

Standard treatment for cBSI and IE typically involves prolonged antimicrobial therapy, often lasting 4–6 weeks [4, 7, 8]. To reduce hospital length of stay, transition to oral therapy regimens or outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) are increasingly utilized [4, 9, 10]. However, both strategies present important drawbacks. Oral therapy may be limited by poor adherence, gastrointestinal absorption issues, or limited efficacy against multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens [11, 11, 12]. OPAT, on the other hand, often requires central venous access, structured clinical follow-up and frequent laboratory monitoring, which may not be feasible for all patients, particularly older adults or those with unstable social conditions, such as people who inject drugs [13–15]. In these contexts, prolonged hospitalization may become unavoidable, increasing both the risk of nosocomial complications and overall healthcare costs.

Oritavancin is a semisynthetic lipoglycopeptide with potent bactericidal activity against a wide range of Gram-positive pathogens, including methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) harboring VanA or VanB resistance genotype [16, 17]. Although oritavancin is approved only for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSIs) [18, 19], its pharmacokinetic profile—characterized by an extended terminal half-life (approx. 393 h) and sustained tissue concentrations [20, 21]—makes it an appealing alternative to conventional oral transition or OPAT strategies, particularly in patients for whom standard outpatient regimens are impractical. However, current evidence supporting its use in cBSI or IE remains limited to case reports and small single-center studies [22–24].

In this study, we describe our multicenter real-world experience on the use of oritavancin for the treatment or consolidation therapy of cBSI and IE in hospitalized patients across Italy and Spain.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a multicenter retrospective, observational study conducted across eight hospitals located in six urban centers in Italy and Spain. All consecutive adult patients who received at least one dose of oritavancin for the treatment of cBSI or IE between April 1, 2023 and February 28, 2025 were eligible for inclusion. The decision to prescribe oritavancin was made by the treating physician according to routine clinical judgment; no protocol-based selection criteria were used.

A retrospective review of medical records was conducted at each site. Collected variables included demographics, comorbidities, Charlson Comorbidity Index, McCabe classification, type of IE (native vs prosthetic valve), source of bacteremia for cBSI cases, pathogen identification, susceptibility profiles, and source control intervention when applicable. Additional data included prior and concomitant antimicrobial therapies, the indication and rationale for oritavancin use, dosing regimen, total treatment duration, adverse events (AEs), clinical outcomes, recurrence rates, and the estimated hospital days potentially avoided. The main recorded reasons for initiating oritavancin were treatment simplification to facilitate early discharge, antimicrobial resistance to prior therapy, or intolerance to previous antibiotics. Microbiological investigations—including culture, identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing—were performed locally at each institution in accordance with standard protocols.

Definitions

Gram-positive cBSI were defined by the isolation of Gram-positive organisms from at least one blood culture in patients exhibiting signs of systemic inflammatory response (e.g., fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, or leukocytosis). Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) were considered true pathogens only when isolated from ≥ 2 blood cultures drawn from separate venipunctures.

A BSI episode was classified as complicated if ≥ 1 of the following criteria were met:I.Persistent bacteremia, defined as isolation of the same pathogen from blood cultures ≥ 72 h after initiation of pathogen-directed therapy.II.Refractory bacteremia, defined as persistent positive blood cultures beyond 7 days of appropriate treatment in the absence of a removable infectious focus and with ongoing signs of infection.III.Presence of prosthetic material, defined as the presence of any endovascular foreign device (excluding temporary vascular access catheters), such as prosthetic heart valves, vascular grafts, or intracardiac electronic devices.

IE was classified according to current Duke—International Society for cardiovascular Infectious Diseases criteria [25].

Disease severity was assessed using the McCabe and Jackson classification [26]. Adequate source control was defined as removal of infected prosthetic material or drainage of fluid collections in cBSI, and as surgical valve replacement or cardiac device removal in IE.

Patients were followed for a minimum of 6 months after oritavancin administration. Clinical success was defined as resolution of signs and symptoms of infection with no evidence of microbiological recurrence during follow-up. Treatment failure was defined as the lack of clinical response, microbiological recurrence, or death attributable to infection.

AEs were classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [27]. Serious AEs were defined as events resulting in death, life-threatening conditions, prolonged hospitalization, or permanent disability. All other events were classified as non-serious. AEs were recorded based on investigator judgment and temporal association with oritavancin treatment.

Oritavancin Administration

Oritavancin was administered intravenously in accordance with standard clinical practice. Multiple-dose regimens were defined as ≥ 2 doses given no more than 14 days apart. The decision to prescribe single or multiple doses, as well as the specific dosing schedules, was at the discretion of the treating physician. No dose adjustment was required for patients with severe renal dysfunction [18, 19].

Cost Analysis

We conducted an exploratory cost-minimization analysis from the hospital perspective, comparing the projected cost of standard inpatient therapy with daptomycin to the total cost incurred with the oritavancin-based regimen. Potential cost savings were defined as the difference between the estimated cost of inpatient daptomycin therapy and the total cost of oritavancin treatment.

The cost of standard therapy included (i) the placement of a midline catheter, priced at €460 per procedure; (ii) the daily acquisition cost of daptomycin 500 mg (corresponding to approximately 6 mg/kg for an 80-kg adult), calculated at €100.01 per dose in both Spain and Italy; and (iii) inpatient hospitalization costs, estimated at €325.01 per day for an internal medicine ward [22]. To estimate the potential reduction in length of stay attributable to oritavancin, treating physicians were asked whether earlier discharge would have been feasible for each patient and, if so, how many inpatient days might have been avoided. The total number of potentially avoidable days reported across the cohort was incorporated into the model as hospitalization days attributable to the standard daptomycin pathway. The cost of the oritavancin strategy included (i) the placement of peripheral intravenous catheters for each infusion, priced at €90 per insertion; (ii) acquisition costs for all administered doses, calculated at €1508.95 for each 1200-mg vial and €862.25 for each 800-mg vial; and (iii) nursing time required for each administration, estimated at €180 per 3-h outpatient visit.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with Regulation (EU) No. 536/2014, Spanish Royal Decree 957/2020, and the Italian Medicines Agency (AIFA) determination on observational studies, which allow multicenter studies to be conducted on the basis of a single favorable ethical opinion. The study protocol received a favorable opinion from the Ethics Committee of Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón, Madrid, Spain, acting as the promoter Ethics Committee (reference number MICRO.HGUGM.2024–010). All participating institutions conducted the study in accordance with this ethical approval and applicable local requirements.

Statistical Analysis

Given the descriptive nature of this retrospective study, data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. No formal statistical comparisons were performed. Analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 18.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

The median interval between bacteremia onset and oritavancin administration was 4.5 days (IQR 0.5–8.0). Five patients were treated with a single 1200-mg dose of oritavancin, while one patient received an 800-mg single dose (the clinical rationale for this lower dose was not available). The remaining eight patients received multidose regimens with a median of 2 doses (IQR 2–2.5). Of these, six received 1200 mg administered in two (n = 5) or three doses (n = 1), with intervals ranging from 7 to 14 days. The remaining two patients were treated with 1200 mg followed by 800 mg doses, administered either at 7- or 10-day intervals (Table 2).

The most common pathogen was S. aureus (5/14; 35.7%), with methicillin resistance present in 40% of these isolates. Enterococcus faecalis accounted for 28.6% of isolates, CoNS for 21.4%, and Enterococcus faecium for 14.3%; all E. faecium strains were vancomycin resistant.

Among the 14 patients with infective endocarditis, the median interval from bacteremia onset to oritavancin initiation was 21 days (IQR 14–29). At treatment initiation, 3 of the 14 patients with infective endocarditis (21.4%) still had positive blood cultures, whereas the remaining patients had already achieved clearance of bacteremia. The primary indication for oritavancin was treatment simplification to facilitate early discharge (85.7%); one patient received it as a result of intolerance and resistance during prior linezolid therapy, while another because of poor venous access. Surgical indications were present in five cases (35.7%); among these, two underwent surgery: one underwent prosthetic mitral valve replacement 24 h after the first positive blood cultures for E. faecalis, and another had pacemaker extraction 48 h after diagnosis of lead-associated endocarditis due to E. faecium. Oritavancin was administered as a single 1200-mg dose in two patients (14.3%); the remaining received multidose regimens, most commonly 1200 mg every 14 days. Additional details on dosing schedules are provided in Table 3.

Clinical Effectiveness

All 13 patients with cBSI achieved clinical success. Post-treatment blood cultures were negative in all cases, including two patients with concurrent osteomyelitis (due to E. faecalis and vancomycin-resistant E. faecium, respectively) for whom surgical drainage was indicated but not performed. No recurrences occurred during the 6-month follow-up period.

Among the 14 patients with IE, clinical and microbiological success was achieved in 12 (85.7%). Treatment failure was documented in two cases: one involved a patient with CoNS prosthetic valve IE who experienced recurrence 32 days after completing a consolidation regimen of three 1200-mg doses; this patient had not undergone surgical intervention. The second failure occurred in the patient with vancomycin-resistant E. faecium lead-associated IE. This patient received two doses of oritavancin (on days 1 and 8 after the first positive blood culture) but died of uncontrolled infection shortly after the second infusion, with blood cultures remaining positive on the same day. No surgical intervention was performed. In contrast, all patients with native valve IE (n = 6) were successfully managed with medical therapy alone, and none experienced infection-related mortality during the 6-month follow-up.

Safety and Tolerability

Oritavancin was well tolerated in the overall cohort. Two patients (7.4%) experienced serious adverse events temporally associated with treatment (Table 1). One patient, with pre-existing ischemic heart disease, developed a non-fatal acute myocardial infarction with normal coronary angiography following the second 1200-mg dose, administered 14 days after the first. The second patient experienced severe nausea while receiving concomitant trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; symptoms emerged 4 days after oritavancin initiation. In both cases, therapy was discontinued.

Impact on Hospitalization and Costs

On the basis of physician-reported estimates of projected inpatient duration under standard daptomycin therapy, oritavancin could have enabled earlier discharge in 20 of 27 patients (74.0%), corresponding to a total of 369 potentially avoidable inpatient days across the cohort. These estimates represent the maximum number of inpatient days that could potentially have been avoided, assuming that patients receiving standard daptomycin would have required hospitalization for the entire treatment course. Using this framework, the projected cost of the standard inpatient daptomycin regimen was €219,757, whereas the total cost of the oritavancin-based strategy was €105,446, resulting in an estimated cost saving of €114,312, or approximately €4235 per patient (Supplementary Table 2). In a descriptive analysis restricted to the 19 patients (70.4%) who were ultimately managed as outpatients, the model estimated a mean reduction of about 30 inpatient days per patient, translating into a projected saving of roughly €8200 for each individual in this subgroup.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study, we describe the clinical experience of oritavancin in 27 adult patients with cBSI and IE. Our findings suggest that oritavancin is a well-tolerated and potentially effective therapeutic option that may contribute to reducing hospital length of stay, facilitating early patient discharge, and ultimately decreasing overall healthcare resource utilization. This is an encouraging finding for this off-label use of oritavancin and adds to the evidence base supporting the effectiveness of oritavancin for managing complex Gram-positive endovascular infections, particularly in patients for whom standard intravenous therapy is not feasible or carries substantial logistical or clinical limitations [22, 24].

During the last decade, the incidence of cBSI has increased [28]. Despite considerable efforts by clinicians to reduce mortality, no major improvements in prognosis have been observed, especially when the infection is caused by S. aureus or E. faecium, which primarily affect older individuals [29–31]. Oritavancin is a long-acting lipoglycopeptide that demonstrated non-inferiority to twice-daily vancomycin for the treatment of skin and soft tissue infections in its only randomized clinical trial. Regarding BSI, only 18 patients with positive blood cultures were treated with oritavancin in the registration trial and none of these cases were classified as complicated [32]. The largest study currently available includes 72 patients with both uncomplicated and complicated Gram-positive bacteremia who received oritavancin as sequential therapy. In this cohort, 79.2% received a single dose and 20.8% received two doses. However, the median duration of active antibiotic therapy before the first dose of oritavancin was 11 days [23]. Therefore, isolating the specific effect of oritavancin remains challenging, particularly in light of evolving evidence on optimal treatment durations for Gram-positive bacteremia [33].

In our study, among patients with cBSI, clinical success was achieved in 100% of cases, regardless of whether oritavancin was used as first-line therapy (n = 6; 46.2%) or as salvage treatment in two refractory cases following inadequate response to previous antimicrobial regimens. Notably, no recurrence or BSI-related mortality was observed during the follow-up period. This finding is particularly noteworthy considering that 30.8% of patients in our cohort had endovascular prosthetic material, a well-established risk factor for treatment failure and recurrence [34].

Evidence supporting the use of oritavancin in IE remains scarce [22]. Available data are limited to individual case reports and small case series, generally involving fewer than a dozen patients. In these reports, oritavancin was typically administered as sequential therapy following initial bacteremia clearance, with clinical success rates ranging from 43% to 60% [23, 35]. In our cohort, most IE cases were caused by S. aureus (40% MRSA), and most patients could not undergo surgery—both factors typically associated with a higher risk of treatment failure. Despite this, clinical failure occurred in only two patients. However, the use of oritavancin as a part of sequential therapy in 78.6% of IE cases, and in combination with other antibiotics in nearly one-third of patients, limits our ability to draw firm conclusions regarding its efficacy as monotherapy.

From a safety perspective, oritavancin was well tolerated. Even in cases requiring extended treatment duration, the incidence of AEs was comparable to that reported in randomized controlled trials [32, 36, 37], as well as in real-world data, including a registry of 112 patients treated with oritavancin [38]. The non-fatal acute myocardial infarction temporally associated with oritavancin should be interpreted with caution. It occurred in a 72-year-old patient with known ischemic heart disease and normal coronary angiography, with no plausible mechanism linking oritavancin to myocardial ischemia and no similar events reported in clinical trials or post-marketing surveillance. Taken together, these elements suggest a temporal coincidence rather than a drug-related reaction [32, 36–38].

Previous studies have reported hospital stays ranging from 50 to 120 days in patients with cBSI [39] or infective endocarditis, depending on the causative pathogen, valve involvement, surgical need, and associated complications [40]. In our experience, oritavancin appeared to be a cost-effective therapeutic option primarily used as a consolidation strategy to facilitate early hospital discharge, reduce length of stay, and improve treatment adherence.

On the basis of our model, the oritavancin strategy was associated with an estimated saving of approximately €4235 per patient across the entire cohort. When focusing on the 19 patients who were ultimately managed as outpatients—the subgroup most likely to benefit from early discharge—the projected saving increased to about €8200 per patient. These values represent upper-limit estimates for patients who can be safely transitioned to outpatient care, reflecting the maximum reduction in inpatient days reported by treating clinicians. However, this analysis did not incorporate additional cost domains that could further influence the economic impact of oritavancin, including complications related to prolonged intravenous access such as catheter-related BSI, thrombosis, or secondary endocarditis, as well as indirect costs linked to lost productivity or caregiver burden. Because these events disproportionately affect patients requiring extended inpatient IV therapy, the overall economic benefit of oritavancin is likely conservative and may in fact be underestimated.

Our study has some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design, absence of a control group, and limited sample size restrict causal inference and generalizability; therefore, the findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. In addition, although sex-disaggregated analyses were considered, the small cohort and the descriptive nature of the study did not allow meaningful stratification, and detailed sex-specific comparisons were not performed. Second, long-term follow-up data beyond 6 months were not available, preventing assessment of potential late recurrences. Third, the decision to initiate oritavancin, including dosage, dosing intervals, and the addition of concomitant therapies, was made at the discretion of the treating physicians. As such, there was no standardized protocol for the use of the drug. Fourth, echocardiographic assessment—especially transesophageal studies—was not routinely performed, so unrecognized endocarditis cannot be excluded. However, routine echocardiography is not standardized or universally indicated in all cBSI cases, and evaluations were performed when clinically warranted. Fifth, our definition of recurrence does not allow differentiation between relapse and reinfection, as genotypic analyses were not available and susceptibility profiles alone cannot establish clonality. This limits our ability to draw firm conclusions regarding recurrence patterns. Sixth, because AEs were reported solely on the basis of temporal association, a causal relationship with oritavancin cannot be inferred. Some events—such as the myocardial infarction described above—may reflect underlying comorbidities rather than true drug-related toxicity.

Conclusions

This multicenter real-world study provides new evidence supporting the use of oritavancin as an effective option for treating—or consolidating treatment for—complicated Gram-positive infections, specifically cBSI and IE. Beyond confirming its clinical effectiveness and safety, our findings help define its practical therapeutic positioning in scenarios where continuation of standard intravenous therapy is difficult or impractical. By quantifying the potential reduction in hospitalization days and healthcare resource utilization, the study also clarifies the economic implications of its use. Overall, these results expand current understanding of long-acting lipoglycopeptides in complex infections and offer clinically relevant directions for future prospective studies aimed at optimizing dosing, sequencing, and patient selection.
