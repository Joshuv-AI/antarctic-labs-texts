Mature CD209+CD83+CCR7+ dendritic cells infiltrate the arterial wall in giant cell arteritis and derive from in-situ monocyte differentiation
Authors: André Ramon, Hélène Greigert, Baptiste Lamarthée, Corentin Richard, Alexis Varin, Claudie Cladière, Coraline Genet, Marion Ciudad, Noémie Klopfenstein, Roman Praliaud, Guillaume Brenac, Georges Tarris, Laurent Martin, Louis Arnould, Pierre-Henry Gabrielle, Catherine Creuzot Garcher, Paul Ornetti, Sylvain Audia, Romain Boidot, Jean-Francis Maillefert, Bernard Bonnotte, Maxime Samson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to characterize arterial dendritic cells (DCs) in polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) and giant cell arteritis (GCA). Bulk RNA-sequencing, RT-PCR and immunofluorescence analyses were performed from temporal arteries from GCA, PMR and control patients. Public single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data on Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) were analyzed from three GCA and three control patients. Bulk RNA-Seq and RT-PCR analyses demonstrated a high level of expression of DC lineage markers (CD209), DC maturation markers (CD83, CCR7) and chemokines associated with DC maturation in GCA arteries. The level of expression of DC lineage and DC maturation associated genes was significantly lower in PMR than in GCA arteries and similar between PMR and control arteries. GCA arteries expressed high levels of GM-CSF and IFNG mRNA. ScRNA-seq analysis of GCA PBMCs demonstrated high expression of IFNGR and CSF2R by classical monocytes and cultures of CD14+ monocytes with GM-CSF and IFN-γ were able to promote their differentiation into monocyte derived-DCs (mo-DCs). This work provides evidence that mo-DCs infiltrate GCA lesions and could be generated under the influence of GM-CSF and IFN-γ from monocytes infiltrating the arterial wall. Mo-DCs could play an important role in GCA pathogenesis and be targeted by GM-CSF and/or IFN-γ inhibitors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20436-5.Keywords: Dendritic cell, Arterial wall, Giant cell arteritis, Polymyalgia rheumatica, MaturationSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is a large-vessel vasculitis that occurs in patients > 50 years1. Its diagnosis relies on evidence of vasculitis which can be provided by a temporal artery biopsy (TABs) showing non-necrotizing granulomatous arteritis with infiltration of the arterial wall by T cells, macrophages and multinucleated giant cells2. The presence of resident dendritic cells (DCs), which are specialized antigen-presenting cells, was demonstrated in the arterial wall of GCA patients by a former study3. These DCs expressed S100 and CD11c and display markers of maturation, such as the expression of CD83 and CCR74,5. In control arteries, DCs were also shown to be present but in an immature state. In GCA, DCs are thought to be activated by a danger signal through Toll-like receptor (TLR) 4 and TLR5 signaling6, which triggers the release of chemokines inducing the recruitment of CD4+ T cells and then their activation, proliferation and polarization into Th1 and Th17 subsets7. An increase in CD83 mRNA was also demonstrated in temporal arteries from GCA patients, suggesting the presence of activated DCs in the arterial wall4. In addition, treatment with anti-GM-CSF receptor-alpha (mavrilimumab) led to a decrease in CD83 mRNA expression in an ex vivo culture model of temporal arteries from GCA patients, suggesting that this cytokine has a role in the maturation of DCs within the arterial wall8. In polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), a rheumatological condition associated with GCA in 16% to 21% of cases, previous studies reported the presence of mature DCs (CD83+CCR7+) without T-cell infiltrate in the arterial wall of temporal arteries3, raising the question of a pathophysiological continuum between GCA and PMR. DCs are the main antigen-presenting cells. They specialize in activating T cells, inducing their proliferation and producing cytokines controlling their polarization in the main T-helper subsets9. Current classification of DCs is based on their cellular and molecular ontogeny. In humans, DCs are classified into three different subsets: plasmacytoid DCs (CD123+CD303+CD304+) and conventional DCs (cDCs), the latter being divided into two groups: CD141+DC (cDC1) and CD1c+DC (cDC2)10. Myeloid DCs (cDC1 and cDC2) arise from common progenitor DCs (CPD) and their differentiation relies on different transcription factors such as FLT3 (Fms-like tyrosine kinase) and IRF8 (interferon regulatory factor 8)11. CD1c+DCs are the most abundant DCs with a profile that tends to be more heterogeneous than in CD141+DCs12. More recently, the development of single-cell RNA-sequencing has led to the identification of new DC subtypes, revealing significant heterogeneity within human DC subsets and some transitional states among these cells13. Following recent advances in the knowledge of different DC populations, this study aimed to analyze the presence and phenotype of arterial wall DCs in GCA, PMR and control patients.

Results

PMR and GCA temporal arteries exhibit a differential transcriptomic profile

Principal component analysis of the transcriptomic profile of temporal arteries from GCA, PMR and control patients is depicted in Fig. 1A. Notably, the transcriptome of PMR and control arteries was homogenous and similar. By contrast, GCA arteries showed a very different transcriptomic signature compared to PMR and control arteries. This was confirmed by differential gene expression analysis, which showed no difference between PMR and control arteries (Fig. 1B), whereas a total of 1,132 DEGs were identified when comparing GCA to PMR arteries (adjusted p < 0.05; 439 downregulated and 693 upregulated genes; Fig. 1C). Analysis of the top ten enriched pathways highlighted those known to be implicated in GCA pathogenesis, such as “phagosome”, “hematopoietic cell lineage”, “antigen processing and presentation” and “leukocyte transendothelial migration” signaling pathways (Fig. 1D). Hierarchical clustering heatmaps focusing on the top thirty DEGs upregulated in GCA demonstrated a homogenous transcriptome between GCA arteries while PMR and control arteries shared a similar profile (Fig. 1E).

DCs infiltrating the arterial wall of GCA arteries express DC maturation markers

We then focused on dendritic cell signatures. Pathway analysis14,15 revealed an upregulation of five pathways related to DCs in GCA arteries: “phagosome”, “antigen processing and presentation”, “Toll-like receptor”, “chemokines” and “C-type lectin receptor” signaling pathways (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1). Using bulk RNA-seq data from TABs, CIBERSORTx analysis (Fig. 2B) and hierarchical clustering heatmaps (Fig. 2C) revealed a clear DC signature in GCA arteries, which was absent in PMR and control arteries. Among analyzed genes, those associated with myeloid DCs (ITGAX [encoding CD11c], CD209 [DC-SIGN], CD1C and TLR4), and with DC maturation (CCR7, CD83, CD80, CD86, CCL19, HLADQ, HLADP) were also strongly expressed in GCA arteries compared to PMR and control arteries. Expression of THBD (encoding CD141, associated with cDC1) and CCL18, CCL20 and CCL21 (produced by mature DCs) was more heterogeneous between groups (Fig. 2C).

RT-PCR analyses were performed on a higher number of TABs (fourteen GCA TABs, fifteen PMR TABs and fourteen control TABs) to confirm the data obtained by RNA sequencing. It evidenced the presence of mature DCs in GCA arteries, as illustrated by the significantly higher expression of CD209, ITGAX, CD83 and CCR7 in GCA arteries compared to PMR and control arteries. No difference was observed between GCA and PMR arteries regarding the expression of CD1C mRNA whereas the mRNA expression of THBD was higher in PMR and control arteries than in GCA arteries. In addition, we observed a significant increase in the expression level of transcripts of chemokines produced by mature DCs (CCL18, CCL19, CCL20) in GCA arteries compared to PMR and control arteries (Fig. 2D).

Following the high expression of CD209 mRNA, we use immunofluorescence to identify the cells involved. Cells expressing CD209, in the wall of GCA arteries, were mainly located in the adventitia and at the adventitia–media junction (Fig. 3). A contingent of CD209+ cells was also observed in the neo-intima of GCA arteries (Supplementary Figs. 2 A–C). Quantitative analyses showed that CD209+ cells were mainly located in the adventitia and at the media–adventitia junction (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Staining for maturation markers of DCs (CD83 and CCR7) revealed that some CD209+ cells co-expressed CD83 and CCR7, consistent with the phenotype of mature DCs (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 3). These CD209+ cells showed close interaction with CD3 T cells at the media-adventitia junction (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In PMR arteries, the DC signature is heterogenous with weak expression of DC maturation markers

In PMR arteries, bulk RNA-seq analysis revealed a quite similar DC signature to control arteries. Indeed, no expression of ITGAX or CD209 was found in PMR arteries. However, few PMR arteries expressed CD1C and THBD, suggesting that few DCs could be located here (Fig. 2C).

PMR and control arteries expressed similar mRNA levels to genes associated with mature DCs (CD83, CCR7, CCL18, CCL19, CCL20) (Fig. 2D).

Immunofluorescence revealed no CD209+ cells in the arterial wall of PMR or control TABs. In addition, no cells were found to express CD83 or CCR7 in PMR and control arteries (Supplementary Fig. 5). Quantitative analyses of CD209, CCR7 and CD83 MFI revealed no difference between PMR and control arteries (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Taken together, these results show that mature DCs (CD83+, CCR7+) are present in GCA arteries and associated with the production of CCL18, CCL19, CCL20 and CCL21. By contrast, no DCs were found in the walls of control arteries, regardless of the analysis technique used. In PMR, results were more heterogeneous between patients because some DC markers, such as CD1c or THBD, were found in some arteries but not in others, which may explain why they were not identified by confocal microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 7).

CD209+ cells fit with macrophages in the intima while CD209+ cells fit with monocyte-derived DCs (mo-DCs) in the adventitia

Confocal microscopy of GCA arteries revealed that CD209+ cells located in the neointima co-expressed CD68, which was compatible with a phenotype of macrophages (Figs. 4A–C). By contrast, CD209+ cells located in the adventitia and the adventitia–media junction did not express CD68, consistent with a phenotype of mo-DCs (Fig. 4D). These data were concordant with quantitative analysis of fluorescence levels, which showed comparable fluorescence intensity between CD209 and CD68 in the cells from neointima (Fig. 4C), whereas CD68 fluorescence was very low compared to CD209 in the adventitia, indicating that the vast majority of CD209+ cells present in the adventitia do not express CD68 and correspond to mo-DCs (Fig. 4E).

A high level of expression of genes associated with the differentiation of monocytes into mo-DCs is observed in PBMCs and in arteries from GCA patients

As we have identified many mo-DCs in the arterial walls of GCA patients, and these cells are thought to differentiate from monocytes, we used public single-cell RNA-seq data from PBMCs of three GCA patients and three controls16 to investigate whether there was any evidence of the activation of pathways implicated in the differentiation of monocytes into DCs. A total of 31,378 cells passed the filters (Supplementary Fig. 8 A), and data quality control showed that the batch effect was minimal in this dataset (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Furthermore, the proportions of the different immune populations were similar between GCA patients and controls (Supplementary Fig. 8 C). We then focused on monocytes and myeloid dendritic cells (Fig. 5A). Pseudotime trajectories showed that classical monocytes followed two distinct trajectories of differentiation: one into myeloid dendritic cells and the other into nonclassical monocytes. In particular, the divergence of trajectories starts shortly before node 3 (Figs. 5B–C).

Genes driving the monocytes’ differentiation into either nonclassical monocytes or mo-DCs were then assessed by unsupervised analysis along the two trajectories (Fig. 5D). Notably, classical monocytes’ differentiation trajectories showed 2 clusters (cluster 3 and cluster 1) of upregulated genes associated with DC differentiation (Fig. 5D). Focusing on DC trajectory, we observed that genes from cluster 3 started to be upregulated from node 2 and are associated with cytoskeleton changes (VIM, CAPG), C-type lectin receptors (GPR183, SELL), adhesion molecules (LGALS2) and antibacterial enzymes (LYZ). Genes from cluster 1, which started to be upregulated from node 3, are closely related to antigen presentation as demonstrated by the high expression of MHC-II-associated genes such as HLA-DM, HLA-DQ, HLA-DP, HLA-DR, CD74 (coding for Class-II-associated invariant chain peptide), CIITA (positive regulator of class II major histocompatibility complex gene transcription) and CLEC10A (C-type lectin receptor gene). Interestingly, and in contrast to the nonclassical trajectory, the expressions of macrophage-associated genes such as CD68 and MAFB were repressed after node 3 (cluster 4) in the myeloid DC trajectory.

Then, to confirm these results at tissue level, we analyzed 11 arteries by bulk RNA-seq in order to create a hierarchical clustering heatmap of this panel of myeloid DC trajectory-associated genes (i.e. clusters 1 and 3). The results, which are depicted in Fig. 5E, show that these genes were also highly expressed in GCA arteries compared to PMR and control arteries, supporting the hypothesis of in-situ differentiation of monocytes into mo-DCs in GCA arteries.

GM-CSF and IFN-γ promote mo-DC differentiation

Since IL-4 and GM-CSF are known to be involved in the in-vitro differentiation of monocytes into mo-DCs17,18, we measured the expression of their mRNA by bulk RNA-seq and RT-PCR in arteries. Analyses demonstrated high expression of CSF2 (encoding for GM-CSF) in GCA arteries but no significant expression of IL4, especially in RT-PCR analyses (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 9).

In addition, we found high expressions of GM-CSF and IFN-γ genes and their receptors (CSF2RA/CSF2RB and IFNGR1/IFNGR2 respectively) in arteries (supervised analysis) and circulating classical monocytes from GCA patients (Figs. 6A–B).

As IL-4 was not significantly expressed in GCA arteries, we hypothesized that IFN-γ, a key cytokine of GCA pathogenesis, could also promote the differentiation of monocytes into mo-DCs. The gating strategy of flow cytometry analysis is depicted in Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 10. As expected, non-stimulated CD14+ monocytes strongly expressed CD14 and CD163 at baseline (Fig. 6D). Additions of GM-CSF and IL-4 to the culture led to a marked decrease in these markers at day 3 and the strong induction of CD209 (median MFI = 2894 vs. 21.50, p < 0.0001) and HLA-DR (median MFI = 3674 vs. 2831, p < 0.0001). Similar results were observed at day 7 with a slight decrease in CD209 (median MFI = 673).

In-vitro culture in the presence of GM-CSF and IFN-γ led to the increased expression of HLA-DR (median MFI = 9349) at day 3, which remained steady at day 7 (median MFI = 10492). CD209 increased at day 3 but to a lesser extent compared to GM-CSF + IL-4 and reached higher expression at day 7 (median MFI = 1083).

In addition, the expression of CD163, a specific marker of monocyte and macrophage populations19,20, had almost disappeared at day 3 in the presence of GM-CSF and IL-4 or GM-CSF and IFN-γ. Similar findings were obtained with CD14 in the presence of GM-CSF and IFN-γ. Results for non-stimulated monocytes at days 3 and 7 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Altogether, these results demonstrate the ability of GM-CSF and IFN-γ to trigger the differentiation of monocytes into mo-DCs, without the generation of macrophages and with a similar proportion but longer delay of differentiation compared to treatment with GM-CSF and IL-4.

Discussion

This study confirms the presence of DCs within the arterial walls of GCA patients. CD209 (DC-SIGN), CD83 and CCR7 were co-expressed, consistent with a mature DC phenotype. Unlike a previous study3, and although we observed an heterogenous signal of conventional DCs in some PMR arteries, our observations do not suggest that temporal arteries from PMR contain similar amounts of mature DCs to GCA ones. Our contradicting results could be explained by differences in the selection of patients included at baseline. Specifically, it is possible that some patients classified as PMR in a previous study3 actually had subclinical GCA with predominant large-vessel involvement (hence a negative TAB). Indeed, such involvement was less commonly sought at the time that the study was published. Therefore, some patients with GCA may have been classified as PMR, which could explain the presence of activated DCs in their temporal arteries3. In our study, all patients underwent a CT scan and/or18FDG-PET to ensure that associated LV-GCA was not overlooked in those with PMR symptoms, especially since it has been demonstrated that subclinical GCA occurs in 10% to 30% of PMR patients21. It is possible that some PMR patients have activated DCs in the walls of their temporal arteries and others do not. It would be interesting to see if these patients go on to develop GCA, which would support the hypothesis that there is a continuum between PMR and GCA. In addition, our results suggest the value of studying the temporal arteries of GCA patients with negative TABs as a separate group in pathophysiological studies3,5.

Our study confirms the presence of activated DCs in GCA lesions and provides new data on their phenotype and ontogeny. First, we confirmed the expression of DC maturation markers such as CD83 and CCR7 and the expression of CCL18, −19, −20, −21, as previously demonstrated3,5. We then showed that these DCs were mainly located in the adventitia and had a phenotype of mo-DCs rather than resident DCs, and that the CD209 cells located in the neointima were mainly macrophages. Because of their monocytic origin, we think that these mo-DCs are probably not involved in initiating the immune response in the pathogenesis of GCA. Bulk RNA-seq and RT-PCR showed a higher expression of CD11C gene in arterial wall of GCA arteries. This strong expression is probably due to the presence of macrophages22, although the possibility of some resident dendritic cells cannot be excluded. A more in-depth analysis of dendritic cell lineage using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which was not conducted in this study, could offer stronger evidence regarding the distinct DC subsets present within the arterial wall of GCA-affected arteries.

Previous studies have described the implication of mo-DCs in rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis23. Mo-DCs express a high diversity of C-type lectin receptors compared to conventional or plasmacytoid DCs, which allows them to react to a large variety of exogenous and endogenous molecules24. From our results, mo-DCs could differentiate from circulating classical monocytes under inflammatory conditions. This differentiation is controlled by several genes implicated in structural changes and closely related to antigenic presentation, as demonstrated by their high level of expression of genes related to MHC-II. Goudot et al. have demonstrated that the repression of MAFB by AHR (Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor) promotes the preferential differentiation of monocytes into DCs rather than macrophages25. Interestingly, our results showed that classical monocytes that differentiate into mo-DCs repress the expression of these genes associated with macrophage differentiation (CD68 and MAFB). Therefore, some monocytes could be predisposed to differentiate into dendritic cells rather than macrophages under the influence of local factors. One of the essential mediators of monocytes’ differentiation into mo-DCs is GM-CSF, which is found in excess in GCA arteries26 and has recently been shown to play a major role in the pathogenesis of GCA27. GM-CSF also appears to be a promising therapeutic target to spare glucocorticoids28. When mo-DCs are generated from monocytes in vitro, GM-CSF is usually combined with IL-417,18. However, we did not find IL4 gene expression in GCA arteries. Another key cytokine that could prime monocyte differentiation into DCs is IFN-γ, since it was demonstrated in previous studies29. Indeed, IFN-γ is a key cytokine inducing MHC-II expression30. Consistently, we have demonstrated in this study that IFN-γ and GM-CSF could trigger the differentiation of CD14+ monocytes into mo-DCs with a delay compared to a combination of GM-CSF and IL-4.

One limitation of this study could be that some patients were receiving glucocorticoid therapy at the time of temporal artery biopsy. However, bulk RNA sequencing results show consistency in the transcriptomic profile of the arteries studied between patient groups. Similarly, for RT-PCR analysis, the median duration of glucocorticoid therapy was short (< 4 days) for GCA patients, and the number of arteries from PMR patients was relatively low, with again, homogeneous results. Another limitation of this study is the lack of functional analyses, particularly regarding cytokine production by mo-DCs and their capacity to activate T cells.

Even if the role of mo-DCs is not totally clear, it has been demonstrated that these cells produce IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-12 and IL-23 and then promote the polarization of Th1 and Th17 cells24, which are the two main subsets observed in GCA lesions31,32. Therefore, their presence in GCA lesions is particularly relevant given these cytokines’ role in GCA2. Furthermore, a previous study demonstrated that mo-DCs could be generated from the circulating monocytes of GCA patients in the presence of GM-CSF and IL-4. These DCs were characterized by a low level of expression of PD-L1 and were able to promote T-cell proliferation18. Therefore, mo-DCs or cytokines involved in their differentiation from monocytes such as GM-CSF could be a potential target for the treatment of GCA. Along this line, a recent study demonstrated that tofacitinib reduced the frequency of mo-DCs in rheumatoid and psoriasis arthritis23.

In addition, we also found CD209+ cells co-expressed CD209 and CD68 in the intima layer, which is consistent with a macrophage phenotype. CD209 can be expressed by M2 macrophages, which are known to be present in the neo-intima of GCA arteries33. Macrophages also play a role in antigen presentation, enhancing the recruitment and activation of T cells and their subsequent polarization into Th17 cells31. A previous study26 demonstrated that various phenotypes of macrophages can be differentiated under the influence of GM-CSF (CD206+ macrophages) or M-CSF (FRβ macrophages), the latter being mostly located in the intimal layer. Therefore, we think that circulating monocytes recruited into the adventitia in the GCA pathogenetic process could actually differentiate into either CD206 macrophages or CD209 mo-DCs depending on the microenvironment.

Conclusion

Our study confirms the presence of mature DCs in the walls of GCA arteries. However, these cells exhibited a phenotype of mo-DCs distinct from residents DCs. GM-CSF and IFN-γ may induce the differentiation of classical monocytes into mo-DCs. These cells recruited at a later stage could strengthen the activation of T cells and thereby amplify the inflammatory response in GCA. However, functional analyses are required to confirm this hypothesis. Targeting these cells by blocking GM-CSF with mavrilimumab or IFN-γ with JAKi could be of interest for the treatment of GCA.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients were prospectively enrolled at the Department of Internal Medicine and Clinical Immunology and the Department of Rheumatology, Bourgogne-Europe University Hospital, Dijon, France. Fresh TABs with histopathological features of GCA were obtained from fourteen patients newly diagnosed with GCA according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) 2022 criteria34. Negative TABs were obtained from fifteen PMR patients who fulfilled the 2012 ACR/EULAR criteria35 and had no associated GCA, and from fourteen control patients without GCA or PMR. For the fifteen patients with PMR, the diagnosis of GCA was ruled out by a negative TAB and examination of large vessels by [18 F] fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) and/or CT angiography scan36, which showed no evidence of vasculitis in all cases. Patients with a final diagnosis of GCA but a negative TAB (i.e. isolated large-vessel GCA) were excluded (n = 4) (Supplementary Fig. 12). Details of the clinical, biological and therapeutic characteristics of enrolled patients and controls are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE tissue sections from control (n = 3), PMR (n = 5) and GCA (n = 3) TABs (Supplementary Table 2) using the Maxwell RSC RNA FFPE kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA sequencing was performed as described previously37. Details of the bulk RNA-seq analysis are provided in the supplementary methods. The bulk RNA-seq data have been deposited in BioStudies under accession number E-MTAB-13739. We also used public data (GSE174694) from a large sample of temporal arteries via TempO-seq38 to confirm the bulk RNA results (Supplementary Fig. 13). Deconvolution analysis was performed using the CIBERSORTx pipeline39 on bulk RNA-seq data. The reference signature used for the CIBERSORTx analysis is detailed in the supplementary methods and Supplementary Fig. 14.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from fresh temporal arteries and cDNA obtained by reverse-transcription employing a random hexamer priming kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Applied Bioscience) in a final volume of 100 µL. Then, specific predeveloped TaqMan probes from Thermo Fisher Scientific (TaqMan Gene Expression Assays) were used for PCR amplification (Supplementary Table 3). Fluorescence was detected with the CFX96™ Real-Time PCR Detection System and results analyzed with CFX Manager™ software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Gene expression was normalized to the expression of the endogenous control GUSB using the comparative ΔCt method. mRNA concentration was expressed in relative units with respect to GUSB expression (relative expression).

Immunofluorescence-confocal microscopy

Artery sections were fixed in cold paraformaldehyde (4%) and exposed to increasing concentrations of saccharose (15–30%). Then, sections were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) (Sakura, Flemingweg, The Netherlands) and stored at −80 °C. 10 μm cryostat sections were fixed, blocked and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 4) and then donkey secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorochromes (Supplementary Table 5). Visualization was performed with Leica TCS SP8 fluorescence microscopes. Quantification analysis was performed using FIJI software by determining mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Background noise was defined as MFI < 0.5.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Previously published single-cell data on Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) from three GCA patients and three controls were used (GSE198891)16. The raw gene expression matrices generated per sample were merged and analyzed in R (version 4.3.2) using the Seurat (version 5.0.1) package40. Seurat objects were created and filtered with the following parameters: cells with < 2,000 or > 25,000 total counts per cell detected and with more than 20% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Data were normalized and scaled using Seurat’s respective functions NormalizeData and ScaleData, principal components were calculated using RunPCA and the 2,000 most variable features were used for integration following the IntegrateLayers and CCAIntegration methods to limit the batch effect. UMAP coordinates were calculated using Seurat’s RunUMAP function and the first seventeen principal components and visualized using Seurat’s DimPlot function. Automated annotation of single cells was performed using the SingleR (version 2.4.1) and Celldex (version 1.12.0) packages41 with Monaco’s reference expression dataset and fine curated cell type labels. The pseudotime trajectories analysis is detailed in the supplementary materials and methods.

In-vitro differentiation of CD14+ cells

PBMCs were obtained by density gradient (Milieu de Séparation des Lymphocytes, Eurobio) centrifugation from ten healthy volunteers. CD14+ cells were sorted using positive magnetic cell sorting following the manufacturer’s instructions (CD14+ isolation kit, Miltenyi Biotec) using an automated cell separator (autoMACS, Possel protocol, Miltenyi Biotec). CD14+ cells were seeded in 6 well plates (1 million CD14+ cells/mL) with RPMI medium and cultured alone or with GM-CSF (100 ng/mL) (Miltenyi Biotec) and IL-4 (10 ng/mL) (Miltenyi Biotec) or GM-CSF and IFN-γ (100 ng/mL). Flow cytometry analysis was performed at day 0 (no stimulation), and after 3 and 7 days of culture. Cells were incubated with antibodies or control isotypes (Supplementary Table 6) for 30 min at 4 °C in Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.5% of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Analyses were performed on Attune NxT flow cytometers and analyzed with FlowJo v10 software.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as numbers (%) for categorical variables and medians (IQR) for continuous variables. Chi-2, Fisher, Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed to compare data, as appropriate. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Analyses were performed with GraphPad PRISM software.
