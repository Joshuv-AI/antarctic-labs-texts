Impact of patient neighborhood area deprivation index on prognostic factors and outcomes in endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the endometrium: a retrospective multi-center healthcare network analysis
Authors: Anna Weimer, Savannah Tollefson, Sneha Padamati, Sarah Bell, Reethi Padmanabhan, Jessica Berger, Sarah Taylor, Ronald Buckanovich, Robert Edwards, Alison Garrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeighborhood vulnerability, as measured by area deprivation index (ADI), has been associated with disparities across the cancer continuum; however, little is known about its impact on endometrial cancer. We sought to understand the impact of neighborhood deprivation on clinicopathologic features, stage at diagnosis, and outcomes for endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC) within a large multicentered healthcare network.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of Pennsylvania residents within a multicenter healthcare network diagnosed with EEC between 2018 and 2021(n = 1388). Demographic and pathologic factors were compared against dichotomized state-level ADI quantiles. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression explored the association between stage at diagnosis and ADI. Cox proportional hazard models assessed the association between ADI and progression-free (PFS) and overall survival (OS).ResultsHigh ADI was associated with elevated BMI, non-White race, current smoking, and recurrence/progression on therapy. Although not statistically significant, the high ADI cohort tended to have increased rates of negative prognostic tumor features. Controlling for age and BMI, increasing ADI decile increased the odds of being diagnosed with a more advanced stage by 16% (p = 0.0004). In the adjusted cox regression model, higher ADI was associated with decreased PFS (aHR 1.42 [95% CI: 1.07, 1.88]), but not OS (aHR 1.16 [95% CI: 0.88, 1.55]). Additionally, when controlling for clinicopathologic tumor features, higher ADI was not associated with PFS or OS.ConclusionAmong patients with EEC, higher neighborhood ADI was associated with higher odds of diagnosis at a later stage of disease and decreased PFS in multivariable analysis. Further research is needed to explore potential drivers of this relationship.Keywords: Area deprivation index, Endometrial cancer, Neighborhood deprivation

Background

Endometrial cancer incidence and mortality rates are rising in the United States and across the global community. In the United States, the annual incidence of endometrial cancer is predicted to reach 99,000 by 2040 compared to 58,000 in 2018. Mortality due to endometrial cancer is estimated to outpace other malignancies and is expected to move from the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death in 2021 among women to the fourth by 2040 (Rahib et al., 2021). Furthermore, disparities in endometrial cancer outcomes along racial and socioeconomic lines remain one of the highest among all cancer types for women, prompting ongoing calls to better understand the underlying drivers of these disparities (Donkers et al., 2019; Siegel et al., 2024; Madison et al., 2004). Disparities in outcomes are likely driven by a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic differences, access to quality care, racial discrimination, and environmental factors (Chapman-Davis et al., 2023).

Neighborhood deprivation is increasingly recognized as a unique and meaningful driver of cancer outcomes. Neighborhood quality is broadly defined as the quality of the social and physical environment of the neighborhood, including building quality, infrastructure, and physical spaces, and the impact on residents (Gocer et al., 2023). Alterations in this environment are thought to impact cancer outcomes secondary to differential access to quality care, material and resource deprivation, associated alterations in health behavior and health-seeking behavior, and differential environmental and psychosocial exposures (Gomez et al., 2015). Given the broad definition, measurement of neighborhood deprivation is inconsistent in the literature and includes a variety of metrics. Neighborhood area deprivation index (ADI) is a validated composite measure of neighborhood level disadvantage based on 17 measures of education, employment, housing-quality, and poverty measures from Census and American Community Survey (ACS) data (Kind and Buckingham, 2018). Increased neighborhood vulnerability has been associated with worse outcomes across the cancer continuum, including in other gynecologic malignancies (Fuemmeler et al., 2023; Borho et al., 2025; Hicks et al., 2025).

There are several studies that have examined the association of neighborhood vulnerability on endometrial cancer outcomes, and the results are mixed (Gottschlich et al., 2025; Gamble et al., 2024; Ackroyd et al., 2025). One retrospective cohort of endometrial cancer patients in Detroit demonstrated increased hazard of endometrial cancer-specific mortality among those living in the highest area deprivation compared to the lowest, even when controlling for stage of disease and race (Gottschlich et al., 2025). Another retrospective cohort from a state registry (n = 34,356) found a similar association between neighborhood deprivation and decreased survival (aHR 1.24) with race mitigating this impact. The authors found that the greatest disparities in survival between Black and White patients was among those living in the most affluent neighborhoods suggesting the protection provided by decreased neighborhood vulnerability is not afforded equally to the Black population (Gamble et al., 2024). In contrast, a retrospective analysis of 797 individuals with stage I-III endometrioid endometrial cancer treated at single tertiary facility found no association between national ADI or race and progression free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS). The authors suggested that access to a tertiary care facility and its resources may mitigate the impact of neighborhood deprivation (Ackroyd et al., 2025).

Prior studies have focused on either a single urban setting or across an entire state with multiple health systems, thus resulting in potential confounders. Given the mixed results in the previously conducted studies, we sought to understand the impact of patient neighborhood ADI on clinicopathologic features, stage at diagnosis, and progression free and overall survival of individuals with endometrioid endometrial adenocarcinoma within a large single institution multicentered healthcare network across the majority Western and Central Pennsylvania.

Methods

A retrospective review was conducted of all patients newly diagnosed with endometrioid endometrial adenocarcinoma who had their first encounter between 2018 and 2021 at one of ten health care sites across the UPMC's multicenter healthcare network in Pennsylvania (n = 1387). Patients with current addresses outside the state were excluded. The study was conducted in accordance with all regulatory guidelines, and the methods were approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY22060080). All statistical analyses were completed in STATA/SE 16.0 software (STATA Corp) and a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Retrospective chart abstraction was utilized to collect baseline demographic data at the time of diagnosis including age, body mass index (BMI), home address, self-described race and ethnicity, smoking status, and facility where they received care (tertiary hospital versus a satellite site in the same network). Only patients with isolated endometrioid histology were included; all mixed histology was excluded. Clinicopathological characteristics of the tumors were abstracted including stage, grade, presence of lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), depth of myometrial invasion, tumor size, and molecular classification when available including mismatch-repair (MMR) status and p53 immunohistochemical staining. Stage was defined according to the 2009 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics staging system for endometrial cancer (Creasman, 2009). Treatment course and outcome data including initial treatment modality or modalities, recurrence status, time-to-recurrence, and disease/vital status at last follow-up were also collected.

The neighborhood ADI was used to determine patient neighborhood vulnerability. ADI can be mapped to census blocks or 9-digit zip codes and scaled on a state or national level. On the state level, ADI is divided into deciles with lower values signifying lower levels of deprivation. State-level ADI scores from 2020 were assigned based on 9-digit zip codes; if only a PO box address was available, this was used as the patient's address (Kind and Buckingham, 2018; University of Wisconsin School of Medicine and Public Health, 2020). As no standardized or recommended division for comparison exists, ADI was dichotomized at the median ADI value to account for the skewed distribution of ADI in our cohort with the majority of patients living at a higher ADI and to maximize sample size for further comparison. Low ADI contained all those who lived in deciles 1–7 and high ADI contained deciles 8–10. ADI was also used as a continuous variable in survival analysis.

Demographic and pathologic factors were compared against dichotomized ADI groups using two sample t-test, chi-square, Wilcoxon rank sum, and Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Ordinal logistic regression was used to assess the impact of demographic variables, including ADI, on cancer stage at the time of diagnosis. Stage was coded as an ordinal variable with increased value for each increasing subcategorization of stage (e.g., stage IIIC1 was classified as a lower stage and discrete stage compared to stage IIIC2). Variables that were independently associated with stage in univariable regression were included in the multiple ordinal logistic regression model. Factors independently associated with ADI were included in additional modeling to ensure they did not confound any identified relationships.

Survival analyses were completed using Kaplan-Meier survival methods and Cox proportionate hazard regression models. Overall survival was defined as the time from start of any therapy, regardless of therapy intent (including curative and palliative therapies), to the time of last follow-up with any physician on or before September 1, 2025, or death. Progression free survival was similarly defined as the duration between the start of any therapy and the date of first documented recurrence, including pathologic and radiographic recurrences, or death. Multiple regression controlled for pre-determined demographic characteristics including patient age, BMI, smoking status, race, and care facility, and stage of disease at diagnosis. Additional modeling was used to further control for tumor characteristics including LVSI, tumor grade, MMR status, and depth of myometrial invasion. The proportionate hazard assumption was tested using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. When utilized as a continuous variable, age and BMI violated the proportionate hazard assumption and, as such, were dichotomized and no longer violated the assumption. Stage was also further categorized to stage I, II, III, IV as it violated the assumption when broken down into further subdivisions. Correlation matrices were used to assess collinearity between variables, and there was no significant collinearity identified. Subgroup post-hoc analysis was conducted to explore the association of ADI with PFS and OS by race and by stage at diagnosis.

Results

There were 1388 patients with endometrioid endometrial cancer included in the cohort. Age was normally distributed with a mean age of 63.7 years old (95% CI 63.3, 64.6). BMI ranged from 16.1 to 81.2 kg/m2 and the distribution was skewed to the right as 90% of the population was considered overweight with BMI greater than 25 kg/m2. The population was racially homogenous and predominantly identified as White (96.3%). Patients were primarily diagnosed with early-stage disease with 60.2% diagnosed with stage IA disease and the majority lived at or above an ADI of 7 (Table 1).

Although stage at time of diagnosis was not significantly associated with ADI when comparing dichotomized categories, ordinal logistic regression demonstrated an association between stage and ADI (p = 0.033). In multivariate regression, controlling for the variables associated with stage in univariate analysis, age and BMI, for every increase in ADI by one, patients had 16% higher odds of being diagnosed at a more advanced stage of disease (p = 0.004). Additional modeling showed that this association remained significant (p = 0.002) when controlling for variables associated with higher ADI, including smoking status and race, neither of which were independently associated with stage in ordinal regression.

In survival analyses, the average follow-up time was 1582.6 days or approximately 4.3 years (range 0 to 2922 days). Three-year and five-year PFS in the overall cohort were 86.5% and 83.6%, respectively. Three-year and five-year OS were 90.0% and 84.2%, respectively. In univariate Kaplan-Meier analysis, high ADI was associated with a decrease in both PFS (chi (Donkers et al., 2019) p = 0.0050) and OS (chi (Donkers et al., 2019) p = 0.0473) (Fig. 1). After adjusting for patient demographic characteristics and stage at diagnosis, high ADI was significantly associated with earlier disease progression (PFS aHR 1.42, 95% CI 1.07–1.88, p-value 0.014), but not OS (aHR 1.16, 95% CI 0.88–1.55, p-value 0.298). When further controlling for tumor characteristics including LVSI, depth of myometrial invasion, tumor grade, and MMR status, high ADI was no longer statistically significantly associated with PFS (aHR 1.25, 95% CI 0.89–1.76) or OS (aHR 1.23, 95% CI 0.81, 1.87) (Table 3). When ADI was included in the analysis as a continuous rather than dichotomized variable, the results were similar.

In subgroup analysis by stage, the impact of ADI on PFS was driven by stage I disease (HR 1.67, p 0.006) and remained significant when controlling for demographic characteristics and stage (aHR 1.64, p 0.011), but not when controlling for tumor characteristics as well (aHR 1.39, p 0.125). The relationship between ADI and PFS in more advanced stages was not statistically significant in univariate or multivariable analysis. There was no impact on OS in any stage of disease.

In subgroup analysis by race, ADI was associated with PFS and OS in the White population in univariate analysis (PFS HR 1.35, p 0.033; OS HR 1.33, p 0.043), but not when controlling for stage, demographic, and/or tumor characteristics. Neither PFS nor OS were associated with ADI among the non-White population in either univariate (PFS HR 6.14, p 0.080; OS HR 0.79, p 0.743) or multivariable analysis.

Discussion

Our analysis suggests a complex relationship between neighborhood deprivation, clinicopathologic features, and outcomes of endometrioid endometrial carcinoma. To our knowledge, no prior study has examined potential associations between neighborhood deprivation and specific tumor characteristics of endometrial cancer, apart from stage at diagnosis. Although not statistically significant, we found that the higher ADI group tended to have higher rates of negative prognostic tumor features including rates of LVSI, greater than 50% myometrial invasion, higher tumor grade, and higher rates of dMMR tumors and hypermethylation of the MLH1 promotor among dMMR tumors. We also found that higher area deprivation had increased odds of being diagnosed at a later stage of disease, similar to prior studies (Helpman et al., 2020).

Although not explored in this study, one potential biologic explanation of these findings could be higher rates of delay in care-seeking or treatment among those living at higher areas of deprivation, as has been shown in prior cancer studies, and leading to higher rates of negative prognostic tumor features and more advanced disease stage (Abu Zhaya et al., 2025; Graboyes et al., 2024; Moncayo et al., 2025). This may help to explain increased rates of LVSI, which has been associated with delays in care; however, endometrial tumor characteristics and stage of disease are likely to be impacted by tumor biology and microenvironment rather than being solely driven by prolonged time to receiving care (Mitric et al., 2020; Marcickiewicz et al., 2022; Abal et al., 2007). Prior studies have suggested that the connection between area deprivation and more aggressive tumor biology may be driven by higher levels of inflammatory stress and epigenetic changes in DNA methylation, as both have been shown to be associated with high neighborhood deprivation, and have been shown to be associated with aggressive tumor biology in other disease sites (Guerrero-Preston et al., 2019; Koster et al., 2006; Reuben et al., 2020 l Wing et al., 2023; Damiano et al., 2025). However, a causal pathway of this connection is yet to be determined. Further studies are warranted to explore these potential associations and their underlying biologic mechanism in endometrial cancer specifically.

Our findings add to the complexity of the literature on the impacts of neighborhood deprivation on endometrial cancer outcomes. Interestingly higher ADI was associated with decreased PFS in univariate and multivariable analysis when controlling for demographic characteristics and stage, but not when controlling for other tumor characteristics. As the tumor characteristics included in the analysis are known drivers of recurrence risk, the loss of association may partially be explained by differential rates of these negative prognostic tumor characteristics among those living at higher ADI, although these differences were not statistically significant (Morice et al., 2016). It also suggests that the association between neighborhood deprivation and PFS is driven in part by altered tumor biology among those living within a higher ADI group; however, no causal inferences can be drawn. Of note, tumor characteristics data is also incomplete, with a higher proportion of incomplete data for advanced stages, potentially biasing results and limiting the sample size in analysis.

Post hoc analysis demonstrated that the associations between area deprivation and PFS were driven by stage I disease. This aligns with prior work that demonstrates the greatest socioeconomic disparities among cancers with the highest survival rates and those most amenable to treatment, as there is more opportunity for intervention (Alcaraz et al., 2020; Tehranifar et al., 2009). Early-stage endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the endometrium is a curable pathology often requiring surgical intervention alone with recurrence risk stratification guiding the need for adjuvant therapy based on histopathology and molecular profile. This suggests that it is highly amenable to medical intervention. Therefore, differential access to medical therapy and receipt of appropriate surgical staging and use of adjuvant therapy when indicated has been suggested as a potential driver of disparities in outcomes along socioeconomic and racial lines (Gamble et al., 2024; Anastasio et al., 2025; Rodriguez et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2021). This finding is of interest in this study, as one of the study's strengths is the ability to compare a population across a large geographic space, but within a single network of providers thereby standardizing practice patterns and adherence to guideline-concordant care. This suggests that an alternative explanation other than care access or appropriateness of therapy decisions may underly the disparity. More nuanced differences in timeliness or quality of care between a tertiary cancer facility and a satellite site may still exist and this was controlled for in our analysis, albeit with smaller numbers of patients from satellite sites. It is also possible that an association between area deprivation and recurrence exists at more advanced stages, but the analysis is underpowered to understand the relationship given the significantly smaller sample size at advanced stages was less than 15% of our cohort.

We found that neighborhood deprivation was associated with OS only in univariate analysis, but not in multivariable analysis. Of the studies examining OS differences in endometrial cancer by ADI, one had findings consistent with ours and did not find a difference in OS, while the other two studies demonstrated differences in endometrial cancer-specific and all-cause mortality by neighborhood deprivation (Gottschlich et al., 2025; Gamble et al., 2024; Ackroyd et al., 2025). Possible explanations for these findings include differences in the underlying cohorts as the two cohort that found a difference in OS included high grade histologies, and non-endometrioid pathology, and had differences in care provision. Our study and the other study that did not demonstrate a difference in OS had standardized care across a single institution or network while the others were inclusive of a whole geographic region. Additionally, differences in how ADI is categorized, the choice of comparator group, and differences in data sources (e.g. registry data vs. chart abstraction) potentially bias results. There may be nuanced ways in which neighborhood deprivation has differential impacts on survival in endometrial cancer depending on tumor biology, integration into a care network, and geographic location that warrant further exploration.

Our study has several limitations. First, we are limited to endometrioid histology, precluding the understanding of ADI's impact on alternative histologies. We chose to limit the analysis to endometrioid pathology as it is the predominant pathology, the most amenable to treatment, and, therefore, the most likely to demonstrate a disparity in outcomes if one were to exist.We were also limited in the molecular classification that was available for this cohort. MMR status was included in the analysis as it was documented in 84.1% of cases, but POLE and p53 were excluded from the analysis as there was no POLE documentation and only 31.6% of cases included p53 classification. Additionally, the cohort is comprised of majority stage I disease, limiting conclusion in advanced stages, particularly stage IV. The analysis was not controlled for treatment course or adjuvant therapy utilization, which is consistent with other works in this space, but limits conclusions about the interplay of neighborhood deprivation and care utilization.

Another potential limitation of this study is the racial homogeny of the cohort. Our cohort was 96.3% White, in part due to limiting histology to endometrioid given the increased prevalence of high-grade histology in non-White populations, and due to the underlying racial make-up of the healthcare network's catchment area which is largely seated in western Pennsylvania. Although in 2020 the overall population of Pennsylvania was 75.6% White, many of the counties in Western PA are predominantly White, composing up to 95.8% of the population (Census Bureau, n.d). Prior studies have suggested an interaction between race and neighborhood deprivation and endometrial cancer outcomes, but the results have shown varied outcomes. Within a Detroit cohort area deprivation attenuated the relationship between race and survival, whereas among those with advanced stage disease a New York City cohort demonstrated no improvement in 5-year survival with increased neighborhood affluence among the Black population compared to significant improved survival among the White population (Gottschlich et al., 2025; Gamble et al., 2024).

Understanding the potential interaction between race and area deprivation in this cohort is challenging given the overall small non-White population, and the particularly small non-White population living within a lower ADI. Given this limitation, the post-hoc analysis examining the impact of ADI by racial subgroups is underpowered, and the results should be interpreted cautiously. The post-hoc analysis failed to demonstrate a statistically significant difference in PFS and OS among the non-White subgroup, and, while it did find a statistically significant difference in PFS and OS among for the White population in univariate analysis, this may be driven by type II error given the small population rather than a true lack of association.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the relationship between neighborhood deprivation and endometrial cancer clinicopathological features and outcomes is complex and not fully understood. Further investigation is warranted to understand the interplay between neighborhood deprivation and care access, as well as consideration of biological drivers of potential molecular and pathological associations to identify causal pathways of disparities and identified targetable solutions.
