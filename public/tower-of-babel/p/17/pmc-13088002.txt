Cooperative Roles of Pds5a and Pds5b Constrain Long‐Range Chromatin Interactions in Vertebrate Embryos
Authors: Shinra Ikeda, Hiroyuki Takeda, Ryohei Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThree‐dimensional (3D) genome organization is dynamically restructured during early vertebrate development, yet how chromatin domains are established remains poorly understood. In particular, the contribution of individual cohesin regulators to this process during embryogenesis is unclear. PDS5 proteins are key modulators of cohesin dynamics, but their depletion has been reported to cause context‐dependent and sometimes contrasting architectural effects in cultured cells. Here, we investigated the roles of the cohesin regulators Pds5a and Pds5b during early development using the medaka embryo. Developmental transcriptome analysis revealed distinct but overlapping expression dynamics of pds5a and pds5b around the transition from zygotic genome activation to gastrulation. Morpholino‐mediated depletion of either paralog resulted in only mild morphological phenotypes, whereas simultaneous depletion caused more severe developmental defects. In situ Hi‐C analysis showed that single depletion of pds5a or pds5b induced only modest changes in 3D genome organization. In contrast, double depletion led to pronounced architectural alterations, including increased long‐range chromatin contacts and de novo formation of extended chromatin loops. Transcriptome analysis revealed largely shared, with some condition‐specific, gene expression changes in both single‐ and double‐knockdown embryos, indicating that transcriptional effects can occur even in the absence of major architectural disruption. Together, our findings demonstrate that Pds5a and Pds5b act cooperatively to constrain cohesin‐mediated long‐range interactions during embryogenesis and highlight the importance of analyzing cohesin regulator function within a developmental context to understand how 3D genome organization is established in vivo.Keywords: 3D genome organization, cohesin, development, medaka, Pds5

Introduction

The three‐dimensional (3D) organization of the genome is a crucial regulator of gene expression during early vertebrate development (Bonev and Cavalli 2016; Spielmann et al. 2018). Chromatin architecture undergoes dramatic remodeling during the transition from maternal to zygotic control, beginning at zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and continuing through subsequent developmental stages (Zhang and Xie 2022; Ing‐Simmons et al. 2022). Topologically associating domains (TADs) are self‐interacting genomic regions enriched for internal chromatin contacts, often demarcated by CTCF (CCCTC‐binding factor)‐anchored chromatin loops that constrain regulatory interactions (Dixon et al. 2012; Nora et al. 2012; Rao et al. 2014). In many vertebrates, including teleosts, TADs are largely absent during the earliest cleavage stages and are not fully apparent at ZGA. Instead, TAD organization emerges progressively and becomes pronounced during gastrulation, following extensive large‐scale chromatin reorganization (Nakamura et al. 2021). The mechanisms that drive this de novo emergence and maturation of TADs remain poorly understood, and it is unclear how individual components of the cohesin machinery contribute to this developmental restructuring.

Current models propose that TADs and chromatin loops are generated through cohesin‐mediated loop extrusion (van Ruiten and Rowland 2018; Davidson et al. 2019; Fudenberg et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2019; Rao et al. 2017). In this model, cohesin complexes are loaded onto chromatin by the cohesin loader NIPBL (Nipped‐B‐like protein), translocate along DNA to extrude loops, and are eventually released through unloading mediated by WAPL (Wings apart‐like protein). Loop extrusion is further constrained by boundary elements, such as convergently oriented CTCF sites, which limit cohesin progression and define domain borders. Proper 3D genome organization therefore depends on a dynamic balance between cohesin loading, processive loop extrusion, boundary recognition, and timely unloading (Figure 1A). While this framework has been extensively characterized in cultured cells, how these processes are coordinated during early embryogenesis—when chromatin architecture is being established de novo rather than maintained—remains largely unknown.

Cohesin's residence time, activity, and turnover on chromatin are tightly regulated by multiple cofactors, among which the PDS5 (precocious dissociation of sisters 5) family plays an important role. Vertebrates possess two PDS5 paralogs, Pds5a and Pds5b, which are large HEAT‐repeat‐containing proteins with a conserved N‐terminal region that mediates interactions with RAD21 (RAD21 cohesin complex component), WAPL, and Sororin (CDCA5), and a more divergent C‐terminal region thought to confer paralog‐specific functions (Neuwald and Hirano 2000; Sumara et al. 2000). Consistent with partial redundancy but essential non‐overlapping functions, knockout of either Pds5a or Pds5b in mice results in embryonic lethality at different developmental stages (Carretero et al. 2013). Biochemical and cell‐based studies have shown that PDS5 participates in cohesin stabilization by promoting SMC3 (structural maintenance of chromosomes protein 3) acetylation through ESCO1/2 (establishment of sister chromatid cohesion N‐acetyltransferases 1 and 2) and facilitating Sororin binding, thereby antagonizing cohesin release (Chan et al. 2013; Minamino et al. 2015). Conversely, PDS5 can also bind RAD21 and recruit WAPL through a region that competes with Sororin, promoting opening of the cohesin ring and unloading from chromatin (Nishiyama et al. 2010; Chan et al. 2012; Muir et al. 2016). Through these interactions, PDS5 proteins are thought to fine‐tune cohesin dynamics by balancing cohesin residence, loop extrusion, and timely release from chromatin. Consistent with this regulatory role, depletion of PDS5 has been reported to produce divergent effects on chromatin architecture across experimental systems, including reduced loop and TAD strength or, alternatively, enlarged chromatin loops, highlighting a strong dependence of PDS5 function on cellular and regulatory context (Wutz et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2022; van Ruiten et al. 2022). Despite extensive studies in cell culture and mammalian models, the roles of Pds5 paralogs in regulating cohesin loading, unloading, and loop extrusion during early embryogenesis—particularly during the developmental window when TADs are progressively formed and refined—remain largely unexplored.

Beyond their architectural functions, Pds5 proteins have also been implicated in the regulation of gene expression (Arruda et al. 2022; Bsteh et al. 2023). Mouse genetic studies suggest that loss of Pds5a or Pds5b leads to tissue‐specific defects, and partial redundancy masks the severity of single‐gene disruptions (Carretero et al. 2013). However, the mechanisms by which Pds5 influences transcription remain poorly understood. It remains unclear whether the transcriptional effects arise from changes in chromatin architecture, altered cohesin dynamics, or additional cohesin‐independent functions.

Teleost embryos provide a unique system for dissecting these processes. In medaka ( Oryzias latipes ), the major establishment of TAD organization occurs predominantly during gastrulation, well after ZGA (Nakamura et al. 2021). This contrasts with mouse embryos, in which ZGA and TAD formation occur at much earlier stages with limited cell numbers (Du et al. 2017; Ke et al. 2017). The delayed establishment of TADs in medaka enables genetic perturbation and chromatin analysis during a developmental window with abundant material, making this system particularly advantageous for in vivo studies of 3D genome organization.

In this study, we used morpholino‐mediated knockdown of Pds5a and Pds5b in early medaka embryos, followed by Hi‐C and RNA‐seq, to examine the consequences of depleting these cohesin regulators in medaka embryos in which chromatin domains are actively established and refined. By comparing single and double knockdowns, we aimed to elucidate both shared and paralog‐specific contributions of Pds5a and Pds5b to chromatin architecture and developmental gene regulation. Our findings provide novel insights into how cohesin regulatory factors orchestrate genome organization during early vertebrate development.

Results

Developmental Expression Dynamics of Cohesin and Cohesin Regulators During Early Embryogenesis

To characterize the developmental expression dynamics of cohesin components and their regulators, we reanalyzed our previously published RNA‐seq dataset obtained from normally developing medaka embryos at stages 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 21, and 27 (Nakamura et al. 2021). Genes encoding core cohesin subunits and known cohesin regulators were examined, and their relative expression levels across developmental stages were visualized using a heatmap (Figure 1B). This analysis revealed pronounced stage‐dependent changes in the expression states of multiple cohesin‐related genes. Notably, many genes showed coordinated shifts in relative expression around the developmental window corresponding to the transition between zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and gastrulation, a period that coincides with the emergence of TADs during embryogenesis. Both core cohesin components and cohesin regulators exhibited developmentally dynamic expression patterns.

Among these regulators, pds5a and pds5b showed clearly distinct developmental expression dynamics. To further examine the expression dynamics of pds5a and pds5b, their normalized expression values (RPKM) were plotted across stages (Figure 1C). This analysis demonstrated that pds5a expression increases after ZGA and peaks around gastrulation, whereas pds5b expression is highest at early stages and progressively decreases toward gastrulation, while remaining detectable throughout later stages. The complementary but overlapping expression profiles of pds5a and pds5b around the ZGA‐gastrulation transition suggest a developmental switch in the relative contribution of these paralogs, while also indicating that both genes are expressed during this critical period of TAD formation. To further examine their spatial expression patterns, we performed whole‐mount in situ hybridization at the neurula stage (stage 18). Both pds5a and pds5b transcripts were broadly detected throughout the embryo without obvious tissue‐specific restriction (Figure 1D), indicating that the two paralogs are widely co‐expressed ubiquitously at this stage. Based on these expression dynamics, we selected pds5a and pds5b for subsequent functional analyses to investigate their roles in embryonic development and chromatin organization.

Morpholino‐Mediated Knockdown of Pds5a and Pds5b Causes Developmental Defects

To investigate the roles of pds5a and pds5b during early medaka embryogenesis, we performed morpholino (MO)‐mediated knockdown of pds5a, pds5b, and both genes simultaneously. MOs were microinjected into embryos at the one‐cell stage, and embryos were allowed to develop for 24 h post‐fertilization (hpf), corresponding to approximately stage 18 (late neurula) under normal conditions, when TAD organization has progressed to a near‐mature state following extensive chromatin reorganization during gastrulation (Nakamura et al. 2021).

Phenotypic analysis revealed distinct developmental outcomes depending on the knockdown condition. Upon single knockdown of either pds5a or pds5b, the majority of injected embryos developed normally and reached the late neurula stage without overt morphological abnormalities (53% for pds5a and 64% for pds5b; Figure 1D,E). A subset of embryos reached the neurula stage but exhibited abnormal morphology and/or localized cell death, while an additional fraction showed delayed development or arrested during gastrulation and earlier stages (Figure 1D,E). In contrast, simultaneous knockdown of pds5a and pds5b resulted in a marked increase in developmental defects. Under this condition, only approximately 30% of embryos developed normally, whereas the majority displayed either abnormal neurula‐stage phenotypes or delayed development, including gastrulation‐stage arrest or embryonic lethality (Figure 1D,E).

To assess whether MO‐mediated depletion induced gross nuclear structural abnormalities or defects in cell division, nuclei were visualized by DAPI staining followed by confocal microscopy (Figure 1F). This analysis did not reveal significant differences between control and MO‐injected embryos (Figure 1F), indicating that Pds5 depletion does not cause detectable widespread nuclear structural alterations or mitosis defects at this stage.

Together, these results indicate that pds5a and pds5b function redundantly to support normal embryonic development in medaka and that simultaneous depletion of both paralogs leads to severe defects during early embryogenesis.

Distinct Effects of Pds5a and Pds5b Depletion on Chromatin Interactions and Loop Organization

To examine the effects of Pds5 depletion on higher order chromatin organization, we performed Hi‐C analysis on control, pds5a‐, pds5b‐, and double‐knockdown embryos at the late neurula stage (stage 18). Genome‐wide contact maps were generated at comparable resolution and sequencing depth for quantitative comparison (Table S1). At the level of Hi‐C contact maps, single knockdown of either pds5a or pds5b did not produce overt global changes in chromatin organization relative to control embryos (Figure 2A). In contrast, double‐knockdown embryos displayed strengthened chromatin loop signals at a number of genomic loci, including interactions spanning several hundred kilobases (Figure 2A, arrows). The marked difference between single‐ and double‐knockdown conditions, despite the lower concentration used for each morpholino in the double knockdown, supports the interpretation that this architectural phenotype reflects cooperative depletion of the two Pds5 paralogs rather than a nonspecific effect of morpholino injection. To examine whether these architectural differences persist during further development, we also generated Hi‐C maps from control and double‐knockdown embryos at a later developmental stage (stage 21). The enhanced long‐range interaction patterns observed in double‐knockdown embryos were maintained and appeared even more pronounced at this stage relative to controls (Figure 2B). These observations indicate that the strengthened loop signals represent a robust architectural feature associated with simultaneous depletion of Pds5a and Pds5b.

To further characterize these architectural changes at the genome‐wide level, we examined contact probability as a function of genomic distance. Both single knockdowns exhibited reduced contact frequencies at short genomic distances (up to several hundred kilobases). In contrast, double‐knockdown embryos showed a marked increase in long‐range chromatin contacts, particularly within the range of several hundred kilobases to approximately 2 Mb (Figure 3A, arrow).

At the TAD scale, canonical TAD organization was largely preserved across all conditions (Figures 2A and 3B). Neither pds5a nor pds5b single knockdown resulted in substantial changes in TAD size or positioning (Figure 2A). Quantitative analysis revealed that TAD boundary strength was modestly reduced in pds5a‐knockdown embryos (Figure 3B, arrow), whereas no significant boundary weakening was detected in pds5b‐knockdown or double‐knockdown embryos (Figure 3B).

A striking feature specific to the double‐knockdown condition was the appearance of novel long‐range chromatin loops that were otherwise absent or extremely weak in control and single‐knockdown embryos. While TADs of typical size appeared largely unchanged, Hi‐C maps from double‐knockdown embryos revealed additional loop structures spanning extended genomic distances, often exceeding 400 kb (Figure 2A, arrows). When loop strength was stratified by loop size, small loops showed little or no change, whereas loops larger than ~400 kb displayed a pronounced increase in interaction strength specifically in double‐knockdown embryos (Figure 3C arrows, and 3D). Moreover, these newly emerging long‐range loops were frequently accompanied by enhanced stripe‐like interaction signals extending from loop anchors, a feature not observed in control or single‐knockdown conditions (Figure 2A, triangles). The concomitant appearance of novel long‐distance loops and strengthened stripe signals indicates a substantial reorganization of chromatin interactions unique to the loss of both Pds5 paralogs.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that simultaneous depletion of Pds5a and Pds5b leads to the de novo formation of long‐range chromatin loops, in addition to strengthening of pre‐existing long‐range loops, without gross disruption of canonical TAD organization. This phenotype contrasts sharply with the relatively mild effects observed in single knockdowns and highlights a cooperative role of Pds5 proteins in constraining long‐range chromatin interactions during embryonic development.

Transcriptional Changes in Pds5‐Depleted Embryos at the Neurula Stage

To characterize transcriptional changes associated with Pds5 depletion, we performed RNA sequencing of control, pds5a‐knockdown, pds5b‐knockdown, and double‐knockdown embryos at the late neurula stage (stage 18). Differential expression analysis identified 1256 upregulated and 1617 downregulated genes in pds5a‐knockdown embryos and 2159 upregulated and 2426 downregulated genes in pds5b‐knockdown embryos (adjusted p < 0.05) (Figure 4A). Double‐knockdown embryos exhibited a comparable overall number of transcriptional changes to those observed in single‐knockdown embryos, with 1293 upregulated and 1520 downregulated genes detected relative to controls (Figure 4A). Comparison of differentially expressed genes revealed both shared and paralog‐specific expression changes among the three knockdown conditions. A large subset of genes was commonly affected in all knockdowns, whereas other genes showed selective sensitivity to pds5a or pds5b depletion (Figure 4B).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed distinct patterns between downregulated and upregulated gene sets (Figure 4C). Downregulated DEGs were largely shared among the three knockdown conditions and were consistently enriched for terms related to developmental processes, indicating a common transcriptional response to Pds5 depletion. In contrast, upregulated DEGs showed more condition‐specific enrichment patterns. In pds5a‐knockdown embryos, upregulated genes were enriched for transport‐related terms, including genes such as apol1, pltp, and npc1. In pds5b‐knockdown embryos, enrichment was observed for RNA processing‐related terms, with representative genes including sf3b4, sart1, and dhx8. Notably, double‐knockdown embryos exhibited enrichment of upregulated genes associated with gene regulation‐related terms (e.g., mettl16, taf1c, gtf2b). Together, these analyses suggest that downregulation of developmental genes represents a shared transcriptional outcome of Pds5 depletion, whereas upregulated gene profiles vary among knockdown conditions. The observed transcriptional changes at the neurula stage likely reflect a combination of direct and indirect effects on embryonic development.

To further examine the potential transcriptional consequences of the architectural changes observed in pds5a/5b double‐knockdown embryos, we analyzed genes located near chromatin long‐range loops that become strengthened in the double‐knockdown condition. Because loop signals were more robust at later stages, loops were identified from the stage 21 double‐knockdown Hi‐C dataset and used to assess loop strength changes at the neurula stage (stage 18). In double‐knockdown embryos, genes located near strengthened long‐range loops (Table S2) showed a modest upward shift in expression compared with genes associated with smaller loops (Figure 4D). This tendency was not apparent in either pds5a or pds5b single‐knockdown datasets. However, the difference in expression between gene groups remained modest and did not reach statistical significance in two‐sided testing (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.09). These results suggest that although extended chromatin loops represent a prominent architectural feature of double‐knockdown embryos, their impact on nearby gene expression is limited or may be masked by secondary effects associated with developmental perturbation.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that the cohesin regulators Pds5a and Pds5b act cooperatively to constrain excessive long‐range chromatin interactions during early vertebrate development. In medaka embryos, single depletion of either paralog has only modest architectural effects, whereas simultaneous depletion induces extended chromatin loops and enhanced architectural stripes without gross disruption of canonical TAD structures.

PDS5 proteins are conserved regulators of cohesin, yet their roles in shaping 3D genome organization are not fully clear. Studies in mammalian cell lines have established that PDS5A and PDS5B modulate cohesin dynamics, but the architectural consequences of their depletion vary substantially across systems, suggesting strong context dependence. In HeLa cells, simultaneous depletion of PDS5A and PDS5B reduces the loop number, loop strength, and TAD insulation without affecting loop length, leading to the proposal that PDS5 stabilizes loop structures (Wutz et al. 2017). Degron‐based studies in liver cancer cells refined this view by demonstrating that single depletion reduces the loop number while increasing the loop size, whereas co‐depletion alters cohesin composition at loop anchors and increases overall chromatin‐bound cohesin, indicating that PDS5 influences cohesin regulation beyond simply determining the amount of cohesin bound to chromatin (Yu et al. 2022). In contrast, studies in HAP1 cells identified a dominant role for PDS5A, where loss of PDS5A—but not PDS5B—led to extended chromatin loops and elongated architectural stripes, suggesting that PDS5A restricts excessive loop enlargement (van Ruiten et al. 2022). Differences in paralog abundance were proposed as a contributing factor to this asymmetry. Taken together, these studies converge on the idea that PDS5 proteins fine‐tune cohesin dynamics, while also demonstrating that the architectural outcomes of PDS5 loss vary widely—from weakened loops and domains to enlarged loops and extended stripes—depending on cellular context.

Our findings in the context of a developing vertebrate embryo provide a distinct perspective. pds5a expression peaks during gastrulation, and its depletion leads to moderate weakening of TAD boundaries and chromatin loops. In contrast, pds5b is more highly expressed prior to gastrulation and gradually decreases thereafter, and its depletion alone has little impact on global 3D genome organization. Notably, simultaneous depletion of Pds5a and Pds5b reveals a mode of cohesin regulation that promotes extended chromatin interactions, including long‐range loops that are not prominent upon single‐paralog depletion. This phenotype aligns more closely with reports of loop enlargement and stripe extension than with models in which PDS5 primarily stabilizes loops and domains. Our results could thus point to a mode of cohesin regulation that is specific to developmental contexts in which TADs are newly formed after extensive chromatin reorganization.

Previous studies have suggested that PDS5 proteins exert dual functions in cohesin‐mediated chromatin looping. On one hand, PDS5 interacts with WAPL to limit loop extension by promoting cohesin release, thereby constraining excessive loop extrusion. On the other hand, PDS5 has been implicated in stabilizing chromatin loops, contributing to the maintenance of loop anchors and domain structure. In the developmental system examined here, our findings are consistent with a model in which these functions are partially buffered by the presence of either Pds5 paralog. When one paralog is present, Pds5‐mediated suppression of loop extension can be largely compensated, whereas loss of a single paralog preferentially affects the stabilizing role of Pds5, resulting in moderate weakening of TAD boundaries and loops. In contrast, simultaneous depletion of both Pds5a and Pds5b abolishes this compensatory regulation, leading to unrestrained cohesin‐mediated loop extension and the emergence of extended long‐range chromatin interactions.

A key feature of early embryos compared with many cultured‐cell systems is the developmentally regulated and overlapping expression of pds5a and pds5b during the window when chromatin domains are actively established. Differences in the abundance and balance of cohesin regulators such as WAPL and NIPBL may contribute to these context‐dependent effects. In line with this possibility, our expression data revealed dynamic changes in the expression of wapl and nipbl around the ZGA‐gastrulation transition. Dynamic regulation of these cohesin regulators has also been reported in mouse embryos and implicated in TAD establishment, despite differences in the direction and timing of expression changes (Yu et al. 2025). Together, our findings extend existing cell‐based models and highlight the importance of analyzing cohesin regulator function within a developmental context to fully understand how 3D genome organization is shaped in vivo.

Our transcriptome analysis of Pds5‐depleted embryos suggests a nuanced relationship between chromatin architecture and gene expression. Developmental genes were strongly affected even upon single depletion of either Pds5 paralog, despite only modest or limited changes in global 3D genome organization under these conditions. This observation raises the possibilities that a large fraction of transcriptional changes, particularly the downregulation of developmental genes, are independent of major alterations in chromatin looping and that they may instead reflect indirect consequences such as developmental delay. Double‐knockdown embryos exhibited condition‐specific transcriptional changes, particularly among upregulated genes, that were not observed in single knockdowns. Given that double depletion uniquely induces pronounced long‐range chromatin loops and extended interactions, these transcriptional changes may be linked to altered 3D genome organization.

Finally, it should be noted that the morpholino‐based approach used in this study results in depletion from the one‐cell stage onward, with phenotypic and transcriptional analyses performed at the neurula stage. Consequently, a substantial fraction of the observed transcriptional changes likely reflects indirect or secondary effects associated with altered developmental progression, including p53 activation reported in response to cohesin perturbation (Ghiselli 2006). To directly assess the role of PDS5 proteins in transcriptional regulation and to determine the causal relationship between enhanced loop extension and gene expression, future studies will require experimental strategies that enable acute and temporally controlled depletion of Pds5 during defined developmental windows.

Materials and Methods

Fish Strains and Embryo Collection

The medaka ( Oryzias latipes ) d‐rR wild‐type strain was used in this study. Fish were maintained and bred under standard laboratory conditions. Embryos were collected after natural spawning, dechorionated, and incubated at 28°C. Developmental stages were determined according to established morphological criteria as described previously (Iwamatsu 2004).

Injection of Morpholino Antisense Oligo (MO)

Translation‐blocking morpholino antisense oligonucleotides (MOs) targeting the start codon regions of pds5a and pds5b were obtained from Gene Tools (Philomath, OR, USA). The sequences of the MOs used in this study are listed in Table S3.

For single knockdown, 500 μM MO in water was injected into one‐cell‐stage (stage‐2) embryos. For pds5a/5b double knockdown, 200 μM pds5a MO and 200 μM pds5b MO were injected into one‐cell‐stage (stage‐2) embryos.

DAPI Staining

MO‐injected embryos were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS at 24 h post fertilization (hpf) stage. Fixed embryos were incubated at room temperature for 4 h and at 4°C overnight, washed with PBS, dechorionated in PBS, and stored at −4°C until use.

Fixed embryos were permeabilized with 1% Triton X‐100 in PBS, blocked with 2% BSA in PBSDT (PBS/1% DMSO/0.1% Triton X‐100), and stained with DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole). Stained embryos were mounted with 50% glycerin in PBS in a slide glass and were imaged by FV3000 IX83 (Olympus).

Generation of In Situ Hybridization Probe

Antisense RNA probes were generated from medaka d‐rR cDNA prepared from embryos at the 9‐somite stage (stage 22). pds5a and pds5b gene fragments were amplified from cDNA using gene‐specific cloning primers (Table S3) and PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase (Takara, R045A). PCR products were cloned into the pCR‐BluntII‐TOPO vector (Invitrogen, 450245). Templates for DIG‐labeled probe synthesis were generated by PCR from TOPO vectors using primers containing either an SP6 promoter (for sense probes) or a T7 promoter (for antisense probes) (Table S3), again using PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase. DIG‐labeled RNA probes were synthesized using SP6 RNA Polymerase (Roche, 10810274001) or T7 RNA Polymerase (Roche, 10881767001) in combination with DIG RNA Labeling Mix (Roche, 11277073910).

Whole‐Mount Embryo In Situ Hybridization

Embryos were fixed at 24 hpf with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (PBST). Fixed embryos were incubated at room temperature for 2 h and then at 4°C for two nights, dehydrated in methanol, and stored at −20°C. All solutions and reagents were RNase‐free. Fixed embryos were rehydrated through decreasing concentrations of methanol and finally in PBST. Embryos were washed three times with PBST and treated with 10 μg/mL proteinase K in PBST at 25°C for 7 min. Embryos were then refixed with 4% PFA in PBST at 25°C for 25 min and washed three times with PBST. For pre‐hybridization, embryos were incubated in hybridization buffer at 65°C for 2 h. The probe was diluted to 0.2 ng/μL in hybridization buffer and incubated with embryos overnight at 65°C. Embryos were washed with preheated wash buffer (50% formamide, 5× SSC, 0.1% Tween‐20) at 65°C for 10 min, twice with 2× SSCT at 65°C for 10 min, twice with 0.2× SSCT at 65°C for 30 min, and once with 0.1% Tween‐20 in MAB (MABT) at room temperature for 10 min. Embryos were then incubated in blocking buffer (5% sheep serum, 5% blocking reagent in MABT) for 1 h. Anti‐digoxigenin antibody was diluted 1:5000 in blocking buffer and incubated with embryos overnight at 4°C. After antibody incubation, embryos were washed six times with MABT at room temperature, and signals were developed using BM Purple. The reaction was stopped by washing three times with MABT followed by fixation with 4% PFA. Imaging of in situ hybridization signals was performed using an M165 FC stereomicroscope equipped with a DFC7000T digital camera (Leica).

In Situ Hi‐C

In situ Hi‐C experiments were performed as previously described (Rao et al. 2014) with some modifications. For all samples, two biological replicates were generated.

MO‐injected embryos were incubated at 28°C for 24 h, collected in 1× PBS, and dissociated by pushing through a micropipette. Dissociated embryos were collected by centrifuging at 500 g for 3 min and cross‐linked with 3% formaldehyde in 1.4 mL 1× PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Cross‐linking was quenched by adding 2.5 M glycine (125 mM final) and incubating for 5 min at room temperature, then 15 min on ice. Cells were pelleted at 500 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed and stored at −80°C.

Cells were thawed on ice, washed with PBS, and resuspended in 250 μL of ice‐cold Hi‐C lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% Igepal CA‐630) containing 50 μL of protease inhibitors (Sigma‐Aldrich, P8340) and incubated on ice for 20 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 2500 g for 5 min, washed once with ice‐cold Hi‐C lysis buffer, resuspended in 50 μL of 0.5% SDS, and incubated at 62°C for 10 min. After adding 145 μL of water and 25 μL of 10% Triton X‐100, samples were incubated at 37°C for 15 min.

Next, 25 μL of 10× NEBuffer 2 and 100 U of MboI were added, and chromatin was digested at 37°C for 2 h with rotation. MboI was inactivated by incubation at 62°C for 20 min. DNA ends were labeled with biotin by adding 50 μL of fill‐in master mix (37.5 μL of 0.4 mM biotin‐14‐dATP [Life Technologies], 1.5 μL each of 10 mM dCTP, dGTP, and dTTP, and 8 μL of 5 U/μL DNA Polymerase I Large Klenow Fragment [NEB]) and incubating at 37°C for 1.5 h with rotation.

Proximity ligation was performed by adding 900 μL of ligation master mix (669 μL of water, 120 μL of 10× NEB T4 DNA ligase buffer, 100 μL of 10% Triton X‐100, 6 μL of 10 mg/mL BSA, and 5 μL of 400 U/μL T4 DNA ligase [NEB]) and incubating at room temperature for 4 h. Samples were centrifuged at 2500 g for 5 min and the supernatant was removed. Proteins were digested by adding 12.5 μL of 20 mg/mL Proteinase K, 330 μL of 1% SDS, and 32.5 μL of 5 M NaCl and incubating at 55°C for 30 min. Cross‐links were reversed by incubation at 68°C overnight.

Biotinylated DNA was recovered by ethanol precipitation and resuspended in 130 μL of 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). DNA was sheared using a Covaris S220 with the following parameters: peak incident power, 140; duty factor, 10; cycles per burst, 200; and time, 80 s. The sheared DNA (100–500 bp) was size‐selected and purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and eluted in 300 μL of Tris buffer.

Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 beads (Life Technologies; 150 μL of 10 mg/mL) were washed with 400 μL of 1× Tween Washing Buffer (1× TWB: 5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20) and resuspended in 300 μL of 2× Binding Buffer (2× BB: 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl). The beads were mixed with 300 μL of DNA sample and incubated at room temperature for 15 min with rotation. Bead‐bound biotinylated DNA was collected with a magnet, the supernatant was discarded, and the beads were washed twice with 600 μL of 1× TWB. Beads were transferred to a new tube, incubated at 55°C for 2 min on a Thermomixer, and the supernatant was discarded, washed with 100 μL of Tris buffer, and resuspended in 50 μL of Tris buffer.

DNA was end‐repaired and A‐tailed by adding 7 μL of End Repair & A‐Tailing Buffer (KAPA Biosystems) and 3 μL of End Repair & A‐Tailing Enzyme (KAPA Biosystems) and incubating at 20°C for 30 min, followed by 65°C for 30 min. Indexed Illumina adapters (2.5 μL of 30 μM), 7.5 μL water, 30 μL ligation buffer (KAPA Biosystems), and 10 μL DNA ligase (KAPA Biosystems) were then added, and samples were incubated at 20°C for 15 min. Beads were washed twice with 600 μL of 1× TWB, transferred to a new tube, and washed with 100 μL of Tris buffer before being resuspended in 50 μL of Tris buffer.

Hi‐C libraries were amplified directly from the beads using 6–7 PCR cycles with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems) and purified with AMPure XP beads. Paired‐end sequencing of the Hi‐C libraries was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 1500 platform.

RNA‐seq

RNA‐seq experiments were performed as previously described (Nakamura et al. 2021) with some modifications. For all samples, two biological replicates were generated.

MO‐injected embryos were dechorionated at 1 h after MO injection. Twenty‐four hours post fertilization, embryos were homogenized in 1 mL of Isogen (Nippongene). Then, 200 μL of chloroform was added and total RNA was isolated using RNeasy MinElute Cleanup kit (Qiagen). Ribosomal RNA was removed using RiboMinus Eukaryote System v2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA‐seq libraries were generated using a KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit (KAPA Biosystems). Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 1500 platform.

Hi‐C Data Processing

The sequenced reads were mapped, filtered, and normalized using Juicer (version 1.5) (Durand et al. 2016). Medaka reference genome (ASM223467v1) was used for mapping. Normalized contact matrices at 10 kb resolution were used for representative views.

Balanced contact matrices were generated using cooler (Abdennur and Mirny 2020), and aggregate analyses of TADs, boundaries, and loops were performed using coolpuppy (Flyamer et al. 2020).

Contact probability as a function of genomic distance (P(s)) was calculated using cooltools (expected‐cis) (Abdennur et al. 2024). Expected contact frequencies were computed from unbalanced Hi‐C matrices at 10 kb resolution to avoid distortions due to balancing. P(s) curves were normalized by the total number of valid intra‐chromosomal contacts for each sample.

RNA‐seq Data Processing

RNA‐seq reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.32 (Bolger et al. 2014) and aligned to the medaka reference genome (ASM223467v1) using STAR (Dobin et al. 2013). Only reads with mapping quality ≥ 20 were used for downstream analyses. Gene‐level read counts were obtained, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Genes with an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Chromatin Loop Analysis and Gene Association

For analyses of loop strength across different genomic distances, a previously defined loop set identified in wild‐type medaka fibroblast cells was used (Nakamura et al. 2021). Loop strength at each condition was quantified by measuring observed‐over‐expected contact enrichment at loop centers using balanced expected values from cooltools expected‐cis, and loops were grouped according to genomic span.

To examine the relationship between loop strengthening and gene expression changes in pds5a/5b double‐knockdown embryos, chromatin loops were additionally identified from the stage 21 double‐knockdown Hi‐C dataset using cooltools call‐dots at 10 and 25 kb resolutions. Loop calls from the two resolutions were merged to obtain a unified loop set. Loop strength at the neurula stage (stage 18) was quantified as the observed‐over‐expected contact enrichment at loop centers, and the change in loop strength between double‐knockdown and control embryos was calculated as log2 fold change. Loops were classified as strengthened (log2FC ≥ 0.5).

Loop anchors were associated with genes based on proximity to transcription start sites (TSSs). Genes within 5 kb of loop anchors were considered loop‐proximal.
