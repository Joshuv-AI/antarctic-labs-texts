Optimal exercise modalities for post-intervention motor function changes in stroke survivors: a pairwise and network meta-analysis
Authors: Liqun Jiang, Huimin Ding, Hyun Seo, Buongo Chun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, particularly among older adults, and severely compromises functional independence and quality of life. Exercise interventions are well established as effective strategies for promoting motor recovery through improvements in neuroplasticity, fitness, strength, and psychosocial well-being, yet most evidence to date has focused on immediate post-intervention outcomes. The durability of these benefits during follow-up periods—defined as the post-intervention phase when patients are observed after completing structured rehabilitation and begin transitioning to self-directed activities—remains poorly understood. Clarifying functional trajectories during this phase is critical for assessing the sustainability and real-world value of rehabilitation strategies. A systematic evaluation of motor function outcomes beyond formal intervention is therefore urgently needed to inform the design of programs that prioritize long-term recovery.MethodsThis systematic review and network meta-analysis followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251117014). Five databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library) were searched up to March 13, 2025. We included randomized controlled trials comparing structured exercise interventions in post-stroke adults during follow-up periods, reporting motor function outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2.0, and evidence quality was graded via CINeMA. Pairwise and Bayesian network meta-analyses were conducted using netmeta and gemtc packages. Heterogeneity, inconsistency, regression, sensitivity, and publication bias analyses were also performed.ResultsSixty-four trials (n = 3074) were included, covering 23 exercise modalities. For the 6-min walk test, resistance training demonstrated the greatest effect [mean difference (MD) = 21.79, 95% CI 8.19–35.40], followed by robot-assisted therapy (MD = 19.78, 95% CI 3.30–36.27). In the 10-m walk test, robot-assisted therapy ranked highest (MD = 0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.12). Task-oriented training significantly improved balance [standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.35, 95% CI 0.07–0.64]. No significant effects were observed in lower or upper limb Fugl-Meyer outcomes. Consistency and heterogeneity were generally acceptable, and no publication bias was detected.ConclusionsResistance training and robot-assisted therapy appear most effective for enhancing walking capacity after stroke. Task-oriented training benefits postural balance. Evidence for upper and lower limb recovery remains inconclusive due to the small number of trials and wide confidence intervals. These findings can inform individualized, modality-specific rehabilitation strategies for older adults recovering from stroke.Trial registrationRegistered on PROSPERO (CRD420251117014).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01756-z.

Background

With the global population aging rapidly, the burden of age-related diseases is becoming increasingly prominent. It is estimated that by 2050, the number of individuals aged 60 years and older will reach 2.1 billion, effectively doubling from around 1.05 billion in 2020 and accounting for approximately 22% of the global population [1, 2].

Among the leading causes of disability in older adults, stroke represents a major threat to functional independence and quality of life. Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally and a major contributor to long-term disability in adults, significantly compromising quality of life and social participation among middle-aged and older individuals [3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 estimates, there were approximately 93.8 million people living with stroke worldwide and 11.9 million new cases in that year, making stroke the third leading cause of death (7.3 million deaths, 10.7% of all deaths) and the fourth leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (160.5 million DALYs, 5.6% of all DALYs) [4]. These substantial years of life lost are not only driven by mortality, but also by long-lasting impairments, among which motor dysfunction is the most prevalent and disabling consequence of stroke. The degree of motor function recovery following stroke is a critical determinant of a patient’s ability to live independently [5]. Improving motor function not only enhances self-care and daily activity performance but also reduces hospital readmission rates, ultimately contributing to a better overall quality of life.

A growing body of evidence supports exercise intervention as a cornerstone strategy for functional recovery after stroke, exerting beneficial effects on neuroplasticity, cardiopulmonary fitness, muscle strength, and psychosocial well-being [6]. Commonly employed modalities include aerobic training, resistance training, core stability exercises, virtual reality–based interventions, and task-oriented approaches [7–11]. Findings from systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that exercise interventions significantly improve key functional outcomes—such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT), and the Berg Balance Scale (BBS)—indicating substantial rehabilitative benefits across domains of motor control, gait performance, and balance function [8, 12–14].

However, existing studies have primarily focused on the immediate effects observed at the end of intervention, with limited systematic evaluation of functional changes during the post-intervention follow-up period. In reality, the evolution of motor function during follow-up offers a more accurate reflection of the durability and real-world translational value of rehabilitation strategies, carrying significant implications for long-term recovery management. Within the field of rehabilitation medicine, the follow-up phase is increasingly recognized as a critical window for assessing the sustainability and generalizability of treatment effects [15]. After discharge or the cessation of intensive rehabilitation, patients often undergo a transition from structured training to self-directed daily activities. The trajectory of motor function during this period is shaped by a complex interplay of factors, including delayed neurophysiological adaptations, mobilization of physiological reserves, lifestyle restructuring, variations in adherence to exercise, and the extent of familial and social support [16, 17].

Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have indicated that different exercise modalities may show varying degrees of functional retention during follow-up, systematic synthesis and direct comparisons remain scarce. Most previous meta-analyses have concentrated on immediate post-intervention outcomes, thereby overlooking whether the rehabilitative benefits of exercise persist once structured training ceases [18]. Yet, the follow-up phase provides a more accurate reflection of long-term recovery, encompassing delayed neurophysiological adaptations, lifestyle adjustments, and adherence challenges. In this context, evaluating the comparative durability of intervention effects across exercise modalities is essential for optimizing resource allocation, informing personalized rehabilitation planning, and enhancing the sustainability and real-world value of functional recovery. The absence of a comprehensive review on motor function trajectories during this phase therefore represents a critical evidence gap.

The present study addresses this gap by systematically reviewing and meta-analyzing RCTs that report motor function changes between the end of intervention and the follow-up period. To ensure both clinical relevance and comparability across trials, we focused on five outcomes that are widely recommended in stroke rehabilitation research and practice: the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for upper (FMA-UE) and lower extremities (FMA-LE), the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and two performance-based walking measures—the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) and the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT). These measures were selected because they capture the core domains of motor recovery—upper limb function, lower limb function, balance, and walking capacity—are frequently reported across RCTs, and have established validity, reliability, and responsiveness in stroke populations. By focusing on these standardized and clinically meaningful outcomes, the analysis provides a robust and comparable basis for evaluating the durability of exercise interventions during follow-up [19–23]. Using pairwise and Bayesian network meta-analyses, we compared the sustained effects of different exercise modalities across these outcomes. Through rigorous risk-of-bias and consistency assessments, this study aims to identify the exercise approaches most capable of delivering lasting benefits, thereby supporting a clinical shift from emphasizing immediate recovery to prioritizing long-term functional sustainability.

Materials and methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251117014) [24].

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in five electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library, with the search finalized on March 13, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) with free-text terms. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S2.1–S2.5.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were established according to the PICOS framework. Studies were included if they: (1) involved participants aged ≥ 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of stroke via CT or MRI; (2) implemented any structured exercise intervention; (3) compared an exercise intervention either against a non-exercise control group or against a different type of exercise modality. All abbreviations related to exercise interventions are detailed in Supplementary 1—Definitions of Exercise Training Interventions (Table S1)； (4) reported at least one motor function outcome (e.g., 6MWT, 10MWT, BBS, FMA-LE, or FMA-UE); and (5) were RCTs. (6) reported follow-up data, defined as outcome assessments conducted at the end of a post-intervention follow-up period, after the intervention had ceased. Exclusion criteria were: identical interventions in both groups, lack of follow-up data, non-original articles (e.g., reviews, case reports, conference abstracts), incomplete data or inaccessible full text, and duplicate publications.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the literature and extracted data using a double-check method. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Extracted data included study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention and control details, sample size, outcome measures, and effect estimates. For continuous outcomes, the primary effect size was the mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) in motor function change between the end of the intervention and the end of the follow-up period.

When standard deviations (SDs) were not reported, we estimated them using available 95% confidence intervals (CIs), p-values, or standard errors (SEs). Risk of bias and quality of evidence assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0), evaluating five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. A macro-enabled Excel tool was used to generate visual summaries of the assessments. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by a third independent assessor [28].

Quality of evidence was evaluated using the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) platform, following the GRADE framework. Ratings were based on six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Evidence was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low, with downgrading applied in the presence of serious limitations [29].

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

Direct comparisons were analyzed using the netmeta package (version 3.2-0) in R to construct network models and extract direct evidence via league tables. Mean differences (MDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each pairwise comparison. Direction and statistical significance of effects were visualized using color-coded plots [30].

Network meta-analysis

Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using the gemtc package (based on JAGS). When effect sizes needed to be standardized, mean differences (MDs) were converted into standardized mean differences (SMDs) in Stata to account for variations in outcome measurement scales. Multi-arm trials were appropriately split and adjusted to contribute to the network structure. The Bayesian framework was applied to generate network evidence plots, perform the global inconsistency test, estimate the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values, and produce SUCRA-based ranking heatmaps, which visually represent the probability of each intervention being the most effective across outcomes. Local inconsistency was assessed through node-splitting, and heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic [31–33]. This approach was chosen because it provides a coherent probabilistic framework for treatment ranking and directly quantifies the uncertainty within intervention hierarchies.

Frequentist network meta-analysis was conducted using the netmeta package. This framework was employed to generate league tables, which summarize all pairwise comparisons between interventions in a single matrix, and corresponding league table heat plots to visually display the relative magnitude of effects among interventions. Publication bias was examined through funnel plots and Egger’s regression test [30, 34–36]. In addition, a network meta-regression was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, incorporating covariates such as mean age, intervention cycle, follow-up duration, gross domestic product (GDP, PPP-adjusted per capita), and sample size. The frequentist approach was selected because it provides straightforward estimation procedures, produces interpretable comparative outputs, and complements the Bayesian framework by enabling cross-validation of results.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach, in which each study was sequentially excluded from the network [37].

Results

A total of 45,995 records were identified, of which 27,114 remained after removing duplicates; 26,694 were excluded after screening, leaving 420 full texts for assessment. Ultimately, 64 RCTs involving 3074 participants were included (Fig. 1). These trials were conducted across Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Oceania, covering high-, middle-, and low-income settings. Participants were adults with stroke, mean age about 60 years (trial-level range mid-40 s to mid-70 s), with men slightly overrepresented where sex was reported. Most cohorts included patients with mild to moderate deficits, though some enrolled moderate-to-severe cases, and both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke were represented; subacute and chronic stages predominated, with relatively few acute cases. Interventions generally lasted 6–12 weeks, delivered two to three times per week for 40–60 min, though some were shorter, intensive inpatient programs (2–4 weeks) and others extended beyond 6 months. Follow-up periods varied widely, ranging from 2 weeks to 48 months, most commonly between 4–12 weeks or 3–6 months. Training was delivered in inpatient, outpatient, or home/telerehabilitation settings, with intensity prescribed by %HRR/%HRmax, %1RM, treadmill speed, or perceived exertion, and most trials incorporated progressive load increases. In total, 23 distinct exercise modalities were evaluated, including aerobic exercise (AE), resistance training (RT), body weight-supported treadmill training (BWSTT), and task-oriented training (TOT), among others. Primary outcomes were the 6MWT, 10MWT, BBS, FMA-LE, and FMA-UE, synthesized as SMDs due to heterogeneity in versions and scoring ranges. Primary outcomes were the 6MWT, 10MWT, BBS, FMA-LE, and FMA-UE. Risk of bias and quality of evidence (CINeMA assessment)

Risk of bias assessment

All 64 included RCTs were assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool across five domains. The majority of studies showed low risk in the randomization process and completeness of outcome data. Overall, 6 studies (9.4%) were rated as having a low risk of bias. However, due to the inherent challenges of blinding in exercise interventions, over 90% of studies were judged as having “some concerns” in domains related to intervention implementation or outcome measurement. A few studies (e.g., Study 235 and Study 384) were rated as having a “high risk” of bias. Additionally, several trials lacked protocol registration or showed signs of selective reporting, raising concerns about potential systematic bias. Full assessment details are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Quality of evidence assessment (CINeMA assessment)

According to the CINeMA system, most network comparisons were rated as having moderate to very low quality of evidence, with only a few comparisons—such as BWSTT versus RC—reaching high-quality ratings. The most common reasons for downgrading included within-study bias, imprecision, and indirectness. High-quality evidence was particularly scarce for functional outcomes such as BBS and FMA. Overall, these findings highlight ongoing methodological limitations and inconsistencies in the current body of stroke rehabilitation trials involving exercise interventions. Detailed ratings are presented in Supplementary Table S6.1–S6.5.

Results of pairwise and network meta-analyses

The network plot for the 6MWT is shown in Supplementary Figure S10.1. Pairwise meta-analysis indicated a significant positive effect of robotic-assisted training (RAT) compared to routine care (RC) (MD = 19.97, 95% CI 2.47–37.48). In the network meta-analysis, RT showed the most pronounced improvement (MD = 21.79, 95% CI 8.19–35.40), ranking highest in SUCRA values (Supplementary Figure S12.1), followed by RAT (MD = 19.78, 95% CI 3.30–36.27), both showing statistically significant differences (Supplementary Figure S11.1).

For the 10MWT, the network plot is presented in Supplementary Figure S10.2. Pairwise analysis showed that core stability training (CST) had a significant positive effect (MD = 0.12, 95% CI 0.01–0.24). In the network analysis, RAT again demonstrated the greatest improvement (MD = 0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.12), with the highest SUCRA ranking (Supplementary Figure S12.2) and statistical significance (Supplementary Figure S11.2).

The network diagram for the BBS is shown in Supplementary Figure S10.3. Pairwise comparisons indicated a significant difference between no intervention and RC. In the network analysis, BWSTT had the highest SUCRA ranking (Supplementary Figure S12.3), although its effect compared to RC was not statistically significant (SMD = 0.23, 95% CI − 0.12 to 0.57). TOT, ranked second, demonstrated a significant improvement over RC (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI 0.07–0.64) (Supplementary Figure S11.3).

For the lower extremity subscale of the FMA-LE, the network diagram is shown in Supplementary Figure S10.4. Pairwise analysis indicated a significant difference between no intervention and RC. In the network analysis, mind–body exercise (MBE) achieved the highest SUCRA ranking (Supplementary Figure S12.4), but the comparison with RC was not statistically significant (SMD = 0.06, 95% CI − 0.55 to 0.67). BWSTT ranked second, though the wide confidence interval (SMD = 0.04, 95% CI − 0.85 to 0.92) indicates substantial uncertainty regarding its effect (Supplementary Figure S11.4). Given the relatively small number of eligible studies, these findings should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no benefit.

For the upper extremity subscale of the FMA-UE, the network diagram is shown in Supplementary Figure S10.5. Pairwise analysis revealed significant differences between no intervention and RC. In the network analysis, electrical stimulation plus exercise (ESX) ranked highest in SUCRA (Supplementary Figure S12.5), but its effect compared to RC was imprecise and not statistically significant (SMD = 0.13, 95% CI − 0.87 to 1.13). The second-ranked intervention, bilateral virtual reality and gaming (BVRG), also showed a wide confidence interval (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI − 0.55 to 1.53), underscoring the uncertainty of the estimate (Supplementary Figure S11.5). As with FMA-LE, the limited number of trials precludes firm conclusions and highlights the need for further research on limb-specific recovery.

Consistency and heterogeneity assessments

Global consistency analyses indicated that, with the exception of the 10MWT outcome, the deviance information criterion (DIC) values for consistency models were either lower than or comparable to those of inconsistency models across all outcomes (DIC differences ranged from 0.01 to 0.07), suggesting good model fit and an overall coherent network structure (Supplementary Table S7). Node-splitting analyses further supported local consistency, as most comparisons showed no significant difference between direct and indirect estimates (P > 0.05), such as AE vs. HIIT, FT vs. RC, and MBE vs. NPF. Minor inconsistencies were observed for a few comparisons within the BBS outcome—specifically FT vs. MBE and FT vs. NE (P < 0.01)—but these had limited impact on the overall network integrity (Supplementary Tables S8.1–S8.5).

Regarding heterogeneity, global I2 values were low across all outcomes, indicating minimal between-study variability (e.g., 6MWT: 0%, 10MWT: 0%, FMA-UE: 0%, FMA-LE: 0%, BBS: 35%). Local heterogeneity assessments also revealed generally small or acceptable I2 values for most pairwise comparisons. However, certain comparisons—such as FT vs. NE, MBE vs. NPF, and RC vs. TOT—exhibited high heterogeneity (I2 > 70%), warranting cautious interpretation (Supplementary Tables S9.1–S9.5).

Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression analyses were conducted for three key outcomes: 6MWT, 10MWT, and BBS. Covariates included mean participant age, intervention duration, follow-up period, national income level, and sample size (Supplementary Table S14). None of the covariates showed statistically significant associations with effect sizes across outcomes (all P-values > 0.18), suggesting that intervention effects were not systematically moderated by these study-level characteristics. This indicates that heterogeneity may primarily stem from differences in intervention content or outcome measurement tools.

To test the robustness of our findings, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed across all included studies. The pooled effect estimates and confidence intervals for primary outcomes (6MWT, 10MWT, BBS, FMA-UE, FMA-LE) remained stable upon exclusion of any single study, with no substantial fluctuations observed. These results confirm the overall reliability and robustness of the meta-analytic conclusions (Supplementary Figures S17.1–S17.15).

Publication bias

Egger’s regression test was used to assess the risk of publication bias for each primary outcome. The results revealed no statistically significant evidence of small-study effects or publication bias for 6MWT, 10MWT, BBS, FMA-UE, or FMA-LE (all P-values > 0.05), indicating no apparent risk of bias related to selective reporting (Supplementary Tables S13.1–S13.5).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study systematically evaluated the follow-up effects of 23 exercise interventions in stroke survivors, based on 64 randomized controlled trials. Through both pairwise and network meta-analyses, we found that certain interventions were able to maintain—or even extend—the functional gains achieved during the intervention phase. Specifically, RT demonstrated the most sustained benefit in the 6 MWT, while RAT showed the greatest improvement in the 10 MWT during follow-up. For the BBS, although BWSTT ranked highest in SUCRA values, its effect did not reach statistical significance. Regarding the FMA-UE/LE, most interventions failed to show significant improvements during the follow-up phase, indicating ongoing challenges in achieving long-term functional maintenance. Importantly, the overall network showed good consistency, low heterogeneity, and stable results in sensitivity analyses, which strengthens the credibility of the findings.

It should also be emphasized that our analysis focused on functional change from the end of intervention to the end of follow-up, rather than from baseline to the end of intervention. Accordingly, the observed mean differences (e.g., 6MWT ≈ 19–20 m) should not be judged solely against conventional minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds, which are typically defined for pre-to-post intervention changes. From a follow-up perspective, even modest improvements—or the maintenance of previous gains—are clinically meaningful, as they reflect prevention of functional decline once supervised training ceases.

Comparison with previous evidence

To date, no systematic meta-analysis has specifically focused on functional changes during the follow-up period after exercise interventions in stroke survivors. This study is the first to synthesize available RCT evidence to address this important gap in the literature. Prior research has predominantly concentrated on immediate post-intervention effects. For instance, Huang et al. [38] and Mou et al. [39] reported significant short-term improvements in outcomes such as the 6 MWT and BBS following structured exercise programs. However, these studies generally lacked an in-depth investigation of functional trajectories after the intervention period, limiting their ability to capture the long-term sustainability and translational value of exercise-based rehabilitation. By quantifying functional change from the end of intervention to the end of follow-up, the present study adopts a more dynamic and longitudinal perspective. This approach allows for a comprehensive evaluation of various exercise modalities in the post-intervention phase and provides novel evidence that extends beyond traditional short-term assessments. In addition, unlike most previous studies, our analysis explicitly considers whether observed changes exceed established MCID thresholds, thereby offering more clinically grounded interpretations of follow-up outcomes.

Mechanistic explanations and clinical implications

The maintenance and progression of motor function during the follow-up period are unlikely to result solely from residual short-term effects of prior interventions. Rather, they may reflect a complex interplay among neuroplastic remodeling, physiological adaptations, and the consolidation of behavioral patterns. Notably, Evidence from stroke rehabilitation research suggests that different exercise modalities may differ in their potential to influence structural and functional reorganization of the brain. RT, for instance, enhances motor unit recruitment, neuromuscular drive efficiency, and proprioceptive feedback, thereby increasing activation thresholds and improving output stability. This gives rise to a form of “functional inertia,” where the decline in motor performance may be delayed even after the intervention ceases [40–44]. Additionally, RT-induced release of myokines such as irisin may promote synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis [42]. RAT, with its high repetition and precise movement guidance, facilitates the reconstruction of corticospinal pathways and reinforces task-specific central control networks, enabling sustained autonomous motor output during follow-up [45, 46]. Moreover, physiological adaptations acquired during intervention—such as improved cardiorespiratory fitness, enhanced skeletal muscle metabolism, and recovery of lower-limb power—build a “physiological reserve” that supports functional stability in the absence of continued training [47–49]. Electrostimulation-assisted training (ESX) may modulate cortical excitability through peripheral afferent input, fostering synaptic plasticity and increasing brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels [50, 51]. This enhances corticomuscular coherence and motor control [52–54], while concurrently engaging subcortical motor circuits such as the corticospinal tract and sensorimotor integration areas, thereby supporting central mechanisms of function preservation during follow-up [55–58]. Importantly, the interpretation of these mechanistic findings must be balanced with clinical meaningfulness. While some improvements did not exceed conventional MCID values, their significance lies in preserving prior gains during the follow-up phase, which itself represents an important clinical benefit. In contrast, interventions such as MBE and virtual reality training (VRG), while effective during the intervention phase, often rely heavily on external stimuli and may lack the capacity for deep neurostructural remodeling. Without successful behavioral integration into daily routines, their effects tend to diminish over time [59]. Interventions that stimulate intrinsic motivation and are embedded in real-life contexts—such as task-oriented training or home-based reinforcement—may better promote long-term behavioral internalization, forming a “self-driven rehabilitation pathway” that enables sustained physical activity and functional performance beyond formal supervision [46]. These findings underscore the importance of designing rehabilitation strategies with an emphasis on post-intervention sustainability. Rehabilitation programs should prioritize frameworks that ensure continuity across different phases of recovery—from inpatient or subacute rehabilitation through outpatient care and into community- or home-based programs. Such frameworks should incorporate mechanisms for follow-up reinforcement and ongoing support to facilitate the transition from structured therapy to lifestyle integration. This approach is essential for achieving long-term, durable recovery of motor function.

Limitations and future directions

This study represents the first network meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the follow-up effects of various structured exercise interventions in stroke survivors. A substantial number of RCTs were included, encompassing a diverse range of intervention types and core motor function outcomes. Methodologically, both Bayesian and frequentist models were employed, in alignment with PRISMA, RoB 2.0, and CINeMA guidelines, ensuring transparency and methodological rigor. Additionally, the robustness of findings was enhanced through multiple layers of analysis, including pairwise and network comparisons, meta-regression, sensitivity analyses, assessments of consistency and heterogeneity, and publication bias evaluation. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the inherent difficulty of blinding in exercise-based interventions, the majority of included studies were rated as having “some concerns” in domains related to intervention delivery or outcome assessment, thereby lowering the certainty of some evidence. Second, the limited number of studies reporting on FMA-UE and FMA-LE outcomes precluded meta-regression and sensitivity analyses for these measures, which may have restricted our ability to fully identify sources of delayed-effect heterogeneity. Third, variability in the definition of follow-up duration, lack of standardization in intervention intensity, and differences in co-interventions (e.g., conventional rehabilitation, assistive technologies) may have introduced potential confounding factors. Fourth, some intervention comparisons were supported by sparse data, leading to an unbalanced network structure and limiting the interpretability of SUCRA-based rankings for certain modalities. Finally, although rigorous and comprehensive analytical methods were used, the overall quality of evidence was predominantly moderate to low. Future high-quality RCTs with extended follow-up periods are needed to validate and strengthen the present findings.

Conclusions

This study is the first to systematically evaluate the effects of different structured exercise interventions on motor function during the follow-up period in stroke survivors. Through pairwise and network meta-analyses, we established a comparative ranking of intervention efficacy during this critical post-intervention phase. The findings indicate that RT and RAT are the most promising modalities for sustaining or even further improving motor function after the cessation of structured rehabilitation, although the current evidence base is characterized by moderate- to low-certainty estimates. Nevertheless, this work provides important evidence-based insights into long-term recovery. Our results highlight the potential mechanisms underlying sustained benefits, including prolonged neuroplastic reorganization, physiological adaptation, and the consolidation of motor behaviors. Importantly, this study identifies the follow-up phase as a key time window for assessing the durability of rehabilitation outcomes. Future RCTs should extend follow-up durations, systematically track functional trajectories beyond the intervention period, and incorporate multimodal assessments—such as neuroimaging, physiological monitoring, and behavioral data analytics—to further elucidate mechanisms of functional maintenance and change. Rehabilitation paradigms need to evolve from a focus on short-term improvements toward approaches that prioritize sustained recovery, thereby enabling the development of personalized, phase-spanning, and durable strategies for stroke rehabilitation.
