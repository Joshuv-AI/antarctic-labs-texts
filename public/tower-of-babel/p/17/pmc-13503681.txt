Stretching decelerates ring-opening metathesis at cis-olefins in stressed molecular bows
Authors: Liang Jiang, Guoao Li, Jiali Liu, Yu Wang, Jingqi Chen, Zheng-Bin Tang, Kejiang Liang, Jin Yin, Zhen Peng, Wenjie Dou, Shuhua Li, Zhichang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRing-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) of strained cycloalkenes, driven by angle strain relief, has emerged as a powerful and versatile approach for synthesizing macromolecular materials. Nevertheless, the ROMP of C=C bonds upon stretching remains unexplored. Here, we employ a molecular-strain engineering approach by incorporating stretched C=C bonds into bowstrings of molecular bows (MBs) with different tension levels. This approach enables us to investigate the ROMP of the C=C bonds under varying tensile forces. Remarkably, increased tensile force applied to the cis-C=C bond leads to significant deceleration of the ROMP reaction, whereas the trans-isomer exhibits a pronounced acceleration under the same conditions. This contrasting response was theoretically established to originate from the differential strain-induced modulation of the activation energy. Critically, applied tensile force thus governs directly both the kinetics and mechanistic pathway of ROMP, selectively promoting cyclic oligomer formation and establishing a molecular-strain-engineering paradigm that transcends conventional ring-strain-driven polymerization.Subject terms: Polymers, Supramolecular polymers, Polymer synthesis

Introduction

Ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) has emerged as a powerful and widely applicable method for synthesizing well-defined polymer architectures1–6. The typical monomers used in ROMP are cyclic strained olefins (Fig. 1a), including cyclooctene7,8, norbornene4,9–11, and cyclobutene12,13, as well as their functionalized derivatives14–20. These monomers possess considerable angle strain, which serves as the major driving force to overcome the unfavorable entropy change during polymerization. Because of the ring size limitation, the C=C bond in these cyclic olefins adopts mostly the cis-configuration, resulting in a contractive ∠C−C=C angle ( < 120°) and suffering primarily from the angle strain. In addition, cyclic trans-olefins are also excellent candidates for ROMP21–25. On account of the tedious synthetic routes, however, only trans-cyclooctene and its derivatives have been investigated extensively7,26. trans-Cyclooctene has a higher ring strain (16.7 kcal mol−1) compared with the cis-cyclooctene (7.4 kcal mol−1)27. Consequently, cyclic olefins with greater angle strain generally exhibit higher reactivities in ROMP and are often targeted for the synthesis of linear, high-molecular-weight polymers28.

In the realm of ROMP of cyclic olefins, the introduction of functional groups via side chains is a common strategy employed to modify polymer properties. In order to increase the diversity of the backbone composition within ROMP, however, the polymerization of macrocyclic monomers is often employed. It is noteworthy that most macrocyclic monomers possess minimal or no ring strain, creating a trade-off in monomer selection29. Monomers with high ring strain offer an enthalpic driving force, whereas macrocyclic systems provide greater diversity without it. The utilization of polymers derived from macrocyclic olefins shows great potential in various applications involving liquid crystalline polymers, stimuli-responsive polymer materials, and sequence-controlled polymers5,30–33. For instance, Boydston and coworkers introduced a novel class of strained macrocyclic olefins that provided a pathway to synthesize stilbene-based polymers, which are typically obtained through uncontrolled polymerization methods34. Cui and coworkers reported a photoinduced strain-assisted approach for synthesizing main-chain stiff-stilbene polymers with excellent photoresponsive properties using ROMP35. Nevertheless, developing efficient and universally applicable methods for the synthesis of highly strained macrocyclic monomers remains a significant challenge.

Herein, we present (Fig. 1b) a comprehensive approach to fabricate highly strained molecular bows (MBs) by stretching the bowstring, which incorporates a cis- or trans-C=C bond. These MBs serve as unique monomers for investigating ROMP reactions of C=C bonds under direct tensile force, offering a distinct strategy complementary to the conventional reliance on angle strain. The tensile force is defined as the directional mechanical stretching force exerted along the longitudinal axis of the bowstring chain, originating from the intrinsically curved and rigid backbone. It is noteworthy that under our experimental conditions, the ROMP of these MBs predominantly yielded well-defined cyclic oligomers rather than high-molecular-weight linear polymers. Despite this outcome, the polymerization behavior of these strained monomers provides valuable and fundamental insights into the reactivity of C=C bonds under mechanical activation and the factors influencing the cyclooligomerization pathway.

Results

Drawing inspiration from the strained mechanical structure of a bow, we developed a molecular-strain engineering (MSE) approach that harnesses intramolecular tension through the use of precisely tunable strained conformations36–40. The overall design of our approach involves the incorporation of a flexible chain, featuring either a cis- or trans-C=C bond (bowstring), that connects both ends of the methyl-substituted oligoparaphenylene moiety (bow-limb)41. Because of the shorter length of the bowstring relative to the bow limb, ring strain is induced, resulting in a taut bowstring with directly stretched C=C bonds. The level of ring strain in the MBs can be adjusted by modifying the length of the bowstrings, with longer bowstrings resulting in lower levels of ring strain. Building upon this approach, we designed (Fig. 2) two different classes of MBs, each characterized by their unique bowstring lengths. The first type includes six atoms (cis-5 and trans-5) in the bowstring, while the second type features eight atoms (cis-Bn5 and trans-Bn5).

The syn−1,4-dimethoxy-2,5-cyclohexadiene moiety was employed in the construction of the bow-limb on account of its ability to be converted into a phenylene unit through reductive or thermal aromatization processes42,43. The key synthetic strategy involved obtaining macrocycles (4 and Bn4) with minimized ring strain and subsequently utilizing the reductive aromatization of syn−1,4-dimethoxy-2,5-cyclohexadiene to generate the desired MBs (5 and Bn5). These macrocycles were constructed using two different 2 + 2 cyclization methods: nucleophilic substitution and Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reactions, respectively. In order to enhance the solubility of the MBs and target polymers, methyl groups were introduced into the bow-limb moiety. The preparation of several precursors (Supplementary Figs. 1 − 2) followed established synthetic routes for cycloparaphenylenes44,45. The synthetic route involved monolithiation of 2,5-dimethyl-1,4-dibromobenzene, followed by nucleophilic addition to p-benzoquinone and subsequent methylation with methyl iodide, resulting in the formation of syn−1,4-bis(2,5-dimethyl-4-bromophenyl)−1,4-dimethoxycyclohexadiene (1). Bisphenol (3) was then synthesized through a Suzuki coupling between 1 and 3-hydroxyphenylboronic acid. The structures of the MBs (5 and Bn5) and precursors were confirmed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Supplementary Figs. 3 − 27), high resolution electrospray mass spectrometry (HRESI-MS) (Supplementary Fig. 28), and X-ray crystallographic analyses (Supplementary Figs. 29−31 and Supplementary Tables 1−5). The ring strain energies (Estrain) for the molecular bows (cis-Bn5, cis-5, trans-Bn5, and trans-5) were estimated based on homodesmotic reactions with appropriate cap molecules, designed to generate the corresponding ring-opening products46. These reactions, as modeled by density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Fig. 39), provide further support for the distinctive structural characteristics of the MBs. Collectively, the synthetic, structural, and energetic evidence presented here establishes MBs as a distinctive and well-defined model system for investigating the reactivity of C=C bonds upon direct stretching through ROMP.

The 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 2a, b) of cis-Bn5, cis-5, trans-Bn5, and trans-5 reveal one set of resonances for aryl protons in the bow-limb on account of their high symmetry. A comparison of the chemical shifts of protons in cis-Bn5 and cis-5 demonstrated a significant downfield shift of 0.22 ppm for the protons of the cis-C=C bond in cis-5, which can be attributed to the increased strain energy from 27.43 kcal mol−1 in cis-Bn5 to 35.63 kcal mol−1 in cis-5. Even though the strain energies increased greatly in trans-MBs from 25.96 to 37.73 kcal mol−1, however, only a slight downfield shift of 0.04 ppm was observed for the trans-C=C bond protons. These results suggest that cis- and trans-C=C bonds are affected differently by intramolecular direct stretching.

Single crystals of MBs suitable for X-ray diffraction were obtained by slow liquid-liquid diffusion of hexane or methanol into their CH2Cl2 solutions. X-Ray crystallographic analyses of cis-Bn5, cis-5, trans-Bn5, and trans-5 at 100 K provide unambiguous evidence confirming their bow-shaped structures (Fig. 2c). Specifically, both cis-Bn5 and cis-5 exhibited Cs symmetry with a symmetry plane (σ) perpendicular to the cis-C=C bond. In order to quantify the degree of bending in the bow-limb, the distance (d) between two aryl carbon atoms at the ether ends was measured (Supplementary Fig. 29) to reveal relationships between structures and strain energies47. As the bowstring length decreased, the distance d became shorter, decreasing from 9.43 Å in cis-Bn5 to 7.56 Å in cis-5. As the distance d decreases, the strain energy increases, indicating a negative correlation. In the case of cis-MBs, the strain energy increased from 27.43 kcal mol−1 in cis-Bn5 to 35.63 kcal mol−1 in cis-5 as d decreased. This trend is also observed in trans-MBs.

In order to examine how the tensile force affects C=C bonds, the bond lengths and angles were measured (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 1) from their X-ray structures. With the increase in strain energy from cis-Bn5 to cis-5, the length of the cis-C=C bond stretched from 1.300 to 1.336 Å, while the ∠C−C=C angle decreased slightly from 125.4° to 124.8°. In trans-MBs, however, the bond length of trans-C=C bond and the mean ∠C−C=C angle remained relatively unchanged as strain energy increased from trans-Bn5 to trans-5. These observations indicate that the cis- and trans-C=C bonds respond differently when subjected to intramolecular tensile force directly.

We investigated the ROMP reactions of cis-Bn5, cis-5, trans-Bn5, and trans-5 using the 3rd generation of Grubbs catalyst (G3(Py)2) with an initial monomer to initiator ratio of 10 : 1 in CDCl348. The progress of polymerization was monitored (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Figs. 32−35) using 1H NMR spectroscopy at room temperature. The experiments involved an initial monomer concentration of 0.01 mol L−1 in CDCl3, with spectra collected every 10 minutes within a 1-hour period. Our analysis revealed that the ROMP reactions display pseudo-first-order kinetics with respect to the consumption of MBs. We calculated (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 36) the rate constant k by examining the decrease in resonances of cis- or trans-C=C bonds in MBs. Error analyses of kinetic parameters were performed (Supplementary Table 6) based on three parallel experiments. The experimental results demonstrate that the ROMP reaction of cis-Bn5 proceeded rapidly and completed within 50 min (k = 1.68 × 10−3 s−1). In striking contrast, the ROMP reaction of cis-5 was approximately 19.5 times slower (k = 8.62 × 10−5 s−1). Interestingly, the ROMP reaction of trans-Bn5 was significantly slower than that of cis-Bn5, with a rate constant of approximately 6.96 × 10−6 s−1. When trans-5 was involved, however, the rate constant increased to 1.14 × 10−4 s−1 as a result of higher strain energy. This finding indicates that an increased tensile force applied to the cis-C=C bond impedes the ROMP reaction49,50, contrasting our initial expectations. The mechanical response of the applied tensile force is dictated by the details of the molecular coupling51, with the distinct conformations of C=C bonds accounting for these differences.

To the best of our knowledge, the ROMP reaction is driven by the release of ring strain, which serves as the major driving force to overcome the unfavorable entropy change during polymerization. Consequently, a higher strain energy in the monomer enhances generally its reactivity in the ROMP reaction. However, this is not the case for cis-MBs, which can be attributed to the different effects of tensile force in contrast to the opposing angle strain acting on the cis-C=C bond. In addition, we believe that these two forces play distinct roles in influencing the trans-C=C bond, although both superficially accelerate ROMP reactions. The variation in activity between cis- and trans-C=C bonds under tension was examined through Laplacian bond order (LBO) analysis52, and the polarizable continuum model (PCM) was employed to assess solvation in CHCl3 (Supplementary Table 8). The ROMP reactivities of C=C bonds in MBs exhibit a strong correlation with the bond order, wherein the rate constant decreases as the bond order increases. While several theoretical models49,50,53 exist to predict the force-rate relationship in the mechanical dissociation of loaded and unloaded σ-bonds and accelerated Diels-Alder reactions through mechanical distortion54, our results do not align with any of these models. This discrepancy is likely attributed to the involvement of more intricate systems, specifically the presence of cis- and trans-C=C bonds as well as the catalyst. We believe that in the case of cis-C=C bond, while the introduction of strain can increase the energy of the substrate, the energy increase of the reaction transition state may be even greater. As a result, the activation energy ΔG‡ of the reaction can actually increase after the strain is intensified, leading to a slower reaction rate.

On the basis of the addition of an enthalpic driving force and the ring strain of MBs, we anticipated controlled chain growth with controllable molecular weight (Mw) and low polydispersity during the ROMP reaction. In order to validate this expectation, we synthesized MB polymers using varying amounts (2.5 to 10 mol%) of G3(Py)2 catalyst in CDCl3. Within 4 hours at 60 °C, the conversion exceeded 95% for each monomer, as confirmed (Supplementary Table 7) by TLC and 1H NMR spectroscopy. The multimodal Mw distribution of these polymers, with Mw ranging from 1.0 to 4.0 kDa, was determined (Supplementary Fig. 37) via gel permeation chromatography (GPC) using a refractive index detector. The structures were verified (Supplementary Fig. 38) by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF/MS) and 1H NMR spectroscopy. In the case of cis-Bn5, the most prominent peak in the mass spectrum was 1100.5832 Da, precisely twice the monomer’s Mw, an observation which confirms the presence of a cyclic dimer. In addition, peaks for other cyclic oligomers, including trimer, tetramer, and pentamer, were also observed. The 1H NMR spectra revealed distinct signals at 5.89 and 5.92 ppm, corresponding to the protons in cis- and trans-C=C bonds of cyclic oligomers, respectively, with a ratio of approximately 43 : 57 and no specific configuration selectivity. No linear polymers were observed in any of the analyses conducted.

In order to elucidate the differential rate modulation of cis- and trans-C=C bonds (decelerated for cis versus accelerated for trans) as well as the preferential formation of cyclic oligomers over linear polymers, we calculated energy profiles for the ROMP reactions of these MBs. The Gibbs free energy profile for the oligomerization of cis-Bn5 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 40–41) revealed that the reaction initiates with the dissociation of the pyridine ligand from catalyst G3(Py)2 to generate the active Ru-carbene species G3Py55,56. Subsequent coordination of the olefin cis-Bn5 to the Ru center forms the η2-complex Int0, followed by the second pyridine ligand dissociation to form Int1. This endothermic activation process (9.7 kcal mol−1, via Int0) suggests a fast induction. Next, the first ROM process can easily take place to form another Ru-carbene species Int3 through a four-membered-ring intermediate Int2. The ROM process involves 4e and retro-4e cyclization (through TS-1 and TS-2, Fig. 4b), with the relative Gibbs free energy barriers with respect to Int1 as 5.0 and 8.6 kcal mol−1. Subsequently, another olefin monomer cis-Bn5 coordinates to the Ru center in Int3 to form the η2-complex Int4. A second ROM process through TS-3 and TS-4 then affords the dimerization intermediate Int6. The activation energy barriers with respect to Int4 for these steps are 6.1 and 5.9 kcal mol−1, respectively, which are slightly lower than the first ROM process. Strong [π···π] interactions in Int7 stabilize the intermediate and prompt intramolecular coordination of the free olefin to the Ru center, which affords the η2-complex Int7 in an exothermic step (ΔG = − 7.7 kcal mol−1 relative to Int6). Subsequently, a ring-closing metathesis (RCM) process proceeds through TS-5 and TS-6. This process is followed by a ligand exchange, in which the cyclic dimer product is displaced by a new molecule of cis-Bn5, thereby releasing the product and regenerating the initial η2-complex Int1 to close the catalytic cycle.

The overall rate-determining step of the dimerization is the retro-[2 + 2] cycloaddition of the first ROM stage, which occurs through TS-2. To further examine the kinetic behavior of the reaction in the computational scope, we calculated the relative Gibbs free energies of similar structures at the positions of Int1, TS-1, and TS-2 for the reagents cis-5, trans-Bn5, trans-5, and the free ethylene C2H4, which is considered to have no steric effects. The feasibility of a trimerization pathway was also assessed by calculating the third ROM stage which starts from Int6. The dimerization rates reveal (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 9) that for cis-olefins, rcis-Bn5 > rcis-5, whereas the reverse trend (rtrans-Bn5 <rtrans-5) is observed for trans-olefins. In this case, trimerization is disfavored on account of a higher free energy barrier, consistent with experimental results.

Energetically, increased strains from cis/trans-Bn5 to cis/trans-5 destabilize the η2-complexes Int1, which enhances their metathesis reactivity. This destabilization is quantified (Fig. 4c) by free energy increases of 3.9 and 1.0 kcal mol−1 for the cis and trans series, respectively. The increased strains, however, also elevate the metathesis barriers. For the cis series, shortening the side chain raises (Fig. 4c) the energy of the retro-[2 + 2] cycloaddition TS-2 by 5.5 kcal mol−1. In this case, the substantial destabilization of TS-2 outweighs the beneficial activation of the η2-complex, which results in a slower overall reaction rate. In contrast, the more flexible trans series exhibits a minimal increase in energy of TS-2, which allows the enhanced reactivity of the η2-complex Int1 to dominate the kinetic profile and accelerate the reaction. The mechanistic rationale for these differential energy changes is further supported by a geometrical analysis of the rate-determining TS structures (Supplementary Tables 10−11). The TS structures for cis-Bn5 and trans-5 more closely resemble later, less sterically hindered intermediates and free C2H4 than their counterparts do, a geometry which results in reduced steric congestion and a lower associated energy penalty.

Discussion

In conclusion, our investigation introduces a molecular-strain engineering (MSE) approach for strain-driven ROMP reactions of stretched C=C bonds. We successfully incorporated both cis- and trans-C=C bonds into the bowstrings of MBs, stretching them directly with the aid of oligoparaphenylene units as the bow-limb. Through manipulation of the bowstring length, we achieved precise control over the gradient regulation of ring strain in the MBs, quantified by strain energies. The ROMP reaction of MBs exhibited pseudo-first-order kinetics, with exclusive formation of cyclic oligomers, predominantly cyclic dimers. We proposed a mechanism involving intramolecular [π···π] interaction-enabled folding that drives the cyclic oligomerization process. Remarkably, we observed a dual effect of tensile force on the ROMP reaction: deceleration for cis-MBs and acceleration for trans-MBs, which was also theoretically demonstrated to originate from the differential effect of strain on the activation energy. This unexpected finding not only challenges conventional understanding and contradicts established theoretical models but also provides novel insights into the reactive properties of functional groups under tension. Overall, the MSE approach holds promise for investigating the influence of mechanical forces on small organic molecules, expanding the understanding of strain applications in synthetic chemistry. It opens avenues for studying the impact of strain on other chemical reactions and the interplay between mechanics and reactivity.

Methods

General procedure for kinetic measurements of MB metathesis

In a glovebox, the 3rd generation Grubbs catalyst (G3(Py)2, 3.0 mg, 3.4 μmol) was dissolved in argon-degassed CDCl3 (1.0 mL). Meanwhile, cis-Bn5 (2.2 mg, 4.0 μmol) was added to an NMR tube and dissolved in CDCl3 (280 μL). The freshly prepared solution of G3(Py)2 (120 μL, 0.4 μmol, 10%) was added before being sealed with a rubber cap. After mixing by a vortex agitator, the NMR tube was placed in an NMR spectrometer and 1H NMR spectra were collected every 10 min within 1 h at room temperature.
