Electroacupuncture activates the TET1/PPARα pathway to protect mitochondrial function in MASLD
Authors: Shurui Yang, Yiwen Li, Bowen Lu, Congcong Cheng, Yayuan Wang, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction. It may be ameliorated by electroacupuncture (EA) through activation of the ten-eleven translocation 1 (TET1)/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPARα) pathway, thereby restoring mitochondrial function.MethodsC57BL/6 mice were fed L-amino acid rodent diet with 60 kcal% fat, low methionine and no added choline (CDAHFD) and high fat diet (HFD) for three weeks to induce MASLD, followed by EA treatment and daily intraperitoneal injections of a TET1 agonist or antagonist. Hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and mitochondrial function were evaluated by biochemical assays, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), flow cytometry, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), western blotting (WB) and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).ResultsEA reduced hepatic total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and free fatty acids (FFA), and decreased the size of lipid droplets. It restored mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and cristae integrity, while attenuating ROS accumulation and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Moreover, EA upregulated TET1 expression, enhanced genomic 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) enrichment, and elevated PPARα expression. These effects were comparable to those of a TET1 agonist, whereas administration of a TET1 antagonist partially reversed them.ConclusionEA ameliorates MASLD by activating the TET1/PPARα pathway, thereby enhancing mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative stress and inflammation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12950-026-00500-1.Keywords: Acupuncture, MASLD, TET1, PPAR α, Mitochondrial function

Introduction

MASLD includes various liver injuries, from hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and MASLD-associated hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. MASLD and MASH were previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), respectively [2]. Metabolic imbalance induces oxidative stress and inflammation, which accelerate hepatic lipid accumulation and hepatocyte injury [3]. By 2040, the global prevalence of NAFLD is projected to reach 55.7% [4]. Current management primarily relies on lifestyle interventions, including dietary modification and exercise. Pharmacological treatments such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists or pioglitazone, and bariatric surgery in selected patients, provide additional options but are often limited by adverse effects, including increased risks of heart failure, gallstone formation, and pancreatitis [5, 6].

Epigenetic regulation is critical in the development of MASLD. DNA methylation, catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases, generates 5-methylcytosine (5-mC), whereas DNA hydroxymethylation, mediated by the TET enzymes, converts 5-mC to 5-hmC, thereby facilitating demethylation and transcriptional activation [7–9]. Among TET family members, TET1 has been implicated in liver injury and apoptosis [10]. Deficiency of RAS protein activator like 2 enhances hepatic TET1 expression. This promotes microsomal triglyceride transfer protein expression via DNA hydroxymethylation, resulting in increased production and secretion of very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), which regulates lipid metabolism and alleviates hepatic steatosis [11]. TET1 also forms a complex with Sirtuin 1 and regulates its deacetylase activity, leading to the deacetylation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) and forkhead box O1, maintaining their nuclear localization. This process activates the transcription of glucose metabolism-related genes in hepatocytes [12]. Conversely, TET1 knockdown diminishes the effects of growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible β in reducing TG content [13]. These findings suggest that TET1 modulates glucose and lipid metabolism by regulating DNA methylation.

PPARα is a ligand-activated nuclear receptor predominantly expressed in the liver, initially identified as the molecular target of xenobiotics that induce peroxisome proliferation in rodent models [14]. This receptor plays a crucial role in regulating fatty acid transport and catabolism within hepatic tissues [15]. In addition, the PPARα signaling pathway is intricately linked to hepatic lipid homeostasis and redox balance, with its dysregulation implicated in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis [16]. In patients with NAFLD, PPARα expression is suppressed due to DNA hypermethylation [17]. Activation of PPARα enhances fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis while concurrently reducing inflammatory responses [18]. Furthermore, the upregulation of the PPARα/PGC-1α signaling pathway facilitates increased mitochondrial β-oxidation, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and diminishing lipid accumulation in the liver [19].

In NAFLD mice, acupuncture reduced lipid absorption by downregulating small intestine apolipoproteins [20]. Proteomic analyses revealed that differentially expressed proteins post-acupuncture treatment were predominantly enriched in KEGG pathways associated with the PPAR signaling pathway and various facets of fatty acid processes, including biosynthesis, metabolism, elongation, and digestion [21]. Acupuncture effectively treats NAFLD by enhancing metabolic balance, reducing inflammation, correcting lipid issues, and alleviating oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress [22–25]. It may also protect mitochondrial function by modulating DNA methylation of target genes [26]. Despite these findings, the precise molecular mechanisms remain inadequately elucidated. Of particular interest is the TET1/PPARα pathway, where TET1 facilitates the hydroxymethylation of the PPARα promoter, thereby upregulating PPARα expression, enhancing fatty acid oxidation, and ameliorating steatosis [27]. While limited evidence indicates that acupuncture may influence TET1 activity in neuropathic pain models [28], its regulatory role in MASLD through the TET1/PPARα pathway has not been defined. We propose the hypothesis that EA enhances the expression of PPARα through TET1-mediated DNA demethylation. This epigenetic modification is postulated to attenuate oxidative stress and inflammation, restore mitochondrial function, and enhance the antioxidant defense system, thereby contributing to the amelioration of MASLD.

Materials and methods

Animals

Animal procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and ARRIVE guidelines [29]. A total of 70 eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the National Rodent Laboratory Animal Resource Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The mice were housed under controlled conditions, with a temperature range of 20–26 °C, relative humidity of 40–70%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They were provided with ad libitum access to food and water for the duration of the study. To induce a MASLD model, the CDAHFD + HFD group was fed CDAHFD (Deitz Biotechnology (Wuxi) Co., Ltd.) and HFD (Wuhan Chunyu Hong Co., Ltd.) for three weeks [30, 31]. CDAHFD mainly contains 23.1% protein, 26.3% carbohydrates, and 35.7% fat. HFD primarily contains 18.28% protein, 25.81% fat, and 40.16% carbohydrates.

After 3 weeks of modeling, three animals from the standard rodent diet group and three animals from the CDAHFD + HFD group were randomly selected using a random number table. The animals were fasted for 16 h but had unrestricted access to water. Following euthanasia, the liver was examined macroscopically for visible changes, and a portion of the tissue was fixed in 10% formalin for HE staining and oil red O staining. Successful modeling was confirmed by fat droplet accumulation [32, 33].

Allocation

From the 15 mice fed a standard diet, 10 were randomly selected to form the normal group (N; n = 10). Among the 55 mice subjected to the CDAHFD + HFD regimen, 40 were randomly allocated into four groups: the model group (M; n = 10), the agonist group (AG; n = 10), the EA-plus-antagonist group (EA + AN; n = 10), and the EA group (EA; n = 10). Interventions

EA methods

Mice were immobilized using a specialized fixation device to ensure calmness and prevent movement. EA was applied at the acupoints Zusanli (ST36), Fenglong (ST40), Guanyuan (CV4) and Zhongwan (CV12) using 0.30 × 15 mm stainless steel needles (Global, China). The acupoints were identified based on anatomical landmarks and standard guidelines for mouse acupoint positioning.

The acupoints CV4 and CV12 were connected to one electrode pair of the Han’s acupoint nerve stimulator (HANS LH202H, China), whereas ST36 and ST40 were connected to a separate electrode pair. The treatment was conducted once daily, with each session lasting 10 min, for a total duration of 6 weeks. EA was applied with a continuous wave at 2 Hz and an intensity of 1 mA. Treatment was performed once daily for 10 min per session for 2 weeks, during which mice continued to receive the CDAHFD + HFD. The parameters for stimulation frequency, intensity, treatment duration, and total treatment period were established based on previous studies and preliminary experimental results [34, 35].

Intraperitoneal injection

To prepare the solution, mix 1000 µL of a 25 mg/mL DMSO stock with 4000 µL PEG300, 500 µL Tween-80, and 4500 µL saline to make 10 mL. Add 1 mg each of OAC1 (MedChemexpress, USA) and NSC-370,284 (MedChemexpress, USA) to separate 10 mL portions. Administer OAC1 and NSC-370,284, both selective TET1 agents, at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg body weight [36–38]. Adjust the injection volume based on mouse body weight for accurate dosing. Perform intraperitoneal injections daily for six weeks.

Animal anesthesia and euthanasia

Mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol (20 µL/g, i.p.) prior to tissue collection. At the end of the experiment, animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Body mass (BM) measurement and liver weight-to-body mass ratio (LW/BM ratio) calculation

BM was measured at a fixed daily time using an electronic analytical balance (0.01 g). Values were recorded weekly, and changes were analyzed to assess the intervention. At study end, mice were anesthetized, livers excised and weighed on the same balance, and the LW/BM ratio was calculated.

HE staining

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned (4 μm), and stained with hematoxylin–eosin for microscopic examination. Images were captured using a Nikon Fi3 microscope (Nikon, Japan). The images were analyzed using ImageJ version 1.54f (Maven Repository, USA).

Oil red O staining

Frozen liver Sect. (10 μm) were stained with Oil Red O, counterstained with hematoxylin, and imaged using a Nikon Fi3 microscope. Lipid deposition was quantified with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA).

ELISA

ELISA kits used include Complex I, III, IV, V 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), catalase (CAT), nitrated proteins (NP) (Enzyme Immunoassay, China), and 5-hmC (EPIGENTEK, USA) for liver tissue. ELISA was performed according to standard procedures, including coating, blocking, incubation with samples and detection antibody, substrate development, and termination with stop solution. Measure absorbance at 450 nm using a Multiskan FC (Thermo Scientific, USA) and quantify results using a standard curve.

Biochemical assay

Biological samples, including liver tissue and serum, were prepared by centrifuging to remove debris and diluted as required. Assay kits for TC, TG (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), and FFA (Solarbio, China) were used for both liver tissue and serum. Liver tissue was used for the measurement of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (Solarbio, China), ROS (Biyuntian, China), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH) (Nanjing Jiancheng, China), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Solarbio, China). Samples or standards were added to wells, incubated at 37 °C, developed with substrate, and the reaction was terminated with stop solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Measure absorbance at 340–450 nm using an EnVision microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Calculate the analyte concentration by comparing sample absorbance to a standard curve.

TEM

Liver tissues were fixed, embedded in resin, sectioned into ultrathin slices, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for TEM observation. After drying, ultrastructural observation was performed using a HT7800/HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) at 80 kV, and images were captured for analysis.

Flow cytometry

Prepared liver cells were stained with JC-1 at 37 °C for 20 min. The JC-1 staining buffer (1X) (Biyuntian, China) was prepared and used to wash the cells twice. After washing, cells were resuspended in 500 µL of buffer and analyzed by CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter Inc, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo™ software (Becton Dickinson, USA), with gates set based on FMO controls to assess fluorescence intensity, reflecting mitochondrial health.

WB

A standard protocol was used to detect liver tissues. The concentrations of the primary antibodies were: β-actin (1:10000, 66009-1-Ig; Proteintech, China), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (1:6000, 16806-1-AP; Proteintech, China), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (1:1000, 17590-1-AP; Proteintech, China), NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) (1:1000, DF7438; Affinity, USA), TET1 (1:1000, A1506; Abclonal, China), PPAR α (1:2000, A22887; Abclonal, China). Protein levels were normalized to β-actin and quantified from triplicate blots using ImageJ 1.54f (Maven Repository, USA).

RT-qPCR

Gene expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1), fatty acid synthase (FAS), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), IL-1β, TNF-α, NLRP3, GPX1, CAT, TET1, and PPARα was quantified by RT-qPCR. Total RNA was isolated from mouse liver tissue using the RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (VAZYME, China) and quantified by measuring absorbance at 260/280 nm using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total RNA (1 µg) was reverse-transcribed using HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix (VAZYME, China), and qPCR was performed with ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (VAZYME, China) on a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad, USA). Reactions were run in triplicate and normalized to β-actin. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned at 4–5 μm, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with CD86 primary antibody (1:200, 28058-1-AP; Proteintech, China) and washed with PBS: Phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The operation was carried out according to the instructions provided by the Immuno-coloring reagent iVision™ Poly-HRP Goat Anti-Mouse/Rabbit Secondary Antibody (Talent Biomedical, China). Diaminobenzidine (Servicebio, China) was used as the chromogen, with incubation for 5–10 min. Slides were washed, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips. The stained slides were examined under a Nikon Fi3 microscope. The area of density was quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Statistical analysis

Data from each experiment were analyzed separately and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s). Prior to hypothesis testing, data distribution was assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). For normally distributed data with equal variances, comparisons between two groups at a single time point were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, and comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparison test when appropriate. If the assumptions for parametric testing were not met, the corresponding non-parametric tests were used, such as the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple groups. Values of P < 0.05 was deemed indicative of statistical significance.

Results

EA enhanced MASLD and normalized lipid metabolism, but the TET1 antagonist partially inhibited these benefits

BM in the M group remained significantly higher than that in the N group throughout weeks 0–6 (P < 0.01). During weeks 0–3, BM did not differ significantly between the M group and the AG, EA + AN, or EA groups (P > 0.05). By week 4, BM declined in both the AG and EA groups (P < 0.05), whereas no marked change was observed in the EA + AN group (P > 0.05). Similar trends persisted through weeks 5 and 6 (Fig. 2a). The LW-BM ratio was markedly increased in the M group relative to the N group (P < 0.01), while this ratio was significantly reduced in the AG, EA + AN, and EA groups (P < 0.01). The improvement induced by EA was partially blocked by the TET1 antagonist (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2b). Serum and hepatic levels of TC, TG, and FFA were substantially elevated in the M group (P < 0.01) but were significantly decreased in the AG, EA + AN, and EA groups (P < 0.01). The lipid-lowering effect of EA was again partially attenuated by TET1 inhibition (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2c, d). In liver tissue, mRNA expression of SREBP and FAS was markedly upregulated in the M group (P < 0.01), whereas CPT1A expression was significantly reduced (P < 0.01). These changes were reversed in the AG, EA + AN, and EA groups, in which SREBP and FAS were significantly downregulated (P < 0.01, P < 0.05) and CPT1A expression was significantly increased (P < 0.01). The effect of EA on the mRNA levels of SREBP, FAS, and CPT1A was partially inhibited by the TET1 antagonist (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2e). In the N group, liver tissue exhibited an intact morphological structure with evenly distributed hepatic plates, relatively regular hepatocyte arrangement, no significant dilation of hepatic sinusoids, and no notable pathological alterations. In the M group, microscopic observation revealed intact liver tissue morphology with irregular hepatocyte arrangement, partial hepatocyte damage, and structural dispersion, accompanied by evident multifocal fat-like vacuolar degeneration. In the AG group, liver tissue displayed an intact morphological structure under the microscope, with irregular hepatocyte arrangement, mild edema in a small number of hepatocytes, and the presence of minimal fat-like vacuolar degeneration. In the EA + AN group, the liver tissue structure was intact, though hepatocyte arrangement was disorganized. Some hepatocytes showed edema with cytoplasmic loosening, and significant fat-like vacuolar degeneration was observed. In the EA group, liver tissue morphology appeared intact under microscopic observation, with irregular hepatocyte arrangement, mild hepatocyte edema accompanied by cytoplasmic loosening, minimal fat-like vacuolar degeneration, and no notable inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2f). The M group showed a marked accumulation of hepatic lipid droplets relative to the N group (P < 0.01). This increase was significantly reversed in the AG, EA + AN, and EA groups (P < 0.01). However, the ability of EA to reduce lipid droplet deposition was partly suppressed when the TET1 antagonist was administered (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2g, h).

Discussion

CDAHFD addresses the issue of weight loss and high mortality rates in mice caused by traditional methionine-choline deficient diets. By controlling methionine at 0.1%, it allows for weight maintenance while rapidly inducing hepatitis and liver fibrosis [39]. The HFD-induced model also exhibits characteristics similar to the development of human NAFLD/NASH [40]. SREBP1 and FAS mediate lipogenesis in the liver [41, 42], while CPT1A is involved in fatty acid oxidation [43]. The combination of CDAHFD and HFD for modeling better reflects the clinical characteristics of MASLD, which often involves both hepatic lipid accumulation and associated metabolic disturbances. The study showed that EA reduced lipid buildup and enhanced liver function in MAFLD, as evidenced by changes in body metrics and lipid levels. EA also influenced key lipid metabolism genes and improved liver histology.

In NAFLD, liver function gradually declines due to mitochondrial dysfunction, and the liver is unable to transfer excess lipid droplets out of the liver [44]. Mitochondrial ultrastructural damage, disrupted mitochondrial dynamics, reduced activity of respiratory chain complexes, and impaired ATP-generating capacity collectively promote excessive lipogenesis and diminish fatty acid β-oxidation, ultimately causing triglyceride buildup and increased ROS in hepatocytes [45–47]. The study demonstrated that EA significantly enhanced mitochondrial function and reduced oxidative stress in MASLD. This was shown by improved mitochondrial respiratory activity, membrane potential, ATP production, and preservation of mitochondrial morphology. EA also decreased oxidative damage markers like ROS, 4-HNE, TBARS, MDA, and NP, while boosting antioxidant levels such as GPX1, CAT, SOD, and GSH. Furthermore, EA treatment restored the oxidative-antioxidative balance, indicated by increased GPX1, CAT, and COX IV expression.

NLRP3 is primarily expressed in macrophages and is an important component of the inflammasome within these cells. The activation of NLRP3 promotes the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype [48]. As has been confirmed, mitochondria contribute to NLRP3 inflammasome activation through several pathways, one of which is the generation of mitochondrial ROS [49]. And mitochondrial DNA that escapes from stressed mitochondria can initiate inflammatory responses by activating the cGAS–STING pathway, and its oxidized form can bind to cytosolic NLRP3, further enhancing inflammasome activation [50]. This study found that EA effectively alleviated macrophage infiltration and reduced the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and NLRP3.

A deficiency in PPARα exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction [51]. The absence of PPARα in hepatocytes triggers compensatory mechanisms to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, such as increased mitochondrial biogenesis, morphological remodeling, and alterations in autophagic activity [52]. Upregulation of PPARα can reduce lipid accumulation, alleviate liver injury, and improve mitochondrial biogenesis [53]. Consequently, PPARα is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function, thereby potentially mitigating MASLD. In mice, a HFD decreases hepatic Tet1/2 expression, leading to hypermethylation of PPARα, which in turn causes lipid metabolic disorders and obesity [54]. The study found that EA significantly increased TET1 expression and enhanced the activation of the PPARα pathway, as shown by increased 5-hmC levels and the upregulation of TET1 and PPARα protein and mRNA expression in the liver.

EA at the ST36-ST40 and CV4-CV12 acupoint combinations, known as “Biao-Ben,” has been shown to mitigate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetic nephropathy rat models [55]. Specifically, EA at the ST36 acupoint has been demonstrated to restore the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion within skeletal muscle, thereby ameliorating both structural and functional mitochondrial damage and subsequently enhancing energy metabolism [56]. Furthermore, acupuncture at the ST36 and CV4 acupoints modulates the expression of the toll-like receptor 9/myeloid differentiation primary response 8/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells pathway, effectively suppressing inflammatory responses [57]. Additionally, EA at the ST40 and Sanyinjiao acupoints has been observed to improve lipid metabolic disorders in insulin-resistant rats, potentially through the downregulation of enzyme activity involved in fatty acid synthesis and metabolic regulation [58]. In this study, EA was applied at ST36-ST40 and CV4-CV12, with stimulation parameters that have been demonstrated in multiple studies to improve glucose and lipid metabolism [59–61].

The study has several strengths, including the integration of histological, molecular, functional, and biochemical assessments, which demonstrated consistent and statistically significant improvements, thereby enhancing the reliability of our conclusions. The bidirectional pharmacological modulation of TET1 further supported the causal relationship within the EA and TET1/PPARα pathway. However, there are limitations. We did not utilize hepatocyte-specific genetic models, and pharmacological methods may have off-target effects. Additionally, we only measured global 5-hmC levels and did not conduct site-specific assays such as hMeDIP-qPCR or oxBS-seq to directly link hydroxymethylation at the PPARα promoter to transcriptional activation. Moreover, we did not assess key metabolic fluxes such as hepatic fatty acid β-oxidation, very low-density lipoprotein secretion, or mitochondrial respiration, which limits the functional analysis of metabolic reprogramming.

Furthermore, the systemic administration of the TET1 agonist and antagonist may involve extrahepatic contributions, which we have acknowledged as a limitation of our study. To resolve tissue specificity, future studies will incorporate strategies such as liver-targeted delivery, liver-specific manipulation of TET1, and multi-tissue profiling to distinguish hepatic from systemic effects. These approaches will help further elucidate the liver-specific actions of the TET1 agonist and antagonist and improve the understanding of their therapeutic potential for MASLD.

An area requiring enhancement in this study is the lack of a sham EA group, which would have facilitated the control of non-specific physiological effects potentially induced by physical restraint or the stress of needling. In the absence of this control, it becomes difficult to accurately differentiate the specific effects of EA from those arising due to handling or needle insertion. Future research should incorporate a sham control group, such as stimulation at non-acupoints or needling without electrical output, to more rigorously account for these non-specific effects. The inclusion of such controls will improve the interpretation of EA-specific effects and provide a more precise understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

EA alleviated the MASLD phenotype and was accompanied by increased hepatic TET1 expression and enhanced PPARα signaling. Mechanistically, TET1, an epigenetic dioxygenase, is hypothesized to remodel cytosine modifications at the PPARα promoter by promoting 5-hydroxymethylation, a change that is consistent with reduced promoter methylation and relief of transcriptional repression, thereby facilitating PPARα transcription. As a key regulator of fatty acid β-oxidation, PPARα drives the expression of oxidative enzymes and supports mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Consequently, activation of the TET1–PPARα axis under EA was associated with improved mitochondrial function, reduced ROS accumulation, and preservation of mitochondrial ultrastructure. However, we acknowledge that the involvement of DNA methylation in this process is hypothesized based on existing literature and our observed molecular changes, rather than being directly demonstrated in this study. In contrast, TET1 deficiency or inhibition diminished PPARα induction and compromised oxidative metabolism, highlighting the potential role of TET1 in mediating EA’s effects. Furthermore, EA-associated restoration of oxidative/antioxidative balance may contribute to the reduction of hepatic inflammatory responses. Collectively, these findings suggest an epigenetic–metabolic mechanism by which EA may exert hepatoprotective effects in MASLD, providing a rationale for further translational and clinical validation of acupuncture-based interventions in metabolic liver diseases.
