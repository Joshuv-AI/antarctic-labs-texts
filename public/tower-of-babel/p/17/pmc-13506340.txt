Mutational profile and cardiovascular risk factors impact prognosis in triple-negative essential thrombocythemia
Authors: Gonzalo Carreño-Tarragona, Rodrigo Gil-Manso, Juan Carlos Hernández-Boluda, José Carlos Martínez-Ávila, Beatriz Bellosillo, Raúl Pérez-López, Adrián Segura, Francisca Ferrer-Marín, Eduardo Arellano-Rodrigo, Anna Angona, Valentín García-Gutiérrez, María Soledad Noya, Ángela Blanco, Lurdes Zamora, Miguel Navarro, Alicia Senín, Elena Magro, Irene Pastor-Galán, María Isabel Mata-Vázquez, María Luz Morales, Beatriz Cuevas, Patricia Vélez, Elvira Mora, Cristina Martínez-Bilbao, Rafael Colmenares, Juan Manuel Alonso, Manuel Pérez-Encinas, Gonzalo Caballero-Navarro, Miguel Ángel Cortés, Marta Santaliestra, José María Raya, Alberto Blanco-Sánchez, Jesús María Hernández-Rivas, Joaquín Martínez-López, Rosa Ayala, Alberto Álvarez-Larrán

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative essential thrombocythemia (TN-ET) represents a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. The aim of the present study was to identify prognostic factors useful for tailoring treatment. 241 TN-ET patients with myeloid panel sequencing and confirmatory bone marrow biopsy were selected. Pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants were identified in 19.5% of patients. Mutation carriers were older (median age 66 years vs. 53, p < 0.001) and had a higher frequency of prior thrombosis (19.6% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.013). Presence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants was associated with leukemic progression (HR 12.608; 95% CI: 2.616–60.775, p = 0.002) and lower overall survival (HR 3.008; 95% CI: 1.43–6.327, p = 0.004). ASXL1 (p = 0.004), CBL (p < 0.001), EZH2 (p < 0.001), and ZRSR2 (p < 0.001) mutations were associated with inferior leukemia-free survival. Age over 60 years, previous thrombosis, and cardiovascular risk factors were associated with higher thrombotic risk. Revised IPSET-thrombosis was useful for risk stratification (10-year probability of overall thrombosis: 30%, 15%, and 6% for high-, intermediate-, and very-low risk patients, respectively, p < 0.001). ARTS score refined arterial thrombosis stratification (10 years probability: 25% and 6% for high- and low-risk, respectively, p < 0.001). Progression to myelofibrosis was a rare event in this cohort (2.5%). These results highlight the biological and prognostic relevance of molecular profile in TN-ET.

Introduction

Triple-negative essential thrombocythemia (TN-ET) comprises the 10–15% of ET cases without JAK2, CALR, and MPL disease driver mutations and represents both a diagnostic and a therapeutic challenge [1–7].

It is generally considered an indolent disease with lower complications than its disease driver mutated-counterparts [5, 8]. This, together with the fact that majority of cases do not harbor any mutation and some TN-ET might be of polyclonal nature, has raised the doubt of the true nature of many of these cases [9–11]. In fact, polygenic inheritance might be behind many of these traits, all these making necessary a multidisciplinary approach in the diagnosis of TN-ET with a special support from histology [12–14].

This biological heterogeneity, along with the scarcity of this entity has prevented the standardization of prognostic scores and management of these patients [5, 8, 15]. Most of the cases fall into lower categories of the revised IPSET-thrombosis score, for which careful observation without treatment may be the recommended approach [5]. However, some patients suffer events, both vascular and progression to either myelofibrosis or acute leukemia. This makes the search of predictive factors warranted to select which patients could benefit from cytoreductive, antiplatelet or disease modifying therapy.

Molecular profiling of MPN has not only improved our knowledge on MPN but also our management of MPN patients [1, 8, 10, 16]. This includes prognostic scores in ET, but in the case of TN-ET it can also confirm the clonal nature of a thrombocytosis [3, 4, 17–20]. Furthermore, it allows the identification of both non-canonical variants of JAK2 and MPL genes (some of them constitutional) and low-frequency typical variants not identified by standardized methods [21, 22].

In this study, we utilized the Spanish Registry of Essential Thrombocythemia to analyze the clinical characteristics and molecular profile of a cohort of 241 TN-ET patients with available NGS data and confirmatory bone marrow biopsy, aiming to identify risk factors that could inform personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Patients

The Spanish Registry of Essential Thrombocythemia is a nationwide, non-interventional prospective study started in 2015 by the Spanish Group of Ph-negative Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (GEMFIN). ET diagnosis was established locally at the treating hospital, according to the prevailing World Health Organization (WHO) / International Consensus Classification (ICC) criteria at the time of diagnosis. Prefibrotic myelofibrosis was not allowed to be included in the Registry, and therefore no triple-negative prefibrotic myelofibrosis were included in this study. When ET diagnosis was previous to the inclusion in the registry, clinical data were collected retrospectively and prospectively updated for therapy changes and complications [23–25]. Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) ET diagnosis confirmed by compatible bone marrow biopsy findings (as considered by expert pathologist/hematologist and integrating WHO/ICC criteria), (2) NGS information or available sample for sequencing and (3) Absence of canonical mutations in JAK2, MPL, or CALR. A total of 241 patients were included, 136 with NGS panel information while 105 were sequenced at Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre as previously described [26]. Sequencing was performed either in bone marrow or blood samples. For molecular and histological integration, the new classification of thrombocytosis without JAK2, CALR or MPL mutation, proposed by Godfrey et al. [27] was used: Triple-negative ET with clonal marker for cases with pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants and classic ET megakaryocyte atypias (large forms, hyperlobated), Idiopathic thrombocytosis with atypical megakaryocytes (ITAM) for cases with ET megakaryocyte atypias but absence of mutations, Clonal thrombocytosis of undetermined significance (CTUS) for thrombocytosis without atypias but presence of clonality, and Idiopathic thrombocytosis of undetermined significance (ITUS) for cases without mutations and without megakaryocyte morphological alterations. The study was approved by Hospital del Mar (GEE-AAS-2015-01) and Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre Ethics Committees (21/378) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Genes and variants selection

Despite the use of different myeloid panels and sequencing platforms all centers included information of at least, the following genes: ASXL1, CALR, CBL, CEBPA, CSF3R, DNMT3A, ETV6, EZH2, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, JAK2, KIT, KRAS, MPL, NPM1, NRAS, RUNX1, SETBP1, SF3B1, SH2B3, SRSF2, TET2, TP53, U2AF1, WT1 and ZRSR2. Variants were locally classified as benign, likely benign, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely pathogenic or pathogenic following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association of Molecular Pathology (AMP) Recommendations [28]. Any reported variant, irrespective of its allele frequency, was considered for analysis. According to the genomic classification proposed by Grinfeld et al. [10], patients carrying pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations in TP53 or in chromatin/spliceosome genes (EZH2, IDH1, IDH2, ASXL1, GNAS, PHF6, CUX1, ZRSR2, SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, KRAS, NRAS, CBL, RUNX1, STAG2, or BCOR) were considered as high molecular risk (HMR) whereas the remaining were considered as low molecular risk (LMR). Accordingly, patients were stratified in three molecular subgroups: TN ET with high-risk mutations, TN ET with other pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations, and TN with no known pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations. Variants of unknown significance were not considered for risk stratification.

Statistical analysis

Mean and standard deviation (SD) were used for descriptive statistics of continuous variables, except for time-to-event variables or non-normal variables, in which median and interquartile range (IQR) was used. Absolute and relative frequencies and odds ratios were used to present categorical variables. Student t test or analysis of variance and χ2 or Fisher exact test were used to compare quantitative and categorical variables, respectively. Time-to-event analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier univariate analysis and a Cox proportional hazards model corrected for possible confounders in a multivariate analysis. Event analysis was performed using a logistic regression model corrected for possible confounders. The Cox or logistic model, as appropriate, was calculated under minimum AIC criteria using an initial model with all feasible explanatory variables, in a backward and forward algorithm until the model with minimum AIC was found [29]. Analyzed events included overall survival (OS), thrombosis-free survival (TFS), hemorrhage-free survival (HFS), myelofibrosis-free survival (MFS), acute myeloid leukemia-free survival (AMLFS) and event-free survival (events were death, thrombosis, hemorrhage, MF or AML). To control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) when performing multiple comparisons to identify significant genes, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied [30]. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Statistical Computing was performed using R under RStudio (www.R-project.org). R package survminer was used in time to event analysis (https://rpkgs.datanovia.com/survminer/index.html).

Results

Clinical presentation and treatment

In this cohort of 241 patients, median age at ET presentation was 57 years and 71.2% were female. Median hemoglobin level and platelet and white blood cells (WBC) counts were 136 g/L, 681 × 109/L and 8.3 × 109/L, respectively. At presentation, splenomegaly, pruritus and microvascular symptoms were reported in 4%, 6% and 18% of patients, respectively. Thrombosis and hemorrhage history was present in 9% and 2.9%, respectively. Cytoreduction was started at diagnosis or during follow-up in 69.6% of cases, antiplatelet therapy in 83%, and oral anticoagulation in 7.8%. Cytoreduced patients presented with more hypertension (41.3 vs. 19% p = 0.005) and seemed older (median age 61 vs 52 years, p = 0.058) than patients that didn’t received cytoreduction. No differences were found between patients receiving antiplatelet therapy or not.

Mutational profile of TN-ET patients

Median time from diagnosis to NGS testing was 7 years (0-30). All samples were obtained before progression to AML o MF. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were found in 47 patients (19.5%). The most frequently mutated genes were DNMT3A (11 cases), TET2 (9), ASXL1 (8), MPL (5), SH2B3 (5), CBL (4), JAK2 (2), EZH2 (2), PPM1D (2), TP53 (2), SF3B1 (2), and ZRSR2 (2). Other genes with pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were BCORL1 (1 case), CHEK2 (1), FLT3 (1), GNAS (1), IDH1 (1), KMT2C (1), KRAS (1), PTPN11 (1), SRSF2 (1), RAD21 (1), VHL (1). Median number of variants among carriers was 2 (range 1-6). Based on genomic risk profile, 20 (8.3%), 27 (11.2%), and 194 (80.5%) patients were classified as TN ET with HMR, TN ET with other pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutation, and TN ET with no known mutation, respectively. A list of identified variants (both VUS, likely pathogenic, and pathogenic) can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Clinical characteristics and outcomes according to the presence of pathogenic/likely-pathogenic variants are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1. Mutated patients were older at ET diagnosis (median age: 68 years vs. 54, p < 0.001) and had higher incidence of prior thrombosis (19.6% vs. 6.5%, p = 0.013) than unmutated patients. Cytoreductive therapy was most frequently indicated in mutated patients (86.4% versus 65.7% for mutated and unmutated cases, respectively, p = 0.013). Antiplatelet therapy was given in 95.3% versus 80.4% (p = 0.033) of mutated and unmutated patients, respectively. Thrombosis and bleeding

With a median follow-up of 7 years a total of 23 thrombotic events (arterial n = 19, venous n = 4) were registered resulting in a probability of thrombosis (arterial or venous) at 10 years of 11.5% in the whole series. Among clinical factors, age older than 60 years at diagnosis (p = 0.0058), previous thrombotic event at ET diagnosis (p < 0.001), and cardiovascular risk factors (p = 0.045), especially hypertension (p = 0.0011) were associated with a higher probability of thrombosis. About treatment, no differences were found between patients receiving or not cytoreductive or antiplatelet therapy at the time of thrombosis. Presence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants showed higher probability of thrombosis although differences were not statistically significant (10 years probability of 16.3 vs. 10.3% p = 0.088). No association was found between the presence of DNMT3A/TET2 mutations and arterial thrombosis.

On multivariable analysis, pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations were not associated (HR = 1.4, 95% CI (0.52–3.8), p = 0.498) with thrombosis risk after adjustment by age at diagnosis (HR = 1.4, 95% CI (0.56–4.3), p = 0.394), history of thrombosis (HR = 3.4 95% CI (1.12–10.1), p = 0.031) and hypertension (HR = 3.1, 95% CI (1.15-8.3), p = 0.026). (Supplementary Fig. 1). According to revised IPSET-thrombosis score, 21 (9%) cases with history of thrombosis were classified as high risk, 90 (39%) as intermediate-risk due to age > 60 years and, the remaining 119 (52%) as very low risk. The probability of thrombosis at 10 years was 30.3%, 14.7% and 5.8% for high- intermediate- and very -low-risk patients, respectively (p < 0.001, Fig. 2). Differences between high- and intermediate- and intermediate- and low-risk groups were also statistically significant. Finally, since most of thrombotic events were arterial (19, 82.6%), we evaluated the applicability of the ARterial Thrombosis Score (ARTS) [27, 31]. Sixty-five (26.7%) and 178 (73.3%) patients were classified as high risk and low risk respectively. The probability of arterial thrombosis at 10 years was 25.3% and 6% for high- and low-risk, respectively (p < 0.001, Fig. 2). We could not find any factor significantly associated with the risk of hemorrhage during follow-up.

Disease progression

Nine patients (3.7%) progressed to AML with a median time from ET diagnosis of 8.4 years, range (3.3-26.9). Probability of progression to AML was 4.2% at 10 years. Main clinical characteristics of patients progressing to AML are shown in Supplemental Table 3. Progression to AML was significantly higher in patients with pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations (15.2% vs. 1%, p < 0.001, unadjusted HR of 12.608, 95%CI, p = 0.002, Fig. 1). After multiple comparisons adjustment, pathogenic mutation in ASXL1 (p = 0.004), CBL (p < 0.001), EZH2 (p < 0.001) and ZRSR2 (p < 0.001) were associated with worse AMLFS (Supplementary Fig. 2). According with the genomic classification proposed by Grinfeld et al. Six patients (2.5%) progressed to myelofibrosis with a median time from ET diagnosis of 8.3 years, range (2.6-18.3). Probability of progression to MF was 2.7% at 10 years. Main clinical characteristics of patients progressing to MF are shown in Supplemental Table 4. Only one of them presented pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants. Two of them progressed to acute AML after a median of 4.4 years. We could not find any factor significantly associated with the risk of MF during follow-up.

Survival

Twenty-eight (11.6%) patients died. The probability of survival was 88.6 and 71.7% at 10 and 20 years after diagnosis, respectively. Patients carrying pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants showed lower survival than unmutated cases (81.5% versus 90.5%, respectively p = 0.0023, Fig. 1). Again, high-risk mutations were associated with lower survival, with a 10 year survival of 71.9%, 89.2% and 90.6%, respectively (p = 0.0063, Fig. 3). Age over 60 at diagnosis and previous thrombosis were associated with a lower probability of survival. On multivariate analysis, presence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations were associated with a higher risk of death (HR = 2.232, 95% CI (1–4.8) p = 0.042) after correction by age (HR = 5.892, 95% CI (2–17.4), p = 0.001) and history of thrombosis (HR = 2.958, 95% CI (1.3–6.7), p = 0.009) (Suppl. Fig. 3). High-risk mutations were also associated with survival on multivariate analysis (HR = 3.4, 95% CI (1.4–8.2), p = 0.007) although there was no statistical difference with other mutated patients (p = 0.145).

Molecular and histological integration

As the diagnosis of TN-ET needs a multidisciplinary approach and relies in a compatible histology we decided to integrate molecular and pathology data as suggested by Godfrey et al. [27]. For that, we reviewed 162 (67.2%) available bone marrow trephine biopsy reports and strictly differentiate cases with and without described atypical megakaryocytes (large forms, hyperlobated, typical of ET) irrespective of pathologist or hematologist conclusion. Seventy-three percent (118) of patients were classified as with atypical megakaryocytes. No differences were found between patients with or without atypical megakaryocytes, although all cases that progressed to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia presented with atypical megakaryocytes (Suppl. Table 5).

We then classified patients depending on megakaryocytes morphology and the presence or not of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants. As proposed by Godfrey et al. [27], patients with available data were classified as triple-negative ET with clonal marker n = 23 (14%), idiopathic thrombocytosis with atypical megakaryocytes (ITAM) n = 95 (59%), clonal thrombocytosis of undetermined significance (CTUS) n = 4 (2.5%), and Idiopathic thrombocytosis of undetermined significance (ITUS) n = 40 (25%). TN-ET with clonal marker were older, with a higher proportion of males and higher revised IPSET thrombosis scores (p < 0.001, Suppl. Table 6). Also, all high-risk mutations carriers were classified within the TN-ET with clonal marker group. No differences in outcomes or time to-event were found (Suppl. Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Effect of VUS and variants in disease driver genes

To study if variants of uncertain significance could play a role in TN-ET prognosis we analyzed VUS carriers (38, 9 of them carrying a JAK2/CALR/MPL variant, without any pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant) in comparison with mutated and not mutated patients. VUS patients presented similar clinical characteristics and outcomes than unmutated patients (Suppl. Table 7 and Suppl. Fig. 5), whereas mutated cases had worse OS and AMLFS.

Twenty cases (8.2%) presented either a non-canonical JAK2, CALR or MPL variant. For JAK2, 7 cases were identified with 4 different variants (L393V (2), E846D (2), R867Q (2), D873A). For CALR, 3 cases presented with 3 different variants (S189T, E380V, and E398_D400del). For MPL, 10 cases were identified with 7 different variants (F105L, V114M, S204P (2), S204F (3), D261N, W515_Q516del, and R592Q). As shown in Suppl. Table 1, only 1 (R867Q) JAK2 and 2 (S204P and S204F) MPL variants were classified as pathogenic. Most of variants presented an allele frequency within the germinal range (Suppl. Table 1) although this was not ruled out for patients in this cohort.

Only age was different between patients with or without mutations in JAK2, CALR or MPL (69 vs. 55 years old, p = 0.001) with no differences in main outcomes (i.e., 10% vs. 3,3%, p = 0.357 for progression to AML or 15 vs. 9%, p = 0.638 for thrombosis, Suppl. Table 8, Supplementary Fig. 6).

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to identify risk factors that could allow us to select which patients could benefit from an earlier intervention. Although TN-ET is generally considered an indolent form of ET [5, 32, 33], our findings indicate that molecular profiling combined with thrombotic risk stratification may identify a subset of patients at significant risk of developing thrombosis and leukemia transformation.

The main limitation of our study relies on the absence of a centralized pathology and molecular biology evaluation. Although more than 70% of the available histology reports strictly mentioned large/hyperlobated megakaryocytes, there is a chance that a minority of cases were misclassified idiopathic thrombocytosis. Nevertheless, it must be mentioned that our cohort presents similar characteristics as previously reported series with higher prevalence of females, lower complications in comparison with disease driver mutations carriers, and low revised IPSET scores [33]. Also, only 20% of our patients presented with pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations, consistent with previous studies. Therefore, we consider our cohort representative of the real-world setting where TN-ET cases comprise a diagnostic challenge.

Recently, a new classification of unexplained thrombocytosis has been proposed based on histology and clonality [27, 34]. All progression events in our cohort occurred in patients showing atypical megakaryocytes, highlighting the relevance of morphology in the sequencing era. Furthermore, patients with TN-ET with clonal markers, which represents 14% of our cohort, were older and presented with higher thrombosis scores, highlighting the biological and clinical differences with idiopathic thrombocytosis with atypical megakaryocytes (ITAM) a group involving 59% of the patients. We could not identify significant differences in the main clinical outcomes according to the groups proposed by Godfrey et al. [27], indicating that larger cohorts are needed to validate a different therapeutic approach according to this classification. In addition, only 23 (9.5%) patients presented thrombosis during follow-up, with most of them already receiving cytoreductive (78%) and antiplatelet therapy (83%), thus making us unable to find differences according to therapy.

Nevertheless, the most significant event in mutated patients was evolution to AML. This raises the question of whether TN-ET with clonal markers, especially when high-risk mutations are present, should be considered as an entity closer to the MDS/MPN continuum. Out of notice, these high-risk mutated patients were older and presented the highest rate of events, including thrombosis, although numbers were scarce to raise solid conclusions (Suppl. Table 2). All these findings highlight the importance of making a diagnostic review at a referral center in those cases with TN-ET with clonal markers as well as reassessment of the disease during follow-up, especially in cases of development of cytopenias or appearance of immature cells.

On the other hand, no effect on myelofibrosis progression was shown even when pathogenic variants were identified. It has been demonstrated that TN, JAK2-mutated, and CALR-mutated ET share a unique gene expression signature with an upregulation of platelet proliferation drivers and NFκβ pathway [35]. Whether this signature is more implicated in progression to MF than mutational profile remains to be elucidated.

In our cohort of TN-ET, the revised IPSET-thrombosis worked adequately [24, 25, 36]. However, only 9% of patients were classified as high risk, whereas the remaining 39% and 52% were classified as intermediate- or very-low risk, respectively. In this sense, ARTS score, which adds cardiovascular risks factors and DNMT3A/TET2 mutations to conventional risk factors such as age and history of thrombosis, discriminates between a low- and high-risk group comprising 73% and 27% of patients, respectively [31]. This better discrimination is probably due to the inclusion of cardiovascular-risk factors as the presence of mutation in DNMT3A/TET2 was not associated with a higher risk of thrombosis in our population. Therefore, both revised IPSET-thrombosis and ARTS might be used for deciding the indication of antiplatelet or cytoreductive therapy in TN ET. High-risk patients by both score systems were associated with a rate of thrombosis over 20% at 10 years, compatible with very high cardiovascular risk and reinforcing the need for more intensive treatment. The remaining patients, stratified as very-low risk or low-risk according to revised IPSET-thrombosis and ARTS, respectively, would be candidates for careful observation since the probability of thrombosis at 10 years was below 6%, a value within the range of the healthy population [37].

Finally, in our cohort, we found no differences between carriers of variants in disease driver genes (JAK2, CALR, MPL) and those who didn’t. Non-canonical variants are reported in 5 to 20% of cases of TN-ET, and the functional effect of many of them is unknown [21, 22]. Furthermore, most of them are suspected of germinal origin. Since molecular characterization of our patients lacked germinal studies, the interpretation of some VUS and specially of non-canonical variants in JAK2, CALR, and MPL was difficult. This prevented us of identifying some these cases as familial thrombocytosis or these variants as disease modifiers. Larger series and experimental work are needed to raise recommendations on non-canonical carriers [22, 38–42].

Heterogeneity in the design of the myeloid panels used by each of the centers, and different timing of sampling, with a median time from diagnosis of 7 years, might have influenced the probability of mutation detection which could have also been influenced by treatment. In addition, it is possible that some clonal cases of the present study might correspond to hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), especially those with TET2 or DNMT3A mutations. It must be pointed out in this regard that only 4% of our patients corresponded to clonal thrombocytosis of undetermined significance (CTUS), confirming that this is an infrequent situation in the present study.

In conclusion, our study highlights the value of myeloid panel sequencing in the case of triple-negative essential thrombocythemia, not only supporting bone marrow morphology for diagnostic purposes but also for prognostic and therapeutic decision-making. In addition, established thrombosis risk tools, such as the revised IPSET-thrombosis and the ARTS score, may help implement more personalized treatment approaches in this patient population. Nevertheless, larger cohorts will be needed to design specific risk models scores and to define the role of earlier cytoreductive and antiplatelet therapy in patients with cardiovascular risk factors or identifiable somatic mutations.
