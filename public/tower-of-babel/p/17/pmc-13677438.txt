Artificial intelligence powered decoding of Plasmodium falciparum sporozoite motility patterns
Authors: Chanel Milène Naar, Kateryna Pirkovets, Clarize M. de Korne, Roos van Schuijlenburg, Meta Roestenberg, Fijs WB van Leeuwen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sporozoites (SPZ), the infective mosquito stage of Plasmodium falciparum malaria, are widely studied as therapeutic targets for antibodies either endogenously induced by vaccines or administered as exogenous monoclonal antibody treatment. The unicellular protozoa are highly motile and adapted to migrate through tissues, facilitating the transition from mosquito-based skin inoculation to the infection of hepatocytes in the liver. Because of this, SPZ motility is considered a prime characteristic for SPZ viability, infectivity, and therapeutic response. To evaluate the quality of parasite batches reared in malaria insectaries and monitor innovative malaria therapies, a standardized tool is needed to rapidly assess sporozoite infectivity. Previously, we designed video-based motility analysis techniques (SMOOT) that yields a multi-metric kinematic output (9 metrics). Based on the SMOOT metrics acquired for 26,682 individual SPZ tracks (80% were used for training) we have now developed an Artificial Intelligence (AI) based motility index model (unsupervised) to easily capture nuances in sporozoite quality whilst preserving data depth. The AI-model was validated formally using common AI validation methods, such as reconstruction losses, stability and robustness evaluation. Furthermore, we applied data-driven analysis, using existing monoclonal antibody SPZ inhibition ranges, showing potential for quality control for P. falciparum SPZ batches, as well as documenting antibody inhibition response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61858-z.

Introduction

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium sporozoites (SPZ), the infective mosquito-borne parasite stage which is introduced into the human skin with the bite of an Anopheles mosquito. Typically, the small number of SPZ residing in inoculated saliva actively migrate to the liver of the host and ultimately establish an infection in hepatocytes that is ‘shielded’ from the environment1. In this skin-liver transition SPZ are ‘exposed’ thus providing a window of opportunity for antimalarial interventions. The recently approved RTS, S and R21 subunit vaccines are prime examples of vaccines that are aimed at targeting ‘exposed’ SPZ2; The vaccine induces endogenous antibody production against circumsporozoite protein (CSP), a surface protein of SPZ. The binding of these antibodies inhibits SPZ motility, which then renders the SPZ noninfective2. Unfortunately, antibodies induced after CSP-based vaccination have been estimated to have a biological half-life of 3–4 months, meaning vaccine efficacy wanes over time3,4. To offer short term protection, development of synthetically engineered (exogenous) antibodies that can be intravenously administered has been investigated5. To optimize these and new therapy candidates, preclinical research is done to assess the effect on SPZ in vitro and in vivo.

In laboratories across the globe, the performance of SPZ-directed anti-malaria interventions is commonly assessed using live SPZ. For these experiments SPZ are manually harvested from infected mosquitoes, reared in dedicated insectaries. Commonly, four different in vitro SPZ motility assays are used to provide functional screening of SPZ quality: circle counting in an image of gliding assays6, semi-automatic analysis of gliding images and video motility assays in ImageJ7–11, diffusion analysis in a pillar assay12, or detailed kinematic analysis with SMOOT over time13. Among these options SMOOT provides the most detailed output and depth of data necessary to find small differences between conditions. Yet, the data which is extracted from SMOOT exhibits considerable complexity.

The nine metrics extracted from SMOOT kinematic analysis are velocity, angular dispersion, straightness index, angle, acceleration, smoothness, normalized jerkiness index, curvature, and displacement. These parameters provide high dimensional data of complex movement trajectories13, which needs integration of all the different output metrics. In previous work, we reported the change in those metrics induced by: 1) radiation (in human skin)14, medium formulation13, inoculation routes (in mouse skin)15, antibody inhibition2, SPZ maturity16 and adjuvanted SPZ formulations17. We noted that the biologically relevant differences between SPZ conditions were highly complex, non-linear, and distributed across multiple interdependent kinematic parameters, making them difficult to resolve with conventional linear or low-dimensional analytical methods. Unsupervised dimensionality reduction approaches were subsequently explored to identify latent motility phenotypes, but this was not consistent across studies16,17. What is currently lacking is a framework that integrates the various motility metrics and allows for an easier interpretation of the multidimensional behavior of SPZ motility without losing depth. As neural network-based approaches are specifically suited to identify subtle, higher-order patterns and interactions within high-dimensional datasets that may otherwise remain undetectable using traditional clustering or dimensionality reduction techniques, we pursued this approach. When combined with large data sets Artificial Intelligence (AI) allows to extract integrated patterns from complex multidimensional data using either unsupervised and/or supervised learning18. The former is typically used to extract patterns and relationships that are difficult to capture with conventional methods. Specifically, autoencoders, which are a type of unsupervised AI models, can be used to decrease data dimensions and increase interpretability of latent variables in large data sets, while preserving essential information19,20.

In this study we set out to train an AI-autoencoder model to extract a fitness index from multi dimension SMOOT SPZ motility data of tens of thousands individual SPZs (Fig. 1). This fitness index was subsequently validated as a tool for SPZ quality assessment capturing nuanced behavioral differences and as a tool to monitor response to antibody therapy.

Results

Model training

Transforming multiparametric kinematic data into MIraw and categorized MItransformed

The full dataset consisted of 26,682 individual SPZ tracks analyzed in SMOOT, of which 80% was used to train the model (training set; 21,345 SPZ tracks; and the remaining 20% was used for validation (test set; 5,336 SPZ tracks). The AI-autoencoder was used to reduce the multiparametric kinematic data output from SMOOT into a single raw MIraw output (Fig. 2A). This (MIraw) helped distinguish between two distinct populations of SPZ: non-motile SPZ and those that are motile. Based on fitted gaussian distributions, a cutoff for separation of the two lies at −1.67 (Fig. 2B). This resulted in a classification of 49% of SPZ tracks as motile, e.g. above this threshold (MIraw between − 1.67 and 8.21, Formula 6; Fig. 3A), we have defined this percentage as MI+ motile%. The rest of SPZ tracks were classified as non-motile (MIraw between − 9.16 and − 1.67). Arctangent transformation to a 0–100 scale, while preserving rank order and compressing extreme values, yielded a MItransformed: MI− non-motile in the 0–17 range and MI+ motile in the 17–100 range. This transformation helped assign MI+ motile SPZ to one of four different motility categories, based on different distribution curves that could be estimated: category (1) range 17–45, category (2) range 45–64, category (3) range 64–79, and category (4) range 79–96 (Fig. 2C). Hereby the SPZ in category 1 display the lowest MItransformed and those in category 4 the highest. Assignment of the overall training set to these categories converted to: 2,268 tracks of category 1 (9%), 1,695 of category 2 (6%), 3,148 of category 3 (12%), and 5,984 of category 4 (22%; see Fig. 3A). These findings indicate that category 4 is most dominant.

We consequently developed the Fitness index (FI), in which both parameters are multiplied (formula 8).

Motility characteristics in different experimental conditions

Each SPZ track in SMOOT corresponds to a SPZ batch that falls under one of the 26 experimental conditions included (for description of the conditions see Supplementary Table 1). When comparing the training-set output of these conditions, it becomes apparent that MI+ motile% derived from MIraw, the MItransformed categories, and the fitness index (FI) vary between conditions (Fig. 3B-D). The FI adds the context of the MItransformed categories to MI+ motile% thus leading to a combined score of highly motile SPZ in the sample describing sample fitness. Condition 19 represents ‘normal’ SPZ (control) that have not undergone any form of treatment, and whereby SPZ have previously been shown to infect hepatocytes in vitro2,16,17. This control group contains a high MI+ motile% (58%) and almost half (47%) of them could be assigned to category 4. Interestingly, conditions 15–18 and 20, all representing mild therapeutic condition, align with condition 19 based on MI+ motile% (Fig. 3B). MItransformed categories do vary for all conditions, while the differences are minimal for conditions 17–22 (Fig. 3C). This impacted the FI in a way that conditions 20–22 now all end up having a very similar FI score as 19 (Fig. 3D).

When condition yielded stronger effects on the SPZ, all metrics decreased. This adds the context of SPZ motility as therapeutic measure. Interestingly, effects on MItransformed categories vary across conditions. For example, under condition 5 most category 3 and 4 SPZ have been disseminated when compared to the surrounding conditions (Fig. 3B). The MI+ motile%, however, remains higher for condition 5 (Fig. 3D). The FI score nevertheless describes the amount of fit highly motile SPZ, instead of all motile.

Condition 1 represents 50 µg/mL of 3SP2, which is the highest concentration measured of this monoclonal antibody (negative-control). At this condition the MI+ motile% is 4 (Fig. 3B), all of which belong to MItransformed category 1 (Fig. 3C) and show a FI of 0 (Fig. 3D).

To test whether the output of the AI models, MI+ motile%, category 3 and 4 SPZ or FI are a useful addition to SMOOT, we also looked at the original SMOOT output parameters and performed unsupervised dimensionality reduction approaches. But on their own none of these single outputs reflects different categories of SPZ motility (Supplementary Fig. 1, and 2 A). In addition, neither t-SNE (Supplementary Fig. 2B) nor PCA resulted in clear separation of SPZ clusters, with PCA additionally capturing only a limited proportion of the overall variance (52.1% for PC1 and 18.7% for PC2, Supplementary Fig. 2 C). Importantly, deriving a composite motility formula from PCA loadings also failed to detect differences between experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

Model validation

Formal model validation

Validation revealed that FI distributions across individual movies for training and test sets are closely matched across experimental conditions (Fig. 4A). The model achieved comparable reconstruction losses, defined as differences between predicted and ground truth values (MSE loss), for the training and testing sets (Train MSE = 0.54; Test MSE = 0.59; Fig. 4B), also indicating good generalization and absence of overfitting. The stability of the latent representation was evaluated by retraining the model with multiple random initializations (seeds), yielding correlations between motility indices ranging from r = 0.2 to 1 (Fig. 4C, D). Importantly, the median of these seed correlations is 0.48, showing the model is robust. Robustness was further supported by the observation that the distributions of latent values in the training and testing subsets are statistically indistinguishable (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p = 0.39). Sensitivity analysis, performed by adding Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01) to the input features, resulted in an average change of only 0.01 in the MIraw, confirming resistance to minor perturbations. Finally, clustering analyses revealed that the latent space exhibited substantially higher separability (Silhouette score = 0.7) compared to the original feature space (0.4), demonstrating that the AI model effectively captured the discriminative structure of motility in the data.

Possibility for QC of normal SPZ – sensitivity validation

The positive control SPZ (condition 19) had 53% MI+ motile% that combined with the distribution of SPZ categories, converting to a FI of 38 (See Supplementary Fig 3). Specifically, the normal SPZ condition has 16% of category 1 SPZ, 12% category 2 SPZ, 27% of category 3 SPZ, and 43% category 4 SPZ (See Supplementary Fig 3). In the positive control unattenuated SPZ (condition 19 in Figs 2-4), which included 15 batches of SPZ, we noted significant variability in MI+ motile% SPZ (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.05, Fig. 5A), percentage of category 1 SPZ (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.05, Fig. 5B), percentage of category 2 SPZ (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.05, Fig. 5C), percentage of category 3 SPZ (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.001, Fig. 5D), percentage of category 4 SPZ (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.001, Fig. 5E), and FI (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.01, Fig. 5F).

Assessing response to antibody therapy

Using the new SMOOT outputs were able to define a dose specific response to a known therapeutic monoclonal antibody against the NANP repeat region of CSP 2A10 (conditions 3, 4, 6, 7, 11, 16, 18, and 21)21,22. Figure 6C indicates that 2A10 exhibits an IC50 of 4.0 µg/mL, which could be estimated based on the FI. Another monoclonal antibody targeting the central repeat region of CSP, 3SP2 (conditions 1, 2, 13, 15, 17, 20, and 22)23 yielded a different dose-specific response (Fig. 6D, E) with an IC50 of 9.9 µg/mL based on the FI. These results indicate that 2A10 is twice as potent as 3SP2, which is in line with previous estimates using SPZ velocity only (of 3.6 µg/mL and 10.6 µg/mL, respectively)2.

The new AI-based composite outputs now allows us to study the difference in responses to antibody treatments in more detail, making specific functional effects apparent. First, at high concentrations > 25 µg/mL of 3SP2 the MI+ motile% SPZ is lower than for 2A10 (conditions 1, 2 and 4, 7; 4% and 4%, 15% and 24%, respectively for both antibodies at 25 µg/mL and 50 µg/mL). Second, at these higher concentrations 3SP2 wiped out all MItransformed category 4 SPZ, an effect that could not be achieved using 2A10 (Fig. 6A, D). This is confirmed by the visual output of the SPZ motility movies (Fig. 7A). Third, the therapeutic effect varies across MItransformed categories. 2A10 yielded a similar IC50 as obtained with the FI for the percentage category 3 SPZ (IC50 = 4.1 µg/mL), and category 4 (IC50 = 5.2 µg/mL; Fig. 6A-C), the categories 1 and 2 did not allow proper curve fitting. For 3SP2 only category 4 allowed curve fitting (IC50 = 10.4 µg/mL; Fig. 6D, E).

Biological validation of motility index with infectivity measures of SPZ

As motility is hypothesized to influence the parasites infectivity in vitro, we studied this aspect in HC-04.j7 hepatocytes. The MI+ motile% did not significantly correlate to infectivity as measured by PCR (Pearson correlation coefficient r=−0.69, p = 0.086, Fig. 7C), but FI did (Pearson correlation coefficient r=−0.77, p = 0.044, Fig. 7F). Specifically, both the percentage category 3 and 4 SPZ correlated significantly (r=−0.82, p = 0.024 and r =−0.85, p = 0.017, respectively; Fig. 7D, E). Based on these results, it is suggested that both category 3 and 4 provide valuable insights into motility dynamics and potentially SPZ infectivity and therefore FI, which combines both, might be a more relevant final output than MI+ motile% in this small dataset.

Discussion

AI-autoencoding allowed us to collapse multi-modal SMOOT metrics into a SPZ FI output that reveals latent behavioral patterns not captured by a single metric. Complementary to this, the categorical motility assignment, based on their MItransformed, helped provide more insights in the heterogeneity of the SPZ batches. Following training (80% of the data set) we were able to validate the AI-autoencoder with a test set (remaining 20%) and demonstrate its potential for QC and antibody response monitoring in a small pilot dataset. This analysis allows us to capture nuanced differences in SPZ motility categories among non or only slightly inhibited SPZ.

In pharma, AI is increasingly being used to support the extraction of unique new insights from complex multi-modal biomedical data24. Such strategies are being used to drive target discovery and drug design, among others25,26. A key challenge for implementing AI is the need for high-quality training data27. Obtaining such data can be especially challenging for neglected diseases and experimental therapeutic therapies. To complicate matters further, in malaria, the infective SPZ stage of the disease can both be considered a drug (live attenuated vaccine) and a therapeutic target. Thus, out-of-the-box strategies are required. Because SPZ motility has been directly linked to infectivity28, we, and others, consider SPZ motility as critical performance indicator for vaccine utility and response to therapy. By using our in house developed SMOOT kinematic analysis of SPZ for a period of > 7 years2,13–17, we have been able to generate a high-fidelity data set that incorporates SPZ motility under 26 different experimental conditions (See Fig. 3). In total we analyzed 26,682 SPZ tracks that show complex interrelations between 9 kinematic metrics. Compared to other motility assessments, this high-complexity assessment helps to overcome critical data-scarcity issues. By using an AI-autoencoder strategy we have been able to combine multiple metric descriptors for SPZ motility into a simplified scoring system which includes MI+ motile%, %category 3 + 4 SPZ and FI. Because FI includes both MI+ motile% and %category 3 + 4 SPZ, we believe this to be the most balanced metric, however, whether this consistently translates in a better prediction of infectivity remains to be investigated. In the current study, MI+ motile%, %category 3 + 4 SPZ and FI seemed to perform equally well. As compared with the raw data output of SMOOT, however this approach shows how unsupervised data-driven approaches can capture nuanced SPZ motility phenotypes. The value of these findings is substantiated by the weight of the large dataset that can be used for reference. Herein, standardized reference conditions such as normal SPZ (condition 19), non-motile controls (condition 5 and condition 1), as well as variation between batches have supported model assessment. Using input data extracted from the video analysis of live SPZ and hepatocyte infection studies for validation, the data-driven AI-scoring remains controllable and connected to real-life biological conditions.

Given the manual dissection of SPZ from infected mosquito’s reared in a laboratory setting, an inherent variability between batches can be expected. Previous reports indicate an intrinsic transcriptional heterogeneity can occur within genetically homogeneous SPZ batches29. Other studies support this by indicating that heterogeneity can also occur in metabolic activity as presented by the SPZ single mitochondrial membrane potential17. In malaria research, this batch heterogeneity may influence the interpretation of data and in the context of experimental live attenuated SPZ vaccines30–34, it may also influence vaccine efficacy. The need for SPZ QC convents into a need for objective, quantitative criteria that can be consistently applied across batches and experimental conditions.

In addition to QC, there is strong need for assays that help define how effective and how long antibodies effectively inhibit SPZ. These assays can provide a quantitative means to monitor the clinical impact of e.g. RTS, S and experimental vaccines in terms of inhibiting the SPZ stage of the infection. Our initial validation studies demonstrate that AI-autoencoding can establish dose response relationships for the mAbs 2A10 and 3SP2 and can be used to derive IC50 values (Fig. 6). Uniquely, the antibody response in terms of the FI, percentage category 3 and 4 SPZ are correlated to the SPZ infectivity of hepatocyte cultures in vitro even in a small dataset (Fig. 7). While in vitro analyses cannot fully predict in vivo protection, they provide an important step towards a reproducible, quantitative framework to guide early-stage screening of antibody candidates35,36. They also open the possibility to document the functional effect of antibody titers in volunteers after vaccination and their durability. While antibody titers are commonly reported following clinical trials30–34, the effect of these measures on SPZ motility have not yet been defined. The response monitoring data obtained with the AI-autoencoder also helps establish a base for druggable targets. For example, our data indicates that targeting of the category 3 and 4 SPZ within a SPZ batch may be most useful as these SPZ seem to be most infective based on in vitro data. Future studies will reveal if the endogenous antibody responses induced in volunteers inoculated with an experimental SPZ vaccine34 affect these targets.

In this work we have limited our analysis to in vitro SPZ SMOOT assays, which constitute to a two dimensional (2D) environment. Theoretically, however, SPZ motility analysis can be extended to a 3D in vivo environment. Various studies have reported on SPZ motility in mouse skin12,37–39. Some studies with excised human or mouse skin have used SMOOT14,15,40. Here unfortunately photo-physics dictate that only superficial SPZ trajectories can be accurately analyzed14. Combined with the well-known tissue heterogeneity it thus will be challenging to generalize behavior across batches and conditions. But most critically the amount of available in vivo SMOOT data needs to be expanded substantially before being able to train an AI-algorithm in 3D substrates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have successfully used AI to decode SPZ motility patterns. These data create a fundament for future standardized SPZ quality control and precise understanding of anti-SPZ antibody effects, specifically in conditions with only limited inhibition this novel analysis provides more insights than previous work. Furthermore, it illustrates the potential power of implementing AI in complex drug-discovery research for neglected diseases.

Methods

Production of SPZ

Plasmodium falciparum NF54 SPZ were obtained by infecting Anopheles stephensi female mosquitoes (line Nijmegen SDA500, originating from Pakistan) by feeding them gametocyte blood cultures by standard membrane feeding as described previously. Transgenic fluorescent green SPZ expressed the fluorescent reporter green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the circumsporozoite (CSP) promotor (NF54 − ΔPf47–5’csp-GFP-Luc, kindly provided by Dr. Koen Dechering; TropIQ Health Sciences). Transgenic fluorescent red SPZ expressed the fluorescent reporter protein mCherry under the Sui1 promotor (line Pf-Exp. 159 as described by Miyazaki et al.41. 14–16 days post blood meal the salivary glands of the infected mosquitoes were harvested via manual dissection. Salivary glands were homogenized to release the SPZ in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI; Thermo Fisher Scientific) enriched with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The SPZ were then counted with a Bürker counting chamber and a phase-contrast microscope, whereafter they were used for experiments immediately.

Database - experimental conditions

This retrospective study integrated all historical SPZ motility movies from our research group that assessed SPZ motility on days 14–16 using a standardized protocol across diverse experimental conditions, as reported previously (Fig. 1)2,16,17. Individual SPZ tracks from historic experiments were used to build up the database. Experimental conditions that were included are an ethanol concentration range, no Fetal Calf Serum (FCS) non motile controls17, adjuvanted SPZ (with either TLR7 agonist CL307 or NOD2 agonist MDP which were attached to SPZ using a Cy5 bridge)17, old SPZ (day 20)16, and 2A10 and 3SP2 monoclonal antibody concentration ranges2. Specific experimental details for all 26 conditions are provided in the SI section.

SPZ motility assay

The motility assay was performed using a standardized protocol2,16,17. In short, SPZ harvested from mosquito glands were diluted to 15,000 SPZ per well in a NUNC 96 wells optical glass bottom plate (ThermoFisher Scientific). Incubation of with experimental compounds or agents with SPZ is then done for 30 min at room temperature in RPMI + 10% FCS, whereafter the sample was immediately centrifuged for 3 min at 1.200 RPM at room temperature. Directly after centrifugation 200 frames were measured by Leica TCS SP5 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 using the 488 laser to excite GFP and emission detection was set to 500–570 nm or the 561 nm laser to excite mCherry with emission detection at 600–650 nm or the 633 nm laser to excite Cy5 with emission detection at 650–700 nm. Each condition was measured in duplo per well and in three independent experiments, resulting thus in 6 movies per condition.

Video-processing

The movies were further processed using the most updated version of the in-house produced SPZ motility orienting and organizing tool (SMOOT) software to ensure no differences occurred because of the version used17, which segments out individual SPZ from each frame of the movies based on their fluorescence signal, size and shape. The median pixel locations of segmented SPZ visible on different frames could be connected into full SPZ tracks to analyze their movement in the movies. For each tracked SPZ, SMOOT provided a series of 9 kinematic metrics characterizing its motion dynamics: angular dispersion, straightness index, mean velocity, mean angle, acceleration, smoothness, normalized jerkiness index (nnd), curvature, and displacement. Rows containing missing curvature values were excluded to ensure the AI model input compliance. Each metric was z-score scaled using the mean and standard deviation of the training subset only.

Artificial intelligence model architecture and training

An AI-autoencoder, was implemented in PyTorch (2.8.0) and consisted of a symmetric feedforward network with an encoder (input dimension → 4 → 1) and decoder (1 → 4 → input dimension). Rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations were used in all hidden layers to allow the network to model nonlinear relationships in the input data. The bottleneck neuron enforced dimensionality reduction, yielding a one-dimensional latent representation that was interpreted as a motility index. The network was optimized using the Adam algorithm (learning rate = 0.01) to minimize the mean squared reconstruction error (MSE) between input and reconstructed features over 200 iterations (epochs). Training was performed exclusively on the training set (80% of the included individual SPZ tracks), with fixed random seeds to ensure reproducibility. The model was then applied to the new data, the remaining 20% of the data (test subset).

SPZ hepatocyte infection assay

SPZ infectivity of the matched 2A10 and 3SP2 monoclonal antibody concentration ranges, as measured by in vitro hepatocyte infection with PCR, were also included in the database. Infectivity of SPZ was assessed using in vitro infection of hepatocytes as previously described2. In short, 40,000 HC-04.j7 hepatocytes (kindly provided by Dr. Rhoel Dinglasan) were seeded in a NUNC 96 wells optical glass bottom plate (ThermoFisher Scientific), which was first coated with 50 µg/mL collagen type I (Corning, USA) for 30 min at RT. After overnight incubation at 37 °C + 5% CO2, the medium was refreshed with infection medium: 100 µL DMEM + 5% FCS supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 15 mM glucose (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 0.01 mg/mL insulin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). SPZ were then dissected and incubated with compounds if necessary for 30 min at RT. SPZ were added at 60,000 SPZ per well in the infection medium. The plate was centrifuged for 3 min at 1200 RPM and incubated at 37 °C + 5% CO2. Output was PCR-based after 4 days, for which the cells were lysed, RNA was isolated, and cDNA was generated to determine 18 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) levels.

Statistics

For model validation the following tests were used; stability of the latent representation was evaluated by retraining the model with multiple random initializations (seeds), distribution of latent variables comparison using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, sensitivity analysis by adding Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01) to the input features, and clustering analysis evaluating Silhouette scores. All these tests were performed in Python 3.12.12.

To evaluate the differences between SPZ groups, we performed a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test using scipy library (1.16.3) in Python. This test was selected because the data did not meet the assumptions of normality required for parametric ANOVA. When a significant overall effect was detected, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were conducted. To enable statistical comparisons across antibody concentrations, the analysis pipeline operated on individual movie-level measurements rather than condition-level summary values; all per-movie values within each condition were used for statistical testing and boxplot visualization.

IC50 values for antibody inhibition were calculated using a non-linear regression analysis in GraphPad (10.6.1).
