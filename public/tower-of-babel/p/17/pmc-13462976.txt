Automated detection and fissure width quantification of ground fissures using FastICA-enhanced distributed fiber optic sensing
Authors: Shijia Mei, Bin Shi, Yuehua Jiang, Jingwen Su, Zhengyang Jia, Hai Yang, Zi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing impact of ground fissures on urban infrastructure demands monitoring approaches that not only provide high spatial resolution but also allow robust and automated interpretation across heterogeneous sensing systems. However, interpreting long-term distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) data remains challenging, particularly when different cable types respond to the same deformation processes with comparable spatial patterns but substantially different measurement scales. In this study, a DFOS-based monitoring framework integrating fast independent component analysis (FastICA) with a segmented integration strategy is proposed to enable automated detection of fissure-related strain anomalies and quantitative estimation of fissure width. Continuous observations were conducted using simultaneous measurements from a metal-reinforced strain cable (C1) and a micro-anchored fiber optic cable (C2) deployed across three active ground fissures. The proposed method successfully localized all active fissure zones and identified their initiation timing and temporal evolution. Results show that both cable types consistently detect the same fissure locations and temporal deformation features. The C1 cable exhibits reliable sensitivity to fissure occurrence across all monitored sites but yields fissure width estimates with limited quantitative accuracy. In contrast, the C2 cable enables robust reconstruction of the spatiotemporal evolution of the main fissure width, which shows strong agreement with differential settlement derived from interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) observations, with correlation coefficients reaching up to 0.97. For fissures wider than 10 mm, the temporal responses of the two cable types are highly correlated (0.88–0.94), indicating stable identification of dominant deformation events despite pronounced amplitude discrepancies. Overall, the proposed DFOS–FastICA framework facilitates automated, real-time tracking of ground fissure dynamics while explicitly accounting for cable-dependent response characteristics. The results highlight the complementary roles of different DFOS cable designs and demonstrate the potential of multi-cable integration for reliable urban geological hazard assessment and infrastructure risk management.Keywords: DFOS, FastICA, Fissure width, Geological hazard, InSARSubject terms: Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Ground fissures are surface-penetrating fractures extending from the ground surface into the subsurface and are widely recognized as a major geological hazard threatening infrastructure safety1. Their initiation and propagation are strongly associated with geologically weak zones and areas experiencing differential land subsidence, particularly under the combined influence of natural factors–such as seismic stress and groundwater-level fluctuations–and human activities, including excessive groundwater extraction, mining-induced subsidence, and urban load redistribution2–7.

Subsidence-induced ground fissures have been reported worldwide, notably in the Fenwei Basin (China), Arizona (USA), Najran (Saudi Arabia), as well as parts of Mexico and Iran8–12. These fissures can extend for several kilometers, reach widths of several meters, and penetrate to depths of tens of meters; in high-risk regions, their cumulative number may exceed one thousand13. As a result, subsidence-induced ground fissures pose severe and often latent threats to buildings, transportation networks, and buried utilities, substantially increasing remediation costs and the risk of catastrophic engineering failure1,2,13,14.

Owing to the abrupt and concealed nature of ground fissure hazards, proactive monitoring and high-risk zone identification are essential to inform governmental strategies for disaster mitigation and structural reinforcement2,3. Ground fissures typically manifest as separation or displacement of adjacent soil masses; thus, precise measurement of ground displacement represents the most direct and effective means of characterizing fissure geometry and evolution15. However, existing monitoring techniques exhibit inherent limitations: visual inspection is constrained by operator subjectivity, resulting in restricted accuracy and timeliness; leveling surveys, while offering sub-millimeter precision, entail high implementation costs and offer limited spatiotemporal resolution16; GPS/GNSS methods are susceptible to multipath effects and sparse spatial sampling, thereby impeding comprehensive detection of fissure networks17,18; terrestrial laser scanning provides high-resolution point clouds, yet faces challenges related to large-scale data processing and scene occlusion19; and interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) techniques are prone to atmospheric delays and vegetation cover, thereby increasing phase unwrapping errors7,20.

With the advancement of optical fiber sensing technology, distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) has demonstrated significant advantages in applications such as landslide detection21, ground subsidence monitoring22–24, and mining activity surveillance25,26. This technique enables real-time acquisition of continuous strain data27, thereby facilitating high-resolution, dynamic monitoring of geohazard evolution and enhancing the early detection of incipient disaster signals. However, two major challenges persist in data analysis. First, strain anomalies associated with extreme geological events are often masked by the accumulation of high-dimensional redundant information and environmental noise inherent to long-term monitoring28. Second, existing data-processing workflows continue to rely on expert judgment grounded in domain-specific knowledge, while robust, transferable, and quantitatively grounded diagnostic paradigms remain largely unexplored29.

In recent years, fast independent component analysis (FastICA) has achieved significant advances in the decoupling of complex system signals30. In contrast to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which relies solely on second-order statistics and employs orthogonal linear transformations for dimensionality reduction, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) utilizes higher-order statistics and maximizes signal non-Gaussianity, thereby enabling blind source separation without prior knowledge of the mixing matrix. This approach overcomes the theoretical limitations of conventional methods in decoupling non-Gaussian linear mixtures31. The application of ICA has demonstrated success in the interpretation of InSAR data and has provided a novel mathematical foundation for nonlinear feature extraction in geological hazard monitoring32,33. Building on this foundation, the present study introduces the FastICA algorithm into DFOS for ground fissure monitoring. By effectively separating and extracting non-Gaussian anomalous components from monitoring sequences, this method substantially enhances the sensitivity and accuracy of detecting sudden geological events.

This study focuses on the geologically high-risk region of southern Jiangsu in the Taihu Lake Basin34 (Fig.1). Owing to prolonged and excessive groundwater extraction (with a peak volume of ), the area experienced a land subsidence rate of up to 26 mm per year during 2002–200335. The substantial thickness of Quaternary unconsolidated sediments (60–140 m), combined with pronounced bedrock undulation, resulted in significant differential subsidence. This process led to the development of 20 NE–SW-trending ground fissures, each ranging from 100 to 2000 meters in length5,19. These ground fissures have induced structural damage, such as building cracking and road dislocation. Notably, the spatial distribution of the fissures closely corresponds to abrupt changes in bedrock depth, indicating a direct genetic relationship36. Consequently, this region provides an ideal natural laboratory for the evaluation and validation of ground fissure monitoring technologies.

To overcome the aforementioned technical obstacles, this study proposes a real-time dynamic monitoring framework for ground fissures based on DFOS followed by the FastICA. The principal innovations are as follows: (1) For the first time, the FastICA algorithm is integrated into the processing of long-term optical fiber strain time series, significantly enhancing the sensitivity and accuracy of abrupt geohazard detection; (2) A piecewise integration method is employed to quantify fissure width, facilitating a systematic comparison of two types of optical cables in terms of detection performance and data consistency.

Methods

Sensing fiber optic cable deployment

The sensing system employed a dual-cable cooperative monitoring scheme, comprising a metal-reinforced strain cable (ø5 mm, E = 5.4 GPa, effective stiffness 106 kN) with robust structural integrity to ensure effective fiber protection (C1), and a microanchored fiber optic cable (ø5 mm, E = 1.9 GPa, effective stiffness 37 kN) anchored mechanically at 2 m intervals to facilitate efficient strain transfer from the soil and enable precise measurement of fissure deformation, achieving a strain transfer efficiency of up to 99.98 % (C2)37,38. The two cables were selected to represent typical configurations used in field DFOS practice, reflecting realistic deployment conditions. Data acquisition from both cable types was implemented in real time using a distributed optical fiber interrogator, operating at a sampling frequency of 50 MHz and a spatial resolution of 0.05 m (Fig. 2).

Structural designs and mechanical characteristics of the two fiber optic cables used for DFOS monitoring. a Metal-reinforced strain cable (ø5 mm, E = 5.4 GPa, effective stiffness = 106 kN), designed with multiple metal reinforcers to ensure high structural integrity and robust fiber protection (C1). b Microanchored fiber optic cable (ø5 mm, E = 1.9 GPa, effective stiffness = 37 kN), incorporating mechanical anchoring points spaced at 2 m intervals to enhance strain transfer efficiency (up to 99.98 %) and enable precise fissure-deformation measurement (C2).

The deployment of the monitoring line was conducted as follows: (1) The orientation of the principal surface rupture was determined based on the distribution of ground fissures and the pattern of structural damage; (2) A 180-meter monitoring line was installed perpendicular to the rupture, with an effective sensing segment of 170 meters (the initial 10 meters serving as the lead-in section). Due to site constraints, the angle between the monitoring line and the rupture was approximately 73.8°; (3) The trench, with a depth of 1 meter, was backfilled using a layered vibratory compaction technique to ensure optimal coupling between the fiber optic cable and the surrounding soil; (4) As the optical fiber is only capable of tensile strain measurement, an initial tensile strain of 1200 ± 300 was pre-applied to the cable to establish the measurement baseline.

InSAR time-series processing

Given that the study area is predominantly characterized by farmland and villages, the Small Baseline Subset (SBAS) method of InSAR was selected over Persistent Scatterer (PS)-based approaches, as it provides more reliable deformation estimates under conditions of limited coherent scatterer density41. Sentinel-1A C-band data acquired in Interferometric Wide (IW) swath mode using the Terrain Observation with Progressive Scans (TOPS) technique were employed. The area of interest was defined and acquisition scenes were filtered within the Alaska Satellite Facility (ASF) data portal; all available ascending Sentinel-1A scenes spanning June 2017 to December 2019 (Track 171) were selected and downloaded for subsequent processing. Topographic information was derived from the ALOS World 3D 30 m DEM (AW3D30), and ESA’s Precise Orbit Ephemerides (POEORB) were applied to refine satellite orbital parameters. The SBAS time-series InSAR processing was performed with a multi-look factor of 2 (range) × 6 (azimuth), yielding an effective spatial resolution of approximately 30 m. The resulting time-series products were exported to QGIS for further spatial analysis.

Quantification of fissure width

Figure 9 shows time-series fissure width measurements from 2013 to 2019 at three monitoring sites obtained using dual-cable DFOS. The main result is a clear deformation-dependent difference in measurement magnitude between the two cables, while their temporal agreement mainly supports the reliability of these differences. The measurement scales of the two cables diverge across the three fissures. At F1 (Fig. 9a), both C1 and C2 recorded small and gradual deformation of 4 mm with similar amplitudes. At F3 (Fig. 9c), an abrupt deformation event around 2014 produced fissure widths of about 4 mm for C1 and 12 mm for C2, showing a moderate scale difference. The largest disparity occurs at F2 (Fig. 9b), where C2 recorded a cumulative deformation of approximately 60 mm, nearly ten times larger than that measured by C1 ( 4 mm). Despite these substantial differences in measured amplitudes, both cables consistently captured the same temporal deformation features, including gradual trends and synchronous abrupt events. Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.547 (F1), 0.888 (F2), and 0.941 (F3) indicate moderate to strong temporal agreement across the three fissures, suggesting that the observed scale differences are systematic and likely associated with cable-dependent response characteristics.

Spatial pattern of InSAR deformation

SBAS-InSAR was employed in preference to PS-based methods, as it generally provides more reliable deformation estimates in suburban and rural areas where the density of coherent scatterers is insufficient for persistent scatterer analysis41. Figure 10 presents the mean line-of-sight (LOS) deformation velocity over the monitoring period. Negative values (red to pink) indicate surface movement away from the satellite, interpreted here as subsidence, whereas positive values (light to dark blue) represent uplift. Given the flat topography of the plain and negligible horizontal displacements, the LOS velocity is approximated as the vertical deformation rate. The DFOS monitoring site (triangle marker) is also indicated for subsequent comparison.

The deformation map reveals pronounced spatial heterogeneity in surface displacement. A prominent subsidence bowl is observed in the east-central portion of the study area, where LOS rates reach −15.8 to −10 mm/yr, constituting the most intense deformation center within the region. Additional moderate subsidence zones (−10 to −5 mm/yr) are scattered across the south-western and central sectors, each exhibiting abrupt velocity gradients at their boundaries with the surrounding stable areas. Notably, the red dashed line delineating the ground fissure trace identified by DFOS monitoring coincides spatially with the zone of maximum deformation gradient—specifically, the transitional boundary between the strongly subsiding domain to the east and the near-stable domain to the west. This spatial correspondence indicates that differential displacement induced by non-uniform regional subsidence constitutes the primary mechanical driver of ongoing fissure propagation. The SBAS-InSAR results thus provide independent, macro-scale corroboration of the tectonic–geodynamic context within which the ground fissure has developed.

Discussion

Performance comparison between PCA and ICA

To further validate the effectiveness of ICA in ground fissure identification, PCA was introduced as a comparative method. PCA was implemented in MATLAB using the built-in pca function based on variance maximization42, with results illustrated in Fig.11.

Principal components(PC1–PC4) of ground fissures detected by C1 (a) and C2 (b) cables.

In terms of identification performance, as shown in Figs. 12a and 13a, both PCA and ICA successfully detected three ground fissures in profile C1, suggesting comparable detection capability when fissure signals are of relatively similar amplitude. However, in profile C2, although PC1 exhibits a single dominant peak with significantly higher amplitude compared to the other components, the remaining two fissure signals are suppressed and indistinguishable in each component (Fig. 12b), whereas ICA successfully detected all three fissures across IC1 to IC4 (Fig. 13b). This discrepancy stems from the fundamentally different decomposition mechanisms of the two methods. PCA seeks to maximize variance, so when multiple fissures exhibit significant differences in signal amplitude, the dominant anomaly monopolizes the first principal component, suppressing weaker fissure signals into higher-order components where they become difficult to isolate. In contrast, ICA decomposes signals based on statistical independence, distributing fissure signals of varying intensities into separate independent components and effectively preventing strong signals from masking weaker ones.

Ground fissure identification using PCA for profiles a C1 and b C2. Red anomalies across principal components indicate detected ground fissures.

Ground fissure identification using ICA for profiles a C1 and b C2. Red anomalies across independent components indicate detected ground fissures.

Furthermore, the ICA score matrix (Fig. 6) offers an additional advantage not readily achievable with PCA. Since each independent component corresponds exclusively to a single fissure, the temporal variation of its scores directly reflects the activity intensity and developmental trend of that fissure. As illustrated in Fig. 6a and b, F1, F2, and F3 exhibit distinct temporal activity patterns in both profiles, enabling independent tracking of each fissure’s evolutionary behavior. In contrast, PCA components typically represent linear superpositions of multiple fissure signals, making independent temporal analysis of individual fissures impractical. In summary, ICA demonstrates superior decomposition capability and more complete fissure detection in complex scenarios involving multiple coexisting fissures with considerable amplitude variability, making it better suited for detailed spatiotemporal analysis of ground fissures.

Monitoring efficacy and development mechanism

This study aims to evaluate the performance of the FastICA method when applied to DFOS monitoring data, and to employ multiple approaches for comparative validation to ensure the reliability of the monitoring results. The results indicate that FastICA successfully identified the locations and occurrence timings of three ground fissures, demonstrating its capability to significantly enhance monitoring effectiveness. After calculating fissure widths using both the FastICA and segmented integration methods, data from the two types of fiber optic cables confirm that all three ground fissures were successfully detected. However, discrepancies in measurement performance were observed between the two cables. The C2 cable, which is more tightly coupled to soil deformation and equipped with anchoring points, recorded larger fissure widths and more pronounced deformation signals, whereas the C1 cable, although capable of identifying fissure events, exhibited lower agreement with actual soil deformation and comparatively reduced measurement accuracy. These differences in measured fissure widths are consistent with the distinct installation conditions and soil–cable coupling characteristics of the two cables, in agreement with expectations and supported by previous studies37,43.

Additionally, deformation time series from 2017 to 2019 were derived from Sentinel-1A ascending acquisitions using the SBAS-InSAR method. As the study site is predominantly covered by farmland, long-term interferometric coherence within the monitored area is generally poor. Therefore, as illustrated in Fig. 14, two SBAS measurement points located on exposed bare ground on either side of the fissure, in proximity to the respective endpoints of the fiber optic cable, were selected for differential analysis; these points exhibit comparatively higher coherence and thus greater measurement reliability. The deformation difference between the two points was computed to characterize the relative vertical displacement across the fissure, and the resulting differential subsidence time series was subsequently compared with the fissure width evolution recorded by DFOS. Due to the limited volume of DFOS monitoring data, a time-segmentation approach was adopted for analysis. The relationship between fissure deformation rates and InSAR-derived surface deformation velocities was evaluated for each time segment. The results show strong temporal and spatial consistency between the main fissure F2 monitored by the DFOS system and surface subsidence detected by satellite InSAR (Fig. 15). Correlation analysis revealed a high correlation (r = 0.97) between deformation velocities derived from the two methods across multiple time segments, confirming the DFOS system’s accuracy in identifying fissure deformation and indicating that the ground fissures in this area are primarily caused by uneven surface settlement. These findings align with previous research36,44,45.

Limitations of the measurements

Prior to 2018, effective methodologies for processing data from fiber optic cables C1 and C2 were lacking. Due to the minimal magnitudes of deformation observed, it was generally believed that the ground fissures had stabilized, which consequently led to the infrequent use of DFOS monitoring after 2017. The sudden reactivation and significant widening of the fissures in 2018 prompted a renewed focus on the DFOS dataset. The data processing challenge was subsequently resolved through the application of the FastICA method. Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed: The FastICA method should be incorporated as a supplementary technique for DFOS data processing to facilitate the early detection of minor deformations. Besides, an increase in monitoring frequency and implementation of real-time DFOS monitoring systems should be considered.

Wider implications of the FastICA-DFOS monitoring

Fiber optic monitoring technology is now extensively employed to observe various geohazards such as landslides, ground subsidence, fissures, as well as structural health conditions of buildings. The FastICA method enables improved interpretation of monitoring data, allowing even operators with limited experience to process such information effectively, thereby facilitating broader adoption of DFOS systems.

In our study, we applied the FastICA-DFOS methodology to conduct long-term monitoring of a region susceptible to ground fissuring, successfully identifying three fissures. The mechanisms underlying fissure formation were further verified and interpreted using InSAR technology. This offers a valuable perspective: InSAR can first pinpoint areas experiencing differential settlement, followed by targeted long-term monitoring with FastICA-DFOS, thereby enhancing monitoring efficiency. The findings may serve as a scientific reference for government decision-makers to avoid geohazards in critical infrastructure such as highways and subways.

Overall, the FastICA-DFOS monitoring system proves to be a reliable, cost-effective, and efficient approach for identifying large-scale geological hazards including landslides, subsidence, and ground fissures, while providing researchers with novel insights into monitoring data.

Conclusion

This study proposes an automated DFOS–FastICA–based framework for monitoring ground fissure activity and validates its performance in a fissure-prone area in southern Jiangsu Province, China. The method reliably identifies fissure locations and initiation timing, including subtle deformation signals that are often difficult to detect using conventional processing approaches, thereby enhancing the interpretability of long-term optical fiber sensing data.

A comparative analysis of two fiber optic cable types reveals their complementary response characteristics. The C1 cable consistently identifies all active fissure locations and provides robust spatial positioning, although its fissure width estimates show limited quantitative accuracy. In contrast, the C2 cable enables accurate reconstruction of the spatiotemporal evolution of the main fissure width, exhibiting strong agreement with differential settlement derived from InSAR, with correlation coefficients reaching up to 0.97.

Overall, integrating multiple DFOS cable designs enhances both the robustness and quantitative reliability of fissure monitoring. The proposed framework is readily extendable to other geological hazards involving localized or distributed deformation, such as land subsidence and landslides.
