The hybrid implant-flap breast reconstruction—pearls and pitfalls: a narrative review

Introduction

Background

Autologous abdominally based free tissue transfer is considered by many to be the gold-standard for breast reconstruction. The lower abdominal skin and fat provides soft, pliable soft tissue that allows for breast shaping and adequate recreation of breast aesthetics in properly selected patients. In some instances, the patient may not be a good candidate for abdominally based free tissue transfer due to paucity of tissues, poor skin laxity or previous surgery. In these cases, other donor sites such as the flanks, thigh, and buttock may be considered with similar benefits.

Despite the favorable outcomes associated with autologous reconstruction, insufficient donor-site volume remains a challenge in thin patients and in women with larger native breasts. Several strategies have been described to address this limitation, including delayed fat grafting, stacked free flaps, and combinations of multiple autologous tissue sources to optimize breast volume and projection (1-3). These approaches have expanded the reconstructive options available to patients with inadequate donor tissue and have helped improve aesthetic outcomes while preserving the benefits of autologous reconstruction.

Rationale

Hybrid flap-implant reconstruction has emerged as an important option for patients in whom autologous tissue alone may be insufficient to achieve the desired breast volume and projection. The combination of a free flap and prosthetic implant can be performed either immediately at the time of mastectomy or in a delayed fashion following autologous reconstruction. As experience with this technique has grown, different approaches regarding implant timing, implant plane, and the use of mesh or acellular dermal matrix have been described in the literature. Understanding these concepts is important to optimize outcomes and minimize complications. A review of the available evidence may help guide clinical decision-making and optimize outcomes.

Objective

The authors sought to share their point of view with the hybrid implant-flap breast reconstruction and to review the available literature on the subject. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-399/rc).

Methods

A review of the literature was performed by query of MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Library databases. Search terms used for the search were “breast reconstruction”, “free flap breast reconstruction”, “abdominal flap breast reconstruction”, “implant based breast reconstruction”, “flap-implant breast reconstruction” (Table 1). Inclusion criteria included articles in the English language retrospective and prospective studies that used free flaps for breast reconstruction (abdominal and other sources), studies that categorized their data into immediate and staged placement of implant, case series, case reports and review of literature. Studies from the last 30 years were included [1995–2025]. Studies that did not analyze the use of free flaps combined with implants for breast reconstruction, studies not in the English language were not included for review. The reference section of these studies was further scrutinized for other studies that met the inclusion criteria.

Results

Seventeen studies were identified (details of the studies on Table 2). Of these 13 reported on patients who had free flaps augmented with an implant for breast reconstruction. The studies dated from 1998 to 2025. Patient data were reported through case reports, case series, and retrospective reviews. A total of 359 patients and 598 breasts were reviewed. The majority of the of the flaps [590] were abdominally based flaps [muscle-sparing transverse rectus abdominis muscle (MS-TRAMs) or deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flaps], the remainder were 6 transverse upper gracilis (TUG), one superior gluteal artery perforator (SGAP) flap and one fasciocutaneous infragluteal (FCI) flap. The timing for implant placement was roughly evenly split between delayed (52%, 300 breasts) and immediate (48%, 298 breasts) were performed immediately at the time of the free flap reconstruction. Tissue expanders were used with the flap prior to the silicone implant in a minority of cases—11% of cases. The preferred plane of implant placement was prepectoral in 92% of breasts, with silicone implants the preferred type of implant (89%) with average volumes ranging from 150–231 mL. Mesh support (acellular dermal matrix or synthetic) was used in approximately 57% of cases. Several of the index cases received pre-operative radiation 17%.

Discussion

Post mastectomy reconstruction is a mainstay of breast cancer treatment in the United States with approximately 75% of women undergoing mastectomy seeking reconstruction. Implant based reconstruction is the main method of reconstruction as it is a simpler procedure, performed as an outpatient in most centers, with an acceptable risk profile and able to be performed by the majority of plastic surgeons. Autologous reconstruction is sought by a number of women who are drawn to it by the fact that the reconstruction is performed with natural tissues, eliminating the use of a foreign body and in many instances improving the aesthetics of the donor site such as in abdominally based free tissue transfer. However, there are occasions in which the volume of the donor site does not match the volume of the breasts to be reconstructed, being smaller than the patients breast skin envelope or simply smaller than the patient’s desired final volume. Several solutions have been described to remedy this situation. These solutions are either focused on decreasing the size of the skin envelope or the increasing the volume of the reconstruction itself (1). In situations where the residual skin envelope is to large and requires augmentation, options include: the use of implants for augmentation (2), stacked free flaps (3,21), autologous fat grafting or the use of stacked acellular dermal matrices (22). Based on the focus of this manuscript, this discussion will be limited to augmenting an autologous reconstruction with an implant.

The use of implants in autologous reconstruction is not new, having been proposed for pedicled and free transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous (TRAM) flaps as well as for pedicled posterior trunk flaps (4-6). The use of implants in DIEP flaps is also not new, being described as early as 2009 with Figus et al.’s experience (8).

Aside from the aesthetic advantages of the hybrid reconstruction, a favorable complication profile in relation to implant only reconstruction has been demonstrated. In analyzing the complication profile of performing a hybrid DIEP + implant compared to DIEP flap alone and implant alone reconstruction Wang et al. demonstrated that the hybrid DIEP flap + implant group had less postoperative infections than the implant only group. In fact, performing a hybrid flap + implant procedure decreased the odds of infection by 90% (18).

In a systematic review of prosthetic augmentation of autologous microvascular breast reconstruction, Piper et al. reviewed 11 studies comprising 230 patients and 378 flaps and demonstrated favorable outcomes with hybrid reconstruction. The authors reported low rates of implant-related complications and no total flap losses, supporting the safety and reliability of combining prosthetic devices with autologous tissue transfer in properly selected patients. Furthermore, the review highlighted the versatility of the hybrid approach across multiple flap types and reconstructive strategies, reinforcing its role as an important option for patients with inadequate donor-site volume or those seeking greater breast volume than can be achieved with autologous tissue alone (17).

The use of implants as an augmentation for the reconstruction is a straight forward method to increase volume of the reconstructed breast while maintaining the look and feel of the natural breast. In considering this procedure, several key factors should be taken into account for the success of the operation: timing, plane of implant placement, use of mesh/acellular dermal matrix (ADM) and history of radiation.

Timing of implant placement

The implant can be placed in an immediate setting, i.e., at the time of the flap reconstruction or in a delayed fashion, usually several months after the flap has been performed. In cases where the surgeon is confident that the size of the flap is too small for the pocket and/or it is known that the patient would like a larger size breast, the immediate placement of the implant will complement the reconstruction and potentially achieve the final result in one surgical procedure. Alternatively, the hybrid procedure can be performed by placing a tissue expander at the time for autologous reconstruction. The tissue expander can be serially expanded and exchanged for a permanent implant after complete expansion.

In some cases, the final outcome following autologous reconstruction may be deemed insufficient based on volume. In these situations, options include placement of a small implant under the flap or the use of fat grafting. Fat grafting has demonstrated success for the correction of contour deformities of the breast but in the authors’ experience, this technique has not demonstrated reproducible results when used for volume restoration on a delayed basis. In addition, multiple fat grafting sessions may be needed to achieve the desired outcome and in thinner patients, this may pose additional challenges. The use of a small implant on the other hand, has the advantage of immediate volume restoration. Some surgeons have opined that this strategy is preferred to immediate device placement at the time of mastectomy and flap because it reduces the risk of compromising the flow of the vascular pedicle. Advocates for this approach point out the complexity of the flap procedure in and of itself and adding an implant to the reconstruction would add to the difficulty of the case in hand. Furthermore, performing the augmentation as a delayed procedure allows the surgeon and the patient to gauge the breast size as the flap settles and the swelling decreases setting up a more predictable result if the patient desires larger breasts.

The safety of the delayed approach has been repeatedly confirmed and viability of the flap is not at risk, especially because months after the procedure the flap would have formed accessory vascular connections beyond the pedicle, thus minimizing the risk of flap compromise in the unlikely event of pedicle injury. This aspect has been well demonstrated by Mirzabeigi in his experience of delayed flap augmentations with pre-pectoral implant placement and of prepectoral hybrid conversions. In this study, the author routinely ligates the flap pedicle for proper pre-pectoral pocket creation with no compromise to flap viability (20). In patients who have been previously radiated who may have less soft tissue compliance or in whom sufficient vascular collateralization is doubtful, then a precise pocket creation avoiding injury to the vascular pedicle can be achieved by minimizing aggressive medial dissection assuming the microvascular anastomosis was performed to the internal mammary vessels. This will be discussed in greater detail later in the manuscript.

In delayed hybrid implant placement, the authors have several suggestions to achieve safety and predictability. Patients who have had prior radiation often will have some degree of fibrosis the pectoralis major muscle, making it less compliant. In these situations, the prosthetic device is typically placed above the pectoralis major muscle rather than beneath it. In addition, the authors have found that the use of a saline implant is sometime preferred to that of a silicone gel implant due to the lack of soft tissue compliance. The saline device is inserted in a deflated state and then gradually inflated to a reasonable and desirable volume. It should be remembered that overfilling an implant in a previously radiated space may increase the risk of incisional dehiscence.

In patients who have not had previous radiation, then the prosthetic device can be placed in the prepectoral or subpectoral pocket; however, the prepectoral position is generally preferred. This can be performed with implant or tissue expanders based on the goals of the patient and the amount of soft tissue expansion desired. The implants can be saline or silicone gel because the soft tissues are notable more compliant and will be able to stretch and accommodate the implant.

In any case, the decision to augment the flap either immediately or in a delayed fashion must be made taking into account the surgeon’s experience and comfort with the autologous free flap procedure and the patient’s ultimate desired breast size.

Momeni et al. have reported on their experience with immediate insertion of silicone implants at the same time of the abdominally-based free tissue transfer (11,12,14). The authors stress the benefits of performing the insertion of the prosthesis at the same time as the flap as this decreases the number of operations needed to finalize reconstruction. They emphasized the importance of compartmentalizing the implant using a mesh (Vicryl) or acellular dermal matrix to delineate the implant boundaries and to avoid pedicle compromise. No flap losses were noted in any of the studies.

Black et al. have also reported on their experience with hybrid abdominally-based free tissue transfer and silicone implants in a delayed fashion. The time interval between the flap procedure and the implantation of the prosthesis ranged from 2 months to 6 years (mean 1 year). The authors report no flap losses and a 97.5% success rate with only one loss of implant due to peri-prosthetic infection (15). The delayed approach was also highlighted in Bach et al.’s experience (16) in which the timing for implant placement ranged from 6–87 months.

Momeni and Kanchwala reported their experience delayed-immediate hybrid reconstruction in a series of 31 patients following bilateral breast reconstruction. The salient aspects of their technique included performing the initial reconstruction using a prepectoral tissue expander and ADM. Following complete expansion, the second stage was performed in a hybrid fashion with and abdominally based free flap and silicone gel breast implant. The main advantage of this technique was that it improved their ability to determine the desired final breast size as the patient had direct input during the expansion process (13).

Role of expanders in the hybrid approach

Using tissue expanders as first step to the hybrid flap-implant reconstruction may be considered prior to the final implant placement. Even though the majority of the published series do not include this step, starting with a TE can be beneficial in certain cases.

In Serletti’s experience (4), tissue expanders were placed in the immediate setting either underneath a submuscular pocket may by sewing the pectoralis major muscle to the free TRAM or under the flap directly. The authors highlight the advantage of being able to allow the patient to participate in the decision of the size of the implant.

Pien et al. share the authors’ experience with the use of a delayed approach to implant insertion (10). In the majority of the hybrid flap-implant cohort (11 or 17 patients), they selected to start with a tissue expander in a subflap plane. The main benefits to this approach are to enhance the pocket and lower pole projection, as well as to allow for expansion of the native breast skin in cases of large areas of flap skin exposure, allowing the native skin to be expanded and subsequently redraped over the flap to minimize reconstruction stigma.

Plane of implant placement

As with breast augmentation and alloplastic breast reconstruction, different planes can be chosen for implant placement that include prepectoral or subpectoral placement. The proximity of the implant to the pedicle usually dictates the chosen plane such that the implant should not occlude or kink the pedicle. As previously mentioned, with the delayed approach, the flap has usually acquired peripheral accessory vascularity from the surrounding tissues; thus, pedicle occlusion is not an issue (20). Momeni and Kanchwala have reported on the use of an immediate hybrid abdominally based free flap-silicone implant reconstruction in 19 patients (38 breasts). The authors highlight the submuscular plane as the preferred choice as well as the use of mesh (ADM and Vicryl) for infero-lateral sling to properly define the implant pocket. No major complications were reported (12).

In two separate studies, Momeni and Kanchwala as well as Silverstein and Momeni reviewed data on patients undergoing hybrid autologous free tissue transfer-silicone implant breast reconstruction in a pre-pectoral plane. The authors used ADM or Vicryl mesh in every case, again to compartmentalize the implant away from the pedicle. The authors also highlight the evolution of their own technique in these papers, placing the implants in the pre-pectoral plane to minimize morbidity of the recipient area and simplify the procedure. There were no flap failures in the studies (11,19).

Black et al. have reported on their experience with delayed hybrid reconstruction in the setting of prior radiation therapy (15). The plane of implant placement was selected intraoperatively and based on whether there was evidence of radiation fibrosis to the pectoralis major muscle. In patients that received chest wall irradiation, the preference was to place implants in the prepectoral position for the reasons previously described.

Use of acellular dermal matrix and mesh

As in implant-based reconstruction the use of mesh or ADM may be used in hybrid free flap-implant reconstruction. The main purpose of mesh is to support the implant and keep it stable in its chosen plane and at the same time isolating the implant from the pedicle. When prosthetic devices are placed under a flap, the mesh is typically used to wrap the implant either completely or over its anterior surface. In a submuscular hybrid free flap augmentation, the mesh is used as a sling, providing inferior coverage of the implant and at the time supporting the reconstruction.

The benefits of mesh use in hybrid reconstruction has been described by Momeni, Kanchwala and Silverstein (11-14,19). The authors highlight the use of the mesh (Vicryl mesh)/ADM as an important step in isolating the implant pocket for its support as well as its isolation from the pedicle. It is important to note that the implantation of the silicone implants in these studies were all performed at the same time as the flap procedure, so demarcating the area where the implant will be is paramount, as the breast pocket space is not yet defined, and the flap is not adjusted to its boundaries.

Black et al. have described hybrid reconstruction without using mesh (15). In this series of patients, the implants (saline or silicone) were placed in a delayed fashion, months after the flap inset. In this scenario, the dissection of the implant pocket (sub-muscular or sub-flap) happens seamlessly much like in an augmentation. The implant will be held in position by the outline of the flap itself and no mesh/ADM is warranted. This approach was also described by Bach et al., experience in which no mesh/ADM was used in his cases of hybrid reconstruction (16).

Interestingly, the approach to hybrid flap-implant reconstruction with no mesh/ADM in our review were all performed in delayed cases, when a pocket can be well defined underneath the flap or pectoralis major muscle. With immediate hybrid reconstruction, defining the breast pocket and boundaries may be more difficult especially when there has been extensive dissection performed during the mastectomy. In these situations, the use of a mesh is highly recommended as it will allow for optimally determining the breast borders. In cases of implant malposition, the use of mesh may be warranted especially in revision cases. An absorbable mesh can be used as an inferior sling to support the implant and avoid inferior migration of the device (20). Ultimately, the type of mesh selected (ADM or synthetic) will be dictated by the surgeon’s experience and timing of the procedure.

Type of flap

The use of the hybrid approach in other types of free flap breast reconstruction has also been described with good outcomes. Bach et al. described his experience using a delayed approach of flap augmentations both in DIEP flaps as well as flaps such as SGAP flaps, FCI flaps and TUG (16). In a case report, Trignano et al. describe the implant/TUG hybrid reconstruction and highlights is safety and efficacy of this procedure (9).

Pitfalls of radiated vs. non radiated

It is well understood that breast irradiation increases the risk of implant infection, implant loss and reconstruction failure in implant based reconstruction when performed before or after the reconstruction (23).

The use of autologous tissue in the setting of radiation, including abdominally based free flaps, either before or after radiation has been shown to have less complications when compared with implant-based reconstruction. In an elegant retrospective review of nearly 1,000 patients, Chang et al. demonstrated that the combination of autologous flaps with implants for breast reconstruction after radiation therapy reduced implant related complications compared with implant only reconstructions (7). It may be that the vascularity associated with the autologous reconstruction will improve the vascularity of the surrounding tissues, thus promoting a more favorable environment for the implant to reside. Black et al. have further studied the choice of plane in the setting of prior radiation (15). They concluded that pre-pectoral placement should be favored in patients with radiation. Subpectoral placement in the setting of radiation will more likely than not result in malposition and capsular contracture due to the fibrotic nature of the pectoralis muscle.

The timing of the implant placement in the setting of radiation is really dependent of the timing of the flap surgery. Definitive reconstruction with autologous based free flaps can be performed before or after radiation treatment. At present the authors preference is for delaying autologous based reconstruction until radiation is finished (>6 months from the end of radiation) to avoid acute changes in the flap(s). In our review, no studies comparing the outcomes of hybrid reconstruction flap-implant before and after radiation to the construct (flap-implant) were identified.

Conclusions

Performing hybrid flap-implant breast reconstruction has been established as a great tool in the reconstructive surgeon’s armamentarium. It is a safe and reliable approach on patients with larger breasts who desire autologous reconstruction and have paucity of autologous donor tissue. The timing of placement of the implant can be immediately during the flap reconstruction or in a delayed fashion without differences increased complications with either approach. The plane of implant placement may be pre-pectoral (beneath flap), subpectoral with or without the use of mesh—these approaches have also not been shown to differ in terms of outcomes. The use of hybrid reconstruction in the setting of radiation is well supported. In this case, a prepectoral/sub flap plane is preferred.
