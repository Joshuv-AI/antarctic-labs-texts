A comparative study on sequential green hybrid techniques (ultrasonication, microwave and high shear homogenization) for the extraction of date seed bioactive compounds and its application as an additive for shelf-life extension of Oreochromis niloticus
Authors: Jennifer Osamede Airouyuwa, Hina Khan, Hussein Mostafa, Priti Mudgil, Sajid Maqsood
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Green extraction techniques, Hybrid extraction techniques, Deep eutectic solvent, Date seed, Shelf-life study, Antimicrobial activity

Introduction

The effects of global warming and climate change have drawn attention to drought-tolerant plants that reflect sustainable practices and conserve natural resources [1]. One of the most important drought-tolerant plants is the date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.), which is mainly cultivated in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) [2]. The Food and Agriculture Organization statistics reported an increment in the production of date palm fruit from 7.1 to 9.66 million metric tons from 2010 to 2021 (FAOSTAT, 2021). The increase in the production, processing and consumption of date fruits is directly proportional to the amount of waste generated, including date leaves, pollen, spikelets, and seeds [2]. Date seeds comprises of approximately 15 % of the overall date fruit weight and are considered a major source of date waste [3]. Date seeds have ample nutritional and functional compounds, including carbohydrates, proteins, dietary fiber, minerals, and bioactive compounds (carotenoids, flavonoids, and polyphenols) [4]. The bioactive compounds from date seeds could contribute immensely to the circular bioeconomy as functional food ingredients and additives, in line with the United Nations (UN) sustainable developmental goals 2 of zero hunger [5], [6]. Owing to the role of bioactive compounds play in the cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and food industries, attention has been drawn toward the utilization of green, safe, and sustainable extraction techniques [7]. The emergence of this multidisciplinary approach known as “Green Extraction” is the merging of Green Chemistry principles with the Circular Economy. The objective of this strategy is to create novel, effective, and environment-friendly extraction techniques that maximize the utilization of natural resources, reduce the need for organic solvents, enhance extraction yields, and save time and energy [8]. With regard to the valorization of agro-by-products such as date seeds, the goal is to create high-value products in an environmentally friendly manner, thus introducing the concept of “benign-by-design” [9], [10]. Some of the most common green approaches utilized for the extraction of bioactive compounds include homogenization-assisted extraction (HAE) [10], microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) [11], ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) [12], pulsed electric field (PEF) [13], and accelerated solvent extraction (ASE) [14]. In addition to green extraction techniques, there is a need for greener solvents that are eco-friendly, less toxic, non-volatile, affordable, biodegradable, recyclable, and sustainable. Deep eutectic solvents (DES) are among these greener solvents [15], synthesized by the combination of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), and hydrogen bond donors (HBA) at a known molar ratio [16]. DES as an extraction solvent has recently gained attention as a green sustainable solvent and a good substitute for conventional solvents because of its high extraction efficiency for bioactive compounds from natural sources [15], [16], [17]. However, the urgent need to increase process sustainability and efficiency at an industrial scale may make the ground-breaking adoption of these green techniques insufficient on their own. This challenge can be overcome by combining extraction technologies to build integrated systems that can minimize individual shortcomings and produce better outcomes by utilizing synergistic effects and integrating various mechanisms [18]. This can be performed by sequentially employing two extraction techniques often known as pre-treatment followed by subsequent extraction. The evolution of this combined extraction technique is known as a hybrid technology, which usually involves the use of two techniques: MAE and UAE or HAE and MAE or HAE and UAE. Interest in sequential hybrid systems is currently gaining attention, which shows potential development in overcoming the challenges associated with the use of single extraction techniques, while interest in pre-treatments seems to be based on well-established values [9].

Therefore, in this study, the feasibility of using biodegradable and renewable polyol-based DES (P-DES) comprising of choline chloride a metabolite of provitamin A was utilized as HBA. While polyol such as ethylene glycol, diethylene glycol, triethylene glycol, glycerol, and 1,4-butanediol, which are derivatives of glycerol surplus from biofuel processing were used as HDB. These P-DESs in combination with green extraction techniques (HAE, MAE, and UAE) were utilized for the extraction of bioactive compounds from date seeds. Following the selection of specific P-DES with the highest extraction efficiency of date seed bioactive compounds, various green extraction techniques were further optimized using response surface methodology (RSM) based on the central composite design (CCD) for (HAE, MAE, and UAE). The optimized conditions from three green techniques were further subjected to sequential hybrid techniques using a combination of various green approaches. Furthermore, the utilization of extracts from biological resources and their by-products has gained relevance as natural food additives to extend the shelf life of foods because of their rich bioactive compounds that possess antioxidant and antimicrobial properties [19]. The date seed extract from the best performing green extraction technique was used to extend the shelf life of refrigerated Oreochromis niloticus fillets. The novelty of this study lies in the first-time application of hybrid techniques in combination with P-DES for recovery of date seed bioactive compounds. In addition, the investigation includes the use of hybrid techniques involving ternary extraction methods.

Material and methods

Material

Al-Foah Date Company in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates supplied the Khalas type of date seeds. Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) supplied the P-DES reagents, which include diethylene glycol, ethylene glycol, triethylene glycol, glycerol, and 1,4 butanediol. Glentham (UK) supplied the choline chloride. The following chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA): 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), gallic acid, sodium acetate, 6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox), rutin (quercetin-3-O-rutinoside), and Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Four distinct strains of human-pathogenic bacteria were obtained from the Biology Department of the United Arab Emirates University. Two of the strains are Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and Listeria monocytogenes DSM 20649), while the other two are Gram-negative (Salmonella typhimurium ATCC 14028 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922). The other reagents not mentioned were analytical grades.

Sample preparation

Date seeds were washed with distilled water and oven dry at 50 °C, before grinding them using a laboratory scale Ultra-Centrifugal Mill (Retsch GmbH, Germany). The grounded date seed powder was later sieved through a mesh of 250 µm to obtain fine date seed powder which was stored at 4 °C until further analysis.

Polyol-based deep eutectic solvents (P-DESs) preparation

The P-DESs shown in Table 1 were prepared following the method described by Airouyuwa et al., [20]. In all the prepared P-DESs, 20 % water was added before being utilized as an extraction solvent.

P-DESs viscosity

After the various P-DESs were prepared, Brookfield digital viscometer (AMETEX, USA) was used to determine the viscosities of the P-DESs at temperatures of 25±1 °C and 50 ±1 °C and a speed of 100 rpm using spindle number of 6 [20].

Green extractions of bioactive compounds from date seeds

Homogenization-assisted extraction (HAE) of date seeds

The HAE of date seed bioactive compounds was extracted using a T 25 digital S3 ULTRA-TURRAX, (UK); with a solid-solvent ratio of 1:30 (g/ml), a speed of 10,000 rpm for 5 min. After the HAE, the samples were centrifuged at 10,528 g for 10 min at 4 °C and the recovered supernatants were stored at −4 °C prior to further analysis.

Microwave-assisted extraction (MAE) of date seeds

The MAE of date seed bioactive compounds was carried out using a microwave, LABOTRON 12 T, Lyon, (France); with a solid-solvent ratio of 1:30 (g/ml), at 50 °C for 10 min and a power of 600 W. After the MAE, the samples were centrifuged at 10,528 g for 10 min at 4 °C and the recovered supernatants were stored at −4 °C prior to further analysis.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) of date seeds

The UAE of date seed bioactive compounds was carried out using an ultrasonic bath SONOREX DIGIPLUS DL Germany(S DL, Germany); with a solid-solvent ratio of 1:30 (g/ml), at 50 °C for 50 min and an amplitude of 80 %. After the UAE, the samples were centrifuged at 10,528 g for 10 min at 4 °C and the recovered supernatants were stored at −4 °C prior to further analysis.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activity

The extracts from the various green techniques were subjected to TPC and DPPH radical scavenging assays following the method described by Mostafa et al., [20] with slight modifications. While the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was analysed by the method of Gullón et al., [21]. The detailed methodology of TPC, DPPH, and FRAP assays is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

The P-DESs with the highest extraction efficiency in terms of TPC and FRAP were subjected to optimization using different green extraction techniques including HAE, MAE and UAE. Optimization was carried out using the design expert software (version 11.0, Stat Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA), response surface methodology (RSM) and central composite design (CCD) were used to evaluate the optimum conditions suitable for HAE, MAE, and UAE using choline chloride: ethylene glycol (ChCl:Eg) as extraction solvents for date seeds which will result in maximum responses for TPC and FRAP. Sequential hybrid extraction techniques

After optimizing the extraction conditions from the various green techniques (HAE, MAE and UAE); the sequential hybrid extraction techniques were implemented using UAE + HAE (UHAE); this extraction techniques implies that sample was initially subjected to UAE followed by HAE, UAE + MAE (UMAE), HAE + UAE (HUAE), HAE + MAE (HMAE), MAE + UAE (MUAE), and MAE + HAE (MHAE)). Furthermore, the combined effect of ternary hybrid techniques (UAE + HAE + MAE (UHMAE); this extraction techniques means that sample was initially subjected to UAE followed by HAE and later MAE. The other ternary hybrid techniques including HAE + UAE + MAE (HUMAE), and MAE + UAE + HAE (MUHAE) were also investigated to determine the significant percentage increase in extraction efficiency of the date seed bioactive compounds in terms of TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity.

Identification and quantification of individual phenolics from date seeds

The resulting extracts from the HAE, MAE, and UAE optimization processes, along with the hybrid techniques, were analyzed using the Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC to identify major individual phenolics. The specifics of this analysis can be found in supplementary material S 2.

Antimicrobial activities of date seed extracts obtained using P-DES in combination with the green extraction techniques

Microbial strains and preparation of standard microbial inoculum

The antimicrobial activity of the date seed extract was evaluated by using Gram-positive bacteria, including Listeria monocytogenes and Staphylococcus aureus, as well as Gram-negative bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium. Throughout the investigation, the cultures were maintained in glycerol stocks at 4 °C. Every strain was selected based on its frequent utilization in antimicrobial testing protocols, and the microorganisms were transferred into a brain heart infusion (BHI) broth for 18–24h at 37 °C. The turbidity of the inoculum was diluted with the media to meet the McFarland turbidity threshold of 0.5, which is roughly equivalent to 105 CFU/mL for the bacteria [21].

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration

The microplate broth dilution method was utilized to evaluate the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of all bacterial species used in this study. Specifically, the MIC and MBC were assessed in BHI Broth via the broth two-fold microdilution technique outlined by Al-Akayleh et al., [21], with certain adjustments made. The procedure involved successive dilutions of NADES and NADES-based extracts in 100 µL BHI. An aliquot of 100 µL of P-DES and P-DES-based date seed extract stock solutions was added to the first well with BHI, and an aliquot (100 µL) was serially diluted to the 10th well of a 96-well microplate plate. To serve as positive and negative controls, 100 µL of medium was added to the remaining two wells. Ten microliters of the microorganism standard inoculum (105 CFU/mL) were added to all wells, except the negative control well. The bacteria were cultured in a covered microplate at 37 °C for 24 h. Resazurin indicator solution (30 µL) was added to each well, and the microplate was incubated for 2 h before observation. The violet hue of the well contents suggests that no bacteria had grown. However, the transition to pink indicates bacterial development. In order to calculate MBC, a 5 µL sample was obtained from wells corresponding to the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and from wells with greater concentrations. On BHI agar plates, these samples were subcultured before being incubated for 18 h at 37 °C to detect microorganisms. MBC is the lowest concentration of the antimicrobial agent that does not cause any bacterial growth; this is usually the concentration that is slightly above the MIC. Every test was carried out in duplicates.

Preparation of Oreochromis niloticus fillets

Freshly harvested Oreochromis niloticus was obtained from a Falaj Hazza fish farm at the United Arab Emirates University and transported to the Department of Food Science. Upon arrival, the fish was dressed and filleted which were cut into 10 g size pieces. The MIC concentration of the 10 % (accounting for 1.37 g/ml of the TPC) date seed P-DES based extract obtained from MAE was used to treat the fish fillets by immersing the fillets in the extracts for 5 min followed by allowing the extract to be drained, while the untreated fillets were used as the control samples. The treated fillets and control samples were stored in polyethylene zipper bags at 4 °C for 10 days. Microbiological analysis was conducted on days 1, 5, and 10.

Microbial load of Oreochromis niloticus fillets

Ten grams of fish fillet were weighed, crushed and transferred to a stomacher bag, and 90 mL of sterile 0.1 % peptone water was added. The mixture was then homogenized for two minutes at 250 rpm. To prepare 10-fold dilutions, an aliquot of 1 mL of the homogenized solution was pipetted into 9 mL of sterile 0.1 % peptone water. Additionally, 1 mL from each serial dilution was poured to Plate Count Agar (PCA) (CM107, Land Bridge, Beijing, China) to determine the total bacterial count (TBC). Coliform proliferation was assessed using Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar (VRBGA). After placing the plates in a biochemical incubator set to 37 ± 1 ◦C, incubation was carried out for 48 h. A Scan 1200 colony counter (Interscience, UK) was used to count the bacterial colonies, and the results were expressed as log CFU/g [19].

Statistical analysis

Three independent experiments were conducted to obtain the data, and the average results were reported. SPSS (version 23) was used for statistical analysis of the TPC, DPPH, and FRAP antioxidant activity results. RSM data were analyzed using design expert software (trial version 13.0, Stat Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA). The coefficients for the quadratic, interaction, and linear regressions were assessed using an analysis of variance (ANOVA); measured at 95 % (P<0.05) significant levels. The coefficients of determination of R2, and the adjusted coefficients of determination (adjusted R2) based on the polynomial equation were at the 95 % (P<0.05) significance level.

Results and discussion

Viscosity of polyol-based DES

The intrinsic resistance to flow and strength of intermolecular interactions within a fluid are functions of its viscosity. The viscosities of the studied polyol-based deep eutectic solvents were in the following order: ChCl:But > ChCl:Gly > ChCl:Tg > ChCl:Dg > ChCl:Eg, as shown in Table 1. The viscosities of P-DESs are largely influenced by the interactions of the hydrogen bonds within the components, which hinders the free flow of molecules within the DES. In addition, other forces that could have contributed to the viscosity of DES are van der Waals or electrostatic forces between the DES components [22]. Owing to the application of DES as a green sustainable solvent, a low-viscosity solvent is desirable, as it significantly reduces the operational cost of several processes, such as pumping, mixing, and stirring [23]. The viscosity of DES is greatly impacted by temperature, which is a critical concern since very viscous DES are not suited for practical use at ambient temperatures. However, they can also be considered for technical applications at elevated temperatures [24]. It is clear that as the temperature of the P-DES increased from 25±1 °C to 50±1 °C, the viscosity of the DES decreased, which weakened the interactions between the DES components, leading to decreased viscosity. This indicates that the slow mass transfer processes that occur at room temperature owing to the high P-DES viscosity would accelerate at higher temperatures. Similar trends were observed in the viscosity of choline chloride-based deep eutectic solvents with polyols by [24], [25].

Effect of P-DESs coupled with green extraction techniques on the extraction of polyphenols and their antioxidant activity of date seed

A variety of polyols comprising tri-ethylene glycol, ethylene glycol, diethylene glycol, and glycerol 1,4-Butanediol were used as hydrogen bond donors (HBD), and choline chloride as hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA). The feasibility of using P-DESs coupled with emerging extraction techniques as alternatives to conventional extraction solvents and methods was investigated. Five polyol-based DES and two conventional solvents coupled with HAE, MAE, and UAE were evaluated to study their efficiency in the extraction of polyphenols and their effects on the antioxidant activity of date seed bioactive compounds. Polyol-based DES were chosen based on their ready availability, non-toxicity, renewability, and biodegradability [26], [27]. Regardless of the extraction technique, choline chloride: ethylene glycol (ChCl:Eg) demonstrated higher polyphenolic content and antioxidant activity than other P-DESs and conventional solvents, as shown in Fig. 1 a and b. The high recovery efficiency of ChCl:Eg could be attributed to its comparatively low viscosity of 60 mPa.S at 25±1 °C compared to other P-DESs with higher viscosities, as shown in Table 1. According to studies, low mass transfer and extraction efficiency are caused by the very viscous DES utilized in the extraction of bioactive chemicals because the DES cannot penetrate the dense cell wall matrix [28], [29]. However, the addition of water helps reduce DES viscosity [28], [30], [31] because a low-viscous DES enhances diffusivity and possesses greater extraction efficiency for the solvent [31]. In this study, 20 % water was added to all the prepared P-DESs. Wan et al., [27] reported that ethylene glycol is a non-corrosive, non-toxic, and a polar solvent, with significant polar interactions like hydrogen bond and dipole interaction with target compound which could be responsible for its higher extraction yield. In addition, other P-DESs possess a higher number of carbon atoms and hydroxyl groups, which could result in steric hindrance, preventing interactions between the choline chloride anion and the compounds of interest. Hence, a decrease in the extraction efficiency was observed for the other P-DESs [32]. When compared to traditional solvents (80 % ethanol and methanol), ChCl:Eg also demonstrated a higher extraction efficiency for date seed polyphenols. This finding was consistent with a study conducted by Liu et al. [25], who reported that ChCl:Eg significantly increased the extraction yield of polyphenolics from Chlorella vulgaris when compared to other polyol-based DES. On the other hand, 80 % ethanol possessed a significantly higher extraction efficiency than the other P-DESs, which could be attributed to its high polarity.

Among the various green techniques (HAE, MAE, and UAE) coupled with P-DESs and conventional solvents, HAE has high selectivity for ChCl:Eg in extraction of bioactive compounds from date seeds compared to other P-DESs and conventional solvents. Date seed extracts treated with HAE and ChCl:Eg showed higher TPC, DPPH and FRAP antioxidant activity of 111.22±3.2 (mg GAE/g DSP), 762.66±2.02(mM TE/g DSP) and 1111.79±11.96 (mM TE/g DSP), respectively. The higher extraction of date seed bioactive compounds from HAE coupled with ChCl:Eg could be due to the high-speed rotation of the homogenizer which creates strong shear forces that physically break down the cell walls of the date seed matrix and the low viscosity of ChCl:Eg facilitating the mass transfer and enhancing the release of bioactive compounds. MAE exhibited a higher extraction efficiency for all the P-DES and conventional solvents, as observed in Fig. 1a, which could be attributed to the irradiation temperature of 50 °C. Doldolova et al., [33] reported that microwave temperature plays a major role in influencing the extraction yield of bioactive compounds. Microwaves, along with higher temperatures, disrupt the cell matrix more efficiently, decreasing DES viscosity and surface tension, thereby enhancing the mass transfer between the cell wall matrix and DES molecules, consequently resulting in a higher extraction efficiency of date seed polyphenols [34]. In UAE, higher molecular weight P-DESs with high viscosity (ChCl:Tg, ChCl:1,4-But, and ChCl:Gly) showed relatively higher extraction yields for TPC, DPPH, and FRAP antioxidant activities than HAE and MAE. For instance, the TPC of ChCl:1,4-But was 72.92±3.1, 82.03±0.8 and 87.88±1.8 (mg GAE/g DSP) for HAE, MAE, and UAE, respectively, with a significantly higher extraction efficacy of for the UAE than for the other extraction techniques. The higher extraction efficiency of date seed polyphenols observed from highly viscous DES coupled with UAE, compared to HAE and MAE, could be attributed to the long extraction time of UAE (50 min at 50 °C), which was sufficient to reduce DES viscosity and enhance the dissolution of DES in the cell wall matrix, resulting in increased extraction efficacy. A similar trend was also observed for antioxidant activity, as depicted in Fig. 1b and c. Furthermore, when conventional extraction method by stirring for 12h at 750 rpm was used to extract polyphenol from date seed using 80 % P-DES (ChCl:Eg), ethanol and methanol, the TPC of P-DES (ChCl:Eg) was significant higher (81.22±0.6 mg GAE/g DSP) compared to the conventional solvents ethanol and methanol (73.74±0.5 and 60.78±07 mg GAE/g DSP), respectively. Similar trends were observed for DPPH and FRAP antioxidant activities, as indicated in the Supplementary Material (S. Table 1). The results revealed that regardless of the extraction method and solvent type, P-DES (ChCl:Eg) showed profound extraction efficacy, which could be attributed to the fact that DES are effective in the dissolution of cell wall components owing to the presence of high hydrogen bonds, which can promote efficient intermolecular interactions between the DES and the compounds of interest [35]. Based on the high extraction efficiency of ChCl:Eg, it was selected for optimization and further experiments.

Modelling and optimization

Modelling and optimization of the HAE process for TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity from date seeds using RSM

The strong shear force generated by the HAE sharply increases the rate of motion of the date seed matrix molecules to be extracted, resulting in the disruption, splitting, and even release of the target bioactive compounds in the medium [36], [37]. However, a long shear force can lead to oxidation, decreased activity, or gelatinization of the compounds of interest [36]. In addition, polyphenol oxidase may be produced during prolonged shear times, which oxidizes the extracted molecules and reduces their activity [36]. To overcome this limitation, the highest speed and extraction time were set to 10,000 rpm for 6 min. A total of 18 experimental runs with three center points were generated using Design Expert and central composite design (CCD). P-DES (ChCl:Eg) was selected as the solvent for this process owing to its physicochemical properties and excellent extraction efficiency for date seed bioactive compounds (Fig. 1). The influence of independent variables (time (X1), speed (X2), and % DES content (X3)) on HAE and their corresponding responses (TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity) are presented in Table 2. The TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity values ranges from 22.87±0.9–––128.87±1.1 (mg GAE/g DSP) and 279.04±7.8–––1196.28±5.8 (mM TE/g DSP), respectively.

The results of the regression coefficients were used to create the second-order polynomial reduced equations (2), (3), as indicated in Table S2. The model's p-value was less than 0.0001, signifying that it was a highly significant model with R2 values of 0.9741 and 0.9751 for FRAP and TPC, respectively. Furthermore, the Adjusted R2 of 0.9616 and the predicted R2 of 0.9027 for TPC showed a reasonable agreement, meaning that the difference was less than 0.2. With a predicted R2 of 0.8723 that is reasonably in line with the Adjusted R2 of 0.9599, a similar pattern was noted for FRAP antioxidant activity.

The effects of the various parameters on TPC and FRAP are shown in Table S2. It is evident that the extraction time (X1) and % DES content (X3) had a significant (P<0.001) positive linear effect on the responses, implying that increasing the extraction time and % DES content (X3) could achieve higher extraction efficiency for TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity. There was also a significant interactive effect between the extraction time and DES content (X1 X3) and between the extraction time and speed (X1 X2) for both TPC and FRAP antioxidant activity.

Sequential hybrid techniques

The aim of this study was to investigate the synergistic effect of using binary (UHAE, UMAE, HUAE, HMAE, MUAE, and MHAE) and ternary (UHMAE, HUMAE, and MUHAE) hybrid extraction techniques under previously optimized conditions from green techniques. Specifically, the objective was to determine the extent to which the extraction efficiency of bioactive compounds from date seeds could be increased using hybrid extraction techniques. Significant differences p < 0.05 in TPC were observed among the binary and ternary extraction techniques in the following order: UMAE > HMAE > UHMAE > HUMAE > MUHAE > MHAE = MUAE > HUAE > UHAE as shown in Fig. 4a. The results of the hybrid extraction techniques, which involved the initial extraction of date seeds with UAE followed by HAE, produced a 12.7 % increase, whereas when the extraction technique was reversed in the sequence of operations starting with HAE, followed by UAE, it produced only 8.3 % increase in the total phenolic content. The increase in the phenolic content obtained using this binary hybrid technique may be attributed to the incomplete extraction of bioactive compounds from a single extraction technique coupled with DES (ChCl:Eg) as the extracting solvent. The binary hybrid extraction of UMAE and HMAE showed the highest extraction efficiency, with 52 % and 49 % increases in TPC, respectively. A possible explanation for the increased recovery of date seed polyphenols in the binary hybrid techniques could be the synergistic effect of UAE+MAE and HAE+MAE on increasing extraction efficiency. Sequential UMAE involves processes such as dielectric heating and acoustic cavitation, which are strongly associated with the amplitude of ultrasound, dipole rotation, and temperature of the microwave, can significantly influence the extraction efficiency [43]. While in the HAE and MAE (HMAE) process, a synergistic combination of high speed that disintegrates the cell wall matrix, irradiation temperature, and dipole rotation of the microwave produces internal heating that weakens the cell walls and boosts the mass transfer rates, with a significant release of polyphenols into the solvents. This synergetic effect of UMAE and HMAE upsets the cytoarchitecture and leads to an increase in the release of total polyphenols [44]. There were no significant differences in the increase in TPC when MAE was initially utilized in the extraction of date seeds, followed by UAE or HAE, which displayed increases of 7.3 % and 8.2 % respectively, for MUAE and MHAE.

Furthermore, three-fold ternary hybrid extraction techniques involve a combination of two binary hybrid techniques and a third additional method. When binary hybrid techniques involving UHAE and HUAE were further subjected to MAE, an increase of 38 % and 29 % in TPC level was observed in UHMAE and HUMAE, respectively. This increase in extraction efficiency may be due to the fact that the binary hybrid techniques of UHAE or HUAE could not have successively disrupted the cell wall matrix of the date seed bioactive compound; hence, after the binary treatment, date seed polyphenols were still bound to the cell wall, which was eventually released by application of ternary techniques. However, when the binary hybrid techniques involving MUAE were subjected to HAE (MUHAE), TPC decreased by 9 % (MUHAE), which could be attributed to the oxidation of polyphenol molecules. Similar trends were observed in the FRAP antioxidant activity for both the binary and ternary hybrid extraction techniques, as shown in Fig. 4b. The study of hybrid techniques revealed that no single extraction technique could successively disintegrate the tough date seed matrix, penetrate the matrix, and separate the targeted molecules from the cell wall matrix when P-DES (ChCl:Eg) was used as the extraction solvent. The hardness of date seed could further be justified by the study of Mubaiwa et al.,[45] who reported that date seeds possess a hard seed coat structure that necessitates heavy-duty grinding to reduce their size and produce powder. This characteristic presents challenges during the milling process, requiring a considerable amount of energy and potentially causing wear and tear to the machinery. Furthermore, DES is a viscous liquid that can retard the mobility of biomolecules, which may require further mechanical techniques such as MAE to successively mobilize the solvent and penetrate the disintegrated matrix to dissolve and separate compounds of interest from the date seed matrix. In summary, the hybrid techniques produced higher extraction efficiencies than the single green extraction techniques used. Additionally, this study revealed that a higher extraction efficiency was observed when MAE was employed in the later part of the hybrid extraction techniques. In the investigation of ternary hybrid extraction, the initial extraction of UHAE or HUAE followed by the application of MAE led to a considerable improvement in the extraction yield. A decrease in phenolic content was observed following the initial binary extraction of MUAE and subsequent treatment with HAE. This suggests that the initial extraction of date seed bioactive compounds using MAE extracted most of the bioactive compounds, a further application of UAE only led to a 7.3 % increase in TPC. However, the addition of a ternary technique (HAE) resulted in a 9 % decrease in TPC. This study revealed that binary hybrid extraction techniques are more suitable than ternary hybrid extraction techniques, provided that MAE is utilized in the later part of the extraction process.

Identification and quantification of individual phenolic compounds of date seeds using different extraction techniques

Using UHPLC, the primary phenolics in the date seed extract produced by the various methods were determined and quantified based on the external standard calibration curve, an HPLC reverse-phase C18 column, and retention time. Notably, different quantities of phenolic compounds at varied quantities were detected in the date seed extracts obtained using different green extraction techniques for each treatment. A total of ten (10) major phenolic compounds were identified in the date seed extract which includes, gallic acid, catechin hydrate, 1,2-dihydroxybenzoic acid, 4 hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillin, ferulic acid, coumaric acid, chlorogenic acid, rutin hydrate and quercetin as depicted in Table 6. The order of maximum number of phenolic compounds found in date seed extracts recovered using their quantity for green extraction techniques was UAE (8) = and MAE (8), followed by HAE (7). Catechin hydrate was the predominant phenolics found in date seeds with UAE-based extract having the highest content (85.4±4.3 mg/100g), followed by HAE (69.72±4.9 mg/100g) and MAE (7.36 mg/100g). Furthermore, chlorogenic acid was found to be predominant in MAE (22.95±0.7 mg/100 g) followed by HAE (9.94±2.7 mg/100 g) and UAE-derived (4.78±1.6 mg/100 g) extracts, while rutin hydrate was only present in HAE-derived extract (10.75±0.4 µg/100g). When phenolic extracts were prepared using hybrid techniques, UMAE (10) revealed highest number of phenolic compounds, followed by UHAE (9), MUAE (8), MHAE (8), HUAE and HMAE (7). The results indicated that the synergistic effect of the binary hybrid extraction techniques was able to recover higher date seed phenolics than the single and ternary hybrid extraction techniques, with catechin hydrate, 1,2-Dihydroxybenzoic acid and 4-Hydroxybenzoic acid being the most predominant date seed phenolics. The number of phenolic compounds detected in ternary hybrid techniques followed the order UHMAE (10) > MUHAE (9) > HUMAE (8), with catechin hydrate, 1,2-Dihydroxybenzoic acid and quercetin being the predominant phenolics, although quercetin was not present detected in HUMAE. The HPLC results from this finding showed that the synergistic effect of UMAE and UHMAE might have completely disrupted the cell wall matrix more effectively, leading to a higher quantity recovery of the date seed phenolic bioactive compounds released [44], which was also similar to their TPC and antioxidant activity, as shown in Fig. 4a and 4b. The hybrid techniques involving an initial extraction of date seeds with UAE followed by MAE enhanced the release of more date seed bioactive compounds compared to other combinations.

Antimicrobial activity of DES and date seed extracted with different emerging techniques

The ability of date seed extract to inhibit microbial growth provides evidence for its potential application as an additive in food preservation. The antibacterial activity of pure P-DES (ChCl:Eg) and date seed DES-based extracts treated with different extraction techniques, including HAE, MAE, and UAE, was evaluated using the broth dilution method to record the MIC and dot plating method for MBC against two Gram-positive bacteria (Listeria monocytogenes and Staphylococcus aureus) and two Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium), as shown in Table 7. The 80 % of the pure P-DES (ChCL:Eg) sample was used to investigate antimicrobial activity of all the tested bacterial strains, and the lowest MIC and MBC of P-DES (ChCl:Eg) recorded was 40 %. The MIC of pure polyol-based DES (choline chloride: glycerol) against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria was reported to be 25 % by Jurić et al. [44], which is comparable to the current data. Staphylococcus aureus, one of the Gram positive bacteria, was resistant to P-DES (ChCl:Eg) bactericidal action. This may be because Gram positive bacteria have a thicker peptidoglycan coating that makes them less vulnerable to NADES/DES [45], [46]. With the exception of the date seed P-DES based extract treated with MAE, which had a different MIC and MBC values of 10 % and 20 % respectively; accounting for 1.37 and 2.72 mg/ml of its TPC. The date seed extracts derived from the UAE and HAE exhibited the same lowest MIC and MBC of 20 % against the tested bacterial strains; accounting for TPC of 2.06 and 2.18 mg/ml for UAE and HAE, respectively. The date seed P-DES based extracts demonstrated significant antibacterial properties against selected bacterial strains. This effectiveness can be attributed to the polyphenolic compounds within the extracts, which likely interact with the bacterial cell membranes through hydrophobic interactions. These interactions enhance membrane permeability and disrupt cellular structures [19], [46]. The resultant excessive loss of essential cellular ions and molecules primarily contributes to bacterial cell death [47]. Furthermore, it could be assumed that P-DES (ChCl:Eg) contributed to the antibacterial activity of the date seed extract against the tested bacterial strains. In P-DES-based extract samples, the interaction between P-DES and the date seed extract may have a synergistic effect, amplifying their antimicrobial efficacy.

The total bacterial count (TBC) and coliforms of Oreochromis niloticus fillets treated with 10 % of date seeds extract recovered from MAE coupled with ChCl:Eg and untreated Oreochromis niloticus fillets stored at 4 °C for 10 days. On day 5, the untreated sample's TBC was approximately 6.0 log CFU/g, which implies that Oreochromis niloticus fillets which have not been treated can be stored for five days at 4 °C, as per the ICMSF (2002) study, which states that the acceptability limit for both freshwater and marine species is 6.0 log CFU/g. The TBC of the treated sample was TBC 4.3 log CFU/g on day 5, which was still within the normal range for the freshwater and marine species samples. As the storage period increased to 10 days, the TBC of both treated and untreated Oreochromis niloticus fillets significantly increased (p<0.05) to 5.33 and 7.5 log CFU/g, respectively. The treated samples on day 10 were still within the general acceptability limit, which is safe for consumption. This result revealed the potency of date seed extract in inhibiting microbial growth in Oreochromis niloticus during refrigerated storage. Olatunde et al., [19] reported a reduction in TBC for Asian sea bass slices treated with coconut husk extract as compared to untreated samples. Furthermore, the sanitary quality of fillets was assessed using coliform, which was observed on day 5, and the microbial coliform loads of the treated and untreated samples were within the acceptable safe limit for consumption. As the storage time increased to 10 days, the microbial coliform load in the untreated sample was beyond the acceptability limit, with a log cycle load of 6.9 CFU/g unlike the treated samples, which showed a load of 5.6 CFC/g. The results demonstrated the efficient antimicrobial effect of the P-DES-based date seed extract in retarding microbial growth, which could be attributed to the antibacterial properties of date seed polyphenolic compounds. The antimicrobial properties of plant extracts are attributed to their capacity to interact with bacterial cells and disrupt cell wall components, leading to their destruction [48]. Selim et al. [33] reported that gallic acid from date seed polyphenols can inhibit both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, buttressing the antimicrobial effects of date seed extracts.

Conclusion

Date seed bioactive compounds were extracted using polyol-based deep eutectic solvents (P-DESs) coupled with green extraction techniques comprising HAE, MAE, and UAE. Among the various poly-based DES, choline chloride: ethylene glycol (ChCl:Eg) stood out as the most efficient green solvent, whereas MAE was the most efficient green extraction technique. A conventional extraction method was also employed in the extraction of date seed bioactive compounds, using 80 % ChCl:Eg, ethanol, and methanol. The ChCl:Eg has a superior extracting solvent than traditional solvents, as evidenced by the order of phenolic recovery, which was 80 % ChCl:Eg > Ethanol > Methanol. In contrast to the traditional extraction approach, all green extraction techniques yielded increased bioactive component recovery. Central composite design (CCD) and response surface methodology (RSM) were used to improve the three extraction methods (HAE, MAE, and UAE) in conjunction with P-DES (ChCl:Eg); and the revealed that MAE was the most efficient extraction techniques. The optimized extraction conditions were further employed in the hybrid extraction techniques, which involved six binary and three ternary techniques to determine the percentage increase in the extraction efficacies of date seed bioactive phenolics. The binary hybrid extraction techniques consisting of UAE and MAE (UMAE) and HAE and MAE (HMAE) recovered significantly higher bioactive compounds with 52 % and 49 % increases in the total phenolic content compared to single extraction techniques. The lowest MIC and MBC of P-DES (ChCl:Eg) and date seed P-DES based extract recorded against all the tested bacterial strains was 40 % and 20% respectively; with exception to date seed P-DES based extract obtained from MAE. Furthermore, date seed phenolic extract was used to extend the shelf life of Oreochromis niloticus fillets stored at 4 °C for 10 days. The results revealed the potency of date seed extract in inhibiting microbial growth in Oreochromis niloticus during refrigerated storage, as all the treated samples were within the recommended acceptability limit of < 6 log CFU/g compared to the untreated samples, which was > 6 log CFU/g. This study revealed the different extraction efficiencies and antimicrobial activities of date seed bioactive compounds extracted using various green extraction techniques. In addition, sequential hybrid techniques booted and intensified the recovery of bioactive compounds compared to single green techniques.
