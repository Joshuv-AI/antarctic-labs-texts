Automated inspection of P&ID object recognition using deep learning
Authors: Ji-Beob Kim, Yoochan Moon, Seung-Tae Han, Duhwan Mun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Numerous studies have focused on digitizing piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs) to enhance their applicability across industries. Even with the application of digitization technology, correcting errors in object recognition remains time-consuming, and because correction is performed manually, errors may persist. To address these issues, we propose a novel method for inspecting object recognition results in P&IDs. For unrecognized object inspection, patches are generated, and a deep learning-based classifier identifies missing elements. For misrecognized object inspection, optimal inspection methods are applied depending on the type of error, enabling effective detection of misrecognized objects. Specifically, the proposed misrecognition inspection methods include deep learning-based feature vector similarity calculation, text error detection based on distance-based detection, and line error detection through intersection-case inspection method. The proposed method was validated through experiments conducted using P&IDs from actual industrial sites. The results showed that unrecognized object inspection achieved a recall of 100%. Misrecognized object inspection achieved an accuracy of 99.2% and an F1 score of 96.7% for symbols, an accuracy of 95.8% and an F1 score of 97.3% for text, and 100% accuracy and F1 score for lines. Overall, error correction time was reduced by approximately 40%.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25506-2.

Introduction

A piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) is a widely used engineering diagram that provides detailed information about the equipment, piping, and instrumentation used to monitor, measure, and control industrial processes. Therefore, P&IDs serve as primary documents for the operation, maintenance, and safety management of process plants. They include components such as pipes, valves, instruments, and control systems, along with process lines that transport materials and signal lines that transmit analog or digital signals for measurement and control. In the diagram, components are represented by standardized symbols; process and signal lines are shown as lines, with arrows indicating the direction of the flow. Additionally, tag numbers, line numbers, and other annotations related to these elements are displayed as text.

P&IDs are essential for various applications such as process design verification, risk assessment, process analysis, simulation, operational monitoring, and regulatory compliance, and thus must be managed in a structured digital format compatible with computer processing.

However, due to the project-based nature of plant construction, the involvement of multiple stakeholders, and the long lifecycle spanning several decades, a large volume of unstructured P&IDs continues to be used in industrial sites, which significantly reduces the efficiency of P&ID utilization.

To address these challenges, numerous studies have focused on the digitization of unstructured P&IDs1–6. The process typically involves three main stages: recognition of symbols, texts, lines, and arrows; identification of connectivity among the recognized objects; and the generation of a digital P&ID. During recognition, the objects are classified, and bounding boxes are computed for each object. Connectivity identification follows, in which the relationships between the recognized components are established, enabling the reconstruction of the topological structure. In the final stage, the corresponding shape of each object is retrieved from a symbol catalog and placed within its bounding box. Additionally, engineering data extracted from the associated text are stored as attributes of the corresponding objects.

Previous studies on P&ID digitization have reported that despite the use of optical character recognition (OCR), deep learning, image processing, and rule-based approaches, fully recognizing symbols and lines remains challenging, and errors persist in the recognition results. Therefore, before advancing to the second stage of digitization—connectivity identification among objects—a manual inspection and correction process is required to address misrecognized components. This manual process accounts for the majority of the digitization time1. Additionally, errors may still occur because of the manual nature of the inspection. Therefore, developing a technology to compare the original P&ID with the recognition results would enable the identification and correction of errors.

In this paper, we propose a new approach for inspecting object recognition results in P&IDs. The proposed framework consists of two stages: detecting unrecognized objects and identifying misrecognized objects. In the unrecognized object detection stage, the original P&ID image is first preprocessed to generate inspection patches. These patches are then fed into a multi-label classification deep learning model to detect unrecognized objects. The misrecognized object identification stage employs different methods depending on the object type. For symbols and arrows, recognition errors are determined by calculating similarity with reference images using a deep learning-based method. For lines, errors are identified using both similarity calculation and an intersection-case inspection method. For texts, inspection images are generated based on the detected coordinates, and OCR is applied to detect recognition errors.

The academic contributions of this study are as follows: First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to inspect the recognition results of all objects in P&IDs. Second, the inspection of recognition results is categorized into two types: unrecognized object inspection and misrecognized object inspection, and optimized inspection methods are developed for each. Third, by applying the proposed method, the inspection time was reduced while improving the accuracy compared to manual inspection methods.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the related research. Section 3 explains the overall method for inspecting object recognition results. Section 4 proposes a method for inspecting unrecognized objects and Sect. 5 presents a method for inspecting misrecognized objects. Section 6 discusses the experimental results obtained by applying the proposed inspection methods to P&IDs used in industry. Finally, Sect. 7 concludes the study.

Related study

Deep learning-based image classification

In recent years, deep learning-based image classification has emerged as one of the most important research topics in the field of computer vision. Image classification involves assigning a given image to a specific class, and deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have gained significant attention for their outstanding performance in this domain7–9.

Research in image classification has advanced significantly with developments in CNNs. LeNet-5, an early CNN model, demonstrated high performance in handwritten digit recognition and established the fundamental structure of CNNs10. Later, AlexNet’s victory in the ImageNet competition marked a major breakthrough in image classification using deep neural networks11. AlexNet achieved high classification performance by utilizing multiple convolutional and pooling layers12,13. Subsequently, several CNN-based models have been developed. VGGNet enhances performance by adopting a deeper network architecture, whereas GoogLeNet introduces an inception module to improve both computational efficiency and accuracy14–17. ResNet proposes a residual learning technique to address the training challenges associated with deeper networks, enabling stable training even in very deep models18–20.

In recent years, transfer learning has been used to improve the performance of image classification21. Transfer learning leverages models pretrained on large-scale datasets to achieve good classification performance even with small datasets. A representative approach is fine-tuning, in which models pretrained on the ImageNet dataset are adapted to target domains22,23. Data augmentation is also employed to enhance classification performance24–26. Methods such as rotation, scaling, and color modification are used to address problems caused by class imbalance or limited training data. In addition, regularization techniques such as batch normalization and dropout contribute to improving image classification performance27–29.

Recently, transformer-based vision architectures with attention mechanisms have gained attention30–32. Among them, the vision transformer divides an image into patches and processes them as sequences, achieving classification performance that surpasses CNNs when trained on large-scale datasets.

CNNs, which serve as the foundation of these image classification models, are also used in the field of object detection. YOLO, a widely used object detection framework, is built on CNNs. YOLO predicts both the location and category of objects in an image simultaneously, and compared to traditional CNNs, it significantly improves both computational speed and detection accuracy, making it suitable for various industrial applications33–35.

Object recognition and error inspection in engineering drawings

Object recognition in engineering drawings involves identifying various objects, such as mechanical components, piping, and electrical elements, through automated systems. This process is crucial in industrial applications, as it reduces time and costs while improving the accuracy of drawing interpretation and design change management. Related studies have focused on symbol and text recognition, line and arrow recognition, and line classification (Table 1).

First, we reviewed studies related to symbol and text recognition. Mani et al.3 proposed a pipeline that performed symbol recognition, text interpretation, and connection detection through lines for the automatic digitization of P&IDs. Notably, they were the first to utilize graph traversal and applied a CNN model with high scalability and low density. Kim et al.4 proposed a deep learning-based method for recognizing symbols and text in P&ID images, developing a pipeline that includes image preprocessing, symbol and text recognition, and result storage. Yun et al.36 proposed a CNN-based model to detect symbols in P&IDs. They employed k-means and deep adaptive clustering to handle negative classes. Yang et al.37 proposed a framework for recognizing single-line diagrams. To prevent interference between objects during recognition, they separated text and symbols, and they addressed class imbalance through data augmentation. In addition, they effectively detected symbols of various sizes using a swin transformer-based faster region CNN.

The following studies are related to line and arrow recognition. Moon et al.5 proposed an improved line detection method by enhancing a continuous line detection algorithm for P&ID digitization. Yu et al.38 developed a deep learning-based object recognition framework for P&IDs. They combined AlexNet, a connectionist text proposal network (CTPN), and traditional image-processing techniques to detect symbols, text, lines, and tables. Song et al.39 used a faster region CNN and CTPN to recognize objects in electrical drawings. They performed symbol, text, and line detection, as well as topological connection analysis.

The following studies are related to line classification. Han et al.6 used a rule-based method to classify continuous lines recognized in P&IDs. Based on the shapes and positional relationships of objects, they classified continuous lines into eight categories. Zhang et al.40 applied a graph convolutional neural network to classify components in engineering drawings into semantic types. They vectorized raster images and generated a graph based on the connectivity between components to classify them into text, dimension lines, and contour lines.

Although research on object recognition in engineering drawings has been actively conducted, studies on inspecting errors in the recognition results remain relatively scarce. Rica et al.41 proposed a method for verifying symbol recognition results in P&IDs by comparing the recognition and predicted identities using graph-based embeddings and neural networks. As shown in Table 1, their approach inspects errors based on Computer-Aided Design (CAD) data generated from the object recognition results, focusing solely on symbols. In contrast, this study examines all recognized objects and adopts an approach that directly processes and verifies the recognition results, aiming to minimize the time required for digitization.

Object recognition result inspection method

Data used for inspection

To study the inspection method for P&ID object recognition results, P&IDs from actual industrial settings were used. Based on previous studies, symbols, texts, lines, and arrows in the P&ID were recognized4–6. Object recognition results are stored in an XML file, with the structure shown in Fig. 1. Symbols and texts are recorded under the < symbol_object > tag, while lines and arrows are recorded under the < line_object > tag.

Fig. 1Format of recognized object information.

Format of recognized object information.

The symbol_object tag includes the type, class, and position tags4. The type tag defines whether an object is a symbol or text. If the type is a symbol, the class tag specifies the symbol it represents; if the type is text, the class tag contains the recognized characters extracted from the object image. The position tag represents the bounding box coordinates of the object. In Fig. 1, the xmin and ymin tags denote the coordinates of the top left corner of the bounding box, whereas the xmax and ymax tags indicate the coordinates of the bottom right corner.

The line_object tag includes the type, class, position, and flow tags5,6. The type tag represents the line type, which is classified based on its shape. The class tag provides detailed classification results for lines of the type continuous_thick_line, which refers to a solid line that serves a specific function. The position tag indicates the location of the line within the image. In Fig. 1, the xstart and ystart tags represent the coordinates of the starting point of the line, whereas the xend and yend tags represent the coordinates of the ending point. The flow tag generates child-arrow tags based on the number of arrows that merge with the line. Each arrow tag consists of a type, direction, and location tag. The type tag classifies the arrow based on its shape. The direction tag specifies the direction in which arrows points: a value of 0 indicates the arrow points toward the starting point of the line, while a value of 1 indicates the arrow points toward the ending point. The location tag represents the relative position of the arrow along the line. The formula used to calculate this location is shown in Fig. 1.

Error cases in object recognition

The object recognition accuracy of P&IDs, based on previous studies, has been reported to be in the low 90% range, with various types of errors occurring during object recognition4–6. As shown in Fig. 2, errors in P&ID object recognition can be categorized into two types: unrecognized and misrecognized. Unrecognized errors occur when an object present in the P&ID fails to be recognized. Misrecognized errors occur when the information of an object is incorrectly identified. The object recognition results consist of three elements: position, type, and class. Misrecognized errors can be further classified into position errors, type-classification errors, and class-classification errors. Position errors occur when the position information of an object is incorrect. This may include cases where an object is incorrectly identified in terms of its location or when unnecessary objects are recognized. Type-classification errors occur when an object is incorrectly classified. Class-classification errors occur when an object is incorrectly assigned to the wrong class within its category.

Fig. 2Recognition error types: unrecognized and misrecognized errors.

Recognition error types: unrecognized and misrecognized errors.

Details of object recognition errors are presented in Table 2. Recognition errors are highlighted in red, while the correct annotations are shown in green. For symbols, position errors, class-classification errors, and unrecognized errors occur. Position errors are classified into cases where the bounding box is incorrectly generated or where other objects are over-recognized as symbols. Class-classification errors occur due to the nature of symbols, as many types have similar shapes. For texts, position errors and class-classification errors are the main issues. Position errors arise when only part of the text is recognized, resulting in separation, or when the rotation angle is incorrectly estimated during recognition. Rotation angle errors occur due to insufficient training data, since most texts in P&IDs are horizontally aligned, and also due to the periodicity problem of the regression-based prediction method42. Class-classification errors mainly occur when special characters are included. For lines, position errors occur when only part of a line is recognized, especially for ‘O sign line’ type or when a symbol is misrecognized as a line, and type-classification errors occur when line discontinuities or thickness are misjudged. For arrows, unrecognized errors are the primary error, typically occurring when surrounding objects obscure the arrow recognition. Across all object types, unrecognized errors tend to occur when objects are densely packed in the image, increasing the overall complexity.

P&ID object recognition procedure using the recognized object inspection method

To address the various errors that occur during object recognition, the recognized object inspection method is broadly divided into two categories: unrecognized and misrecognized object inspection.

Unrecognized object inspection involves generating inspection patches and using a deep learning model to inspect unrecognized objects within these patches. Methods for generating inspection patches can be divided into two approaches. The first approach assumes that unrecognized objects are located outside the recognized objects. In this case, the bounding boxes of the recognized objects in the original P&ID image are filled with the background color. The P&ID image is then divided into patches of a fixed size. The second approach assumes that unrecognized objects exist within the recognized objects. A symbol may contain text, lines, or arrows within its recognized bounding box. If the recognized symbol is expected to enclose other objects within its bounding box, it is converted into an image patch. The generated patches are preprocessed and fed into a deep learning model that determines whether a patch contains an unrecognized object. Further details are provided in Sect. 4.

Misrecognized object inspection adopts different approaches depending on the type of object in order to detect errors. First, symbols and arrows are classified into types/classes using a deep learning model. In addition, feature vectors are extracted from the same model and compared with predefined reference vectors. If the cosine similarity between the two vectors does not exceed a given threshold, the object is determined to be misrecognized. For texts, inspection images are generated based on recognition information for error checking. OCR is then applied to the generated images to determine misrecognition. For lines, the same method used for symbols and arrows is applied. However, due to the characteristics of lines, certain errors cannot be detected using similarity calculation alone, so an intersection-case inspection method is additionally applied. Detailed explanations are provided in Sect. 5.

The overall P&ID recognition procedure with the proposed inspection method is shown in Fig. 3, and further details are provided in Appendix A. First, a deep learning model recognizes symbols and texts in the P&ID image, and their classes and types are assigned4 (Fig. 3-a). A misrecognition inspection is then carried out to check for errors in these results (Fig. 3-a1). At this stage, unrecognized objects are not inspected because lines and arrows have not yet been processed. Details of this step are explained in Sect. 5. If errors are found, the symbol and text information is corrected (Fig. 3-a2). Next, lines and arrows are detected and their classes and types are classified5 (Fig. 3-b). Line class classification is not yet applied. Misrecognition inspection is again performed for lines and arrows (Fig. 3-b1), as described in Sect. 5. Any errors are corrected in this step (Fig. 3-b2). Afterward, unrecognized object inspection is conducted based on the corrected results (Fig. 3-c). Section 4 provides details of this process. If unrecognized objects are found, they are added using the object creation tool (Fig. 3-c1). Finally, line class classification is performed6 (Fig. 3-d). As described in Sect. 3.1, this step further categorizes continuous thick lines by function. Since its accuracy is about 97–99% with very few errors6, errors from this stage are excluded from the inspection process.

Unrecognized object inspection

Unrecognized object inspection is conducted as follows. First, inspection patches are generated using the object recognition results and the P&ID image. The method for generating inspection patches is explained in Sect. 4.1. Then, a deep learning multi-label classification model is used to determine whether the inspection patch contains unrecognized objects. The classification method for the inspection patches is described in Sect. 4.2.

Inspection patch generation

An inspection patch is a sub-image that is likely to contain an unrecognized object. The purpose of generating inspection patches is to reduce inspection time by allowing users to focus on smaller areas rather than scanning the entire P&ID image. The two methods for generating inspection patches are based on the location of the unrecognized object.

Patch generation method for unrecognized objects located outside other objects

This section explains the method for generating inspection patches when an unrecognized object is located outside a recognized object. Figure 4 illustrates the procedure for generating inspection patches. First, the original P&ID image is binarized. Image binarization converts all pixels in the image to either black or white. Next, the colors are inverted, making the pixels corresponding to non-background areas white and those corresponding to background areas black. Subsequently, in the preprocessed P&ID image, the regions corresponding to the bounding boxes of recognized objects are filled with black.

Fig. 4Method for generating inspection patches for unrecognized objects.

Method for generating inspection patches for unrecognized objects.

After masking all recognized objects, the P&ID image is divided into multiple patches using a sliding window method, as shown in Fig. 4. The patch size is determined based on object size, excluding lines due to their large variation in length. To obtain object size information, the legend containing object shape details is used. The width and height of all objects are then plotted as histograms, and outliers such as large symbols are removed. This is because generating patches with excessively large sizes may cause too many objects to be included in a single patch, which increases the risk of readers overlooking them. Finally, the maximum approximated size of the objects, excluding outliers, is selected as the width and height of the patch.

However, the patches generated in this way cannot guarantee that objects are always fully contained while preserving their shape. To address this, stride was applied during patch generation so that patches overlap. This ensures that even if an object is cropped at the boundary of one patch, it can be fully contained in another patch. Furthermore, even when objects are partially cropped in some patches, their main shapes remain identifiable, preventing issues in human interpretation within patches.

Symbols and texts can directly have their bounding boxes generated using the recognition coordinates. However, lines and arrows require a bounding box generation process, as shown in Fig. 5. The method for generating line bounding boxes (LBPs 1–4, see Fig. 5) is as follows. If the line slope is between − 90° and 90°, the bounding box coordinates are determined by adding and subtracting half of the line thickness (wline) from the y-coordinates of the start and end points. If the line slope is −90°, the bounding box coordinates are determined by adding and subtracting half of the line thickness from the x-coordinates of the start and end points. The method for generating arrow bounding boxes (ABPs 1–4, see Fig. 5) is as follows: The arrow points (xarrow, yarrow) are identified in the image using the location information from the recognition results. Then, the bounding box coordinates are generated using the degree information, arrow thickness (warrow), and arrow height (harrow). Finally, the bounding box is rotated according to the slope of the merged line with respect to the arrowhead point to adjust the coordinates.

Process for generating bounding boxes for lines and arrows.

Patch generation method for unrecognized objects located inside other objects

In certain cases, unrecognized objects are located within the bounding box of a recognized symbol. Only a few symbols exhibit this characteristic and are referred to as composite symbols. The class of a composite symbol, containing the object shape information, can be identified using the legend. Table 3 presents the composite symbols included in the P&IDs used in this study. The larger symbols in Table 3 represent significant equipment used in the P&ID. Although most composite symbols contain only texts, spec_breaker symbols include texts, lines, and arrows.

Unrecognized objects within composite symbols cannot be detected using the inspection patches generated by the method described in Sect. 4.1.1. Therefore, a different inspection patch generation method is required for these cases. The patch generation method for unrecognized objects within composite symbols follows these steps. First, the P&ID image is preprocessed according to the method shown in Fig. 4. However, the interior of the bounding box of the composite symbols is not filled. Next, the images of the recognized composite symbols are cropped from the P&ID image to generate inspection patches.

Checking the presence of unrecognized objects in inspection patches

Examples of the generated inspection patches are shown in Fig. 4. The inspection patches are classified into those that contain unrecognized objects and those that do not. Most of the inspection patches generated by dividing the P&IDs, which do not contain unrecognized objects, include meaningless objects. Meaningless objects include table borders, text-highlighting shapes, and small symbols. Meaningless objects have shapes similar to unrecognized objects, making them difficult to distinguish using conventional methods. Therefore, a deep learning technique is used to filter inspection patches that contain only meaningless objects.

The process for determining whether an unrecognized object is present within an inspection patch is as follows: First, inspection patches containing no objects are filtered out. Because these patches consist only of the background color, they are filtered based on the pixel values. Next, as shown in Fig. 6, the filtered inspection patches are input into a deep learning model to determine whether they contain unrecognized objects. At this stage, because the inspection patches generated by segmenting the P&ID image have a fixed size, they can be resized and input into the deep learning model. However, the inspection patches generated from composite symbols vary in size. Therefore, a deep learning model with an added spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) layer, which can handle variable-sized inputs, is used for inspection43. The SPP layer applies pooling at multiple scales to the input feature map, producing a fixed-size output. This allows the model to generate a consistent output vector, regardless of the input image size.

Fig. 6Deep learning network for unrecognized object inspection.

Deep learning network for unrecognized object inspection.

In this study, the labels of inspection patches were constructed in a multi-label format to distinguish unrecognized objects from meaningless objects. This approach enables simultaneous prediction of the presence of symbols, texts, lines, arrows, and meaningless objects within an inspection patch. However, inspection patches containing unrecognized objects are fewer in number compared to those containing meaningless objects, which leads to label imbalance. To address this issue, a new loss function was designed. The loss function is based on Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and incorporates class weights as well as the concept of focal loss. As a result, the contribution of positive labels containing unrecognized objects is increased, and the model can effectively learn even from hard samples. The designed loss function is defined in Eq. For the classification model to effectively apply this loss function, a ResNet-based architecture, which is widely used in the field of image classification, was employed44. ResNet stacks multiple residual blocks to form a 50-layer deep network, using the bottleneck residual block structure. Each bottleneck block splits the input into two paths. The first path applies a sequence of 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and 1 × 1 convolution layers. Batch normalization is applied between the convolutional layers, and ReLU is used as the activation function to enhance training stability. The second path retains the input unchanged and adds it to the output from the first path. This residual connection facilitates efficient gradient flow and helps mitigate the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. The network is organized into four main blocks: Block 1 uses 64 filters, Block 2 uses 128 filters, Block 3 uses 256 filters, and Block 4 uses 512 filters. The overall architecture consists of an input layer, initial convolutional layers followed by max pooling, four residual blocks (Block 1–4, see Fig. 6), an SPP layer for handling variable input sizes, and a fully connected (FC) layer with multiple nodes for multi-label classification.

Misrecognized object inspection

Misrecognition cases that occur during object recognition are described in Sect. 3.2. Accordingly, the misrecognized object inspection process is conducted as illustrated in Fig. 7. First, inspection images are generated for symbols, lines, and arrows. These images are generated differently depending on the type of object. Then, deep learning is used to extract feature vectors from each inspection image. Misrecognition is determined by calculating the cosine similarity between the extracted feature vector and the feature vector extracted from the corresponding reference image. Detailed information on this process is provided in Sect. 5.1. For texts, an image is generated based on the recognized coordinates, preprocessed, and then passed through an OCR process. The resulting string is compared with the recognition result to determine whether a misrecognition has occurred. In cases where only part of the string is recognized, a distance-based error detection method is also applied to assess misrecognition of texts. This is further explained in Sect. 5.2. For lines, in addition to similarity-based error detection, line-symbol confusion errors are identified by intersection-case inspection method. This process is described in Sect. 5.3.

Generation of inspection and reference images

To inspect misrecognized objects, the recognition results are converted into images. The method for generating these inspection images is shown in Fig. 8-(a). For symbols, the recognized region is directly cropped to create the inspection image. For arrows, the bounding box coordinates are calculated using the method described in Fig. 5, and the inspection image is generated in the same manner as for symbols. Since lines can vary greatly in length compared to other objects, they are processed not as entire line images but in segments. Here, a line segment refers to a cropped image of a certain length that sufficiently preserves the features of the corresponding line. Table 4 presents feature images and the corresponding segment lengths for each line type. Based on this information, the inspection image is generated by cropping a predefined length centered on the midpoint of the line. If the total length of the line is shorter than the feature length, the entire line image is used as the inspection image.

Image generation method: (a) Inspection target object, (b) Reference image generation.

The method for generating these reference images is shown in Fig. 8-(b). Although symbols, lines, and arrows exhibit some variations among individual instances, they generally have standardized shapes and a limited number of types. Therefore, the reference images were manually extracted from original P&IDs and labeled according to their respective types and classes. To account for variations in orientation and noise, multiple versions of reference images were created for each class. However, since not all P&IDs within the project were used to generate reference images, some object types may be missing due to the limitations of manual labeling. Similarity-based misrecognized object inspection method

Traditional image comparison methods often require significant computational time for similarity calculations and can be vulnerable to noise45. The inspection images generated using the method in Sect. 5.1 may contain cropped sections of objects or parts of connected objects, which can introduce noise during the image comparison. Because of the importance of minimizing noise and reducing the time required for P&ID digitization, a deep learning-based approach was used for image comparison.

The process of calculating the similarity between the inspection image and the reference image is shown in Fig. 9. This process consists of two steps: the first step is to identify class/type classification errors, and the second step is to detect positional errors. To determine class/type classification errors, the inspection image is first input into a deep learning model to classify the type of object. For symbols, whose sizes vary, a model combining ResNet-50 with an SPP layer is used, while for lines and arrows, whose sizes are consistent, ResNet-50 is applied. The predicted results are then compared with the recognition results. If the two results do not match, the object is judged to be misrecognized, which corresponds to a class/type classification error.

However, classification results alone cannot fully detect positional errors. Therefore, when the predicted and recognized results are consistent, an additional method is required to determine positional errors. For this purpose, an inspection feature vector is extracted from a layer prior to the classification layer of the deep learning model. Reference feature vectors are precomputed and stored in advance using the same model structure, with multiple reference vectors included for each class. The cosine similarity is then calculated between the inspection feature vector and the averaged reference feature vector belonging to the predicted class. Misrecognized text inspection using OCR and distance-based error detection method

In text recognition, the most common errors are position errors and class-classification errors. Class-classification errors typically occur due to noise in the diagram or incorrect estimation of the text’s rotation angle during recognition. To inspect such errors, an image is first generated that includes the target text along with nearby objects. These surrounding objects are included to estimate the reference angle (θ) of the text. The reference angle is estimated using canny edge detection followed by the Hough transform. In parallel, noise removal preprocessing such as binarization and blurring is applied to improve OCR accuracy. Then, based on the recognized bounding box, the text object image is extracted and rotated by the inverse of the reference angle (-θ) to achieve horizontal alignment. The bounding box is also adjusted to fit the rotated image. The resulting text image is fed into a custom OCR model to extract the character string. The custom OCR model is a fine-tuned version of a commercial OCR model, designed to improve recognition of special characters. Finally, the extracted string is compared with the initially recognized string to detect recognition errors. This process is illustrated in Fig. 10-(a).

Misrecognized line inspection using intersection-case inspection method

Most errors in line recognition can be inspected using the similarity calculation method described in Sect. 5.1. However, among line position errors, line-symbol confusion cannot be inspected using shape-based methods. Therefore, intersection-case inspection method is employed to inspect line-symbol confusion, which relies on the spatial relationship between the line and the symbol. This process is shown in Fig. 11. In P&ID diagrams, lines are connected to symbols or other lines. In this case, the boundary between the line and the symbol must be clearly distinguished. Discussions

A portion of the results obtained by applying the proposed method to the test P&IDs is presented in Fig. 17. Unrecognized errors were successfully detected for symbols, lines, and arrows. However, it tends to confuse noise that resembles lines with meaningful objects. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. 14, these cases account for only a small portion of the total objects, and users can distinguish the noise patches classified as unrecognized objects in a short amount of time. Inspection performance for misrecognized errors varied depending on the object type. For symbols, both position errors and class-classification errors were identified by calculating similarity with reference images. While errors involving general symbols were well detected, some position and class-classification errors for large symbols were missed, which is presumed to be due to insufficient training data. Additionally, there were cases where correctly recognized objects were misclassified as errors; in all such cases, flanges were incorrectly classified as flange nozzles. A flange nozzle has the shape of a flange with an additional line attached, and the similarity arises because of adjacent lines connected to the flange. This problem can be addressed by introducing post-processing techniques that distinguish the boundaries between symbols and other objects and by expanding the training dataset.

Examples of Inspection results.

For texts, errors in angle estimation and type classification occurred during the text recognition stage, mainly due to the angle estimation errors of the deep learning model mentioned in Sect. 3 and the presence of noise. In this study, the text angle was re-estimated based on the recognition results. This approach was generally accurate; however, in some cases, nearby diagonal lines caused incorrect angle estimation, which led to distorted OCR outputs.

In addition, a distance-based error detection method was applied to identify string separation errors caused by noise, and this method successfully detected such separated strings. For more accurate inspection in the future, noise-handling techniques should be improved and the angle estimation algorithm should be made more robust.

For lines, the proposed method effectively detected errors such as partial recognition of O sign lines and type-classification errors by calculating similarity with reference images. In addition, line-symbol confusion errors, which are difficult to detect through shape classification alone, were successfully identified using the intersection-case inspection method. However, lines connected to annotations were treated as exceptions and were not included in the inspection process. Therefore, it is necessary to develop additional algorithms to handle these cases.

Conclusions

We propose a novel method for inspecting errors that occur during P&ID recognition. Based on the classification of error types and analysis of cases, an optimized inspection method was developed for each error type. To inspect unrecognized objects, inspection patches were generated and classified. To inspect misrecognized objects, errors occurring during the recognition of symbols, texts, lines, and arrows were analyzed, and inspection methods tailored to each error type were developed. The applied inspection methods include deep learning-based similarity calculation, text OCR, distance-based error detection, and line-symbol confusion detection through the intersection-case inspection method.

An experiment was conducted to evaluate and validate the proposed method. The test results on three P&IDs used in actual industrial settings demonstrated that the proposed technique exhibited excellent object inspection performance. For unrecognized object inspection, all unrecognized objects were successfully detected, achieving a recall of 100%. For misrecognized object inspection, the average accuracy and f1-score for symbols were 99.2% and 96.7%, respectively. For the texts, the accuracy was 95.8%, and the f1-score was 97.3%. For lines, both the accuracy and f1-score reached 100%. Additionally, when using the proposed method, the error correction time was reduced by over 40% compared to manual correction methods. These experimental results demonstrate that applying the proposed method can enhance the accuracy of the recognition results provided to users and reduce the costs associated with P&ID digitization.

In future work, we plan to develop an algorithm to distinguish line-shaped noise, which is the main source of errors in unrecognized object inspection. We also aim to enhance the classification deep learning network by improving data augmentation techniques. These advancements are expected to further improve the performance of the recognition result inspection methods. Furthermore, the proposed method will be expanded to accommodate various types of drawings beyond P&IDs. Moreover, based on the proposed post-recognition inspection method, we intend to further reduce manual correction and, in the long term, extend it into an automatic correction technique for recognition errors, thereby increasing the level of automation in P&ID digitization.
