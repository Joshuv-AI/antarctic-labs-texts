The mediating effect of self-compassion and supportive communication between critical thinking and emotional intelligence in Chinese male nursing students
Authors: Xuemei Zhu, Wei Yang, Han Su, Huifang Li, Jiaheng Yang, Xuewei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the emotional intelligence of male nursing students in China should be given high priority. Exploring the level of emotional intelligence among male nursing students and its underlying mechanisms is a crucial research topic. This study aims to investigate the mediating effect between critical thinking and emotional intelligence, with a focus on the mediating role of self-compassion and supportive communication, to provide evidence for developing strategies to enhance the emotional intelligence of male nursing students.MethodsThis study adopted a cross-sectional design. From June to November 2021, a convenience sampling method was used to survey 227 male undergraduate nursing students from four universities in China. Data collection tools included a general information questionnaire, emotional intelligence scale, critical thinking scale, self-compassion scale, and supportive communication scale. Model construction and mediation path testing were performed using AMOS 23.0, while data analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS 25.0 software.ResultsThe average emotional intelligence score of male nursing students in China was 80.123 ± 16.781.The mediation model demonstrated excellent fit (X²/df = 1.406, GFI = 0.994, NFI = 0.977, IFI = 0.993, CFI = 0.993, RMSEA = 0.042). The mediation effect model revealed that critical thinking indirectly influences emotional intelligence through supportive communication and self-compassion.ConclusionThe emotional intelligence level of male nursing students in China is in the moderately low range. The functional pathways between emotional intelligence and critical thinking indicate that supportive communication and self-compassion play significant roles in enhancing the emotional intelligence of male nursing students. The research findings provide valuable insights for nursing educators, suggesting that during the cultivation of male nursing students, emphasis should be placed on fostering critical thinking while simultaneously strengthening their abilities in self-compassion and supportive communication. This not only helps improve their emotional intelligence but may also have long-term impacts on their professional growth and personal well-being.Keywords: Male nursing students, Emotional intelligence, Critical thinking, Self-compassion, Supportive communication

Introduction

With the aging population and growing demands for chronic diseases and healthcare services, global health departments face increasingly severe challenges [1]. As frontline medical professionals, nurses shoulder substantial treatment and nursing responsibilities. In this process, they frequently encounter various pressures that lead to significant emotional exhaustion. This fatigue stems from stress, workload overload, and the inherent challenges of nursing work [2, 3]. Lopez-Cabarcos’s research found that enhancing emotional intelligence is an effective approach for nursing staff to cope with occupational stress and alleviate emotional exhaustion [4].

The term “emotional intelligence” was first proposed by Salovey and Mayer, defined as the ability to perceive, express, understand, and manage emotions [5]. Research indicates that emotional intelligence not only impacts caregivers ‘mental health but also directly affects communication quality with patients and nursing service standards [6]. Studies by Lu [7] and Su [8] revealed that Chinese nurses’ emotional intelligence levels are moderate, a finding corroborated by Moradian’s research [9].

According to data from the International Council of Nurses, the global number of nurses is estimated between 28 and 29 million, with China having over 5.6 million registered nurses, accounting for approximately 20% of the global nursing workforce [10]. By the end of 2024, the proportion of male nurses in China had increased from 2% in 2019 to 3%, with their numbers rising from 125,000 to 178,500 [11, 12], making them an indispensable force in the development of China’s nursing industry [13]. Christov-Moore and Sutil-Rodriguez’s research found [14, 15] that male students generally exhibit lower emotional intelligence levels than females, which may be attributed to men’s slower speed and accuracy in identifying emotions compared to women. Yao argues [16] that male nursing students often need to overcome more social biases and self-perception barriers when interacting with patients. Therefore, there is an urgent need to emphasize the systematic cultivation of emotional intelligence during undergraduate education [17]. Budler posits that emotional intelligence evolves with educational years and age, indicating its potential for development and improvement [18]. Given this context, as future reserves of male nurses, undergraduate students majoring in nursing (male nursing students) should receive heightened attention and proactive efforts to focus on cultivating and developing their emotional intelligence.

Previous studies have demonstrated correlations between emotional intelligence and communication skills, critical thinking, and self-compassion [19–21].However, these studies exhibit notable limitations: first, most conducted only simple correlation analyses without elucidating the complex underlying pathways and mechanisms of interaction among variables; second, existing research predominantly treats nursing students as a homogeneous group, overlooking gender differences and the unique characteristics of male students, resulting in a scarcity of empirical studies examining the mechanisms of emotional intelligence in male nursing students [22], which hinders the development of targeted intervention strategies. The Emotional Intelligence Ability Model developed by Salovey and Mayer [5, 23] stands as one of the most scientifically validated frameworks, widely used to assess emotional intelligence levels and interpret its structural components. Building upon this theoretical foundation, this study employs path analysis to investigate variable interactions, addressing gaps in existing research on underlying mechanisms. The findings aim to provide targeted theoretical support and empirical evidence for future interventions aimed at enhancing emotional intelligence levels among male nursing students.

Critical thinking

Critical thinking, also known as critical analysis, originated in the early 20th century when German scholars first proposed it as an innovative teaching concept [24]. By 2000, Scheffer et al. established a widely accepted definition through the Delphi method [25]. They defined critical thinking as the process by which nursing professionals apply theoretical knowledge, practical experience, and evidence-based research findings to engage in effective reflection, reasoning, application, and decision-making within complex clinical care contexts [26]. The Emotional Intelligence (EI) model [5] emphasizes that when individuals perceive cognitive and perceptual information from their emotional systems, they process this information through cognitive processing to enhance emotional judgment and guide their focus on critical issues—a crucial aspect of developing critical thinking skills. Studies by Li [19] and SK [27] both indicate correlations between critical thinking and EI. Individuals with higher critical thinking proficiency demonstrate more rational and effective decision-making, better situational analysis, and improved understanding of others’ emotions [28]. Based on these findings, we propose Hypothesis A: Critical thinking directly influences emotional intelligence.

Supportive communication

The theory of emotional intelligence posits that communication competence serves as a key determinant influencing emotional intelligence [5]. As an essential ability for internal-external information exchange [29], research demonstrates correlations between communication competence and both emotional intelligence and critical thinking [20, 30, 31]. Studies involving IT professionals and Iranian university students [32, 33] further reveal connections between emotional intelligence and communication skills. Zhao’s research indicates [34] that nursing staff with impaired critical thinking abilities struggle to establish effective patient communication channels, which may compromise care efficacy and potentially lead to nurse-patient conflicts. Effective communication facilitates the transformation of negative emotions into positive ones by avoiding emotional suppression or exaggeration, thereby enabling balanced emotional regulation [35]. Based on these findings, we propose Hypothesis b: Supportive communication acts as a mediating factor in the relationship between critical thinking and emotional intelligence.

Self-compassion

Self-compassion, also known as self-pity or self-care, refers to an individual’s approach of not avoiding personal pain and failure but adopting a tolerant, compassionate, and non-judgmental attitude toward their experiences. By recognizing that hardships are part of life’s journey, it helps alleviate emotional distress [36], serving as a key protective factor for mental health [37].Rushforth argues that self-compassion correlates with positive mental health outcomes across various occupational groups, particularly for healthcare professionals, as it provides a way to cope with pain through kindness and understanding [38]. The theory of emotional intelligence suggests that individuals with higher emotional intelligence can proactively manage emotions, demonstrating resilience during setbacks while enhancing emotional recovery capabilities to quickly adjust mindsets—a crucial factor influencing emotional intelligence abilities [5].Literature reviews indicate correlations between emotional intelligence, critical thinking, and self-compassion [39, 40]. Healthcare workers with strong critical thinking skills can regulate emotions through self-compassion when facing work pressure and challenges, maintaining calmness and rationality to make effective nursing decisions and actions. Self-compassion encourages caregivers to embrace their shortcomings rather than blame themselves or avoid failure, fostering growth through learning. This attitude helps maintain professional dedication and work efficiency. Based on these findings, we propose Hypothesis Pathway c: Self-compassion mediates the relationship between critical thinking and emotional intelligence.

Method

Research design

This study adopted a cross-sectional survey method and conducted convenience sampling at four universities in China between June and November 2021. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval No.: KY-2022-021). Prior to the survey, informed consent was obtained from all participants, who committed to protecting the anonymity and privacy of their information.

Participants and processes

Eligible participants were full-time undergraduate students aged 16 years or older. Students diagnosed with any mental disorder (including depression, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, etc.), or those in statuses such as academic leave, grade retention, or major change, were excluded. According to the sample size criteria for structural equation modeling and path analysis proposed by Kline (2015), a sample size of 200 or more was required to ensure stable parameter estimation and reliable standard errors for the model [41].

Prior to the survey, researchers trained investigators through WeChat (a social media platform). The investigators explained the study’s purpose, content, and significance to students, who participated voluntarily. Nursing faculty members distributed QR codes for the online survey tool via WeChat in a group chat for nursing students. A total of 244 questionnaires were collected, with 227 valid responses, achieving an effective response rate of 93.03%.

Instruments

The Demographic Statistics Study was developed by researchers based on previous literature studies and the objectives of this study [42]. The survey items included gender, years of education, only-child families, and religious beliefs.

The Self-Compassion Scale(SCS), developed by Neff [36] and adapted for Chinese use by Chen [43], comprises 26 items across six dimensions: self-kindness (5 items), common humanity (4 items), mindfulness (4 items), self-criticism (5 items), isolation (4 items), and overindulgence (4 items). All items are scored on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always), with total scores ranging from 26 to 130 points. Higher scores indicate greater levels of self-compassion. The scale demonstrates a Cronbach’s α-coefficient of 0.84 and a test-retest reliability of 0.89.

The Supportive Communication Scale(SCS), developed by Whetten and translated into Chinese by Chinese scholars [44], consists of 20 items across three dimensions: guidance and counseling (3 items), provision of effective negative feedback (6 items), and communication competence (11 items). It uses a 5-point Likert scale scoring system with options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100 points. Higher scores indicate better communication proficiency. The Cronbach’s coefficient of the scale is 0.867.

The Critical Thinking Scale, developed by Huang [39], employs a Likert 5-point rating system with four dimensions: universal skepticism, self-reflection, open-mindedness, and critical thinking. Comprising 22 items, the scale measures scores from 22 to 110, where higher values indicate stronger critical thinking tendencies. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale is 0.827.

The Emotional Intelligence Scale, developed by Schutt and adapted into Chinese by Liu [42], comprises 21 items across five dimensions using a five-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate greater emotional intelligence levels, with the Cronbach’s α-correlation coefficient reaching 0.816.

Data analysis

The study employed IBM SPSS 25.0 for data analysis. First, descriptive statistics were used to present participant characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was then conducted to explore the relationships between self-compassion, supportive communication, critical thinking, and emotional intelligence. Finally, path analysis was conducted using AMOS 23.0 to examine multiple mediating pathways and quantify the mediating effects of self-compassion and supportive communication between critical thinking and emotional intelligence.

Path analysis model

Figure 1 illustrates the mediating model of emotional intelligence, self-compassion, supportive communication, and critical thinking among male nursing undergraduates. All paths in Fig. 1 reached statistical significance (P < 0.05). The indirect mediating effect of critical thinking through supportive communication and self-compassion on emotional intelligence was calculated as 0.875 × 0.911 + 0.377 × 0.078 = 0.827. Bootstrapped method tests for mediating effects showed a 95% confidence interval of 0.756–0.877 (not including zero), indicating significant mediation. The hypothesized path was thus validated.

Discussion

The study revealed that the emotional intelligence scores of this group (80.123 ± 16.781) were statistically lower than those reported by Su et al. [8] (81.03 ± 15.26). In Su’s research, 85.51% of female nursing students demonstrated moderate emotional intelligence levels. This suggests that male undergraduate nursing students in this cohort exhibited moderate to lower emotional intelligence, likely influenced by factors such as career identity confusion, social biases, and academic pressure [45]. Lopez-Cabarcos emphasized the growing importance of emotional intelligence education for nursing students, advocating its cultivation during higher education as part of continuous professional development [4]. However, current nursing curricula rarely incorporate dedicated courses on emotional intelligence theory and practical applications [46]. Dehnavi recommended establishing specialized courses in nursing schools [47]. Emotional intelligence training can be implemented through immersive narrative nursing, role-playing, and scenario-based simulations [48]. Green’s research demonstrated that mindfulness meditation effectively alleviates stress and anxiety among nursing staff, helping them cope with painful experiences and emotions while maintaining mental and physical health [49]. Nursing institutions are advised to integrate mindfulness meditation and other emotion-regulation techniques into emotional intelligence programs, equipping nursing students to handle academic challenges, daily life pressures, and future career obstacles with greater resilience.

The findings of this study indicate no direct and significant path connection between critical thinking and emotional intelligence in male nursing students. This result aligns with Kaya’s [50] research but differs from the findings of Li [51] and Christodoulakis [52], possibly due to differences in study subjects’ gender or research methodologies. In Li’s [51] study, female participants accounted for 64.2% of the sample, while Christodoulakis [52] included 74.5% female participants. This discrepancy suggests future research should prioritize this aspect. Given the differences in research tools used between Christodoulakis [52] and our team, future studies could adopt multiple methodologies to verify the stability of relationships between critical thinking and emotional intelligence in male nursing students under different experimental conditions. Additionally, while our results suggest that male nursing students’ critical thinking abilities do not directly influence emotional intelligence through direct pathways, this does not preclude the possibility of other forms of association or mutual influences. Therefore, further exploration is needed to determine whether there exists a direct relationship between these factors and potential indirect mechanisms mediating their interactions.

Mediation analysis revealed an indirect effect between critical thinking and emotional intelligence (β = 0.789). Critical thinking influences emotional intelligence through supportive communication (β = 0.789), with a statistically significant mediating effect. In-depth analysis of their intrinsic mechanisms demonstrates that critical thinking does not directly act on emotional intelligence but indirectly affects emotional perception, understanding, and regulation by shaping communication patterns and enhancing communication efficacy. Watson posits that decision-making and action in nursing processes exhibit high complexity, requiring not only theoretical foundations [53] but also the core driver of critical thinking. As a key capability for resolving complex nursing practice challenges, critical thinking enables caregivers to transcend habitual thinking patterns, integrate clinical information rationally, analyze problem essence objectively, and evaluate solutions. This systematic thinking ability forms the solid foundation for effective communication [54]. Specifically, efficient and productive critical thinking allows individuals to accurately capture, integrate, analyze, and evaluate information conveyed by others in interpersonal interactions. It facilitates clear logical structuring of problem cores, articulate personal perspectives, and prevent communication misunderstandings or biases, thereby fostering positive interactions and providing cognitive support for supportive communication implementation [55]. Supportive communication serves as a crucial link between critical thinking and emotional intelligence. Its core value lies in achieving accurate emotional recognition and appropriate responses through effective information delivery and emotional interaction. Effective supportive communication enhances individuals ‘empathy and understanding of others’ emotions, enabling them to keenly detect emotional shifts, identify latent emotional needs, and respond promptly with suitable reactions [56]. Superior communication skills convey trust and security to others. This emotional connection not only forms the foundation for healthy nurse-patient relationships but also directly reflects one’s emotional intelligence level—whether individuals can establish deep connections through communication with others’ cognition and feelings directly determines their emotional comprehension and regulation abilities, further validating the pivotal role of supportive communication in mediating processes [57]. Considering the demographic characteristics of this study’s subjects, this mediating mechanism holds significant practical implications for clinical practice. The study found that male nursing students in China are not adept at proactive communication [58]. The formation of this situation is not only closely related to the training model of nursing education in China but is also deeply influenced by traditional gender culture: current domestic educational institutions place relatively insufficient emphasis on interpersonal communication skills during the training of male nursing students, often overemphasizing the imparting of professional knowledge and the training of operational skills while neglecting the systematic cultivation of communication abilities [59]. In traditional gender cognition in China, males are expected to embody roles of “rationality and restraint,” with expressing emotions and actively empathizing being perceived as “not strong enough” [60]. This perception leads male nursing students to deliberately suppress their emotional expressions in interpersonal interactions, lacking awareness and skills for proactive communication, and struggling to engage in emotionally warm supportive communication. To some extent, this also results in male nursing students, even with certain critical thinking abilities, being unable to translate them into improved emotional intelligence through effective supportive communication. Liu proposed that cultivating communication skills can be approached from three aspects: theoretical instruction, clinical internships, and clinical practice (offering courses related to communication skills, integrating relevant content into classroom teaching, and providing targeted education by instructors during clinical practice stages) [61]. Based on the mediating analysis results of this study, we further suggest that educational institutions need to establish a collaborative training system of “critical thinking-supportive communication-emotional intelligence” when adjusting teaching content and methods, thereby achieving comprehensive improvement in emotional intelligence levels and laying a solid foundation for their future nursing careers and handling complex clinical interpersonal interactions.

Another indirect pathway involves how critical thinking influences emotional intelligence through self-compassion (β = 0.030), with significant mediating effects of self-compassion. These findings further elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms linking critical thinking and emotional intelligence, while providing new insights for cultivating emotional intelligence among male nursing students. Self-compassion serves as a prerequisite for caring for others, and critical thinking—as a rational cognitive and reflective ability—provides core support for developing and enhancing self-compassion [62]. This constitutes the fundamental logic behind critical thinking’s indirect impact on emotional intelligence. Neff similarly emphasizes that self-compassion is not selfish behavior but rather helps individuals establish positive self-perception, thereby improving empathy and compassion [63]. This aligns perfectly with the nursing profession’s essence of “care-centeredness” —nurses must first learn to care for themselves to deliver high-quality care services with full physical and mental capacity. Considering the unique characteristics of nursing professions and male nursing students, this mediating pathway holds particularly significant practical value. Nurses often neglect self-compassion due to continuous caregiving responsibilities, leading to fatigue and emotional distress [36]. Those with strong critical thinking skills can break the “self-neglect” mindset by rationally analyzing psychological needs, emotional fluctuations, and work conditions. Through scientific psychological adjustment methods, they effectively manage negative emotions, alleviate stress, and enhance personal well-being and professional satisfaction [64]. The enhancement of this self-psychological regulation ability essentially represents an optimization of the self-emotional management dimension within emotional intelligence, enabling nursing staff to maintain a calm and efficient working state in complex and dynamic clinical nursing environments, thereby reducing the impact of emotional exhaustion on work [65, 66]. This further corroborates the practical value of critical thinking influencing emotional intelligence through self-compassion. Dugue posits that female nursing students are more prone to internalizing negative emotions compared to male students [6], and this tendency toward emotional internalization also makes females more attentive to their emotional states and proactive in seeking self-care, consequently demonstrating higher levels of self-compassion competence [36]. In contrast, male nursing students often exhibit deficiencies in emotional expression and self-care [67], and their indirect promotion of emotional intelligence through critical thinking may be limited. The traditional gender norms in China, which hold that “men should bear responsibilities and endure pressure,” lead male nursing students to suppress their negative emotions, avoid actively addressing their physical and mental needs, and even perceive self-care as a manifestation of “selfishness and vulnerability” [60], further exacerbating their weak self-compassion abilities. This highlights the necessity for targeted cultivation of self-compassion skills in male nursing students. Numerous studies have confirmed that self-compassion abilities can be improved through systematic training. The “self-pity experience modification” study by Neff and colleagues [63], McEwan’s “compassionate mind training” (CMT) [68], and Kabat-Zinn’s mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) [69] have been demonstrated to significantly improve self-compassion. Accordingly, it is recommended that educational institutions develop self-compassion skills among male nursing students based on their critical thinking levels through specialized courses or psychological training programs, thereby enhancing their emotional intelligence.

A comparison of the effect strength between the two pathways reveals that supportive communication (β = 0.789) exerts a more pronounced influence on emotional intelligence than self-compassion (β = 0.030), suggesting this pathway may play a more significant role in the relationship between critical thinking and emotional intelligence. Based on this finding, nursing education practices could appropriately emphasize the cultivation of supportive communication skills, providing more targeted intervention directions for enhancing emotional intelligence among male nursing students.

In conclusion, this study reveals the mediating role of supportive communication and self-compassion in the relationship between critical thinking and emotional intelligence among male nursing students in China, emphasizing their importance in enhancing emotional intelligence. This finding provides valuable insights for nursing education institutions. The working environments male nurses may encounter in the future could be more complex and variable than those faced by female nurses, exposing them to different work pressures and challenges [70]. For instance, in certain emergency or high-intensity work scenarios, male nurses may need to demonstrate stronger decision-making abilities and problem-solving skills [71]. Critical thinking proves particularly crucial in this process, enabling male nurses to make accurate judgments and take effective actions in complex environments. Meanwhile, strong communication skills remain essential qualities for male nurses, helping build trust with patients and their families while improving service satisfaction and quality. Self-compassion serves as a key factor for male nurses to maintain physical and mental health and deliver high-quality care consistently. Through effective communication and self-compassion, male nurses can better regulate their emotions, alleviate work stress, and demonstrate more proactive and professional attitudes in nursing practice.

The clinical internship phase represents a critical period for male nursing students, during which they first comprehensively engage with clinical environments, confront patient suffering, endure work pressure, and experience doctor-patient conflicts, while also exhibiting the highest concentration of negative emotions [72]. Studies indicate that this stage serves as a pivotal period for the cultivation and development of emotional intelligence in male nursing students [73]. Therefore, supportive communication and self-compassion training should be consistently integrated throughout the entire clinical internship period. First, the pre-internship training program for clinical practice has been enhanced with a dedicated module on “Clinical Emotional Management and Self-compassion, Supportive Communication.” Leveraging AI-powered dialogue generation and scenario simulation capabilities, interactive virtual roles or clinical scenarios (e.g., simulated patient consultations) are created to provide real-time feedback, thereby cultivating students ‘clinical communication skills and self-compassion abilities [74]. Second, during the internship period, a “mentor accountability system” is implemented where instructors provide one-on-one guidance tailored to male nursing students’ specific emotional challenges and communication difficulties. This includes teaching emotional regulation techniques and optimizing self-compassion strategies, while correcting communication biases through clinical demonstrations and scenario simulations to improve listening, empathy, expression, and feedback skills [73]. Third, a clinical internship emotional support and communication platform has been established. This integrates internship content through sharing sessions and emotional counseling groups, encouraging male nursing students to exchange experiences regarding emotional dilemmas and communication challenges. Professionals facilitate peer learning and collaborative improvement, supplemented by case study discussions to reinforce practical application of supportive communication techniques. This ensures male nursing students effectively apply self-compassion and supportive communication skills, transform critical thinking into practical competencies, enhance emotional intelligence, and meet professional nursing demands.

Research limitations

This study has several limitations. Although the subjects were from four universities in different provinces of China, they still cannot represent the entire population of male nursing students. Additionally, the convenience sampling method used in this study may lead to biased results due to practical conditions, sample accessibility, and time constraints during implementation. Future research should adopt more representative random sampling methods to expand sample coverage and representativeness, reduce the impact of sampling errors on study outcomes, and further explore the emotional intelligence levels and underlying mechanisms among male nursing students. Longitudinal tracking surveys could also be employed to investigate the dynamic patterns of emotional intelligence changes in male nursing students. Comparative studies between male and female nursing students on emotional intelligence could be conducted to identify targeted intervention strategies.

Conclusion

This study reveals that male nursing students in China generally exhibit moderate to low levels of emotional intelligence, highlighting the necessity for targeted educational interventions to enhance their emotional intelligence. Additionally, no significant direct correlation was found between critical thinking and emotional intelligence among male nursing students; instead, this relationship is mediated through self-compassion and supportive communication skills. This mediation mechanism partially explains the core developmental challenges faced by male nursing students: insufficient self-compassion exacerbates chronic emotional suppression, while inadequate supportive communication skills further impede effective clinical interpersonal interactions—a phenomenon potentially influenced by traditional gender stereotypes within sociocultural contexts. Based on these findings, nursing educators should develop systematic, targeted curriculum interventions focused on critical thinking, self-compassion, and supportive communication to cultivate emotional intelligence in male nursing students. Specific measures include introducing integrated courses on emotions and communication, implementing one-on-one mentorship programs during internships, and establishing peer-based emotional support platforms. These approaches aim to transform students’ critical thinking abilities into practical skills for emotional regulation and clinical communication, thereby comprehensively improving their emotional intelligence to meet the demands of clinical nursing practice.
