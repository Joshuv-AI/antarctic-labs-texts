Marine predator strikes rectally inserted eel fish causing colonic perforation: a rare case report and review of literature
Authors: Akash Saha, Mohammad Mashiur Rahman, Abidur Rahman, Israt Jahan, Faisal Nasim Sunny, Md. Imran Ahmed, A. M. Zubayed Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Gastrointestinal perforation is a very common surgical emergency with significant mortality and morbidity, which needs urgent intervention. Foreign body-related perforations are very rare, accounting for less than 1% of cases. However, Colonic perforation by Eel fish is an extremely rare phenomenon; only three cases have been reported in the existing literature. Here, we report a rare case of traumatic colonic perforation with septic shock by a rectally inserted Eel fish.Case Presentation:A 25-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of abdominal pain, accompanied by high-grade fever and a loss of consciousness lasting 1 day. Initial workups suggested the case might be a case of intestinal perforation with septic shock. However, in the abdominal X-ray, there was a bizarre radiopaque shadow in the right iliac fossa. Emergency exploratory laparotomy was done, and a large perforation was found at the rectosigmoid junction. Multiple pieces of fish bone were found in the peritoneal cavity. A putrefied Eel fish was found in the peritoneal cavity, approximately 25 cm in length.Clinical Discussion:After thorough peritoneal toileting, a Hartmann-like procedure, where the distal end was closed and secured with a silk suture and the proximal exteriorized for fecal diversion, was done, and the patient was shifted to the intensive care unit. However, despite the best medical care, the patient expired on the third postoperative day.Conclusion:Live animals causing rectal perforation have rarely been documented in the literature. Unusual healthcare beliefs, inadvertent sexual conduct, or criminal aggressiveness may be involved. However, the cause may remain a mystery. Rapid diagnosis with proper history, aided by imaging, with urgent surgical intervention, may increase patient survival. Therefore, emergency care physicians should also be conscious of the rare and unusual causes of intestinal perforation to reach the diagnosis promptly.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal perforation (GI) is a prevalent cause of acute abdomen in the emergency department that warrants immediate surgical intervention[1,2]. It is an acute medical condition with considerable morbidity and mortality, varying from 30% to 50%[3]. GI perforations resulting from foreign objects are infrequent yet critical surgical emergencies, constituting fewer than 1% of all abdominal trauma cases[4]. Colorectal perforation is rare, with the majority of instances resulting from iatrogenic perforation during therapeutic colonoscopies. Additionally, severe acute appendicitis, colon cancer, diverticulitis, or, infrequently, rectally introduced foreign objects may also result in perforation[5]. Foreign bodies in the rectum can result from sexual activity, psychological illnesses, violence, iatrogenic introduction during medical procedures, or accidentally[5–7]. Patients typically come to emergency rooms after unsuccessful attempts to remove a retained object on their own. Fish-related intestinal perforations present diagnostic challenges due to their rarity and often nonspecific clinical presentations. While fish bones dominate clinical reports as the primary causative agent, cases administrating live animals into the anus -particularly eels-represent infrequent phenomena with unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges[5,9]. Patients typically present with abdominal pain of varying intensity, which may not initially suggest peritonitis. Because of the late admission of the patient to the hospital, the management can be difficult and may be associated with complications[8]. Considering that this condition can lead to embarrassment or stem from psychiatric issues, patients might not be open about their experiences. Therefore, health care providers must maintain a heightened level of awareness during clinical assessments. Complications may include mucosal lacerations, migration to proximal regions of the intestine, intestinal obstruction, perforations, peritonitis, and sepsis resulting from attempts or failures to remove them[5,6,8].

Establishing a diagnosis and formulating a management strategy presents a significant challenge for emergency physicians and surgeons in such cases with a bizarre and inconsistent history, making a high index of suspicion. Proper privacy and broad-minded history taking, physical examination, and choice of investigations are key to finding out the diagnosis and associated complications early[6]. Clinical features of perforation, aided by imaging, play a key role in diagnosing such a condition. In some cases, the diagnosis was made during exploratory laparotomy[10]. However, by an extensive literature review, only three cases with anorectal perforation by an eel fish were found globally. The geographical distribution is mostly in Asia, with a male predominance. Most of the cases presented as acute abdomen with or without septic shock. The sigmoid colon and rectum were the main location of perforation found perioperatively. Exploratory laparotomy and proximal colostomy were performed in most of the cases, and the survival rate was quite good. Here, we will present a rare case of traumatic colorectal perforation by an eel fish with septic shock. The case report is written according to SCARE 2025 guidelines[12].

Case presentation

A 25-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of abdominal pain, accompanied by high-grade fever and a loss of consciousness lasting 1 day. The pain was spread throughout the entire abdomen. Unfortunately, the patient was in an unconscious state, preventing the collection of a detailed history. The individuals accompanying the patient exhibited a lack of cooperation and were unable to provide an accurate medical history. Upon evaluation, the patient presented as unconscious, with a GCS of 8/15 [E2M3V3]. The temperature recorded was 103°F, pulse rate was 125 beats/minute and weak, blood pressure measured at 70/50 mmHg, and oxygen saturation was at 92% on room air. The abdomen was tense and tender, accompanied by muscle guarding and rigidity. The upper border of liver dullness was obliterated. A per-rectal examination was done by digital rectal examination (DRE) and proctoscopy, which revealed no abnormality.

Our primary diagnosis was that it may be a case of acute abdomen due to intestinal perforation with septic shock. After proper resuscitation, the vitals were stable. Inotrope (Noradrenaline) support was given to maintain blood pressure and broad spectrum intravenous antibiotics(Ceftriaxone, Meropenam and Metronidazole) was ensured in their standard doses. An abdominal X-ray was done, revealing a bizarre radiopaque shadow in the right iliac fossa, creating a diagnostic dilemma; there was also a crescentic gas shadow under the right dome of the diaphragm. All the debris, pus, and intestinal content were cleared off with a vigorous saline wash of the peritoneal cavity. The entire intestine was further checked for other perforations. The entire rectosigmoid junction was sloughed out. So, a Hartmann like procedure, where the distal end was closed and secured with a silk suture and the proximal exteriorized for fecal diversion, was done. A pelvic drain was given, hemostasis was ensured, and the abdomen was closed in layers. After the operation, the patient was shifted to the Intensive Care Unit for further management. Meticulous post-operative care was given. Inotrope support and broad-spectrum antibiotics (Ceftriaxone, Meropenam and Metronidazole) were ensured in their standard doses. Despite all support, the patient’s condition was deteriorating, and unfortunately, the patient expired on the third postoperative day. The patient party refused any kind of autopsy. After interviewing the patient’s family members, the manner of injury still remained a mystery.

Discussion

Colonic perforation by eel fish is an exceedingly rare but life-threatening condition, primarily associated with cultural practices or occupational exposures. In Bangladesh, there are hundreds of species, but mostly Anguilla bengalensis is found in freshwater aquatic sources[13]. The pathophysiology of eel-induced colonic perforation may involve direct trauma from the eel’s physical characteristics. Eels possess sharp teeth and a slippery, elongated body, enabling vigorous movement[13,14]. In the case by Lo et al, the eel was found biting the colon, indicating that its teeth can penetrate mucosal and muscular layers[11]. The eel’s movements may also cause mechanical stress, tearing the bowel wall. Furthermore, the existence of a live foreign entity may initiate local inflammatory reactions, compromise the integrity of the colon, and increase the risk of perforation[3]. Unlike static foreign bodies like fish bones, the active nature of eels exacerbates tissue damage, making these cases particularly severe.

Patients typically present with acute, severe abdominal pain, often accompanied by signs of peritonitis, including guarding, rigidity, and rebound tenderness[10]. Fever and leukocytosis may indicate infection or inflammation[5]. A critical challenge is obtaining an accurate history, as patients may be reluctant to disclose the cause due to embarrassment. In two cases, patients were admitted to eel insertion only upon further extensive questioning.[5,11] However, getting a proper history might be difficult in comatose patients. Occupational history, such as fishing, may also provide clues, as seen in the case by Mokbul et al[10]. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion and imaging. Plain X-rays may reveal pneumoperitoneum, indicated by free air under the diaphragm[10]. Computed tomography (CT) scans are more sensitive, identifying the foreign body and perforation site. However, prospective identification of the site of perforation on CT imaging has become an essential part of the preoperative evaluation in modern practice. It can determine the site and cause of perforation with an accuracy of 86%[1,5,15]. In the case by Lo et al, an X-ray showed the eel’s shadow, aiding diagnosis[11]. Similarly, in our case, X-ray was the main key to diagnosis, though pneumoperitoneum was confirmed, we were not sure about the etiology. A CT scan was not performed due to the patient’s condition and the availability of urgent scans. A thorough history is essential to guide imaging and avoid diagnostic delays, which can worsen outcomes[1].

Management involves emergency surgical intervention to remove the eel, repair the perforation, and address complications like peritonitis. Exploratory laparotomy is the standard approach, allowing comprehensive inspection of the abdominal cavity[16]. In all reported cases, surgery involved eel extraction and creation of a colostomy or sigmoidostomy to divert fecal flow[5,10,11]. Broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting gram-negative and anaerobic bacteria are administered to prevent sepsis[16]. Postoperative care includes monitoring for complications, nutritional support, and early mobilization.

Colorectal perforation carries the highest complication rate (55%) compared with other GIT perforations. This is unsurprising considering the bacterial content of the large bowel that might evoke bacterial peritonitis[3,16]. Colonic perforation causes significant risks, including peritonitis and sepsis, with mortality rates for untreated cases approaching the overall mortality rate is 30%, and the mortality rate of cases that also have diffuse peritonitis is up to 70%[17]. The rate of postoperative complications, such as complicated fluid collection, leakage, surgical site infection, and other systemic infections, is higher than for patients receiving elective abdominal surgeries[17]. Prompt intervention is critical, as demonstrated by the uneventful recoveries in all three cases[5,10,11]. In our case, the patient was taken to the tertiary care hospital after 4 days of the primary event, which worsened the outcome. Clinicians must consider unusual etiologies, particularly in regions where cultural practices involve live animals. Patient education to discourage such practices is vital, especially in East Asia, where eel insertion for constipation relief is a known folk remedy. We found such types of cases in different mass media, but they are mostly unreported cases in scientific literature[18,19].

Conclusion

The introduction of a live animal into the rectum, resulting in rectal perforation, has rarely been documented in existing literature. This could be associated with an unusual belief in health care, unintentional sexual conduct, or an act of criminal aggression. However, the actual cause might remain a mystery as the manner of the injury was not clear in our case but can be predictable with sociodemographic characteristics and peroperative findings. Rapid diagnosis with proper history, aided by imaging, and urgent surgical intervention may increase patient survival. Therefore, physicians should also be conscious of the rare and unusual causes of intestinal perforation to reach the diagnosis early in a hospital setting.
