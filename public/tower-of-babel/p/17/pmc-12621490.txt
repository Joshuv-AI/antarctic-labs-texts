Obesity-attributable risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the United States: a Bayesian network analysis
Authors: Sunny Sheth, Samantha Clark, Anthony Fabricatore, Mads Faurby, Azadeh Houshmand-Oeregaard, Joshua Toliver, Warren Stevens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity and related complications present clinical and financial burdens for patients and health care systems. Quantification of the impact of obesity on cardiovascular disease (CVD) would aid in disease management. This study aimed to apply Bayesian networks to nationally representative data to analyze the relationship between obesity and CVD.MethodsSelf-reported medical history, risk factor status, and physiological measurements were longitudinally adapted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (1999–2018). Respondents were required to have weight history and ≥1 CVD outcome, including myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or overall CVD. The cohort was restricted to respondents ≥35 years to ensure recent weight measurements were sufficiently captured. Population attributable fractions were estimated via the traditional approach (assessing direct effects of exposures on outcomes) and Bayesian networks (assessing indirect effects of interdependent relationships) for each CVD outcome.ResultsObesity was reported in 31 %-32 % of respondents with any CVD outcome (n = 29,388–29,529). CVD outcomes attributed to obesity through the traditional approach were estimated to be <4 %. Using Bayesian networks, estimates of myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and CVD cases attributable to obesity were 21 %, 16 %, 38 %, and 19 %, respectively. For all Bayesian networks, >80 % of the total effect of obesity was attributable to its effect on intermediate medical conditions.ConclusionsThese data highlight obesity as a significant CVD risk factor. This work has provided valuable insight into the relationship between obesity and CVD-related outcomes, indicating a need for greater clinical attention to obesity to lessen the burden of downstream complications.

Introduction

The World Health Organization currently defines obesity as the accumulation of abnormal or excessive fat that poses a risk to health [1]. The American Medical Association first recognized obesity as a disease in 2013 [2]. Over the last two decades, the prevalence of obesity in the US has grown substantially, increasing from 32.3 % in 2003–2004 to 42.8 % by 2017–2018 [3]. Obesity is associated with both excess mortality [4,5] and an increased risk of developing a multitude of obesity-related complications, including, but not limited to, type 2 diabetes, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis or steatotic liver disease, sleep apnea, knee osteoarthritis, heart failure, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [6,7].

Historically, the complex and multifaceted nature of the mechanisms through which obesity affects CVD has been challenging to understand and address in clinical practice [8,9]. Although empirical evidence supports the pathophysiological link between obesity and CVD, the magnitude of influence of obesity on CVD and the means by which the relationship is maintained, particularly with respect to intermediate risk factors such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension, are not well established [10,11]. A deeper understanding of the relationships between obesity, intermediate risk factors, and CVD is crucial for informing changes to the current standard of care in obesity management, and ultimately, reducing the significant health and economic burden associated with CVD. This burden remains the leading cause of death in the US (928,741 deaths in 2020) and resulted in an estimated $407.3 billion in direct and indirect costs from 2018–2019 [12].

Studies assessing the relationship between obesity and CVD commonly focus on the estimation of relative risks and odds ratios. While these measures of association are useful for understanding disease etiology, they do not highlight the potential impact of preventive interventions on population health [13]. The underlying prevalence of a given risk factor in the population is inherently ignored due to its relative nature. Population attributable fractions (PAFs) are an alternative measure that estimate the proportion of disease cases that could be averted by eliminating a given risk factor from the population by accounting for both prevalence and risk [13,14].

Traditional PAF methods vary, but they typically involve a single logistic regression model adjusting for confounders and other risk factors [13,15]. When the interest is in elucidating causal pathways, the approach involves controlling for mediators. By doing so, the resulting PAF will represent the direct effect of an exposure on outcomes rather than the total effect. This approach ignores the impact of indirect effects occurring through other etiologic pathways, and the additive structure of these models assumes independence between risk factors [16]. For complex, interdependent relationships—such as those between upstream risk factors, obesity, intermediate medical conditions, and cardiovascular outcomes—failing to properly account for these indirect effects may significantly underestimate the PAF [15].

Bayesian networks offer a more natural setup for modeling complex etiologic relationships [17,18]. These models are well suited for multifactorial diseases and have been implemented in the context of stroke, diabetes, COVID-19 vaccination, and frailty [15,[19], [20], [21]]. Unlike traditional methods, Bayesian networks can properly identify the joint distribution of risk factors by directly modeling the interdependencies between them [22]. This allows for the total effect of a given risk factor to be deconstructed into its direct and indirect effects occurring through downstream risk factors on the causal pathway. The ability to estimate indirect effects, which quantify the extent to which the relationship between an exposure and an outcome is mediated by one or more variables, is particularly valuable in the context of CVD because obesity may exert most of its influence through an increased risk of more proximate medical conditions such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

Current estimates of obesity-attributable CVD burden in the US are limited by the use of traditional methods, which either underestimate PAFs when downstream risk factors are considered or fail to assess the extent to which obesity affects CVD through downstream risk factors [15,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Additionally, the use of nonrepresentative sources (eg, claims data representing commercially insured individuals) and the presence of measurement error due to a reliance on International Classification of Diseases coding to determine weight status make drawing population-level conclusions challenging.

In this study, we aim to extend previous work and address evidence gaps regarding the relationship between obesity and CVD by accounting for the complex, interdependent nature of CVD risk factors through the novel application of Bayesian networks using nationally representative data. Our primary objective was to determine the proportion of CVD burden attributable to obesity in the United States and to identify the mechanisms through which obesity affects CVD using Bayesian networks.

Methods

Data acquisition

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a cross-sectional, population-based survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which collects information on the health and nutritional status of the civilian, noninstitutionalized population in the United States [29]. The survey is conducted in 3 phases: screening, an in-home interview, and a physical exam at a mobile examination center. All data collected through this survey is self-reported by the patient. Since 1999, data from a representative sample of approximately 5000 respondents have been collected annually and released in two-year cycles. For this analysis, pooled sample weights accounting for the complex survey design of NHANES were calculated for the 1999–2018 study period and applied to obtain nationally representative estimates [30]. Medical history (eg, history of stroke) for each respondent was collected from patient responses to yes/no questions about past medical events. Laboratory parameters (eg, blood pressure, height, weight) were collected from the results of the physical exam.

Study design

To be eligible for study inclusion, cross-sectional NHANES data were longitudinally adapted, and respondents were required to be ≥18 years of age at survey administration. Using self-reported medical history and risk factor status in addition to current physiological measurements, temporality was established based on a rough approximation of event progression in each patient by using age rather than survey wave. Through the course of analysis, total population counts and projections were restricted to respondents ≥35 years of age. This was to account for sparse weight measurements prior to age 35, as well as the fact that cardiovascular disease is uncommon in individuals younger than 35 years and likely caused by nonmodifiable risk factors, such as genetic disorders.

Respondents with missing weight history for all outcomes of interest (myocardial infarction [MI], stroke, heart failure [HF], and CVD) were excluded. Respondents with information on at least one outcome were retained in the study and only excluded from the outcome-specific Bayesian networks in which they were missing information. Additionally, respondents for whom it was either not possible to establish that obesity preceded model risk factor and outcome development or who developed weight-related medical conditions (eg, diabetes) prior to the first reported instance of obesity were excluded. Obesity, MI, stroke, HF, and CVD diagnoses, as well as height and weight metrics, were self-reported by the patient to NHANES. These NHANES fields were used to confirm diagnosis status for obesity and each CVD-related outcome; obesity diagnosis was further confirmed using the patient’s BMI (obesity defined as BMI ≥30) which was derived from the patient’s reported height and weight. Additional methods related to the longitudinal adaptation of NHANES data and missingness are included in Appendix A.

Two known non-obesity risk factors for CVD include older age and modifiable lifestyle factors such as smoking [[31], [32], [33]]. To isolate the impact of obesity on CVD by removing patients with confounding risk factors, a subgroup analysis was completed in a population of nonsmoking respondents younger than 65 years for whom obesity would be expected to most affect CVD outcomes.

All analyses were conducted in compliance with recommendations from the Joint International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Special Task Force on good practices for real-world data studies of treatment and/or comparative effectiveness [34].

CVD outcomes and risk factors

CVD outcomes and corresponding risk factors included in the analysis were defined based on a targeted literature review and expert opinion (Table 1) [15,[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Final risk factors included in the Bayesian networks were defined using the following broad groupings: nonmodifiable (immutable characteristics), modifiable (factors that can be changed through individual intervention), and intermediate medical conditions (post-obesity and pre-CVD diseases associated with excess weight). Statistical methods

Discrete Bayesian networks for each outcome were constructed in two consecutive steps. First, the network structure relating nonmodifiable risk factors, modifiable risk factors, intermediate medical conditions, and CVD based on their respective roles in disease development was mapped out via directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figures A.5 and A.6). DAGs provide an intuitive graphical representation of the etiologic pathways leading to disease development, with nodes representing relevant factors and directed edges between nodes establishing causal relationships between these factors [16]. This can be conducted manually using empirical evidence and expert knowledge, through an automated data-driven optimization process, or based on a combination approach involving both methods [39]. The focus of this analysis was exclusively on causal reasoning; therefore, the foundation for the network reported here was grounded in empirical literature and supplemented by expert opinion.

Once the network structure was established, the parameter learning step, in which the relationship between each variable given those that precede and affect it is learned from the data, was implemented via logistic or proportional odds models depending upon variable structure [22]. The resulting estimates can be considered to have a causal interpretation if identifying assumptions are met. These include the local Markov property, causal faithfulness, conditional independence (no unmeasured confounding), and correct model specification [40]. Even in the instance that one or more of these assumptions do not hold, Bayesian networks remain a valid and consistent estimator for any given causal relationship under a properly specified model [17,22].

The fitted networks were then used to conduct counterfactual inference by estimating the probability of each CVD outcome based on the observed distribution of obesity in the sample and the likelihood of each outcome if obesity were eliminated from the population. This was completed by universally setting obesity status to “non-obese” and updating each Bayesian network to generate newly estimated probabilities for all sequalae of obesity (eg, diabetes, hypertension, prior cardiovascular event, and CVD). Resulting probabilities were then incorporated into outcome-specific PAF equations (see MI example below).PAF=CurrentMI−MIwithoutobesityCurrentMI

All analyses followed NHANES recommendations for analytic weighting and were conducted using the Comprehensive R Archive Network bnlearn package in R version 4.4.1 [30,41,42].

Results

Patient characteristics

Descriptive characteristics for each sample were stratified by obesity status (Table 2). While sample sizes for each Bayesian network varied slightly due to the impact of outcome-specific inclusion/exclusion criteria, these differences were minimal (less than 200 respondents). The final samples included between 29,388 and 29,529 respondents each, with approximately 31 % of individuals having obesity and 69 % without obesity across all cohorts. While both groups had a comparable gender distribution (female respondents with vs without obesity: 55.1 %−55.9 % vs 50.0 %−50.3 %), respondents with obesity were younger than those without obesity (mean age, 53.1–53.4 years vs 56.2–56.3 years, respectively). A larger proportion of individuals with obesity identified as non-Hispanic Black (26.7 %−27.0 %) than those without obesity (17.7 %−17.9 %), with the reverse being true of non-Hispanic White individuals (42.8 %−43.1 % vs 49.2 %−49.3 %, respectively). Respondents with obesity were also more likely than those without obesity to have diabetes (7.6 %−8.4 % vs 2.4 %−2.8 %, respectively) and hypertension (41.1 %−41.4 % vs 26.2 %−26.5 %, Estimating attributable disease burden

Estimation of the percentage of CVD outcomes attributable to obesity was conducted using both the traditional approach and Bayesian networks. These estimates are presented in Table 3. Discussion

For this study, we constructed Bayesian networks mapping out the causal pathways relating risk factors and CVD to quantify the direct and indirect effects of obesity on disease development using nationally representative data. Comparison with traditional PAF methods highlights the advantages of accounting for network effects within a causal framework when the relationships among risk factors are of interest.

Results indicate that obesity plays a significant role in the development of CVD, with the normalization of body weight estimated to reduce MI, stroke, HF, and CVD cases by 21.2 %, 16.0 %, 38.4 %, and 18.9 %, respectively. By classifying these estimates as direct and indirect effects, we found that the majority of the impact of obesity (more than 80 %) occurred indirectly through an increased risk of intermediate medical conditions, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and prior cardiovascular events. When controlling for these downstream risk factors via traditional methods, the estimate of obesity-attributable disease was less than 4 % for all CVD outcomes.

Our findings are consistent with studies indicating that obesity is significantly associated with the development of diabetes and hypertension, both of which are strongly linked to an increased risk of CVD events [[43], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48]]. The relationship between obesity and HF was particularly strong, accounting for 38 % and 65 % of cases in our main and subgroup analyses. These estimates are also consistent with association analyses such as the Framingham Heart Study and Johns Hopkins Precursors Study [49,50]. When specifically evaluating nonsmoking respondents aged <65 years, a population that would be expected to have fewer competing CVD risk factors, the impact of obesity elimination increased substantially, with estimates for obesity-attributed CVD outcomes almost doubling. This supports previous studies that found a stronger link between obesity and CVD outcomes in this subpopulation [43,51] and suggests that obesity is particularly attributable to early development of CVD and HF.

Findings from this analysis have important implications for clinical practice and policy regarding CVD reduction strategies. While proper management of type 2 diabetes and hypertension are crucial to reduce CVD burden, our results suggest that targeting obesity, which occurs upstream of both conditions, may also be an effective strategy. Addressing obesity early—either through prevention or treatment—may help mitigate the onset of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, HF, and other related comorbidities. This approach can ultimately reduce the clinical burden of managing chronic obesity-related conditions and improve long-term quality of life and mortality.

These results are particularly important for a wide range of people in the US, as the prevalence of obesity [3] and CVD [52] continues to rise. It should be noted that the exclusion of respondents younger than 35 years will limit the applicability of these findings to younger individuals. Importantly, the prevalence of obesity in children and adolescents has increased by more than 150 % between 1990 and 2021 in both male and female adolescents [55]. By 2050, prevalence of overweight and obesity in US children and adolescents is projected to exceed 40 million [55]. The adulthood risk of mortality and morbidity, including the risk of developing chronic diseases such as CVD, is significantly increased by excess adiposity during childhood [56]. Further, studies have demonstrated that young adults (aged 20–29 years) are among the most at-risk for development of obesity [57], and weight gain between the ages of 20 and 35 years may predict all-cause mortality in later years (50–69 years of age) [58]. Future studies should give special consideration to the relationship between obesity during childhood and/or young adulthood and lifetime risk of obesity and CVD.

Limitations

There are several limitations associated with this study. Responses from the interview portion of NHANES, as with any self-reported measure, are subject to both recall and social desirability bias; however, the impact of recall is expected to be lower for CVD outcomes given the severity and the high-test performance of these measures in validation studies [53]. Although behavioral validation studies are limited, certain risk factors such as smoking have demonstrated moderate to high agreement with the data [54]. Additionally, although the use of nationally representative NHANES data allowed us to directly estimate BMI while capturing important lifestyle factors (eg, smoking), the cross-sectional nature of these data complicated their incorporation into the fundamentally longitudinal nature of these estimates. The longitudinal adaptation of these data can partially mitigate the impact of issues such as reverse causality by combining weight history with reported outcome and risk factor timing; however, its effectiveness is entirely dependent on the accuracy and completeness of interview questions.

Potential measurement error, misclassification of disease and weight status, and precise temporal ordering of events are also important limitations. It is important to note that BMI is an imperfect measure of metabolic health, and the results reported here represent the general risk of CVD in people with obesity, but cannot account for subclassification of respondents with obesity as metabolically healthy or unhealthy. Estimates reported here are roughly consistent with similar estimates of total effects, indicating that these issues may not have significantly affected data adaptation [23]. It is also possible that not all the assumptions required for causality within the network of our Bayesian networks hold, particularly conditional independence. Even in this scenario, Bayesian networks are a valid and consistent estimator that better represent the underlying relationships of interest due to their ability to capture complex relationships between variables, represent dependencies between risk factors, and include risk factors that do not affect the outcomes directly. Additional key risk factors of interest, such as cholesterol, were not captured through the data source. Therefore, these estimates may be conservative and would benefit from replication in a truly longitudinal data set.

Conclusion

In the present work, a sophisticated network modeling approach was used to estimate the obesity-attributable CVD burden in the United States. Results highlight the role of obesity as a CVD risk factor, particularly among younger nonsmokers, and provide insight into the nature of this relationship. Future research should focus on confirming these findings using nationally representative, longitudinal data from a unique data source, such as an EHR databases or an alternative national dataset, such as the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. Collected data should include information on both physiological and lifestyle factors (eg, smoking or alcohol use).
