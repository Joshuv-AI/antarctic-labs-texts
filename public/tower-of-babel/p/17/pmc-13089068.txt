Physicochemical properties of amyloid-like fibrils from ultrasonication-induced plum seed protein-catechin conjugates
Authors: Fan Zhao, Yongtuo Zhang, Jinyao Zha, Jianming Cheng, Feng Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amyloid-like fibrils show promise as natural functional ingredients in the food industry owing to their distinctive structure and properties. However, the compact conformation of many plant proteins often limits the efficient production of functional amyloid-like fibrils. In this study, amyloid-like fibrils were fabricated from plum seed protein-catechin conjugates prepared via an ultrasonication-induced method. The fibrils were systematically characterized by microscopy and spectroscopy, and their emulsifying and rheological functionalities were evaluated. Compared with fibrils from native protein, ultrasonicated protein, and alkaline-induced conjugates, those derived from the ultrasonication-induced conjugates exhibited a more ordered structure, increased contour length and branching, and significantly enhanced surface hydrophobicity. These structural improvements translated into superior emulsifying performance and a unique heat-induced gelation ability. The results demonstrate that ultrasonication-induced covalent modification is an effective pretreatment strategy for enhancing both the formation and functional properties of plant protein-based amyloid-like fibrils.

Introduction

Amyloid-like fibrils were initially identified in association with pathological conditions such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases (Chiti & Dobson, 2006). Recent studies; however; have demonstrated that these fibrils can be utilized to enhance the functional properties of proteins (Kutzli et al., 2023). Their formation is commonly promoted by protein unfolding or partial hydrolysis; which can be induced under acidic conditions and/or by heat treatment (Josefsson et al., 2020). Owing to their structural and functional characteristics; amyloid-like fibrils have gained interest for applications in the food industry; for example as emulsion stabilizers (Peng et al., 2016); gelation agents (Zhang et al., 2014); and wall materials for the microencapsulation of active compounds (Roode et al., 2019). It is noteworthy that while amyloid formation involves protein denaturation and structural rearrangement, the resulting fibrils often exhibit novel functional properties that are distinct from those of the native protein. Thus, the process represents a functional transformation rather than mere degradation.

In recent years, various plant proteins have been employed to produce amyloid-like fibrils. Examples include soy protein (Han et al., 2026); coconut protein (Song et al., 2026); quinoa protein (Meng et al., 2025); chickpea protein (Xiao et al., 2025) and rice protein (Zhang et al., 2026). Following amyloid fibrillization; these proteins demonstrate significant improvements in emulsifying capacity; foaming properties; and the ability to encapsulate active ingredients. With the growing exploration of medicinal and edible resources in China; many plant-derived seeds from such resources have gained attention as novel protein sources (Zhao, Liu, & Xue, 2023). One example is plum seed; a protein-rich medicinal and edible material. However; it has been primarily used for the extraction of amygdalin; during which a large amount of protein is discarded as a by-product (Xue et al., 2018). Therefore; investigating whether plum seed protein can be utilized to prepare amyloid-like fibrils represents an important approach and reference for promoting the sustainable utilization of medicinal and edible resources. Compared with other plant proteins that have been used to prepare amyloid-like fibrils (such as soy protein); plum seed protein exhibits distinct advantages. First; our previous research found that plum seed protein demonstrates a stronger heat-induced gelation ability than soy protein (Xue et al., 2018); suggesting a greater propensity for intermolecular interactions. Given that intermolecular interaction is a key driving force for protein assembly into amyloid-like fibrils; this characteristic makes plum seed protein an ideal candidate for fibril preparation. Second; plum seed protein itself is rich in bioactive peptides with antioxidant; antihypertensive; and hypoglycemic activities (Xue, Mejares, & Dokouhaki, 2025). Therefore, amyloid-like fibrils derived from this protein may not only serve as functional food ingredients but also potentially possess inherent biological activities, thereby providing additional health benefits.

As a novel protein source, the fundamental physicochemical properties of plum seed protein are gradually being elucidated. Our preliminary studies have determined several key parameters: its isoelectric point is approximately 4.5 (Xue, Li, & Cheng, 2024); and its thermal denaturation temperature is around 50 °C (Zhao, Liu, & Xue, 2023). It is primarily composed of three major subunits with molecular weights of approximately 40; 25; and 15 kDa (Zhao, Liu, & Xue, 2023) and the α-helix constitutes the main component of the secondary structure (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). Over the past few years; we have been investigating the physicochemical properties of plum seed protein and its modification approaches. These primarily include ultrasonic treatment (Xue et al., 2018); enzymatic hydrolysis (Xue, Mejares, & Dokouhaki, 2025); polyphenol conjugation (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025); polysaccharide complexation (Li et al., 2020); probiotic fermentation (Xue, Li, & Cheng, 2024); and high-pressure processing (Zhao, Liu, & Xue, 2023). Through these modification techniques, the solubility, emulsifying activity, gelation ability, and film-forming properties of plum seed protein have been significantly improved, greatly expanding its potential applications in the food industry. However, no study has yet explored how modification strategies can promote the formation of amyloid-like fibrils from plum seed protein.

The capacity of proteins to form amyloid-like fibrils is fundamentally governed by their structural attributes. In the case of plant proteins, their compact conformation and limited solubility often restrict hydrolysis and unfolding under acidic conditions, consequently impeding efficient amyloid-like fibril formation or leading to low yields (Liu et al., 2024). Therefore; various modification techniques are employed as pretreatments to alter protein structures; thereby enhancing their propensity for amyloid fibrillization. Technologies such as electric (Leal et al., 2024) and magnetic field (Meng et al., 2025) treatment can induce unfolding of protein conformations; which is beneficial for subsequent fibril formation. Among the various pretreatment techniques; ultrasonication offers unique advantages along with certain limitations. Compared to these alternative approaches; the powerful cavitation effect not only effectively induces protein unfolding; exposing more hydrophobic groups and reactive sites; but also generates free radicals that can directly mediate covalent cross-linking between proteins and polyphenols (Liu, Shi, et al., 2025). However; ultrasonication technology also faces challenges; including energy efficiency considerations during industrial scale-up and noise pollution during operation; which warrant consideration for future practical applications (Xue et al., 2021). Our preliminary research indicates that modifying proteins with polyphenols not only facilitates covalent cross-linking between proteins but also promotes protein unfolding; consequently improving emulsifying and gelation properties (Li et al., 2025; Liu, Shi, et al., 2025). However, whether covalent polyphenol modification can promote amyloid-like fibril formation remains unexplored.

In preparing conjugates of proteins and polyphenols, the most commonly used method is the alkaline approach, under which conditions the phenolic hydroxyl groups of polyphenols are oxidized to quinones, subsequently forming covalent bonds with the free amino or sulfhydryl groups of proteins (Sun & Zhong, 2022). Although this method is straightforward; the reaction typically requires a prolonged duration; often exceeding 24 h. In recent years; we have found that ultrasonication can rapidly induce covalent cross-linking between polyphenols and proteins (Li et al., 2025). The underlying mechanisms primarily involve the following aspects. First; free radicals generated by ultrasound can induce the formation of amino radicals from protein amino groups; which can then covalently couple with the phenolic hydroxyl groups of polyphenols. Additionally; ultrasound-generated radicals can also oxidize phenolic hydroxyl groups to quinones; thereby promoting covalent bonding between quinones and the free amino or sulfhydryl groups of proteins. Finally; the cavitation effect induced by ultrasound can promote protein unfolding; exposing more reactive sites for covalent conjugation. We have successfully applied this approach to prepare conjugates of various proteins (such as soy protein; casein; hemp seed protein; and plum seed protein) with polyphenols (Liu, Shi, et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). Moreover, our studies have shown that the protein-polyphenol conjugates produced via ultrasonication exhibit superior functional properties compared with those obtained by the conventional alkaline method (Liu et al., 2023b; X. Zhao, Li, & Xue, 2023). However, whether conjugates of protein-polyphenol induced by ultrasound can be used for subsequent amyloid-like fibril formation remains to be investigated.

Among the diverse array of polyphenolic compounds, catechin (a flavanol naturally abundant in tea) has attracted considerable attention due to its potent antioxidant activity (Saleem et al., 2024) and well-documented ability to interact with proteins (Zhao et al., 2026). In the present study; catechin was selected as the model polyphenol based on our preliminary screening experiments. We compared the modification effects of several polyphenols (including catechin; curcumin; and proanthocyanidin) on plum seed protein and found that catechin exhibited the most pronounced improvement in protein functional properties (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). Therefore, this study employs catechin for covalent modification of plum seed protein to investigate its subsequent impact on amyloid-like fibril formation.

Therefore, this study first utilizes ultrasonication to induce the covalent cross-linking of plum seed protein with polyphenols. The resulting conjugates will then be employed to prepare amyloid-like fibrils, and their structural and physicochemical properties will be characterized. For comparison, three control groups will be included: native plum seed protein, the protein subjected solely to ultrasonic treatment, and the conjugate prepared via the conventional alkaline method. This approach is designed to compare the effects of different preparation methods on the structure and functionality of the resulting amyloid-like fibrils, thereby providing insights for the targeted improvement of plant protein-based amyloid-like fibrils in future research.

Materials and methods

Materials

Plum seeds were sourced from Jiangsu Chengkai Traditional Chinese Medicine Co., Ltd., Huaian, China. Plum seed protein isolate (PSPI) was extracted from defatted seed meal through a process of alkaline solubilization followed by acid-induced precipitation (Xue et al., 2018). Catechin (purity ≥90%), thioflavin T (10 mM) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, purity ≥90%) were supplied by Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. 1,8-Anilinonaphthalenesulfonate (ANS) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Preparation of PSPI-polyphenol conjugates

The ultrasound-assisted alkaline conjugation was performed following a previously established protocol (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). Briefly; 1 g of PSPI and 15 mg of catechin were dispersed in 100 mL of distilled water and homogenized (FM200 homogenizer; FLUKO; China) at 10;000 rpm for 1 min. The pH of the dispersion was then adjusted to 9.0 using 1 M NaOH. Ultrasonication was performed using a 0.636 cm diameter titanium probe (NingBo Scientz Biotechnology Co. Ltd.; China) operated at a frequency of 20 kHz and an output power of 400 W. The treatment was applied in pulse mode (2 s on; 2 s off) for 30 min; with the sample container immersed in an ice-water bath to maintain the temperature below 25 °C. These parameters were selected based on our previous studies (Liu, Shi, et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Following sonication, the pH was readjusted to 7.0 with 1 M HCl. The sample was dialyzed against deionized water at 4 °C using an 8 kDa molecular weight cut-off membrane for 48 h, then freeze-dried (LABCONCO, USA) and stored at 4 °C. For the conventional alkaline-induced conjugation, a dispersion containing 1 g PSPI and 15 mg catechin was adjusted to pH 9.0 and stirred continuously in the dark for 24 h. The subsequent steps (neutralization, dialysis, and freeze-drying) were identical to those described above. Control samples included native PSPI and PSPI treated with ultrasonication in the absence of polyphenols.

Preparation of amyloid-like fibrils from conjugates

Freeze-dried protein or conjugate samples were dissolved in distilled water (pH 2.0) at a concentration of 1% (w/w). After stirring for 30 min at ambient temperature, the pH was readjusted to 2.0, followed by centrifugation (Beckman, Allegra 64R, USA) at 10,000 ×g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble material. The supernatant was then incubated at 90 °C in a water bath for 8 h under continuous stirring at 500 rpm. Finally, the heated solution was rapidly cooled in an ice-water bath to terminate the reaction and the pH was readjusted to 7.0. The amyloid-like fibrils prepared from native PSPI were designated as PF. Those prepared from ultrasonically treated PSPI were abbreviated as PFU. The fibrils derived from the conjugates obtained via the conventional alkaline method were named PCFA, while those from the ultrasonically prepared conjugates were termed PCFU.

Microscopic image, particle size and zeta potential of amyloid-like fibrils

The microstructure was examined by placing the solution on a glass slide and observing it under a microscope (DM2500, Leica, Germany). The particle size of the sample was assessed by laser diffraction (Beckman Coulter LS 13,320, USA) using a 1% (w/v) aqueous dispersion. Subsequently, the ζ-potential was determined using the electrophoretic light scattering mode of a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). Electrophoretic mobility of the samples was measured by laser Doppler velocimetry and converted to ζ-potential values using the Henry equation. Given that the samples were dispersed in an aqueous medium with moderate ionic strength, the Smoluchowski approximation (f(κa) = 1.5) was applied for ζ-potential calculation. Measurements were performed at 25 °C with an equilibration time of 120 s, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Determination of thioflavin T fluorescence

Thioflavin T fluorescence was measured following a previously described method with minor modifications (Ni et al., 2025). Briefly, a thioflavin T stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 0.05 mM in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10 mM, pH 7.0). An aliquot of 50 μL of each sample was combined with 5 mL of the thioflavin T working solution. The fluorescence intensity was then recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F380, Tianjin Gangdong Sci. & Tech. Development Co., Ltd., China) with excitation at 440 nm and emission at 482 nm.

X-ray diffraction

Amyloid-like fibrils were isolated from solution by centrifugal filtration (MWCO 100 kDa) at 6000 ×g for 25 min at 20 °C. The retentate was washed three times with distilled water via repeated centrifugation. Following freeze-drying (−50 °C, 48 h; LABCONCO, USA), X-ray diffraction analysis of lyophilized sample was carried out using a SmartLab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.540 Å). Sample preparation followed previously established protocols (Yang, Zeng, et al., 2025). In brief, each sample was placed onto a glass substrate and carefully flattened to obtain a uniform surface. Diffractograms were recorded over a 2θ range of 10° to 80° at a scanning speed of 10°/min, with a slit width of 0.5 mm. The generator was operated at 40 kV and 60 mA throughout the measurement.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity

The lyophilized sample was reconstituted in phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 7.5) at 0.05–2.0 mg/mL for subsequent determination of surface hydrophobicity (H₀) using the ANS-binding method (Xue, Li, & Adhikari, 2024). Briefly, 20 μL of 8.0 mM ANS solution was added to each sample dilution. Fluorescence intensity was then measured (λex 390 nm, λem 470 nm) using a Tecan Spark 10 M spectrophotometer (Switzerland). The H₀ value was derived from the initial slope of the plot of fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration.

Fourier transforms infrared (FTIR) analysis

Prior to analysis, the lyophilized samples were compressed into pellets. FTIR spectra were then collected on a Nicolet 380 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in the range of 500–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, accumulating 32 scans per spectrum (triplicate measurements per sample). To ensure the accuracy of secondary structure calculations, all spectra were baseline-corrected prior to deconvolution. Deconvolution and curve fitting (as shown in supplementary materials, Fig. S1) of the amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) were performed according to previous study (Li et al., 2025). The assignment of sub-bands to secondary structure components was as follows: 1610–1640/1680–1690 cm−1 for β-sheets; 1640–1650 cm−1 for random coils; 1650–1660 cm−1 for α-helices; and 1660–1670 cm−1 for β-turns. The relative area percentages of each sub-band were calculated by Gaussian curve fitting using PeakFit v4.12 software, and the content of each secondary structure component was determined as the percentage of its corresponding sub-band area to the total amide I band area.

Intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence

The intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of the samples (1 mg/mL) was measured using a Spark 10 M spectrophotometer (Tecan, Switzerland) with an excitation wavelength of 285 nm and an emission scan from 300 to 600 nm.

Observation of microstructure

For a more detailed examination of the microstructure, the lyophilized sample was then coated with gold by spraying and observed using a scanning electron microscope (Regulus8100, Hitachi, Japan). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed using a Dimension Icon system (Bruker Corp., Karlsruhe, Germany) operated at a scan rate of 1.5 Hz. For imaging, samples were first dispersed in distilled water at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, after which an aliquot was deposited onto freshly cleaved mica flakes. The prepared specimens were subsequently dried at 25 °C for 12 h prior to analysis.

Emulsifying properties

To determine the emulsifying properties, 80 mL of protein dispersion (2 mg/mL) was homogenized with 20 mL of corn oil at 20,000 rpm for 1 min. The emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) were calculated by measuring the absorbance at 500 nm of the bottom emulsion layer, both immediately and after 10 min of standing, following dilution with sodium dodecyl sulfate solution (Xue & Li, 2023). The emulsion was then further processed with a high-pressure homogenizer (Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) at 50 MPa for three cycles. The emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) were calculated using the following equations:

EAI (m2/g) = (2 × 2.303 × A₀ × DF) / (c × φ × θ × 10,000).

where A₀ is the absorbance at 0 min; DF is the dilution factor (100 in this study); c is the protein concentration (g/mL); φ is the oil volume fraction (0.2); and θ is the path length of the cuvette (0.01 m).

ESI (min) = A₀ / (A₀ - A₁₀) × Δt.

where A₀ is the absorbance at 0 min; A₁₀ is the absorbance at 10 min; and Δt is the time interval (10 min).

For further investigation of emulsion stability, the emulsion prepared as described above was homogenized using a high-pressure homogenizer (Ningbo Xinzhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) at 50 MPa for three cycles. Subsequently, 10 mL of the emulsion sample was placed into a transparent glass bottle (internal diameter: 13 mm, height: 50 mm) for image acquisition. After storage at 4 °C for seven days, images of the emulsions were taken, and the heights of the cream layer and the serum layer were measured to determine the creaming index (X. Liu et al., 2023a). For microstructural observation; the emulsion was stained with FITC solution (final concentration 0.1 mg/mL in dimethyl sulfoxide) and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica CMS; Germany). Additionally; the interfacial tension between the protein solution (1% m/v) and the oil phase was measured using the Du Noüy ring method on a KRUSS DSA25 tensiometer (Germany) (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025).

Rheological behavior

Rheological measurements were performed on an HR-1 rheometer (TA Instruments, UK) using a 60 mm parallel plate geometry with a 1 mm gap. For viscosity analysis, a 1 wt% sample solution was prepared in distilled water. Steady shear measurements were conducted over a shear rate range of 0.1 to 1000 s−1. For gelation analysis, a 10 wt% solution was used (Liu, Shi, et al., 2025). Temperature sweeps from 25 to 95 °C (heating rate: 5 °C/min) were performed at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz and a strain of 0.05%. The sample was held at 95 °C for 20 min before cooling back to 25 °C at the same rate.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) tests

DSC measurements were conducted on a thermal analyzer (TA Instruments, USA). Approximately 5 mg of each sample was hermetically sealed in an aluminum pan and heated from 25 to 200 °C at a scanning rate of 10 °C·min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting thermograms were processed using TA Universal Analysis software. The peak denaturation temperature, corresponding to the maximum heat flow, was determined from the thermograms.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent replicates. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) among groups was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test using SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0, IBM Corp., USA).

Results and discussion

Microscopic image

As shown in Fig. 1, PSPI formed fine, needle-like amyloid-like fibrils under acidic and thermal conditions. This is consistent with the behavior of other proteins, primarily because amyloid-like fibrils typically exhibit chain-like structures in which β-sheet protein subunits twist along the fibril axis and stack through aggregation (Xu et al., 2023). Notably; the fibrils formed by PSPI were predominantly linear; with no branched structures observed; which aligns with previous reports on amyloid-like fibrils derived from ovalbumin (Liu, Tang, et al., 2025) and coconut protein (Song et al., 2026). It is worth noting that after ultrasonic treatment; a clear increase in the length of PSPI-derived amyloid-like fibrils was observed; indicating that ultrasonication promotes fibril formation. This is likely because ultrasonic treatment alters protein structure; inducing unfolding and thereby facilitating amyloid-like fibril assembly. This result is consistent with prior studies on arachin (Yang, Yu, et al., 2025) and soy protein (Ni et al., 2025); which also demonstrated that ultrasonication enhances amyloid-like fibril formation. Furthermore; previous research has found that amyloid-like fibrils formed under ultrasonication exhibit improved functional properties; such as emulsifying and foaming capabilities (Yang et al., 2024). When amyloid-like fibrils were prepared from conjugates; a significant increase in fibril length along with distinct branching structures became evident. This suggests that conjugation with polyphenol also favors amyloid-like fibril generation. This effect is related to the impact of polyphenol modification on protein structure. Our earlier study confirmed that conjugation with polyphenol induces protein unfolding (Liu, Shi, et al., 2025); a structural change crucial for fibril formation. Notably; compared to fibrils prepared from alkali-induced conjugates; those derived from ultrasonically-assisted conjugates showed marked increases in number; length; and degree of branching. This may be attributed to the less compacted structure of the ultrasonically-induced conjugates. Our previous work revealed that; through free radical and cavitation effects; ultrasonication not only promotes covalent cross-linking between proteins and polyphenols but also induces protein unfolding (Li et al., 2025). Consequently; ultrasonically-induced conjugates outperform their alkali-treated counterparts in key functional properties; including emulsifying capacity and solubility (Liu et al., 2023b). Particle size and zeta potential

As shown in Fig. 2A, the trend in particle size variation aligns with microscopic observations. Specifically, the fibrils formed by PSPI after ultrasonic treatment exhibited a larger particle size. This is consistent with the microscopic observation that ultrasonication leads to an increase in fibril length. In contrast, previous studies have reported that the particle size of fibrils did not change significantly after ultrasound treatment, and prolonged exposure even led to a reduction in fibril size. The authors attributed this phenomenon to the enhanced decomposition of amino acids into polypeptides under acidic conditions induced by ultrasound (Ni et al., 2025). Previous studies on the preparation of amyloid-like fibrils from legume proteins have also found that the heat treatment and acid hydrolysis lead to ‌degraded polypeptides and disrupted structure‌; which consequently results in a reduction in the fibril size (Li et al., 2021). However; such an effect of ultrasound was not observed in PSPI in our study; indicating that the response to ultrasonication varies among proteins from different sources. This is consistent with earlier findings that proteins derived from different origins exhibit inconsistent responses to ultrasound treatment (Hu et al., 2013). Fibrils prepared from the conjugates also showed a larger particle size, which correlates with the observed increase in fibril length and the appearance of branching structures under the microscope. Regarding surface charge, the ζ-potential of PSPI-derived fibrils was approximately −25 mV (Fig. 2B). Our previous study on native PSPI reported a potential of around −32 mV (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). This difference suggests that during fibril formation; negatively charged groups may align and transfer toward the interior of the fibril structure. Fibrils from ultrasonically treated PSPI showed a less negative ζ-potential (a higher value); which is consistent with previous reports indicating that sonication reduces the net surface charge density of protein fibrils (Ni et al., 2025). Similarly; fibrils from conjugates possessed a lower surface charge; with the lowest value observed for fibrils prepared from ultrasonically-induced conjugates. These results indicate that as the degree of branching and the contour length of amyloid-like fibrils increase; their surface charge density decreases. This trend aligns with the generally accepted mechanism of amyloid-like fibril formation; where fibrillation relies on intermolecular interactions between proteins (Rostamabadi et al., 2025). A lower surface charge density facilitates fibril assembly by reducing intermolecular electrostatic repulsion, thereby promoting molecular aggregation and structural rearrangement into fibrils.

Thioflavin T (Th T) fluorescence

As shown in Fig. 2C, the fibrils formed after ultrasonication exhibited a higher Th T fluorescence intensity, suggesting that ultrasonication promotes fibril formation. This observation is consistent with previous studies, which have reported that soybean protein fibrils formed upon ultrasonication display increased Th T fluorescence intensity (Ni et al., 2025). Furthermore, covalent conjugation with polyphenols led to a further increase in Th T fluorescence intensity, indicating that polyphenol-induced protein unfolding may further facilitate fibril formation. Notably, the amyloid-like fibrils derived from conjugates prepared using the ultrasonication-assisted method exhibited the highest Th T fluorescence intensity, suggesting a synergistic effect between the two modifications.

X-ray diffraction

As shown in Fig. 2D, all fibril samples exhibited a distinct diffraction peak at approximately 19°, which is consistent with the characteristic cross-β-sheet structure of amyloid-like fibrils reported in previous studies (Xu et al., 2025). Similar findings have been observed in other amyloid-like fibril systems; such as those derived from coconut protein; where a prominent peak at around 19° was also detected (Song et al., 2026). Notably, fibrils formed after covalent conjugation with polyphenols displayed a less pronounced peak at this position, which may be attributed to the formation of branched fibrillar structures that potentially disrupt the original ordered arrangement.

Surface hydrophobicity

As shown in Fig. 3A, the fibrils formed by ultrasonicated PSPI exhibited higher surface hydrophobicity than those from native PSPI. This enhancement can be attributed to ultrasonication-induced surface hydrophobicity, which promotes the arrangement of hydrophobic groups and facilitates the formation of an ordered fibrillar structure. This observation aligns with previous findings on soy protein-derived amyloid-like fibrils, where ultrasonication similarly increased fibril surface hydrophobicity (Ni et al., 2025). The underlying mechanism likely involves the cavitation effect of ultrasound; which directly exposes interior hydrophobic groups of proteins-a phenomenon widely reported in studies on soybean (Xue et al., 2020); quinoa (Li et al., 2018); and hemp seed (Liu et al., 2022) proteins. Covalent conjugation with polyphenols further increased the surface hydrophobicity of the amyloid-like fibrils. This increase is likely due to formation of branched fibril structures and extended chain lengths requires molecular rearrangement; which may also contribute to enhanced surface hydrophobicity. Consistent with this; increased surface hydrophobicity has been observed during the growth kinetics of ovalbumin-derived amyloid-like fibrils. (Pi et al., 2023). Notably, the amyloid-like fibrils prepared from the conjugates obtained via the ultrasonication-assisted method displayed the highest surface hydrophobicity. FTIR spectra

The band observed at 3200–3600 cm−1, known as the amide-A band, is associated with the stretching vibration of hydrogen-bonded groups (Yang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3B, the intensity of the amide-A band increased in fibrils treated with either ultrasonication or covalent polyphenol modification. This suggests that hydrogen bonding may play a role in the formation of branched fibrils and the elongation of fibrillar chains. Consistent with previous studies on protein interaction forces, an enhancement of hydrogen-bonding interactions between proteins has been reported to lead to increased absorbance of the amide-A band in FTIR spectra (Wu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). In addition to hydrogen bonding, the covalent interactions between PSPI and catechin induced by ultrasonication also contributed to the structural reorganization. As demonstrated in our previous study, ultrasound-generated free radicals facilitate the formation of covalent bonds between phenolic hydroxyl groups of catechin and amino acid residues of PSPI (Xue, Zhao, et al., 2025). This covalent conjugation promotes protein unfolding and exposes previously buried reactive sites, thereby enhancing intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding associations during fibril formation.

The secondary structure

As shown in Fig. 3C, a clear reduction in α-helix content and a concurrent increase in β-sheet structure were observed in the fibrils treated with either ultrasonication or covalent polyphenol modification. This indicates that during the formation of branched fibrils and the elongation of fibrillar chains, the protein secondary structure undergoes a transition from α-helix to β-sheet. Such a shift aligns with the general theory of amyloid-like fibril formation, in which the native protein structure is disrupted and reorganized through the rearrangement of intramolecular hydrogen bonds, ultimately leading to a more ordered structure (Li et al., 2021). It should be noted that the FTIR measurements reflect the overall secondary structure of the sample; which includes both fibrillar and non-fibrillar components. The increase in β-sheet content is thus indicative of enhanced fibrillation; but may also be influenced by the amount of fibrils formed. Consistent with our findings; previous studies on soy protein-derived fibrils have also reported that ultrasonication reduces α-helix content while enhancing β-sheet formation (Ni et al., 2025). Similarly; research on polyphenol-covalently modified proteins has shown that conjugation can induce protein unfolding and aggregation; which is reflected in secondary structural changes as a decrease in α-helix and an increase in β-sheet content (Li et al., 2025). This structural rearrangement further enables the conjugates to form more stable gels upon subsequent heat induction through enhanced intermolecular interactions (Kim et al., 2024). Furthermore, fibrils prepared from conjugates obtained via ultrasonication exhibited the highest β-sheet content, suggesting that the restructuring process in this system yields a more ordered supramolecular assembly.

Endogenous fluorescence analysis

The intrinsic fluorescence spectra, which monitor aromatic amino acids such as tryptophan (Trp) and tyrosine (Tyr), can reveal structural and conformational changes in biomacromolecules by reflecting alterations in the local tertiary environment around these residues (Gasymov et al., 2010). As shown in Fig. 3D; a distinct decrease in fluorescence intensity at the maximum emission wavelength was observed for fibrils treated with either ultrasonication or polyphenol conjugation. This indicates that both treatments induce differences in the tertiary structure of the resulting amyloid-like fibrils. The decrease in fluorescence intensity without a shift in emission maximum suggests that tryptophan and tyrosine residues are not significantly exposed to a more polar environment during fibrillation. Instead; the reduction likely results from shielding of aromatic residues within the densely packed fibrillar structure; or from differences in fibril yield and bundle formation induced by ultrasonication and polyphenol conjugation. Consistent with this finding; previous studies on soy protein-derived fibrils have also reported a reduction in fluorescence intensity following ultrasonication (Ni et al., 2025). Notably; the fibrils prepared from the conjugates obtained via ultrasonication exhibited the lowest fluorescence intensity; suggesting the formation of a more ordered and compact structure. This orderliness likely enhances the shielding of aromatic residues due to protein rearrangement and/or increases the proportion of amorphous fibril regions that mask these fluorophores (Liu; et al.; 2025; Pang et al., 2025). Similar observations have been reported in studies on other protein-based amyloid-like fibrils; where more ordered and densely packed structures were associated with lower fluorescence intensities (Song et al., 2026). These fluorescence results are consistent with the secondary structural changes discussed earlier, further supporting the conclusion that the combined ultrasonication and polyphenol conjugation promotes a more organized fibrillar architecture.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

As shown in Fig. 4A, SEM clearly revealed the surface morphology of the amyloid-like fibrils. Fibrils prepared from native PSPI primarily consisted of protein aggregates. These particles are likely peptide fragments released from PSPI due to acid hydrolysis during incubation at acidic pH and heat treatment. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies on β-lactoglobulin (Ng et al., 2016; Oboroceanu et al., 2010) and hemp seed protein (Kutzli et al., 2023). When ultrasonicated PSPI was used for fibril formation; protein aggregates remained predominant; however; the continuity between aggregates was notably enhanced. This improvement in connectivity explains the increased fibril length observed after ultrasonic treatment. Previous studies have similarly found that ultrasonic treatment promotes soybean protein fibril formation; a phenomenon attributed to the release of active peptide monomers capable of assembling into oligomeric nuclei (Ni et al., 2025). These nuclei then serve as seeds for amyloid-like fibril growth under acid-heating conditions, leading to the formation of elongated fibrils. Notably, when PSPI modified via conjugation with polyphenols was employed, protein aggregates were largely absent. Instead, the fibrils exhibited a more uniform and dense surface structure, further supporting that polyphenol modification promotes amyloid-like fibril formation. Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

As shown in Fig. 4B, fibrils derived from PSPI or ultrasonication-treated PSPI predominantly exhibited short, linear structures, which were consistent with the observations under optical microscopy. However, fibrils formed from ultrasonication-treated PSPI were noticeably thicker and more robust, further indicating that ultrasonication promotes fibril formation. In contrast, fibrils formed after conjugation with polyphenols displayed distinct branched structures that entangled with one another, forming a network-like morphology. This structural feature was particularly pronounced in the amyloid-like fibrils prepared from conjugates obtained via the ultrasonication-assisted method, with most fibrils measuring approximately 400 nm in length. In comparison, previous studies on hemp seed protein-derived amyloid-like fibrils reported lengths of around 7 μm (Kutzli et al., 2023), highlighting substantial structural differences in fibrils formed from proteins of different origins.

Emulsifying properties

As shown in Fig. 5A and B, compared with the emulsifying properties of native PSPI reported in our previous study (Xue et al., 2018); the amyloid-like fibrils exhibited superior emulsifying performance. This enhancement can be primarily attributed to the ability of amyloid-like fibrils to act as colloidal particles; thereby facilitating the formation of a stable Pickering emulsion (Pang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024). Consistently, earlier research has also indicated that amyloid-like fibrils adsorb more rapidly at the oil/water interface than their native protein counterparts, leading to improved emulsifying capacity (Ji et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2025). Our findings further demonstrated that both ultrasonic treatment and conjugation with polyphenols significantly improved the emulsifying properties of the fibrils. In particular, fibrils prepared from the ultrasonication-induced conjugates showed the highest emulsifying capacity. This may be associated with their increased surface hydrophobicity and reduced surface charge. Previous studies have suggested that higher surface hydrophobicity promotes protein adsorption at the oil/water interface (Xue & Li, 2023). Moreover; a lower surface charge density diminishes intermolecular electrostatic repulsion; which favors greater adsorption at the interface and consequently enhances emulsifying performance (Xue et al., 2017). Rheological behavior

As shown in Fig. 7A, all fibril samples exhibited a decrease in viscosity with increasing shear rate, indicating shear-thinning behavior typical of non-Newtonian fluids (Silva et al., 2015). Compared to fibrils formed from PSPI alone; those derived from conjugates displayed higher apparent viscosity. Notably; fibrils prepared from the ultrasonication-induced conjugate showed a significantly greater viscosity than all other samples. This can be primarily attributed to their more branched architecture and longer contour length; which promote stronger intermolecular interactions and consequently lead to higher viscosity. Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of ultrasonication-induced covalent conjugation between plum seed protein and polyphenols on the formation and physicochemical properties of amyloid-like fibrils. The fibrils produced through this strategy showed the highest degree of structural order among all prepared samples. This was attributed to the enhanced fibrillation process, which was evidenced by their highest β-sheet content, most compact morphology, and lowest intrinsic fluorescence. These structural improvements directly enhanced interfacial functionality, as the fibrils exhibited higher surface hydrophobicity, longer branched chains, and lower surface charge, leading to superior emulsifying performance and the formation of stable, uniformly dispersed emulsions. Furthermore, rheological analysis demonstrated that only fibrils from ultrasonication-induced conjugates could form a heat-induced gel network. This unique behavior could be attributed to their branched and elongated structure, which promotes molecular entanglement and hydrophobic interactions, while simultaneously reducing electrostatic repulsion and lowering the thermal denaturation temperature. In summary, ultrasonication-induced conjugation represents an efficient pretreatment strategy that optimizes protein structure to comprehensively enhance amyloid-like fibril functionality. Future studies should explore additional protein-polyphenol pairs and validate the applicability of these functional fibrils in complex food systems such as emulsion gels and edible coatings.
