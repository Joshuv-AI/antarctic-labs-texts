Exoscopic Keyhole Clipping of Unruptured Middle Cerebral Artery Aneurysms Using Multiple 4K 3-dimensional Monitors
Authors: Shingo TOYOTA, Tomoaki MURAKAMI, Kosei OKOCHI, Koichi NAKASHIMA, Shuki OKUHARA, Motoki NAKAMURA, Shuhei YAMADA, Takamune ACHIHA, Takanori FUKUNAGA, Maki KOBAYASHI, Haruhiko KISHIMA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We aimed to report our experience with exoscopic keyhole clipping of unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms using multiple 4K 3-dimensional monitors. We performed sphenoid ridge keyhole clipping of unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms using the ORBEYE exoscope (Sony Olympus Medical Solutions, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) with multiple 4K 3-dimensional monitors in 19 patients in our institution from 2020 to 2023. A 55-inch monitor was placed facing the surgeon at the end of an imaginary line extending through the Sylvian fissure ipsilateral to the target aneurysm. Two 32-inch movable sub-monitors were placed facing the surgeon at 45° angles to the left and right. Comparisons were made with a historical control group that comprised 21 patients who underwent the same procedure as above from 2017 to 2019 but with a surgical microscope rather than an exoscope. All exoscope group procedures were successfully accomplished using ORBEYE alone. Mean aneurysm diameter, craniotomy size, duration of intradural procedure, blood loss volume, and times of indocyanine green video angiography were similar in the exoscope and microscope groups. The mean indocyanine green dose was significantly lower in the exoscope group. Keyhole clipping for unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysms using an exoscope and multiple 4K 3-dimensional monitors was safe and efficient, and not inferior to using a surgical microscope, suggesting that there are no major barriers for neurosurgeons to switch from microscopic to exoscopic keyhole clipping.

Introduction

Even in the endovascular era, microsurgical clipping can achieve excellent clinical outcomes. It remains the standard treatment for unruptured middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms.1) Several effective keyhole approach clipping techniques for these aneurysms have been previously reported.2-5) The ORBEYE exoscope (Sony Olympus Medical Solutions, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), which enables surgeons to maintain ideal ergonomic positions while operating, has recently been introduced for neurosurgical use.6-8) The advantages of ORBEYE exoscopy over use of a conventional optical microscope during surgery for dural arteriovenous fistula in the anterior cranial fossa, retrosigmoid approaches to the posterior fossa, carotid endarterectomy, aneurysm clipping, and intracerebral hematoma removal have been previously reported.9-13)

When using ORBEYE, a semi-fixed 55-inch 4K 3-dimensional (3D) monitor is usually placed in front of the primary surgeon. Such a movable monitor enables use of the ergonomic merits of the exoscope, which is important during operations that require numerous operative visual axes. Ideally, the monitor should be set up in a position that enables the surgeon to view it while maintaining a comfortable posture; when the surgeon changes position as needed during surgery, the monitor's position is adjusted accordingly.11) Here, we report our experience with the use of ORBEYE and multiple 4K 3D monitors during keyhole clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms, discussing its efficacy and safety.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We performed sphenoid ridge keyhole clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms using ORBEYE with multiple 4K 3D monitors in 19 patients in our institution from 2020 to 2023. In general, we use the sphenoid ridge keyhole approach for small unruptured aneurysms (diameter <10 mm) located at the M1-M2 bifurcation; short M1 aneurysms, M1 aneurysms, distal MCA aneurysms, and those >10 mm in diameter are treated using other means. A total of 21 patients who underwent the same procedure of keyhole approach as above from 2017 to 2019 but with a surgical microscope (OPMI Pentero Infrared 800; Carl Zeiss Co., Tokyo, Japan) rather than an exoscope were used as historical controls for comparison. In both groups, the following data were recorded and analyzed: aneurysm diameter, craniotomy size, duration of intradural procedure, blood loss volume, the number of times of indocyanine green (ICG) video angiography, ICG dose used, incidence of incomplete clip application, and morbidity/mortality 3 months after surgery (Student's t-test). This study was conducted following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the approval of the institutional review board was obtained (approval number 23D021 g). Informed consent has been obtained from the patients in this study. The patients consented to the publication of their images.

As previously reported,5) after induction of general anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine with the head fixed slightly vertex down and rotated approximately 40° to the contralateral side. A 50-to-60 mm curved skin incision was performed in the hairline at the temporal region. After subfascial dissection, the temporalis fascia was cut, and a flap of skin and fascia was reflected anteriorly. The temporalis muscle was split in the direction of the muscle fibers, and a keyhole craniotomy (approximately 30 mm in diameter) was created posterior to the McCarty keyhole without exposing the temporal line, such that the lateral edge of the sphenoid ridge was the center of the craniotomy. With this precise craniotomy placement, the Sylvian fissure emerges in the center after dural opening. The dura was incised in a V shape, and the fissure was dissected using standard microsurgical techniques. The target aneurysm was then identified, dissected, and clipped (Fig. 2).

Results

All exoscope group procedures were successfully accomplished using ORBEYE alone (no conversion to surgical microscopy was required). Mean aneurysm diameter in the exoscope and microscope groups was 5.6 ± 1.2 mm and 5.6 ± 2.4 mm, respectively. Mean craniotomy size was 29.7 ± 1.6 mm and 30.8 ± 2.5 mm, respectively. Mean duration of intradural procedure was118 ± 29 mins and 114 ± 29 mins, respectively. Mean blood loss was 41 ± 34 mL and 44 ± 27 mL, respectively. The number of times of ICG video angiography was 1.8 ± 0.9 and 1.7 ± 0.7, respectively. The mean ICG dose was significantly lower in the exoscope group (2.1 ± 1.1 mL vs. 8.1 ± 3.7 mL; p = 0.000000042; Student's t-test; Table 1). No instances of incomplete clipping, morbidity, or mortality occurred in either group.

Discussion

Exoscopic clipping of unruptured MCA aneurysms using the sphenoid ridge keyhole approach showed results similar to those of conventional microsurgical clipping in terms of safety, intraoperative blood loss, duration of intradural procedure, and successful clip application. Exoscopic keyhole clipping appears to be non-inferior to conventional microsurgical clipping, suggesting that there are no major barriers for neurosurgeons to switch from microscopic to exoscopic clipping.8)

One of the major advantages of exoscopic clipping is that it allows the surgeon to change the visual axis while staying in a position in which it is comfortable to operate. Through the keyhole approach, to fully visualize and dissect the Sylvian fissure and the structures around the aneurysm as widely as possible, the surgical exo- or microscope needs to be tilted fully in all directions.5) When the keyhole approach is used, the craniotomy is smaller, and the visual axis is more constrained and steeper than with a conventional craniotomy (Fig. 3). Use of the exoscope here still allows the surgeon to perform surgery in a comfortable position even when the scope is tilted fully. This makes it possible to perform precise microsurgical maneuvers over long periods.

Comparison of visual axis in the keyhole and conventional craniotomies.

The surgeon’s visual axis is more constrained and steeper with the keyhole craniotomy (right) than the conventional craniotomy (left) because the window is smaller.

Our exoscopic method using 3 monitors has the surgeon facing the visual axis directly. The main monitor is placed at the end of an imaginary line extending through the patient's Sylvian fissure with sub-monitors at 45° angles to the left and right to accommodate the visual axis to the side. The placement of movable sub-monitors resolves the restrictions on surgeon posture that are an issue when a single monitor is used. As a result, the surgeon does not have to twist their body to see the monitor. This allows the surgeon to take full advantage of the ORBEYE ergonomic benefits.11) Moreover, installation of multiple monitors alleviates the surgical assistant's postural restrictions.

In this study, the number of times ICG was used was similar in the exoscope and microscope groups; however, the ICG dose used was significantly lower in the exoscope group. The ICG dose in the exoscope and microscope groups was 0.08 mg/kg and 0.41 mg/kg, respectively. Some studies have used a dose of 0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg of ICG for patients who underwent clipping using a surgical microscope.14,15) Our findings suggest that the exoscopic technique was able to obtain effective observations using a small dose of ICG.

Exoscopic keyhole clipping has some points to keep in mind. With the keyhole surgery, the approach angle between the visual axis and surgical instruments is limited. By tilting the exoscope sufficiently to perform extensive dissection of the Sylvian fissure, we were able to gain mobility for the M1 and M2 branches and perform a perpendicular clip on the M1 branch in almost all cases. However, because the keyhole approach has limitations in flexibility of clip application compared with the standard approach, we believe that short M1 aneurysms, M1 aneurysms, distal MCA aneurysms, and those >10 mm in diameter should be excluded. Although there are individual differences, we believe 1 of the problems with exoscopic surgery is the lack of immersion. To solve this, in addition to proper placement of the monitors, it may be effective to dim the lights in the operating room. To obtain ideal information on the area of interest with exoscope, it is important to diligently adjust the visual axis, magnification, and focus, as with a conventional microscope.

Conclusions

Sphenoid ridge keyhole clipping for unruptured MCA aneurysms using an exoscope and multiple 4K 3D monitors was safe and efficient, and not inferior to using a surgical microscope. Our method of placing 3 monitors enables the surgeon to perform surgery in an ergonomically comfortable position. It is suggested that there are no major barriers for neurosurgeons to convert from microscopic to exoscopic keyhole clipping.
