Phytic Acid/ADP Interfacial Assembly for Flame Retardancy and Self-Extinguishment in Cellulose Paper
Authors: Yangli Cui, Haoran Jiang, Yong Hu, Song Kong, Chuanwen Sun, Feifei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni composites were prepared by the interfacial assembly of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (ADP) with transition metal–phytic acid (PA) complexes. These composites were then mixed with cellulose fibers in an aqueous solution to produce flame-retardant paper. Morphological analysis revealed that the original ADP particles exhibited a smooth, cubic morphology; however, after functionalization with phytic acid, the particles became rougher. The presence of P–O–Mn and P–O–Ni bonds confirmed the bridging effect between phytic acid and ADP, which led to reduced crystallinity and promoted synergistic flame-retardant effects during combustion. Within all concentration ranges tested, both ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni exhibited significantly improved flame retardancy compared with pristine ADP and reduced the flame spread rate by up to 50%, showing remarkably enhanced flame-retardant efficiency. Notably, even at low concentrations, these two novel flame retardants endowed cellulose paper with self-extinguishing properties. Additionally, increased carbon and oxygen contents in residual char, along with cross-linking via P–C, P–O, and PO bonds, indicated the formation of a dense carbonaceous layer. Consequently, ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni significantly enhanced the flame resistance of cellulose paper while maintaining its excellent writing performance, moisture resistance, and mechanical properties.

Introduction

Cellulose-based paper materials are widely recognized for their eco-friendliness, flexibility, and renewability, serving as indispensable carriers for cultural dissemination and information storage/transmission. However, the inherent flammability of cellulose paper renders it both a fuel source and a casualty in fire incidents, underscoring the urgent need to develop flame-retardant paper products for practical applicationsincluding thermal/fireproof layers in building envelopes (walls, ceilings, and floors), insulating materials in electronics/electrical equipment, and interior fittings for transportation vehicles. To date, three primary strategies have been established to enhance the flame retardancy of cellulose paper: (i) bulk incorporation of flame retardants into pulp matrices via physical mixing; (ii) covalent grafting of reactive flame retardants onto cellulose fibers; and (iii) surface impregnation of flame retardants. For instance, Dong et al. integrated graphene oxide into hydroxypropyl cellulose slurries to fabricate flame-retardant graphene paper that retains structural integrity and undergoes mild carbonization for 90 s during combustion. While physical/chemical flame retardant incorporation effectively improves paper flammability, poor interfacial compatibility often compromises the mechanical performance, flexibility, and processability. Zhang et al. addressed this challenge by developing a chitin nanofiber-based flame retardant as a pulp additive, which endowed cellulose paper with self-extinguishing behavior upon ignition.

Compared with alternative approaches, surface impregnation offers a more efficient and facile route to impart flame retardancy to cellulose paper while preserving its intrinsic material properties. He et al. demonstrated that layer-by-layer assembly of polyhexamethylene biguanide and sodium polyphosphatedeposited onto paper via 30 s immersionsimultaneously enhanced flame retardancy and antibacterial and antifungal performance. Xu et al. reported that ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (ADP) solution impregnation significantly improved paper flame retardancy, and Yang et al. achieved enhanced flame retardancy in corrugated paper using silicon–nitrogen–phosphorus flame retardant impregnation. This impregnation strategy effectively suppresses flame propagation and mitigates the fire risks associated with combustible cellulose substrates. Notably, however, commercially viable flame retardants for cellulose paper are predominantly mineral-derived phosphorus-based compounds, and phosphate rock resourcestheir primary feedstockare globally depleted and classified as critical raw materials by the U.S. Geological Survey. This scarcity highlights the imperative to develop bio-based alternatives to mineral phosphorus flame retardants for sustainable cellulose paper applications.

Ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (ADP) is a widely used flame retardant in rubber, plastics, and construction materials due to its eco-friendliness and high flame-retardant efficacy. Ni et al. prepared a furfurylated poplar flame retardant using ADP/boric acid and biomass-derived furfuryl alcohol (FA), extending wood ignition time from 10 to 385 s. Yang et al. impregnated delignified wood with ADP and nanosilica to fabricate flame-retardant/smoke-suppressive nanopoplar wood, reducing total heat release (THR) by 73.9% and total smoke production (TSP) by 64.4%. Guo et al. combined ADP with a bio-based phosphorus-containing polyester polyol to enhance flame retardancy in bio-based polyurethane adhesives, achieving antidripping and self-extinguishing behavior at just 10 wt % ADP loading. Despite these advances, pristine ADP fails to meet modern environmental requirements for flame-retardant feedstocks, necessitating modification with bio-based flame-retardant moieties to reduce reliance on mineral phosphorus sources.

Phytic acid (PA), a biomass-derived organic phosphoric acid and the primary phosphorus storage form in plant seeds, is an ideal candidate for bio-based flame retardants due to its strong metal ion chelation capability. In the flame retardant system, ADP is commonly used as a phosphorus source and is usually combined with nitrogen-containing carbonization promoters to enhance the carbonization effect in the condensed phase. The multiphosphorylated structure and high carbon yield characteristics of PA enable it to serve not only as a structural analogue of the classic nitrogen-containing carbonization promoter but also as an additive to synergistically enhance the carbonization ability of ADP, thereby replicating and enhancing the carbonization-promoting effect of the traditional expansion-type formulation. Laufer et al. pioneered PA’s application in flame retardancy by fabricating fully renewable, eco-friendly electrolyte membranes via layer-by-layer assembly of PA and chitosan on cotton fabric, yielding a 60% improvement in flame-retardant performance. Hu et al. further exploited PA’s metal chelation properties to functionalize graphene oxide with iron-based PA complexes, drastically reducing heat release rates in flame-retardant composites. Amid the drive for sustainable development, combining PA with Mg­(OH)2 has emerged as a promising strategy to synergistically enhance cellulose paper flame retardancy, opening new avenues for eco-friendly flame-retardant material development.

In this study, we report the fabrication of bio-based flame-retardant cellulose paper via a two-step strategy: first, interfacial assembly of PA and transition metal (Mn2+/Ni2+) chelates on the surface of ADP particles (driven by electrostatic interactions and van der Waals forces), yielding two novel ADP-core flame-retardant particles (ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni) with PA–metal composite shells. At low concentrations, PA effectively chelates Mn2+ and Ni2+ ions, which react with ADP to form stable phytate layers on the ADP particle surface. Subsequent impregnation of cellulose paper with an aqueous suspension of preassembled ADP-P@Mn/Ni particles confers self-extinguishing behavior upon open flame exposure, significantly enhancing the flame-retardant performance of the paper while preserving its intrinsic material properties.

Synthesis and Characterization

Raw Materials

The chemical reagents used in this study were sourced from commercial suppliers without further purification. Ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (ADP) with a purity of ≥99% and a 70% phytic acid (PA) solution were obtained from Sinopharm Shanghai Co., Ltd. Manganese­(II) chloride (MnCl2) with a purity of ≥99% and nickel­(II) chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O) at 98% purity were acquired from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Deionized water, prepared in-house to ensure high purity and consistency, was used as the solvent throughout the experiments.

Sample Preparation of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni

As shown in Figure . ADP (4 g) was dispersed in 600 mL of deionized water to form a uniform suspension. Solution A was prepared by dissolving MnCl2 (0.35 g) in 50 mL of deionized water, and Solution B was prepared by dissolving 0.45 g of PA in 100 mL of deionized water. Solution A was added to the ADP suspension, followed by the dropwise addition of Solution B under stirring. In aqueous solution, phytic acid (PA) with multiple phosphate groups rapidly chelates Mn2+ ions to form PA–Mn complexes. These complexes are then assembled on the surface of ADP particles via electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, and interfacial coordination, rather than forming a core–shell structure. The interfacial reaction between the PA–Mn complexes and ADP leads to the formation of P–O–Mn chemical linkages, generating a unified interfacial assembly structure. The mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 1 h under argon, cooled, filtered, washed with deionized water, dried at 70 °C in a vacuum for 12 h, ground, and sieved (200 mesh) to obtain ADP-P@Mn. ADP-P@Ni was prepared similarly using 0.55 g of NiCl2·6H2O instead of MnCl2.

Illustration of the preparation of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni.

Characterization

To elucidate the characteristics of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni, a combination of scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi Regulus8100) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI Tecnai F20) was employed. SEM provided insights into the surface morphology, including structure and topography, while TEM delved into the internal microstructural details.

Chemical structure analyses were conducted using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Nicolet iS 10) to characterize chemical bonds and functional groups in char residues; FTIR spectra were acquired over 4000–400 cm–1 with a resolution of 4 cm–1 and 32 scans per sample, with baseline correction and peak deconvolution performed via OMNIC software (Thermo Fisher). X-ray diffraction (XRD; X’Pert PRO MPD) was utilized to elucidate the crystal structure of the flame-retardant composites. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo ESCALAB 250XI) was employed to quantify the surface chemical composition of char residues, with data processed using Advantage software (Thermo Fisher) under the following standardized parameters: (1) charge correction: C 1s peak at 284.8 eV as the universal calibration reference; (2) peak fitting: fixed Gaussian–Lorentzian mixture ratio of 30:70 for all peaks, with fwhm constrained to 1.2–2.0 eV for C 1s/O 1s/P 2p peaks and 1.5–2.2 eV for metal ion (Mn2+/Ni2+) peaks (no overlap between adjacent peaks, no additional fwhm constraints); (3) background type: Shirley-type background for spectral deconvolution; (4) atomic percentage quantification: average of three randomly selected spots per sample to reduce surface heterogeneity bias.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; TA TGA55) coupled with derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) was employed to evaluate the thermal stability of cellulose composite powder samples doped with flame retardants (ADP-P@Mn/ADP-P@Ni). The tests were conducted under a high-purity nitrogen (N2) atmosphere with a constant flow rate of 50 mL/min, at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 800 °C; each test used a precisely controlled sample mass of 3.0 ± 0.2 mg, and all samples were thoroughly dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h prior to measurement to eliminate moisture interference.

Flame-retardant performance was evaluated via limiting oxygen index (LOI) and vertical burning tests following established standards: LOI per ASTM D2863-22 (ISO 4589-2:2017, 150 × 50 × 0.1 mm specimens, 10 ± 0.5 L/min gas flow) and vertical burning per UL-94:2021 (125 × 13 × 0.1 mm specimens, V0/V1 rated by combustion time (≤30/50 s total) and flaming drip behavior).

Results and Discussion

Characterization

The micromorphology and particle size distribution of the pristine substrate ADP and two as-prepared modified flame retardants (ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni) were characterized by SEM, and the results are displayed in Figure . The pristine ADP powder exhibits excellent dispersibility without obvious agglomeration or caking, presenting regular cuboid particles with a size of approximately 200 μm × 100 μm (length × width) and a smooth, compact surface.

SEM images of the (a) ADP, (b) ADP-P@Mn, and (c) ADP-P@Ni.

Compared with ADP, the morphology of ADP-P@Mn changes significantly: the original cuboid structure disappears completely, and irregular particles with a size range of 50–200 μm are formed instead. The surface of individual particles is rough and uneven, showing the feature of fine particles coated by a gel-like substance. This phenomenon is attributed to the partial dissolution and fragmentation of ADP in the reaction system, while the synergistic phytate particles adhere tightly to the surface of the fragmented ADP particles via van der Waals forces. The coordination rate, nucleation kinetics, and precipitation behavior of transition metal ions Mn2+ with Ni2+ and phytic acid exhibit inherent differences, resulting in different precipitation rates, grain sizes, and distribution states of phytic acid–metal complexes on the particle surface. Eventually, these two complexes present significantly different microscopic morphologies and particle sizes.

The particle size refinement effect is more pronounced for ADP-P@Ni, whose particle size is further reduced to 10–50 μm, with significantly higher surface folds and roughness compared to those of ADP-P@Mn. It can be attributed to the faster coordination and precipitation kinetics between Ni2+ and phytic acid, as well as the higher supersaturation of the system, which results in the formation of finer precipitates. The particle size decreases gradually with the modification process, and the refined particle size distribution effectively accelerates the dispersion rate of the particles in the combustible matrix, facilitating the rapid formation of a protective barrier layer on the combustion interface. This provides critical physical support for the optimization of flame retardant performance and fully demonstrates the practical application value of particle size regulation.

Next, the chemical structures of the base material ADP and two modified flame retardants were characterized and analyzed. Figure shows the FT-IR curves of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni. The infrared spectra of the two modified materials exhibit similarity, which can be attributed to their shared primary constituent (ADP), and the structural consistency of their coordinating ligands and host frameworks. Absorption peaks near 3141 cm–1 in all three samples are related to H2O in the materials, while the peaks at 1638 cm–1 and 1404 cm–1 are characteristic of ADP. Compared to ADP, the peaks at these characteristic positions are weaker in ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni due to the phytic acid salt coating on the base material ADP, which reduces its sensitivity under infrared testing. Additionally, the absorption peaks at 1292 cm–1 and 1103 cm–1 in ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni are associated with the P–O–Mn and P–O–Ni bonds formed after complexation reactions with the ligands in the phytic acid salt, whereas the vibrational absorption band at 942 cm–1 can be attributed to the stretching vibration of the PO3 2– bond present in the phytic acid ligand. These three peaks are not observed in the ADP sample. By comparing and analyzing the characteristic functional groups observed in the three samples, it can be preliminarily concluded that the modified phytic acid salt has been successfully loaded onto the target base material ADP.

FT-IR spectra of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni.

Further study of the material structure of the three samples is shown in Figure , which presents the XRD spectral images of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni. The base material ADP used in the experiment shows distinct and sharp absorption peaks, indicating its high crystallinity and regular molecular chain arrangement. By integrating the characteristic peaks, the XRD spectrum of the modified flame-retardant ADP-P@Ni showed that the peak intensity decreased by approximately 50%, but the position of the absorption peak remains essentially unchanged, suggesting that the coating of phytate reduces its crystallinity. Although low crystallinity affects the stability of intermolecular arrangements and thermal stability, in flame-retardant applications, lower thermal stability allows for rapid evaporation into the gas phase to exert flame inhibition effects. The ADP-P@Mn sample did not show any obvious diffraction peaks. It presented only a broad and diffuse envelope peak within the wide-angle range, indicating an amorphous structure. Based on the XPS and FTIR analysis results, it can be concluded that Mn, P, O, and C and other characteristic signals have been detected in ADP-P@Mn, proving the completeness of the elemental composition. However, the original crystal structure of ADP has completely disappeared, indicating that the product is not a simple physical mixture of ADP and phytic acid metal complexes. Instead, during the interface assembly and heat treatment processes, significant chemical interactions and structural reconstructions occurred between ADP and phytic acid manganese complexes, forming a new amorphous coordination compound. The disappearance of the crystalline structure is due to the disruption of the original lattice-ordered arrangement of ADP by the coordination reaction, which then transforms into a long-range disordered amorphous phase.

XRD patterns of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni.

When the flame acts on the flame retardant, the PA framework undergoes dehydration and dephosphorylation reactions, generating active phosphate free radicals. These radicals cross-link with the hydroxyl groups of cellulose and the phosphate ammonium groups of ADP, promoting the formation of a six-membered carbon ring in the mixture and inhibiting its thermal decomposition into small-molecule hydrocarbons. These carbon rings further aggregate to form continuous, graphite-like, high-thermal-stability carbon layers, physically blocking the diffusion of oxygen and the transfer of heat, thereby enhancing the flame retardancy of cellulose paper.

To investigate the elemental composition and content distribution of the samples, XPS characterization was conducted. As shown in Figure a, the XPS spectra of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni display a prominent P 2p characteristic peak at 132.4 eV, attributed to the phosphorus-based flame retardant nature of the base material ADP and the phosphorus-rich ligands in the modified materials. Additionally, due to the different transition metal elements selected in the complexes of the modified materials, the two flame retardant samples, ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni, show characteristic peaks of Mn 2p at 642.1 eV and Ni 2p at 839.2 eV, respectively. To further confirm the content of effective flame-retardant components in the flame retardant samples, the XPS data were deconvoluted and calculated using Advantage software for semiquantitative elemental analysis. As illustrated in Figure b, there is a limited difference in phosphorus content among the three samples, but ADP-P@Ni has an advantage in the metal content.

(a) XPS survey spectra and (b) elemental content of the ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni.

To further analyze the elemental valence states of the effective flame-retardant components in ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni, peak fitting was performed on the characteristic peaks of C 1s, P 2p, Mn 2p, and Ni 2p using Advantage, and the results are shown in Figure . In Figure a, the high-resolution spectrum of C 1s in ADP-P@Mn is presented. The C 1s peaks appear at binding energies of 284.26, 292.48, and 295.18 eV, respectively. The peak at 284.26 eV corresponds to the standard C–C bond, while the peaks at 292.48 and 295.18 eV are attributed to adsorbates. Considering the preparation process of ADP-P@Mn, this can be explained by the adsorption of phytic salt on the surface of ADP and its subsequent reaction. The interaction between the carbon atoms in the phytic salt and the sample shifts the binding energy toward higher values. This phenomenon is also observed in the C 1s characteristic peak of ADP-P@Ni, indicating the successful bridging between the base material ADP and the modified phytic salt. As shown in Figure b, the peak at 133.58 eV corresponds to standard P 2p. For Mn 2p and Ni 2p in Figure c and d, due to their limited content in the sample, the original peaks exhibit some fluctuations. Among them, the binding energy of the 2p3/2 orbital electrons of Ni is inherently significantly higher than that of common elements such as Mn, P, and C. Moreover, nickel has a higher nuclear charge and a stronger binding force on the extranuclear electrons; therefore, its characteristic peak is naturally located at a higher binding energy position. At the same time, after Ni2+ coordinates with phytic acid and phosphate roots to form P–O–Ni bonds, the electron cloud density around nickel decreases, which further causes a slight shift of the Ni 2p3/2 peak toward higher binding energy. This also indicates that a coordination reaction has occurred between the transition metal and oxygen- and phosphorus-containing ligands.

XPS survey spectra of (a) C 1s, (b) P 2p, (c) Mn 2p for ADP-P@Mn, and (d) Ni 2p for ADP-P@Ni.

As shown in Figure , under the test conditions of 800 °C, the char yield of pristine ADP was only 20.77%, with a P/C atomic ratio of 0.36 and an initial decomposition temperature T 0 (defined as the temperature at 5% mass loss) of 215 °C. During thermal decomposition, most components of pristine ADP decomposed and volatilized, making it difficult to form a continuous and stable solid-phase char layer. For the modified ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni samples, the char yield increased to 62.53% and 29.95%, respectively; their P/C atomic ratios were 0.13 and 0.43, and the initial decomposition temperatures T 0 were 244 and 198 °C, respectively. The two modified samples exhibited significant differences in char yield, which directly determined the disparity in their condensed-phase flame-retardant efficiency. This finding serves as the core evidence for the structure–performance relationship elucidated in this study.

Based on the high-temperature thermal decomposition laws of transition metal phosphides and the previous structural characterization results, the core essence of the difference in residual carbon between the two is due to the different types of metal ions, which cause differences in the formation and stability of high-temperature phases rather than a simple difference in surface loading amounts: In the high-temperature thermal decomposition process of ADP-P@Mn, Mn2+ in the system undergoes in situ solid-phase reactions with the phosphate groups in the raw materials and the phosphorus–oxygen active groups generated by phytate degradation, gradually forming highly stable phosphate manganese and pyrophosphate manganese dense ceramic phases. These inorganic ceramic phases have high melting points and stable chemical properties and are not prone to decomposition and volatilization at high temperatures, which can form a continuous and complete barrier layer on the material surface. It can not only prevent the transfer of heat and oxygen and inhibit further thermal decomposition of the internal substrate but also firmly fix the solid-phase carbon layer, minimizing the volatilization loss of the thermal decomposition products. Therefore, it shows an ultrahigh residual carbon rate far exceeding that of the original ADP.

In the ADP-P@Ni system, the phosphate nickel and pyrophosphate nickel phases generated by the reaction of Ni2+ with the phosphorus source have much lower high-temperature thermal stability than the Mn-based phosphate salts. They will partially decompose and lose weight above 600 °C, and they cannot form a continuous and dense ceramic phase barrier layer. The solid-phase residual carbon fixation effect is extremely poor; therefore, the residual carbon rate is only slightly higher than that of the original ADP, and the gap is significant compared to ADP-P@Mn. This analysis is based on the overall thermal decomposition behavior and high-temperature phase evolution laws of TGA, avoiding the limitations of surface characterization, objectively revealing the regulatory mechanism of metal ion types on the thermal decomposition behavior and carbonization performance of the composite material, ensuring that the discussion is rigorous, in line with the actual thermal decomposition process, and forms a complete correspondence with the coordination structure and phase analysis content in the previous text.

Flame Retardant Performance

ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni were impregnated onto cellulose paper, with flame-retardant performance quantified via the downward fire spread rate (DFSR)a standardized assay for cellulosic material fire resistance. , The DFSR is calculated by Vk=UkUcel where U k is the flame propagation rate of the specimen adsorbed by the flame retardant k after drying. U cel represents the flame propagation rate of the paper sample after it has been soaked in pure water and dried, V k is a dimensionless quantity. The experiment utilized a customized downward flame propagation testing system, which included a vertical sample holder (dimensions: 150 mm × 50 mm × 0.1 mm) and a 1.5 A electric ignition device. The experiment was conducted in a cylindrical glass container with upper and lower openings. The points where the flame spread to the paper sample were 10 mm from the front edge and 10 mm from the rear edge, respectively, serving as the starting and ending points for the time calculation.

Cellulose paper with a uniform thickness of 0.12 mm was sourced from Hangzhou Xinhua Paper Co., Ltd. As shown in Figure , the experimental procedure is as follows: The paper was cut into strips measuring 140 mm by 15 mm and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h to remove all water. After drying, the strips were weighed (W s ). The dried strips were then soaked in deionized water solutions containing dissolved retardants. By controlling the soaking time, different concentrations of retardant adsorption on the paper strips were achieved. The retardant concentration (C k ) absorbed per unit weight of the sample can be calculated using the following formula: Ck=(Ws−W0M)/W0 where M represents the molar mass of the retardants.

Procedures for preparing sheet samples adsorbed with ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni.

Figure shows the trend of the normalized flame propagation parameters Vk of ADP, ADP-P@Mn, and ADP-P@Ni under different load concentrations (the vertical coordinate Vk is a dimensionless quantity without units; the coordinate axis is broken, and the interval from 0.1 to 0.7 without data is omitted). The size of Vk represents the relative flame retardancy efficiency. The smaller the value, the slower the flame spread and the better the flame retardancy performance. When Vk = 0, the sample can extinguish itself during the combustion process, achieving an efficient flame retardant state. Each group of samples was subjected to 20 experiments at different additive concentrations, plotted as points, and linearly fitted (R 2 values were 0.67, 0.82, and 0.93, respectively) to determine the trend of flame retardant performance varying with concentration.

DFSR of ADP-P@Mn, ADP-P@Ni, and ADP.

Both ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni exhibited better flame retardant effects than ADP in both low- and high-concentration regions, as their v values were consistently lower than that of ADP. It is noteworthy that ADP-P@Mn outperformed ADP-P@Ni and achieved self-extinguishment at lower concentrations. The intercepts of the fitting curves of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni were significantly lower than those of the original ADP, and their slopes were similar, indicating that the trends of their flame retardant performance with concentration were consistent with that of ADP, but the flame retardant efficiency significantly improved at the same load quantity. The TG results in Figure show that the thermal decomposition trends of the two samples are similar to that of ADP, but the residual carbon content of ADP-P@Mn is the highest (62.53 wt %), followed by ADP-P@Ni (29.95 wt %), and the pure ADP has the lowest (20.77 wt %). A higher residual carbon content means that a dense and continuous carbon layer can be formed more quickly during combustion, playing a stronger role in heat and oxygen insulation in the condensed phase. Meanwhile, the thermal decomposition of ADP-P@Mn is more moderate and slower, which is conducive to the continuous release of phosphorus-containing free radicals and inert gases in the early stage of combustion, enhancing the flame retardant effect in the gas phase. ADP-P@Ni is between the two, while pure ADP has the weakest effect in both the gas and condensed phases. These quantitative TG results are completely consistent with the flame retardant performance ranking obtained from the DFSR test, ADP-P@Mn > ADP-P@Ni > ADP, jointly confirming the rationality of this flame retardant performance sequence from both the condensed phase carbon formation and gas phase inhibition aspects.

In Figure a, when pure cellulose burns, the flame appears bright yellow with strong heat, flickering, and rising characteristics. When observed from the front and side, the flame structure appears relatively intact. Compared with pure cellulose, the flame structures of samples containing ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni are significantly more contracted. Moreover, In Figure b and c, the brightness of the flame significantly decreases, appearing pink when observed from the front and blue when observed from the side. Therefore, based on the flame structure and color, it can be preliminarily determined that the addition of the flame retardant will affect the normal propagation of the flame on the cellulose fuel. The ignition time of pure cellulose paper is 58.54 s, and the entire sample is burned completely. The ignition time of the sample with pure ADP is 67.46 s, and the entire sample is burned completely. The ignition time of the ADP-P@Ni sample is 28.10 s, and it extinguishes automatically after burning only one-fourth of the sample. The ignition time of the ADP-P@Mn modified sample is 29.01 s, and it quickly extinguishes after burning one-fifth of the sample. It confirms that the modified flame retardant can effectively enhance the ignition resistance of paper and significantly delay the ignition time.

Instantaneous flame morphology in cases of (a) pure cellulose paper, (b) cellulose paper with ADP-P@Mn, (c) with ADP-P@Ni, and (d) with ADP.

The flame-retardant performance of cellulose-based materials is directly reflected by combustion flame behavior, which is primarily governed by the inhibitory effect of flame retardants on the combustion process. During thermal decomposition, gaseous products generated by the flame retardant scavenge reactive free radicals critical to combustion chain reactions, leading to incomplete combustion and reduced combustion temperature. As illustrated in Figure b, the sample incorporating ADP-P@Mn exhibited pronounced flame inhibition, with the flame self-extinguishing within 29 s of ignition. Specifically, upon ignition, the flame-retardant-modified sample underwent a mild combustion phase, where flame propagation was confined to a narrow downward region and subsequently suppressed from further spreading by the flame retardant. In contrast, as shown in Figure d, cellulose treated with pure ADP exhibited a marginally longer flame spread time than pristine cellulose, accompanied by a lower flame height and dim flame luminescence (dark red in the frontal view and pale blue in the lateral view). Combustion-derived heat is localized to the burning zone, inducing high-velocity motion of ambient air moleculesa phenomenon closely correlated with flame temperature and luminescence intensity. Collectively, the flame luminescence intensity and propagation duration confirm that the flame-retardant efficacy of pure ADP is inferior to those of modified ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni composites.

The reduction in peak flame radiation temperature is a critical indicator for evaluating the performance of flame retardants. Figure shows the temperature change trends at three monitoring points (20 mm from the starting point of the paper sample, in the middle of the sample, and 20 mm from the tail of the sample) on the paper samples. A K-type thermocouple with a diameter of 0.5 mm is adopted, and the response time is ≤0.1 s. It should be emphasized that to prevent the thermocouple from directly contacting the sample surface and thereby affecting the flame propagation speed, the contact point of the thermocouple was kept 1 cm away from the samples, so the measurement result is the radiant temperature, which is lower than the flame temperature. The radiant temperature can still be used to compare the differences in the cooling effects of various flame retardants. The highest flame temperature observed during the combustion of pure cellulose was 668.2 °C (as shown in Figure a), which is the maximum among all samples. In contrast, the flame temperatures of cellulose samples containing ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni were significantly reduced, with temperatures at the first monitoring point dropping to 228.3 and 324 °C, respectively (as seen in Figure b and c). Due to the self-extinguishing nature of the flame upon reaching the first monitoring point, attributed to the action of the flame retardants, no temperature data were recorded for the second and third monitoring points. In conjunction with the visual observations of flame morphology and brightness depicted in Figure , a consistent conclusion regarding the ranking of flame-retardant effectiveness can be drawn: ADP-P@Mn outperforms ADP-P@Ni, which, in turn, exceeds ADP.

Application Performance

The flatness and writing performance of flame-retardant cellulose paper were evaluated. As shown in Figure b and c, the samples treated with ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni, respectively, exhibited almost no obvious morphological changes, and the surface flatness was well maintained, which is attributed to the ultralow loading of the flame retardant. Notably, qualitative handwriting tests were carried out to characterize the writing performance, and the results showed that the writing traces on the modified substrates were clear and smooth, without ink breakage or paper surface fluffing. The modified paper retained favorable basic writing performance, meeting the daily basic writing requirements with no significant deterioration in writing smoothness and clarity compared with the untreated original paper.

Flatness and writing performance of (a) raw paper, (b) ADP-P@Mn-treated paper, and (c) ADP-P@Ni-treated paper.

The practical flame retardancy and mechanical properties of the paper samples were characterized using the Limiting Oxygen Index (LOI) and Tensile Index, respectively. As shown in Figure a, samples with a low adsorption concentration and a high adsorption concentration were selected for evaluation: L-ADP, L-ADP-P@Mn, L-ADP-P@Ni (low concentration group, at 15.36 × 10–5, 15.31 × 10–5, and 15.91 × 10–5 mol/g, respectively); as well as H-ADP, H-ADP-P@Mn, H-ADP-P@Ni (high concentration group, at 41.62 × 10–5, 41.01 × 10–5, and 40.78 × 10–5 mol/g, respectively). Each LOI test was conducted in accordance with the standard testing specifications. The test was repeated 5 times, and the average value was taken. The errors were marked in Figure a. The Tensile Index is a primary indicator for evaluating the mechanical strength. Although all groups experienced a decline in tensile performance with increasing flame retardant concentration, the Tensile Index of all samples remained above 53 N·m/g, meeting the requirement of the standard GB/T30130-2013 that the tensile index of paper should be greater than 45.0 N·m/g. In conclusion, these two innovative flame retardants not only possess excellent practical flame retardancy but also have a minimal impact on tensile strength. Additionally, vertical burning tests indicated that composite materials H-ADP-P@Mn and H-ADP-P@Ni achieved the highest flame retardancy rating of V0, while all other samples obtained a V1 rating. In Figure a, a systematic comparison was conducted between the concentration of flame retardants and LOI, UL-94, and mechanical strength. The results showed that LOI significantly increased with an increase in concentration. At high concentrations, both ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni could reach the UL-94 V-0 grade. Although the tensile strength of the paper slightly decreased with the increase in concentration, considering the comprehensive flame retardant efficiency, self-extinguishing property, mechanical properties, and practical characteristics, this system could achieve the best balance of flame retardant performance and mechanical properties within the concentration range of 15 × 10–5 to 25 × 10–5 mol/g.

To assess the durability of flame retardants in humid environments, the flame-retardant paper was subjected to a 7-day exposure at 25 °C and 80% relative humidity (this condition is represented as H), followed by retesting under identical conditions using the DFSR method. The resulting samples were designated as ADP-P@Mn­(H), ADP-P@Ni­(H), and ADP­(H). As illustrated in Figure b, after exposure to 80% relative humidity at 25 °C for 7 days, all samples exhibited a slight decrease in the DFSR values. This minor reduction should be attributed to physical heat absorption and gas dilution effects from trace adsorbed moisture during combustion, rather than an intrinsic enhancement of flame-retardant activity. The moisture uptake values of all samples were below 3%, indicating negligible hygroscopicity. Such a low moisture uptake did not cause cellulose plasticization or obvious changes at three different points (at 1 cm from the starting point, at the midpoint, and 1 cm away from the ending point) in the dispersion state of flame retardants, confirming the excellent hydrolytic stability and durable flame-retardant performance of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni in high-humidity environments. The moisture uptake of all samples after 7 days was less than 3%, ensuring no obvious plasticization or redistribution of flame retardants occurred. Furthermore, the results in Figure can further illustrate that the interface bridging structure formed by PA-Mn/PA-Ni and ADP will reduce the crystallinity of ADP, enhance the formation of the condensed-phase carbon layer and the integrity of the carbon layer protection, and thereby, compared to the paper treated solely with pure ADP, it can increase the downward flame propagation rate and humidity stability of the cellulose paper.

Notably, the printing performance of the ADP-P@Mn/ADP-P@Ni-modified cellulose paper, a critical metric for its commercial application, was not evaluated in this work and is suggested as a valuable research direction. Future studies will focus on the systematic characterization of printing performance (e.g., ink absorption, print resolution, ink adhesion) of the flame-retardant paper and further optimize the flame-retardant modification process to achieve a better balance between flame-retardant efficacy and printing adaptability, thus enhancing the practical commercial application value of the prepared flame-retardant cellulose paper.

Flame Retardant Mechanism

The flame-retardant mechanisms of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni were systematically explored via microscopic morphology, chemical structure, and elemental composition analyses of residual char, combined with preceding thermal and combustion characterization data.

The scanning electron microscope images of the carbon residues from ADP-P@Mn- and ADP-P@Ni-modified cellulose paper (Figure ) show significant morphological differences compared with the original and unmodified samples, mainly due to the core role of ADP in both flame retardants. The ADP-based modifiers promoted a uniform distribution of residual carbon, forming continuous carbon layers covering carbon sheets with a diameter of approximately 2 μm, which acted as a physical barrier, reducing oxygen diffusion and heat transfer, thereby lowering the DFSR of ADP-P@Mn, while the carbon yield in ADP-P@Ni was lower and the phosphate residues were discontinuous. Regarding the issue of low phosphorus content in ADP-P@Ni carbon, quantitative XPS analysis confirmed that the initial PA loading in ADP-P@Mn (P/C = 0.13) and ADP-P@Ni (P/C = 0.43) was comparable, ruling out the possibility of insufficient PA loading. The outstanding performance of ADP-P@Mn is attributed to the condensed phase. During the flame heating process, ADP and its coordinated PA decompose into viscous phosphorus-containing compounds (such as phosphoric acid and hypophosphoric acid), which act as physical barriers to prevent heat transfer and the diffusion of oxidants, while promoting uniform carbon formation and generating a dense crack-like carbon morphology, as shown in Figure . After the flame retardant effect, the residual components aggregated as fine particles of approximately 1 μm on the carbon surface; these phosphorus-rich residues uniformly covered the combustion substrate and exerted typical condensed phase flame retardant effects.

SEM images of carbon residues containing (a) ADP-P@Mn and (b) ADP-P@Ni.

To further elucidate the condensed-phase mechanism, FTIR and XPS were utilized to characterize the chemical structure and elemental composition of flame-retardant char residues. As presented in Figure a, FTIR spectra of ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni show similar profiles, with distinct absorption peaks at 3441, 1619, and 1263 cm–1. The peak at 3441 cm–1 corresponds to adsorbed H2O, while those at 1619 cm–1 and 1263 cm–1 are assigned to CO and C–O bonds, respectively, which reflect the carbonization degree of the insulating char layer. ADP-P@Mn exhibits stronger CO and C–O bond absorption intensities than ADP-P@Ni; combined with earlier TG thermal stability results, this confirms a higher degree of char carbonization and stronger condensed-phase flame-retardant efficiency for ADP-P@Mn.

(a) FTIR and (b) XPS curves for the carbon residue.

XPS elemental distribution analysis of char residues is displayed in Figure b. Characteristic peaks of Mn 2p, O 1s, C 1s, and P 2p are detected at 642.28, 532.27, 284.89, and 134.29 eV for ADP-P@Mn, while only the peaks of O 1s (531.39 eV) and C 1s (284.04 eV) appear for ADP-P@Ni, with no obvious P 2p or Ni 2p signals. Advantage software-based peak fitting and semiquantitative analysis show that the total C and O content in ADP-P@Mn char reaches 97.45%, far higher than 77.71% for ADP-P@Ni. This indicates more sufficient carbonization of cellulosic paper induced by ADP-P@Mn, explaining its superior flame-retardant performance in the DFSR test. Furthermore, it can also be explained that Ni ions are stably bound in the phosphate–carbon cross-linked network rather than existing as free ions, and no obvious Ni leakage was observed under our experimental conditions and typical usage environments; these results confirm good biosafety and stability for practical applications.

The ADP-P@Mn carbon residue with better char-forming properties was selected for detailed XPS spectrum analysis, specifically at the C 1s and P 2p positions, to determine the characteristic chemical bonds that affect the condensed-phase flame retardancy, as shown in Figure . Specifically, the C 1s peaks at 284.28, 285.05, and 286.28 electron volts correspond to C–C, C–O/P–O, and CO bonds, indicating the formation of a distinct carbon layer. Additionally, the P 2p peaks at 133.48, 134.18, and 134.98 eV correspond to P–C, P–O, and PO bonds. The formation of these bonds helps to form an insulating carbon layer. The homogeneous carbon flake layers and high char yield of ADP-P@Mn lead to a substantial reduction in DFSR and a notable increase in LOI, while the discontinuous phosphoric acid residue and low char yield of ADP-P@Ni result in only moderate improvements in both DFSR and LOI. This confirms that the char layer uniformity and yield are the dominant factors determining DFSR/LOI performance.

Besides the condensed-phase mechanism, the gas-phase pathway provides a critical synergistic effect. Transition metal components act mainly in the condensed phase due to low volatility, while ADP and PA dominate the gas-phase activity. During thermal degradation, phosphorus compounds release radical scavengers including •PO, •PO2, and HPO2, which efficiently quench highly reactive combustion chain radicals (e.g., H•, OH•). Meanwhile, ADP pyrolysis generates massive amounts of inert gases (NH3, H2O vapor) that dilute flammable volatiles and reduce oxygen concentration at the flame front. This coupled gas-phase and condensed-phase effect significantly boosts the overall flame-retardant performance of modified cellulosic paper.

Furthermore, Mn2+ acts as a more effective catalyst for cellulose dehydration and aromatization during thermal degradation, promoting the formation of stable, graphitized carbon ring structures; simultaneously, Mn forms thermally stable phosphate complexes that anchor phosphorus in the condensed-phase char matrix, enhancing the char yield. In contrast, Ni2+ forms less thermally stable phosphates, leading to rapid phosphorus volatilization into the gas phase instead of retention of P in the char. While Ni phosphates may exhibit higher gas-phase radical-scavenging efficacy, their poor condensed-phase stability results in discontinuous 1 μm phosphoric acid residue layers, insufficient char yield, and thus inferior flame-retardant performance compared to Mn-based composites. This clarifies that Mn’s superiority stems from its dual role in catalyzing condensed-phase carbonization and stabilizing phosphorus in char, whereas Ni’s less stable phosphates prioritize gas-phase phosphorus release over condensed-phase char formation.

Conclusions

In this study, modified ADP with PA coordination was successfully synthesized and immobilized onto cellulose paper via an impregnation strategy to fabricate flame-retardant cellulose paper. Compared with our previously reported PA–metal composite systems, , this novel flame-retardant system exhibits enhanced flame-retardant efficiency and reduced preparation costs owing to ADP serving as the core functional component. The pristine ADP particles featured a smooth surface with a rectangular morphology, whereas surface roughening was observed after functionalization with metal phytate complexes. Furthermore, the formation of P–O–Mn and P–O–Ni covalent linkages facilitated the immobilization of effective flame-retardant moieties within the ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni formulations, endowing these modified flame retardants with distinct advantages in phosphorus and transition metal content over unmodified ADP. Specifically, the char residues of ADP-P@Mn (62.53 wt %) and ADP-P@Ni (29.95 wt %) were significantly higher than that of pristine ADP (20.77 wt %). Beyond charring capability, ADP-P@Mn and ADP-P@Ni exhibited superior flame-retardant performance (evaluated by downward flame spread rate (DFSR) and limiting oxygen index (LOI)) across all concentration ranges compared with unmodified ADP, and they notably achieved self-extinguishing behavior even at low additive loadings. Additionally, the resultant flame-retardant cellulose paper maintained excellent writing usability, tensile strength, and humidity resistance, demonstrating a balanced functional performance and practical applicability.
