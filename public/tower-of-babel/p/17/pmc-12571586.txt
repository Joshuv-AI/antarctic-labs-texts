The Emotion and Symptom‐Focused Engagement (EASE) Psychotherapeutic Intervention for Parents of Children With Acute Leukemia: A Feasibility Trial
Authors: Lindsay A. Jibb, Ally Yu, Stephanie Nanos, Carmine Malfitano, Laura Foran, Kate Hunt, Argin Malakian, Camilla Zimmermann, Katharina Schultebraucks, Nhu‐An Pham, Anne Rydall, Sarah Alexander, Gary Rodin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundParents of children newly diagnosed with acute leukemia (AL) experience significant traumatic stress that adversely affects their well‐being, caregiving capacity, and family functioning. However, rigorous evidence of the feasibility and effectiveness of psychosocial interventions for this group remains limited.AimsTo evaluate the study feasibility and acceptability of Emotion And Symptom‐focused Engagement (EASE), a brief, manualized psychotherapeutic intervention designed to mitigate traumatic stress symptoms in individuals facing a life‐threatening illness or in their close family member.MethodsThis single‐arm, pre‐post feasibility study recruited parents of children with AL who participated in EASE sessions delivered by trained clinicians. Measures of traumatic stress, depressive symptoms, and other psychosocial outcomes were obtained at baseline, 3‐ and 6‐months. Feasibility was assessed based on enrollment, attrition, adherence to EASE, and questionnaire completion rates, with predefined criteria guiding trial progression. Acceptability was evaluated via thematic analysis of qualitative interviews. Descriptive statistics summarized outcome data.ResultsOf 59 parents approached, 40 (68%) enrolled. The study attrition rate was 15%. Participants attended six EASE sessions on average, with 88% receiving the minimum dose of three sessions. Outcome measures were completed by ≥ 78% of participants at each timepoint. Qualitative findings showed that EASE was well‐received and provided emotional relief and enhanced coping to parents. Virtual intervention delivery and flexible scheduling facilitated engagement.ConclusionEASE is a feasible and acceptable intervention for parents of children with newly diagnosed AL, addressing critical caregiver support needs. Randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate its efficacy in reducing traumatic stress and improving caregiver well‐being.Trial registrationNCT05236296 ClinicalTrials.gov.

Background

Acute leukemia (AL) is the most common childhood cancer, presenting as a life‐threatening condition that requires urgent, intensive treatment in the first months and maintenance treatment for 2–3 years subsequently. Parents and other family caregivers of children with AL typically face a substantial burden of caregiving, financial toxicity, uncertainty about their child's survival and treatment effects, and vicarious trauma from witnessing their child's suffering [1, 2]. Traumatic stress symptoms (TSS) are common in this group, with as many as half of such parents reporting TSS that are compatible with the diagnosis of acute stress disorder (ASD) or subsequent post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [3, 4]. TSS of this severity have been linked in other populations to a ten‐fold increase in suicide risk, and elevated rates of cardiovascular, metabolic, and musculoskeletal disorders, and all‐cause mortality [5, 6, 7]. Research has identified key predictors of parental stress, including child symptom severity, financial strain, and limited social, spousal or professional support [8, 9, 10]. Structured and semi‐structured professional support and caregiver education, could play a critical role in mitigating stress and improving coping outcomes [11, 12, 13], but research is needed to demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of such interventions.

TSS in parents of children with cancer are associated with a reduction of caregiving capacity, quality of life (QOL), and satisfaction with healthcare, and with increased distress in their child and spouse [14, 15]. In recognition of these effects, guidelines for pediatric cancer treatment recommend routine psychosocial assessment and care for parents of children with cancer [16]. A number of psychosocial interventions have been developed for this population, with varying levels of evidence for their effectiveness. The most well‐established of these is the Bright IDEAS problem‐solving intervention, for which efficacy in reducing distress and negative affectivity among mothers of children newly diagnosed with cancer has been demonstrated in multiple large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [17, 18, 19, 20]. Other approaches, including cognitive–behavioral, supportive–expressive, and skills‐based interventions, have also been trialed, though results have been mixed and studies are limited in their theoretical grounding, intensity (e.g., single‐session approaches), and accessibility (e.g., in‐person only) [21]. These findings underscore the potential benefits of psychosocial interventions for this population and the need for further research to develop and evaluate interventions that are feasible, effective, and scalable.

Our team developed Emotion And Symptom‐focused Engagement (EASE), a brief, psychotherapeutic intervention designed to alleviate traumatic stress and improve well‐being in individuals facing a life‐threatening cancer and in their family caregivers [22]. Drawing upon relational and attachment theory, EASE is a supportive‐expressive intervention that is present‐focused and intended to provide relational support and to enhance attachment security and affect regulation. We demonstrated its feasibility and acceptability in a phase II RCT with adult patients with AL and found preliminary evidence for its positive effects on TSS, depression, QOL, and satisfaction with care after 4 weeks of EASE and at the 12‐week follow‐up [22].

Given the high prevalence and adverse effects of TSS in the parents of children newly diagnosed with AL and the lack of evidence for the benefit of targeted psychological interventions, we aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of EASE in this population. The results of this study will inform a subsequent large randomized clinical trial of EASE.

Methods

Design, Setting, and Participants

This study utilized a single‐arm, pre‐post study design. Participants were recruited from an ongoing longitudinal study investigating the prevalence and severity of TSS in caregivers of children with AL [23]. All participants first provided informed consent for the longitudinal study, with a participation rate of 80%. Those who met eligibility criteria were subsequently approached and invited to participate in the intervention trial through a separate consent process. Recruitment took place at the Hospital for Sick Children (Toronto, Canada). The trained therapists who delivered the EASE intervention were based at the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre (Toronto, Canada).

Eligible parents met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) sufficient English proficiency to complete study tasks, and (3) self‐identified as the primary caregiver (i.e., person assuming most caregiving responsibilities) for a child diagnosed with AL within the past 6 months and receiving treatment with curative intent. Parents were excluded if they were currently receiving formal psychotherapy.

The target sample size of 40 participants was determined based on established criteria for feasibility studies [24], which recommend a sample size large enough to evaluate feasibility metrics while remaining pragmatic for pilot testing. The minimum sample size for the qualitative study component was based on our previous research with parents of children with cancer [25, 26], in which at least 20 participants were required to reach data saturation.

EASE Intervention

EASE is a semi‐structured, manualized intervention designed to mitigate TSS in individuals affected by life‐threatening illnesses. EASE is a present‐focused supportive‐expressive therapy that provides relational support and is intended to enhance attachment security and affect regulation. EASE sessions are delivered by trained mental health clinicians including social workers and advanced practice nurses via secure videoconferencing, telephone, or in person at the hospital, depending on participant preference. This intervention is comprised of up to 12 sessions over three months, with each session lasting 30–60 min. Parents could attend sessions individually or with a co‐caregiver. The frequency, duration, and timing of sessions were tailored to the participant's clinical needs and scheduling constraints.

Procedure

Recruitment began following approval from the Ontario Cancer Research Ethics Board (Project ID: 2401). As part of the larger longitudinal study, parents completed self‐report measures at 2 weeks, and at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the diagnosis of AL in their child. In alignment with the longitudinal study's assessment schedule, each parent's baseline was defined as the outcome assessment occurring closest in time to their first EASE session and the follow‐up assessments were at 3‐ and 6‐months following this baseline.

Following the end of study involvement, participants were invited to take part in qualitative interviews, and all who agreed were subsequently interviewed. Interviews were conducted by a trained medical sciences graduate student with lived experience as a childhood leukemia patient or by a trained researcher and healthcare provider. Interviews lasted 30–60 min, were video/audio recorded, and transcribed verbatim.

Measures

The REDCap‐based electronically‐administered questionnaire package, was informed by those administered in our previous EASE pilot in adult patients with AL [22] and was comprised of outcome measures assessing: (1) PTSD symptoms, using the PTSD Checklist for DSM‐5 (PCL‐5), a 20‐item measure, with a scale range of 0–80 and clinical cut‐point of 31 [27]; (2) ASD symptoms using the Stanford Acute Stress Reaction Questionnaire (SASRQ), a 30‐item measure with a scale range of 0–150 and clinical cut‐point of 40 [28]; (3) depressive symptoms using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9), a 9‐item measure with a scale range of 0–27 and clinical cut‐point of 10 [29]; (4) caregiver burden using the 20‐item version of the Caregiver Reaction Assessment (CRA) scale, which measures caregiver perceptions of the impact of caregiving on their health and schedule and finances, a lack of family support, and their own caregiver self‐esteem (the last being the only measured positive reaction to caregiving) [30, 31]; (5) caregiver perceived social support using the 7‐item ENRICHD Social Support Instrument (ESSI) [32]; and (6) caregiver satisfaction with patient care using the 10‐item FAMCARE [33]. In addition, the Experiences in Close Relationships (ECR‐M16) scale was administered at baseline to assess attachment security. The ECR‐M16 is a 16‐item measure of attachment security, with anxious and avoidant attachment subscales [34]. Subscale scores range from 8 to 56 with higher scores indicating higher attachment anxiety or avoidance. All measures have been validated for use in the context of cancer.

Study Feasibility and Intervention Applicability and Acceptability

Study feasibility data were collected using secure digital logs. EASE acceptability was evaluated through semi‐structured interviews exploring participants' experiences with EASE, caregiving, and available support.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means (M), standard deviations (SD), and 95% confidence intervals (CI), were used to summarize feasibility outcomes. To describe symptom trajectories, descriptive data from parents who reported clinically significant levels of TSS or depressive symptoms at baseline and those who did not are presented separately. Qualitative data from interviews were managed using NVivo 14.0 software. A reflexive thematic analysis [35] was used to analyze these data, which involved data familiarization, coding, theme development, and iterative refinement. The entire dataset was coded twice in different interview sequences to enhance rigor. Preliminary themes were developed and reviewed collaboratively (AY, LJ, GR) during frequent team meetings.

Criteria for Feasibility

Predefined feasibility criteria were based on similar feasibility studies with pediatric cancer patients [36] and our successful EASE pilot trial in adult patients with AL [22]. The criteria for this trial were: (1) > 60% of approached parents agree to participate; (2) ≥ 40 parents enrolled within 18 months; (3) < 25% participant attrition; (4) ≥ 64% of participants complete ≥ 3 EASE sessions (minimum effective dose); and (5) ≥ 84% of participants complete all outcome measures at each of the baseline and follow‐up timepoints.

Parents were enrolled into the EASE pilot within a mean of 2.0 months of their child's leukemia diagnosis (SD: 1.2, range: 0.3–5.8). Two participants withdrew after completing baseline outcome measures and beginning EASE sessions, one participant withdrew before completing demographic data or baseline measures or EASE, and three participants were lost to follow‐up before completing baseline outcome measures, resulting in an overall attrition rate of 15% (6/40). Reasons given for withdrawals were caregiving burden, confidentiality concerns, and the burden of both caregiving and completing study outcome measures.

Outcome Measure Completion

Complete baseline measures were obtained from 34 (94%) of 36 participants. Complete follow‐up measures at 3‐months were available for 30 (83%) participants, and for 28 (78%) participants at the 6‐month follow‐up.

Intervention Delivery

Three trained therapists conducted 209 EASE sessions for a total of 185 clinical hours. Participants completed a mean of six sessions (SD: 3, range: 0–15), with 88% (n = 35) attending three or more sessions. Sessions lasted a mean of 53 min (SD: 12, range: 6–90) and occurred over a mean span of 3 months (SD: 1, range: 1–6). When EASE sessions were completed in less than the expected 3‐month period, this was because: participants discontinued the intervention due to caregiving responsibilities (n = 2), scheduling permitted parents to complete sessions more quickly than anticipated (n = 6), or unknown reason (n = 3). When sessions extended beyond three months, and in one case beyond 12 sessions, this was to allow for greater flexibility in scheduling or reflected mutual agreement between the therapist and parent that additional sessions were warranted to address ongoing parent needs. All participants engaged in sessions virtually, with 92% of sessions being held online and 8% conducted via telephone. Five parents (13%) attended at least one session with their spouse, who also was the child's parent. No adverse effects were reported.

Intervention Acceptability

A total of 25 interviews were conducted with participants, one of whom withdrew from the study before their first EASE session to focus their attention on their child. The demographic characteristics of interviewed parents are shown in Table 1. Three themes were derived from the qualitative analysis of the interview data, which describe parent‐identified need for support and the acceptability of EASE.

The Impact of the Child's Disease and Treatment and Perceived Support Needs

Constantly on Call

Parents consistently emphasized a shift in their personal focus entirely to the care of their child from the time of the child's diagnosis and indicated that they often felt guilty when considering their own needs. Parents described feeling constantly “on call,” particularly during extended hospital stays, when their child's needs took precedence over their own. While they regarded this focus as necessary, they reported that it led to neglect of their own personal health, and disruption of their sleep and social interactions. One parent shared:From the start of this [cancer journey], I’ve been approaching it with a very numb, one‐track mind—whatever I have to do for [my child] to get better or to help [my partner]. Everything revolves around the care of [my child]. So, I’m probably tired. Well, no, I am tired. I sleep a lot less.(Father, 34 years)

From the start of this [cancer journey], I’ve been approaching it with a very numb, one‐track mind—whatever I have to do for [my child] to get better or to help [my partner]. Everything revolves around the care of [my child]. So, I’m probably tired. Well, no, I am tired. I sleep a lot less.

Beyond managing their child's needs, parents struggled to balance employment, caregiving for other children, household responsibilities, and supporting their partners. Many expressed guilt about not being able to give usual attention to these roles, further compounding their stress:It's not about me. This is about my daughter. If anyone is gonna cry, [it] should be my daughter.(Mother, 35 years).

It's not about me. This is about my daughter. If anyone is gonna cry, [it] should be my daughter.

Feeling the Weight of the World

Parents described caregiving as an immense emotional and physical strain. Their thoughts were often consumed by their child's diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. However, many found it difficult to process their emotions, often tying their well‐being directly to their child's condition. One parent explained:I’m experiencing a lot of emotions that I’ve never experienced before in my life. Trying to be a pillar of support for my entire family and coping with my own very confusing emotions and mental state—it’s heavier than the weight of the world. It’s just a lot for a person to have to carry with them every single day.(Mother, 35 years)

I’m experiencing a lot of emotions that I’ve never experienced before in my life. Trying to be a pillar of support for my entire family and coping with my own very confusing emotions and mental state—it’s heavier than the weight of the world. It’s just a lot for a person to have to carry with them every single day.

Parents reported that this mental load led to cognitive difficulties such as forgetfulness, difficulty concentrating, and a sense of being perpetually distracted, which affected their ability to work, process medical information, and maintain relationships. Many also described a distressing sense of hypervigilance, constantly monitoring their child's health: I realized how close [to death] she can be, and that just frightened me. So, now I'm on edge just in terms of watching her…I don't think I'll let that up for years.(Mother, 59 years)

I realized how close [to death] she can be, and that just frightened me. So, now I'm on edge just in terms of watching her…I don't think I'll let that up for years.

Nearly every parent reported physical exhaustion, including fatigue and pain.

Alone in a Crowd

Parents frequently reported difficulties accessing personal psychosocial support. While they acknowledged receiving emotional and practical help from friends, family, and their child's clinical team, they also noted challenges in these relationships. Comments from others such as “you're so strong,” “I can't imagine what you're going through,” and “just be positive,” often left them feeling “annoyed,” “mad,” or “lonely.” Many parents felt expected to display resilience for the sake of their partners and families, making it difficult for them to share their vulnerabilities. One parent shared:As months and months go by, it's hard to be so sad with your friends and family all the time. They move on with life, life moves on.(Mother, 59 years)

As months and months go by, it's hard to be so sad with your friends and family all the time. They move on with life, life moves on.

Satisfaction With EASE

A Support Space for Parents

Parents noted that available support had been primarily geared toward their child's needs rather than their own mental and emotional well‐being. Parents felt that EASE addressed this gap, with many experiencing emotional relief from the intervention:[EASE] was the only place of support that was provided to me through all that we’re going through with [my child]. He’s got tons of support. He’s got physiotherapists, he’s got doctors, he’s got all these different people that are looking out for him. So, spending time with [EASE therapist] was the only thing that I had for me.(Mother, 46 years)

[EASE] was the only place of support that was provided to me through all that we’re going through with [my child]. He’s got tons of support. He’s got physiotherapists, he’s got doctors, he’s got all these different people that are looking out for him. So, spending time with [EASE therapist] was the only thing that I had for me.

Lightening the Emotional Load

Participants highly valued EASE because they felt that it addressed the emotional toll of caregiving and improved their overall family well‐being. These parents expressed appreciation for their therapists, describing them as a strong source of support:After a session, I just feel lighter and a little bit happier…It's like a weight has been lifted off of you.(Mother, 34 years)

After a session, I just feel lighter and a little bit happier…It's like a weight has been lifted off of you.

Parents experienced EASE as a non‐judgmental space for them to process their emotions and to reflect on their caregiving journey:There was a moment where [EASE therapist] just confirmed this is normal. These are [the feelings] to be feeling in the first 3 weeks. It added a sense of not feeling entirely alone or that we're the only people this is happening to.(Mother, 35 years)

There was a moment where [EASE therapist] just confirmed this is normal. These are [the feelings] to be feeling in the first 3 weeks. It added a sense of not feeling entirely alone or that we're the only people this is happening to.

EASE supported parents to become more self‐aware, better able to manage their anxiety, and to maintain a perspective on their life beyond caregiving. One parent described:[EASE therapist] was able to help me compartmentalize, figure out what was causing me anxiety, and separate it into different places so that it wasn't so overwhelming.(Mother, 47 years)

[EASE therapist] was able to help me compartmentalize, figure out what was causing me anxiety, and separate it into different places so that it wasn't so overwhelming.

Facilitators of EASE Engagement

Convenient Connection

Parents reported that the virtual therapy made participation more convenient, allowing them to remain engaged in EASE, despite busy schedules. One parent explained:I don't think it's reasonable to ask…a caregiver in a situation like this to leave their home to go to a meeting to talk about things. It has to be convenient, and [the virtual option] was very convenient.(Mother, 51 years)

I don't think it's reasonable to ask…a caregiver in a situation like this to leave their home to go to a meeting to talk about things. It has to be convenient, and [the virtual option] was very convenient.

Support That Fits the Schedule

Parents reported that the flexible scheduling of sessions to accommodate their schedules and the unpredictable demands of caregiving facilitated their engagement in EASE. One shared:[EASE therapist] has been very, very flexible with me and my schedule, given everything that I'm going through. He's always like, I can see you 3 days from now. …He's always been very flexible and made it easy.(Father, 42 years)

[EASE therapist] has been very, very flexible with me and my schedule, given everything that I'm going through. He's always like, I can see you 3 days from now. …He's always been very flexible and made it easy.

Proactive Parent Recruitment

Several parents emphasized that the proactive approach to psychotherapy recruitment enabled their participation in EASE:I probably would have never done it or reached out to anybody if it wasn't offered to me.(Mother, 36 years).

I probably would have never done it or reached out to anybody if it wasn't offered to me.

Discussion

The study findings suggest that EASE delivered to the parents of children with AL is both feasible and acceptable, with high recruitment and retention rates, and an overwhelmingly positive experience of participants with the intervention. There was a 68% recruitment rate and an attrition rate of only 15%, both of which surpassed pre‐defined feasibility criteria and met or exceeded those that have been reported in other psychotherapeutic interventional research [37, 38]. Recruitment of the target sample size of 40 parents required 27 months, a longer timeframe than specified within the feasibility criteria. We note that parents were recruited into this pilot from our ongoing longitudinal study of caregiver distress, which may have influenced the accrual rate relative to recruitment from non‐study contexts. Future trials should consider incorporating active outreach and flexible scheduling of recruitment contacts with broader pools of parents to enhance enrollment efficiency and achieve recruitment targets within feasible timelines.

Clinical and Research Implications

Within this pilot, a large majority of participants (88%) received the minimum EASE dose of three sessions, with a mean of six sessions completed per parent. This high level of adherence and engagement suggests that EASE is well‐suited to support parents in the distressing context of pediatric leukemia caregiving. The measurement completion rate fell short of our feasibility goal, with 77.8% of participants rather than ≥ 84% completing all measures at each timepoint. Caregiver burden may have limited the completion of all measures by some participants. Symptom trajectories among parents who received EASE are encouraging, suggesting possible improvement for those with elevated symptoms and a preventative effect for those without. However, given the small sample and single‐arm study design, these patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of intervention effectiveness and may reflect the phenomenon of regression to the mean.

Parents in this study found EASE to be highly acceptable, emphasizing its benefit in validating their experience as individuals, parents, and caregivers. The participants found EASE to provide a critical outlet for emotional expression and distress management, addressing an often‐overlooked dimension of their well‐being. They reported that they often overlooked their own well‐being to prioritize caregiving responsibilities, a pattern associated with burnout, and long‐term psychological distress [10, 39]. Parents reported that the caregiving demands that they experienced in the absence of adequate support led to emotional exhaustion, hypervigilance, and social isolation, consistent with the findings of other studies of the parents of children with cancer [40, 41]. Many parents also reported that EASE was their only structured source of emotional support during their child's treatment. Prior studies have similarly demonstrated that distress is common among parents of children with cancer and that access to mental health services is limited due to logistical barriers, stigma, or a lack of provider‐initiated referrals [42, 43].

The logistical aspects of EASE, including its virtual delivery and flexible scheduling were instrumental in facilitating sustained parent engagement in the intervention. Other research has also shown that virtual interventions improve access to mental health care for caregivers [44], including those managing chronic pediatric illnesses [45]. Further, these modes of delivery may be as effective as in‐person psychotherapy for reducing distress and depression [46, 47]. We note, however, that while virtual options may enhance accessibility, in‐person sessions can sometimes be a convenient mode of delivery, particularly during the intensive early treatment period when families spend considerable time in hospital.

Parents also emphasized the importance of proactive therapy recruitment, noting that they likely would not have sought support independently. These observations align with research on help‐seeking behaviors, which suggests that caregivers of children with serious illnesses often underutilize mental health services due to limited time and the need to prioritize their child's care [48]. This highlights the importance of offering flexible recruitment and delivery schedules for psychotherapy, both in routine clinical practice and within clinical trials. Taken together, our findings suggest that EASE is feasible to deliver to the parents of children with AL and that those who receive it report a high degree of perceived benefit. Should effectiveness be demonstrated in a large RCT, next steps in integrating EASE into routine pediatric oncology care would entail practical considerations such as therapist training, allocation of clinical time for session delivery, and access to secure videoconferencing platforms within existing hospital infrastructure.

Study Limitations

The external validity of our findings is limited by study recruitment at a single, large metropolitan pediatric oncology center. Additionally, while we recruited a sample of parents who varied in characteristics, including race/ethnicity, primary language, and child age, our study was limited to parents of children with AL. As such, it is not yet known whether the findings will generalize to parents of children with other cancers, chronic health conditions, or to families with different sociodemographic characteristics, such as lower household income or less formal education. Further research is therefore needed to evaluate the applicability and acceptability of EASE across broader parent populations. This single‐arm feasibility study was not designed to determine the effectiveness of EASE. However, based on its findings, we are now undertaking a large RCT of EASE in the parents of children with AL and other cancers (Clinicaltrials.gov ID: NCT06367556; CIHR PJT 190188, LJ and GR, Co‐Principal Investigators) to determine its effectiveness in relieving TSS and improving the well‐being of parents of children with cancer.

Conclusion

This single‐arm study demonstrated that EASE is a feasible intervention showing great acceptability for parents of children with newly diagnosed AL. These parents reported substantial emotional burden and distress and indicated that EASE allowed them to attend to their own emotional concerns, to have their experience be validated, and to find relief from their distress. These findings provide strong justification to undertake a large RCT, which is now underway in parents with and without elevated TSS at baseline and with protocol adjustments that have been informed by this feasibility study. This RCT will determine the efficacy of EASE in alleviating traumatic stress and other manifestations of distress in the parents of children with AL and other cancers.
