Low-concentration norepinephrine in the French operating theatre
Authors: Manon Iraqi, Fabrice Vallée, Joaquim Mateo, Alexandre Mebazaa, Emmanuel Weiss, Juliette Oliary, Jacques Callebert, Jérome Cartailler, Jona Joachim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTraditionally, ephedrine and phenylephrine have been used to maintain blood pressure during anaesthesia. The use of low-concentration norepinephrine (‘baby norepinephrine’, bNOR) appears to be increasing.OBJECTIVESTo better define the current role of norepinephrine in the management of intra-operative hypotension and to identify the challenges associated with its use.DESIGNA practice survey (including collection of vasopressor usage data and a questionnaire on the use of bNOR in French operating rooms), combined with pharmacological assessments of norepinephrine dilutions.SETTINGVasopressor usage data were collected between 2017 and 2022 in 21 French hospitals. The survey was conducted from July 2021 to April 2022 among anaesthesia personnel within the French-speaking community. Pharmacological analyses were performed in two French university hospitals.PATIENTSNone.INTERVENTIONNone.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESTo assess vasopressor use in operating theatres in France, and to evaluate the accuracy and stability of manually prepared bNOR dilutions.RESULTSUsage data demonstrated an increase in norepinephrine use from 28 518 to 59 598 vials (+92.4%), whereas phenylephrine use decreased over the same period from 49 407 vials and prefilled syringes to 14 252 (−71.1%). Survey findings indicated that a majority of respondents (72.5%) use bNOR diluted to concentrations of 10 and 16 μg ml−1. The principal barrier to routine use identified was the risk of dilution inaccuracy. Norepinephrine concentrations were then measured in 16 samples of 10 μg ml−1 and 50 samples of 5 μg ml−1 manually prepared bNOR. Median concentration errors were 10.8% (± 1.34 μg ml−1) for 10 μg ml−1 dilutions and 13.4% (± 0.54 μg ml−1) for 5 μg ml−1 dilutions. Stability testing revealed a reduction in norepinephrine concentration of up to 32% over 24 h.CONCLUSIONbNOR is emerging as a standard approach for the management of intra-operative hypotension in French operating theatres. However, dilution inaccuracies and stability limitations highlight the need for ready-to-use formulations in order to reduce errors and improve peri-operative care.

Introduction

Intra-operative hypotension (IoH) is associated with increased postoperative mortality, acute kidney injury and myocardial injury.1–5 The prevention of IoH relies heavily on effective blood pressure management, often requiring the use of vasopressors. Ephedrine and phenylephrine are among the most commonly administered agents in the operating room.

Ephedrine exerts its effects indirectly by promoting the release of endogenous norepinephrine, thereby producing both α and β-adrenergic stimulation.6 However, its clinical utility is limited by tachyphylaxis resulting from the depletion of endogenous norepinephrine stores.7 Phenylephrine, a selective α1-adrenergic agonist, induces arterial and venous vasoconstriction, leading to an increase in blood pressure.8 This vasoconstriction may trigger baroreceptor-mediated bradycardia and a reduction in cardiac output.9 Consequently, neither agent provides an optimal long-term solution for the prevention of IoH with an ideal safety profile.

More recently, norepinephrine has gained increasing popularity in the operating room. Its combined α1 and β1-adrenergic effects allow for an increase in blood pressure while preserving cardiac output. In surgical settings, norepinephrine is typically administered in diluted form, commonly referred to as ‘baby norepinephrine’ (bNOR). Futier et al. 10 demonstrated a significant reduction in organ dysfunction in patients treated with bNOR compared with those receiving standard ephedrine regimens. These findings have led to discussions regarding whether norepinephrine should be considered the first-line vasopressor in the operating room.11

Despite its advantages, concerns have been raised regarding peripheral administration of norepinephrine, particularly the risk of extravasation and subsequent tissue necrosis. However, previous studies have not demonstrated an increased risk.12 A more substantial concern relates to the requirement for manual bedside dilution. Prefilled syringes have been shown to be more reliable than manual preparation.13 Given the potent haemodynamic effects of norepinephrine, preparation errors may have severe consequences.14 Indeed, norepinephrine has been identified as having a higher susceptibility to high-risk medication errors compared with other agents.15

To better characterise current practices, we first conducted a survey to assess the use of bNOR in French operating rooms. We then evaluated the accuracy and stability of manually prepared bNOR solutions through pharmacological assays performed under real-life conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethics

Ethical approval was not required, as the study did not involve human or animal subjects.

Vasopressor use at AP-HP

Data on vasopressor consumption were obtained through collaboration with the Agence Générale des Équipements et Produits de Santé (AGEPS), which oversees procurement and distribution within the Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris (AP-HP) network. From 2017 to 2022, detailed records of ephedrine, phenylephrine and norepinephrine usage (vials and prefilled syringes) were collected from 21 hospitals within the AP-HP system.

National survey

A 29-item questionnaire was developed to evaluate the use and perception of bNOR among anaesthesia professionals in France (see Supplemental Digital Content SDC1). The target population of this survey comprised anaesthesiologists, anaesthesia nurses, anaesthesia residents and student nurse anaesthetists primarily practicing in French operating rooms. The survey included multiple-choice and open-ended questions and was distributed between July 2021 and April 2022 through hospital networks, professional societies and social media platforms.

Assessment of dilution accuracy and stability

Norepinephrine solutions were prepared by anaesthesia staff unaware of the study. Samples were collected immediately after preparation (H0), and after 8 h (H8) and 24 h (H24), under typical operating room conditions (room temperature and light exposure), then frozen at −20°C. bNOR was prepared from Mylan ampoules of norepinephrine tartrate at a concentration of 2 mg ml−1. The study focused on two specific dilutions: 5 and 10 μg ml−1. These dilutions were prepared in accordance with the protocols established at Lariboisière (AP-HP) Hospital (5 μg ml−1; Supplemental Digital Content SDC2) and Beaujon (AP-HP) Hospital (10 μg ml−1; Supplemental Digital Content SDC3).

Norepinephrine concentrations were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection. Following dilution in hydrochloric acid to achieve concentrations within the calibration range, the resulting solution was injected into a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a dedicated column (Chromosystems, Gräfelfing, Germany) and a mobile phase optimised for the quantification of biogenic amines. The calibration range spans from 10 to 1000 nmol (10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 nmol). The limit of detection is 1 nmol.

Results were initially expressed in nmol ml−1 and subsequently converted to μg ml−1 using the molar mass of norepinephrine tartrate (319.26 g mol−1). All concentrations are reported as μg of norepinephrine tartrate.

Statistical analysis

Nonparametric tests (Friedman and Wilcoxon) were used due to nonnormal data distribution. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Trends in vasopressor use

We analysed trends in vasopressor use across 21 AP-HP hospitals between 2017 and 2022 (see Fig. 1). Phenylephrine use declined markedly, decreasing from 49 407 vials in 2017 to 14 252 in 2022, representing a 71% reduction. This decrease occurred concurrently with a steady increase in norepinephrine use, with the corresponding usage curves intersecting in late 2018. Ephedrine use initially increased by 14%, rising from 70 114 vials in 2017 to 79 283 in 2019, before stabilising at an average of approximately 64 000 vials annually after 2020. Overall, the total consumption of vasopressors (ampoules and prefilled syringes combined) decreased by 8%, from 148 039 in 2017 to 136 737 in 2022. On the basis of the mean unit prices paid by AP-HP hospitals during this period, this corresponded to a reduction in total expenditure from €420 000 in 2017 to €373 000 in 2022.

Survey findings

A total of 2105 responses were collected. Most respondents were based in France (96%) and included anaesthesiologists (49%), nurses (30%) and trainees (9%). Nearly half of the respondents were employed in university hospitals (48%), while the remainder worked in general hospitals (26%), private hospitals (18%) and nonprofit private institutions (7.1%).

The term ‘baby-norepinephrine’ was recognised by 94% of the respondents.

It was administered predominantly during general anaesthesia (99%) and almost exclusively via peripheral venous access, with only 2% of respondents considering the use of a central venous catheter necessary for its administration. Only 3.5% considered invasive arterial catheter monitoring essential when administering bNOR, and just 4.3% systematically combined its use with cardiac output monitoring. Most clinicians reported a positive impact on practice, with 88% considering it essential for maintaining target blood pressure.

The survey also examined the specific dilutions of bNOR used across operating rooms (Fig. 2). Most respondents (72%) reported using concentrations of 10 and 16 μg ml−1. However, only 53% reported the existence of a written dilution protocol.

Notably, 18% of respondents had encountered dilution errors during preparation, and 31% reported adverse events, most commonly prolonged hypertensive episodes (17%) and severe bradycardia (14%). Despite these concerns, 72% of respondents reported no limitations to the use of bNOR in the operating room. Among the 28% who did report limitations, the most frequently cited issues were the risk of dilution errors, inappropriate packaging and the complexity of the dilution process.

Dilution accuracy and stability

We analysed 16 samples of bNOR diluted to 10 μg ml−1 and 50 samples diluted to 5 μg ml−1 (Fig. 3a). Median [IQR; range] concentrations were 11.34 μg ml−1 [9.65 to 13.48; 8.04 to 15.65] for the 10 μg ml−1 group and 5.54 μg ml−1 [4.93 to 6.46; 3.76 to 9.4] for the 5 μg ml−1 group. Median concentration errors were 10.8% ±1.34 μg ml−1 for 10 μg ml−1 dilutions and 13.4% ±0.54 μg ml−1 for 5 μg ml−1 dilutions.

Norepinephrine stability was then assessed at H0, H8 and H24 (Fig. 3b, c). In the 10 μg ml−1 group (n = 10), median concentration decreased from 11.57 μg ml−1 [10.12 to 14.47] at baseline to 8.99 [8.49 to 12.11] at H8 (P = 0.002) and 8.21 [6.32 to 10.22] at H24 (P = 0.002).

In the 5 μg ml−1 group (n = 50), median concentration decreased from 5.54 μg ml−1 [4.93 to 6.45] to 4.60 μg ml−1 [3.94 to 5.36] at H8 (P < 0.0001) and 3.91 μg ml−1 [3.31 to 4.45] at H24 (P < 0.0001).

After 24 h, concentrations decreased by 32% in the 10 μg ml−1 samples, with reductions of 18% between H0 and H8 and 16% between H8 and H24. Similarly, in the 5 μg ml−1 samples, concentrations decreased by 31%, with reductions of 17% between H0 and H8 and 17% between H8 and H24.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of bNOR use across multiple clinical centres, combining practice surveys with pharmacological analyses. Our findings demonstrate a marked increase in bNOR use in French operating rooms, suggesting that it has become the predominant vasopressor in peri-operative care.

These results are consistent with emerging literature supporting norepinephrine as a first-line vasopressor during anaesthesia.16,17 They also align with previous reports indicating that a large majority of French clinicians consider bNOR essential for achieving and maintaining target mean arterial pressure.18

Our study identifies key safety concerns related to the manual preparation of bNOR. The conventional use of norepinephrine in critical care involves administration via a central venous catheter, at typical concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 1 mg ml-1, to avoid the risk of skin necrosis in case of extravasation.19 This practice is not routine in anaesthesia, where placement of a central venous catheter is uncommon outside of major surgery. Consequently, the expansion of norepinephrine use in the operating room has depended on its administration in a diluted form, via bedside dilution. This represents a major limitation: manual preparation introduces multiple sources of error, including inaccuracies in dose calculation, imprecision during handling and human factors such as fatigue or emergency conditions, all of which may result in clinically significant adverse events.

We have demonstrated a time-dependent decrease in norepinephrine concentration in bNOR syringes. While previous studies have reported stability for up to 48 h under controlled conditions,20,21 our results suggest that stability is compromised beyond 8 h at room temperature. To our knowledge, only one study has reported stability of norepinephrine at lower concentrations at 24 h.22 This discrepancy may reflect real-world operating room conditions, including variable temperature and light exposure. Although not directly assessed, solution pH may further influence norepinephrine degradation, with less acidic environments accelerating breakdown.23 However, the clinical relevance of this instability may be mitigated by the relatively short duration of intra-operative infusions, which rarely exceed 8 to 10 h before transfer to the intensive care unit. It is to be noted that our study focused on the commonly used norepinephrine tartrate formulation and findings may not translate to other compounds such as norepinephrine hydrochloride.24

Therefore, the development of prediluted, prefilled norepinephrine syringes represent a rational and necessary evolution in France. Such formulations have the potential to improve safety, reduce preparation errors and ensure drug stability.

This study has several limitations. The survey component is subject to response bias, and the distribution strategy precluded calculation of a response rate. In addition, the study population was restricted to French anaesthesia providers, limiting generalisability.

The COVID-19 pandemic in France may have affected vasopressor consumption in 2020 due to reduced surgical activity; however, the observed shift from phenylephrine to norepinephrine preceded the pandemic and persisted thereafter.

Finally, the sample size for stability analysis in the 10 μg ml−1 group was limited, and storage conditions prior to freezing were not standardised. Nevertheless, these conditions reflect routine clinical practice and therefore enhance the external validity of our findings.

Conclusion

The use of low-dose norepinephrine in the operating room is increasing in France, progressively replacing phenylephrine and complementing ephedrine. However, manual dilution introduces risks related to preparation errors and drug instability, highlighting the need for ready-to-use formulations in France. Despite these challenges, bNOR is emerging as a preferred vasopressor for intra-operative blood pressure management.
