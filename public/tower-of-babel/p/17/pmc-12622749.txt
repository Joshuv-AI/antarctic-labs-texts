The threshold effect of a body shape index on phenotypic age acceleration in U.S. adults: Insights from NHANES 1999 to 2010
Authors: Yu-Jie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines the relationship between a body shape index (ABSI) and accelerated aging in a nationally representative U.S. population. Data from the 1999 to 2010 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were analyzed. ABSI numerically equals to waist circumference divided by the square of height and two-thirds power of body mass index. Phenotypic age (PhenoAge) is a biological age estimation based on a combination of clinical markers, while phenotypic age acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel) is calculated as the difference between 1’s PhenoAge and chronological age. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. Smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were performed to explore the nonlinear relationship between these variables. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association across different demographic and clinical strata. A total of 23,045 participants were included, with a mean age of 49.15 years and 49.1% male representation. After adjusting for multiple covariates, the multivariable linear regression model showed a positive correlation between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel (β: 1.05, 95% CI: 0.77–1.34). An L-shaped relationship was observed between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel (P for nonlinearity < .001), with an inflection point at 7.97. Below this inflection point, no significant association was observed, while above it, ABSI was positively associated with PhenoAgeAccel (β: 2.63, 95% CI: 2.28–2.97, P < .001). Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel was more pronounced in individuals aged 20 to 39 years compared with those aged ≥ 40 years, in men compared with women, and in those without hypertension or diabetes compared with those with these conditions. This study demonstrates a positive L-shaped correlation between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel in U.S. adults.

1. Introduction

Aging is a process of gradual decline in physiological functions of organisms over time, involving accumulation of cell damage, changes in gene expression, and cell cycle arrest.[1,2] The proportion of individuals in the U.S. aged 50 and above is projected to increase by 61.11% in 2050, with a significant increase in the prevalence of chronic diseases.[3] This demographic shift imposes an immense burden on healthcare systems, straining resources, and necessitating the development of novel therapeutic strategies to mitigate the impact of aging-related morbidity and mortality. In recent years, phenotypic age (PhenoAge), which is calculated based on the following 10 biomarkers including albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, white blood cell count, and chronological age (CA),[4] has attracted increasing attention. Unlike single molecular markers, PhenoAge offers a more holistic assessment of health status, provides enhanced prediction for health outcomes, and facilitates the identification of individuals at elevated risk for age-related diseases.[5] PhenoAgeAccel is determined by subtracting CA from PhenoAge, with higher values suggesting a more rapid rate of biological aging.[4]

The global rise in obesity, with World Health Organization data showing a doubling of rates since 1980, poses major health risks.[6] Traditional metrics like body mass index (BMI) and waist–hip ratio, are capable of indicating body size. However, they have certain drawbacks. BMI confounds fat and lean mass, and fails to measure the distribution of fat in the body.[7] And as for waist–hip ratio, it has been testified that waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference are far better treated as separate independent variables.[8] The a body shape index (ABSI), integrating height, weight, and WC, more accurately reflects fat distribution and outperforms traditional indices in predicting cardiovascular disease (CVD), and mortality,[9,10] offering a new paradigm for obesity assessment.

ABSI is an important indicator of fat distribution and can predict certain health outcomes, while phenotypic age acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel) is an important indicator of accelerated aging. Clarifying the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel may offer novel insights into the mechanisms driving age-related health disparities and provide new opportunities for early detection and intervention of accelerated aging processes.

Previous studies have preliminarily demonstrated the association between ABSI and biological aging.[11] PhenoAgeAccel is a U.S. population-based measure of aging that is more representative of the U.S. In this study, we utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to explore relationships between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. We expect that clarifying this relationship will facilitate the development of more effective strategies for assessing and managing aging-related health risks.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This study utilized data from the NHANES conducted between 1999 and 2010. NHANES is a national project aimed at assessing the health and nutritional status of individuals in U.S. through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. All analyses incorporated the 2-year Mobile Examination Center weights (WTMEC2YR) and Interview weights (WTINT2YR), along with the corresponding strata (SDMVSTRA) and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), to account for the complex multistage survey design of NHANES. For combined cycles, each 2-year weight was divided by the number of cycles to create the overall analytic weight.

The initial dataset encompassed 6 consecutive cycles from 1999 to 2010, totaling 62,160 participants. Following the exclusion of 29, 696 individuals aged under 20 years of age, the remaining cases were further restricted to exclude those with incomplete data on ABSI or PhenoAgeAccel or with missing covariate data. The final analytic sample comprised 23,045 participants. The screening process of the data is shown in Figure 1.

2.3. Covariates

To ensure comprehensive adjustments in the analysis, relevant sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and comorbid health conditions were included as covariates. The included covariates were: age (years), sex (male, female), ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other; self-declared via NHANES interview questionnaires), education level (less than high school, high school, more than high school), poverty income ratio (PIR), calculated by dividing an individual or family’s total income by the government – set poverty threshold, which varies based on family size and composition, (<1.3, ≥1.3, and < 3.5, ≥3.5),[13] marital status (never married/widowed, divorced, or separated/married, or living with partner), smoking (Never smoker, former smoker, current smoker), drinking (has at least 12 alcoholic drinks per year: Yes/No) and physical activity (inactive, moderate, vigorous, “inactive” = no moderate/vigorous activity, “moderate” = 150–300 minutes/week of moderate or 75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity, “vigorous” = >300 minutes/week of moderate or > 150 minutes/week of vigorous activity[14]). Diabetes was defined by any of the following criteria: a prior diagnosis of diabetes, current use of insulin or diabetes pills, fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, or hemoglobin A1c level ≥ 6.5%.[15] Hypertension was identified by a prior diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medications, or blood pressure levels of ≥ 130 mm Hg systolic and/or > 80 mm Hg diastolic.[16] CVD was determined through self-reported diagnoses by healthcare professionals, including coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, heart attack, and stroke.[17]

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate NHANES sampling weights and considering the complex multistage cluster survey design. Survey weights (WTMEC2YR and WTINT2YR), primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), and strata (SDMVSTRA) were integrated into all analyses to ensure the results were representative of the U.S. adult population. Participants were divided into 4 groups according to ABSI quartiles. Baseline characteristics were described with weighted means (95% CI) for continuous variables and weighted percentages (95% CI) for categorical variables. Differences in characteristics across ABSI groups were assessed using 1-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

We employed a multiple linear regression model to evaluate the relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. ABSI was included as both a categorical variable (quartiles) and a continuous variable (with β coefficients calculated per unit increment). For analysis, 3 models were established: an unadjusted model, Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity, and Model 2 further adjusted for education, marital status, PIR, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. We adjusted for these covariates with reference to previous literature examining the relationship between body shape index and biological aging[18,19] to minimize bias, improve precision, and ensure that our results are generalizable to a broader population. Additionally, smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were conducted to explore the potential nonlinear association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. A likelihood ratio test was performed to compare the 1-line linear regression model with the 2-piecewise linear model.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel across various age groups, sexes, ethnicities, PIR groups and disease histories, including diabetes, hypertension, and CVD. Interaction tests were performed to determine the consistency of these associations within each subgroup.

All the analyses were conducted with R (http://www.R-project.org, version 4.3.1) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com, version 4.2). Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05.

3.3. Smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analysis

The smoothed curve fitting revealed a nonlinear relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel (P for nonlinearity < .001) (Fig. 2). Table 3 displays the threshold effect analysis of ABSI on PhenoAgeAccel, conducted using both a standard linear model and a 2-piecewise linear regression model. In the standard linear model, ABSI exhibited a significant positive correlation with PhenoAgeAccel, with a β of 1.80 (95% CI: 1.57–2.04, P < .001) in the adjusted model. Utilizing the 2-piecewise linear regression model, an inflection point (K) of 7.97 was identified in the adjusted model. Below this inflection point, no significant association was observed between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. However, above the inflection point, ABSI was strongly associated with an increased PhenoAgeAccel, with a β of 2.63 (95% CI: 2.28–2.97, P < .001) in the adjusted model.

4. Discussion

In this study within a U.S. population, we explored a positive association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel. Utilizing extensive data from NHANES, we have identified a robust positive L-shaped relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel with an inflection point at 7.97. Subgroup analysis results showed the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel was more pronounced in individuals aged 20 to 39 years, men, individuals without hypertension, diabetes, or CVD.

There is substantial evidence indicating that obesity may hasten the aging process.[20] Aging is possibly linked to a rise in abdominal obesity, which significantly contributes to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome.[21] Additionally, other studies have demonstrated a positive nonlinear association between cardiovascular indicators, like atherogenic index of plasma, and PhenoAgeAccel,[22] aligning with our findings. Plus, ABSI has certain advantages compared to other indicators. Notably, ABSI has emerged as an independent predictor of mortality risk, compared with BMI.[10] A key limitation of BMI is its inability to differentiate between fat mass and lean mass,[23] often leading to the misclassification of muscular individuals as obese. Moreover, BMI fails to account for fat distribution.[24,25] In contrast, ABSI addresses these shortcomings by incorporating WC in relation to height and weight, thereby offering a more accurate reflection of central adiposity.[12] Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that ABSI is more sensitive than BMI in detecting alterations in circulating insulin levels and lipoprotein profiles,[26] highlighting its potential utility in assessing metabolic risk beyond traditional anthropometric measures. Our study differs from previous research[11] by including a threshold effect analysis, identifying an inflection point at 7.97, which is a novel finding. The previous study has tentatively demonstrated a relationship between ABSI and biological aging using Klemera–Doubal Method Age Acceleration as the outcome variable. PhenoAgeAccel serves as a population-based metric of aging in the U.S., offering a more accurate representation of the aging process within the U.S. population. In addition, our study included a larger sample size, which could increase the reliability and power of the results.

The threshold effect analysis utilizing a 2-piecewise linear regression model revealed a significant increase in PhenoAgeAccel at an ABSI value of 7.97. No significant association was observed below this threshold. This finding may be attributed to the cumulative effect of ABSI on physiological parameters, which may not be substantial enough to induce significant PhenoAgeAccel at lower ABSI values. The human body’s adaptive responses to gradual increases in ABSI may counteract initial adverse effects through various physiological mechanisms.[27,28] However, these adaptations may be insufficient when ABSI surpasses the threshold, resulting in a notable increase in PhenoAgeAccel. The identified threshold effect implies that individuals with ABSI values above 7.97 are potentially at greater risk for PhenoAgeAccel, warranting earlier or more intensive interventions.

Our subgroup analysis revealed significant differences in the association between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel across various demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, hypertension, and diabetes. Several factors may account for these observations. Individuals aged 20 to 39 are typically in a phase of high physiological and metabolic activity, potentially rendering them more sensitive to fluctuations in ABSI.[29] As a metric of body fat distribution and muscle mass, ABSI may more accurately reflect metabolic health and physiological age in this demographic. Sex differences also play a role. Males, with their higher muscle mass and distinct fat distribution patterns,[30] may exhibit greater sensitivity to ABSI changes. Additionally, men are often more inclined towards unhealthy lifestyle choices,[31] which could exacerbate phenotypic aging by influencing ABSI. Chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes may confound the measurement and interpretation of ABSI. Metabolic disorders such as hypertension and diabetes may partially mask the effect of ABSI on aging.[32,33] Plus, the treatment of chronic diseases such as antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering drugs may affect the relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel.[34,35] Identifying variations in the relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel across distinct subgroups facilitates the early detection of individuals at elevated health risks.

The relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel may be explained by several mechanisms as follows. The relationship between obesity and oxidative stress is well-established.[36] Oxidative stress is a pivotal driver of cellular senescence, instigating a cascade of intethnicityllular perturbations such as mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, telomere attrition, lipid peroxidation, and protein oxidation.[37] These events are conducive to apoptosis and are implicated in the aging process. Also, obesity correlates with inflammatory responses,[38] which are integral to the concept of inflammatory aging, a recognized pathway in the aging paradigm.[39,40] Furthermore, fat distribution indicated by ABSI, may modulate phenotypic age acceleration through gut microbiota alterations. Fatty acids are known to shape the gut microbial composition, thereby impacting the host’s metabolic health.[41] Consumption of high-fat diets has been associated with an increase in pro-inflammatory bacteria within the gut.[42] Additionally, certain gut microbial profiles have been found to correlate inversely with DNA methylation PhenoAgeAccel,[43] suggesting a potential role in the aging process.

ABSI serves as a valuable clinical metric for evaluating the aging risk profile in patients. Monitoring ABSI fluctuations enables clinicians to detect signs of accelerated aging promptly, allowing for the timely institution of preventive strategies. The proactive management of ABSI could contribute significantly to the amelioration of aging-related health burdens.

This investigation utilized NHANES database to examine the relationship between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel, benefiting from its robust national representation. However, the study’s cross-sectional design excludes the establishment of causality, necessitating future longitudinal cohort studies for a more definitive exploration. Additionally, self-reported data on certain covariates may introduce potential biases. Although covariates were adjusted for, some confounding factors may have been overlooked, like environmental and genetic factors. Omission of treatment data may also represent a limitation, as it influences outcomes. Furthermore, the study’s focus on the U.S. population suggests that the findings may not be generalizable to other regions or demographic groups.

5. Conclusion

Based on a nationally representative population, this study shows a positive L-shaped correlation between ABSI and PhenoAgeAccel in the U.S. adults, which offers insights into the intricate relationship between fat distribution and aging, enhancing our current arsenal of obesity assessment methodologies.
