Introduction

Cardioinhibitory vasovagal syncope (VVS) refractory to pharmacotherapy poses a significant therapeutic challenge in young patients, for whom permanent pacemaker implantation carries substantial long-term risks. Cardioneuroablation (CNA)—catheter-based selective denervation of cardiac parasympathetic ganglia—has emerged as a promising pacemaker-sparing strategy.1 An updated meta-analysis of 37 studies and 1585 patients reported an overall syncope recurrence rate of 12.5%2; however, median follow-up across included studies often does not exceed 12 months, and long-term recurrence data remain limited in scope.

Although cohort data confirm sustained vagal denervation beyond 5 years as assessed using serial Holter monitoring and heart rate variability (HRV) analysis,3 individual cases combining concurrent dual-modality phenotyping—spontaneous implantable loop recorder (ILR)–documented asystole alongside tilt-table testing—with continuous objective monitoring throughout follow-up remain exceptionally rare, particularly in the adolescent population. We report such a case: biatrial 3-dimensional (3D) mapping-guided CNA with dual-modality phenotyping and 23-month continuous objective follow-up by ILR, documenting a complete and reproducible management pathway in an adolescent with refractory cardioinhibitory VVS.

Case description

A 17-year-old woman presented in January 2024 with recurrent syncope (1–2 episodes/mo) causing traumatic falls. Nonpharmacologic measures (increased fluid intake 2.5–3 L/d, salt supplementation, compression stockings, counter-pressure maneuvers) and midodrine (titrated to 10 mg 3 times daily over 2 months) failed to reduce episode frequency or severity. This report adheres to the CARE case report guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). A written informed consent was obtained from the patient and her parents.

Baseline electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm at 52 beats per minute (bpm) with first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (PR 240 ms). Echocardiography was normal (left ventricular ejection fraction 65%). Head-up tilt-table test (Italian protocol, nitroglycerin provocation) was positive at 11 minutes with a 9-second asystole (VASIS 2B). An ILR (implanted February 2024) documented a spontaneous 12-second sinus pause during syncope in March 2024 (Figure 1A). This dual-modality phenotyping—objective documentation of both provoked and spontaneous cardioinhibitory events—provided unambiguous procedural justification and is rarely achieved in clinical practice. 72-hour Holter monitoring revealed episodic Mobitz I second-degree AV block.

Preprocedural findings. A: 12-second sinus pause on an implantable loop recorder during spontaneous syncope (implantable loop recorder implanted February 2024; pause recorded March 2024). B: Baseline electrocardiogram: sinus rhythm at 52 beats per minute, first-degree atrioventricular block (PR 240 ms). C: Preablation electrophysiological study: Wenckebach point at 581 ms.

After Heart Team discussion and shared decision making with the patient and her parents—who strongly preferred to avoid permanent pacing—biatrial CNA was performed in March 2024 under general anesthesia. The EnSite Precision™ 3D electroanatomic mapping system (Abbott, Minneapolis, MN) was used. High-frequency stimulation (HFS) (20 Hz, 5–10 ms pulse width, 10–20 mA) identified 6 ganglionated plexi clusters: 4 left atrial peripulmonary and 2 right atrial ganglia along the superior vena cava and interatrial septum. Radiofrequency ablation was delivered with an irrigated contact-force-sensing catheter (30–35 W, 10–25 g, 60 s/site; Figure 2A). Procedural endpoints were (1) complete abolition of vagal response on repeat HFS; (2) heart rate increase from 52 to 77 bpm, rising to 94 bpm after atropine 0.04 mg/kg; and (3) Wenckebach cycle length shortening from 581 to 543 ms (Figure 2B), with fluoroscopy time of 9.1 minutes and total procedure duration of 98 minutes. No periprocedural complications occurred.

B: After ablation: sinus rhythm at 77 beats per minute, Wenckebach point 543 ms, and 94 beats per minute after atropine 0.04 mg/kg.

Outcomes

At 6-month follow-up, ILR interrogation showed no pauses lasting >3 seconds, no AV block, and only occasional exertional sinus tachycardia (Figure 3). At 23 months (final ILR interrogation January 29, 2026, before device removal), the patient remained completely asymptomatic with no syncopal or presyncopal episodes, no significant pauses, and no AV block (Figure 4). The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey quality-of-life score improved from 57 to 90 (+33 points). The patient resumed full academic and unrestricted sports activity. Occasional sinus tachycardia episodes were misclassified as atrial fibrillation by the device but were clinically well tolerated and self-limited. No chronotropic incompetence was documented at any time point.

Implantable loop recorder interrogation at 6-month follow-up: no sinus pauses lasting >3 seconds, no atrioventricular block. Occasional exertional sinus tachycardia noted, consistent with postablation sympathetic predominance.

Final implantable loop recorder interrogation (January 29, 2026): sustained freedom from syncope and absence of significant pauses or atrioventricular block at 23 months, confirming durable procedural success by continuous objective, device-based monitoring.

Discussion

This case contributes 3 elements of specific interest that distinguish it from the existing literature. First, dual-modality phenotyping—spontaneous ILR-confirmed 12-second asystole concurrent with a positive tilt-table test (9-second asystole, VASIS 2B)—is rarely achieved in adolescents and provided unambiguous, objective procedural justification. Most published series, including the landmark cohorts that established the long-term durability of CNA,3 relied on symptom-based follow-up or tilt test alone for patient selection and outcome assessment. Second, 23-month continuous ILR monitoring provides objective, device-based evidence of sustained freedom from recurrence, independently of patient-reported symptoms. Third, the complete management pathway—failed optimized pharmacotherapy, biatrial 3D-guided CNA with triple denervation endpoints, and objective long-term ILR follow-up—is fully documented in an adolescent, a population for whom reproducible pathways are particularly needed given the decades of follow-up ahead.

A biatrial approach was chosen based on emerging evidence of superior durability over unilateral strategies.4 Acute endpoints (HFS abolition and atropine response) confirmed adequate denervation and align with predictors of long-term success.5 Postablation transient sympathetic predominance—manifesting as occasional exertional sinus tachycardia—is consistent with published series reporting partial vagal reinnervation over 6–12 months.6 The absence of chronotropic incompetence or atrial fibrillation at 23 months is reassuring. Nevertheless, potential long-term autonomic consequences of vagal denervation—including chronotropic incompetence, persistent sinus tachycardia, and theoretical extracardiac effects of parasympathetic denervation—warrant ongoing surveillance, particularly in young patients with decades of life ahead.7 Long-term autonomic surveillance (Holter monitoring, HRV analysis, exercise testing) remains warranted in young patients.7 CNA is less effective in vasodepressor-dominant syncope, underscoring the importance of precise phenotyping.8

Limitations

This is a single-case report with inherent limited generalizability. The CNA technique requires specialized expertise and 3D mapping equipment. No extracardiac vagal stimulation tests were performed, limiting comprehensive assessment of reflex sensitivity across vagal pathways9; this technique offers complementary information to HFS and should be considered in future cases. Dedicated long-term autonomic testing (exercise testing, serial HRV analysis) was not performed beyond routine ILR interrogation. Follow-up of 23 months, although sufficient to document midterm outcomes, does not allow conclusions on very long-term durability beyond what has been reported in larger cohort studies.3

Conclusion

Biatrial 3D mapping-guided CNA yielded sustained freedom from syncope at 23 months in an adolescent with refractory cardioinhibitory VVS, with significant quality-of-life improvement and no complications. The distinctive contribution of this case lies in its dual-modality objective phenotyping—combining spontaneous ILR-documented asystole with tilt-table testing—and continuous device-based follow-up, providing a level of evidence rarely achieved in individual case documentation. This case illustrates a complete, reproducible management pathway for young patients in whom permanent pacing is undesirable.
