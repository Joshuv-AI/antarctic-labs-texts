Mitigating negative data bias to enhance TCR–epitope binding and residue interaction prediction
Authors: Xue Mi, Jinghua Zhu, Zhu Dai, Yuheng Zhu, Bo Ding, Hao Lin, Yang Shen, Guochun Cao, Zhongdang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of the binding specificity between T-cell receptors (TCRs) and epitopes, along with the elucidation of their molecular interaction mechanisms, is pivotal for advancing immunotherapy and vaccine development. In this study, we propose a negative dataset construction strategy based on region-directed random mutations as an effective complement to traditional negative sampling methods. This strategy preserves the conserved amino acid motifs encoded by the V and J gene segments of the CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequence while introducing key residue mutations within the central junctional region. By constructing hard negatives, this approach encourages the model to capture more discriminative TCR-epitope binding features. Based on this optimized dataset, we developed TranTCR, a computational framework comprising two models: TranTCR-bind, which focuses on global sequence-level binding probability prediction, and TranTCR-map, which leverages transfer learning to translate global binding knowledge into fine-grained characterizations of residue-level interactions, such as inter-residue distances and contact scores. Experimental results demonstrate that TranTCR-bind exhibits superior predictive performance and generalization robustness across various negative sampling protocols. Furthermore, TranTCR-map utilizes attention mechanisms to deeply resolve complex inter-amino acid associations, enabling the identification of latent binding patterns and the revelation of TCR cross-reactivity characteristics. This study provides an efficient computational tool for the high-throughput screening of TCR repertoires and the digital characterization of immune recognition mechanisms.

Main

The interaction between T-cell receptors (TCRs) and antigenic peptides presented by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is essential for initiating immune responses against pathogens. The specificity of TCR–epitope interactions is crucial for developing optimized therapeutic strategies [1–4]. The TCR is a heterodimer typically composed of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} polypeptide chains. While the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha$\end{document} chain is formed by the recombination of variable (V) and joining (J) genes, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} chain undergoes V, diversity (D), and J gene recombination, further diversified by random non-templated nucleotide insertions and deletions at the junctional regions. Theoretically, the human immune system can generate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${10}^{15}$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${10}^{20}$\end{document} unique TCR clonotypes [5, 6], providing a vast repertoire capable of recognizing a myriad of epitopes. This diversity is primarily concentrated in the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), with the CDR3 loop serving as the central determinant of antigen recognition due to its direct contact with the peptide [7]. Notably, TCR-mediated immunity is characterized by high cross-reactivity, where a single TCR may recognize multiple peptide–MHC (pMHC) complexes and vice versa [8]. Despite advances in high-throughput sequencing that allow for rapid TCR repertoire profiling [9, 10], accurately identifying the specific cognate epitopes for a given TCR remains a formidable challenge in computational biology.

Current computational models for predicting epitope-CDR3 specificity predominantly rely on known interaction data of CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} or paired CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} chains. Existing tools vary significantly in their feature engineering; while some utilize the epitope-CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} region (occasionally incorporating V and J segments) as features [11–18], others require paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} sequences [19–22]. Despite employing diverse algorithms—ranging from distance-based methods to sophisticated deep learning architectures—recent studies suggest that the performance gap between these algorithms remains narrow. Instead, the primary bottleneck for accurate prediction lies in the quality and coverage of training data. High-quality datasets must encompass broad epitope diversity and representative negative samples that capture a wide range of non-binding features. Although recent findings from the IMMREP [23, 24] competition suggest that paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} models outperform single-chain models, this conclusion was drawn from filtered datasets where paired sequences were as abundant as single chains. Analysis of the VDJdb database reveals a significant disparity in data coverage. Specifically, the number of unique epitopes associated with CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} data is approximately 1.7 times greater than that available for paired CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} data (65 vs. 38 epitopes) (Fig. S1). Given that broader epitope coverage is critical for the generalizability of binding predictors, we developed the TranTCR-bind framework to offer dual functionality: a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}-chain-based predictor to maximize data volume and an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document}-paired predictor to leverage richer structural features. Due to the higher diversity of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}-chain and its superior data availability, this study focuses primarily on the CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}–epitope interaction.

A critical yet often overlooked aspect of TCR-epitope modeling is the generation of negative (non-binding) data. Since experimental methods like pMHC-tetramer staining [25] and single-cell sequencing [26] primarily identify positive interactions, negative samples are typically generated through artificial pairing of CDR3 and epitopes. This artificial generation extends to the negative samples within independent test sets; when the training and testing negative data share the same underlying distribution, it can create an illusion of superior model performance. Currently, two primary strategies dominate negative data construction. The first involves the random mismatching of known positive pairs; however, this approach is susceptible to false negatives arising from TCR-epitope cross-reactivity. To mitigate this, existing methods either pair TCRs with biochemically distant epitopes [23] or assign matching weights based on observed binding frequencies [22]. The second strategy involves pairing epitopes from known positive sets with reference TCR data. This method is often flawed due to inherent structural differences between positive samples and reference-derived CDR3 sequences. Such discrepancies can induce “shortcut learning”, where the model predicts labels based solely on CDR3 features while ignoring the interactions between epitope and CDR3 residues. To address these issues, we propose a novel negative data generation strategy that emphasizes the learning of key binding site features as a complement to existing methods. Utilizing TCR-seq data from 31 single-cell studies processed via MiXCR [27] (Table S1 and Fig. S2), the analysis confirms that for a given V–J gene combination, CDR3 sequences consist of two highly conserved terminal regions (generally starting with cysteine and ending with phenylalanine) and the central junctional region. Critically, the amino acid insertions or deletions that not only determine CDR3 length but also constitute the primary binding interface for epitope interaction occur within this central junctional region. By introducing mutations (at least two residues) within this hypervariable junctional region while maintaining the V–J framework, we construct hard negatives. This approach helps preserve the TCR-HLA structural context associated with the V-gene framework while forcing the model to learn the specific biochemical constraints of epitope recognition, rather than relying on sequence biases.

In this study, we present TranTCR-bind, a Transformer-based model for TCR–epitope binding prediction, alongside TranTCR-map, a specialized model for predicting residue-level contact maps and distances. The Transformer architecture was selected not only for the ability to extract complex relational features via the self-attention mechanism but also for its inherent interpretability. By visualizing attention weights, we can pinpoint the specific amino acids within the TCR and epitope that drive the binding decision. To address the extreme scarcity of TCR-pMHC crystal structure data, we employed transfer learning [28]. TranTCR-map was initialized with the global binding knowledge acquired by TranTCR-bind and subsequently fine-tuned on curated structural data. This multi-task learning approach significantly enhances the efficiency of residue-level interaction modeling. Our results demonstrate that TranTCR-map successfully identifies critical binding residues located in the central junctional region of CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}. Furthermore, we show that mutations in these sequences lead to increased inter-residue distances and a concomitant decrease in binding probability. Collectively, the TranTCR framework provides a robust and interpretable tool for visualizing TCR–epitope interactions and accelerating the discovery of antigen-specific T cells.

The CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequence is conventionally defined as the residues delimited by the conserved Cys104 of the V gene and Phe119 of the J gene [29]; in this study, these boundary anchor residues were included to ensure structural integrity (Fig. 1C and D). While the flanking V and J motifs exhibit high sequence conservation due to direct germline encoding [29, 30], the central junctional region displays extreme heterogeneity. A primary challenge in TCR-epitope modeling is avoiding “shortcut learning”, where a model discriminates samples based solely on global CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequence biases rather than genuine intermolecular interactions. To evaluate whether our mutation strategy inadvertently introduced such biases, we performed dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis (Fig. 1B and Fig. S3). Results from both raw sequence space and latent features extracted via autoencoders showed no significant separation between positive and negative samples. This statistical consistency indicates that our generated negatives reside within the same sequence distribution as natural binders, compelling the model to rely on complex TCR–epitope interaction patterns for classification. The biological validity of this tripartite deconstruction was further corroborated using Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) analysis across CD8+ T-cell repertoires [31] (Fig. 1D and E). High NMI values were observed at the P104–107 and P114–119 positions, reflecting strong germline-driven dependencies. Conversely, the N-D-N junctional region exhibited near-zero NMI, confirming its stochastic nature and independence from germline constraints. To further validate our generated sequences, we first utilized the MiXCR [27] software to parse and expand a baseline CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequence set from collected TCR sequencing data. Subsequent sequence redundancy analysis against the TCRdb, a large public repository of experimental TCR data, showed significant overlap with our generated sequences (Table S2). This confirms that our mutation strategy generates sequences that are not only statistically indistinguishable from natural TCRs but also biologically plausible within the V(D)J recombination space.

To investigate the sequence determinants of specificity, we analyzed 138 antigen-specific CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequences encoded by the TRBV19-01 and TRBJ1-1 genes (Fig. 1C and Fig. S11). Despite the inherent diversity of the TCR repertoire, sequences targeting the same epitope often converge on specific motifs within the junctional region, such as the “IRS” motif in the CASSIRSNTEAFF clonotype. Using a controlled-variable approach, we fixed the TRBV19-01 germline framework to maintain baseline affinity for the HLA molecule, thereby focusing the learning capacity of the model on the junctional recombinant region. In the construction of negative samples, targeted mutations were introduced exclusively within this hypervariable core while preserving the V and J framework. This targeted perturbation specifically disrupts antigen complementarity while maintaining the TCR-HLA structural context, effectively simulating the loss of binding specificity caused by critical residue substitutions. This rigorous design ensures that the model accurately identifies the core residues responsible for TCR-epitope recognition.

Construction of near-native negative samples and performance validation across independent test sets

The findings of this study demonstrate that enhancements in the performance of the model are driven not only by architectural innovations but also by the intrinsic quality of training data. The impact of negative sampling strategies on predictive capacity was systematically investigated, under the hypothesis that a training set encompassing precise and comprehensive biological features is essential for capturing cross-sequence interaction patterns. To this end, a “Near-native Negative Sampling” strategy was proposed. Unlike distal negatives generated through random pairing, near-native negatives maintain the germline framework of the TCR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} chain (TRBV and TRBJ genes) while introducing region-directed random mutations in two to five key residues within the CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} junctional recombinant region. To avoid excessive perturbation in short junctional regions, mutation burdens were applied conditionally, and larger mutation settings were skipped for sequences with insufficient junctional-region length. The core logic of this approach is to simulate antigen recognition failure induced by multi-point mutations, thereby eliminating the interference of “shortcut learning” driven by sequence frequency biases. To rigorously evaluate the quality of the negative samples, a data partitioning strategy was employed that strictly ensured no overlap between the training set and the independent test sets (Fig. 2A). Using the PDB structure 5EU6 (YLEPGPVTV-CASSFIGGTDTQYF) as a benchmark, non-binding pairs were constructed by mutating two to five non-conserved residues in the junctional region (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}-epitope pairs were retrieved from the ImmuneCODE and IEDB databases to construct independent test sets after the removal of any sequences overlapping with the training data (see Methods). It is noteworthy that the positive epitopes in these test sets are entirely independent of those in the training set (unseen epitopes). While some epitopes in the negative data may overlap with the training set, all unique CDR3-epitope sequence pairs remain strictly non-redundant. The impact of different mutation gradients on the performance of the model was then evaluated using these independent test sets (Tables S1–S2 and Tables S3–S6). The results indicate that increasing the number of mutated residues improves the recall of true-positive binding pairs. While the false-positive rate exhibited a marginal increase, the overall predictive precision remained robust (Fig. 2C and D). Based on the balance between precision and recall, it was determined that mutating two to five amino acids in the junctional region is the optimal strategy for generating high-quality negatives and enhancing the generalization of the model.

The influence of negative sample composition on four core metrics was further assessed: Average Precision (AP), AUC, Recall, and Precision. The proposed method was compared against two prevalent schemes: 10× Genomics reference negatives and the random mismatch strategy. Analysis of the 10× Genomics reference data revealed significant distributional biases due to the limited variety of epitopes, which often induces “shortcut learning” where the model predicts binding based on epitope frequency rather than molecular interactions. Similarly, the random mismatch strategy, which pairs CDR3 sequences with non-cognate epitopes, often hinders the convergence of the model due to TCR cross-reactivity. If a single TCR recognizes multiple epitopes with similar physicochemical properties, randomly labeling these potential interactors as “negative” introduces severe label noise. While some studies attempt to mitigate this by filtering epitopes based on sequence distance, the risk of potential false negatives remains high in immunodominant clones. Such incorrect supervisory signals compel the model to capture pseudo-correlations, thereby weakening the ability of the architecture to discern authentic binding motifs. In contrast, our hybrid strategy, which integrates near-native mutated negative samples with the 10× Genomics reference pool, significantly enhances recognition accuracy and robustness (Tables S7 and S8). By introducing minor perturbations to the junctional core while fixing the germline framework, the model is compelled to decouple sequence statistics from physical binding specificity. This approach forces the architecture to resolve the microscopic physicochemical interaction logic between the CDR3 core and epitope residues, leading to superior generalization on complex and unseen datasets (Fig. 5).

To isolate the contribution of data quality from architectural variance, comparative benchmarking was conducted using the NetTCR 2.0 core architecture, which is based on a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN). For this analysis, all CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequences underwent standardized preprocessing by appending the conserved cysteine (C) to the N-terminus and phenylalanine (F) to the C-terminus to maintain structural alignment. The baseline models, trained on standard 10× Genomics single-cell immune profiling data, were compared against models trained on the proposed VMX dataset, defined here as the V/J-framework-preserving mutation-based negative sampling dataset, in which near-native negative samples were generated by introducing targeted mutations within the CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} junctional region while preserving the conserved V- and J-derived framework (see Methods for details). The MIRA dataset, comprising 192 unique CDR3–epitope pairs, served as an independent benchmark for this comparison. Results demonstrated that under identical 1D-CNN architectural constraints, the VMX-driven models outperformed the baseline across core metrics (Fig. 2G). Specifically, the VMX-trained model successfully identified 95 True Positives (TP) while generating only 40 False Positives (FP). In contrast, the original NetTCR 2.0 model identified fewer TPs (78) and suffered from more than double the FPs (98) (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, at a stringent fixed recall of 0.4, the VMX-trained model maintained markedly higher precision than the baseline (Fig. 2F). These empirical findings confirm that a training set encompassing a broader range of physicochemical features, derived from junctional mutations, substantially enhances the discriminative power of the model and reduces false discovery rates. Finally, the universality of this strategy was verified within the TranTCR-bind framework. Hyperparameter tuning revealed that while the positive-to-negative ratio (1:1 vs. 1:5) had a negligible impact on performance, the choice of negative sampling strategy remained the decisive variable for predictive accuracy (Fig. 2I and Figs S4–S5). In double-blind independent tests from IEDB and ImmuneCODE, where neither the epitopes nor the sequences appeared in the training set, the VMX-driven model clearly demarcated binding from non-binding pairs (Fig. 2H). Across six independent test sets, the strategy led to a significant and consistent increase in AUC regardless of the sample ratio (Fig. 2I). These results confirm that high-quality negative sampling is the primary driver of the reliability and objective evaluation of the model.

TranTCR reveals TCR–epitope interaction

To achieve a comprehensive understanding of TCR-epitope specificity, a two-stage hierarchical training framework was implemented (Fig. 3A). In the first stage, TranTCR-bind was developed as a Transformer-based model to predict global binding probabilities from CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} and epitope sequences. In the second stage, the inherent scarcity of residue-level structural data was addressed by employing transfer learning to develop TranTCR-map. While sharing the same input architecture as TranTCR-bind, TranTCR-map does not output a binary binding probability. It generates high-resolution attention maps that are decoded into distance and contact matrices representing the spatial relationship between all residue pairs. By utilizing TranTCR-bind as a pretrained foundation, the model successfully captures latent residue-level interaction features from extensive sequence data, which significantly accelerates and stabilizes the subsequent fine-tuning of TranTCR-map on limited structural datasets.

The data representation pipeline adheres to IMGT standards to ensure biological consistency. Sequences were standardized to fixed lengths, specifically 12 amino acids for epitopes and 20 for CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document}, to match the natural length distributions (Fig. 3D) and were encoded using the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix. To preserve the spatial context of the sequences, positional embeddings were integrated with multiple self-attention layers (Fig. 3A and B). To ensure computational integrity, a padding mask protocol was implemented, effectively neutralizing the influence of null-data residues on the attention weights. The dual-input architecture adopted by TranTCR-bind enables flexible handling of variable-length sequences and optimizes the final binding score prediction via fully connected layers. Furthermore, the model was fine-tuned using 128 native crystal structures from the STCRDab database [32]. By extracting and concatenating the attention matrices from the decoder layers, TranTCR-map gained the ability to accurately project the spatial distances and contact probabilities between TCR and epitope residues.

TranTCR-bind was trained and tested on a meticulously filtered, multi-source dataset integrated from VDJdb, IEDB, McPAS-TCR, ImmuneCODE, and 10× Genomics (Fig. 3C). The 10× Genomics dataset contributed 31 unique 9-mer epitopes and associated non-binding pairs, which were utilized as negative controls (Fig. S6). To ensure a rigorous assessment of generalization, the data were strictly partitioned to exclude all epitopes present in the ImmuneCODE and IEDB independent test sets from the training phase, thereby guaranteeing that the evaluation of the model was performed on entirely unseen epitopes (Figs S7 and S8). Ablation studies indicated that performance was relatively insensitive to the number of self-attention heads (ranging from five to nine) or the positive-to-negative sample ratio. Consequently, a five-head architecture was selected to optimize computational efficiency without compromising accuracy (Fig. S4A–D).

As a data-driven, interpretable model, TranTCR possesses the core advantage of revealing critical binding hotspots through its attention weights. This interpretability allows for a granular analysis of residue-level interaction patterns, providing a powerful tool for uncovering the underlying molecular mechanisms of immune recognition. Validation results further emphasize that model performance is highly sensitive to the negative sampling strategy (Figs S4A–D and S5A–D). Unlike the label ambiguity often introduced by random mismatching, the proposed junctional region-directed mutation strategy serves as a critical prerequisite for the model to precisely capture authentic binding motifs. Thus, the TranTCR framework offers a robust and biologically grounded complement to existing TCR-epitope prediction methodologies, effectively bridging the gap between sequence-level specificity and structural interaction mechanisms.

Results indicated that a five-head self-attention architecture combined with the junctional region-directed mutation strategy provided the optimal balance between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. To evaluate the generalization boundaries of the model, the test set and the training set remained entirely mutually exclusive at the sequence level. Based on the degree of difficulty in predicting immune recognition, the test set was further partitioned into three progressive evaluation dimensions: (i) Unseen Epitopes, to verify generalization to novel antigens; (ii) Unseen CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} sequences, to assess recognition precision for novel TCR clonotypes; and (iii) Strictly Unseen Pairs, which are completely novel to the model and challenge the capacity for fundamental molecular inference in a “double-blind” scenario.

A systematic benchmarking of TranTCR-bind against established predictive tools was performed to rigorously assess performance. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were evaluated using identical training datasets. Specifically, for tools where the source code was available, such as NetTCR 2.0 and TEIM, the models were retrained using the VMX data strategy developed in this study. For pMTnet, the standard training set provided by that tool was used to train TranTCR-bind. As the scale of the test data expanded, the performance advantage of TranTCR-bind became more pronounced, particularly in the F1-score, which balances precision and recall (Fig. 5B and C).

To eliminate potential evaluation biases arising from shared data distributions, TranTCR-bind was further validated using multiple independent external experimental datasets. In the Justina validation set, TranTCR-bind successfully identified 72 candidate binding pairs, comprising 70 True Positives (TP) and only two False Positives (FP). In comparison, NetTCR 2.0 identified 66 candidate pairs (comprising 65 TP and 1 FP), while TEIM identified 50 candidate pairs (comprising 49 TP and 1 FP) (Fig. 5A). Statistical analysis confirmed that on the Justina dataset, while TranTCR-bind maintained a slight lead in AUC and AP, it demonstrated a significant advantage in ACC and F1-score (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, in tests utilizing the MIRA experimental set (derived from NetTCR 2.0), TranTCR-bind exhibited superior discriminative power compared to the native NetTCR 2.0 model, while TEIM showed a marked disadvantage across all metrics (Fig. 5E). For the pMTnet tool, which did not provide retraining scripts, the public positive training set of that tool was utilized to ensure a fair comparison. Training tasks for TranTCR-bind were constructed by employing both the original random shuffling strategy (with a ratio of positive to negative samples of 1:10) and the negative data generation strategy developed in this study. Conversely, the use of the random shuffling strategy yielded suboptimal results within the framework of the model. These findings strongly confirm that the Transformer-based architecture, when combined with the improved data strategy, achieves more robust and precise predictions.

To comprehensively evaluate the generalization capability across diverse training and testing scenarios and to verify the robustness of the strategy, a benchmarking analysis was performed against the four negative sampling protocols of TEINet [33] (Random Epitope, Uniform Epitope, Random TCR, and Reference TCR). The results (Fig. 5H) demonstrate that the performance of TranTCR is superior to TEINet in both the independent PDB and TBAdb test sets. In an extended evaluation involving PanPep [34], ImRex14, and ATM-TCR15, each model was trained using the optimal hyperparameters and negative sampling strategies proposed by the original authors. The test set integrated positive interaction pairs from the independent PDB and TBAdb databases, combined with the four different negative data distributions proposed by TEINet as well as the junctional region-directed mutation strategy proposed in this study. This design aimed to evaluate the capability of each model to recognize interactions against heterogeneous negative backgrounds. The results (Fig. 5I) indicate that the AUC performance of TranTCR-bind is strongly influenced by the consistency between the negative data distributions of the training and test sets. When the negative distribution of the test set is highly consistent with the junctional mutation strategy proposed in this study, TranTCR-bind exhibits exceptionally high AUC values, reflecting the precise capacity of the model to capture this specific distributional pattern. A horizontal comparison shows that the overall AUC performance of TranTCR-bind is superior to ImRex and PanPep, though it remains lower than the Transformer-based ATM-TCR model. For the AP, TranTCR-bind significantly outperforms all other methods, including ATM-TCR, across all negative data distribution conditions (Fig. 5J). This result directly validates the technical advantage of the junctional region-directed random mutation strategy in suppressing false positives. By learning the minute physicochemical differences within near-native negative samples, TranTCR-bind effectively filters non-binding sequences that exhibit high feature similarity to positive samples, thereby markedly enhancing the reliability of the prediction results.

By fine-tuning pre-trained attention matrices, TranTCR-map converts the interaction patterns implicitly learned by TranTCR-bind into biologically interpretable mappings. A dataset of 128 complex structures from the STCRDab database was utilized for supervised training, enabling the model to predict spatial distances and contact probabilities between TCR and epitope residue pairs. Owing to the scarcity of structural data, a transfer learning strategy was adopted to migrate large-scale sequence-level knowledge to the residue-level prediction task. Through the refinement of attention maps, the model accurately captured binding topological rules (Fig. S9B and C).

Methods

Dataset

The TCR–pMHC interaction data utilized in this study were integrated from five primary public repositories: VDJdb, IEDB, McPAS-TCR, ImmuneCODE, and 10× Genomics. To ensure rigorous evaluation of performance, data from VDJdb, McPAS-TCR, and 10× Genomics were aggregated to form the training set. Independent test sets were constructed using the non-overlapping segments of the IEDB and ImmuneCODE databases. Furthermore, to evaluate the generalization capability of the model, five external benchmarking datasets were incorporated: the NetTCR 2.0 (MIRA) test set (comprising 192 HLA-A*02:01-restricted samples), the Justina et al. [35] validation set, the pMTnet test set, and curated TCR–pMHC structures from the PDB and TBAdb databases.

The raw data from VDJdb were updated as of 28 December 2025. Analysis of the epitope types revealed that the samples predominantly encompass human tumor-associated antigens and various viral-derived antigens (Fig. S1). Negative sample generation strategy

With the exception of the 10× Genomics repository, public databases predominantly record confirmed positive binding events. Consequently, constructing a discriminative prediction model necessitates the generation of high-quality negative samples through computational means. The quality of negative samples directly determines the generalization capability and robustness of the model. Initially, a conventional random mismatching method was adopted as a baseline strategy. For each known epitope in the database, 10 sequences were randomly selected from the CDR3 sequence pool for mismatched pairing, excluding known positive pairs. This method constructed non-binding pairs through random combinations and adjusted the distribution of positive and negative samples at ratios of 1:1 or 1:5. However, experimental observations revealed that relying solely on random mismatching may introduce potential false negative labels due to TCR cross-reactivity. Randomly generated “negative samples” may exhibit biological binding activity, thereby increasing the difficulty of training and limiting the learning capacity of the model. As a robust complementary strategy, this study adopted a combined approach based on junctional region-directed random mutation and experimental reference. To construct the VMX training set, random substitutions were introduced at no fewer than two amino acid positions within the hypervariable region (junctional region) of the CDR3 to disrupt the original binding motifs. In this study, the training set generated using this strategy is designated as VMX. Previous studies [35] have confirmed that mutations involving two or more key residues typically result in the loss of binding affinity. Negative samples generated through this method exhibit high similarity in sequence features to positive samples, compelling the model to learn more subtle binding rules. Subsequently, these mutant negative samples were integrated with real experimental negative pairs (UMI <10) extracted from the 10× Genomics single-cell sequencing data to construct a multi-dimensional negative sample pool.

Research findings indicate that a singular reliance on 10× Genomics experimental data leads to a significant deviation in the CDR3 sequence distribution between positive and negative samples. Such a bias simplifies the classification task, causing the model to recognize only statistical features rather than fundamental binding laws. The introduction of directed mutations effectively constrains the distribution of negative samples, making them more closely resemble positive samples in terms of sequence features. This approach significantly enhances the ability of the model to distinguish minute sequence differences. Furthermore, to ensure the objectivity of the evaluation and to avoid data leakage, strict physical isolation of negative samples between the training and test sets was enforced (Fig. 2A). By simultaneously utilizing the junctional region-directed random mutation pool and the 10× Genomics negative sample pool, the VMX training set provides a more robust and biologically meaningful data foundation for the model to capture core binding features.

Independent test set

This study used the ImmuneCODE and IEDB databases to construct 12 independent test sets. To objectively evaluate the performance of the model across diverse generalization scenarios, test samples were categorized into three evaluation dimensions based on the degree of overlap with the training set. The first dimension is epitope-level independence, where the antigenic epitope sequences are entirely unseen during the training phase (unseen epitopes). This dimension aims to assess the zero-shot prediction capability of the model regarding emerging antigens or mutated epitopes. The second dimension is CDR3-level independence, which ensures that the TCR chain sequences within the test set do not appear in the training set, thereby verifying the robustness of the model when encountering novel T-cell clonotypes. The final dimension is strictly unseen pairs, where neither the epitopes nor the CDR3 sequences are present in the training set. This requires the model to perform inference on entirely unknown TCR–pMHC combinations, validating whether the model has truly mastered the underlying physical and chemical laws of molecular interaction. Based on these dimensions, 12 independent test sub-datasets were constructed, encompassing various positive-to-negative sample ratios (1:1 and 1:5) as well as permutations of epitope-level, CDR3-level, and dual-level independence. To ensure the rigor of the experimental design and prevent data leakage, two physically isolated resource pools were established during the negative sample generation phase: a training negative sample pool and a test negative sample pool. All negative samples were independently sampled from their respective pools, ensuring that the negative samples in the test set do not overlap with the training set at the sequence level (Fig. 2A).

Despite the lack of overlap with the positive samples of the training data, the negative datasets were generated using known sequence pairs involving epitopes from the ImmuneCODE and IEDB databases. Consequently, the negative data within the training set and the independent test sets constructed from these sources typically follow a similar distribution. This distributional consistency may inherently reduce the difficulty of prediction. To eliminate the impact of distributional homogeneity in the negative data between the training set and the ImmuneCODE or IEDB-derived test sets, several external independent experimental datasets were constructed. These include the Justina dataset, MIRA datasets, TBAdb dataset, and the PDB dataset.

Model architecture

The proposed framework comprises two distinct modules, each tailored to a specific prediction task. The first module, TranTCR-bind, is designed to predict the binding between the CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} and epitope, estimating binding probabilities at the sequence level. The second module, TranTCR-map, focuses on characterizing pairwise residue interactions, specifically predicting residue distances and contact scores between CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} and epitope residues. Both versions are based on the Transformer network framework, accepting CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} and epitope sequences as inputs. TranTCR-bind predicts binding probabilities from these sequences. Subsequently, transfer learning utilizing crystal structure data is employed to further train the model, enabling the prediction of residue-level interactions. TranTCR-map is fine-tuned based on the representations learned by TranTCR-bind, with adjustments to the network architecture and label types to meet the specific requirements of the residue interaction task. This transfer learning strategy mitigates the data scarcity issue inherent in residue interaction datasets by leveraging the large-scale knowledge acquired from the sequence-level binding task.

Validation settings

Building upon previous studies [38] on epitope and TCR binding prediction, five-fold cross-validation (CV) and independent test set evaluation were employed. During the training phase, five-fold CV was utilized to evaluate and optimize the model. The dataset was partitioned into five mutually exclusive subsets. In each iteration, four subsets were designated for training, while the remaining subset functioned as the test set. The model was trained on the four training subsets and evaluated on the test subset. This procedure was repeated five times, ensuring that each subset served as the test set exactly once. The final performance metrics were derived by averaging the results across all iterations. This methodology provides a comprehensive evaluation of model performance and mitigates potential bias associated with reliance on a single training–testing split. Furthermore, during model selection and hyperparameter tuning, five-fold CV offered reliable performance assessments for various model configurations, facilitating the selection of optimal hyperparameters while preventing overfitting to a specific data partition.

Conclusion

The accurate prediction of TCR and pMHC interactions remains a cornerstone of computational immunology. While significant progress has been achieved through diverse algorithmic approaches, the field continues to refine strategies for addressing data sparsity and the inherent complexity of immune recognition. This study presents the TranTCR framework as a complementary approach to existing methodologies, integrating a specialized negative sampling strategy with a multi-scale Transformer architecture to enhance the interpretability of these interactions.

A primary observation of this research is the potential for further optimizing negative sample quality to better define the generalization boundaries of a model. Conventional methods, such as random mismatching, have provided foundational datasets for the development of many successful predictors. However, this study suggests that incorporating the junctional region-directed mutation strategy can serve as an effective supplement to these established techniques. By constructing near-native negative samples that introduce targeted mutations within the hypervariable junctional region while preserving the conserved germline framework, the model is provided with an additional layer of granularity. The 2–5-residue mutation gradient was chosen to balance sufficient perturbation with preservation of the near-native CDR3 context. A single-residue mutation may be insufficient to disrupt binding-related motifs, whereas excessive substitutions may distort the junctional-region sequence context and generate less biologically realistic negatives. Although a fixed mutation gradient of 2–5 residues performed well in our benchmark setting, future work may further refine this strategy by adopting proportion-based mutation schemes to better account for CDR3 length heterogeneity.

The development of TranTCR-map further extends the utility of the framework by offering structural interpretability. Given the inherent scarcity of high-resolution crystallographic data, a transfer learning strategy was implemented to bridge the gap between sequence abundance and structural paucity. This approach leverages the global binding representations acquired by TranTCR-bind from large-scale sequence datasets, effectively initializing the structural characterization task. Consequently, the model compensates for the limitations imposed by the small sample size of the structural dataset by utilizing the latent physicochemical features learned during the sequence-level training. The concordance between the predicted contact maps and available crystallographic data suggests that the attention mechanism can provide valuable supplementary insights into the physical center of the interaction within the CDR3 junctional region. Furthermore, the mutagenesis analysis conducted via TranTCR-map offers an additional framework for investigating how specific residue substitutions influence binding affinity. The observation that targeted mutations at key residues correlate with increased spatial distances provides a computational perspective that can assist in the preliminary screening of TCR-based immunotherapies. Such tools may serve as useful adjuncts in the identification of neoantigens [39], where understanding the impact of single amino acid changes is critical for therapeutic design.

Despite the progress made in enhancing the robustness of the prediction, several limitations in the current evaluation system and generation strategy are acknowledged. Primarily, the proposed negative sample generation strategy relies heavily on the coverage of the existing repertoire of known epitopes. Since directed random mutations are derived from confirmed positive CDR3–epitope sequence pairs, the feature space of the generated negative samples remains constrained by the variety of epitopes present in current experimental data. If specific types of epitopes are underrepresented in existing public databases, the discriminative ability of the model may be affected by potential sampling bias when processing epitopes with significantly different physicochemical properties. In addition, although the present study adopted random substitutions within the junctional region to construct near-native negatives, future work may explore substitution strategies guided by physicochemical constraints or amino acid substitution matrices such as BLOSUM62. Such approaches may further improve the biological realism of generated negatives, although in our preliminary tests they did not show a clear performance advantage over the current strategy. A further limitation of TranTCR-map is that its residue-level interaction modeling remains constrained by the limited availability of experimentally resolved TCR-epitope complex structures. Although transfer learning from TranTCR-bind partially alleviates this problem, the current fine-tuning dataset is still relatively small, and the structural generalizability of the map module has not yet been validated on a large scale. Therefore, the contact and distance predictions generated by TranTCR-map should be interpreted as biologically informed approximations rather than definitive structural reconstructions. Future work should perform broader benchmarking using expanded experimental structure collections.

Furthermore, immune recognition is an exceptionally complex biophysical process. Although the present work mainly focuses on CDR3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\beta$\end{document} to ensure broader epitope coverage and stronger statistical support from currently available public datasets, we acknowledge that this design may not fully capture the synergistic contribution of paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} chains in antigen recognition. We have therefore provided a version of TranTCR, i.e. compatible with paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document}-chain inputs. However, comprehensive investigation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document}-mediated recognition remains limited by the scarcity of high-quality paired-chain data. In future work, the integration of larger paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha \beta$\end{document} TCR datasets, broader antigenic peptide libraries, and multimodal three-dimensional conformational information may further improve the framework’s ability to model rare or previously unseen epitope spaces and better reflect the complexity of TCR-pMHC recognition in biological systems.

In conclusion, the TranTCR framework is presented as a supportive tool toward the digital characterization of immune recognition. By combining a biologically grounded data strategy with an interpretable architecture, this work aims to provide a reliable and supplementary resource for high-throughput TCR repertoire screening and the rational design of immune interventions, contributing to the collective progress of the computational immunology community.
