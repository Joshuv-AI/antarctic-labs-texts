Central Message

Transplantation of an anatomically normal heart into a bilateral SVC recipient can be performed without additional anastomoses, reducing warm ischemic time, bleeding, and anastomotic complications.

Persistent left superior vena cava (LSVC) is a rare venous anomaly resulting from failed involution of an embryonic vessel.1 The left internal jugular and left subclavian veins thereby join to pass through the left mediastinum, most commonly draining into the coronary sinus (CS). During orthotopic heart transplantation in patients with LSVC, one must consider how to reestablish LSVC drainage. Conventional techniques have required an additional anastomosis to the donor innominate vein, right superior vena cava (SVC), or right atrial appendage.2, 3, 4 We present a successful case utilizing a technique that preserves LSVC drainage into the CS, thereby sparing an additional anastomosis.

Methods

The described patient provided informed written consent for the publication of this case. Institutional review board approval was not required for this report.

A 37-year-old man with nonischemic cardiomyopathy and ejection fraction of 15% to 20% was transferred to our institution for heart transplant evaluation, where he was found to have a persistent LSVC, which was not unroofed, in addition to his right-sided SVC (Figure 1). Surgical Technique

The patient was placed on cardiopulmonary bypass utilizing bicaval venous cannulation (Video 1). Additionally, the LSVC was cannulated and connected to the venous return. Modified bicaval cardiectomy was performed after aortic crossclamp and snaring the SVC, inferior vena cava (IVC) and LSVC. During preparation of the IVC cuff, care was taken to include the CS ostium. The CS was then dissected off the atrioventricular groove up to the LSVC. The CS was pressurized and venous collateral branches arising from the CS were ligated with surgical clips. The donor heart was then transplanted into the recipient in the following sequence: donor left atrium to recipient left atrial cuff, donor IVC to recipient IVC cuff (including the CS in situ), donor pulmonary artery to recipient pulmonary artery, donor aorta to recipient aorta, and SVC to right atrium. Warm ischemic time was 52 minutes. In its final position, the coronary sinus traveled posterior to the transplanted heart, draining in situ to the LSVC. The patient easily weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass (total time 206 minutes) and laminar flow was confirmed in the LSVC and CS on transesophageal echocardiogram.

Results

The patient extubated on postoperative day (POD) 1. Echocardiogram demonstrated normal cardiac function and normal flow through the IVC. He received standard steroid induction therapy and immunosuppression, with no evidence of rejection on endomyocardial biopsy. He downgraded from the intensive care unit on POD 10 and was discharged to home on POD 18. Computed tomography angiography of the chest via the left upper extremity 3 months later showed a patent LSVC draining into the CS (Figure 2). Conclusions

Previous reports of transplanted LSVC patients have largely described those with isolated unilateral LSVC or with differing anatomy from that of our recipient. In those with comparable anatomy, LSVC drainage was maintained with additional anastomoses and often prosthetic materials, which has generally required rerouting techniques employing the donor innominate vein or autologous/artificial grafts.2, 3, 4

We demonstrate a technique in which we preserved the CS with the IVC cuff, requiring no extra anastomosis. This approach was reported once more than 20 years ago with a successful outcome.5 This technique may reduce bleeding potential, warm ischemia time, and potentially primary graft dysfunction. Furthermore, leaving the LSVC intact with its native drainage may avoid venous anastomotic complications such as stenosis or thrombosis.
