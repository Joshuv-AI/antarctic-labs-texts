Pain and frailty progression in adult survivors of childhood cancer: a report from the St Jude Lifetime Cohort study
Authors: Cassie M Argenbright, Chiara Papini, Zhenghong Li, Matthew J Ehrhardt, Angela Delaney, Robyn E Partin, Deokumar Srivastava, Melissa M Hudson, Kirsten K Ness, Tara M Brinkman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAgeing survivors of childhood cancer experience pain that substantially affects daily activities and health. We investigated the contribution of pain to frailty development or progression in survivors.MethodsIn this analysis of the St Jude Lifetime Cohort (SJLIFE), adult survivors of childhood cancer treated at a single institution in the USA completed a a baseline assessment including a frailty assessment at cohort entry and a follow-up frailty assessment 4–6 years later. Eligible participants were between ages 18 years and 45 years and were at least 10 years from their childhood cancer diagnosis at cohort entry. Women who were pregnant and participants currently receiving treatment for cancer were not eligible. Criteria for frailty were low lean muscle mass, muscle weakness, poor endurance, slow walking speed, and exhaustion; pre-frailty was defined as meeting two of the criteria, and frailty was defined as meeting three or more. Survivors self-reported pain (bodily pain and headache or migraine pain), pain interference, and health behaviours (including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and sedentary behaviour). Outcomes were analysed in participants who completed clinical assessments at both baseline and follow-up. Multinomial logistic regression examined associations between pain and frailty (odds ratios [ORs] and 95% CIs reported).Findings1261 adult survivors of childhood cancer recruited for SJLIFE between Jan 1, 2008, and June 30, 2013 completed clinical assessments at baseline (mean age 30·7 years [SD 7·0]; mean time since diagnosis 22·7 years [7·4]) and follow-up (mean time from baseline 5·2 years [0·7]). At baseline, 471 (37·4%) participants reported pain, with 194 (15·4%) also reporting pain interference; 294 (23·3%) participants were pre-frail or frail. From baseline to follow-up, 262 (20·8%) survivors had persistent pain, 211 (16·7%) had increasing pain, and 370 (29·3%) had persistent or progressive frailty. After adjusting for age, sex, race and ethnicity, time since diagnosis, BMI, anxiety, depression, and chronic health conditions, pain with interference was associated with pre-frailty (OR 1·85, 95% CI 1·19–2·88) and frailty (5·66, 2·75–11·68) at baseline and with persistent (3·44, 1·88–6·31) and progressed frailty (2·09, 1·25–3·49). Persistent (1·70, 1·11–2·61) and increasing pain (1·69, 1·09–2·61) from baseline to follow-up were associated with progressed frailty. Physical activity and sedentary behaviour were associated with frailty across models.InterpretationPain that interferes with daily functioning is associated with frailty and frailty progression in survivors of childhood cancer, underscoring the need for interventions targeting pain and lifestyle factors to mitigate frailty risk and improve long-term health outcomes.

Introduction

Advancements in treatment have resulted in a growing population of long-term survivors of childhood cancer.1 Despite improved survival rates, survivors continue to be at risk of developing disease-related and treatment-related late effects.2 Frailty, a phenotype characterised by diminished physiological capacity and heightened vulnerability to stressors, is observed in young adult survivors of childhood cancer at rates similar to those in adults several decades older without a cancer history.3,4 Among childhood cancer survivors, studies have identified concurrent and longitudinal risk factors for frailty, including demographic characteristics, chronic health conditions, and health behaviours.5,6 A previous study6 showed that the prevalence of frail health among survivors of childhood cancer doubled in as few as 5 years, and accelerated ageing observed in survivors has been linked to a heightened risk of frailty and early mortality.7 These findings underscore frailty as a dynamic, progressive outcome in survivorship, emphasising the importance of identifying early, potentially modifiable risk factors.

In the general ageing population, pain has been consistently linked to both concurrent8,9 and future10,11 frailty, suggesting that it might contribute to functional decline in older adults. Up to 40% of adult survivors of childhood cancer report chronic pain.12 In general, pain is more prevalent in childhood cancer survivors than in individuals without a cancer history12–15 and often worsens in severity and functional impact over time.14 Despite the high prevalence of pain and the fact that survivors are at risk of early-onset frailty, no studies have examined pain as a potential risk factor for frailty in this population. Therefore, here, we sought to cross-sectionally and longitudinally evaluate associations between pain and frailty in adult survivors of childhood cancer and explore the potential contribution of health behaviours to the associations between pain and frailty in this population.

Methods

Study participants

In this analysis of the St Jude Lifetime Cohort (SJLIFE),16 adult survivors of childhood cancer treated at a single institution in the USA (St Jude Children’s Research Hospital, Memphis, TN, USA) completed a baseline assessment including a frailty assessment at cohort entry (2008–13) and a follow-up frailty assessment 4–6 years later (2014–18). Procedures for SJLIFE and the follow-up frailty study have been described previously.3,6,16 Eligibility criteria for the present analysis included enrolment in SJLIFE; completion of a baseline assessment between the ages of 18 years and 45 years; 10 years from childhood cancer diagnosis at baseline assessment; and completion of a follow-up frailty assessment. Women who were pregnant and individuals currently receiving treatment for cancer were not eligible. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at St Jude Children’s Research Hospital (reference numbers SJLIFE and 00004536). All participants provided written informed consent.

Assessment of pain and pain interference

Bodily pain was measured through a single item from the Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item Short Form Survey (MOS SF-36),17 and survivors were considered to have bodily pain if they reported moderate, severe, or very severe bodily pain (vs none, very mild, or mild). Headache or migraine pain was defined as survivors indicating that they had been told by a doctor or other health-care professional that they had or had previously had migraines and/or other severe headaches, and the condition was still present (vs never having had migraines or headaches or having had migraines or headaches in the past but no longer having them). Pain interference was measured using a single SF-36 item (ie, during the past 4 weeks, how much did pain interfere with your normal work [including both work outside the home and housework]?). Survivors were considered to have pain interference if they reported moderate, quite a bit, or extreme daily interference (vs none or a little bit). The SF-36 shows strong reliability (scales >0·80 and summary scores >0·90), with a validated two-factor structure explaining 80–85% of reliable variance across populations.17 This approach conceptualises pain primarily as a self-reported experience, capturing both symptom severity and functional impact, rather than relying on physician-diagnosed conditions.

At baseline and follow-up, pain outcomes were classified as no pain (no moderate-to-very severe bodily pain, headaches, or migraines, with minimal interference or without interference), pain without interference (moderate-to-very severe bodily pain, headaches, or migraines, with minimal or without interference), and pain with interference (moderate-to-very severe bodily pain, or headaches or migraines, with moderate-to-extreme interference). Survivors reporting pain interference but not pain at either timepoint were excluded due to potential misclassification.

Changes in pain from baseline to follow-up were classified as follows: no or decreasing pain, including no pain at both timepoints, decreased pain without interference, decreased pain with interference, or persistent pain with decreased interference; persistent pain, including persistent pain with or without interference; and increasing pain, including persistent pain with increased interference, or increased pain with or without increased interference.

Assessment of frailty

Baseline and follow-up frailty assessments were completed at St Jude Children’s Research Hospital. Participants were classified at each timepoint using the Fried frailty criteria.18 The criteria were as follows: low lean muscle mass, assessed by dual x-ray absorptiometry and defined as 1·5 SDs or less below age-specific, sex-specific, and race-specific Z scores; exhaustion (MOS SF-36 vitality T-score ≤1·3 SDs lower than the population mean); low energy expenditure (<383 kcal per week for men and <270 kcal per week for women, assessed through a physical activity questionnaire at baseline and accelerometry at follow-up); slowness (≥7 s to walk 15 feet for women <159 cm and men <173 cm or ≥6 s for taller individuals); and weakness (hand-grip strength using a dynamometer with sex-specific and BMI-specific cut points; appendix p 3).3 Participants were classified as pre-frail if they met two criteria and frail if they met three or more of the criteria.

Frailty progression from baseline to follow-up was classified as follows: stable healthy, for survivors who were not frail at baseline or follow-up; improved, for survivors whose frailty status changed from frail to pre-frail or non-frail or from pre-frail to non-frail; persistent-frail, for survivors who remained pre-frail or frail at both timepoints; and progressed, for survivors who worsened from non-frail to pre-frail or from pre-frail to frail.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included frailty status, assessed cross-sectionally at baseline, and frailty progression, assessed longitudinally from baseline to follow-up. Outcomes were analysed in participants who completed pain and frailty assessments at both baseline and follow-up.

Demographics, disease, treatment, chronic conditions, emotional health, and health behaviours

Demographic variables included sex, age, and race or ethnicity (White, non-Hispanic; Hispanic; Black; and other). Diagnosis and treatment information was abstracted from medical records. Anxiety and depression were assessed using Brief Symptom Inventory-18 subscales, with T-scores of 63 or higher indicating elevated symptoms.19 A modification of the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 4.03) was applied to clinical, laboratory, medical record, questionnaire, and mortality data to ascertain graded chronic disease status.20 Chronic health conditions were graded as mild (grade 1), moderate (grade 2), severe and/or disabling (grade 3), life-threatening (grade 4), or death (grade 5). Organ systems assessed included cardiac, pulmonary, musculoskeletal, neurological, and endocrine systems.

Self-reported health behaviours included smoking status, alcohol consumption, and sedentary behaviour. The definition of smoking status was consistent with standard public health classifications.21 Current smokers were those who had smoked 100 or more cigarettes in their lifetime and had smoked in the past month; former smokers had smoked 100 or more cigarettes in their lifetime but not in the past month; and non-smokers had never smoked or had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Alcohol use was classified according to guidelines established by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism.22 Heavy drinking was defined as binge drinking (ie, consuming four or more alcoholic drinks for women, or five or more alcoholic drinks for men) on 5 days or more in the past month; risky drinking was defined as having more than three alcoholic drinks per day or more than seven per week, for women, and as having more than four alcoholic drinks per day or more than 14 per week, for men. Survivors classified as engaging in heavy or risky drinking met either criterion. Sedentary behaviour was dichotomised as 4 h or less or more than 4 h of daily sitting, television watching, or computer use over the past 30 days. Physical activity at baseline was self-reported and measured as metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity in a typical week. Physical activity at follow-up was measured by accelerometry in minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity over 1 week.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics compared demographic and treatment characteristics across baseline frailty statuses using Kruskal–Wallis, Fisher’s exact, or χ2 tests. Elastic net regularisation with ten-fold cross-validation (GLMNET package, R) was applied to identify a reduced set of frailty predictors while maintaining model interpretability. Pain and demographic covariates (age, sex, and race or ethnicity) were forced into all models. Candidate covariates included BMI, time since diagnosis, health behaviours, anxiety and depression, and either cancer diagnosis, treatment exposures, or chronic health conditions grade 2 or worse. Separate models accounted for the overlapping influence of diagnosis and treatment, with chronic health conditions lying on the causal pathway to frailty outcomes. Covariates at baseline and follow-up were included in cross-sectional and longitudinal models, respectively. Variables selected via elastic net were subsequently included in multinomial logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for associations between baseline pain and frailty status, baseline pain and frailty progression, and changes in pain and frailty progression. Models were run with and without adjustment for health behaviours to evaluate their influence on observed associations. Analyses were conducted on complete cases using SAS 9.4 and R for elastic net.23

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

1261 survivors of childhood cancer recruited for the SJLIFE baseline visit between Jan 1, 2008, and June 30, 2013, were included in the final analytic sample (37 participants reporting interference but not pain were excluded; appendix p 2). Selection bias analyses between participants and non-participants are reported in the appendix (pp 16–19). 635 (50·4%) participants were male, 626 (49·6%) were female, and most participants were White, non-Hispanic (1040 [82·5%]). Mean participant age was 30·7 (SD 7·0) years at baseline, and mean time from diagnosis was 22·7 years (7·4). The mean time from baseline to follow-up was 5·2 years (0·7). The most common cancer diagnoses included leukaemia (489 [38·8%]), lymphoma (244 [19·3%]), and sarcoma (135 [10·7%]). 416 (33·2%) of 1254 participants had received cranial radiation, and the most common chemotherapy agents included vincristine (875 [69·4%]), anthracyclines (771 [61·1%)], and alkylators (727 [57·7%]). Demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample by frailty status at baseline are provided in table 1.

At baseline, 471 (37·4%) participants reported pain, either without (277 [22·0%]) or with (194 [15·4%]) interference. 228 [18·1%] participants were pre-frail and 66 (5·2%) were frail at baseline (appendix pp 4, 5–6).

The prevalence of pain with interference at baseline was higher in pre-frail (62 [27·2%] of 228) or frail (26 [39·4%] of 66) participants than in non-frail participants (106 [11·0%] of 967; p<0·0001; appendix p 4). In multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, race or ethnicity, time since diagnosis, BMI, anxiety, depression, and chronic health conditions, pain with interference at baseline was associated with pre-frail (OR 1·85, 95% CI 1·19–2·88) and frail (5·66, 2·75–11·68) status (figure). These associations were maintained when health behaviours were controlled for in the model (table 2), as well as in analyses adjusting for demographic and anthropometric characteristics and emotional health, or for cancer diagnosis and treatment, with and without health behaviours (appendix pp 7–9).

262 (20·8%) childhood cancer survivors reported persistent pain from baseline to follow-up, and 211 (16·7%) reported increased pain; most survivors (759 [60·2%]) maintained a stable non-frail status from baseline to follow-up (appendix p 10). 125 (9·9%) participants remained persistently frail, and 245 (19·4%) underwent frailty progression.

After adjusting for age, sex, race or ethnicity, time since diagnosis, BMI, anxiety, depression, and chronic health conditions at follow-up, survivors who reported pain with interference at baseline were more likely to have persistent frailty (OR 3·44, 95% CI 1·88–6·31) or progressed frailty (2·09, 1·25–3·49) from baseline to follow-up compared with survivors who were never frail; these associations remained significant after controlling for health behaviours (table 3; figure). Persistent pain (1·70, 1·11–2·61) or increasing pain (1·69, 1·09–2·61) from baseline to follow-up was associated with frailty progression; the association for increasing pain persisted after adjusting for health behaviours (table 4; figure). Models adjusted for only demographic characteristics, anthropometric characteristics, and emotional health or for cancer diagnosis and treatment (with and without health behaviours) are reported in the appendix (pp 11–15). Overall, findings were consistent across models, with associations between pain and frailty progression remaining directionally similar and largely robust to adjustment for demographic factors, emotional health, chronic health conditions, cancer-related factors, and health behaviours.

In multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, race or ethnicity, time since diagnosis, BMI, anxiety, depression, and chronic health conditions, health behaviours were significantly associated with frailty in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. In analyses evaluating the association between pain and frailty at baseline (table 2), each increase of 30 MET min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity was associated with a reduction in risk of 2% for pre-frailty (OR 0·98, 95% CI 0·98–0·99) and 5% for frailty (0·95, 0·92–0·97), whereas 4 h or more per day of sedentary behaviour was associated with increased odds of pre-frailty (1·65, 1·16–2·35) but not frailty. Heavy or risky drinking was associated with decreased odds of pre-frailty (0·65, 0·45–0·94) and frailty (0·32, 0·15–0·69).

In longitudinal models evaluating associations between baseline pain status and frailty progression (table 3), each 30-min increase in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity was associated with a reduction in risk of more than 30% for both persistent (OR 0·58, 95% CI 0·48–0·69) and progressed frailty (0·68, 0·61–0·75). 4 h or more per day of sedentary behaviour was associated with increased odds of persistent (1·92, 1·12–3·28) and progressed frailty (1·78, 1·21–2·61).

In longitudinal models examining changes in pain in relation to frailty progression (table 4), increases in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity remained protective for both persistent (OR 0·57, 95% CI 0·47–0·68) and progressed frailty (0·68, 0·61–0·75). Similarly, 4 h or more per day of sedentary behaviour was associated with increased odds of both persistent (2·14, 1·26–3·63) and progressed frailty (1·87, 1·27–2·74).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to show that pain is associated with frailty in survivors of childhood cancer while also identifying health behaviours that might contribute to this association, providing a novel insight into potentially modifiable risk factors influencing frailty progression in this population. Our results include several important findings. First, survivors of childhood cancer who had pain with interference had a more than six-fold increased risk of frailty, alongside an increased risk of persistent and progressive frailty over a 5-year period. Second, nearly 40% of survivors reported persistent or worsening pain, which was associated with a doubling of the risk of frailty progression. Third, physical activity and sedentary behaviour emerged as consistent contributors to the association between pain and frailty. These findings suggest that pain might independently contribute to frailty in survivors, highlighting the need for pain management and lifestyle interventions to potentially slow frailty progression.

Pain is prevalent among childhood cancer survivors,12,14 and approximately 37% of our sample reported moderate-to-severe pain, either with or without moderate-to-extreme interference with daily functioning. We observed novel associations between pain and frailty, showing that survivors with pain are at elevated risk of being pre-frail and frail. These associations remained robust after adjustment for chronic health conditions and health behaviours, suggesting that pain affects frailty status and progression independent of overall comorbidity burden and lifestyle factors. Nonetheless, chronic health conditions attenuated the observed associations between pain and frailty, underscoring the importance of overall health status to the development of frailty.

Interestingly, our results suggest that pain with interference might be a stronger risk factor for pre-frailty and frailty in survivors than risk factors previously identified among paediatric, adolescent, and young adult cancer survivors, including smoking,5,24 obesity,5,24 and sedentary behaviours.6 These findings align with research in older adults, for whom chronic pain and pain interference have been identified as significant predictors of concurrent and subsequent frailty.8–11 These similar associations were observed in samples with mean ages of 82 years in the general population compared with 30 years in our sample of childhood cancer survivors.9 Taken together, these parallels suggest that pain-related functional impairment in survivors might reflect processes typically observed later in the life course, consistent with patterns of advanced phenotypic ageing reported in childhood cancer survivors.

Survivors who had persistent or increasing pain over time had a nearly two-fold increased risk of frailty progression at 5-year follow-up. Depression was consistently associated with frailty, reinforcing its role as a marker of reduced physiological reserve.9,24,25 After adjusting for health behaviours, persistent pain no longer predicted frailty progression, whereas the depression–frailty link was strengthened, suggesting overlapping behavioural and emotional risk captured by depression rather than a reduced role for pain. This finding highlights pain as a frailty risk factor and supports interventions addressing both pain and its mental health comorbidities, such as the non-pharmacological gold standard treatment approach of cognitive behavioural therapy,26 to slow frailty progression.

There is some evidence that pain and frailty share mechanistic properties,27 and clinical research suggests that pain might predispose to the development of reduced physiological reserve.11,28 Biologically, frailty onset is hypothesised to inhibit endogenous central pain modulation, contributing to heightened pain. Both pain and frailty are associated with increased cortisol, reflecting dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis.28 Chronic pain and reduced physiological reserve are also linked to inflammatory processes, with preclinical studies highlighting overlapping mechanisms, including alterations in pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers, cytokine release, microglial activation, DNA damage, hormonal function, oxidative stress, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor.28 Although this evidence supports a bidirectional link between pain and frailty in other populations,8,28 it remains unclear whether accelerated ageing mechanisms link pain to poor outcomes in childhood cancer survivors. Cancer therapy exposures in survivors have been shown to increase the risk of both pain14,15 and frailty,3,5 potentially via overlap in damage to physiological systems caused by toxic exposures during key developmental periods. In our analyses, established risk factors such as CNS tumour diagnosis and cranial radiation3,5,6 were associated with frailty, as were pulmonary, musculoskeletal, and neurological conditions.3 However, these diagnosis and treatment effects were largely attenuated after accounting for chronic health conditions and health behaviours, suggesting that lifestyle factors during survivorship exert a proximal, potentially modifiable influence on frailty progression.

Health behaviours were independently associated with frailty status among survivors. Across models, physical activity and sedentary behaviour were consistently associated with frailty outcomes. For each 30-min increase in weekly physical activity, the risk of frailty progression and persistent frailty decreased by 30–40%, consistent with previous findings in long-term survivors.6,25 Regarding changes in pain over time, 4 h or more per day of sedentary behaviour was associated with both progressed and persistent frailty. Notably, depression has been consistently linked to frailty,9,24,25 is highly comorbid with pain,14,15 and is linked to lower physical activity,9,29 suggesting that these factors might interact to compound frailty risk.8,11 Studies in older adults with pain support these findings, as physical activity might mitigate these relationships by enhancing physiological reserve.29 Interestingly, heavy or risky drinking was associated with lower odds of frailty. Although similar trends have been reported previously,3 no consistent protective effect of alcohol on frailty has been established in childhood cancer survivors, likely reflecting residual confounding by unmeasured behavioural, health, or social factors. Overall, these results highlight physical activity and sedentary behaviour as key targets for slowing frailty progression and improving quality of life in pain-affected survivors, consistent with findings in ageing populations.29

Our study has limitations that warrant consideration. Included survivors were slightly older, further from diagnosis, and had higher cranial radiation and platinum exposure than survivors who were missing follow-up pain data. Additionally, follow-up clinic attendees might have differed systematically from non-participants, potentially affecting frailty prevalence and generalisability. The absence of longitudinal data for controls limited our ability to compare pain–frailty trajectories between survivors and non-cancer populations; future longitudinal studies with a control cohort will be crucial for contextualising pain and frailty progression in childhood cancer survivors. Pain and pain interference were assessed via the SF-36, capturing self-reported severity and functional impact but not physician-diagnosed conditions, biopsychosocial features, or specific interference domains—all of which could influence pain perception and engagement in physical activity and provide more nuanced insight into survivors’ experiences. Several variables, including health behaviours, were self-reported and might have been subject to bias, potentially affecting the observed associations. Future studies should evaluate these factors to clarify their contributions to frailty progression in survivors.

In conclusion, this study identified associations between pain and frailty progression in survivors of childhood cancer. Persistent and worsening pain were associated with increased frailty over time, and health behaviours, such as sedentary time and physical activity, were also associated with frailty. Notably, these patterns mirror those observed in geriatric populations, despite the relatively young age of our survivor cohort. To our knowledge, no interventions have specifically addressed pain management in pre-frail or frail survivors.9 These findings underscore the need for interventions and lifestyle modifications for managing pain and frailty progression in this population.
