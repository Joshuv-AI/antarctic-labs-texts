Sex-related differences in antimicrobial dosing for sepsis: Bridging the equity gap
Authors: Helena Barrasa, Goiatz Balziskueta, Jordi Rello
Published: 2025

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a major challenge in critical care and a leading cause of mortality worldwide. Early, appropriate antibiotic administration is among the most effective strategies to improve outcomes, making optimization of antimicrobial therapy a priority. Optimal antibiotic therapy is the careful selection of the agent and dosing regimen that optimizes outcomes while minimizing adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance. Yet, paradoxically, dosing regimens in sepsis often rely on standardized protocols that overlook fundamental biological variables such as sex and gender, as well as intersecting factors like socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and access to care.[1] This omission compromises efficacy and perpetuates clinical inequalities.

Sepsis induces profound physiological disturbances that affect drug pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD).[2] An increased volume of distribution, hypoalbuminemia, altered tissue perfusion, and dynamic changes in renal and hepatic function are common. Consequently, achieving more-aggressive therapeutic targets—such as maintaining β-lactam antibiotic concentrations above the minimum inhibitory concentration for 100 % of the dosing interval—is now recommended, with plasma levels four to five times higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration necessary in the most severe infections.[3] Failure to adjust dosing strategies to these pathophysiological realities is associated with increased mortality and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. In response, recent pharmacological research is beginning to integrate sex as a covariate in PK/PD models to enhance dosing precision in critically ill patients.[4]

The influence of biological sex on drug disposition is well-documented yet insufficiently integrated into clinical practice.[5] Females, compared to males, generally exhibit higher body fat percentages, lower muscle mass, and smaller organ sizes, all of which affect drug absorption, distribution, and metabolism. Hormonal differences, particularly fluctuating estrogen and progesterone levels, further modulate the activity of key metabolic enzymes such as CYP3A4.[6] These interactions may be influenced by comorbidities, especially in elderly or disadvantaged groups, with women being particularly vulnerable due to both biological factors and increased exposure to socioeconomic adversity.[1] These factors substantially alter drug concentrations and their therapeutic and toxic profiles.

Augmented renal clearance (ARC)—characterized by enhanced renal drug elimination—is a recognized phenomenon in critically ill patients. It particularly affects younger males but may also be seen in pregnant women, individuals with early-stage diabetes, and obese patients. In younger men, greater muscle mass and a higher prevalence of glomerular hyperfiltration contribute to an increased risk of underdosing. ARC accelerates the elimination of renally excreted drugs, leading to subtherapeutic exposures and treatment failure.[7] Several antibiotics, including β-lactams (such as piperacillin–tazobactam and meropenem), vancomycin, linezolid, and aminoglycosides, have been shown to frequently fail in achieving PK/PD targets in patients with ARC.[8] In parallel, commonly used equations for estimating renal function systematically underestimate the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in women, complicating dose individualization. The 2024 kidney disease improving global outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guideline notes that the Cockcroft–Gault equation, developed from a cohort excluding women and Black individuals, has persistent inaccuracies.[9] Although newer approaches like European Kidney Function Consortium (EKFC) improve estimation across sexes, challenges remain, especially due to the lower muscle mass and non-GFR determinants of serum creatinine in women.[10] These limitations highlight the need for considering alternative biomarkers to better tailor drug dosing in women with sepsis.

Beyond biological differences, gender—understood as a social construct encompassing behaviors, roles, and access to healthcare—profoundly shapes medical treatment. Gender biases in the perception and reporting of symptoms, differences in clinical interpretation, and disparities in treatment adherence have been documented.[11] Women with sepsis are less likely to receive invasive or aggressive care than men, a discrepancy that can delay appropriate treatment and influence PK indirectly by altering the timing and adequacy of therapy.[12]

These sex-related disparities also extend into acute management practices, further complicating outcomes. Women are less likely than men to receive prompt broad-spectrum antibiotics, potentially delaying effective therapy at a critical time.[13] Such delays can be partly explained by sex-based differences in symptom presentation, different rates of healthcare-seeking behavior, or unconscious gender bias in the clinical recognition of sepsis. In a large U.S. cohort, women with septic shock had higher mortality rates than men despite a similar timing of antibiotic administration, suggesting that factors beyond PK/PD—such as immune response or care disparities—may contribute.[14] Meanwhile, population PK data show higher antibiotic exposure in women under standard dosing, increasing the toxicity risk,[15] with adverse drug reactions reported up to 30 % more often in women than in men. This increased susceptibility to toxicity may contribute to worse outcomes and partially explain observed sex-based mortality differences. In line with these concerns, the World Health Organization has warned of rising drug-resistant community infections in women, often necessitating high-dose β-lactam strategies for resolution.[16] This dual vulnerability underscores the importance of incorporating sex-based considerations into antimicrobial therapy and reinforces the need for PK/PD-guided dosing and therapeutic drug monitoring to optimize safety and efficacy.

Epidemiologically, men exhibit a higher incidence of both sepsis and septic shock, with an annual relative risk approximately 1.3 times greater than that of women.[11] However, mortality patterns are more complex: a Korean study found lower 28-day and hospital mortality rates among women,[11] whereas other investigations reported that men present with significantly higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores, indicating greater organ dysfunction across multiple systems.[[17], [18]]

Sex-based differences in PK and PD are not merely theoretical; they also have substantial clinical implications. U.S. Food and Drug Administration reviews identified sex-based PK differences for about 28 % of studied drugs, and women are more likely to experience adverse drug reactions with standard dosages.[5,19] However, few medications offer sex-adjusted dosing guidelines, largely reflecting the historical underrepresentation of women in clinical research. Women were systematically excluded from drug trials due to concerns about reproductive risks and the flawed assumption that findings in men could be generalized.[19] Despite National Institutes of Health initiatives, fewer than 30 % of studies report sex-stratified outcomes, and even fewer translate findings into dosing guidelines. Regulatory frameworks remain slow to mandate sex-disaggregated analyses, delaying the integration of sex-specific knowledge into practice.

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) optimizes antimicrobial therapy in sepsis by enabling individualized, real-time dose adjustments. TDM addresses variability in drug handling caused by sex, age, critical illness, and supportive therapies. It is especially valuable in populations prone to PK alterations—including young men with ARC, pregnant women, and patients on extracorporeal support—enhancing precision and avoiding subtherapeutic exposure or toxicity. Yet, despite its importance, TDM implementation remains inconsistent, mainly due to logistical barriers. Emerging evidence supports that systematic TDM adoption improves outcomes and helps combat antimicrobial resistance.[20]

Ensuring sex and gender equity in research is vital to scientific integrity. Integrating a sex and gender perspective ensures more comprehensive and equitable results, aligning with the sex and gender equity in research guidelines, which call for the systematic consideration of sex and gender in study design, analysis, and reporting.[21]

In conclusion, recognizing and addressing sex- and gender-related differences in drug dosing represents a critical step toward improving sepsis management. Physiological and social factors significantly influence PK, PD, treatment access, and adherence, ultimately impacting clinical outcomes. Importantly, standardized dosing overlooks biological differences: while men, particularly younger patients, may face therapeutic failure from underdosing, women are more vulnerable to overexposure and adverse drug reactions. Integrating sex and gender considerations into therapeutic decision-making, enhancing the representation of women in clinical research, and systematically adopting TDM represents not only a scientific imperative but also a moral responsibility to improve outcomes across diverse patient populations.
