Pancreatogenic diabetes in immunoglobulin G4–related autoimmune pancreatitis managed with automated insulin delivery
Authors: Saraswathi Saiprasad, Theresa Cao, Narayana Swamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)-related autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) may mimic malignancy and lead to progressive pancreatic dysfunction. We describe a 75-year-old man with pancreatic mass lesions, biliary strictures, and elevated carbohydrate antigen 19-9 levels concerning for malignancy. Repeated pancreatic and biliary biopsies demonstrated chronic pancreatitis and fibrosis without carcinoma. Elevated IgG4 levels of 193.5 mg/dL (SI: 1.94 g/L; reference 4-86 mg/dL [SI: 0.04-0.86 g/L]) together with biopsy findings supported AIP. He developed severe exocrine and endocrine pancreatic dysfunction with worsening hyperglycemia and weight loss. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) increased from 7% (SI: 53 mmol/mol) to >17% (SI: >163 mmol/mol; reference 3.8-5.6% [SI: 18-38 mmol/mol]). Autoimmune diabetes antibodies were negative. C-peptide declined over 6 weeks from 1.77 ng/mL (SI: 0.59 nmol/L) to 0.80 ng/mL (SI: 0.26 nmol/L) (reference 1.10-5.50 ng/mL [SI: 0.36-1.82 nmol/L]), supporting pancreatogenic diabetes. Automated insulin delivery achieved sustained glycemic improvement, with HbA1c improving to 7.6% (SI: 60 mmol/mol) at the most recent 18-month follow-up. This case highlights progressive endocrine failure and the utility of advanced diabetes technology in AIP-associated pancreatogenic diabetes.Keywords: IgG4-related autoimmune pancreatitis, pancreatogenic diabetes, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, automated insulin delivery, continuous glucose monitoring

Introduction

Immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)-related disease is a systemic fibroinflammatory disorder characterized by lymphoplasmacytic infiltration and fibrosis affecting multiple organs, including the pancreas, biliary tree, liver, salivary glands, kidneys, and retroperitoneum [1-3].

Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), the pancreatic manifestation of IgG4-related disease, most commonly affects older men and may present with pancreatic masses, biliary strictures, and elevated tumor markers, mimicking pancreaticobiliary malignancy. Recognition of AIP is essential for appropriate management.

Chronic pancreatic inflammation may result in exocrine and endocrine dysfunction leading to pancreatogenic diabetes (type 3c diabetes mellitus [T3cDM]) [4, 5]. T3cDM is characterized by progressive insulin deficiency and marked glycemic variability [4]. Glycemic management is often complicated by maldigestion, malabsorption, and nutritional deficiencies associated with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency [5]. Advances in diabetes technology, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and automated insulin delivery (AID), have improved glycemic outcomes, reduced glycemic variability, and minimized hypoglycemia [6].

We present a case of IgG4-related AIP initially concerning for pancreaticobiliary malignancy, complicated by severe pancreatic dysfunction and insulin-deficient T3cDM successfully managed with AID.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man with a history of AIP, biopsy-confirmed primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), autoimmune hepatitis, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, Hashimoto thyroiditis with hypothyroidism, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia presented for evaluation of worsening diabetes. Approximately 4 years earlier, he was found to have a pancreatic mass and biliary stricture during evaluation of abdominal symptoms. Carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) increased to 590 U/mL (reference <34 U/mL), raising concern for pancreatic malignancy.

Over subsequent years, serial pancreatic and biliary biopsies demonstrated chronic inflammation, fibrosis, reactive atypia, and low-grade dysplasia without evidence of carcinoma, consistent with chronic pancreatitis and inflammatory biliary disease. IgG4 levels were elevated at 193.5 mg/dL (SI: 1.94 g/L; reference 4-86 mg/dL [SI: 0.04-0.86 g/L]), which together with biopsy findings confirmed AIP.

He underwent more than 10 endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) procedures for recurrent biliary strictures. Liver biopsy and transient elastography findings demonstrated advanced fibrosis (F3). Magnetic resonance imaging with cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) demonstrated diffuse pancreatic atrophy and biliary abnormalities, including abrupt discontinuation and proximal irregularity of the right intrahepatic duct, consistent with PSC-related strictures (Fig. 1A–1C). As disease progressed, he developed severe exocrine pancreatic insufficiency. Fecal pancreatic elastase-1 levels were <10 µg/g (reference ≥200 µg/g), and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was initiated. Diabetes was initially diagnosed during glucocorticoid treatment for AIP and attributed to steroid-induced hyperglycemia. Glycemic control worsened during glucocorticoid therapy (oral prednisone 60 mg/day taper), with hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels ranging between 11% and 14% (SI: 97-130 mmol/mol; reference 3.8%–5.6% [SI: 18-38 mmol/mol]) but improved to 6.4%–7.1% (SI: 46-54 mmol/mol) off glucocorticoids. Insulin therapy was required during glucocorticoid exposure but discontinued following steroid tapering. He was reluctant to use insulin therapy and was treated with oral sulfonylurea therapy when not receiving glucocorticoids.

Glycemic control progressively worsened. Approximately 1 year before endocrinology evaluation, HbA1c increased from 7.1% (SI: 54 mmol/mol) to >17% (SI: >163 mmol/mol), with rapid deterioration during the preceding 6 months. Oral sulfonylurea therapy was discontinued, and basal insulin therapy was initiated, followed later by prandial insulin because of persistent hyperglycemia. During this period, he experienced severe glycemic variability with hypoglycemia-associated seizure, resulting in insulin therapy concerns and prompting endocrinology referral.

At endocrinology evaluation, HbA1c was 13.9% (SI: 128 mmol/mol). He reported unintentional weight loss of approximately 30 kg over 6 months. Body weight was 58 kg and body mass index 18.8 kg/m2 (reference 18.5-24.9 kg/m2), consistent with a catabolic state. He was receiving basal-bolus insulin. HbA1c trends are shown in Fig. 2.

Diagnostic assessment

Autoimmune diabetes antibodies, including glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 antibody, insulinoma-associated antigen 2 antibody, and zinc transporter 8 antibody, were negative. C-peptide declined from 1.77 ng/mL (SI: 0.59 nmol/L) to 0.80 ng/mL (SI: 0.26 nmol/L) over 6 weeks (reference 1.10-5.50 ng/mL [SI: 0.36-1.82 nmol/L]). Concurrent serum glucose during the initial C-peptide measurement was 708 mg/dL (SI: 39.3 mmol/L), supporting impaired endogenous insulin secretion despite marked hyperglycemia.

Liver function tests showed mild transaminase elevation with aspartate aminotransferase 63 U/L (SI: 1.05 μkat/L; reference 0-40 U/L [SI: 0-0.67 μkat/L]) and alanine aminotransferase 51 U/L (SI: 0.85 μkat/L; reference 0-41 U/L [SI: 0-0.68 μkat/L]). Total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, total protein, albumin, and renal function were normal. Additional findings included thyroid-stimulating hormone 3.86 µIU/mL (reference 0.590-4.780 µIU/mL), triglycerides 112 mg/dL (SI: 1.26 mmol/L; reference <150 mg/dL [SI: <1.7 mmol/L]), and serum calcium 9.6 mg/dL (SI: 2.4 mmol/L; reference 8.7-10.4 mg/dL [SI: 2.2-2.6 mmol/L]). Normal triglyceride and calcium levels made hypertriglyceridemia and hypercalcemia-related pancreatic disease less likely.

Elevated IgG4 levels, biopsy-confirmed chronic pancreatitis, severe exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, progressive hyperglycemia, and rapidly declining endogenous insulin secretion supported pancreatogenic diabetes secondary to chronic AIP.

Treatment

Given the need for intensive insulin therapy while minimizing hypoglycemia, he was transitioned to AID using the Omnipod 5 insulin pump system (Insulet Corporation, Acton, MA, USA) integrated with Dexcom G7 CGM (Dexcom, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). CGM and pump data guided glycemic management.

Prednisone was discontinued because of steroid-related adverse effects, and immunosuppression transitioned to azathioprine. Other medications included PERT, levothyroxine, aspirin, prasugrel, statin therapy, and an angiotensin receptor blocker.

Outcome and follow-up

Following initiation of AID, glycemic control improved markedly. Within 3 weeks, CGM demonstrated time in range (TIR; glucose 70-180 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-10.0 mmol/L]) of 70% without clinically significant hypoglycemia (glucose <70 mg/dL [SI: <3.9 mmol/L]) (Table 1, Fig. 3A).

Discussion

AIP may closely mimic pancreaticobiliary malignancy because of pancreatic mass lesions, biliary strictures, obstructive jaundice, and elevated tumor markers. Elevated CA 19-9 levels together with pancreaticobiliary abnormalities prompted evaluation to exclude malignancy. In this case, the elevated CA 19-9 level was likely related to chronic inflammation and biliary obstruction. Multiple pancreatic and biliary biopsies consistently demonstrated chronic fibroinflammatory changes without carcinoma, and progressive normalization of CA 19-9 levels further supported a nonmalignant inflammatory process.

Glucocorticoids remain the cornerstone of treatment for IgG4-related AIP and often result in rapid clinical improvement [3]. Relapse rates are high, and some patients require steroid-sparing immunosuppressive therapy because of recurrent disease or treatment-related adverse effects. In this case, the patient initially received multiple courses of glucocorticoids before transitioning to azathioprine because of steroid intolerance. Despite treatment, he developed pancreatic atrophy with progressive exocrine and endocrine dysfunction, highlighting irreversible pancreatic damage despite immunosuppressive therapy in this condition. The marked autoimmune clustering observed in this patient, including AIP, PSC, autoimmune hepatitis, Hashimoto thyroiditis, and keratoconjunctivitis sicca, highlights coexistence of multiple autoimmune disorders related to chronic immune dysregulation and may provide an important diagnostic clue [7].

Over time, he developed severe exocrine and endocrine pancreatic dysfunction. Severe exocrine dysfunction was supported by fecal pancreatic elastase-1 levels <10 µg/g, as levels ≥200 µg/g are considered normal, 100-199 µg/g indicate moderate insufficiency, and <100 µg/g indicate severe insufficiency. Exocrine insufficiency contributed to weight loss, malabsorption, nutritional instability, and PERT.

Endocrine dysfunction was evidenced by worsening hyperglycemia and decline in C-peptide levels from 1.77 ng/mL (SI: 0.59 nmol/L) to 0.80 ng/mL (SI: 0.26 nmol/L) over 6 weeks despite severe hyperglycemia, reflecting accelerated loss of endogenous insulin secretion. Such rapid deterioration is not commonly described in AIP and highlights the potential for accelerated endocrine failure in advanced chronic pancreatic disease. Negative autoimmune diabetes antibodies argued against autoimmune diabetes and supported pancreatogenic diabetes. Prior to endocrinology evaluation, sulfonylurea therapy in the setting of progressive pancreatic disease and limited insulin reserve may have contributed to accelerated β-cell dysfunction and exhaustion, as chronic sulfonylurea exposure has been associated with β-cell dysfunction and apoptosis in experimental studies [8]. However, some effects on insulin secretory capacity may be reversible following drug withdrawal [9].

Pancreatogenic diabetes is associated with marked glycemic variability because of combined insulin deficiency, impaired glucagon secretion, malabsorption, and altered counterregulatory response [5]. These abnormalities increase risk of severe hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, and related complications including diabetic ketoacidosis, making management challenging.

In the present case, AID integrated with CGM enabled dynamic insulin adjustment with reduction in glycemic variability while minimizing hypoglycemia despite prior hypoglycemia-associated seizure. Early CGM data demonstrated improved TIR without clinically significant hypoglycemia, with sustained glycemic benefit during long-term follow-up. These findings suggest that AID may be particularly valuable in older adults with insulin-deficient pancreatogenic diabetes and marked glycemic variability, especially when hypoglycemia risk limits conventional insulin intensification.

Strengths of this case include long-term follow-up demonstrating progression from autoimmune pancreatic inflammation to exocrine and endocrine failure, serial pathology excluding malignancy, and sustained follow-up after initiation of AID. The case also highlights the complexity of distinguishing AIP from pancreaticobiliary malignancy in the setting of markedly elevated CA 19-9 levels and chronic biliary abnormalities.

Limitations include the observational nature of a single case. In addition, C-peptide measurements were nonfasting, which may limit standardized interpretation of endogenous insulin secretion; however, the markedly elevated concurrent glucose level and progressive decline in C-peptide still supported significant endocrine pancreatic dysfunction.

Nevertheless, this report highlights the potential for progressive and possibly accelerated endocrine pancreatic failure in IgG4-related AIP and supports the role of advanced diabetes technology in insulin-deficient pancreatogenic diabetes.
