Land-use legacies shape soil microbial communities and nutrient cycling functions in rotational shifting cultivation fields of Northern Thailand
Authors: Noppol Arunrat, Wuttichai Mhuantong, Sukanya Sereenonchai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow land-use history—particularly in contrasting systems such as rotational shifting cultivation (RSC) and continuously fallow (CF) fields—influences soil microbial communities and their biogeochemical functions remains insufficiently understood. In this study, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was used to compare the taxonomic composition and functional gene profiles of soils under RSC and CF systems in Northern Thailand. The results revealed distinct microbial assemblages and metabolic potentials shaped by land-use legacy. RSC soils were characterized by a higher abundance of nitrifiers and nitrogen-fixing taxa, including Nitrosocosmicus and Streptomyces, along with enriched genes involved in nitrification (e.g., amoC_B, nxrB) and nitrogen fixation (nifD, nifK), reflecting an enhanced potential for nitrogen acquisition and retention. In contrast, CF soils showed enrichment in Bradyrhizobium, Halobaculum, and Russula, and exhibited higher expression of denitrification-related genes (norB, narJ), suggesting increased nitrogen loss via gaseous emissions. Functional genes related to phosphate metabolism (phoX, glpQ) and nutrient signal transduction were more abundant in RSC soils, indicating active nutrient cycling in response to recent disturbance. Conversely, CF soils demonstrated broader metabolic capabilities, including genes for sulfur oxidation and redox regulation, suggesting microbial adaptation to more stable but nutrient-limited conditions. These findings demonstrate that land-use legacies strongly influence microbial composition and function, with important implications for nutrient cycling and soil fertility restoration in shifting cultivation landscapes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02598-x.Keywords: Rotational shifting cultivation, Fire, Soil microbial communities, Functional gene profiles, Shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Introduction

Soil microbial communities play a critical role in terrestrial ecosystems, driving key processes such as organic matter decomposition, nutrient cycling, soil aggregation, carbon storage, and greenhouse gas fluxes [1, 2]. These microbial assemblages also contribute to plant nutrition, development, and immunity, while acting as important indicators of soil health and ecosystem sustainability [3, 4]. Given their sensitivity to environmental changes, soil microbes are valuable bioindicators for evaluating the impacts of land-use change and management practices on ecosystem functioning [5, 6].

In tropical upland regions, land-use pressures have intensified due to increasing population demands, resulting in shifts from traditional to more intensified agricultural systems. Among traditional systems, rotational shifting cultivation (RSC) remains widely practiced in Southeast Asia, including Northern Thailand [7–9]. RSC typically involves forest clearing via burning, short-term crop cultivation (1–3 years), and subsequent fallow periods for natural regeneration [9, 10]. When managed sustainably—with sufficiently long fallow durations—RSC can support soil fertility, enhance biodiversity, and contribute to carbon sequestration [11, 12]. However, increasing land-use intensity and shortened fallow periods have undermined these benefits, often leading to soil degradation, erosion, and alterations in microbial community composition and function [9, 13]. In contrast, continuously fallow fields (CF)—particularly those left undisturbed for extended periods—represent an alternative system where natural succession promotes ecological recovery. These systems facilitate the restoration of vegetation and microbial diversity, improve nutrient retention and organic carbon stocks, and reduce soil erosion [14, 15]. Despite their ecological significance, the microbial mechanisms underlying soil recovery in long-term fallow systems and RSC fields after harvest remain poorly understood, particularly in tropical upland environments.

Microbial community composition is strongly influenced by land-use history and associated changes in soil physicochemical properties [3, 16]. Different land uses often produce distinct microbial assemblages, with implications for ecosystem functioning and resilience. While some studies suggest that reductions in microbial biodiversity may negatively impact functional stability [17], others argue that functional redundancy within microbial communities can mitigate such effects [18]. This ambiguity underscores the need for detailed investigations into both the taxonomic and functional dimensions of microbial communities under varying land-use regimes. High-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionized microbial ecology by enabling in-depth characterization of soil microbial diversity across ecosystems [19, 20]. Most prior research has relied on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which, while useful for taxonomic profiling, is limited in its ability to capture the functional potential of microbial communities due to biases introduced by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) amplification and primer selection [21–23]. Moreover, the short-read nature of amplicon sequencing often restricts resolution to genus or higher taxonomic levels and does not allow direct analysis of metabolic functions [24–26].

To overcome these limitations, shotgun metagenomic whole-genome sequencing (mWGS) has emerged as a powerful approach for assessing both the taxonomic composition and functional capacity of microbial communities [27]. Unlike amplicon sequencing, mWGS does not rely on targeted PCR amplification, thereby avoiding primer bias and enabling the detection of a broader range of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses [28]. This method allows for the exploration of functional gene abundance, metabolic pathways, and microbial interactions at high resolution, even down to the species or strain level [29, 30]. Although shotgun metagenomics provides a powerful tool for simultaneously assessing microbial community composition and functional potential at high resolution, its application in traditional tropical agroecosystems such as RSC remains limited. Consequently, key scientific questions remain unanswered: How do microbial communities in RSC fields differ from those in long-term fallow soils in terms of taxonomic structure, functional gene expression, and metabolic capabilities? Which microbial taxa and genes are most closely linked to nutrient acquisition, transformation, and loss across these contrasting land-use systems? And to what extent do these microbial communities reflect adaptive responses to disturbance-driven nutrient cycling in tropical upland environments. There is a critical knowledge gap regarding how land-use history—particularly in contrasting systems like RSC and long-term fallow—shapes microbial taxonomic and functional diversity, including contributions to biogeochemical cycles. To address these gaps, this study employed a shotgun metagenomic approach to investigate soil microbial communities in two contrasting upland systems in Northern Thailand: a RSC field and a continuously fallow field left undisturbed for seven years. By analyzing microbial taxonomic composition, functional gene profiles, and metabolic pathways—we aim to: assess how land-use legacies influence microbial community structure and function, and identify key microbial taxa and functional genes associated with soil fertility and nutrient cycling. We hypothesized that land-use legacies significantly influence microbial community structure and functional gene composition, with RSC fields favoring microbial taxa and pathways associated with active nutrient cycling, while CF soils support a broader but more conservative metabolic repertoire. By integrating taxonomic, functional, and community assembly perspectives, this study offers novel insights into the microbial ecology of RSC and fallow systems. Our findings contribute to ongoing discussions about sustainable land management and soil restoration in tropical upland agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study areas

The study sites were located in Ban Mae Pok, Ban Thab Subdistrict, Mae Chaem District, Chiang Mai Province, Northern Thailand (Fig. 1). The annual rainfall ranged from 1,105 to 2,688 mm, with the majority occurring during the rainy season (May to October). During the winter season (October to February), minimum temperatures ranged from 3.2 °C to 22.1 °C, whereas in the summer season (February to April), maximum temperatures ranged from 35 °C to 40 °C [13]. The soils in the highland areas of Thailand with slopes exceeding 35% are classified as slope complex soil series [31].

Study areas

A continuously fallow field for seven years (CF7Y) and a 5-year fallow rotational shifting cultivation (RSC5Y) field were selected based on their similar microclimate and topographic conditions. The CF7Y field (18°23′3"N, 98°11′49"E; elevation 659 m a.s.l.) was previously used for upland rice cultivation and has remained abandoned since the harvest in 2017, resulting in a seven-year fallow period by 2024. The RSC5Y field (18°23′25.5"N, 98°11′47.0"E; elevation 600 m a.s.l.) was left fallow for five years. In 2024, this field was cleared, burned, and used again for upland rice cultivation. After the harvest, the RSC5Y field was left to recover. Neither tillage nor chemical inputs—such as fertilizers, pesticides, or herbicides—were applied in either field. After fallow periods of these fields and subsequent re-cultivation, vegetation undergoes natural succession. The dominant tree species are Pterocarpus macrocarpus Kurz, Xylia xylocarpa (Roxb.) Taub, Lithocarpus ceriferus, and Tectona grandis Linn. f. The shrub and herbal vegetation are Chromolaena odorata (L.) R.M.King & H. Rob, Brainea insignis (Hook.) J. Sm., and Betula alnoides Buch. Ham.

Experimental design and soil sampling

Soil samples were collected in December 2024. The CF7Y (~ 180 m × 270 m) and RSC5Y (~ 94 m × 175 m) fields were each divided into three transects, located at the upper, middle, and lower slopes. Along each transect, five plots were established at equal intervals, and surface soil samples (0–5 cm depth) were collected. The individual samples from each transect were then combined to form a single composite sample per transect. This sampling strategy was employed to account for potential variation in soil nutrients caused by slope-related erosion processes [9], which may also influence soil microbial diversity. Approximately 1 kg of soil was collected per composite sample and stored in plastic bags for analysis of physical and chemical properties. In addition, approximately 100 g of soil was collected in zip-lock plastic bags and kept at 10 °C in the field and at –20 °C in the laboratory for subsequent DNA extraction.

Soil physical and chemical properties analysis

Soil texture was determined using the hydrometer method. Soil pH was measured using a pH meter with a 1:1 soil-to-water ratio [32]. Electrical conductivity (ECe) was assessed from saturation paste extracts using an EC meter [33]. Total nitrogen (TN) was analyzed using the micro-Kjeldahl method [34]. Exchangeable calcium (exch. Ca), magnesium (exch. Mg), and potassium (exch. K) were determined by atomic absorption spectrometry following extraction with 1 M ammonium acetate (NH₄OAc) at pH 7.0 [35, 36]. Available phosphorus (avail. P) was measured using the molybdate blue method with Bray II extraction [35, 37]. Organic carbon (OC) was determined using the potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) oxidation method in sulfuric acid, following the Walkley and Black procedure [35, 38] and converted to soil organic matter (SOM) by multiplying by 1.724.

DNA extraction and metagenome shotgun sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 250 mg of soil per sample. Samples were either processed immediately or stored at –20 °C until extraction. Environmental DNA was isolated using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentrations were quantified using a NanoDrop Eight spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Shotgun sequencing was conducted using 200 ng of DNA per sample for library preparation. The NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) was used to prepare sequencing libraries, with unique index codes assigned to each sample as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. DNA samples were sonicated to approximately 350 bp fragments, followed by end-polishing and purification with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, USA). Library size distribution was evaluated using an Agilent2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and quantification was performed via real-time PCR. Indexed samples were clustered on a cBot Cluster Generation System per the manufacturer’s instructions. Post-clustering, libraries were pooled, diluted to 0.6 nM, and sequenced for 300 cycles in paired-end mode on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., USA).

Diversity analysis and taxonomic classification

Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality control to remove low-quality reads and trim adapter sequences. Reads with an average Phred quality score below 30 were discarded, and adapter sequences were trimmed using FASTP [39]. Cleaned sequences were taxonomically classified using Kraken 2 [40] against the NCBI nt index database (version 2024-May-30; https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2). The Kraken 2 output was further refined using Bracken [41] to estimate relative abundances at the species level. Next, taxonomic profiles were analyzed for diversity using QIIME2 [42] version 2024.10. Alpha diversity metrics, including species richness and the Shannon diversity index, were calculated to assess within-sample diversity. Beta diversity was evaluated using the Bray–Curtis distance matrix to compare between-sample diversity. Statistical significance of differences in microbial communities was determined using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. To investigate the abundance of biogeochemical cycle genes including nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and methane cycles, cleaned sequences were aligned against the curated databases of NCycDB [43], PCycDB [44], SCycDB [45], and MCycDB [46] using DIAMOND [47]. The DIAMOND parameters were set for identity percent ≥ 80 and alignment length > 50%. Raw read counts were subsequently normalized allowing robust quantification and comparison the gene abundance of each pathways using DESEQ [48].

In parallel, quality-filtered short reads were assembled into contigs using metaSPAdes [49] with default k-mer settings. Assembled contigs were filtered to retain those with a minimum length of 500 bp. Gene prediction was performed on the filtered contigs using metaProdigal [50]. Predicted genes were functionally annotated using EggNOG-mapper v2 [51] against the EggNOG database v5.0. Functional annotations, including Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were extracted, and the relative abundance of each functional category was calculated as a percentage of the total annotated genes.

Correlation, statistics, and visualization

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 20; IBM Corporation, USA). Independent sample t-tests were used to evaluate significant differences in soil physicochemical properties between the two sites. Differences in taxonomic composition, alpha diversity indices, and abundances of KEGG pathways and COG genes between the two soil metagenome locations were statistically assessed using Welch’s t-test implemented in STAMP [52]. Correlation analyses between microbial profiles and soil physicochemical properties were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient calculated via SciPy [53]. Visualization of the data, including bar plots, dendrogram-based heatmaps, and ordination plots, was performed using ggplot2 [54] in R and plotnine (https://github.com/has2k1/plotnine) in Python.

Results

Soil physiochemical properties

Soil physicochemical properties of two fields, CF7Y and RSC5Y, with notable differences in several key factors, are presented in Table 1. The pH values indicate that CF7Y has slightly acidic soil (pH 5.14), while RSC5Y exhibits a more neutral pH (6.45), suggesting a less acidic environment in the latter. The ECe was lower in CF7Y (0.16 dS/m) compared to RSC5Y, where it was higher at 0.46 dS/m. In terms of SOM CF7Y contains 5.62%, while RSC5Y has a slightly higher content of 6.03%. The total nitrogen content is also higher in RSC5Y (0.21%) compared to CF7Y (0.17%), suggesting improved nitrogen availability in RSC5Y. Avail.P is significantly higher in RSC5Y (29.75 mg/kg) than in CF7Y (12.53 mg/kg), indicating better phosphorus availability for plant growth in RSC5Y. Regarding exchangeable cations, both fields show similar levels of exch.K, with CF7Y at 219.46 mg/kg and RSC5Y at 233.99 mg/kg. However, exch.Ca is higher in RSC5Y (573.74 mg/kg) than in CF7Y (461.01 mg/kg), suggesting a greater presence of calcium in RSC5Y. A similar trend is observed for exch.Mg, where RSC5Y contains 212.13 mg/kg, while CF7Y has 184.54 mg/kg. In terms of soil texture, CF7Y consists of 30.53% sand, 53.65% silt, and 15.82% clay, while RSC5Y has slightly less sand (29.31%) and silt (47.82%) but a higher percentage of clay (22.87%). This higher clay content in RSC5Y indicates a denser soil texture, which may affect water retention and drainage compared to CF7Y, which, being more sandy, may drain more easily. Overall, RSC5Y tends to have higher values for several key soil properties, such as pH, available phosphorus, exchangeable calcium, magnesium, and clay content, suggesting it may be more fertile, though less permeable, than CF7Y, which has slightly lower ECe, organic matter, and nitrogen content.

Sequencing of metagenome dataset

A total of 424,254,701 raw paired‐end reads were generated from 10 RSC soil samples using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, ranging from 40,120,545 to 46,429,376 reads per sample with an average number of sequences per sample of 42,425,470 reads. Following quality control, based on an average Phred quality score > 30, 92.23% of the reads were retained. Taxonomic classification with Kraken 2 assigned approximately 22,974,863 cleaned reads per sample to bacterial, archaeal, and eukaryotic taxa. Metagenome assembly produced total sequence lengths per sample ranging from 552 to 849 Mbp, with contig counts between 790,403 and 1,152,389. The largest contigs per sample measured from 13,714 to 219,128 bp, and N50 values ranged from 654 to 718 bp. Gene prediction identified between 739,291 and 1,091,783 genes per sample, using a minimum protein length threshold of 100 amino acids.

Richness and Diversity

The species richness and Shannon diversity index of microbial communities varied between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Fig. 2a). The observed features, representing species richness, ranged from 12,973 to 13,193 in CF7Y, with a mean of 13,093. In contrast, RSC5Y exhibited higher richness values, ranging from 13,631 to 13,848, with a mean of 13,755. Similarly, the Shannon index, which accounts for both species richness and evenness, was higher in RSC5Y than in CF7Y. Additionally, the Venn diagram in Fig. 2c indicates that the two soil samples share a substantial core microbiome comprising 13,589 species, whereas 469 and 1,087 species are unique to CF7Y and RSC5Y, respectively. These findings suggest that the RSC5Y soil harbor a more diverse and evenly distributed microbial community compared to the CF7Y soil.

Comparison of alpha and beta diversity. (a) Species richness and Shannon diversity index. (b) Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances. (c) Number of shared and unique species between CF7Y and RSC5Y. (d) Hierarchical clustering constructed using Ward’s linkage method

The PCoA based on Bray–Curtis distances (Fig. 2b) showed a clear separation between CF7Y and RSC5Y samples along PC1, indicating distinct microbial community structures between the two sites. CF7Y samples had negative PC1 values, while RSC5Y samples were positive. Furthermore, the hierarchical clustering in Fig. 2d reveals a consistent pattern, grouping all CF7Y samples into one distinct cluster and all RSC5Y samples into another, thereby confirming a marked differentiation between the two microbial communities. This suggests that land use strongly influences soil microbial composition.

Taxonomic profiles and diversity

Read-based taxonomic profiling using Kraken2 and Bracken assigned 229.7 million reads across all samples, covering Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukaryotes. Bacteria dominated the soil microbiome, averaging 88.3% of total reads, followed by Eukaryotes (11.1%) and Archaea (0.57%). While bacterial and eukaryotic compositions did not differ significantly between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils, archaeal communities showed a significant difference (p < 0.05; Welch’s t-test) (Fig. 3a). A dendrogram heatmap of the top 20 bacterial genera revealed distinct community compositions between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Figs. 3b and 3e). Bradyrhizobium, Mycobacterium, and Streptomyces were dominant in CF7Y, with Bradyrhizobium reaching up to 45.42%. In contrast, RSC5Y soils showed significantly higher abundances of Streptomyces (up to 29.68%), Nocardioides (up to 13.75%), and Nostoc (up to 13.46%), all notably elevated compared to CF7Y. These shifts suggest differing microbial adaptations to land-use histories and soil conditions. The archaeal community was dominated by Euryarchaeota, particularly the class Halobacteria, across both CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Figs. 3c and 3f). Major genera included Halobaculum, Halorubrum, Methanosarcina, and Natrinema, with slightly higher abundances in CF7Y. In contrast, ammonia-oxidizing archaea from the phylum Nitrososphaerota—notably Nitrosocosmicus and Nitrososphaera—were significantly more abundant in RSC5Y, indicating a stronger potential for ammonia oxidation in this field. These shifts reflect possible differences in nitrogen cycling and ecological adaptation linked to land-use history. Eukaryotic community composition differed notably between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Figs. 3d and 3 g). Fungi, Metazoa, and Viridiplantae were the dominant groups. CF7Y exhibited higher fungal diversity, with Russula, Penicillium, and Aspergillus being prominent, along with notable Metazoans like Drosophila and Homo. In contrast, RSC5Y showed a strong presence of Oryza (6.46–48.11%), reflecting plant residue input or regeneration, along with fungal genera like Aspergillus and Colletotrichum, and consistent insect taxa. These differences suggest land-use history influences organic inputs and habitat conditions, shaping eukaryotic community structure.

Correlation between taxonomic profiles and soil properties

Spearman correlation analysis revealed distinct relationships between soil physicochemical properties and the relative abundance of bacterial genera (Figure S1). ECe was positively correlated with Blastococcus, Microbacterium, Micromonospora, Nocardioides, Sphingomonas, and Variovorax, but negatively associated with Bradyrhizobium, Mesorhizobium, Mycobacterium, and Mycolicibacterium. SOM showed a significant negative correlation with Variovorax, while TN was negatively correlated with Microbacterium and Streptomyces. Available phosphorus was positively associated with Conexibacter and Micromonospora, but negatively with Bradyrhizobium and Microbacterium. Exchangeable Mg exhibited strong positive correlations with Blastococcus, Sphingomonas, and Variovorax, and a negative correlation with Mesorhizobium. Regarding soil texture, silt content was positively associated with Bradyrhizobium, Mycobacterium, and Mycolicibacterium, but negatively correlated with Nostoc, Nocardioides, and Microbacterium. Clay content showed a significant positive correlation with Sphingomonas.

Soil physicochemical properties significantly influenced the relative abundance of archaeal genera (Figure S2). Correlation analysis identified ECe as a major driver of archaeal community composition. Nitrosocosmicus exhibited a strong positive correlation with ECe, while halophilic and methanogenic genera such as Methanosarcina, Haloferax, and Halorussus showed significant negative correlations. SOM was positively associated with Halorubrum. Available phosphorus was positively correlated with Nitrosocosmicus, an ammonia-oxidizing archaeon, but negatively associated with Natrinema and Halorussus. Regarding soil texture, Nitrosocosmicus was positively correlated with clay content and negatively with silt, whereas Methanosarcina, Natrinema, and Halorussus favored silt-rich conditions. Exchangeable magnesium was also positively correlated with Nitrosocosmicus and negatively with Methanosarcina and other halophilic taxa.

Figure S3 highlights significant correlations between soil physicochemical properties and fungal taxa across the study sites. ECe was strongly negatively correlated with Aspergillus (r = –0.833) and Coregonus (r = –0.910), but positively associated with Digitaria, Idaea, and Oryza. SOM negatively correlated with Digitaria. TN showed positive correlations with Aspergillus (r = 0.721) and Colletotrichum (r = 0.743), but a negative correlation with Digitaria. Available phosphorus was positively correlated with Digitaria (r = 0.678) and Idaea, and negatively with Colletotrichum (r = –0.694), Danio, and Coregonus (r = –0.671). Exchangeable magnesium exhibited negative correlations with Aspergillus, Coregonus, and Colletotrichum. Soil texture also influenced fungal distribution: silt content positively correlated with Colletotrichum (r = 0.723), Coregonus (r = 0.896), and Solanum, while negatively with Idaea and Oryza. Clay content was positively associated with Idaea and Eupithecia, but negatively with Aspergillus. Sand content negatively correlated with Adineta and Mythimna.

Gene abundance of nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and methane cycles

Differential abundance analysis of nitrogen cycling genes revealed significant variations among the soil types (Fig. 4a). The nxrB and amoC_B genes, associated with nitrification, were substantially enriched in RSC5Y soils, indicating a higher nitrification potential. In contrast, nao, a gene involved in ammonia oxidation, was significantly depleted in CF7Y soils, suggesting reduced nitrification capacity in these soils. The nasB gene, related to assimilatory nitrate and nitrite reduction, was predominantly enriched in CF7Y soils, indicating more active nitrate assimilation processes. Genes associated with nitrogen fixation, including nifD and nifK, were more abundant in RSC5Y soils, reflecting a more active nitrogen-fixing microbial community. Regarding denitrification, norB and narJ were enriched in CF7Y soils, suggesting a higher potential for nitrogen loss through denitrification in these soils. Meanwhile, the gdh_K00262 gene, involved in urea hydrolysis, was moderately enriched in both soil types, indicating active urea cycling across both systems. The results indicate a trend of enhanced nitrification and nitrogen fixation in RSC5Y soils, while denitrification processes were more prominent in CF7Y soils. These findings suggest that microbial communities in RSC5Y soils may promote nitrogen retention and transformation, whereas those in CF7Y soils may contribute more to nitrogen loss.

Differential abundances of functional genes involved in (a) nitrogen, (b) phosphorus, (c) sulfur, (d) methane cycling between CF7Y and RSC5Y soil samples

The relative abundance of genes involved in phosphorus cycling showed marked differences between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Fig. 4b). The phoX gene was significantly more abundant in RSC5Y soils, suggesting increased phosphate metabolism, whereas it was notably depleted in CF7Y soils. Additionally, the glpQ and phy genes were enriched in RSC5Y, indicating enhanced glycerol metabolism and elevated alkaline phosphatase activity in this soil type.

Figure 4c highlights several key genes involved in the sulfur cycle. Notably, dmoA, sorA, comD, and soxD—genes associated with sulfur oxidation pathways—were significantly less abundant in RSC5Y soils, suggesting a suppression of sulfur oxidation activity. Similarly, soxZ, soxD, and soxC also showed marked depletion, further indicating reduced activity of the Sox pathway. In contrast, these sulfur oxidation genes were enriched in CF7Y soils, pointing to a higher potential for sulfur oxidation in this field. Interestingly, the dddP gene, which is involved in the degradation of dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) and the metabolism of methylated sulfur compounds, was enriched in RSC5Y but depleted in CF7Y. The sulfate transport gene sulP was depleted in RSC5Y and enriched in CF7Y, suggesting differences in sulfate transport dynamics between the two soil types. Additionally, the metY gene, related to methionine and sulfur-containing amino acid biosynthesis, was depleted in RSC5Y and enriched in CF7Y, indicating a more active sulfur assimilation process in CF7Y soils.

The genes nxrA, nrfA, pps, and amoA were significantly enriched in RSC5Y soils, suggesting enhanced nitrification and overall nitrogen cycling activity in recently disturbed environments. In contrast, these genes were notably depleted in CF7Y soils. Conversely, xoxF4, hprA, and mttC were more abundant in CF7Y but depleted in RSC5Y, indicating distinct functional capacities likely shaped by long-term fallow conditions (Fig. 4d).

Orthologous genes

A total of 18 functional COG categories were identified across the two sample groups, CF7Y and RSC5Y. These categories span three major functional classes: Cellular Processes and Signaling, Information Storage and Processing, and Metabolism (Fig. 5a). Among all categories, amino acid transport and metabolism was the most abundant across both CF7Y and RSC5Y fields, with relative abundances ranging from 9.64–9.80% in CF7Y and 9.59–10.30% in RSC5Y. This suggests a high level of metabolic activity related to protein biosynthesis and nitrogen utilization in both soil types. The energy production and conversion and carbohydrate transport and metabolism categories also showed consistently high abundance, particularly in CF7Y (8.34–8.52% and 7.03–7.14%, respectively), indicating active microbial respiration and carbon cycling. Functional groups associated with cell signaling and defense mechanisms such as signal transduction mechanisms and defense mechanisms were moderately represented. Notably, signal transduction mechanisms ranged from 6.13–6.19% in CF7Y and up to 7.17% in RSC5Y, suggesting more diverse or responsive microbial communities under RSC. Genes associated with information storage and processing (categories J, K, and L) were also prominent. Particularly, L (replication, recombination, and repair) and K (transcription) occurred at levels above 5.8% in both groups, reflecting microbial genome maintenance and adaptability under varying field conditions. Conversely, categories such as D (cell cycle control) and N (cell motility) were less represented, each contributing less than 1.5% in all samples, suggesting these processes are not dominant functional features in the microbial communities of either soil system. The 15 functional COG categories differed significantly between RSC5Y and CF7Y (Fig. 5b). Moreover, these fields strongly influenced the distribution of orthologous genes, as shown in Fig. 5c.

Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway

The functional potential of the microbial communities was assessed using KEGG pathway analysis, with the top 20 most abundant pathways based on percent relative abundance illustrated for both CF7Y and RSC5Y fields (Figs. 6a and 6b). Across all samples, the most dominant category was metabolic pathways, accounting for approximately 15.17–15.28% in CF7Y and slightly higher in RSC5Y (15.57–15.71%), indicating a high level of general metabolic activity in both fields. The biosynthesis of secondary metabolites pathway was the second most abundant, with values ranging from 6.67–6.79% in CF7Y and slightly elevated levels in RSC5Y (6.87–7.03%), suggesting increased metabolic diversification or microbial adaptation in the RSC5Y field. Interestingly, microbial metabolism in diverse environments showed higher relative abundance in CF7Y (6.41–6.46%) compared to RSC5Y (5.77–6.18%), potentially reflecting more heterogeneous environmental conditions or a broader metabolic repertoire in CF7Y. Other pathways such as carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, ABC transporters, and quorum sensing were relatively consistent across both fields, with minor fluctuations. However, biosynthesis of amino acids and ABC transporters were slightly more enriched in RSC5Y, suggesting enhanced nutrient assimilation or microbial interactions in those soils. Some energy and carbon-related pathways, such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, butanoate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism, tended to show marginally higher relative abundances in CF7Y, whereas oxidative phosphorylation and pyrimidine metabolism were notably more abundant in RSC5Y, possibly indicating differences in microbial energy generation strategies. While both soils shared similar dominant KEGG functions, subtle differences in the relative abundances of specific metabolic pathways may reflect microbial community adaptation to soil conditions under different management histories (Fig. 6c).

Discussion

Land-use legacies influence microbial community structure and function

The greater microbial richness and diversity (as indicated by species richness and the Shannon index) observed in RSC5Y compared to CF7Y may stem from several interrelated factors associated with recent disturbances and dynamic environmental inputs (Fig. 2a). The differentiation in microbial community structure, as illustrated by the PCoA (Fig. 2b), further supports the notion that land-use legacies exert lasting influences on microbial community assembly [55]. The recent slash-and-burn activity and subsequent upland rice cultivation in RSC5Y introduced substantial changes in soil physical and chemical properties—such as elevated pH, increased levels of bioavailable nutrients (e.g., nitrogen and phosphorus), and labile carbon from burned biomass [56]. These inputs can act as nutrient pulses that stimulate microbial activity and support a wide range of fast-growing, opportunistic microbial taxa [57–59], often referred to as copiotrophs. This nutrient flush likely contributed to the temporary enrichment of both richness (number of species) and evenness (balance among species abundances) in RSC5Y. Moreover, the disturbance-diversity may help explain this pattern. According to this ecological theory, intermediate disturbance—such as occasional burning and cropping—can prevent competitive exclusion by dominant species and allow co-existence of multiple taxa. In RSC5Y, disturbance may have created a mosaic of niches and microhabitats, fostering niche diversification and microbial coexistence, which is reflected in the higher Shannon index. In contrast, CF7Y, although undisturbed for a longer time, may have developed a more stable but functionally specialized microbial community, favoring slow-growing oligotrophic taxa adapted to nutrient-limited conditions [60]. Over time, some dominant species may outcompete others, leading to lower evenness and thus a reduced Shannon diversity. Additionally, the lack of recent organic inputs (like root exudates from crops or bioavailable nutrients from burned biomass) might limit the availability of easily decomposable substrates, resulting in lower microbial activity and turnover. Finally, the vegetation type and litter quality in CF7Y versus recently cultivated RSC5Y can also influence microbial diversity. CF7Y may be dominated by mature secondary vegetation with lower-quality litter (high C:N ratio), while RSC5Y likely received residues from both crops and fire-affected materials, contributing to a more heterogeneous and microbially accessible organic matter pool.

Differential abundance of soil microbial taxa associated with contrasting land-use legacies

Land-use change exerts substantial influence on microbial dynamics [61], as varying management practices alter soil quality and nutrient cycling [62], thereby eliciting shifts in the structure and function of microbial communities [63]. In this study, Bradyrhizobium emerged as the most dominant genus in the CF7Y soils (Figs. 3b and e). Bradyrhizobium is a slow-growing, oligotrophic genus well recognized for its capacity to fix atmospheric nitrogen, often in symbiotic association with leguminous plants [64]. Its predominance in CF7Y soils may be attributed to prolonged fallow periods with minimal disturbance, fostering stable, low-nutrient conditions conducive to its survival. The increased presence of native legumes or herbaceous vegetation during fallow may have further supported Bradyrhizobium populations through symbiotic interactions [65]. Moreover, the low nitrogen availability typically associated with long-term fallowed soils likely selects for nitrogen-fixing microorganisms that contribute to sustaining nitrogen inputs. Bradyrhizobium is also known to thrive under acidic soil conditions [66, 67], which are characteristic of aged, undisturbed tropical soils, as supported by the measured pH of 5.14 in the CF7Y field (Table 1). Streptomyces, a copiotrophic and saprotrophic genus renowned for its role in organic matter decomposition and antibiotic production [68, 69], was the most abundant genus detected in the RSC5Y soils (Figs. 3b and e). This elevated abundance may be attributed to recent slash-and-burn practices, which likely enhanced the availability of labile organic carbon and nutrient-rich ash [12, 70], thereby providing favorable substrates for Streptomyces proliferation. The genus is particularly adept at decomposing complex organic compounds such as lignin and cellulose [71], both of which would have increased following biomass burning and crop residue incorporation. Moreover, Streptomyces thrives in well-aerated, disturbed soils—conditions commonly associated with slash-and-burn agriculture. Streptomyces generally prefer neutral to slightly alkaline pH conditions and are typically mesophilic, although some thermophilic strains can tolerate temperatures up to 50 °C or even 60 °C [72]. Its resilience to environmental stressors, including fluctuations in temperature and pH, may further contribute to its competitive advantage in the recently disturbed environment of RSC5Y.

Interestingly, Halobaculum and Methanosarcina were the dominant archaeal taxa in the CF7Y soils (Figs. 3c and f). Halobaculum is known for its remarkable tolerance to extreme conditions, including high salinity [73] and heavy metal contamination [74]. To our knowledge, this is the first report documenting the presence of Halobaculum in fire-disturbed soils, warranting further investigation into its ecological role under such conditions. We hypothesize that Halobaculum may be well-adapted to the low-nutrient, low-competition environments typical of long-term fallows. Methanosarcina, a methanogenic archaeon involved in anaerobic carbon cycling [75], was also abundant in CF7Y (Figs. 3c and f). Its prevalence is likely driven by the accumulation of organic matter from prolonged vegetation cover and the absence of soil disturbance. Additionally, methanogenesis may persist in anaerobic micro-niches formed within compacted or poorly aerated soils during extended fallow periods [76].

Russula species are ectomycorrhizal fungi that form symbiotic relationships with various trees and shrubs, aiding in nutrient uptake and enhancing plant health [77]. Their prevalence in CF7Y (Figs. 3d and g), a field left fallow for seven years, suggests the establishment of a stable, woody vegetation community that supports these fungi. Long-term fallow periods allow for the development of mature plant communities, which in turn foster the growth of ectomycorrhizal fungi like Russula. The high abundance of Oryza (rice) sequences in RSC5Y is expected (Figs. 3d and g), given that this field underwent recent rice cultivation following a slash-and-burn practice. The presence of Oryza DNA in soil samples can result from root exudates, decaying plant material, and residual DNA from the recent crop [78]. Such agricultural practices introduce substantial plant-derived organic matter into the soil, influencing the composition of the soil's eukaryotic community. These findings underscore the significant impact of land-use history and agricultural practices on soil eukaryotic communities. While long-term fallow conditions favor the establishment of symbiotic fungi like Russula, recent cultivation activities, such as those in RSC5Y, lead to a dominance of crop-related sequences like Oryza. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for managing soil health and fertility in agricultural landscapes.

Functional genes associated with soil fertility and nutrient cycling

Nitrogen-cycling gene abundances revealed marked differences between RSC5Y and CF7Y soils, highlighting the influence of land-use history on microbial functional potential (Fig. 4a). In RSC5Y soils, genes associated with nitrification—nxrB and amoC_B [43]—were significantly enriched, suggesting an enhanced capacity for ammonia and nitrite oxidation. This elevated nitrification potential likely reflects the recent burning and rice cultivation, which can increase the availability of inorganic nitrogen substrates (e.g., NH₄⁺), thereby stimulating autotrophic nitrifiers [79]. Moreover, the nitrogen fixation genes nifD and nifK—which encode components of the nitrogenase enzyme complex [43]—were more abundant in RSC5Y, suggesting a more active nitrogen-fixing microbial community in this recently fire-disturbed field. Such conditions may promote colonization by diazotrophs that benefit from increased root exudates or altered C:N ratio post-cultivation [80]. In contrast, the nao gene (involved in ammonia oxidation) [43] was significantly depleted in CF7Y, suggesting a lower nitrification capacity in this long-term fallow soil. However, CF7Y showed greater abundance of denitrification-related genes, particularly norB and narJ [43], pointing to an elevated potential for denitrification. The nasB gene, involved in assimilatory nitrate/nitrite reduction [43], was also predominantly enriched in CF7Y, possibly reflecting microbial strategies to retain nitrogen under conditions of limited inorganic N input. In the CF7Y field, the absence of additional nutrient inputs—such as ash-derived minerals—results in limited replenishment of inorganic nitrogen forms like NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻. Over time, the accumulation of undecomposed plant residues, coupled with slow mineralization processes, leads to reduced conversion of organic nitrogen into plant-available inorganic forms. Furthermore, in the absence of rice cultivation, there is no active nitrogen uptake by plants, increasing the likelihood of nitrogen loss through nitrate leaching or gaseous emissions via denitrification. These processes collectively contribute to the gradual depletion of the soil’s inorganic nitrogen pool.

Analysis of gene abundance related to phosphorus cycling (Fig. 4b) revealed distinct differences between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils, driven by land-use history. In the RSC5Y soil, the phoX gene—which encodes for an extracellular alkaline phosphatase [81]—was strongly enriched. This suggests enhanced phosphate solubilization and mineralization activity, likely driven by microbial demand for phosphorus in post-cultivation soil where plant uptake and microbial turnover are high. Additionally, glpQ, involved in glycerol metabolism and organic phosphorus mobilization [82], and phy, a phytase gene involved in phytate degradation [83], were more abundant in RSC5Y. This pattern indicates a higher microbial potential for accessing both organic and inorganic phosphorus forms following recent biomass input and burning. Conversely, the CF7Y soils showed depletion of phoX, implying a lower microbial capacity for phosphate mobilization. The long-term fallow period, absence of ash-derived minerals, and reduced microbial turnover likely result in limited phosphorus bioavailability and reduced microbial demand for phosphorus-releasing enzymes.

Gene profiling of sulfur metabolism (Fig. 4c) demonstrated distinct patterns between CF7Y and RSC5Y soils, reflecting shifts in microbial sulfur transformation pathways driven by land-use history. In RSC5Y soils, key genes associated with sulfur oxidation—such as dmoA, sorA, comD, and soxD—were significantly depleted, indicating a reduced potential for sulfur oxidation. This suppression is further corroborated by the decreased expression of soxZ, soxD, and soxC, which are integral components of the Sox (sulfur oxidation) multienzyme complex [84]. The decline in these genes may be linked to rapid shifts in the redox environment following post-fire, cultivation, and subsequent crop harvesting, all of which can alter microbial community composition. Fire-induced heat is known to cause substantial microbial mortality, particularly in surface soils where temperatures are highest, disproportionately affecting sulfur-oxidizing bacteria. In contrast, CF7Y soils showed enrichment of soxZ, soxD, and soxC, indicating that microbial sulfur oxidation activity was more pronounced in the long-term fallow site. The stable conditions and organic matter accumulation likely support sulfur-oxidizing microbial populations, especially those that thrive in low-disturbance environments. Interestingly, the dddP gene, which is involved in the degradation of dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) [85]—a key organosulfur compound produced by plants and microbes—was enriched in RSC5Y. This may indicate increased methylated sulfur metabolism, potentially linked to the decomposition of fire-altered biomass and recent plant activity (e.g., rice roots and residues), which contribute to DMSP release.

The genes nxrA and nrfA are involved in key processes within methane and nitrogen cycling (Fig. 4d). Specifically, nxrA encodes a subunit of nitrite oxidoreductase, indicative of active nitrite oxidation and a crucial step in nitrification, which is typically enhanced in disturbed soils [86]. Additionally, both nxrA and nrfA are associated with anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) pathways [46]. The nrfA gene facilitates the reduction of nitrite to ammonium through the dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) pathway, contributing to nitrogen retention under anaerobic conditions [87]. The pps gene, involved in the serine cycle for methane metabolism, catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to phosphoenolpyruvate—an essential reaction in gluconeogenesis—supporting microbial energy regeneration and carbon flow in recently disturbed environments [46, 88]. Furthermore, amoA, a well-established marker gene for ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria, reflects the presence of active nitrification processes in the soil [46]. In contrast, the genes xoxF4, hprA, and mttC were more abundant in CF7Y soils. xoxF4 is implicated in methanol oxidation, a process relevant to methylotrophic metabolism [89], while hprA—part of the serine cycle—converts hydroxypyruvate to glycerate, indicating methylotrophic or autotrophic activity associated with organic matter decomposition [46, 90]. The mttC gene, a component of the methanogenesis pathway, participates in methyl group transfer reactions essential to archaeal methane production under anaerobic conditions [91]. Together, the distinct gene expression patterns between RSC5Y and CF7Y underscore the influence of land-use history on microbial functional potential, with recent disturbances favoring genes linked to rapid nutrient turnover and long-term fallow conditions promoting anaerobic carbon cycling processes.

Soil microbial functional diversity and metabolic activity

The amino acid transport and metabolism category was the most abundant in both CF7Y and RSC5Y soils (Fig. 5a), indicating a high metabolic potential for protein turnover and nitrogen cycling. Amino acid metabolism is essential for microbial growth, community maintenance, and nitrogen transformations, such as ammonification and assimilation [92]. The consistently high abundance across both fields suggests that microbial communities are actively engaged in organic nitrogen utilization, likely fueled by plant residues in RSC5Y and decomposing litter in CF7Y.

The energy production and conversion, along with carbohydrate transport and metabolism, categories were particularly enriched in CF7Y (Fig. 5a). These categories are associated with aerobic respiration, fermentation, and decomposition of complex carbon compounds. Shoemaker et al. [93] reported that genes associated with energy metabolism play a critical regulatory role in bacterial responses to environmental stress. The higher abundance in CF7Y suggests that long-term litter accumulation and stable soil conditions have allowed microbes to develop efficient energy-generating pathways and carbohydrate-degrading capabilities. The CF7Y microbial community may be adapted to slow, sustained decomposition and carbon turnover under nutrient-limited but stable conditions.

Notably, signal transduction mechanisms accounted for up to 7.17% of total functional categories in RSC5Y (Fig. 5a), reflecting a more responsive or adaptable microbial community. Signal transduction genes allow microbes to sense and respond to environmental fluctuations [94, 95]). In this study, these genes likely responded to changes in nutrient availability, root exudate composition, and microclimatic conditions induced by fire and cropping activities. This enrichment suggests a dynamically shifting microbial ecosystem, capable of rapidly adjusting to disturbance and resource pulses. Microbial taxa in RSC5Y likely prioritize environmental sensing and competitive adaptation in a fluctuating, recently disturbed habitat.

According to the KEGG pathway analysis, the metabolic pathways category was the most abundant in both CF7Y and RSC5Y (Figs. 6a and 6b), encompassing a wide array of microbial functions including energy production, biosynthesis, and degradation. This predominance suggests a high level of general metabolic activity across both land-use systems, indicative of robust microbial participation in soil biochemical processes. Notably, the microbial metabolism in diverse environments pathway exhibited greater relative abundance in CF7Y compared to RSC5Y, implying that CF7Y supports a more functionally versatile microbial community. This may result from enhanced microhabitat heterogeneity and prolonged accumulation of organic matter over the seven-year fallow period, which likely favored the persistence of slow-growing, niche-specialized taxa with diverse metabolic capabilities.

Conversely, pathways associated with the biosynthesis of amino acids and ABC transporters were slightly more enriched in RSC5Y (Figs. 6a and 6b). This likely reflects microbial adaptation strategies to recent environmental disturbances (e.g., clearing and burning), characterized by the need for rapid nutrient acquisition and competitive interactions [96]. ABC transporters are critical for nutrient uptake, efflux of toxic compounds, and microbial communication [97], particularly under fluctuating soil conditions.

With respect to energy metabolism, CF7Y exhibited marginally higher expression of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, butanoate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism pathways (Figs. 6a and 6b). These patterns are consistent with microbial communities adapted to stable carbon inputs and sustained degradation of plant-derived substrates. In contrast, RSC5Y showed relatively higher levels of oxidative phosphorylation and pyrimidine metabolism (Figs. 6a and 6b), possibly reflecting increased energy demands and DNA synthesis associated with microbial proliferation following recent disturbance.

Conclusion

Despite similar microclimatic and topographic settings, the CF7Y (continuously fallow) and RSC5Y (recently burned and cultivated) fields demonstrated distinct soil microbial profiles, shaped by their land-use histories. The RSC5Y field harbored higher microbial richness and diversity, reflecting a more responsive and dynamic community likely driven by recent disturbance and regrowth. Functional gene profiles revealed increased expression of genes related to nitrification (e.g., amoC_B, nxrB) and nitrogen fixation (nifD, nifK) in RSC5Y, indicating enhanced nitrogen transformation potential. Conversely, CF7Y soils exhibited elevated expression of denitrification genes (norB, narJ), suggesting a greater propensity for nitrogen loss via gaseous emissions. RSC5Y also showed enrichment in genes linked to phosphate metabolism, while sulfur oxidation genes were more prominent in CF7Y. Additionally, CF7Y soils displayed broader metabolic capabilities, consistent with microbial adaptation under long-term fallow conditions, whereas RSC5Y communities emphasized nutrient assimilation and signaling pathways. These findings support the hypothesis that rotational shifting cultivation significantly alters soil microbial structure and function, with implications for nutrient cycling and ecosystem recovery in tropical upland systems.
