Associated Congenital Anomalies in Gastrointestinal Tract Atresias: An Early Outcome Analysis from a Turkish Tertiary Center
Authors: Özlem Balcı, İbrahim Karaman, Derya Erdoğan, Ayşe Karaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Gastrointestinal (GI) tract atresias occur in approximately 1 in 2000 live births and frequently present with associated congenital anomalies. This study aimed to determine the frequency and spectrum of associated anomalies in neonates with GI atresias and provide screening recommendations.Materials and Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 150 neonates with congenital GI atresia at a tertiary pediatric surgery referral center over 7 years. All patients underwent systematic screening, including echocardiography, abdominal ultrasonography, cranial ultrasonography, and radiographic evaluation. Demographics, atresia type, associated anomalies, and in-hospital outcomes were evaluated.Results:The cohort comprised esophageal atresia/tracheoesophageal fistula in 33.3%, anorectal malformations in 34%, duodenal atresia in 18%, jejunoileal atresia in 10%, colonic atresia in 2.7%, and pyloric atresia in 2%. Associated anomalies were present in 87.3% of patients. Cardiovascular anomalies were most common (57.3%), followed by genitourinary (50%), central nervous system (19.3%), and limb (12.7%) anomalies. Atrial septal defect was the most frequent cardiovascular finding. Overall, in-hospital mortality was 14%. Sepsis was the leading cause of death (33%). Prematurity and low birth weight were significant mortality predictors, while the presence of associated anomalies was not. VACTERL association (66% mortality) was identified as a high-risk subgroup.Conclusions:GI atresias are frequently associated with multisystem anomalies, particularly involving the cardiovascular and genitourinary systems. Systematic screening for associated anomalies is recommended in all neonates with GI atresia for comprehensive care planning. While anomalies are highly prevalent, prematurity and low birth weight remain the primary determinants of mortality.

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) tract atresias represent a significant portion of neonatal surgical practice, occurring in approximately 1 in 2000 live births.[12] Despite advances in surgical techniques and neonatal intensive care, GI atresias remain associated with prolonged hospitalization and considerable mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings.[34]

The presence of an anomaly in one organ system increases the likelihood of anomalies in other systems.[5] GI atresias are frequently accompanied by additional congenital malformations, which contribute significantly to the morbidity and mortality of affected patients.[6] The well-established VACTERL association exemplifies this phenomenon, with vertebral, anorectal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, and limb anomalies occurring together at rates exceeding chance.[6] Recent studies have reported that 52%–82% of patients with VACTERL association have esophageal atresia/tracheoesophageal fistula (EA/TEF), with triple atresia (concurrent EA, duodenal atresia, and anorectal malformations [ARM]) carrying particularly high mortality.[67]

Early recognition and effective management of associated anomalies can prevent adverse outcomes and guide prognosis.[8] Global studies have demonstrated that delayed presentation to surgery significantly increases mortality in GI tract anomalies, with children in low- and middle-income countries having 79% higher probability of delay and correspondingly higher mortality.[4] This study aimed to identify the spectrum of anomalies accompanying GI atresias through systematic screening and provide practical recommendations for anomaly evaluation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No. 26, dated March 27, 2012). All neonates diagnosed and treated for congenital GI tract atresias at a tertiary pediatric surgery referral center, between January 2005 and December 2011, were included. This center serves as a regional referral hub for complex neonatal surgical cases. Follow-up was from admission until discharge or death. During this period, 346 neonates underwent surgical intervention, of which 150 had GI atresias. Patient data were retrospectively extracted from hospital information systems and individual medical records. Demographic information, operative notes, imaging reports (echocardiography, ultrasonography, radiography), laboratory results including chromosomal analyses, and discharge summaries were systematically reviewed using a standardized data abstraction form.

GI atresias were classified by anatomical location: EA/TEF, pyloric atresia, duodenal atresia (including annular pancreas), jejunoileal atresia, colonic atresia, and ARMs. EA/TEF was subclassified according to the Gross classification.[9] Duodenal obstructions were categorized as intrinsic (Types 1–3) or extrinsic (annular pancreas, malrotation).[2] ARM was classified according to the Krickenbeck classification.[10] Jejunoileal atresia was classified by anatomical location (jejunal vs. ileal) and morphological type. Colonic atresia and pyloric atresia were classified by morphological type. Subtype distributions for all atresia types are presented in Table 1; however, subtype-specific outcome analysis was not performed due to small sample sizes within individual categories. Prematurity was defined as gestational age ≤37 weeks, and low birth weight as <2500 g.

All patients underwent systematic evaluation for associated anomalies. The screening protocol included: (1) review of prenatal ultrasonography records when available; (2) detailed physical examination; (3) babygram for primary diagnosis and evaluation of vertebral/skeletal anomalies; (4) echocardiography within 48 h of admission; (5) abdominal ultrasonography for additional intraabdominal anomalies, particularly urinary tract evaluation; (6) transfontanel ultrasonography for central nervous system (CNS) assessment; (7) spinal ultrasonography and sacral radiography for ARM patients; and (8) chromosomal analysis when clinically indicated. Prenatal ultrasonography records were reviewed when available. Detailed physical examination was performed in all patients. Echocardiography was performed within the first 48 h of admission, preferably in the preoperative period. Abdominal ultrasonography for genitourinary evaluation and transfontanel ultrasonography for CNS assessment were performed during the hospital stay. Babygram (chest and abdominal radiography) was obtained in all patients for primary diagnosis and was also re-evaluated for vertebral and skeletal anomalies. For ARM patients, spinal ultrasonography and sacral radiography were additionally performed before discharge. When spinal ultrasonography or sacral radiography revealed pathological findings, neurosurgical consultation and MRI were planned during follow-up. Chromosomal analysis was performed when clinically indicated.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum) and compared using Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of mortality, including sex, gestational age, birth weight, primary diagnosis (atresia location), and presence of associated anomalies as independent variables. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 150 neonates with GI atresia were included. The male-to-female ratio was 1.7:1. Median gestational age was 38 weeks (range: 25–43), and median birth weight was 2850 g (range: 600–4500). Prematurity was present in 33% of patients, and 33% had low birth weight. 53 percent of patients were diagnosed within the first 24 h of life, and 80% within the first 72 h. Proximal GI tract atresias (esophageal, pyloric, duodenal, and jejunoileal) showed significantly higher rates of prematurity (P = 0.02) and low birth weight (P = 0.001) compared to distal atresias (colonic, anorectal). Prenatal ultrasonography records were available for 112 patients (74.7%). Polyhydramnios was detected on prenatal ultrasonography in 22% of proximal atresias but in none of the distal atresias. The prematurity rate was 36% in EA/TEF, 67% in pyloric atresia, 48% in duodenal atresia, 53% in jejunoileal atresia, 0% in colonic atresia, and 16% in ARM.

Associated anomalies were detected in 131 of 150 patients (87.3%). Only 19 patients (12.7%) had isolated GI atresias. Cardiovascular anomalies were the most common (57.3%), with atrial septal defect (ASD) most frequent, followed by patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and ventricular septal defect (VSD). Other cardiac findings included pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary stenosis, aortic stenosis, Tetralogy of Fallot, coarctation of the aorta, dextrocardia, and transposition of great arteries. Genitourinary anomalies were present in 50% of patients, with hydronephrosis being the most common. CNS findings included subependymal cysts, cerebellar hypoplasia, and corpus callosum hypoplasia. Tethered cord was identified predominantly in ARM patients, and sacral/spinal anomalies were significantly more frequent in ARM patients (78% of all spinal anomalies; P = 0.001). Radiographic evaluation (babygram) identified vertebral anomalies in 14 patients (9.3%), including hemivertebrae, butterfly vertebrae, and sacral agenesis. Thoracic and pulmonary anomalies were observed only in EA/TEF and ARM patients. Chromosomal analysis was performed in 35 patients (23.3%). EA/TEF patients (n = 50) showed 98% associated anomaly rate, with cardiovascular anomalies in 70%. Six patients (12%) met criteria for VACTERL association. ARM patients (n = 51) had a 92.2% associated anomaly rate, with cardiovascular anomalies in 58.8%, genitourinary in 58.8%, and tethered cord in 16%. Sacral and spinal anomalies correlated with ARM severity. Duodenal atresia patients (n = 27) had 78% associated anomalies, with cardiovascular in 40.7%, Down syndrome in 22%, and malrotation in 22%. Jejunoileal atresia patients (n = 15) had a 73% anomaly rate, with cardiovascular in 40%, malrotation in 26%, and gastroschisis/omphalocele in 13%.

Median hospital stay was 12 days (range: 1–115). Jejunoileal atresia patients had the longest stay (median 20 days), while ARM patients had the shortest (median 9 days). Atresia location, prematurity, and low birth weight were significant determinants of duration hospital stay (P < 0.05), while associated anomaly was not.

Overall in-hospital mortality was 14% (21/150). The main causes of death were sepsis (n = 7, 33%), anastomotic complications in long-gap EA (n = 4, 19%), cardiopulmonary failure (n = 3, 14%), prematurity-related complications (n = 2, 10%), and other causes, including associated severe anomalies (n = 5, 24%). Mortality by primary diagnosis was: EA/TEF 18% (9/50), ARM 9.8% (5/51), duodenal atresia 11% (3/27), jejunoileal atresia 13% (2/15), colonic atresia 0% (0/4), and pyloric atresia 67% (2/3).

Prematurity (22% vs. 10%; P = 0.044) and low birth weight (22% vs. 10%; P = 0.043) were significantly associated with mortality. The presence of associated anomalies was not significantly associated with mortality (14.6% vs. 10%; P = 0.739). On multivariate logistic regression analysis, prematurity (odds ratio [OR]: 2.8, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.1–7.2, P = 0.032) and low birth weight (OR: 2.6, 95% CI: 1.0–6.8, P = 0.048) were significant independent predictors of mortality. Sex (P = 0.412), primary diagnosis (P = 0.523), and presence of associated anomalies (P = 0.684) were not significant predictors. However, the VACTERL association had 66% mortality in EA/TEF patients.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that GI atresias are rarely isolated malformations. The high prevalence of associated anomalies (87.3%) in our cohort exceeds many literature reports of 30%–70%,[11] likely reflecting our institution’s role as a tertiary referral center for complex neonatal surgical cases and our systematic screening protocol. Our findings, compared with published literature, are presented in Table 5. Major congenital anomalies occur in approximately 1%–2% of live births in the general population;[15] our findings demonstrate that this rate is markedly elevated when a GI atresia is present, supporting the concept that anomalies in one organ system increase the likelihood of anomalies elsewhere.[5]

Advances in prenatal ultrasonography have enabled earlier detection of GI atresias. Polyhydramnios, resulting from impaired fetal swallowing and absorption of amniotic fluid, serves as an important prenatal marker for proximal atresias.[1] In our series, polyhydramnios was detected in 22% of proximal atresias overall, which is somewhat below literature reports of 32%–60% for EA/TEF[19] and 63% for pyloric atresia.[16] Importantly, polyhydramnios was absent in all distal atresias (colonic and ARM), consistent with their location distal to major absorptive surfaces. The association between polyhydramnios and prematurity has significant clinical implications. While the general population’s prematurity rate is approximately 8%, we observed 33% overall prematurity in GI atresias.[117] This proximal-distal gradient was statistically significant (P < 0.001) and has important implications for prenatal counseling when proximal GI tract atresia is suspected.[17]

Cardiovascular anomalies were the most frequent associated findings, present in 57.3% of patients. This dramatically exceeds the general population incidence of congenital heart defects (6–8 per 1000 live births).[18] This association reflects shared embryological origins and the vulnerability of both systems during the critical period of organogenesis.[11] The predominance of septal defects (ASD most common, followed by PDA and VSD) aligns with published literature.[11] As Spitz emphasized, preoperative echocardiography in EA patients serves dual purposes: detecting cardiac anomalies that may impact perioperative management, and determining aortic arch position to plan the surgical approach.[11] While cardiovascular anomalies were prevalent in our series, their presence alone did not significantly increase mortality, reflecting advances in pediatric cardiac care.

Genitourinary anomalies were present in 50% of patients, substantially exceeding literature reports of 5%–24% for EA/TEF patients.[11] This higher detection rate likely reflects our routine abdominal ultrasonographic screening. Hydronephrosis was the most common finding (24% of EA/TEF patients), followed by undescended testis, inguinal hernia, and hypospadias. The high prevalence underscores the importance of routine renal ultrasonography in all neonates with GI atresia. In ARM patients, genitourinary anomalies occurred in 58.8%, consistent with recent literature reports of 50%–60%.[814] Adults with ARM continue to require lifelong management, with recent studies reporting high rates of psychiatric disorders (52%) and substance use (31%), emphasizing the need for comprehensive long-term care.[19] The association between ARM severity and genitourinary anomalies is well-established, with the highest rates in cloacal malformations (up to 90%).[2021]

CNS and spinal anomalies were present in 19.3% of patients, significantly exceeding the general population rate (approximately 3 per 1000 live births). Notably, 78% of all spinal anomalies occurred in ARM patients (P = 0.001). Tethered cord was identified in 16% of ARM patients, and sacral anomalies in 10%. This strong association between ARM and spinal anomalies is well-documented.[22] We observed that sacral and spinal anomaly rates correlated with ARM severity, being lowest in perineal fistulas and highest in rectourethral and rectovesical malformations. This is consistent with findings that anomaly incidence is 13-fold higher in high anorectal defects.[14] Tethered cord has significant implications for urinary and bowel function, although its independent prognostic impact is difficult to separate from associated sacral hypodevelopment.[2223] We recommend spinal ultrasonography within the first 3 months of life in all ARM patients, with MRI follow-up as indicated.

Our EA/TEF patients (n = 50) demonstrated associated anomaly rates of 98%, substantially higher than the 30%–70% typically reported.[11] Comparing our findings with Spitz’s 253-patient series: Cardiovascular anomalies 70% versus 29%, genitourinary 50% versus 14%, limb/vertebral 10% versus 10%, additional GI system 12% versus 13%, and ARM 16% versus 14%.[11] The higher rate of associated cardiovascular anomalies in our series likely reflects comprehensive screening. Recent studies from resource-limited settings report comparable outcomes between one-stage and staged repair approaches, with survival rates of approximately 79%.[24] VACTERL association, present in 12% of our EA/TEF patients, carried strikingly high mortality (66%). This exceeds Spitz’s report of 24% mortality in VACTERL patients[11] and identifies this subgroup as requiring particularly intensive multidisciplinary management. Two EA/TEF patients had concurrent duodenal atresia (4%), consistent with reports of 6% duodenal obstruction in EA patients.[25] EA/TEF mortality in our series was 18%, with survival stratified by Spitz classification: Group 1 (87%), Group 2 (81%), and Group 3 (67%).[11]

Duodenal atresia patients had associated anomalies in 78%, with cardiovascular anomalies in 40.7% and Down syndrome in 22%. National database studies have demonstrated that infants with Down syndrome have a significantly increased risk of concomitant small intestinal atresia and Hirschsprung disease, with this combination associated with 8.6-fold higher mortality.[26] While our Down syndrome rate is somewhat below the 30%–40% typically reported,[212] it aligns with contemporary series showing 26%.[27] An interesting observation was that 5 of 6 syndromic duodenal obstruction patients had annular pancreas rather than intrinsic duodenal atresia, and 23% of all annular pancreas cases had Down syndrome compared to only 3% of intrinsic atresias. This is consistent with findings of 25% Down syndrome in annular pancreas.[28] Malrotation accompanied 22% of duodenal atresia cases. Recent meta-analyses of minimally invasive duodenal atresia repair have shown comparable outcomes between diamond-shaped and side-to-side anastomosis techniques.[29] Enhanced recovery protocols for congenital duodenal obstruction have demonstrated feasibility with earlier enteral feeding without increased complications.[30] Mortality in duodenal atresia was 11%, with prematurity and low birth weight as significant risk factors, consistent with literature reports of survival rates exceeding 90%.[1227]

Jejunoileal atresias are generally considered to result from in utero vascular accidents rather than embryological malformation, which traditionally explains their lower rate of associated anomalies.[31] However, our series showed 73% associated anomaly rate with cardiovascular anomalies in 40%, exceeding the <10% typically reported.[31] Recent UK multicenter data on 159 jejunoileal atresia patients demonstrated that primary anastomosis was associated with shorter time to full feeds (21 vs. 60 days), shorter hospital stay (28 vs. 78 days), and lower mortality (0.9% vs. 11.1%) compared to enterostomy formation.[13] Consistent with the vascular etiology, 26% of our patients with jejunoileal atresia had malrotation, and 13% had gastroschisis or omphalocele. Mortality was 13%, comparable to literature reports of 7%–11%,[31332] with low birth weight as the only significant risk factor. The longest hospital stay in our series (median 20 days) occurred in this group, reflecting the complex nutritional management required.

Colonic atresia is rare, representing 1.8%–15% of GI atresias.[33] Our four patients (2.7%) showed 50% associated anomaly rate with cardiovascular disease in 25%, below the 47% reported by Etensel et al.[33] Zero mortality in our colonic atresia patients compares favorably with literature rates of 25%–61%.[33] Pyloric atresia is exceptionally rare (<1% of GI atresias).[16] Our three cases all had Type 1 atresia with cardiovascular anomalies in 67%. One patient had epidermolysis bullosa, a well-characterized lethal association with over 90 reported cases in the literature, nearly all fatal.[34] ARM patients demonstrated a 92.2% associated anomaly rate with cardiovascular disease in 58.8%, consistent with recent literature reporting >75%.[8] The male predominance in our series (Male: female ratio 7.5:1) exceeds typical reports.[835] ARM mortality was 9.8%, with most deaths attributable to complex associated anomalies rather than the primary malformation.[835]

A key finding of this study is that prematurity and low birth weight, rather than the presence of associated anomalies, were the primary determinants of mortality. Overall mortality was 14%, with 22% mortality in premature/low birth weight infants versus 10% in term/normal weight infants (P < 0.05). The presence of at least one associated anomaly was not significantly associated with mortality (14.6% vs. 10%; P = 0.739). Global studies have similarly identified prematurity as a significant independent risk factor, with mortality exceeding 40% in resource-limited settings where delayed surgical presentation is common.[34] The lack of association between anomaly presence and mortality in our series likely reflects the minor nature of many detected anomalies and improved management of major anomalies. However, the VACTERL association in EA/TEF patients had 66% mortality, identifying this as a high-risk subgroup.

Our cohort of 150 patients includes all types of GI atresias evaluated with a uniform screening protocol. Most published series focus on individual atresia types; this comprehensive single-center data adds valuable comparative epidemiological information. Furthermore, systematic screening yields substantially higher anomaly detection rates than typically reported. Based on our findings, we recommend the following screening protocol for all neonates with GI atresia: Detailed physical examination, babygram, echocardiography within the first 48 h (preferably preoperative), abdominal ultrasonography, and transfontanel ultrasonography for all patients. For ARM patients, spinal ultrasonography and sacral radiography should additionally be performed. Chromosomal analysis should be performed when clinically indicated.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential information bias. The study period (2005–2011) represents a historical cohort; the delay in publication was due to this study originating as an institutional thesis project. However, the embryological associations underlying GI atresias and their congenital anomalies remain fundamentally unchanged, and our findings have been contextualized within contemporary literature to ensure clinical applicability. Some subgroups had insufficient numbers for robust statistical analysis. As a tertiary referral center, our patient population may represent more complex cases than would be encountered in general practice. Variables such as surgical technique and detailed postoperative complication profiles were not systematically analyzed across all atresia types, which we acknowledge as a limitation. The heterogeneous nature of this cohort, encompassing six distinct atresia types with different surgical approaches and complication profiles, precluded a meaningful unified surgical outcome analysis within this anomaly-focused study. However, postoperative complications that directly impacted mortality, such as anastomotic complications in long-gap EA (19% of deaths) and sepsis (33% of deaths), were reported as causes of death. Nevertheless, our findings support systematic screening for associated anomalies in all neonates with GI atresias, including echocardiography, abdominal and spinal ultrasonography, and skeletal survey.

CONCLUSIONS

GI atresias are rarely isolated malformations, with 87.3% of patients having at least one additional organ system anomaly. Cardiovascular (57.3%) and genitourinary (50%) systems are most commonly affected. Proximal atresias are associated with polyhydramnios and increased prematurity. Despite the high prevalence of associated anomalies, prematurity and low birth weight remain the primary determinants of mortality. VACTERL association identifies a particularly high-risk subgroup. Systematic screening for associated anomalies is recommended in all neonates with GI atresia for planning of comprehensive care.
