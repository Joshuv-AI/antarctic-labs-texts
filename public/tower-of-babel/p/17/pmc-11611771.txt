Genomic and transcriptomic profiling of pre‐ and postneoadjuvant chemotherapy triple negative breast cancer tumors
Authors: Tomomi Nishimura, Ravi Velaga, Norikazu Masuda, Kosuke Kawaguchi, Shuji Kawaguchi, Masahiro Takada, Yurina Maeshima, Sunao Tanaka, Yuichiro Kikawa, Takayuki Kadoya, Hiroko Bando, Rikiya Nakamura, Yutaka Yamamoto, Takayuki Ueno, Hiroyuki Yasojima, Hiroshi Ishiguro, Satoshi Morita, Shinji Ohno, Hironori Haga, Fumihiko Matsuda, Seishi Ogawa, Masakazu Toi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur understanding of neoadjuvant treatment with microtubule inhibitors (MTIs) for triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains limited. To advance our understanding of the role of breast cancer driver genes' mutational status with pathological complete response (pCR; ypT0/isypN0) prediction and to identify distinct gene sets for MTIs like eribulin and paclitaxel, we carried out targeted genomic (n = 50) and whole transcriptomic profiling (n = 64) of TNBC tumor samples from the Japan Breast Cancer Research Group 22 (JBCRG‐22) clinical trial. Lower PIK3CA, PTEN, and HRAS mutations were found in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)‐high (HRD score ≥ 42) tumors with higher pCR rates. When HRD‐high tumors were stratified by tumor BRCA mutation status, the pCR rates in BRCA2‐mutated tumors were higher (83% vs. 36%). Transcriptomic profiling of TP53‐positive tumors identified downregulation of FGFR2 (false discovery rate p value = 2.07e‐7), which was also the only common gene between HRD‐high and ‐low tumors with pCR/quasi‐pCR treated with paclitaxel and eribulin combined with carboplatin, respectively. Differential enrichment analysis of the HRD‐high group posttreatment tumors revealed significant correlation (p = 0.006) of the glycan degradation pathway. FGFR2 expression and the differentially enriched pathways play a role in the response and resistance to MTIs containing carboplatin treatment in TNBC patients.

INTRODUCTION

Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) is generally more sensitive to cytotoxic chemotherapy than other breast cancer subtypes. Therapeutic responses such as pathological complete response (pCR) 1 , 2 is known to be a favorable prognostic indicator in TNBC. However, no standard biomarkers for improved treatment methods are available. Identifying the genes involved in the mechanisms underlying tumor biology can assist in improving survival rates in treatment‐resistant TNBC with poor prognosis. Previous studies of TNBC have reported the roles of genomic and transcriptomic mechanisms in driving chemosensitivity and resistance in TNBC subtypes. 3 , 4 , 5

We recently undertook a randomized phase II clinical trial (UMIN000023162) in TNBC patients 6 to determine the effects of the microtubule inhibitors paclitaxel and eribulin in combination with carboplatin. Masuda et al. reported a response benefit of 40%–60% when TNBC patients treated with paclitaxel or eribulin combined with carboplatin followed by anthracycline were stratified by homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) score. 6 Homologous recombination deficiency of a cell is the inability of a cell to effectively repair DNA double‐strand breaks using the DNA repair pathways. This suggests that the tumors with good and low response based on the HRD stratification and treatment with paclitaxel or eribulin combined with carboplatin could have a distinct genomic and transcriptomic identifies, leading to differential response to treatment. Only a handful of studies in cell lines and TNBC patients have explored the role of paclitaxel and eribulin at a molecular level. 7 , 8 , 9 These studies identified epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), CD8+ T cells, and microtubule hub genes as potential markers.

In this study, we analyzed paclitaxel or eribulin plus carboplatin‐treated HRD score‐high and ‐low TNBC tumors from the Japan Breast Cancer Research Group 22 (JBCRG‐22) clinical trial to identify unique response markers, enriched gene sets, and pathways. To do so, targeted DNA sequencing was carried out to analyze mutations in driver genes, then the association between mutational profiles and the HRD score and/or the therapeutic responses such as pCR 1 , 2 was evaluated. Differential gene expression analysis followed by gene set and pathway enrichment analysis was also carried out to identify potential response or resistance biomarkers and to determine upstream regulatory mechanisms that are distinct in the JBCRG‐22 cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort and stratification

To understand the genomic and transcriptomic nature of HRD stratified tumors with varying treatment regimen, we obtained informed consent from all subjects who participated in this study. The study was approved by the Kyoto University ethical committee. Information related to patient stratification and randomization is described in Masuda et al. 6 Patients were stratified based on the HRD score, germline BRCA mutations (gBRCAm) and age. Group A are patients aged <65 years with HRD‐high score (≥42) who received four cycles of paclitaxel plus carboplatin (PC) (A1 group) or eribulin plus carboplatin (EC) (A2 group), followed by four cycles of anthracycline. Group B are patients aged <65 years with HRD‐score <42 (HRD‐low) or aged ≥65 years without gBRCAm who were given six cycles of eribulin plus cyclophosphamide (B1 group) or eribulin plus capecitabine (B2 group). The patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Tumors collected at the time of diagnosis (pretreatment) and surgery (posttreatment) were analyzed in this study.

BRCA mutation analysis and HRD score calculation

Tumor BRCA mutations (tBRCAm) and HRD score were measured by Myriad Genetics using DNA extracted from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues. The HRD score is presented as the total sum of telomeric allelic imbalances, large‐scale transitions, and long regions of LOH chromosomal rearrangements. 10 In this study, tumors with “deleterious,” or “suspected deleterious” variants in BRCA were considered as tBRCAm‐positive tumors.

Targeted DNA sequencing of tumor samples

Genome sequencing was undertaken at Kyoto University. Tumor DNA was extracted from 58 FFPE specimens of biopsy material before preoperative drug therapy using a GeneRead DNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen), then analyzed by targeted‐capture sequencing of 189 genes associated with breast cancer (Table S1) using xGen Predesigned Gene Capture Pools (IDT). Genomic DNA (20–100 ng) was enriched for target exons by xGen Prism DNA Library Prep Kit (IDT), and the captured libraries were then sequenced on a DNBSEQ‐G400RS (MGITech) in 150 bp paired‐end mode. Mutation calling was carried out using the Genomon2 pipeline (version 2.6; https://github.com/Genomon‐Project), as previously described. 11 , 12 Following the removal of sequencing errors and single nucleotide polymorphisms, mutations in 28 driver genes reported in a previous study 13 and hotspot mutations reported in the COSMIC database tumors were considered driver mutations. After excluding poor quality samples, 50 samples for their breast cancer driver mutation profiles were included in the correlation analysis with the pCR and HRD score. Additional information can be found in the Appendix S1, section 2.1.

Tumor BRCA mutation calling

As for the analysis of tBRCAm by targeted sequencing, mutations with variant allele frequency ≥0.4 were considered germline variant candidates. Truncating mutations (nonsense mutations or frameshift indels) and missense, synonymous, and splice‐site mutations registered as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” in ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) 14 , 15 were considered pathogenic and tBRCAm positive. In case of any discrepancy in tBRCAm statuses between the Myriad analysis and targeted sequencing, the tBRCAm status determined by Myriad was utilized. Additional information about the variant status can be found in Appendix S1, section 2.11 and Table S2.

Tumor response assessment

The tumor response after the initial phase of chemotherapy, which consisted of paclitaxel plus carboplatin or eribulin plus carboplatin (PC or EC, respectively), was assessed by MRI. The largest diameter of the percentage tumor size before and after PC or EC treatment was calculated based on the MRI. The comprehensive pathological complete response (CpCR; ypT0/isypN0) was used as the definition of pCR in this translational study.

Sample preparation for RNA sequencing and quality control

Deparaffinization was undertaken by melting, and RNA isolation from the pre‐ and posttreatment FFPE tumor tissue slides was prepared using Qiagen's miRNeasy mini kit standard protocol. RNA isolates were processed using Illumina RNA Prep with Enrichment (L) Tagmentation according to the vendor's instructions. RNA samples were sequenced on a Novaseq S4 2 × 75 bp flow cell by XING Genomic Services. All raw reads from RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data were trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.36). 16 Reads were aligned and mapped to the human genome reference genome (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38) and transcript reference (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.84.gtf) using Illumina's DRAGEN (version 07.021.602.3.8.4). The transcripts per million (TPM) count was obtained using TPMcalculator (version 0.0.3) 17 and unique exons were mapped (Figures S1 and S2, Table S3). After the quality control, 64 transcriptomic profiles were used in the analysis. Additional information about the transcriptomic data processing and quality control can be found in Appendix S1, section 2.12.

Differential gene expression analysis

Analyses of differential gene expression (DGE) and commonly expressed genes among different comparison groups were carried out using Qiagen CLC Genomics Workbench 20.0.2 and 20.0.4 (https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/). 18 The different observational groups from the A and B stratified groups considered for DGE and pathway analyses include tBRCAm‐positive versus tBRCAm‐negative groups, tumors <30.5 versus ≥30.5 mm in size, TP53 mutation‐positive versus ‐negative tumors, PIK3CA mutation‐positive versus ‐negative tumors, HRD‐high versus HRD‐low, A group pre‐ versus posttreatment tumors, A1 posttreatment versus A2 posttreatment tumors, and A group pretreatment versus B group pretreatment tumors can be in found in Appendix S1, section 2.13. The TPM values with a minimum count of 1 for the corresponding group were imported to the CLC Genomics Workbench, including a list of 17,098 genes. Unmatched gene IDs were ignored. Differentially expressed genes were identified as genes with p value ≤0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥1, and false discovery rate (FDR) less than 5% (0.05) according to the Benjamini–Hochberg method 19 in expression among groups.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene expression values (log2FC) from the CLC Genomics Workbench were imported into the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) Preranked tool 20 to identify gene sets that are differentially enriched among the observational group comparisons as mentioned in section 2.7. GSEA Constellation Map 21 was used to plot the heatmap and area under curve (AUC) correlation among the observational groups. Based on the enrichment, GSEA results in multiple significantly enriched gene sets, the Constellation Map tool, a gene network‐based tool, assists in interpreting the enrichment results and scores relative to the phenotype. Constellation Map also takes into consideration the density of genes enriched from GSEA and estimate the normalized mutual information (NMI) score to measure the association significance. The TPM values from CLC Genomics Workbench were used as input values to generate the heatmaps and the corresponding AUC correlations. Genes for which the FDR was less than 25% (0.25) and p value was less than 0.01 (1%) or 0.05 (5%) were considered when determining the enriched gene sets.

Pathway and upstream regulatory analysis

Pathway enrichment and upstream regulatory analyses of samples before and after treatment with TPM values were carried out on the 17,098 genes that were included in the differential expression analysis using Qiagen's Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) tool. 22 The IPA z score, which is based on the known neighborhood gene activity analysis, predicts the overall activation or inhibition of the pathway or gene. An overall positive z score ≥2 predicts pathway activation and z score <2 predicts the pathway inhibition.

Statistical analysis

The significance of the relationships between genomic profiling outcomes, such as tBRCAm status, HRD score, presence or absence of driver gene mutations, and pCR as a measure of the therapeutic response was analyzed with Fisher's exact test for comparison of categorical variables and with t tests for comparison of continuous variables. These analyses were undertaken with R version 3.6.3 (the GNU Project). Multiple testing was corrected based on the Benjamini–Hochberg method. 19 A p value less than 0.05 and q value less than 0.1 indicated a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Target capture sequencing outcomes

Following the JBCRG‐22 clinical trial among TNBC subjects with HRD‐high and HRD‐low scores treated with microtubule inhibitors like paclitaxel or eribulin plus carboplatin, this translational research study was undertaken to identify the correlation between the mutational status, treatment response, DEGs, and pathways among the observational groups as described in the above sections.

TP53 and KMT2C mutation‐positive tumors

A total of 50 TNBC tumors (A1 = 8, A2 = 13, B1 = 15, B2 = 14) were analyzed by targeted‐capture sequencing. Mutations were frequently identified in TP53 (n = 36), PIK3CA (n = 16), KMT2C (n = 6), and PTEN (n = 5) (Figure 1, Table S4). Although TP53 mutations were frequently detected regardless of HRD status, the PIK3CA mutation rate was significantly higher in HRD‐low tumors than in HRD‐high tumors (61.1% and 4.8%, respectively [q value = 1.3 × 10−3]), and the same pattern was found for PTEN and HRAS (Figure 2A). Among tumors with mutations in TP53 and KMT2C, the pCR rate tended to be higher than in tumors without mutations (TP53: 25.0% vs. 14.3% [q value = 0.88]; KMT2C: 50.0% vs. 18.2% [q value = 0.56]). However, among tumors with mutations in PIK3CA and HRAS, the pCR rate tended to be lower than in tumors without mutations (PIK3CA: 12.5% vs. 26.5% [q value = 0.88]; HRAS: 0% vs. 23.9% [q value = 0.88]) (Figure 2B). Higher pCR rate in TP53 and KMT2C mutation‐positive tumors could be a sign of cancer cells failing to repair themselves. This observation led us to evaluate and understand the tBRCAm status and its correlation with pCR among the HRD‐high and HRD‐low groups.

BRCA mutational status, HRD score, and correlation with pCR

Understanding the correlation between the HRD score and tBRCAm status could help assess the tumor's ability to repair DNA damage and could guide treatment decisions. Our analysis showed that HRD scores were significantly higher in tBRCAm‐positive cases than in tBRCAm‐negative cases. Median HRD scores were 64 (range, 45–76) and 21 (range, 2–73) in tBRCAm‐positive and tBRCAm‐negative cases, respectively (p value = 6.7 × 10−4) (Figure 3A, Table 2A), and HRD scores were significantly correlated with CpCR (ypT0/is+ypN0) (Figures 3B and S3, Table 2, Appendix S1, section 3.2). We also investigated the potential role of tBRCAm in the treatment response. All the tBRCAm‐positive tumors were HRD‐high and came from the groups that received a carboplatin‐containing regimen PC or EC followed by anthracycline‐based regimen (A1 or A2 group). In the A1 group, the pCR rates were 66.7% (4/6), 100% (3/3), and 57.1% (8/14) in the tBRCA1m‐positive, tBRCA2m‐positive, and tBRCAm‐negative subgroups, respectively (Table 2). In the A2 group, the pCR rates were 0% (0/5) in the tBRCA1m‐positive subgroup, 66.7% (2/3) in the tBRCA2m‐positive subgroup, and 57.1% (16/28) in the tBRCAm‐negative subgroups (Table 2B).

While the regimen consisting of PC followed by anthracycline (PC‐A) regimen (A1 group) achieved pCR rates of over 60% in both tBRCA1m‐ and tBRCA2m‐positive groups, EC followed by anthracycline (EC‐A) (A2 group) resulted in a high pCR rate in the tBRCA2m‐positive subgroup only (Tables 1, 2, 3).

When stratified by tBRCA1m and tBRCA2m, the pCR rates of tBRCA2m tumors were markedly higher than those of tBRCA1m tumors (83% vs. 36%) (Table 2B). For tBRCAm‐ negative TNBC with high HRD scores, the PC‐A and EC‐A regimens produced pCR rates that were not significantly different (Table 2B). Mutational analysis revealed that two of the six tumors in the A2 treatment group with no response to the treatment had a pathogenic variant in BRCA1 at c.188 T > A (p.Leu63*/L63X) (Table S4). Other tumors showed pathogenic variants in BRCA1 at c.1827del (p.Asn609Lysfs*3), c.2021C > T (p.Pro674Leu), and c.3640G > T (p.Glu1214*). These observations suggest that the type of BRCA mutation and treatment regimen might influence tumor response to chemotherapy. Following this finding, we examined the tBRCAm‐positive tumors for a unique set of DEGs and pathways.

Differential gene expression and pathway analysis in tBRCAm‐positive tumors and role of tumor size

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms in tBRCAm‐positive versus tBRCAm‐negative tumors, we undertook differential gene and pathway analyses. We analyzed the DEGs between pretreatment tBRCAm‐positive (n = 13) and tBRCAm‐negative (n = 24) samples (Table S5). We found that six genes were upregulated and five genes were downregulated in the tBRCAm‐positive cohort with FDR p value <0.05 (Figure 4A, Table S5). Transcriptomic profiling based on tumor size revealed 944 genes to be differentially expressed between tumors <30.5 versus ≥30.5 mm in size (n = 24 vs. 25). Among these genes, 861 genes were found to be upregulated and 83 genes to be downregulated in the tumors with a size ≥30.5 mm at FDR p value <0.05 (Figure 4B, Table S6). Differential enrichment cancer hallmark gene sets were identified between tBRCAm‐positive versus tBRCAm‐negative tumors (Table S7). While detecting the relevant gene sets in tumors with <30.5 versus ≥30.5 mm in size, tBRCAm status was not taken into consideration (Table S8). Identifying these DEGs, such as DLK1 and FGFR2 with significant differential expression and enriched pathways, points to the role these significantly differentially expressed genes and of cancer hallmark gene sets like TNFA, EMT, and p53 pathways in the tBRCAm‐positive group that could serve as potential biomarkers for personalized treatment strategies.

Transcriptomic profiling of tumors with TP53 and PIK3CA mutations

Irrespective of tBRCAm status, identifying uniquely enriched gene sets in TP53 and PIK3CA mutation‐positive tumors could improve our understanding of the nature of tumor response/resistance and cancer progression. Differential gene expression analysis between TP53 mutation‐positive (n = 22) and ‐negative (n = 34) tumors revealed that TP53 mutation‐positive tumors showed downregulated FGFR2 expression with an FDR p value of 2.07 × 10−7, while FRA10AC1 and PSPH were upregulated with FDR p values of 2.07 × 10−7 and 4.35 × 10−3, respectively (Figure 4C, Table S9). A similar analysis of PIK3CA mutation‐positive (n = 7) and ‐negative (n = 42) samples revealed 28,265 downregulated genes, including FGFR2, and 32 upregulated genes in PIK3CA mutation‐positive tumors (n = 266), according to an FDR p value <0.05 (Figure 4D, Table S10). It is known that when FGFR2 dysregulated and TP53 mutation is positive, it can lead to improved cancer cell survival. 23 It would be interesting to further evaluate TP53 mutation‐positive tumors, with differentially expressed FGFR2.

Differential gene expression and pathway analysis of HRD‐high and HRD‐low groups

As the HRD score was used as a molecular marker to stratify patients in the JBCRG‐22 clinical trial, we identified DEGs and differentially activated pathways in pretreatment tumors between the HRD‐high, which includes both A1 and A2 subgroups (A group, n = 27), and HRD‐low groups, which includes both B1 and B2 groups (B group, n = 9), using the transcriptomic profiles that passed quality control. Differential gene expression analysis revealed 806 DEGs, including 636 upregulated and 170 downregulated genes (Figure 4E), with an FDR p value <0.05 in the pretreatment A group (HRD high) compared to the pretreatment B group (HRD low) (Table S11). Further analysis of the association of different microtubule genes can be found in Appendix S1, section 3.3 with relevant supporting figure (Figure S5) and table (Table S21).

Differential gene expression and pathway analyses of the A group with and without pCR (pretreatment vs. posttreatment)

Gene expression levels from the patient RNA‐seq profiles were used to identify differentially expressed gene sets before (n = 11) and after (n = 10) treatment in samples from the A group. A total of 309 and 57 genes were found to be significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively, in the posttreatment group (FDR p value <0.05) compared to the pretreatment samples in the A group without pCR (Figure 4F, Table S12). A few common upregulated genes, such as FOS, EGR1, PLIN4, CD36, JUN, and others (Figure 4G,H, Tables S13 and S14), were observed in both the A1 and A2 posttreatment tumors.

In the group A with pretreatment tumors of patients who achieved pCR (n = 16) and those who did not achieve pCR (n = 11), the DLK1 (FDR p value = 7.84e‐9) and FGFR2 (FDR p value = 0.01) genes and the CSN2 (FDR p value = 4.43e‐7) and LALBA (FDR p value = 3.20e‐5) genes were found to be significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively (Figure 4I, Table S15). The IPA of pretreatment (n = 11) and posttreatment (n = 10) tumor samples from patients in the A group predicted that the tumor microenvironment pathway, regulation of EMT by growth factors pathway, and FAK, interleukin‐17 (IL‐17), and IL‐6 signaling were activated (z score >2.00) in the posttreatment tumors (Figure S4, Table S16). When the posttreatment tumors from the A1 and A2 group tumors were analyzed to further investigate enriched gene sets using GSEA Constellation Map, it revealed a significant correlation with KEGG_OTHER_GLYCAN_DEGRADATION with a significant p value of 0.006 and AUC 0.9 (p = 0.009) (Figure 5A). Likewise, GSEA Constellation Map analysis of A group pretreatment versus posttreatment tumors that did not achieve pCR not only revealed significant correlation in previously known cancer genes like MTOR, AKT, p53, and Cyclin D1, but also with BMI1, LEF1, RELA, ATF2, MEL18, and CRX, genes with significant AUC and p values between the pretreatment and posttreatment tumors in the A group with no pCR achieved (Figure 5B). The results from the Constellation Map analysis suggest a potential role of glycan degradation and abovementioned MTOR, AKT, and other molecules to distinguish HRD‐high tumors treated with paclitaxel and eribulin combined with carboplatin.

Identification of common genes associated with pathological responses in pretreatment tumors from groups A and B

Next, to identify common genes associated with pathological responses, we used Venn diagram analysis to compare the transcriptomic profiles of pretreatment samples from patients who achieved pCR/quasi‐pCR (QpCR) in both the A and B groups. Only FGFR2 was found to be common to both prepreatment tumors from patients in group A who achieved pCR and those from patients in group B who achieved QpCR according to FDR <0.01 (Table S17). Upstream regulatory and causal network analysis using the IPA tool suggested different mechanisms of FGFR2 in A1, A2, and B groups. In pretreatment samples from patients in group A who achieved pCR, IPA predicted the involvement of NRG2 (a growth factor), a master regulator that inhibits CSN2, DLK1, FGFR2, and LALBA through ERBB4, ERK, and RB1 (Tables S18 and S19, Figure 6A,B). In pretreatment samples from patients in group B who achieved pCR, a decrease in FGFR2 expression was found to regulate (activate/inhibit) COL1A1, COL3A1, KRT1, and other genes (Figure 6C,D). It would be interesting to further analyze how NRG2 acts as a key regulator of FGFR2 in the A and B group tumors with pCR to pinpoint its contribution to breast cancer suppression and improved response to microtubule inhibitors in the neoadjuvant setting.

Multivariate analysis of correlation between clinical parameters and pCR

Multivariate analysis with logistic regression analysis resulted in tumor size and nodal status at presentation significantly correlated with the pCR rate (p value: 0.03 and 0.04, respectively). Furthermore, tumor response indicated by MRI (largest tumor diameter <50 vs. ≥50%), tumor size after initial chemotherapy with PC or EC (<30.5 vs. ≥30.5 mm) and the HRD score (42–63 vs. >63) were significantly correlated with the pCR rate (p values = 0.01, 0.048, and 0.043, respectively) (Table 3). Univariate analysis of tumor responses to PC and EC in the two treatment groups (A1 and A2, respectively) showed that the largest tumor diameter (<50 vs. ≥50%) was significantly correlated with the response outcome (p value = 2.4 × 10−3) (Table S20).

DISCUSSION

There is a need for more targeted therapies and relevant biological markers associated with treatment responses in TNBC patients. Furthermore, existing treatment methods for TNBC are limited by tumor heterogeneity and poorly understood clinical response or resistance mechanisms. Based on earlier evidence 7 , 8 on hub and tubulin gene sets, only KLF6 expression was found to be significantly upregulated in tumors treated with eribulin combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

The pathogenic mutations in BRCA1 identified in our cohort are located within the functional domains of BRCA1, 24 , 25 , 26 which could lead to treatment resistance in breast cancer. 27 , 28 , 29 Also, irrespective of HRD status, TNBC patients had higher mutation rates in TP53. Exploratory analysis to identify commonly altered genes associated with the therapeutic response in the A and B groups identified FGFR2 as the only gene that was downregulated. The inhibition of NRG2 in pretreatment tumors from patients in group A who achieved pCR (1) was found to regulate ERBB4, ERK, and RB1 inhibition through FGFR2. It is known that the overexpression of FGFR2 and NRG2 30 , 31 , 32 is associated with poor prognosis and increased cell proliferation. NRG2 activates ERBB4 by binding its promoter region, 31 which results in the activation of ERBB4, leading to cancer cell proliferation and survival. In this study, both NRG2 and ERBB4 were predicted to be inhibited in pretreatment tumors from patients in group A who achieved pCR. However, although ERBB4 is known to be regulated by NRG2, how it interacts with FGFR2 in tumors is not well understood. FGFR2 was also downregulated in TP53 and PIK3CA mutation‐positive tumors. An earlier report in premenopausal women with sporadic breast cancer found a positive correlation of double TP53 and FGFR2 mutation‐positive tumors with decreased breast cancer risk. 33 A recent report highlighted the role of FGFR2 in suppressing BRCA1 through the ERK‐YY1 axis and regulating PD‐L1 to promote tumorigenesis in TNBC. 34 This study highlights the need to further evaluate the role of FGFR2, and how it interacts with NRG2 and ERBB4 and regulates the pathways leading to cell death in the presence of microtubule inhibitors combined with platinum‐based drugs. Zingg et al. 23 reported an enriched coamplification of FGFR2/4/19 when the breast tumors had FGFR2 amplification, TP53 driver mutations along with MYC amplifications, and PTEN loss of function alterations. Previously it was also reported in a gastrointestinal cell line study that the inhibition of FGFR2 signaling can alter the DNA repair mechanism by switching to homologous recombination from nonhomologous end‐joining. 35 The evidence suggests a possible differential role of FGFR2 dysregulation in maintaining tumor integrity and DNA repair mechanisms among HRD‐high and ‐low tumors in the presence or absence of TP53 driver mutations.

The results of GSEA of posttreatment samples from groups A1 and A2 suggest an important role of glycan degradation in the A2 posttreatment tumors. Considering the role proteoglycans or glycolipids play a role in the ECM 36 and their interactions with integrins, 37 it would be interesting to further study the role of glycol degradation in TNBC tumors. A more recent study highlights the potential benefits of targeting unregulated sialoglycans and glycoimmune checkpoints in malignant tumors through alpha‐HER2 Ab‐sialidase conjugate, proposing a novel glycoimmune checkpoint therapy technique. 38 Interestingly, glycan degradation is known to influence cancer cell adhesion and migration. With a positive NMI score and significant p value of glycan degradation in our study it is predicted that the glycan degradation could differentiate A1 and A2 posttreatment tumors. These findings suggest a speculative role for glycan degradation in the outcome of eribulin‐containing neoadjuvant treatment.

The IPA‐predicted activated pathways in posttreatment tumors from group A highlight the role of the tumor microenvironment pathway and regulation of EMT by growth factors pathway, and FAK, IL‐17, and IL‐6 signaling were predicted to be activated. Time‐series blood profiling using 294 blood samples from the JBCRG‐22 clinical trial 6 revealed a significant correlation between five cytokines and good treatment outcomes through enhancement of the immune response. 39 This supports the use of transcriptional profiles of the cytokine markers found in this study and could be used to validate their use as response predictive markers in future studies. Our findings involving tumor size as a predictor of the pathological response concurs with earlier reports. Tumor size is a known predictor of the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. 40 , 41

Overall, our study results suggest potential markers like mutations in TP53 and KMT2C, tBRCAm in HRD‐high tumors in tumors with higher pCR rates, and mutations in PIK3CA and HRAS in tumors with lower pCR rates. We hypothesize that the downregulation of FGFR2 in TP53 mutation‐positive tumors achieving pCR in the HRD‐high group could be a response marker. The role of MAP6, MAP7D1, and MAP7D2 could be speculative as response markers towards MTIs in combination with carboplatin. When differentiating A1 versus A2 tumors, we postulate that glycan degradation could be an interesting biological process to study in TNBC individuals.

A limitation of this study was the use of a small sample size, although a prospective design was analyzed. As the results have not been validated, the use of the HRD score, TP53, tBRCAm, PIK3CA, and KMT2C mutation status, together with clinicopathologic parameters and the enriched gene sets reported in this study could serve as preliminary findings contributing to future studies.

In conclusion, we speculated on the use of HRD score, TP53, KMT2C, and PIK3CA mutational status, and FGFR2 expression as response markers in TNBC. Further investigations are needed to understand the role of the HRD score, TP53 mutational status, FGFR2 regulation, and the function of microtubule genes and glycan degradation, to improve pCR prediction and chemoresistance when using microtubule inhibitors.
