Empowering incoming fellows: a structured workshop to enhance teaching and professional skills
Authors: Allison M. Beaulieu, Brian Merritt, Rowan Kelner, Julia Ruggieri, Megan Fix, Theodore Hartridge, Patrick G. Hughes, Christine Raps, Jeffrey Druck, Robert Stephen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransitioning into a graduate medical education (GME) fellowship presents challenges, including increased responsibilities and expectations. Fellows must supervise and teach trainees while navigating early career development. Many enter a fellowship with limited educational training. There is also a lack of specialty-specific resources and infrastructure to adequately prepare incoming fellows in these key educational areas. To support this transition, we developed a workshop focused on supervisory skills, on-shift teaching, and career development. The goal was to build fellows’ confidence and competence in educational roles while consolidating GME resources.MethodsA 4-hour workshop was delivered in Summer 2025 at a large academic medical center and offered to all incoming fellows with participation was limited to 30 attendees. Invitations were distributed in Spring 2025. The curriculum covered goal setting, supervision, feedback, work–life balance, scholarly work, and help seeking. Participants completed pre and post workshop surveys assessing confidence (5 point Likert scale) and a 10 item knowledge assessment. Paired t tests compared pre and post intervention scores.ResultsAmong 28 fellows from 13 specialties who attended, 22 completed both pre and post intervention surveys (79% response rate). Knowledge scores improved from 9.1 to 9.6 (p < 0.01). Significant increases in confidence were observed across several domains, including goal setting, feedback, supervision, and scholarly activity (p < 0.001). Eighty-six percent found the workshop useful and engaging.ConclusionsThis multispecialty workshop was feasible to implement and led to meaningful gains in fellows’ teaching related knowledge and confidence. As a Kirkpatrick Level 2 evaluation, the findings demonstrate short term improvements but do not assess behavioral change in clinical settings. These results support incorporating structured “fellows as teachers” programming early in fellowship training, with plans to expand faculty involvement across additional specialties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09945-7.

Introduction

The transition from residency to fellowship presents a unique set of challenges for both trainees and program directors. Fellows assume increased clinical responsibilities while simultaneously navigating their own educational advancement and early career development. In many programs, fellows are expected to supervise residents and medical students, contribute to teaching, and begin building their academic portfolios. Although fellows are expected to take on teaching and supervisory roles, most receive minimal instruction in educational techniques before starting their fellowships, largely because fellowship programs have limited numbers of fellows, faculty, and resources [1]. As a result, the main focus for new fellows is typically on mastering a new subspecialty and skill set rather than on educational responsibilities. With the exception of education-focused fellowships, instruction in core teaching competencies, such as resident and medical student management, feedback delivery, and effective teaching methods, is often limited [2].

Fellows play a distinct role in academic medicine: while continuing their own advanced clinical training under the guidance of attending physicians, they are frequently responsible for leading care teams composed of students, interns, and residents. Several studies have explored the preparedness of trainees for supervisory roles. Kilbertus et al. [3] reported that senior residents and early-career attending physicians often develop supervisory skills passively through observation, resulting in inconsistent application and quality. The participants in this study reported difficulty managing competing priorities, which negatively impacted their ability to supervise and assess medical students and residents effectively. Providing constructive feedback and conducting accurate resident and medical student assessments were identified as particularly challenging. In this vein, Humbert et al. [4] developed a curriculum for second-year residents to support their supervision of fourth-year medical students.⁴ Their intervention improved participants’ comfort with supervision, assessment, and feedback and enhanced their understanding of the importance of direct feedback in clinical education. Together, these findings support the need for structured educational interventions that prepare senior trainees for supervisory and teaching roles.

In response to this need, we developed a brief, multispecialty curriculum designed to be feasibly implemented across all graduate medical education fellowship programs, including those with only a single fellow per year where traditional educational structures such as academic half-days may not be practical. The curriculum provides introductory training in supervising residents and medical students, bedside teaching, and career development to support fellows during the transition into supervisory roles. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the “Transition to Fellowship” teaching workshop in improving fellows’ teaching knowledge and self-confidence as they assume educational and supervisory responsibilities.

Methods

Study design & setting

We created a four-hour educational workshop, developed by the institutional Director of GME Educational Development, in collaboration with physician educators with a strong background in medical education, which was delivered in August 2025 during the first two months of fellowship programming (Supplement 1). All fellowship programs have a July 1st or August 1st start date, thus a workshop date in mid-August was chosen for the session. The workshop was offered at a large urban academic institution in the Mountain West region that hosts over 100 fellowship programs [5]. The workshop was created to streamline the transition of incoming fellows into their supervisory and educational roles. This workshop was piloted for Emergency Medicine Fellows in August of 2024 and expanded to other fellowship programs in 2025.

Selection of participants

The workshop was available to all incoming fellowship trainees at the host institution and was filled on a first-come, first-served basis, with a cap of 30 participants. Invitations were sent to program directors and coordinators across all fellowship programs in Spring 2025, allowing sufficient time for interested fellowship programs to register their fellows and plan their schedules ahead of the summer session. Participation in this study and attendance at the workshop were voluntary. The participants were asked to complete both a pre- and post-intervention survey, as well as a knowledge assessment.

Ethical issues pertaining to human subjects

This study was conducted under the umbrella protocol “Assessment of Emergency Medicine Training Techniques for ED Personnel Exemption Umbrella” (IRB_00080204). The study was reviewed by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB) and was determined to be exempt research. The exempt determination was confirmed by a designated University of Utah IRB member on July 18, 2025.

Intervention

The intervention included a four-hour in-person workshop. Areas of focus were selected based on literature and institutional needs [6–8]. Institutional needs centered on establishing a generalizable fellowship orientation program capable of consolidating resources across diverse specialties. The topics included (1) goal setting, (2) supervising medical students and residents, (3) giving and receiving feedback, (4) balancing personal and professional duties, (5) scholarly work, and (6) asking for help. Each session was facilitated by a faculty educator utilizing a combination of didactic content, small group discussions, and interactive activities. The GME funded breakfast for participants at a total cost of $300. The workshop was held on campus, incurring no additional expenses.

Measures

Self-reported demographic information, including gender, specialty, postgraduate year, previous supervisory experience and prior formal education training, was collected and is presented in Table 1. Participant knowledge was assessed with a 10-question multiple-choice assessment administered before and after the workshop (Supplement 2). Confidence in workshop topics was assessed via a pre- and post-workshop survey with a 5-point Likert scale (Supplement 3, 1 = not at all confident, 5 = very confident). After the workshop, the participants assessed their perceptions of the utility and engagement of the overall session (Supplement 4).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was a change in the knowledge and confidence of fellows regarding supervising and educating trainees, as well as their own professional growth and development. A secondary outcome included the participants’ perceptions of the usefulness of and engagement with the workshop.

Data collection

The survey and assessment data were collected electronically via Google Forms. The data were then exported into Microsoft Excel and imported into R for further cleaning and analysis.

Data analysis

Demographic variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Confidence items were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all confident, 5 = very confident). Following common practice and research showing parametric methods work well with five-point ordinal scales, these items were treated as continuous for analysis [9, 10]. Pre- and post-workshop scores were compared using paired t-tests. Results are reported as means with standard deviations (SD) and mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d for paired samples (mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the paired differences). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed as a sensitivity analysis; all results were concordant with the parametric findings. Post-workshop ratings of usefulness and engagement were summarized descriptively. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python 3 with SciPy (v1.15) and pandas (v2.3).

Results

A total of 254 fellows across 112 fellowship programs were invited to participate. The workshop was limited to 30 participants by design; 28 fellows from 13 medical specialties enrolled and attended. Of these, 22 completed both pre- and post-workshop surveys (survey completion rate: 79%). Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Knowledge assessment scores improved significantly following the workshop (Table 2). Despite a notable ceiling effect on the 10-item assessment (pre-workshop mean 9.14 out of 10), a paired comparison revealed a statistically significant increase (mean difference 0.45, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.72; p = 0.002; Cohen’s d = 0.76).

Discussion

This study evaluated the Transition to Fellowship Workshop, a brief, structured teaching curriculum designed to support fellows as they assume supervisory and educational roles. Participation in the workshop was associated with notable improvements in fellows’ self-reported confidence across multiple teaching domains, including goal setting, feedback, supervision, and scholarly activity, as well as a modest but statistically significant improvement in objective teaching knowledge. These findings suggest that even a concise educational intervention can positively influence both teaching knowledge and self-efficacy during a critical transition phase in graduate medical education.

The observed improvements are consistent with prior literature on residents-as-teachers and fellows-as-teachers programs, which have demonstrated similar gains in confidence and teaching-related competencies following structured educational interventions [11–13]. In our study, confidence increased by approximately 0.5–1.0 points on Likert scales in key domains, comparable to effect sizes reported in related curricula [13] Although the improvement in knowledge scores was modest, its statistical significance reinforces the value of focused workshops in enhancing foundational teaching knowledge. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that brief, well-designed curricular interventions can meaningfully support fellows as they transition into supervisory and educational roles.

Beyond effectiveness, the workshop was feasible and well accepted. Most participants rated it as useful and engaging, indicating strong acceptability. The multispecialty format likely contributed to this receptivity by fostering interdisciplinary discussion and mirroring real-world teaching across diverse clinical contexts. Pooling fellows from multiple subspecialties also enhanced feasibility by allowing efficient use of faculty and educational resources. This approach aligns with prior reports of multispecialty fellows-as-educators programs that have demonstrated feasibility and effectiveness while mitigating challenges encountered by single-program efforts [14]. Notably, despite its short duration, the workshop addressed a broad range of teaching competencies, from clinical instruction to mentorship and scholarship, suggesting that limited curricular time can be used efficiently to support fellows’ development as educators.

The findings of this study reinforce the importance of providing structured teaching development for fellows as a standard component of graduate medical education. Fellows occupy a pivotal transitional role between residency and independent practice and frequently supervise residents and medical students while preparing for faculty positions. Enhancing fellows’ teaching skills has the potential for downstream benefits, including improved educational experiences for learners and potential positive effects on team communication and patient care [2]. Our results suggest that integrating educator development into the transition to fellowship is both feasible and valuable, even for time-constrained subspecialty trainees.

This study has several limitations that merit consideration. It was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability despite the interdisciplinary composition of the participant cohort. Outcomes relied on self-reported confidence measures and a brief knowledge assessment; reflecting Kirkpatrick Level 2 (Learning).

Participation also varied across specialties, with Emergency Medicine fellowship programs comprising a higher proportion of attendees, likely reflecting prior pilot implementation and the facilitators’ specialty backgrounds.

The expansion from a single program to multiple fellowships represents meaningful growth, despite overall participation involved a minority of eligible programs. Scheduling constraints, variability in existing orientation structures, differences in perceived relevance, and faculty specialty composition may have contributed to this limited uptake. Future iterations will aim to involve facilitators and stakeholders from a broader range of specialties to enhance relevance, promote wider participation, and improve transferability across training programs.

In summary, this evaluation demonstrates that a brief, multispecialty teaching workshop can improve fellows’ teaching knowledge and confidence during the transition to fellowship. These findings support the inclusion of structured educator development within fellowship training and offer a scalable model that can be adapted across institutions and specialties.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a brief, multispecialty Transition to Fellowship workshop is a feasible and acceptable approach to supporting fellows as they assume supervisory and teaching roles. Participation was associated with improvements in teaching-related knowledge and self-reported confidence across several core educational domains, suggesting that even concise interventions can address key gaps in fellows’ preparation for educational responsibilities. While these findings reflect short-term learning outcomes and do not assess sustained behavioral change, they highlight the potential value of structured educational support during the transition to fellowship. The interdisciplinary design and scalable format of this workshop offer a practical model for institutions seeking to provide educator development opportunities across fellowship programs with varying sizes and resources. Further research is needed to examine long-term impact on teaching behaviors and learner outcomes and to optimize implementation across a broader range of specialties.
