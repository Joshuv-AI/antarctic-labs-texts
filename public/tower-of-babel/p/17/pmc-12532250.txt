Disaster Preparedness depends on Disaster Types Expected and the Training, Equipment, and Functioning of Emergency Services
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

With the interest we read the article by Alhamaid et al. on a cross-sectional study that used a self-created survey to examine the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of emergency department (ED) staff (physicians and nurses) regarding disaster preparedness in 92 hospitals across Saudi Arabia.1 It showed that the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of ED staff regarding disaster preparedness varied.1 The conclusion was reached that repeated training is needed to increase the preparedness of ED staff for disasters.1 The study is impressive, but some issues require discussion.

The 1st point is that the nature of disasters is broader than assumed in the article.1 All possible types of disasters and scenarios need to be taken into account for the management of disasters in EDs. In general, disasters can be categorized as natural disasters, technological disasters, wars, fire disasters, explosions, famines and epidemics.2 Natural disasters can be further subdivided into natural disasters caused by endogenous forces (earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis), natural disasters caused by exogenous forces (avalanches, landslides, rockfalls) or natural disasters caused by weather conditions or climate (floods, storm surges, hurricanes, tornadoes, storms). Technical disasters can be further subdivided into transportation disasters (shipping, aviation, rail transport, road transport, cable car accidents, space accidents, mass panics in stadiums or other major events), chemical disasters, oil spills, mining accidents, accidents in nuclear facilities, bridge collapses, accidents at dams, large-scale power failures and disasters caused by side effects to medications or illicit drugs. Depending on the type of disaster, the requirements for ED care can vary greatly. Emergency department near nuclear facilities or chemical plants should be equipped and prepared differently than EDs during a pandemic. An ED near a chemical factory requires a different orientation than an ED in a ski resort.

The 2nd point is that the service provided by an ED in the event of a disaster also depends on the functionality of the ED in the 1st case. If the emergency rooms are directly or indirectly affected by the disaster, they can no longer function as first aid centers, or only to a limited extent. If the power supply fails, the building in which the emergency room is located collapses or access to the emergency room is no longer guaranteed, care will be lost. If the employees of an ED are put out of action due to a pandemic, the ED can close.

The 3rd point relates to the method of data collection. Electronic surveys have some disadvantages: The reliability of the answers cannot be easily verified; it is difficult to judge whether the addressee is actually the person who replies and whether the respondent understands the questions correctly in order to give appropriate answers.3

In summary, the preparedness of ED staff for disasters depends on the types of disasters considered, the quality of staff training, and the equipment and functionality of EDs in the event of an emergency. Emergency department should focus on the specific local or national threat situation and be trained accordingly. In a region where little or no snow falls, preparation for avalanches is not necessary.
