Impact of cardiometabolic index change on stroke risk in Chinese middle-aged and elderly: a prospective study
Authors: Mei Tian, Xiaoyan Ma, Jianxi Liu, Lingling Tong, Wenqing Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData regarding the cardiometabolic index (CMI) changes and stroke are limited. Thus, our objective was to investigate the correlation between CMI changes and stroke among middle-aged and elderly individuals in China.MethodsThe China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) used a multistage stratified sampling approach to survey individuals aged 45 and above. Changes in CMI were calculated as the differences in CMI between 2015 and 2012 divided by the number of years. The lowest third of the CMI changes were negative and categorized as the ‘loss’ group; the middle third exhibited a steady increase and was classified as the ‘stable’ group; the top third showed positive changes and was categorized as the ‘gain’ group. Subsequently, we monitored the participants from 2015 to 2020.ResultsOut of the 4,365 participants observed during the five-year follow-up period, 356 individuals (8.2%) developed stroke. Compared with the lowering group, the ratio (OR) for stroke in the stable group was 1.503 (95% CI: 1.096–2.061); the OR for stroke in the gain group was 1.528 (95% CI: 1.117–2.089). A nonlinear relationship was observed between CMI changes and stroke (Passociation<0.001, Pnonlinearity<0.05).ConclusionOur findings indicate a nonlinear and positive association between CMI changes and stroke in Chinese middle-aged and older people, which can help to develop interventions to prevent stroke with strategies for high-risk groups.Keywords: Cardiometabolic index, Changes, Stroke, CHARLES, Prospective study

Introduction

Stroke has become an urgent issue in the field of global public health, with China leading in the worldwide burden of stroke [1]. Every year, 2.4 million stroke cases, 1.1 million stroke-related fatalities, and 11.1 million stroke survivors are recorded in China alone [2]. Despite rapid developments in medical interventions, many patients with stroke still face a significant risk of death or disability [3–6]. Hence, the identification of modifiable and manageable factors is pivotal in mitigating the occurrence of stroke.

Obesity is the main contributing factor to stroke, especially central obesity [7]. Compared with normal-weight people, obese people have a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease and other diseases [8–11]. The Body Mass Index (BMI) is a commonly used indicator for assessing obesity. However, the BMI cannot differentiate between fat and non-fat components. thus, it cannot accurately reflect the risk of cardiovascular disease for the target population [12]. Recent studies suggest that it is the distribution of body fat, rather than the overall degree of obesity, that contributes to a higher susceptibility to cardiovascular problems [13]. A cardiometabolic index (CMI) is a novel index to reflect visceral fat distribution and metabolic dysfunction proposed by Ichiro Wakabayashi et al. [14] in 2015, which is obtained by the product of the anthropometric measure WHtR and the biochemical measure TG/HDL-C, where WHtR is a simple and low-cost screen for health risks, predicts the risk of cardiovascular disease, and is superior to BMI and WHtR is more beneficial than BMI and WC in screening for metabolic syndrome, assessing the risk of diabetes, and predicting the future onset of diabetes [15, 16]. TG/HDL-C, as a metabolic marker, can be calculated from lipid profiles. Previous studies have confirmed that TG/HDL-C is significantly associated with IR and accurately predicts IR, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome [17–19]. In conclusion, CMI, combining WHtR and TG/HDL-C, integrates abdominal obesity and lipid metabolism indicators. Some investigations have demonstrated a strong association between an elevation in CMI and a variety of ailments, including type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and acute pancreatitis [20–22]. Previous studies have presented a direct association between CMI and stroke [23]. In the above research, CMI were calculated based on single measurements of WHtR and lipid status. The researchers found that single measurements of lipid parameters may be influenced by factors such as diet and medications, resulting in a potentially large degree of variability in these parameters and not fully reflecting lipid exposure in the study populations.

However, there are few reports on the correlation between the pattern of CMI changes and stroke at home and abroad, and there is not enough evidence to show the influence of the pattern of CMI changes on the occurrence of stroke. Based on the above background, this study investigates the correlation between CMI changes and stroke. It analyzes the influence of CMI changes on the progression of stroke to provide clinicians, especially primary healthcare workers, with a simple and efficient theoretical basis for screening or risk assessment of high-risk patients with stroke.

Methods

Study design and participants

The present investigation utilized CHARLS, an exhaustive and representative scientific inquiry focusing on Chinese individuals aged 45. Further information regarding the study population is available in other published sources [24]. All participants provided written informed consent. The CHARLS baseline survey was conducted in 2012, with subsequent follow-up studies in 2012, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Blood tests were administered during the study’s baseline (2012) and third wave (2015). Inclusion criteria for this analysis required participants to be 45 years of age or older with complete high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TG), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), baseline data, and stroke outcome data. Individuals with a history of stroke were excluded. Finally, 4365 participants were obtained according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study population

Assessment of the CMI and stroke

The stroke status of individuals was assessed through conducting face-to-face interviews by asking questions such as, “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?” or “Do you treat stroke using Chinese Traditional Medicine/Western Modern Medicine/Physical Therapy/Acupuncture and Moxibustion/Occupational Therapy/None of the Above?“.

CMI was computed by considering anthropometric parameters (waist-to-height ratio) and blood biochemical parameters (specifically, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglycerides). The formula utilized for the calculation of the CMI is as follows: [TG(mmol/L)/HDL-C(mmol/L)]*WHtR [14].

Assessment of change in CMI

The change in CMI was treated as a continuous variable, calculated as the difference in CMI between 2012 and 2015, divided by the number of years between the two surveys. Given that the period between surveys may vary among individuals, we utilized the average annual change in CMI as the foundation of the study. It is important to note that the overall magnitude of change in CMI is relatively small due to the short time interval between follow-up visits. Furthermore, the change values of CMI encompass not only negative values but also zero and positive values. More critically, a nonlinear dose-response relationship exists between the change values of CMI and stroke. Based on these characteristics, this study draws upon previous categorizations by dividing the mean annual change values of CMI into three equal parts [25]. Specifically, the lowest third of the CMI change values were classified as the ‘loss’ group due to their negative growth characteristics; the middle third was classified as the ‘stable’ group due to their stable growth; and the highest third was categorized as the ‘gain’ group due to their positive growth trend. This categorization, rather than treating changes in CMI as a continuous variable, enables a more precise explanation of its complex association with stroke.

Covariates

Based on previous literature and consideration of biological mechanisms, covariates known to have possible confounding effects were included in this study whenever possible. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, CMI2012, smoking status, and drinking status. Smoking status included the following: never, former, and current; drinking status included the following: never drank, ever drank and currently drank.

Given diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia have been widely shown to be significant risk factors for stroke, it was necessary to exclude their potential confounding effects. To diagnose diabetes, we employed fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels equal to or greater than 126 mg/dL, current usage of anti-diabetic medication, and a self-reported diabetes history that met any of these criteria. Similarly, for hypertension, we relied on a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg or higher, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mmHg or higher, current usage of antihypertensive medication, or self-reported hypertension history meeting any of these criteria. Likewise, for dyslipidemia, we utilized total cholesterol (TC) levels of 240 mg/dL or higher, triglyceride (TG) levels of 150 mg/dL or higher, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), levels of 160 mg/dL or higher, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), levels below 40 mg/dL, current usage of lipid-lowering medication, or self-reported history of dyslipidemia meeting any of these criteria [26].

Statistical analysis

The mean ± standard deviation represents continuous variables that conform to a normal distribution. In contrast, the median and interquartile range denote non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables are expressed using case numbers and percentages. The variance between groups is analyzed using ANOVA, the chi-square test, or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate. Binary logistic regression analysis explores the association between various categories of change in the CMI and stroke incidence. Tristile stratifies the CMI change values, and the median value of the variables within each stratification is introduced as a continuous variable into the regression model to evaluate trends. The CMI changes, and stroke relationship is investigated using restricted cubic splines models. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests are utilized to identify potential influencing factors. To ensure the robustness of the results, we also performed a sensitivity analysis: a competitive risk model was used to account for competitive risk due to mortality (13 participants died). Statistical analyses are executed using R version 4.3.1 (http://www.R-project.org) and Spss software version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). P < 0.05 (two-sided) were deemed statistically significant.

Odds ratios for incident stroke

Out of the 4,365 participants observed during a five-year follow-up period, 356 individuals (8.2%) developed stroke. With adjustment for covariates, the risk of stroke increased with each 1-unit increase in CMI change (OR: 3.876; 95%CI: 1.848, 8.128). Compared to the loss group, the OR for incident stroke was 1.503 (1.096–2.061) for the stable group, and the OR for incident stroke was 1.528 (1.117–2.089) for the gain group (Table 2).

After adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and CMI2012, further analysis using restricted cubic spline regression (Fig. 2) showed that there was a dose-response relationship between the change in CMI values and the incidence of stroke (Passociation<0.001,) and a nonlinear relationship (Pnonlinearity<0.001). It was found that the CMI change value was a protective factor against stroke when the CMI change value was less than or equal to −0.07, and this protective effect disappeared when the CMI change value increased.

Discussion

In our investigation, we confirmed that there are three different categories of CMI changes among Chinese middle-aged and elderly people, namely, the loss group, the stable group, and the gain group. Compared with those in the loss group, individuals in the gain group had the highest risk for incident stroke. In addition, this study found a nonlinear dose-response relationship between CMI changes and stroke in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.

The stroke risk increased by 59.20% and 60.24% in the stable and gain groups, respectively, compared to the loss group. This suggests that we should pay active attention to the effect of the gain group on stroke. In addition, our study found that people in the gain group had the highest risk of stroke. Even though the CMI level was the lowest in the gain group population at baseline (0.90 vs. 1.59), the elevation of CMI may increase the risk of stroke over time, emphasizing the superiority of the dynamic change model over separate measurements. To our knowledge, no similar studies have been conducted.

However, previous studies have suggested a possible link between CMI and CVD risk based on single CMI measurements. For example, a cross-sectional study found that CMI is a strong prognostic indicator for the progression of atherosclerosis, especially among patients with peripheral arterial disease [27]. A study conducted in China with 11,956 adults showed that CMI was significantly predictive of hypertension risk in individuals aged 35 and above [28]. Additionally, a prospective cohort study on patients with hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea revealed a positive correlation between CMI levels and the incidence of new cardiovascular disease [29]. Studies have also identified CMI as an independent risk factor for stroke [23]. Interestingly, research suggests that the association between CMI and ischemic stroke is significantly higher in women [30]. Our study directly confirms a positive correlation between changes in CMI and stroke risk in the general population. Undeniably, moderate visceral fat is necessary for the human body. As an essential endocrine organ, adipose tissue performs various physiological functions. It has been pointed out that lipocalin secreted by adipose tissue can prevent cerebrovascular diseases by reducing inflammatory responses such as improving insulin resistance, inducing elevation of serum HDL-C, and lowering serum TG [31]. This may explain the negative association between low-level CMI change values (<−0.07) and stroke in the present study. However, the exact value of the threshold may need further study.

The specific mechanisms by which CMI increases the risk of stroke are not yet fully understood. However, several aspects may be involved. Firstly, CMI, as a surrogate marker for visceral fat, indicates the accumulation of visceral fat tissue to some extent. In individuals with central obesity, excessive fatty acids and pro-inflammatory adipocytokines are released by visceral fat cells into the portal circulation. This leads to increased liver fat and insulin resistance. Insulin resistance can further activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, increase sympathetic nerve activity, and enhance procoagulant activity. These factors can potentially cause thromboembolism and increase the risk of stroke [32–34]. Furthermore, individuals with central obesity exhibit adipose tissue that releases a more significant amount of reactive oxygen species(ROS) [35]. The heightened presence of ROS can result in a reduction in nitric oxide (NO) while also allowing for the reaction of superoxides with NO, thereby generating peroxynitrite. Peroxynitrite production has been identified as detrimental to the function of endothelial cells [36]. Moreover, increased ROS levels may contribute to the oxidative modification of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and incite immune responses within arterial atherosclerotic lesions. These processes exacerbate the harm inflicted upon the vascular endothelium and subsequently increase the probability of experiencing a stroke [37].

The primary advantage of this study is its use of the CHARLS database, which employs a stratified multistage probability sampling strategy. This method dramatically enhances the reliability of the research. Furthermore, the study categorized the various changes in CMI and examined how these changes evolved. This method offers a fresh perspective in comprehending the link between the patterns of change in CMI and the likelihood of stroke.

Nevertheless, this research also presents certain limitations. Firstly, stroke diagnoses in this study were based on self-reporting, and self-reported stroke events could not be verified and validated because medical records were not available in the CHARLS dataset. However, large-scale studies, such as the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, have reported satisfactory agreement between self-reported stroke events and medical records [38]. Secondly, This reliance on memory could introduce potential recall bias. We must recognize the existence of selection bias due to loss of follow-up. This bias is influenced by factors such as competing events due to death and may underestimate the association between CMI and stroke. However, sensitivity analyses in which we used competing risk regression analyses were consistent with our main results. Thirdly, the changes in cardiometabolic index did not consider the actual period between the measurements of each participant. Blood samples in the CHARLS study were collected only at waves 1 and 3, and we recognize that this limited our ability to detect more specific dynamic trends in CMI. Fourth, although we progressively adjusted for potential confounders, confounder biases such as lack of information/adjustment for stroke prevention strategies cannot be completely ruled out. It is important to note that the cohort included in this study is exclusively from China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other nations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, three distinct CMI change classes were identified in our study. There might be direct relationships between CMI changes and stroke. Monitoring these distinct long-term patterns makes it possible to detect stroke early and potentially improve quality of life.
