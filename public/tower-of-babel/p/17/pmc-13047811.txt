Beyond protection: navigating biomedical HIV prevention and risk compensation in MSM, 2018–2024
Authors: Junfeng Zhang, Zhan Lin, Jing Yang, Qingcui Wu, Jingran Dong, Xiaoyan Zhang, Nahom Kiros Gebregziabher, Jie Yang, Jianyun Bai, Changping Li, Maohe Yu, Zhuang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMen who have sex with men (MSM) face a disproportionately high HIV risk globally. Biomedical prevention strategies, including pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), reduce HIV transmission, but their combined impact on sexual risk behaviors remains uncertain, raising concerns about risk compensation.MethodsFrom 2018 to 2024, MSM in Tianjin, China were recruited through snowball sampling and followed within a longitudinal cohort linked to routine HIV testing services. HIV testing frequency and HIV incidence were analyzed using Poisson regression and Cox proportional hazards models, respectively. Unprotected anal intercourse (UAI) risk scores were constructed from sexual behavior indicators. Mixed-effects models and trajectory modeling assessed the associations between biomedical intervention use and UAI risk.ResultsAmong 8790 repeat-testing MSM, 1858 (21.14%) used biomedical interventions. Intervention users had lower HIV incidence (0.40 vs. 0.62 per 100 person-years; HR = 0.53, 95% CI 0.35–0.80), more frequent testing (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.27–1.33), higher risk scores (β = 0.191, 95% CI 0.162–0.219), more anal intercourse, and lower condom use at last sex and consistently. Trajectory analysis indicated users were more likely in high-risk group. Risk compensation effects tended to be greater among older MSM and those living in suburban areas.ConclusionBiomedical interventions are associated with increased HIV testing and reduced incidence but may elevate UAI risk, especially in older and suburban MSM. Integrating behavioral support into biomedical prevention is essential to mitigate risk compensation and address the prevention paradox in key subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-026-00864-7.

Introduction

The global pandemic of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection remains one of the most serious public health challenges worldwide. According to the UNAIDS Global AIDS Report 2024, approximately 39.9 million [36.1–44.6 million] people were living with HIV globally by the end of 2023, with an estimated 630,000 [500,000–820,000] AIDS-related deaths recorded during the year [1]. Among all affected populations, men who have sex with men (MSM) demonstrate a markedly higher burden of HIV infection and face significantly elevated acquisition risks compared to heterosexual men. Globally, in 2022, the risk of acquiring HIV among MSM was 23 times higher than that of the general population [2]. The HIV prevalence rate among MSM in China from 2001 to 2018 was 5.7%, indicating a significant HIV burden that has become a major challenge in China’s public health sector [3]. In addition, MSM constituted 25.2% of the 1,131,464 new HIV infections reported in China between 2015 and 2024 [4]. A study conducted in New York City reported that HIV incidence among MSM was over 140 times greater than that among heterosexual males [5]. Similarly, a meta-analysis found that in sub-Saharan Africa, HIV prevalence among MSM was 4.94 times that of the general male population [6]. In response to the heightened vulnerability of this key population, prevention strategies have shifted from sole reliance on condom use to a comprehensive approach involving multiple concurrent interventions [7]. In recent years, biomedical strategies such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) have emerged as highly effective preventive tools among MSM [8–10]. For instance, China has started a pilot exploration of PrEP/PEP service aiming for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) control since 2017 [11]. By 2023, nearly five million people globally had initiated PrEP, with approximately 450,000 users in the United States—over 90% of whom were men [12]. A prospective study in Australia in 2016 demonstrated that widespread implementation of PrEP was closely associated with a rapid decline in new HIV diagnoses in New South Wales [13].

Although PrEP and PEP have been shown to effectively prevent HIV infection among MSM, a growing body of research has highlighted the potential impact of these biomedical interventions on sexual behavior patterns within this population. For instance, the use of PrEP and PEP may be associated with an increased frequency of unprotected anal intercourse (UAI) or a higher number of sexual partners, thereby potentially elevating the risk of acquiring other sexually transmitted infections (STIs). This phenomenon is commonly referred to as risk compensation, wherein the perceived reduction in personal HIV risk due to biomedical prevention leads to an increase in high-risk sexual behaviors [14]. In several open-label studies, PrEP users—being aware of their protective medication use—demonstrated significantly higher rates of UAI and STIs [15]. A longitudinal study conducted in Canada also reported a positive association between PrEP use and increased incidence of gonorrhea among MSM [16]. Similarly, a cohort study on PEP found that, following PEP usage, MSM in China exhibited significantly higher rates of recreational use of rush poppers as well as greater involvement with commercial and casual sexual partners [17]. However, not all studies have found an association between PEP use and increased risk behaviors. A non-randomized controlled study among MSM reported that, within 12 months following PEP initiation, 73% of participants reduced the frequency of their high-risk sexual behaviors compared to baseline, and 85% did not experience an increase in STI incidence [18]. In addition, a cohort study conducted in China found that the PEP group reported fewer anal sex acts, fewer online sexual partners, and a lower total number of sexual partners compared to the control group. Moreover, condom use during the past six months and in the most recent sexual encounter was higher among PEP users [19].

PrEP and PEP are both critical components of biomedical interventions aimed at preventing HIV infection. Although PrEP and PEP differ in timing and application—with PrEP offering ongoing protection against anticipated high-risk exposures and PEP serving as emergency prophylaxis after unexpected exposures—both rely on antiretroviral therapy to inhibit HIV replication. The World Health Organization [20] underscores that combining PrEP and PEP within a comprehensive prevention framework can enhance HIV prevention efforts, as PEP may act as a bridge to PrEP for those with repeated exposures, and conversely, PrEP users with poor adherence may benefit from timely PEP use. This dual approach forms a comprehensive biomedical prevention cycle that addresses the full spectrum of transmission risks [21], particularly benefiting individuals with inconsistent adherence or episodic high-risk behaviors [20, 22] by providing continuous coverage across the exposure timeline. Moreover, in real-world settings, there is considerable temporal and behavioral overlap between PrEP and PEP use. In a longitudinal study conducted in Canada, over one-third of MSM who used PEP subsequently initiated PrEP prescriptions [23]. A repeated cross-sectional online survey among MSM in the United States reported similar findings [24]. This biological and behavioral synergy suggests that PrEP and PEP should be conceptualized and implemented as an integrated biomedical prevention strategy to maximize public health benefits in HIV prevention. However, existing research has largely examined PrEP and PEP separately, with limited attention to risk compensation associated with their combined use. Few longitudinal studies—particularly those based in community settings with extended follow-up—have explored how an integrated PrEP–PEP strategy relates to multidimensional indicators of unprotected anal intercourse, such as composite measures combining frequency and condom use. Such evidence is essential to understand real-world effectiveness and optimize HIV prevention efforts.

To examine patterns of biomedical intervention use and their association with potential risk compensation behaviors, this study drew on a longitudinal cohort of MSM undergoing HIV testing in Tianjin, China, with data collected over nearly six years. The cohort was established through ongoing HIV testing and biomedical intervention programs, allowing for the analysis of real-world PrEP and PEP use over time. We systematically analyzed the association between biomedical interventions and UAI, identified behavioral risk trajectories, and explored heterogeneity across age groups and geographic regions. Additionally, we assessed how biomedical interventions were related to HIV testing frequency and HIV incidence, offering valuable insights into their long-term impact in routine public health practice.

Methods

Study design and data sources

The Men Who Have Sex with Men Health Encouragement Longitudinal Project is a community-based longitudinal cohort study conducted among MSM in Tianjin, China. The project is implemented through collaboration between the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the National Center for AIDS/STD Control and Prevention of the Chinese CDC, Tianjin Municipal CDC, and Tianjin Shenlan Community-Based Organization (CBO), and provides integrated HIV prevention and care services through community-based service stations. These services include HIV counseling and testing, psychological support, and biomedical HIV prevention programs, with post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) available since April 2018 and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) since August 2021. MSM participants are recruited via snowball sampling through gay venues (bathhouse, bar and club house), social media platforms (QQ, WeChat and gay chat room), and peer referrals, and trained MSM peer surveyors administer structured questionnaires to collect sociodemographic and sexual behavior information; referrals for PrEP or PEP are offered based on HIV test results and risk assessments (see Tables S1 and S2), with initiation remaining voluntary. Rigorous quality control measures—including unique mobile verification, ID assignment, IP monitoring, and logical checks—were applied to ensure data quality and participant uniqueness. The project is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2000039500) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University (TMUhMEC20230030), with informed consent obtained from all participants under the supervision of Tianjin CDC.

The initial recruitment began on September 1, 2017, and follow-up continued until December 31, 2024. Eligible participants were biologically male, aged ≥ 16 years, HIV-negative at enrollment, and reported anal intercourse with men in the past 6 months. For inclusion in the analytic cohort, participants were further required to have complete behavioral data and to be repeat testers, defined as having at least two HIV tests during follow-up. Data on sexual behaviors (number of anal sex acts per week, condom use at last sex, and consistent condom use) were collected at each HIV testing visit. Sociodemographic and other covariates were collected at baseline. A total of 19,046 MSM were initially recruited; 166 were excluded for being outside the biomedical intervention period, 327 for missing behavioral data, and 9,763 for having only one HIV test. Ultimately, 8,790 MSM met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of MSM who underwent only a single HIV test were compared with those of participants who received repeat testing (Table S3).

Assessment of unprotected anal intercourse risk score

Based on previous research [25, 26] and following standard practice of combining anal intercourse frequency and condom use to characterize UAI-related HIV risk, we developed an index to assess the HIV infection risk associated with UAI among MSM, referred to as the UAI risk score (hereafter “risk score”). The score was constructed from three indicators: [1] number of anal sex acts per week (> 1 = 1 point, ≤ 1 = 0 point); [2] condom use at last anal sex (no = 1 point, yes = 0 point); and [3] consistent condom use (no = 1 point, yes = 0 point). The risk score ranged from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater HIV risk associated with sexual behavior. The rationale for this construction is that HIV risk related to UAI is jointly determined by the intensity of exposure (frequency of anal intercourse) and the absence of effective protection (inconsistent or no condom use). The number of anal sex acts per week reflects repeated exposure opportunities, while condom use at last anal sex and consistent condom use capture both recent and habitual protective behaviors. These indicators represent complementary but non-overlapping dimensions of sexual risk, and their combination allows a more comprehensive characterization of UAI-related HIV risk than any single measure alone. Each component was dichotomized and equally weighted to facilitate interpretability and to avoid overemphasizing any single behavioral dimension, given the absence of empirical evidence supporting differential weighting in this setting.

Assessment of biomedical interventions

In this study, exposure to PrEP or PEP was assessed using integrated project data, drawing on information collected through longitudinal cohort surveys and routinely recorded medication acquisition data from community-based HIV prevention services. These data sources were jointly used to inform the ascertainment of PrEP or PEP exposure during the follow-up period.

HIV laboratory test

MSM participants undergoing testing were required to provide blood samples for HIV rapid testing (Wan Fu Biotechnology Co., Guangzhou, China). In addition, 5 mL of venous blood was collected from individuals with reactive rapid test results and sent to the Tianjin Center for Disease Control and Prevention for confirmatory testing using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Wan Tai Biological Pharmaceutical Co., Beijing, China) and a Western blot assay (MP Biomedical Asia Pacific, Singapore). The result of the Western blot assay was considered the definitive diagnostic criterion.

Covariates

Covariates of interest included age, marital status, education level, occupation, residential region, and sexual orientation.

Statistical analysis

We present three visualizations to illustrate the patterns of biomedical intervention usage throughout the follow-up period: [1] Annual measures of PrEP and PEP use, along with HIV incidence rates, were evaluated for temporal trends using the non-parametric Mann-Kendall test; [2] the geographic distribution of biomedical interventions in Tianjin; and [3] a Sankey diagram illustrating the transitions and overlaps between PrEP/PEP use.

Baseline characteristics collected at initial registration were summarized using means (SD) for continuous variables, and differences between groups were assessed using t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as n (%), and group differences were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

We used multivariable Poisson regression to estimate the association between biomedical intervention and HIV testing frequency during the follow-up period. A Cox proportional hazards model was employed to assess the association between biomedical intervention and HIV infection risk over time. HIV incidence rates during follow-up were calculated using person-years as the denominator, and were compared between participants who received biomedical interventions and those who did not.

We employed linear mixed-effects models to evaluate the association between biomedical intervention use (based on either follow-up exposure or baseline status) and risk scores. Regression coefficients (β) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with adjustments made for age, sexual orientation, marital status, education level, occupation, and residential region. To account for potential baseline differences in sexual behaviors between the intervention and non-intervention groups, we conducted two secondary analyses: [1] inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on baseline risk score was used to control for confounding. Stabilized inverse probability weights were constructed from predicted probabilities generated by a propensity score model, and the weighted data were reanalyzed using the same mixed-effects models; [2] analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), in which baseline risk score was included as a covariate in the regression model to further adjust for baseline differences. For the three individual behavioral indicators—number of anal sex acts per week, condom use at last sex, and consistent condom use—we used generalized linear mixed models, specifying Poisson distributions for count outcomes and binomial distributions for binary outcomes. The same covariates were included in these models, and results were presented as adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. To further explore heterogeneity of effects across subgroups, we conducted stratified analyses by age (adolescent MSM: ≤24 years vs. non-adolescent MSM: >24 years) and residential region.

Group-based trajectory modeling was employed to fit the annual average risk score trajectories of participants during the follow-up period. Model selection was based on the following criteria: [1] Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) — the model with the lowest BIC value was preferred; [2] Group size adequacy — each trajectory group was required to contain at least 5% of the sample to avoid overfitting to small subpopulations; [3] Classification quality — the average posterior probability (AvePP) for each group had to be ≥ 0.7, ensuring adequate within-group homogeneity and between-group heterogeneity. We performed adjusted multivariable logistic regression to assess associations between biomedical interventions and trajectory group membership.

We conducted the following sensitivity analyses: [1] Considering that linear mixed-effects models accommodate missing data during follow-up, we applied these models to all participants during the biomedical intervention period to estimate the associations between biomedical intervention and both risk score and sexual behavior indicators; [2] Given the substantial imbalance in sample sizes between the intervention and control groups, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) based on age and sexual orientation, using a caliper of 0.25. The same modeling approach was then used to reassess the associations between biomedical intervention and the risk scores as well as sexual behavior indicators; [3] To assess the robustness of the findings to outcome specification, the UAI risk score was additionally modeled as an ordinal outcome (0–3) using mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression, with biomedical intervention use as the exposure.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0, and two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Overview of the use of biomedical interventions

PrEP and PEP utilization among participants in our study increased markedly between 2018 and 2024 (Fig. 2). While PEP was introduced earlier and remained the dominant intervention throughout the study period, PrEP uptake increased rapidly after its introduction in 2021, substantially narrowing the gap with PEP utilization by 2024. Significant upward trends were observed for both interventions (PrEP: p = 0.019; PEP: p = 0.016) using Mann-Kendall tests, whereas HIV incidence demonstrated a non-significant decline (p = 0.072).

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 8790 MSM who underwent repeat HIV testing were included in the final analysis. Participants’ baseline characteristics at initial registration are presented in Table 1. The mean age of all participants was 32.47 ± 10.13 years; 90.98% identified as homosexual; 73.66% were unmarried, divorced, or widowed; and 61.59% held a college degree or higher. Additionally, 10.92% were students and 2.92% were male sex workers (MSW). Over half (52.20%) resided in the central urban area. The average risk score was 1.07 ± 0.92, with a mean of 0.80 ± 0.96 anal sex acts per week. In terms of condom use, 68.85% reported using a condom during their last anal sex, and 37.86% reported consistent condom use.

The usage rate of biomedical intervention among MSM who underwent repeat testing was 21.14%. Significant differences (all P < 0.05) were observed between the biomedical intervention group and the non-intervention group in terms of age, marital status, education, occupation, residential region, risk score, condom use at last sex, and consistent condom use. Individuals who were younger, unmarried/divorced/widowed, had a college degree or higher, were students or MSW, resided in the Five Outer Districts or Binhai New Area, had higher risk scores, did not use a condom at last anal sex, or reported inconsistent condom use were more likely to adopt biomedical interventions compared to their counterparts.

Association between biomedical interventions and testing frequency

Table S4 presents the frequency distribution of the number of HIV tests among preliminarily screened participants. Overall, more than half of participants underwent HIV testing only once during the study period (53.41%). Participants in the biomedical intervention group were more likely to undergo two or more HIV tests during the study period compared with those in the non-intervention group (P < 0.001). Among participants who underwent repeat testing, after adjusting for potential confounders, the HIV testing incidence was 30% higher in the biomedical intervention group compared to the non-intervention group (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.27–1.33).

Association between biomedical interventions and risk of HIV infection

Among participants with repeat testing, a total of 216 HIV infections were observed over a median follow-up time of 4.47 years (IQR: 2.35–6.24). The overall HIV incidence rate was 0.58 per 100 person-years (216 cases over 37,260 person-years; 95% CI 0.51–0.66). The incidence rate was 0.40 per 100 person-years (28/6,931 person-years; 95% CI 0.27–0.58) in the biomedical intervention group and 0.62 per 100 person-years (188/30,329 person-years; 95% CI 0.53–0.72) in the non-intervention group. Compared to the non-intervention group, the biomedical intervention group had a 47% lower risk of HIV infection (HR = 0.53, 95% CI 0.35–0.80) (Table 2).

Association between biomedical interventions and unprotected anal intercourse behaviors

Among participants with repeat HIV testing, the use of biomedical interventions was significantly associated with higher risk scores. When grouping participants by biomedical intervention use during follow-up, the intervention group demonstrated significantly higher risk scores than the non-intervention group (β = 0.191, 95% CI 0.162–0.219). After adjusting for baseline risk scores, this association remained significant in both the IPW analysis (β = 0.132, 95% CI 0.104–0.159) and ANCOVA (β = 0.128, 95% CI 0.103–0.153). When grouping was based on baseline biomedical intervention use, the unadjusted model showed significantly higher risk scores in the intervention group (β = 0.094, 95% CI 0.034, 0.154), whereas both the IPW-weighted (β = 0.007, 95% CI −0.034, 0.049) and ANCOVA (β = −0.058, 95% CI −0.114, −0.001) models showed different patterns of association. Balance diagnostics before and after IPW are presented in Table S5, showing marked improvement after weighting.

In terms of sexual behavior indicators, participants who used biomedical interventions during the follow-up period reported a higher weekly frequency of anal sex (IRR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.09–1.17), were less likely to use condoms during their last anal sex (OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.52–0.61), and had a lower rate of consistent condom use (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.66–0.78). Similar trends were observed when grouping participants based on biomedical intervention use at baseline (Table 3).

Trajectories of risk score and their association with biomedical interventions

Based on model fit indices and classification quality (Table S6), the two-group model was selected. It provided balanced subgroup proportions (43.8% and 56.2%) and acceptable posterior probabilities (> 0.70), whereas models with more groups produced very small classes and lower classification accuracy. Accordingly, two distinct UAI risk trajectories were identified: a high-risk group and a low-risk group. Figure 5 illustrates the trajectories of risk scores over time. Throughout the follow-up period, both groups exhibited an upward trend in risk scores, with consistently higher scores observed in the high-risk group. Table 4 presents the associations between biomedical intervention variables and trajectory group membership. After adjusting for potential confounders, participants who had used biomedical interventions had a 78% higher likelihood of belonging to the high-risk trajectory group compared to non-users (OR = 1.78, 95% CI 1.60–1.99).

Stratified analysis

Age-stratified analyses indicated heterogeneity in the magnitude of associations between biomedical intervention use and sexual risk behaviors between adolescent MSM (age ≤ 24) and non-adolescent MSM (age > 24) (Fig. 6), with generally stronger associations observed among non-adolescent MSM. Specifically, the association with risk scores was larger among non-adolescent MSM (β = 0.212, 95% CI 0.179–0.245) than among adolescent MSM (β = 0.149, 95% CI 0.093–0.206). Similarly, reductions in condom use at last anal sex (OR = 0.52, 95% CI 0.47–0.57 vs. OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.62–0.83) and consistent condom use (OR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.61–0.75 vs. OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.64–0.87) were more pronounced among non-adolescent MSM. In contrast, the association with the frequency of anal sex acts per week was stronger among adolescent MSM (IRR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.27) than among non-adolescent MSM (IRR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.15).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the association between comprehensive biomedical interventions and UAI, HIV testing frequency, and infection risk. While previous studies have explored the impact of PrEP or PEP alone on UAI or examined partial associations between specific interventions and testing behaviors, the overall effects of biomedical interventions as an integrated strategy on sexual behavior patterns, testing adherence, and HIV infection risk have not been thoroughly elucidated.

From 2018 to 2024, biomedical intervention use among MSM in Tianjin increased steadily, with PEP remaining the dominant intervention and PrEP uptake rising rapidly after its introduction in 2021, narrowing the gap with PEP by 2024. Transitions from PEP to PrEP were more common than from PrEP to PEP, while long-term continued use of either intervention remained relatively low. HIV incidence showed a non-significant decline over the study period. Intervention users were more likely to undergo frequent HIV testing and had lower HIV incidence, suggesting a protective effect. However, biomedical intervention use was also associated with higher UAI risk, including increased risk scores, more frequent anal intercourse, and lower condom use. Trajectory analysis suggested intervention users tended to be in the high-risk group, consistent with risk compensation. Age-stratified analyses indicated heterogeneity in effect magnitude between adolescent and non-adolescent MSM, with generally stronger associations observed among non-adolescent MSM for risk scores and reductions in condom use, whereas associations with frequency of anal sex acts were somewhat stronger among adolescent MSM. Spatial analyses revealed marked geographic disparities, with larger effect sizes in outer districts and the Binhai New Area compared with central urban and inner suburban areas, likely reflecting uneven service access and concentrated high-risk populations. These findings highlight the need to integrate biomedical strategies with behavioral support, education, and region-specific approaches to improve adherence and maximize public health benefits.

In our study, uptake of biomedical HIV prevention interventions was 15.46% in the full sample and 21.14% among participants who consistently underwent HIV testing. When compared with estimates from other settings, these levels are close to previously reported uptake. Globally, a meta-analysis estimated that 11.23% of MSM had used PrEP [27]. A 2021 cross-sectional survey across six Chinese cities reported a PrEP usage rate of 22.4% among MSM [28], while in the United States, 25% of MSM with clear indications for PrEP reported use as of 2016 [29]. Uptake of PEP has been lower, with 8.9% of MSM in middle- and high-income countries reporting prior use [30]. While the uptake of biomedical interventions in our study is close to levels reported elsewhere, coverage remains incomplete among MSM in Tianjin. Factors such as limited awareness, stigma driven by sociocultural contexts, and barriers to medication access may contribute to this gap. Further research is needed to better understand these challenges and to inform targeted intervention strategies. Previous studies have reported bidirectional switching between PrEP and PEP among MSM. For instance, Richardson et al.. found that 11% of PrEP users subsequently used PEP, whereas 56% of PEP users switched to PrEP [31]. A similar trend was observed in our study, with a higher rate of transition from PEP to PrEP than vice versa. This may be due to the superior efficacy of PrEP in preventing HIV among high-risk MSM. Compared to PEP, PrEP has been shown to be more effective in reducing the risk of HIV seroconversion [32]. Additionally, our findings suggest that MSM who used biomedical interventions underwent HIV testing more frequently, which is consistent with patterns reported in previous studies [33], and may reflect a positive association between biomedical intervention use and regular testing behavior. However, this finding should be interpreted in the context of programmatic requirements, as HIV testing is often a prerequisite for PrEP and PEP use. Among repeat testers, the HIV incidence rate was 0.62 per 100 person-years in the non-intervention group—substantially lower than the rate of 5.10 per 100 person-years reported in a multicenter non-randomized study conducted in China. Among those receiving biomedical interventions, the incidence further dropped to 0.40 per 100 person-years, also lower than the 0.64 per 100 person-years reported among PrEP users in that same study [34]. The incidence rates estimated in this study were derived from a population of repeat testers, which may have had greater exposure to behavioral interventions, health education, and risk awareness. In addition, the study was conducted in Tianjin, which is located in northeastern China, a region characterized by a relatively low HIV/AIDS epidemic intensity, and this regional epidemiological context may have further contributed to the lower background HIV incidence observed in this study [35].

Previous studies have shown a significant association between PrEP use and increased UAI risk [15, 16], while findings on PEP remain inconsistent [17–19, 36]. Against this background, the present study innovatively analyzed PrEP and PEP as a combined biomedical intervention strategy, and found that their use was still associated with a higher risk of UAI. This finding contributes new evidence to the understanding of the relationship between biomedical interventions and risk behaviors, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive assessment of their potential impact on sexual behavior patterns. Our study identified stronger risk compensation effects specifically among older MSM and those living in outer suburban areas—two groups that may share common structural and psychosocial vulnerabilities. Previous studies have shown that both older individuals and suburban MSM often face limited access to accurate HIV prevention information, reduced availability of biomedical services, and heightened experiences of stigma. For instance, older MSM may hold more conservative views or misconceptions about PrEP and PEP, perceiving them as facilitators of risky sexual behavior, while suburban MSM frequently encounter logistical and geographic barriers to sustained intervention uptake. Additionally, both groups tend to exhibit lower levels of awareness and trust in biomedical strategies, as reflected in earlier research indicating age-related and region-specific disparities in PrEP/PEP knowledge and attitudes [37–42]. These intersecting challenges suggest that biomedical prevention efforts must move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and incorporate tailored behavioral and structural support for these fragile subpopulations.

To mitigate the impact of risk compensation in biomedical HIV prevention among MSM—particularly older individuals and those living in suburban areas—a multi-level strategy informed by the Social Ecological Model represents one useful framework for addressing potential risk compensation [43]. At the individual level, targeted education should promote accurate understanding of PrEP and PEP, sustain risk awareness, and support informed use and adherence. For high-risk subgroups, additional strategies such as peer counseling, motivational interviewing, and behavioral goal-setting can help translate knowledge into safer sexual practices. Special attention should be given to older and suburban MSM, who may have limited access to information or face unique behavioral risks. At the community level, expanding differentiated services [44] and integrating PrEP/PEP delivery with HIV self-testing and STI screening can enhance accessibility and continuity of care. CBOs can play a key role in delivering culturally appropriate interventions and maintaining long-term behavioral support. Group workshops, community-led follow-ups, and ongoing risk-reduction counseling can further reinforce adherence to safer behaviors among older and suburban MSM. At the policy level, efforts should focus on strengthening service coordination, improving referral systems, and reducing financial barriers—particularly in underserved suburban areas. National-level monitoring systems are also needed to track behavioral trends and ensure quality assurance. Together, these approaches can enhance the effectiveness of biomedical HIV prevention while addressing the specific behavioral challenges faced by high-risk subgroups within the MSM population.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, participants were recruited through snowball sampling, which included online and offline recruitment as well as volunteer engagement, to maximize the representativeness of the sample. Nevertheless, potential sampling bias cannot be fully ruled out, and future studies could consider respondent-driven sampling or other probability-based approaches to further improve representativeness. Second, the study did not assess how the duration, interruption, or discontinuation of biomedical interventions may influence UAI risk. Third, exposure to PrEP or PEP in this study was determined using integrated project data, which combined information from longitudinal cohort surveys and routinely recorded medication acquisition data from community-based HIV prevention services. While these sources allowed for follow-up-based ascertainment of PrEP and PEP exposure, they do not confirm actual medication use or adherence. Fourth, as the analysis focused on individuals with repeat HIV testing, the findings may not be generalizable to those who tested only once. However, such single-visit testing is a common phenomenon in large, mobile urban populations like Tianjin. Future research should expand to more diverse settings and integrate adherence monitoring and qualitative approaches to better understand behavioral dynamics.

This study provides evidence that while biomedical interventions are associated with more frequent HIV testing and lower HIV incidence, they may also be linked to an increased risk of UAI—particularly among older MSM and those residing in suburban areas. Targeted health education efforts are therefore needed for MSM receiving biomedical interventions, especially these key subgroups, to reduce the risk of UAI and other STIs and address the prevention paradox within this population. These findings underscore the importance of integrating behavioral support into biomedical HIV prevention strategies.
