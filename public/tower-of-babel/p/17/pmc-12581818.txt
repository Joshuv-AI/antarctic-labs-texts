MYOD represses gene expression from non-E-box motifs
Authors: Chiara Nicoletti, Jimmy Massenet, Andreas P Pintado-Urbanc, Leah J Connor, Monica Nicolau, Swetha Sundar, Mingzhi Xu, Anthony Schmitt, Wenxin Zhang, Zesen Fang, Tsz Ching Indigo Chan, Yu Xin Wang, Stephen J Tapscott, Tom H Cheung, Matthew D Simon, Luca Caputo, Pier Lorenzo Puri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, Nicoletti et al. report that MYOD serves as a transcriptional repressor during fibroblast trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle. Contrary to MYOD-activated gene expression, MYOD interacts with promoters and enhancers of the repressed genes by unconventional binding to non-E-box motifs, showcasing the genetic context-dependent complexity of MYOD's function during skeletal myogenesis.Keywords: chromatin, gene expression, MYOD

MYOD has the unique property of activating skeletal myogenesis upon ectopic expression in nonmuscle cells, also known as MYOD-mediated trans-differentiation or myogenic conversion of somatic cells (Davis et al. 1987; Weintraub et al. 1989), which reflects its physiological function as the endogenous activator of skeletal myogenesis in muscle stem cells (MuSCs) during skeletal muscle regeneration (Berkes and Tapscott 2005; Hernández-Hernández et al. 2017). This property relies on MYOD's ability to bind nucleosomes at previously silent loci in cooperation with pioneer factors, such as Pbx1/Meis (Berkes et al. 2004), followed by signal-dependent recruitment of histone acetyltransferases and the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex (Eckner et al. 1996; Yuan et al. 1996; Puri et al. 1997a,c; Sartorelli et al. 1997, 1999; de la Serna et al. 2001, 2005; Simone et al. 2004; Serra et al. 2007; Forcales et al. 2012) to promote chromatin accessibility and enable full recognition and binding to specific E-box sequences (wherein the central dinucleotide is GC or GG) (Fong et al. 2012, 2015). E-box-driven heterodimerization with E2A gene products (E12 and E47) (Murre et al. 1989) is also required for MYOD to activate transcription of target skeletal muscle-specific genes (Dilworth et al. 2004). Previous studies have identified specific domains that confer on MYOD the property as a DNA sequence-specific transcriptional activator (Puri and Sartorelli 2000). Within the bHLH region (Ma et al. 1994), the basic domain restricts MYOD's DNA binding affinity to myogenic E-box motifs (Davis et al. 1990), and the HLH domain mediates heterodimerization with E12 or E47 (Lassar et al. 1991). Moreover, an acidic activation domain (AD) located at the N terminus (Weintraub et al. 1991b) and two chromatin remodeling domains (CRDs) located at the C/H-rich domain and C terminus (Gerber et al. 1997) cooperate to activate transcription of target genes.

Recent work has extended our knowledge of MYOD-mediated activation of skeletal myogenesis by revealing its pervasive binding throughout the genome (Cao et al. 2010) and its role as an organizer of the 3D genome architecture (Sartorelli and Puri 2018; Dall'Agnese et al. 2019; Ren et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2023). Although MYOD-mediated activation of gene expression has been extensively studied since its discovery as a myogenic determination factor, the expression of MYOD in proliferating, undifferentiated muscle progenitors currently remains a puzzling, outstanding issue. MYOD expression coincides with a stage in which activation of muscle gene expression has not yet been fully induced, suggesting that MYOD could exert functions alternative to the activation of muscle genes. Interestingly, in activated MuSCs during skeletal muscle regeneration, the expression of endogenous MYOD coincides with the downregulation of a subset of genes invariably assigned to gene ontology processes related to cell of origin, alternative lineages, and growth factor-responsive genes (Dong et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2023). Likewise, gene expression analysis has revealed specific patterns of gene repression during MYOD-mediated myogenic conversion of nonmuscle somatic cells (Yagi et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2022). However, although the potential function of MYOD as a transcriptional repressor has been sparsely reported in previous works (Trouche et al. 1993; Chu et al. 1997; Mal et al. 2001; Puri et al. 2001; Yokoyama et al. 2009; Singh et al. 2015; Cui et al. 2017), the potential mechanism that accounts for this putative functional property remains elusive.

Here, we report on the identification of MYOD as a direct repressor of gene expression through unconventional binding to non-E-box motifs, leading to reduced chromatin accessibility and decreased levels of the novel mark of transcriptional activation, acetyl-methyllysine (Kacme) (Lu-Culligan et al. 2023), at promoters and enhancers of MYOD-repressed genes.

Results

MYOD reduces chromatin accessibility at promoters of mitogen-responsive and growth factor-responsive genes during human fibroblast trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle cells

MYOD-mediated trans-differentiation of somatic cells into skeletal muscles (Davis et al. 1987; Weintraub et al. 1989) provides an optimal experimental platform to investigate the molecular, genetic, and epigenetic mechanisms by which MYOD coordinates nuclear reprogramming of nonmuscle cells toward the myogenic lineage. In previous studies, we have exploited this model to demonstrate that, upon inducible expression in IMR90 human fibroblasts, MYOD reorganizes the 3D genome architecture by rewiring high-order chromatin interactions implicated in the formation of boundaries of functional nuclear domains, such as the insulated neighborhoods (INs) within topologically associating domains (TADs) (Dall'Agnese et al. 2019). This process is well appreciated upon exposure to differentiation cues (differentiation medium [DM]), in which cells uniformly undergo terminal differentiation. However, a more dynamic genome reprogramming occurs during the proliferation of MYOD-expressing IMR90 (IMR90–MYOD) cells when they are cultured in high serum (growth medium [GM]). Indeed, this is an intermediate stage of commitment toward the myogenic lineage that requires the erasure of the previous cell of origin lineage genes prior to the activation of the myogenic differentiation program. Moreover, during this transition, culturing cells in high-serum-containing growth factors and mitogens mimics the exposure of muscle progenitors to developmental or regeneration cues, which might activate multiple responses and cell lineages if not properly filtered/interpreted. Thus, we sought to focus our analysis on IMR90 cells cultured in high-mitogen/growth factor-containing serum (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Under these conditions, doxycycline (doxy)-induced MYOD expression did not activate endogenous MYOD or promote the formation of myosin heavy chain (MYHC)-expressing multinucleated terminally differentiated myotubes (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Conversely, MYOD expression coincided with downregulation of genes, including the lung fibroblast lineage gene GATA6 (Supplemental Fig. S1C–E), the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin 6 (IL6), the growth factor-responsive cFos, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) component fibronectin 1 (FN1) (Supplemental Fig. S1D), whereas typical early MYOD-induced differentiation genes, such as integrin α 7 (ITGA7) and troponin T2 (TNNT2), were weakly activated (Supplemental Fig. S1D). A late differentiation marker, the embryonic MYH3, was not induced at this stage (Supplemental Fig. S1D). Consistently, RNA-seq analysis of IMR90–MYOD cells revealed that a large proportion (about one-third) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was downregulated, as compared with control IMR90 cells (Fig. 1A,B). Western blot analysis shows that the expression levels of MYOD in IMR90 cells were nearly sixfold higher than endogenous MYOD detected in protein extracts from primary human myoblasts (Supplemental Fig. S1F). Although, in principle, these levels are supraphysiologic, we argue that they are unlikely to affect MYOD function and genome-wide chromatin binding, as also supported by the experiments shown below. Likewise, ATAC-seq shows analogous patterns of reduced or induced chromatin accessibility at gene promoters (Fig. 1C,D), with a notable higher number of events of reduced chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1D), which indicates that repression of gene expression might occur through extensive chromatin compaction at promoters of repressed genes (Fig. 1D). To determine a causal relationship between MYOD chromatin binding and changes in gene expression and chromatin accessibility, we integrated MYOD binding events at promoters (by ChIP-seq) of DEGs (by RNA-seq) with the chromatin accessibility patterns identified by ATAC-seq in IMR90 versus IMR90–MYOD cells cultured in GM for 24 h. We found that, cumulatively, 33% of MYOD peaks at gene promoters coincided with binding to either promoters of upregulated genes or promoters with increased chromatin accessibility, with only a minority of total MYOD peaks (861 peaks; 7%) associated with promoters of upregulated genes with increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1E,F). Gene ontology analysis revealed that MYOD binding to promoters with increased chromatin accessibility coincided with the upregulation of genes implicated in biological processes related to skeletal myogenesis and general features of differentiation (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Motif analysis of these events showed an invariable association with typical MYOD targets: the myogenic E-box motif (CAGCTG) (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Conversely, more than half of MYOD peaks at gene promoters (6007) detected by ChIP-seq coincided with binding to promoters of a large majority of downregulated genes or overlapped with reduced promoter chromatin accessibility, with 1566 peaks associated with reduced chromatin accessibility at promoters of downregulated genes (Fig. 1G,H). Gene ontology analysis of DEGs indicates that MYOD-bound promoters with reduced chromatin accessibility coincided with downregulation in genes implicated in cell proliferation, regulation of S phase, mitosis, and other phases of the cell cycle (Supplemental Fig. S2C). Motif analysis of these events showed an invariable association with non-E-box motifs, with enrichment in motifs for transcription factors (TFs) implicated in cell cycle regulation and proliferation of muscle progenitors, such as E2F (Wang et al. 1995; Puri et al. 1997b, 1998) and NF-Y (Gurtner et al. 2008; Rigillo et al. 2021), or serum/growth factor-responsive TFs, such as SP1 and Elk family members (Supplemental Fig. S2D; Braun and Gautel 2011). Furthermore, we interrogated the peaks of MYOD that coincided with ATAC-seq peaks of differential chromatin accessibility regions (DARs) at promoters of DEGs for the presence of at least one E-box motif. We annotated 649 MYOD peaks (out of 2351; 27.6%) overlapping with reduced chromatin accessibility at promoters of downregulated genes that did not contain any E-Box motif, whereas only 91 MYOD peaks (out of 1236; 7.4%) overlapping with increased chromatin accessibility at promoters of upregulated genes were devoid of E-box motifs.

Interestingly, although the increased chromatin accessibility at MYOD-bound promoters of upregulated genes was associated with increased levels of H3K27ac as expected (Fig. 1F), the reduced chromatin accessibility at MYOD-bound promoters of downregulated genes did not associate with decreased levels of H3K27ac (Fig. 1H), which appears paradoxical, as this is a conventional mark for promoter (as well as enhancer) activation. Representative tracks for two downregulated genes, cFOS and GATA6, are shown in Figure 1, I and J.

These results suggest that MYOD directly contributes to two distinct programs for genome reprogramming—the activation and repression of different patterns of gene expression—by promoting opposite patterns of chromatin accessibility at promoters of DEGs via binding to either myogenic E-box or non-E-box motifs.

The different outcome in terms of changes in chromatin accessibility induced by MYOD via chromatin binding at E-box versus non-E-box motifs apparently contradicts the current dogma that MYOD chromatin binding is only driven by the selective affinity for specific myogenic E-box motifs (Davis et al. 1990; Ma et al. 1994; Puri and Sartorelli 2000). It also challenges the current knowledge on structural and functional properties of MYOD, whereby the presence of two chromatin remodeling domains predicts that upon recruitment to nucleosomes at target gene loci, MYOD only promotes chromatin accessibility (Gerber et al. 1997; de la Serna et al. 2001, 2005; Simone et al. 2004; Forcales et al. 2012). We therefore sought to further investigate MYOD chromatin binding within the different outcomes of chromatin accessibility by a deeper analysis, in which we fractioned the MYOD ChIP-seq peaks within differential ATAC-seq peaks into three adjacent genomic windows: at the summit and at the two sides of the peak (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Motif analysis revealed again an invariable enrichment of the myogenic E-box at the summit as well as at both sides of the peaks of increased chromatin accessibility that coincided with MYOD-bound promoters of activated genes (Supplemental Fig. S3B). In contrast, no E-box motifs were detected at the summit of peaks of reduced chromatin accessibility that coincided with MYOD-bound differentially accessible region (DAR) promoters (Supplemental Fig. S3C) with low frequency of nonmyogenic E-box motifs detected at the sides.

These data reveal unexpected patterns of MYOD binding to promoters of DEGs, leading to opposite patterns of chromatin accessibility associated with either activation or repression of gene expression. Although MYOD-mediated activation from promoters entails binding to E-box motifs and leads to induction of genes implicated in early/general features of differentiation, MYOD-mediated repression from promoters does not require the presence of E-box motifs and coincides with the repression of mitogen-responsive and growth factor-responsive genes.

To further investigate the mechanism of MYOD recruitment to promoters of repressed genes, we used ChromBPNet (Pampari et al. 2025) to perform footprinting analysis of DAR peaks overlapping with MYOD binding at promoters of DEGs. Canonical MYOD binding to the E-box motif (CAGCTG) was the most represented motif associated with MYOD binding to promoters of upregulated genes in IMR90–MYOD cells when considering the top 30 pairwise motifs’ co-occurrence in all conditions (Supplemental Fig. S3D). Instead, the most represented motifs predicted to be occupied by TFs at promoters of MYOD-repressed genes were the degenerated sequence shared by the E2F family members E2F2, E2F3, and E2F7 (as also annotated by HOMER analysis in Supplemental Figs. S2C,D, S3B,C), and NF-Y (CCAAT) (Supplemental Fig. S3D). Importantly, E-box motifs were not annotated among the top 30 pairs of co-occurring motifs at promoters of MYOD-repressed genes (Supplemental Fig. S3D), whereas motifs predicted to be bound by KLF.SP1/2 were highly represented at promoters of both MYOD-activated and MYOD-repressed genes (Supplemental Fig. S3D). Previous works reported on functional interactions of MYOD with both E2F complexes (Gu et al. 1993; Wang et al. 1995; Puri et al. 1997b, 1998) and NF-YA (Gurtner et al. 2008; Rigillo et al. 2021). In particular, Boyarchuk et al. (2016) showed a specific interaction between MYOD and the E2F family member E2F2. We performed experiments of coimmunoprecipitation, which revealed a physical interaction of MYOD with E2F2 but not with NF-YA or JUNB (another potential MYOD interactor at enhancers) (see below; Supplemental Fig. S3E). Accordingly, we mutated individually two E2F2 binding sites in the promoter of cFos (as a representative MYOD-repressed gene) (Trouche et al. 1993) driving the luciferase gene (cFos-luc2 reporter) (Supplemental Fig. S3F). Supplemental Figure S3G shows that MYOD could effectively repress the full-length cFos-luc2 reporter in IMR90 cells; however, MYOD's ability to repress the cFos-luc2 reporter was largely impaired when the highly conserved E2F2-bound site (mut E2F2 site 1) distal to the transcription start site (TSS) was mutated. Furthermore, mutation of an E2F2-bound site (mut E2F2 site 2) proximal to the TSS drastically reduced the basal activity in IMR90 cells; however, MYOD retained its ability to repress this mutated cFos-luc2 reporter, albeit with a slightly reduced efficiency (Supplemental Fig. S3G). Overall, these results emphasize the interaction between MYOD and E2F2 as a potential mechanism for MYOD-mediated gene repression from promoters.

Changes in chromatin states during IMR90 human fibroblast trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle cells

As MYOD-mediated repression from promoters did not associate with reduced levels of H3K27ac, we wondered whether other changes in chromatin marks could discriminate the different patterns of MYOD chromatin binding. We therefore performed CUT&RUN analysis to profile the changes in histone marks predictive for promoter (H3K4me3) or enhancer (H3K4me1) identity for the formation of facultative (H3K27me3) or constitutive (H3K9me3) heterochromatin, in addition to H3K27ac, which is a common mark for enhancer and promoter activation. Combinatorial analysis of these marks in IMR90 versus IMR90–MYOD cells identified 12 chromatin states (Fig. 2A) whose dynamics revealed few major features of chromatin state transition, including reduction in constitutive heterochromatin, increased formation of facultative heterochromatin and euchromatin, and partial loss of enhancer identity (Fig. 2B,C). Importantly, when integrated with MYOD ChIP-seq analysis performed in IMR90–MYOD cells cultured in GM, CUT&RUN analysis of histone modifications revealed that more than two-thirds of MYOD-bound genomic elements switch their chromatin state (Fig. 2C,D), again indicating a causal relationship between MYOD chromatin binding at regulatory elements of the genome and changes in chromatin states and conformation. Examples of representative loci are shown in Figure 2E. Moreover, integration of CUT&RUN analysis of histone modifications with ATAC-seq data sets revealed a distribution of peaks of increased chromatin accessibility coinciding with increased marks of euchromatin and promoter or enhancer identity across the genome, with a special enrichment at intronic and intergenic elements (Fig. 2F). Conversely, peaks of decreased chromatin accessibility coinciding with increased marks of facultative heterochromatin were observed at promoter and nonpromoter regions and distal elements and were again enriched at intronic and intergenic elements (Fig. 2G).

Further integration of CUT&RUN of histone modifications, MYOD ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq data sets revealed two distinct patterns of changes in chromatin states at promoters of DEGs. Although a moderate enrichment in promoter identity (H3K4me3) and activation (H3K27ac) was observed at MYOD-bound promoters of upregulated genes, in association with increased chromatin accessibility (Supplemental Fig. S4A), the reduction in chromatin accessibility at MYOD-bound promoters of downregulated genes did not coincide with any appreciable changes in histone marks/chromatin states (Supplemental Fig. S4B).

MYOD binds and decommissions superenhancers (SEs) of cell of origin and alternative lineage genes during human fibroblast trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle cells

As a large number of genome-wide MYOD chromatin binding events coincide with decreased chromatin accessibility also at genomic elements distal from gene promoters, we investigated the possibility that MYOD might also repress gene expression from nonpromoter, distal genomic elements. Integration of MYOD ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq data sets showed that MYOD binding to nonpromoter elements can result in either increased or decreased chromatin accessibility (Supplemental Fig. S5A,B) that again was invariably associated with increased levels of H3K27 acetylation (Supplemental Fig. S5C,D). Integration with CUT&RUN analysis of histone modifications further showed that MYOD-bound distal elements exhibiting increased chromatin accessibility were especially enriched in marks of enhancer identity (H3K4me1) and activation (H3K27ac) (Supplemental Fig. S4C). A slight increase in promoter identity (H3K4me3) was also observed at MYOD-bound distal elements with increased chromatin accessibility (Supplemental Fig. S4C). In contrast, reduced chromatin accessibility at MYOD-bound distal elements did not coincide with any appreciable change in histone marks, except for a slight increase in H3K27ac (Supplemental Fig. S4D). An additional feature identified by this analysis was a trend of identity shifts for MYOD-bound active enhancers into elements enriched with the promoter identity mark H3K4me3 (Supplemental Fig. S4E). Interestingly, MYOD peaks detected at nonpromoter elements with increased chromatin accessibility coincided with the formation of euchromatin and enhancer activation (Supplemental Fig. S6A); instead, MYOD peaks with decreased chromatin accessibility exhibited a tendency toward loss of enhancer identity despite retaining marks of euchromatin (Supplemental Fig. S6A). As enhancers are the most relevant distal regulatory elements, and because we have observed a partial loss of enhancer identity in IMR90–MYOD cells (Fig. 2D; Supplemental Fig. S4E), we decided to focus our analysis on MYOD-bound enhancers, as reported previously (Blum et al. 2012). In this regard, we were especially interested in the dynamics of superenhancer (SE) formation/decommissioning upon MYOD expression in IMR90 cells, as SEs are typically implicated in the regulation of lineage identity genes (Whyte et al. 2013). We therefore first determined whether MYOD chromatin binding at nonpromoter, distal genomic elements coincided with SEs, including those already present in IMR90 cells and those formed in IMR90–MYOD cells. A total of 1674 SEs were identified in both IMR90 and IMR90–MYOD cells using ROSE (Whyte et al. 2013). MYOD bound 78% (810 out of 1041) of preexisting SEs detected in IMR90 cells and 91% (1223 out of 1352) of SEs detected in IMR90–MYOD cells (Supplemental Fig. S6B,C). Although MYOD-bound SEs with increased chromatin accessibility were highly enriched with myogenic E-box motifs, MYOD-bound SEs with decreased chromatin accessibility were enriched with non-E-box motifs mostly belonging to the Jun/Fos family member AP1 binding sites (Supplemental Fig. S6D). Because SEs typically activate gene expression from a distance (Hnisz et al. 2013), we used high-resolution (4 kb) Hi-C data sets previously generated in IMR90, IMR90 GM, and IMR90 DM conditions (Dall'Agnese et al. 2019) to identify SE loops with cognate promoter(s). Hi-C-based capture of SEs revealed the dynamics of enhancer–promoter (E/P) loops during the transition from IMR90 fibroblasts to IMR90–MYOD cells, discriminating newly formed versus lost SEs (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, this analysis also revealed several conserved SEs that were identified in both IMR90 and IMR90–MYOD cells (Fig. 3A). The dynamics of these conserved SEs were further investigated by aggregate region analysis (ARA) of Hi-C data in IMR90 and IMR90–MyoD cells cultured in GM for 24 h and then exposed to differentiation medium (DM) for an additional 24 h. This analysis illustrates how conserved SEs between IMR90 and IMR90–MYOD cells are eventually lost along with IMR90–MYOD cell transition from culture in GM to terminal differentiation upon exposure to DM (Supplemental Fig. S6E).

We next used MYOD ChIP-seq data to track the dynamics of MYOD-bound gained or lost SEs. MYOD-bound gained SEs exhibited an increased Hi-C signal (e.g., increased chromatin interaction strength) during the transition from IMR90 to IMR90–MYOD cells (Fig. 3B). In contrast, MYOD-bound lost SEs exhibited a drastic reduction in Hi-C signal related to chromatin interactions that define hubs of contacts typical of SEs, resembling frequently interacting regions (FIREs) (Schmitt et al. 2016). Further integration with ATAC-seq data showed that MYOD-bound gained SEs exhibited increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 3C), were typically marked by histone marks of active enhancers and euchromatin (Fig. 3D, top), and were enriched with myogenic E-box motifs (Fig. 3E, top). Conversely, MYOD-bound lost SEs exhibited a slight decrease in chromatin accessibility (Fig. 3C), were typically marked by histone marks of nonenhancer identity (though they retained marks of euchromatin) (Fig. 3D, bottom), and were enriched with non-E-box motifs, with Jun/Fos family member AP1 binding sites being the most represented (Fig. 3E, bottom). Integration of Hi-C and RNA-seq data enabled the identification of DEGs downstream from promoters looping with MYOD-bound SEs. This analysis revealed distinct patterns of association between lost SEs with gene repression and reduced chromatin accessibility and gained SEs with gene activation and increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 3F). Gained or lost SEs showed a trend of increased or decreased chromatin accessibility, respectively (Fig. 3G). Hi-C analysis of SE-associated promoters revealed that genes regulated by gained SEs belong to biological processes related to general aspects of cellular differentiation and commitment to the myogenic lineage (Fig. 3H, top). In contrast, genes regulated by lost SEs belong to biological processes related to repression of growth factor-induced intracellular processes and signaling (Fig. 3H, bottom).

Overall, these data define two types of MYOD-bound SEs. Gained SEs were not present in IMR90 cells and were generated upon MYOD expression via MYOD binding to E-box motifs at previously silent loci, according to a well-established sequence of events by which MYOD targets compacted chromatin at nucleosomes of silent loci in cooperation with pioneer factors, such as Pbx1/Meis, to promote chromatin remodeling and accessibility (Berkes et al. 2004). Lost SEs were present in IMR90 cells prior to the expression of MYOD and became decommissioned/inactivated upon MYOD binding via compaction and reduction of chromatin accessibility.

Because SEs consist of multiple hubs of chromatin interactions marked by H3K27ac, we performed HiChIP with H3K27ac antibodies and detected MYOD-bound HiChIP bins (Fig. 4A); however, only a few of these MYOD-bound differential chromatin interactions marked by H3K27ac were found to overlap with SEs, as called by the traditional ROSE method (Fig. 4B). Thus, we sought to devise a novel approach to identify MYOD-bound 3D SEs by using ROSE-derived SEs as a starting point and then overlapping linear SEs with the H3K27ac HiChIP bins, followed by clustering of all bins belonging to those interactions. Within the 3D SEs, we called hubs according to an approach reported by Huang et al. (2018), which identified bins within the 3D SEs that participate in more interactions as compared with the genome-wide average (workflow illustrated in Fig. 4C). To further refine the hub calls, we overlapped those bins with H3K27ac ChIP-seq peaks, which narrowed the identification of 3D SE regulatory hotspots (Fig. 4C). This method enabled the capture of considerably larger SEs compared with the conventional call of SEs used before (Fig. 4D) and revealed clear trends of increased (3D SEs gained) or decreased (3D SEs lost) activation during the transition from IMR90 to IMR90–MYOD cells (Fig. 4E). Importantly, although MYOD-bound gained SEs exhibited increased chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac signal, MYOD-bound lost SEs showed decreased chromatin accessibility yet retained high levels of H3K27ac signal (Fig. 4F). The retention of a high H3K27ac activation mark at MYOD-decommissioned SEs mirrors a similar phenomenon observed for MYOD-bound promoters of repressed genes shown in Figure 1, thereby revealing dissociation of reduced chromatin accessibility with H3K27ac levels as an unexpected, general feature of gene repression by MYOD from either promoters or enhancers. At the same time, this finding prompted the question of whether alternative histone modifications might provide a mark of MYOD-mediated gene repression. Although most of the known histone marks did not show any pattern of association with MYOD-bound SEs that undergo chromatin compaction and direct repression of target genes, we turned our attention to a newly identified mark of histone H4 lysine 5 and 12 methylation and acetylation on the same side chain (H4K5/12 Kacme) (Lu-Culligan et al. 2023) as a potential dynamic signal that could associate with MYOD-mediated inactivation of SEs. Indeed, ChIP-seq with Kacme antibodies showed a clear enrichment at MYOD-bound SEs with increased chromatin accessibility, whereas a reduction in Kacme signal marked MYOD-bound SEs with decreased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 4F). Upon integration of 3D SEs with RNA-seq data by HiChIP H3K27ac-detected loops, we identified a larger amount of DEGs downstream from the promoters looping with 3D SEs (Fig. 4G) as compared with 2D SEs (Fig. 3F). Accordingly, gene ontology analysis from a larger number of SE-regulated genes revealed higher number of genes and related processes: SE downstream upregulated genes were mostly related to biological processes of cellular commitment and differentiation (Fig. 4H, top), as also observed with 2D SEs (Fig. 3H). The higher number of SE downstream downregulated genes proportionally expanded the number of biological processes that now included repression of alternative cell lineages in addition to genes implicated in cell proliferation (Fig. 4H, bottom). Another feature of SEs is the frequent enrichment in CTCF-marked chromatin interactions (Schmitt et al. 2016). As previous studies have revealed an association between MYOD and CTCF chromatin binding (Liu et al. 2023), we also performed HiChIP with CTCF antibodies to investigate whether MYOD binding alters CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions at SEs. This analysis identified a coherent trend of reduction in the strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions at lost SEs, whereas an opposite pattern was observed at gained SEs (Supplemental Fig. S7C). Integration with MYOD and Kacme ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq revealed that MYOD-bound lost SEs with reduced strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions exhibited reduced chromatin accessibility and Kacme signal; in contrast, the opposite pattern was observed with MYOD-bound gained SEs with increased strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions (Supplemental Fig. S7D). Surprisingly, in both cases, MYOD binding to SEs led to increased CTCF chromatin binding, indicating that CTCF chromatin affinity/binding can be dissociated from the strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions.

Overall, the data support the identification of a novel property of MYOD as a repressor of gene expression during somatic cell trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle via direct binding to promoters and distal elements (enhancers and SEs) through a common mechanism that entails binding to non-E-box elements, invariably leading to reduction of chromatin accessibility with a decrease in the strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions and reduced Kacme levels at SEs.

Opposite patterns of chromatin accessibility and Kacme levels at MYOD-bound loci during muscle stem cell (MuSC) activation

We next investigated whether MYOD-mediated gene repression could also be observed in physiological conditions. We first tested the function of endogenous MYOD as a repressor in cultured MuSCs. To this purpose, we performed siRNA experiments to knock down MyoD in MuSCs isolated by FACS from wild-type mice. After isolation, MuSCs were cultured in growth medium for 48 h, followed by siRNA for MyoD (or control scramble siRNA), and RNA was collected after an additional 48 h for RNA-seq analysis (Supplemental Fig. S8A–C). We found that MyoD downregulation (Supplemental Fig. S8D) reproducibly perturbed gene expression as compared with control scramble siRNA (Supplemental Fig. S8E), leading to upregulation and downregulation of a similar number of genes (Supplemental Fig. S8F). Importantly, gene ontology analysis showed that the genes downregulated by MyoD RNAi were related to processes associated with skeletal myogenesis, including known MYOD-induced myogenic genes such as Myogenin, Mymk, Ttn, Tnnt1, Tnnt3, and Myh3, among others, as expected (Supplemental Fig. S8G). Conversely, among the genes upregulated by MyoD knockdown, we observed an enrichment in genes of alternative lineages or implicated in extracellular matrix organization and Tgfβ response (which were also annotated as MyoD-downregulated genes in IMR90 cells), in addition to genes implicated in mitochondrial activity and metabolism (Supplemental Fig. S8H). These data support the conclusion that physiological levels of MyoD are required to sustain ectopic gene repression in primary mouse myoblasts.

We next turned our attention to two specific stages of skeletal muscle regeneration in mice; namely, 4 and 60 h after injury. These time points were selected among sequential time points across MuSC activation because they coincided with a lack (or very low levels) or induction of MyoD expression, respectively, as reported by Dong et al. (2022). RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses revealed coherent and parallel patterns of both gene upregulation with increased chromatin accessibility at their promoters and gene downregulation with reduced chromatin accessibility at their promoters (Fig. 5A,B). CUT&RUN analysis of genome-wide chromatin binding of MYOD showed that at 4 h, no MYOD binding was detected at promoters of DEGs with differences in chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5C,D), consistent with the lack or very low levels of MyoD expression at this stage (Cooper et al. 1999; Machado et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2022). However, we detected MYOD recruitment at these promoters in MuSCs isolated at 60 h after injury. Integration of RNA-seq and MYOD chromatin binding detected MYOD peaks at promoters of 28.3% of DEGs, and gene ontology analysis showed enrichment in MYOD-repressed genes linked to repression of transcription and alternative lineages (Supplemental Fig. S9A). Parallel CUT&RUN analysis of Kacme using antibodies that recognize Kacme in any context (pan-Kacme) or antibodies specific for Kacme on histone H4 on K5/12 (H4Kacme) revealed that MYOD binding to promoters of repressed genes coincided with a consensual reduction in Kacme levels and reduced chromatin accessibility. Conversely, increased Kacme levels were detected at MYOD-bound promoters of activated genes with increased chromatin accessibility (Fig. 5C). Likewise, MYOD enrichment at distal elements showed that reduced chromatin accessibility was associated with reduced Kacme levels, whereas MYOD-bound distal elements with increased chromatin accessibility showed enrichment in Kacme levels (Fig. 5D). These data further support the conclusion that MYOD either activates or represses gene expression in muscle progenitors by increasing or reducing chromatin accessibility and Kacme levels, respectively, within the context of muscle regeneration. MYOD-mediated repression requires the integrity of functional domains previously implicated in MYOD-mediated activation of gene expression

Our data suggest a “molecular logic” that discriminates MYOD-mediated gene repression by targeting promoters of mitogen-responsive/growth factor-responsive genes from MYOD targeting of SEs of cell of origin and alternative cell lineage genes. Moreover, the different dynamics of chromatin changes underlying MYOD-mediated gene repression predict different mechanisms implicated in MYOD-mediated gene activation versus repression, raising the issue of whether distinct functional domains of MYOD are involved in these different tasks. To this purpose, we used MYOD mutants in which specific functional domains were deleted. Figure 6A illustrates these MYOD mutants, which include deletion (Δ3–56) of the N-terminal activation domain (ΔActDom), small (Δ102–114) and large (Δ102–135) deletion of the first helix (note that because this deleted fragment contains the nuclear localization signal [NLS], a heterologous NLS was added to induce nuclear localization of these mutants), and deletion of both the N-terminal (Δ92–98) and C-terminal (Δ208–269) chromatin remodeling domains (ΔRem). These mutants were expressed in IMR90 cells in a doxy-regulated manner, with similar protein levels detected between full-length MYOD and the MYODΔ102-114NLS mutant, whereas other mutants showed higher levels of expression (Fig. 6B). They all invariably showed a nuclear localization upon culture in GM without any noticeable changes in cell morphology and induction of the endogenous MYOD transcript (Fig. 6C; Supplemental Fig. S11A); however, when incubated in DM, although both IMR90–MYODΔ102-114NLS and IMR90–MYODΔ102-135NLS cells completely failed to differentiate into multinucleated, MyHC-positive myotubes, and IMR90–MYODΔActDom cells formed very sporadic myotubes, IMR90–MYODΔRem cells showed an impaired differentiation index as compared with IMR90–MYOD WT cells yet eventually formed multinucleated MyHC-positive myotubes, albeit with lower efficiency and reduced caliber (Fig. 6D). Thus, in principle, this evidence indicates that mutations that disrupt domains required for activation of gene expression, such as chromatin remodeling, might ultimately be tolerated/compensated to allow the formation of terminally differentiated muscles.

We performed parallel RNA-seq and ATAC-seq in the above-mentioned cells cultured in GM for 24 h after doxy-induced expression of MYOD WT or mutants. This analysis showed mutant-specific patterns of gene expression and chromatin accessibility at promoters of target genes whereby IMR90–MYODΔ102-114NLS and IMR90–MYODΔ102-135NLS cells exhibited profiles very similar to IMR90 control cells, whereas IMR90–MYODΔActDom and IMR90–MYODΔRem cells showed “intermediate” profiles between IMR90–MYOD WT and IMR90 control cells (Fig. 6E–G). Although these “intermediate” profiles of gene expression and chromatin accessibility are mostly accounted by the partial ability of IMR90–MYODΔActDom and IMR90–MYODΔRem to activate gene expression and chromatin remodeling, a closer inspection revealed individual and common patterns of gene repression and reduction in chromatin accessibility among the mutants that define distinct modules of MYOD-mediated repression (Supplemental Fig. S11B). We identified one cluster of genes whose repression requires the 114–135 amino acid sequence, as they are repressed by all mutants except MYODΔ102-135NLS (Fig. 6E; Supplemental Fig. S11B). Gene ontology analysis revealed that all these genes are responsive to mitogens and implicated in the activation of the cell cycle (Supplemental Fig. S11C). One cluster of genes whose repression required only the 102–114 amino acid sequence included growth factor-responsive genes (Fig. 6E; Supplemental Fig. S11D). Another subset of MYOD-repressed genes required the entire 102–135 amino acid sequence, as they are not repressed by either MYODΔ102-114NLS or MYODΔ102-135NLS but continued to be repressed by MYODΔActDom or MYODΔRem and included growth factor-responsive genes also implicated in cell adhesion and fusion (Fig. 6E; Supplemental Fig. S11E). A subset of MYOD-repressed genes required the ActDom and Rem domains, as they are not repressed by either MYODΔActDom or MYODΔRem but continued to be repressed by MYODΔ102-114NLS or MYODΔ102-114NLS. These genes were implicated in cell of origin or alternative cell lineages (Fig. 6E; Supplemental Fig. S11F–H).

Although these data indicate MYOD domain-specific modules of gene repression, it is worth nothing that individual genes might escape these rules, as shown by GATA6 resistance to repression by MYODΔ102-114NLS (Supplemental Fig. S11I). Repression of these genes often requires simultaneous targeting of both promoter and enhancer elements and might reveal a further degree of complexity of MYOD-mediated gene repression.

Discussion

The results shown here reveal a novel property of MYOD as a transcriptional repressor, thereby challenging the existing dogma that has historically restricted MYOD's biological property to that of a sequence-specific transcriptional activator. As such, in principle, this finding extends our knowledge on MYOD from a monotone activator of gene expression to a versatile regulator of gene expression.

We show that MYOD exerts its function as a dual activator and repressor of gene expression during the process of cellular trans-differentiation into skeletal muscle by coordinating two key events within the nuclear reprogramming toward the myogenic lineage: activation of the genes that establish the new cell identity (i.e., skeletal muscle lineage) and erasure of the cell of origin lineage genes. This function is supported by previous works reporting on alternative mesenchymal lineages adopted by MYOD-deficient MuSCs (Wang et al. 2017; Yamamoto et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019). Likewise, extraocular muscles in which repression of mesodermal genes is incomplete show a reduced expression of MYOD (Girolamo et al. 2024). Because nuclear reprogramming is the fundamental process for the determination of cell lineage identity during development and adult tissue regeneration, as well as for induced pluripotency and for somatic cell trans-differentiation (Yamanaka and Blau 2010), our results might provide a general paradigm for a dual function extended to other tissue-specific transcription factors (TFs) implicated in nuclear reprogramming.

MYOD-mediated repression of transcription also includes the downregulation of growth factor-responsive, cytokine-responsive, and mitogen-responsive genes, suggesting that MYOD might prevent a promiscuous activation of gene expression in muscle progenitors exposed to a multitude of extracellular signals. This condition typically occurs when muscle progenitors are exposed to developmental or regeneration signals during embryonal and adult skeletal myogenesis, respectively. In particular, during skeletal muscle regeneration, the activation of MuSCs entails loss of anatomical insulation from the surrounding environment that is provided by the myofiber basal lamina (otherwise defined as the quiescence niche). Consequently, activated MuSCs are exposed to a plethora of regeneration cues and signals, which would promiscuously activate gene expression if not properly interpreted. In this regard, we propose that MYOD functions as a “storm shelter” to prevent the expression of regeneration-activated genes that could eventually bias MuSC function. This is particularly important when activated MuSCs are challenged by the inflammatory cytokines released during the initial stages of regeneration, by M1 macrophages, and by other immune cells, which would otherwise interfere with MuSC commitment and differentiation into multinucleated myofibers (Theret and Chazaud 2024). Furthermore, the transition of MuSCs from proliferation to cell cycle withdrawal prior to their differentiation into myofibers also requires coordinated expression of genes that regulate the different phases of the cell cycle within the complexity of the regeneration milieu. We propose that MYOD-mediated repression of gene expression provides transcriptional tolerance and competence for the proper response to regeneration cues to coordinate sequential patterns of gene expression in MuSCs along their transition from activation to differentiation into myofibers.

Of note, we identified specific molecular modules used by MYOD to repress gene expression from promoters or enhancers of target genes with dedicated domains whereby repression of mitogen-responsive and growth factor-responsive genes from promoters requires a highly conserved domain within the first helix, whereas repression of the cell of origin and alternative lineage genes from SEs requires the ActDom and CRDs.

Our results reveal an interplay between genetic, epigenetic, and molecular determinants that confers on MYOD a dual function of transcriptional activator and repressor. The genetic determinant that discriminates between these two functions is the alternative MYOD chromatin recruitment through binding to myogenic E-box or non-E-box motifs, which leads to two opposite patterns of chromatin accessibility and histone modifications. Previous works established that MYOD-mediated activation of gene expression from E-box motifs entails a sequence of events prompted by the reported ability of MYOD to bind nucleosomes at previously silent loci in cooperation with pioneer factors, such as Pbx1/Meis (Gerber et al. 1997; Berkes et al. 2004), followed by increased chromatin accessibility, which allows full recognition and binding to myogenic E-box sequences (de la Serna et al. 2001, 2005; Simone et al. 2004; Forcales et al. 2012). Heterodimerization with E2A gene products E12 and E47 enables MYOD to fully activate target gene expression (Murre et al. 1989). Specific domains confer on MYOD the property as an E-box-specific transcriptional activator; namely, the basic domain that restricts its DNA binding affinity to specific E-box motifs, the HLH domain that promotes interactions with E12/47, an acidic activation domain (ActDom) at the N terminus, and two chromatin remodeling domains located at the C/H-rich domain and C terminus (Puri and Sartorelli 2000). Previous studies established that binding to E-box sequences triggers intramolecular changes in MYOD that unlock the ActDom to adopt a conformation to activate transcription (Davis et al. 1990; Huang et al. 1998). Likewise, binding to nucleosomes promotes the chromatin remodeling activity of bHLH TFs and activates the enzymatic activity of transcriptional coactivators, such as acetyltransferases (Kikuchi et al. 2023; Michael et al. 2023). Thus, the evidence that MYOD chromatin recruitment at non-E-box motifs within accessible chromatin promotes the opposite outcome (chromatin compaction and repression of gene transcription) reveals a hitherto unappreciated functional versatility of MYOD that is imparted by the alternative chromatin recruitment through genetic determinants: E-box or non-E-box motifs. This evidence also suggests that chromatin recruitment via E-box-independent interactions might turn functional domains of MYOD into effectors of MYOD-mediated repression.

MYOD-mediated repression of gene expression occurs mostly when muscle progenitors are exposed to growth factors, cytokines, and mitogens, as well illustrated by the model of IMR90/MYOD cells cultured in GM. This condition is incompatible with MYOD heterodimerization with E2A proteins and productive binding to E-box motifs (Benezra et al. 1990; Jen et al. 1992). Likewise, MYOD is expressed in MuSCs a few hours after their activation (a stage that coincides with MuSC proliferation, which is incompatible with the activation of muscle gene expression), thus suggesting that MYOD might exert functions alternative to the activation of muscle gene expression. We propose that at this stage, MYOD pervasively binds the genome through weak interactions at both E-box and non-E-box motifs. Initial binding to E-box motifs primes promoters and enhancers of muscle genes for subsequent activation of gene expression upon exposure to prodifferentiation cues, as proposed by previous works (Bergstrom et al. 2002) and supported by recent evidence (Pollex et al. 2024). Within this context, transient recruitment of transcriptional corepressors holds E-box-driven activation of muscle genes until the exposure to prodifferentiation cues as a mechanism that warrants a timely and coordinated activation of muscle gene expression during myoblast-to-myotube transition (Mal et al. 2001; Puri et al. 2001; Bergstrom et al. 2002; Singh et al. 2015; Cui et al. 2017). In this regard, our discovery that MYOD represses the expression of cell of origin and alternative lineages or growth factor-inducible genes from non-E-box motifs substantially differs from the transient inhibition of transcription from E-box sequences. Still, based on current knowledge, we cannot completely rule out the contribution of E-box sequences for repression of a few individual genes. We have identified reduced chromatin remodeling, decreased levels of Kacme, and the strength of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions as key epigenetic determinants of MYOD-mediated gene repression from non-E-box motifs. However, we did not detect any marker of constitutive heterochromatin at MYOD-bound non-E-box motifs within promoters/enhancers of repressed genes. It is possible that the formation of constitutive heterochromatin at these loci occurs later during the process of muscle differentiation, possibly instigated by MYOD-mediated activation of the additional transcriptional repressors, such as the zinc finger protein RP58 (also known as Zfp238) (Yokoyama et al. 2009) or DNMT3 (Naito et al. 2016). We also note that MYOD-mediated repression of ectopic gene expression during cellular trans-differentiation could be a general feature of tissue-specific TFs with similar activity or structure. For instance, previous studies have shown promiscuous binding to common sites and interchangeable activity between bHLH-containing TFs, resulting in both activation and repression of gene expression (Fong et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2020; Yagi et al. 2021). Likewise, these factors exhibit cell lineage-specific activities despite structural similarities (Casey et al. 2018). It is likely that the gene repression properties of bHLH-containing tissue-specific TFs during trans-differentiation also vary depending on the developmental origin of the cell types, as predicted by the seminal work of Weintraub et al. (1989).

In sum, our data extend the biological properties of MYOD beyond our current knowledge by revealing its dual function as activator and repressor of gene expression through a multifaceted mechanism. The selection of either one of these functions is determined by an interplay between genetic, epigenetic, and molecular determinants upon muscle progenitor transition through cell states and exposure to regeneration cues. This is consistent with the early prediction that MYOD senses and integrates many facets of cell state (Weintraub et al. 1991a). This is also consistent with the emerging evidence from ChIP-seq studies that a significant fraction of binding sites of TFs lacks their typical consensus sequences, as predicted by motif analysis, possibly implicating indirect chromatin binding via interactions with other TFs or through alternative mechanism such as the relative affinity of intrinsically disordered domains (IDRs) for transcriptional condensates (Lambert et al. 2018; Suter 2020; Zaret 2020; Jonas et al. 2025; Muñoz et al. 2025).

We argue that defective execution of MYOD-mediated repression of gene expression might be tolerated as long as potential mutations that impair this property do not affect MYOD's ability to activate skeletal myogenesis. We postulate that one consequence of this trade-off could be the defective long-term maintenance of the functional properties of myofibers, as they represent a perennial tissue with limited nuclear turnover that relies on MuSC-mediated regeneration.

Materials and methods

Cell culture experiments

IMR90-empty or MYOD WT or mutant-expressing IMR90− cells were maintained in growth media (GM) consisting of EMEM (ATCC) supplemented with 10% FBS (Omega Scientific). Cells were regularly tested for the absence of mycoplasma.

Myogenic conversion

Myogenic conversion was performed as described previously by Dall'Agnese et al. (2019). Briefly, MyoD (or MyoD truncation) expression was induced with 200 ng/µL doxycycline at 60% confluency, and cells were collected and analyzed 24 h after induction.

Mouse injury and muscle stem cell isolation

For CUT&RUN experiments, muscle injury and MuSC FACS isolation were performed as described previously by Dong et al. (2022). For siRNA experiments and RNA-seq, the whole limb muscles were dissected and processed as described previously (Liu et al. 2015). For isolation, a BD FACSAria II 16-color high-speed cell sorter was used. DAPIneg CD31neg CD45neg TER119neg SCA1neg ITGApos VCAMpos cells were isolated as MuSCs and plated on Matrigel-coated (Corning 354277) 6 well plate with growth media composed of DMEM F-12 supplemented with 10% FBS (Omega Scientific) and 2% Ultroser G (Sartorius). The antibodies used were as follows: rat monoclonal antibody Pacific Blue (Invitrogen RM5228), rat monoclonal antibody eFluor 450 (Invitrogen 48-0451-82), rat monoclonal antibody (TER-119) eFluor 450 (Invitrogen 48-5921-82), rat monoclonal anti-Ly-6A/E (Sca1) FITC (BD Pharmingen 553335), rat monoclonal anti-ITGA R-phycoerythrin (ABlab 53-0010), rat monoclonal anti-CD106 (VCAM) (BioLegend 105720), and FxCycle violet stain (DAPI) (Invitrogen F10347).

All animal experiments were approved by the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Animal Ethics Committee or the Stanford Burnham Prebys Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

HiChIP H3K27ac and CTCF library preparation

HiChIP experiments were performed using the ARIMA HiC+ kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 5 million IMR90 or IMR90/MyoD cells were resuspended in 5 mL of growth media and fixed with 2% FA for 10 min at room temperature followed by quenching with stop solution1 according to the ARIMA HiC+ protocol. Digestion was performed overnight at 37°C. Biotinylated enriched fragments were pulled down with anti-H3K27ac (Active Motif 91193) or anti-CTCF (Active Motif 91285). H3K27ac- or CTCF-enriched material was then used as input for library preparation using an Accel-NGS 2S Plus DNA library kit (Swift Biosciences 21096) according to the ARIMA HiChIP library preparation protocol. The obtained libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeqS4 PE 2x100 at the Institute for Genomic Medicine at University of California, San Diego (IGM UCSD), at a depth of ∼300 million reads per library.

CTCF ChIP-seq library preparation

For each ChIP-seq replicate, 4 × 106 cells were fixed with 1% FA for 10 min at room temperature followed by quenching with glycine (final concentration 200 mM) for 15 min on ice. Nuclei were then extracted in hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.05% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor) and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 0.5% SDS, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor). Chromatin was sheared with a sonicator (S2 Covaris) to an average DNA fragment length of 200–500 bp. Fifteen micrograms sonicated chromatin was diluted in 500 µL of RIPA dilution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.7% NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor) and precleared for 3 h at 4°C with Protein A/G Dynabeads. In parallel, 10 µL of CTCF antibody (Cell Signaling Technology 3418S) or 1 µg of rabbit IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-2027) was prebound to Protein A/G Dynabeads in 500 µL of PBS containing 5 µg/mL BSA for 3 h at 4°C. Bead-bound antibodies were mixed with precleared chromatin and incubated overnight at 4°C on a rotator. The chromatin-bound fraction was washed four times with RIPA washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% NP-40), followed by LiCl washing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA at pH 8.0, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% NP-40) and TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA), for 10 min at 4°C for each wash. Chromatin was then eluted in elution buffer (1% SDS in TE buffer) at 600 rpm for 6 h at 65°C. Precipitated material and 10 ng of input were used as input for library preparation using an Accel-NGS 2S Plus DNA library kit (Swift Biosciences 21096) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The obtained libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeqS4 PE 2x100 at the IGM UCSD at a depth of ∼100 million reads per library.

CUT&RUN library preparation

For each CUT&RUN experiment, 100,000 IMR90 cells or 250,000 muscle stem cells were used as input. CUT&RUN experiments were performed using the CUT&RUN assay kit (Cell Signaling Technology 86652) with the following modification: Cells were briefly fixed with 0.1% FA for 2 min at room temperature followed by quenching with glycine; antibody binding was performed overnight at 4°C. The antibodies used were anti-MYOD (BD Bioscience 554130), anti-H4Kacme, anti-Kacme, anti-H3K27ac (Active Motif 91193), anti-H3K4me3 (Cell Signaling Technology 9751), anti-H3K4me1 (Active Motif 39635), anti-H3K27me3 (Active Motif 39155), and anti-H3K9me3 (Active Motif 39161). CUT&RUN libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library preparation kit for Illumina (NEB E7645S) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were sequenced at a depth of ∼20 million reads per library on a NovaSeqS4 PE 2x100 at the IGM UCSD.

ATAC-seq library preparation

One-hundred-thousand freshly collected IMR90 cells were subjected to ATAC-seq library preparation using the ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif 53150) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were sequenced at a depth of ∼200 million reads per library on a NovaSeqS4 PE 2x100 at the IGM UCSD.

RNA-seq library preparation

Equal inputs of total RNA (10–100 ng) were used to generate stranded total RNA libraries for sequencing using the Illumina stranded total RNA preparation, as well as ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus (Illumina 20040525). ERCC RNA spike-in mix was added at the start of the protocol according to the manufacturer's instruction (Thermo Fisher Scientific 4456740). Libraries were sequenced at a depth of ∼50 million reads per library on a NovaSeqS4 PE 2x100 at the IGM UCSD.

Hi-C, H3K27ac ChIP-seq (IMR90), and MYOD ChIP-seq (IMR90/MyoD) data are available on GEO (GSE98530 and GSE128527). Public ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data from MuSCs are available on GEO (GSE189074), as well as MYOD ChIP-seq in primary myoblasts (GSE56077).
