The prevalence and risk factors of hyperuricemia in patients with Newly-diagnosed coronary artery disease from 2017 to 2021
Authors: Shaofang Xian, Weiyu Qiu, Shanshan Liu, Shaorong Wu, Jiali Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and determinants of hyperuricemia in patients with newly-diagnosed coronary artery disease (CAD) after percutaneous coronary intervention.MethodsHyperuricemia was defined as a serum urate level of > 5.6 mg/dl. Medical records and face-to-face interviews were used to collect the demographic characteristics, medical history, lifestyle, self-management, physical examination, and laboratory examination information for each patient. Logistic regression was then used to assess the associations between hyperuricemia and several independent risk factors.ResultsThe regression results showed that the overall prevalence of hyperuricemia was 43.2% (42.0% in men and 47.0% in women) and gender, hypertension, triglycerides, body mass index (BMI) and HbA1c were risk factors for hyperuricemia.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that the prevalence of hyperuricemia is very high in newly-diagnosed CAD patients after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), but the associated risk factors we identified can be ameliorated by diet and lifestyle changes.Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Hyperuricemia, Prevalence, Risk factors, Lifestyle modification

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is characterized by increased levels of uric acid in the blood and can be caused by disruption of purine metabolism, excessive uric acid secretion or renal excretion dysfunction. In addition, hyperuricemia has been found to contribute to increased risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality [1, 2], hypertension [3, 4], gout [5] and diabetes [6]. Previous studies have reported the prevalence of hyperuricemia in New Caledonia [7], China [8–11], Korea [12], the United States [13, 14], Ethiopia [15], and Jordan [16], where the rates of HUA in these populations vary from 6.4–67.0% [7, 17].

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death in both developed and developing countries across the world [18]. In fact, with a 13% of all recorded deaths being attributed to CAD, the 2010 Global Burden of Disease Study even characterized it as a modern pandemic [19]. HUA has been shown to affect the development of metabolic diseases in patients, and although many studies have reported the prevalence and risk factors of HUA in the general population, the prevalence of HUA and its related risk factors in newly-diagnosed CAD patients after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has yet to be investigated. Therefore, this study was conducted in order to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of HUA in these patients specifically.

Materials and methods

Study population

From January 2017 to December 2021, a total of 2015 inpatients aged 23–93 years were newly-diagnosed with CAD by coronary angiography and successfully underwent stent implantation in acute or elective PCI in the cardiology department of a general hospital in Guangzhou, Guangdong, China. A total of 367 patients were recruited in 2017, 400 in 2018, 490 in 2019, 351 in 2020, and 407 in 2021.

Patients were excluded if they had a history of severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, malignant tumors, autoimmune diseases, thyroid disorders, or if they lacked complete laboratory data during hospitalization. Patients with acute infections, inflammatory diseases or who were pregnant or breastfeeding were also excluded.

Data collection

Written informed consent was obtained from patients enrolled between 2017 and 2019, but patients enrolled from 2020 to 2021 were only verbally informed consent about the study. To control observation bias, all the data were collected by a case manager in the cardiology department of the hospital from both medical records and face-to-face interviews. The data collected included demographic characteristics (name, age, gender, inpatient number, date of PCI, address, dialect, telephone number, residence, education, marital status, occupation, and monthly family income); medical history variables (hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and history of PCI); lifestyle and self-management information (diet control, daily staple food intake, regular exercise, smoking and drinking history); physical examination (height, weight, waist circumference and hip circumference) and laboratory examination results (total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), LDL-cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP)). Lipid measurements were obtained in the morning after an overnight fast, and TC (enzymatic method), TG (enzymatic method), HDL-C (direct method), and LDL-C (direct method) levels were measured as parenthetically described. All laboratory results were obtained during hospitalization.

Definitions

The 1999 WHO diagnostic criteria were used to diagnose DM as follows [20]: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2h postprandial plasma glucose (2hPG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or diabetic symptoms along with a random plasma glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, and HUA was defined as serum urate concentration > 5.6 mg/dl [21]. Based on the status of self-reported smoking and the definitions from the Global Adult Tobacco Survey, individuals were categorized into three groups of smokers: never, former, and current smokers [22]. Respondents were also asked whether they had an alcoholic drink during the past year.

Body mass index (BMI) was divided into four groups: low weight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m2), overweight (24 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) according to the adult weight standard published by the Ministry of Health of China. Similarly, central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm in women [23]. Light physical activity referred to sitting, standing, or walking, and activities not requiring special muscular function, such as reading, writing, performing office work, assembling and repairing machines, lecturing, performing general laboratory operations and doing housework. Moderate physical activity referred to activities with greater muscular requirements, such as performing daily student-related activities, driving motor vehicles, installing electrical components, cutting metal, and performing wood working operations. Heavy physical activity referred to such tasks as non-mechanized agricultural labor, steelmaking, lathe operation, and sports activities such as swimming, mountain climbing, and football. Finally, regular exercise was defined as exercising at least 3 times a week for at least 30 mins at a time.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (version 20.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, New York), and all quantitative data were expressed as mean ± SD prior to analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies. Comparisons of quantitative data were conducted using the independent sample t test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to assess whether or not the above-described variables were risk factors for HUA in the population. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were expressed. A 2-tailed P value less than 0.05 being considered significant.

A total of 2015 patients with newly-diagnosed CAD were enrolled in the study. The mean age of the patients was 62.21 ± 11.70 years (23–93 years); 1536 (76.2%) patients were men, and 479 (23.8%) were women. Prevalence of Hyperuricemia

The overall prevalence of HUA was 43.2% (n = 870/2015) in the study population, and there was significant difference between patients with various accompanying diseases (P < 0.001). Importantly, the prevalence of HUA significantly increased with BMI (P < 0.001), and the prevalence of HUA in patients with central obesity was higher than in patients without central obesity (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and associated risk factors of HUA in newly-diagnosed CAD patients after PCI from January 2017 and December 2021. We found that the overall prevalence of HUA was 43.2% (42.0% in men and 47.0% in women), which exceeded previous reports from China (from 6.4–34.05%) [17, 24], the United States (21.4% and 20.1%) [13, 14], Korea (11.4%) [12], Ethiopia (22%) [15] and Jordan (28.1%) [16], but was lower than that of New Caledonia (57.5%) [7]. These variations may be attributed to differences in population characteristics, diagnostic thresholds and lifestyle factors. In our study, gender, hypertension, TC, BMI and HbA1c were associated with HUA.

Although previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of hyperuricemia in men [12, 24], our findings showed the opposite, consistent with Bardin et al. [7]. This discrepancy may be partially explained by the older average age of female participants in our cohort (68.58 ± 9.55 vs 60.23 ± 11.60, P < 0.001), as HUA prevalence increases with age [25, 26] as well as the potential influence of postmenopausal hormonal changes, which are known to reduce renal uric acid excretion. In addition, women in our study had higher triglyceride and total cholesterol levels—both metabolic factors closely linked to elevated serum uric acid. These results suggest that sex-specific hormonal and metabolic characteristics may have a stronger impact on hyperuricemia risk when using more sensitive diagnostic criteria, such as the URRAH threshold. Menopausal status and hormonal changes may also contribute to the higher prevalence of hyperuricemia observed in women in our study. Estrogen has been shown to promote uric acid excretion via renal mechanisms, and its decline after menopause may lead to elevated serum uric acid levels. This hormonal shift could partially explain the increased prevalence in our older female cohort. Furthermore, selection bias may also play a role, as the female participants in our study were significantly older than the male participants, potentially representing a subgroup with more comorbidities or advanced disease. These factors combined may account for the discrepancy with previous studies reporting a higher prevalence of hyperuricemia in men.

Hypertension has been widely recognized as a major risk factor for HUA [24]. Consistently, we found that its prevalence was significantly higher among HUA patients compared to non-HUA patients (67.4% vs. 55.5%, P < 0.001), and the risk increased 1.290-fold in hypertensive individuals.

Our findings also support the positive association between TG and HUA [9]. Among lipid parameters, TG remained an independent risk factor for hyperuricemia. For every 1 mmol/L increase in TG, the risk of HUA increased by 0.766-fold. This association may reflect the link between hypertriglyceridemia, insulin resistance, and reduced renal urate excretion, all of which contribute to elevated serum uric acid.

BMI was also significantly associated with HUA, consistent with previous studies [24]. In terms of glycemic control, HbA1c was also significantly associated with hyperuricemia, with a 1.202-fold increase in risk per 1% rise in HbA1c [27]. As a marker of long-term glucose metabolism, elevated HbA1c may indicate subtle metabolic dysfunctions that affect renal urate handling even in the absence of overt diabetes. These findings highlight the importance of early metabolic abnormalities, particularly dyslipidemia and impaired glycemic control in the pathogenesis of hyperuricemia.

While some studies have reported associations between smoking, alcohol, and HUA [12]. Our analysis did not find them to be independent risk factors. This may be due to the older age of our cohort (mean 62.21 ± 11.70 years), in which many individuals may have reduced their tobacco and alcohol consumption because of health concerns.

Our findings are also consistent with recent work by Maloberti and colleagues, who explored the relationship between uric acid (UA), hyperuricemia, insulin resistance (IR), and metabolic syndrome (MS) in hypertensive patients [28]. Using both traditional and URRAH thresholds for hyperuricemia, their study found that elevated UA was significantly associated with IR and a markedly increased risk of MS (OR: 5.13). Similarly, our study identified higher triglyceride levels, BMI, and HbA1c as significant predictors of hyperuricemia, which are also key components of MS. These shared metabolic risk factors reinforce the importance of considering uric acid as a contributor to cardiometabolic dysfunction.

Moreover, in another study conducted during cardiac rehabilitation among patients with acute or chronic coronary syndrome, Maloberti’s group demonstrated that hyperuricemia was associated with lower ejection fraction but not with functional recovery (6-minute walk test) [29]. These findings complement our results by further emphasizing the clinical burden of elevated UA in cardiovascular settings, particularly in patients with compromised metabolic profiles and reduced cardiac function. The overlap in identified risk factors—such as age, BMI, hypertension and triglycerides—highlights a common pathophysiological basis and supports a broader cardiometabolic interpretation of hyperuricemia.

In addition to its association with overt cardiovascular disease, hyperuricemia has been increasingly recognized as a marker of early, subclinical organ damage. Pagliaro et al. [30] reported that elevated serum uric acid levels were independently associated with early signs of cardiac and renal dysfunction in apparently healthy individuals, including increased left ventricular mass and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate [30]. These findings support the notion that hyperuricemia may not only be a consequence of established cardiometabolic disease, but also an early indicator of underlying vascular or metabolic stress. This is particularly relevant to our study, as a significant proportion of newly diagnosed CAD patients may already exhibit undetected metabolic abnormalities or subclinical damage at the time of diagnosis. Incorporating serum uric acid screening into early risk stratification may thus provide clinical benefit by identifying high-risk individuals prior to the onset of symptomatic disease.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, due to its cross-sectional design, the study can only demonstrate associations rather than establish causal relationships between hyperuricemia and the identified risk factors. Finally, given the rising prevalence of chronic conditions beyond coronary artery disease, future studies should consider broader and more diverse patient populations to better understand the distribution and determinants of hyperuricemia.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the results of the current study indicate that the prevalence of HUA is very high in newly-diagnosed CAD patients after PCI. Our findings also suggest that risk of HUA is associated with gender, hypertension, TG, BMI, and HbA1c, most of which can be ameliorated by diet and lifestyle changes. Therefore, we recommend that future work includes a focus on raising awareness of these risk factors among the general public.
