Development and validation of a prediction model for all-cause death in heart failure patients with preserved ejection fraction: a single-centre cohort study in China
Authors: Yunlong Zhu, Wenjiao Zhao, Zhican Liu, Dan Tan, Liang Tao, Weifeng He, Kang Jiang, Haobo Huang, Mingxing Wu, Liang Tang, JianPing Zeng, Shenghua Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify risk factors and develop a predictive model for postdischarge all-cause mortality in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingTertiary care, Xiangtan Central Hospital, Hunan, China.Participants9419 patients with HFpEF, diagnosed between May 2014 and January 2023 according to 2021 European Society of Cardiology criteria (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) ≥125 pg/mL, left ventricular ejection fraction ≥50%, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II-IV). Exclusions were age <18 years, pregnancy, malignancy with life expectancy <1 year, dialysis-dependent renal failure, non-cardiac life expectancy <1 year and in-hospital death during index admission. Patients were allocated to a training cohort (n=6935) and a temporal validation cohort (n=2484).Primary outcome measuresAll-cause mortality within 3 years of hospital discharge.Results10 independent predictors were identified: NT-proBNP, albumin, age, NYHA class III-IV, C reactive protein, right atrial end-systolic diameter, haemoglobin, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hyponatraemia and prior percutaneous coronary intervention. The prediction model demonstrated good discrimination, an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.756 (95% CI 0.743 to 0.769) in the training cohort and 0.764 in the validation cohort. Based on calculated risk scores, patients were classified into low-risk (≤200.36), medium-risk (200.37–232.24) and high-risk (≥232.25) groups, corresponding to 3-year mortality rates of 12.6%, 40.8% and 68.0%, respectively.ConclusionsA simple clinical model using routinely available parameters enables effective 3-year mortality risk stratification in HFpEF patients after discharge. External validation is needed to confirm its generalisability.Trial registration numberNCT06487468.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a unique and complex cardiovascular syndrome characterised by left ventricular diastolic dysfunction with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).1 With the ageing global population and the increasing prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as obesity, hypertension, sedentary behaviour and cardiometabolic disorders, the incidence of HFpEF continues to rise, presenting a major public health challenge.2 3 The latest Chinese Heart Failure Guidelines (2024) highlight a steady increase in HF prevalence due to an ageing population and a rising burden of chronic diseases, including hypertension and diabetes.4 A recent nationwide population-based analysis using data from the National Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance System further confirmed this trend, showing a marked increase in HF incidence and prevalence with age, with HF prevalence reaching 7.55% and an incidence of 1655 per 100 000 among individuals aged ≥80 years.5 Another 2024 nationwide HF registry study estimated that China has approximately 12.1 million HF patients, with HFpEF accounting for nearly 36% of cases.6 As a result, the healthcare burden of HFpEF in China continues to grow. However, there are significant regional and ethnic differences in HFpEF prevalence,7 and Chinese HFpEF patients demonstrate a higher incidence of comorbidities and mortality risk compared with Western populations.8 Given the complex pathophysiology of HFpEF, individualised treatment approaches remain a challenge, necessitating more precise risk stratification and tailored therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes.

Although the medical community’s understanding of the pathophysiology and clinical features has been deepened and treatment options and prognosis of HFpEF have been improved over the past decade, the mortality of HFpEF patients remains high. Data from the China Cardiovascular Association Database-Heart Failure Centre Registry showed that all-cause mortality in HFpEF patients was 11.9%, 25.4% at 1 year and 3 years, respectively.9 Therefore, it is particularly important to assess the risk of all-cause mortality in patients for timely intervention and treatment adjustment among discharged HFpEF patients. In recent years, with the continuous accumulation of medical data and advances in machine learning technology, prediction models based on large datasets have been increasingly developed in the medical field.10 11 These models can integrate multidimensional patient information, including clinical characteristics, laboratory tests and imaging studies, to provide more accurate risk assessment. However, there are still relatively few risk prediction models for all-cause mortality in HFpEF patients.

The present study aims to fill this gap by developing and validating a comprehensive novel all-cause mortality risk prediction model for HFpEF patients post discharge. Identifying discharged HFpEF patients at increased risk of all-cause death with distinct clinical features might help decision making of personalised and precise treatment plans for these patients.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with HFpEF at the Department of Cardiology of Xiangtan City Central Hospital from May 2014 to January 2023, registered in the HF database (NCT06487468). Initially, 12 404 patients were identified with HFpEF according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, which stipulate NT-proBNP ≥125 pg/mL, a left ventricular ejection fraction of ≥50% and New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification II–IV. The exclusion criteria included: (1) age under 18 years; (2) pregnancy; (3) malignant tumours with life expectancy less than 1 year; (4) renal failure requiring dialysis; (5) a life expectancy of less than 1 year due to non-cardiac conditions and (6) patients who died during the enrolment admission. The final cohort consisted of 9419 patients (figure 1). Demographic and clinical data, including patient medical history, initial clinical evaluations, laboratory examination and therapeutic interventions, as well as echocardiographic data, were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health records (EHRs) and relevant databases. To ensure data integrity and completeness, structured clinical data from the hospital’s EHR system were used. All included variables were routinely and systematically recorded as part of standard clinical practice for hospitalised HFpEF patients, ensuring high data completeness.

Study flowchart of participant selection and cohort allocation. Flow diagram illustrating the screening, exclusion criteria and final enrolment of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) patients from May 2014 to January 2023 (n=9419). The cohort was divided into training (n=6935) and validation (n=2484) sets for model development and temporal validation. Key exclusions: age <18 years, pregnancy, malignancies, renal failure and in-hospital deaths.

Follow-up and primary endpoints

A team of three experienced cardiologists and 12 nursing professionals conducted outcome assessments through outpatient consultations, telephone follow-ups and community-based interactions. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality within 3 years following enrolment discharge. This study was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under Registration Number: NCT06487468.

Training set and validation set

To balance robust model development and real-world applicability, we employed a temporal split for training and validation. Patients enrolled between May 2014 and December 2020 (n=6935) formed the training set, while those enrolled between January 2021 and January 2023 (n=2484) comprised the validation set. A 7:3 ratio was selected to balance model training efficiency and validation reliability. The training set was used to develop the prediction model and perform internal validation, while the validation set was used for temporal validation of the established model. We deliberately chose temporal validation as it might better align with real-world dynamic clinical settings. Prediction models are intended to add risk stratification of future clinical cohorts, and temporal validation models help assess independent cohorts from a later period. So, we chose the temporal validation cohort in the absence of external validation datasets.

Prediction model development and risk stratification

Risk factors were identified using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariable Cox regression. A nomogram was constructed in R statistical software to assign point values to each predictor, derived from a fitted Cox proportional hazards model. The total risk score for each patient was calculated by summing these values. Patients were stratified into low-risk, medium-risk and high-risk groups using the MaxStat method, which optimised cut-offs by maximising the log-rank test statistic. Kaplan-Meier estimates were used to assess prognostic performance across risk groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA), R Statistical Software (V.4.3.3; R Core Team 2022) and the Storm Statistical Platform (www.medsta.cn/software). Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All included variables had complete data in the analysis cohort. No imputation for missing values was required in this study.

Continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to verify the normality of continuous variables. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Differences across three groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical or dichotomous variables were expressed as counts and percentages, with differences among groups compared using the χ2 test.

The development and validation of the prediction model used R Statistical Software in conjunction with SPSS. Potential predictive variables associated with mortality were identified through intergroup comparisons. Variables with p values <0.05 were included in LASSO regression for further selection. If more than 10 predictors were identified, multivariable Cox regression with stepwise forward selection based on the likelihood ratio was used to refine the selection of the best predictors for the prediction model. The variance inflation factor was computed to assess multicollinearity among continuous variables.

A nomogram was constructed to visualise the final model results using the rms (V.6.7–1; Frank E Harrell Jr) and nomogram Formula (V.1.2.0.0; Jing Zhang et al) packages. The nomogram score for each patient was calculated using the formula_rd() and point_ca() functions from the nomogram Formula package. Patients are classified into three risk groups based on predefined score cut-offs, which were determined using the MaxStat algorithm, optimising the log-rank test statistic to maximise prognostic separation. Calibration curves were created using the calibrate() function from the rms package, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test was performed using the chisq.test() function from the Resource Selection (V.0.3–6; Subhash R. Lele et al) package. The discrimination ability of the models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves based on C-index, with the area under the ROC curves (AUC) and 95% CIs assessed, employing the survival ROC (V.1.0.3.1; Patrick J. Heagerty) package. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to measure the net benefit of the model, facilitated by the stdca.R.

Internal validation was performed within the training set using a bootstrap resampling approach with 1000 replications, employing the survival ROC and boot (V.1.3.28.1; Angelo Canty) packages, to assess the model’s stability within the training set. For external validation, a temporal validation cohort of patients enrolled from January 2021 to January 2023 was used. The prediction model, developed from the training cohort, was applied unchanged to the validation cohort to evaluate its temporal generalisability and predictive performance. Model discrimination was evaluated using the AUC with a 95% CI, and calibration was assessed through calibration plots and the H-L test.

Results

Baseline description

The mean age and sex distribution of the patients were similar between the training set (mean age 72.0±10.5 years, male 51.5%) and the validation set (mean age 71.9±10.8 years, male 52.8%). Differences in demographic characteristics, clinical parameters and echocardiographic parameters are presented in online supplemental table S1.

Identification of independent risk factors of all-cause death

Intergroup comparisons identified 35 parameters associated with all-cause mortality in the training set (online supplemental table S2). Subsequent LASSO regression analyses (online supplemental figure S1) narrowed these down to 16 risk factors correlated with all-cause mortality (table 1). These factors included age, body mass index, NYHA classification, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischaemic stroke, pericardial effusion, pleural effusion, haemoglobin, albumin, creatinine (Cr), uric acid, C reactive protein (CRP), N-terminal pro b-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), hyponatraemia, right ventricular end-diastolic diameter and right atrial end-systolic diameter (RADs). Through multivariable Cox regression analysis, 10 of these factors were identified as the strongest predictors for the prediction model of all-cause mortality. These factors, which include NT-proBNP, albumin, age, NYHA class, CRP, RADs, haemoglobin, COPD, hyponatraemia and PCI (table 2), were ultimately included in the prediction model.

Model development and evaluation

The established predictive model demonstrated good discriminative ability with an AUC value of 0.756 (95% CI 0.743 to 0.769) for 3-year all-cause mortality in the training set (figure 2). The calibration of the prediction model was assessed using calibration curves and the H-L test. The calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, indicating that the model was well-calibrated (online supplemental figure S2). The H-L test yielded a non-significant result (p=1.000), suggesting no significant deviation between predicted and observed mortality rates, which supports the model’s calibration. DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the developed prediction model (online supplemental figure S3). The DCA results demonstrated that the prediction model provided a higher net benefit compared with both the treat-all and treat-none strategies across various threshold probabilities. At a threshold probability of 0.1, the net benefit of the prediction model was 0.157, while the treat-all strategy had a net benefit of 0.147, and the treat-none strategy had a net benefit of 0. At a threshold probability of 0.3, the model maintained a positive net benefit of 0.065, significantly higher than the treat-all strategy, which had a net benefit of −0.096. Even at a higher threshold probability of 0.5, the model provided a net benefit of 0.016, compared with −0.535 for the treat-all strategy and 0 for the treat-none strategy. These results indicate that the prediction model offers a higher net benefit across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities. This suggests that the model can effectively identify patients at high risk for all-cause mortality postdischarge.

Model validation

Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap replications confirmed the model’s stability, yielding a median AUC of 0.731 (95% CI: 0.719 to 0.743; online supplemental figure S4). The temporal validation cohort had a similar age (71.9±10.8 vs 72.0±10.5 years) and sex distribution (52.8% vs 51.5% male) compared with the training cohort, though differences in comorbidities and laboratory parameters were observed (online supplemental table S1). In the validation cohort, the model demonstrated an AUC of 0.764 (95% CI 0.742 to 0.786) for 3-year all-cause mortality, aligning with the training set (AUC 0.756, 95% CI 0.743 to 0.769). Calibration plots indicated moderate agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (H-L test, p=0.12; online supplemental figure S5).

Nomogram construction and risk stratification

A nomogram was developed to estimate 1-year, 2-year and 3-year all-cause mortality by assigning point values to each predictor (figure 3). The total risk score for each patient was calculated by summing these values. Risk stratification was performed using the MaxStat method, which determined optimal cut-offs: low-risk (≤200.36), medium-risk (200.37–232.24) and high-risk (≥232.25). Among the entire cohort (n=9419), patients were stratified into low-risk (n=7014), medium-risk (n=2042) and high-risk (n=363) groups. A risk score ≥232.25 showed high specificity (0.984, 95% CI 0.981 to 0.987) but low sensitivity (0.125, 95% CI 0.111 to 0.141). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed significant differences in cumulative survival probabilities across risk groups (Log-Rank p<0.001; figure 4). To evaluate the feasibility of simplified thresholds, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using rounded cut-offs: low-risk (≤200), medium-risk (201-230) and high-risk (≥231). The model’s discriminative ability remained unchanged, with a minimal AUC difference (<0.002). Using these thresholds, 3-year all-cause mortality rates were 12.5%, 40.6% and 67.9% for low-risk, medium-risk and high-risk groups, respectively (p<0.001), closely aligning with the original MaxStat-derived cutoffs (12.6%, 40.8% and 68.0%).

Discussion

The study employed a large, temporally validated cohort to develop and assess a risk stratification model for all-cause mortality in HFpEF patients. We chose temporal validation to better reflect real-world clinical practice and, in the absence of an external validation dataset, to ensure the model was tested on an independent cohort under potentially evolving management strategies. This approach also allows for an assessment of the model’s robustness over time, taking into account dynamic changes in clinical practices and patient characteristics that may differ between cohorts from distinct periods. The use of rigorous statistical modelling, including LASSO regression for feature selection and Cox proportional hazards models for outcome prediction, allowed for the identification of key prognostic markers while minimising overfitting. Additionally, internal validation using bootstrap resampling reinforced the model’s stability, while temporal validation demonstrated its applicability over time. Unlike many prior studies relying on cross-sectional datasets or single-centre analyses, this study leveraged a large-scale, multiyear dataset, enhancing its generalisability. 10 factors, which include NT-proBNP, albumin, age, NYHA class, CRP, RADs, haemoglobin, COPD, hyponatraemia and PCI, were included in the prediction model. The total risk score for each patient was calculated by summing the individual scores of each predictor. Patients were then categorised into three risk groups in the whole cohort: low-risk, medium-risk and high-risk. The 3-year all-cause mortality showed a marked and progressive increase across the low-risk, medium-risk and high-risk patients with HFpEF, with rates of 12.6%, 40.8% and 68.0%, respectively (p<0.001). The predictive performance of a risk score ≥232.25 for identifying patients at high risk of all-cause mortality was notable, with a specificity of 0.984 (95% CI 0.981 to 0.987) and a sensitivity of 0.125 (95% CI 0.111 to 0.141).

Identified prognostic markers

Our study confirmed that well-established prognostic markers, including NT-proBNP, CRP, albumin and NYHA classification, are critical determinants of all-cause mortality in HFpEF. NT-proBNP reflects both cardiac dysfunction and systemic inflammation, contributing to a cycle of worsening HF.1214 CRP, an indicator of chronic inflammation, is linked to myocardial fibrosis, adverse cardiac remodelling and worse outcomes.15 Hypoalbuminaemia exacerbates fluid retention and inflammation,1619 further worsening cardiac function20 21 and poor outcomes,22 23 while COPD-related systemic inflammation and hypoxia impose additional cardiac stress.2426 Additionally, anaemia,27 hyponatraemia28 and increased right atrial diameter29 were associated with poor outcomes, underscoring the multifactorial nature of HFpEF prognosis.

Beyond confirming these established risk factors, our findings highlight the need for targeted management strategies. Monitoring NT-proBNP and CRP may help guide anti-inflammatory interventions, while addressing hypoalbuminaemia through nutritional support could mitigate its detrimental effects. Moreover, our model identified PCI as a protective factor,3032 reinforcing the importance of timely revascularisation in ischaemic HFpEF. Future research should explore personalised therapeutic approaches integrating these predictors to improve long-term outcomes in this heterogeneous HFpEF population.

Comparison with existing models

Previous models, such as the SVM(Support Vector Machine-a supervised machine learning algorithm used for classification and regression tasks. It works by finding the optimal hyperplane that best separates data points of different classes in a high-dimensional space, maximizing the margin between them.) model incorporating 18 biomarkers (including growth differentiation factor-15 andTumor Necrosis Factor - α) and the DELIVER model with 11 predictors, have demonstrated good predictive performance for all-cause mortality in HFpEF patients. For instance, Gao et al developed an SVM model to predict 2-year mortality in acute HFpEF patients (AUC 0.853, 95% CI 0.788 to 0.917).33 Similarly, the DELIVER model—derived from data in the DELIVER, PARAGON-HF and I-PRESERVE trials—used 11 predictors to estimate 1-year and 2-year mortality risk, achieving C-statistics of 0.71 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.74) and 0.68 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.70), respectively.34 In another study, Liu et al from northwest China combined systemic inflammatory markers (such as the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio) with traditional risk factors (age, BMI, NYHA class and Cr) to predict 1-year and 3-year all-cause mortality in chronic HF patients, with AUCs of 0.725 (95% CI 0.672 to 0.778) and 0.726 (95% CI 0.639 to 0.813).35 Compared with these models, our model stratifies patients into low-risk, medium-risk and high-risk groups and incorporates DCA to better assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients. This risk stratification enables an interdisciplinary management approach—such as establishing combined cardiology-pulmonology-nutrition clinics—to provide personalised management and dynamic follow-up. Targeted interventions may include optimising inhaler/steroid use, enhancing nutritional support and administering tailored anti-inflammatory treatments, with periodic reassessment of risk factors to adjust treatment plans and reduce postdischarge mortality.

Clinical applications

This prediction model, based on readily available clinical variables, provides a practical tool for risk stratification in HFpEF. By integrating well-established prognostic markers—NT-proBNP, NYHA class, albumin and inflammatory indicators—our model moves beyond conventional risk assessment to support personalised management strategies. Unlike previous studies that assessed these predictors individually, our model optimises their combined predictive value using a large real-world dataset, demonstrating robust discrimination (AUC=0.764 in validation). Identifying high-risk patients early allows for a shift from reactive to proactive care, enabling timely interventions for both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular risks.

High-risk HFpEF patients identified by our model may benefit from comprehensive care strategies that extend beyond HF management. Early screening and intervention for chronic kidney disease, pulmonary disease, malignancies and metabolic disorders may improve long-term outcomes. Nutritional optimisation and frailty assessment should be incorporated into routine follow-up to address the vulnerability of this population. A multidisciplinary approach, involving HF specialists, geriatricians, nutritionists and pulmonologists, could further enhance patient management, particularly for elderly and frail individuals. In advanced cases, integrating palliative care may help improve symptom control and quality of life.

Targeted risk factor modification is another critical implication of our model. Since nine of the 10 identified risk factors—excluding age—are modifiable to varying degrees, tailored interventions may help mitigate their detrimental effects. Monitoring NT-proBNP and CRP could guide anti-inflammatory strategies, while correcting hypoalbuminaemia and metabolic disturbances, such as hyponatraemia and anaemia, may contribute to better clinical outcomes. Additionally, the identification of PCI as a protective factor underscores the importance of timely revascularisation in ischaemic HFpEF patients. Addressing systemic inflammation, malnutrition and sarcopenia, alongside personalised lifestyle modifications such as exercise rehabilitation and cognitive support, could further enhance long-term prognosis.

To improve clinical applicability, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using rounded cut-off values (≤200, 201–230, ≥231) and found that the model’s discriminative ability remained largely unchanged (AUC difference <0.002). This suggests that simplified thresholds can be adopted without significant loss of accuracy, improving accessibility for routine clinical use.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis of real-world data, selection bias may be present. Second, and most importantly, the absence of external validation limits the generalisability of our findings. We applied a temporal validation strategy and observed differences in baseline characteristics between training and validation cohorts, reflecting temporal changes in patient demographics, clinical practice and treatment strategies. While this enhances clinical relevance, temporal validation remains a form of internal validation. It assesses temporal stability within the same data source but cannot substitute for external validation, which evaluates generalisability across independent populations and healthcare settings. Therefore, although our model demonstrated stable predictive performance despite these differences, its applicability to other HFpEF populations (eg, in different regions, healthcare systems or ethnic groups) cannot be guaranteed. Third, data limitations precluded restratification of the cohort to balance baseline characteristics, although the model’s consistent performance suggests some internal robustness. Fourth, certain potential risk factors, such as lifestyle and socioeconomic variables, were unavailable in our database and may have affected predictive accuracy. Finally, the model’s performance for cardiovascular-specific mortality was not assessed due to challenges in cause-of-death adjudication within this cohort. Future research should prioritise external validation in large, independent datasets to rigorously assess generalisability, calibration and discrimination. Further studies should aim to incorporate additional risk factors, evaluate model performance for cause-specific mortality using improved adjudication methods and investigate the impact of emerging therapies—such as sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and the non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone—on predicted risks.

Conclusions

This study developed and internally validated a clinical risk prediction model for 3-year all-cause mortality in patients with HFpEF after hospital discharge. Using readily available clinical parameters, the model demonstrated robust predictive performance and offers a practical tool for individualised risk stratification. Although temporal validation showed stable performance across patient cohorts with differing baseline characteristics, this approach remains a form of internal validation and cannot replace external validation. The lack of independent external validation is therefore a key limitation, restricting the model’s direct applicability to broader HFpEF populations and other healthcare settings. Future research should focus on validating the model in large, independent cohorts and refining risk stratification by incorporating additional variables and accounting for the impact of emerging therapies.
