CT radiomic stratification signature to optimize clinical decisions for ovarian cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy and the underlying biological basis: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Shimin Zhang, Xinyue Li, Siyang Zhang, Meiyao Li, Jiarui Wang, Jiage Zhang, Yutong Liu, Zhuonan Liu, Guan Wang, Yi Zhang, Zhuo Yang, Jiangdian Song, Xin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease, with substantial variation in patients’ responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) and prognosis. Currently, specific stratification biomarkers for patient selection are lacking. Stratifying patients based on noninvasive imaging data prior to treatment could significantly improve treatment decisions.MethodsConsensus clustering of pretreatment radiomic features from computed tomography images identified imaging subtypes in NACT patients. Five classifiers were developed for patient stratification in a subsequent cohort, and the biological behavior behind the radiomics-based clusters was explored.ResultsConsensus clustering identified four patient clusters, with consistent survival trends across both clustering and stable classifiers. Clusters 3 and 4 showed higher complete gross resection rates, longer overall survival, and more BRCA1 mutations, while clusters 1 and 2 had poorer prognoses and lower complete gross resection rates. Despite a higher tumor stage, cluster 4 was more sensitive to chemotherapies and targeted therapies. Cluster 2 had the lowest proportion of well-differentiated tumors, and upregulated genes were enriched in pathways related to epithelial mesenchymal transition and other classic cancer pathways. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified key modules and hub genes in clusters 1 and 2, with RNA signature scoring used for validation.ConclusionsStratification based on noninvasive imaging revealed distinct biological patterns among ovarian cancer patients, helping to identify subgroups with favorable responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and better survival outcomes, thereby facilitating more personalized treatment decisions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07229-0.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Medical imaging, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Patient stratification, Prognosis

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the second most common gynecological malignancy worldwide, surpassed only by cervical cancer [1]. The estimated five-year survival rate for ovarian cancer with distant diseases at diagnosis is approximately 30% [2]. For advanced ovarian cancer, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval cytoreductive surgery (ICS) is an effective treatment approach that can improve surgical outcomes, reduce perioperative complications, and has become one of the initial treatments for advanced ovarian cancer [3, 4]. Ovarian cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease [5], with varying responses to NACT and distinct prognoses among patients [6]. However, there is currently a lack of specific stratifying biomarkers for patient selection. The ability to identify different subgroups of patients before treatment could significantly improve the selection of treatment strategies.

A series of studies, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project, have provided comprehensive genomic and transcriptomic profiles of ovarian cancer, offering profound insights into its molecular foundations. These studies have classified ovarian cancer into distinct molecular subtypes with differing prognoses [7, 8]. However, existing research has primarily focused on broader ovarian cancer populations, rather than those specifically receiving NACT. Moreover, the integration of genomic and transcriptomic data into routine clinical practice is hindered by factors such as increased costs, longer turnaround times, and the need for specialized laboratory infrastructure. In contrast, imaging-based assessments provide a non-invasive means of capturing tumor heterogeneity [9, 10]. Radiomics, which involves the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images, shown great potential in revealing patterns associated with tumor biology, therapeutic response, and overall prognosis. Radiomic features capture the characteristics of tissues and lesions, encompassing statistical, transform-based, shape-based, and other feature categories, and after undergoing processes such as feature selection and dimensionality reduction, these features can be incorporated into model construction for potential clinical applications [11]. In conducting radiomics research, it is also essential to consider whether the dataset is sufficiently large, accounts for heterogeneity, and maintains a balanced distribution [12]. Moreover, radiomics has been increasingly integrated with genomic data, giving rise to radiogenomics, which provides deeper insights into tumor heterogeneity [13, 14]. These advances highlight the potential of radiomics to serve as a non-invasive biomarker for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic response prediction in oncology [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated the predictive potential of radiomics data from tumor images for ovarian cancer prognosis and molecular phenotype [16, 17].

Based on these insights, the present study aims to explore the clinical application of radiomics in the management of ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT. We conducted consensus clustering of pre-treatment radiomics features to identify distinct imaging subtypes and developed five classifiers to stratify patients in a subsequent cohort. Furthermore, we investigated the clinical prognostic significance of these radiomics-based clusters and their correlation with potential genomic and transcriptomic characteristics.

This work not only deepens our understanding of the intrinsic heterogeneity of ovarian cancer but also seeks to integrate non-invasive imaging data into clinical decision-making. By associating radiomics patterns with molecular features, we aim to pave the way for more precise and timely therapeutic interventions, reduce unnecessary treatment-related morbidity, and optimize resource allocation in clinical practice.

Methods

Ethics

This multi-center retrospective study was approved by the institutional review boards of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (approval number 2024PS969K), the First Hospital of China Medical University (approval number [2024] 560), and Liaoning Cancer Hospital and Institute (approval number LH20250304), in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Patient cohorts

The study included patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer between January 2012 and March 2024 at the aforementioned institutions, who had received NACT prior to ICS. Additionally, data from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) and The Cancer Genome Atlas Ovarian Cancer (TCGA-OV) cohort were also incorporated. The inclusion criteria for the three participating hospitals were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) pelvic enhanced computed tomography (CT) within one month prior to NACT. Exclusion criteria were: (1) poor quality of CT images, (2) systemic inflammation or other major diseases, (3) concurrent malignancies, and (4) missing clinical data. For the MSKCC cohort, the inclusion criteria were: (1) pre-treatment pelvic enhanced CT, and (2) ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT-ICS. For the TCGA-OV cohort, the criteria were: (1) availability of pre-treatment pelvic enhanced CT, (2) inclusion of clinical data, and (3) patients who were deemed eligible for NACT by two gynecologic oncologists, with patient selection strictly following commonly used clinical practice criteria, such as the Suidan scoring system. Any cases with uncertainty were excluded to ensure an accurate selection process. Genomic sequencing data used in this study were obtained from the MSKCC and TCGA-OV cohorts, along with sequencing results from patient electronic medical records at three centers. Transcriptomic sequencing data were sourced from the TCGA-OV cohort. The patient inclusion and exclusion process is outlined in Fig. 1.

Clinical data

Information on demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics of patients in the three retrospective cohorts was collected from patients’ electronic medical records. Staging was determined using the ovarian cancer criteria of the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death. Considering the long enrollment period, patients were further stratified into two groups (2012–2018 and 2019–2024) to assess potential temporal heterogeneity. Comparisons of chemotherapy regimens and the number of NACT cycles revealed no statistically significant differences between the two periods (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

CT image segmentation and radiomics features

CT images of the three centers were obtained from the picture archiving and communication system. The CT images from the external dataset MSKCC were obtained from publicly available data from previous studies [18], and the CT images from TCGA were sourced from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) [19]. All researchers involved in region of interest (ROI) delineation received standardized training and independently performed segmentation using ITK-SNAP software (Version 4.2.0, http://www.itksnap.org/), blinded to clinical information. Tumor ROIs were manually delineated on venous-phase contrast-enhanced CT images using the abdominal window settings (level 50, width 400). ROIs were outlined slice by slice, from the first to the last visible frame of the lesion, with particular care taken to accurately follow tumor boundaries while avoiding adjacent tissues and organs. All segmentations were then reviewed by a senior radiologist to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Radiomics features were extracted in Python using the “PyRadiomics” package. Both original images and filter-transformed images (including Laplacian of Gaussian [LoG] and wavelet filters) were utilized for feature extraction. In total, 1,218 features were obtained, comprising 18 first-order statistical features, 14 shape-based features, 68 texture features, and 1,118 filter-transformed features.

To control for variability and ensure the robustness of the extracted features, a pre-specified reliability study was performed. For inter-observer reliability, two independent researchers delineated tumor ROIs in a stratified random sample of 80 patients (sampled proportionally from the five participating centers). For intra-observer reliability, the same researcher re-delineated tumor ROIs in a separate stratified random sample of 50 patients after a two-week washout period. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated, and radiomic features with ICC values greater than 0.85 were considered reproducible and retained for subsequent analyses.

All features were standardized using Z-scores, and feature selection was performed using the t-test. Collinear features with a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.9 with other features were excluded. Finally, 17 radiomic features were selected for subsequent consensus clustering and the development of radiomics-based classifiers. The detailed list of these features is provided in Supplementary Table S3. All selected features were significantly associated with overall survival, and the Kaplan–Meier curves for each feature are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1. To account for the long enrollment period, patients were stratified into two groups (2012–2018 and 2019–2024), and chi-square tests showed no statistically significant differences in the distribution of the 17 selected features between the two periods (Supplementary Table S4).

Consensus clustering based on radiomics features

To enhance the stability and reliability of radiomic feature clustering, we employed the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus”. This comprehensive approach evaluates clustering consistency through iterative subsampling and aggregation of similarity matrices. The optimal number of clusters, k, was determined by testing a predefined range (k = 2 to 6). For each candidate k, the algorithm performed 100 iterations of subsampling, where 80% of the extracted radiomic features were randomly selected in each iteration, followed by hierarchical clustering. To objectively select the optimal k, we visualized the consensus matrix heatmaps, delta area plots, and cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots for each k value, aiding in the identification of the most appropriate cluster number.

Training the radiomics classifier

Based on the extracted radiomic features and their corresponding classification labels (consensus clustering results), this study used five algorithms—support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RF), and AdaBoost—to construct classification prediction models. The implementation was carried out using the scikit-learn machine learning library in Python.

Differentially expressed genes and pathway enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the “DESeq2” package in R, with differentially expressed genes selected based on a p value less than 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2 FC) greater than 1. Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package. The h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols gene set was downloaded from the MSigDB database for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

TME and drug sensitivity analysis

Immune infiltration analysis was performed using single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). The “oncoPredict” package was used to analyze the sensitivity of different clusters to various anticancer drugs [20]. Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) were used to predict the response of different clusters to immunotherapy [21].

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Gene co-expression networks were constructed using the R package “WGCNA” [22]. The optimal soft threshold was selected to construct the co-expression network, followed by the generation of a correlation heatmap for genes across different groups. Genes from the module of interest were extracted for GO pathway enrichment analysis. Hub genes from the module were identified using “Cytoscape” and visualized accordingly. RNA feature scoring was performed using the R package “Singscore”.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2, http://www.R-project.org/). Continuous variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Categorical variables were described using counts (percentages). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for statistical analysis of multi-group continuous data. The Chi-square test and the corrected Chi-square test were used for statistical analysis of categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier (K-M) curves were used to analyze OS, and differences in OS between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Hazard ratio (HR) for OS based on allocated clusters were assessed by Cox proportional hazards analysis. The classification performance of the five classifiers was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Consensus clustering analysis

A total of 943 ovarian cancer patients who received NACT were included in this study, with pre-treatment radiomics data used for analysis. Consensus clustering was performed on data from 686 patients across three centers, with evaluations conducted for k values ranging from 2 to 6. The optimal clustering solution was achieved with k = 4, as it resulted in the best data partitioning (Fig. 2A). The consensus CDF plot and delta area plot are provided in Fig. S2. Therefore, the four clusters identified were considered the most representative data patterns for ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT. Principal component analysis was applied to the four clusters derived from consensus clustering, revealing a clear separation between the patient groups (Fig. 2B). K-M survival analysis was conducted to compare the OS among the four identified subclusters, demonstrating significant differences (p = 0.003; Fig. 2C). Specifically, patients in cluster 3 had the longest median OS, while those in cluster 2 had the shortest. Further pairwise comparisons revealed that cluster 3 had significantly better OS compared to cluster 1 (HR = 0.61[0.45-0.82], p = 0.001; Fig. 2D) and cluster 2 (HR = 0.60[0.42-0.85], p = 0.003; Fig. 2E), while no significant OS difference was observed between clusters 3 and 4 (HR = 0.78[0.56-1.09], p = 0.150; Fig. 2F). Comparisons of OS between other clusters are provided in Fig. S3.

Five classifiers trained on consensus clustering results

Five classifiers, including SVM, LR, KNN, RF, and AdaBoost, were used to identify the optimal cluster prediction classifier. The original 686 patients were randomly split into training and test datasets at a 7:3 ratio. The predictive performance of the five classifiers in both datasets is summarized in Table S5. Among them, the SVM demonstrated the most stable performance, achieving overall prediction accuracies of 96.04% in the training dataset and 93.20% in the test dataset (Figs. 2G and 2H), with AUC values of 0.99 for both datasets (Fig. S4).

The trained SVM classifier was used for cluster prediction on the 257 patients from Center 3 and the TCGA-OV cohort (Data in the two datasets was not used for the above consensus clustering). K-M survival analysis was performed on the four clusters identified through SVM-based classification. Both consensus clustering and trained SVM methods showed consistent survival trends, with cluster 3 having the best median OS, followed by cluster 4, cluster 1, and cluster 2. The p values from the K-M survival curve analysis for both methods were less than 0.05 (0.003 vs. 0.048), indicating significant differences in survival. (Fig. 2I).

Baseline characteristics of different clusters

Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of patients stratified by cluster are summarized in Table 1. Statistically significant differences were observed among the four clusters in terms of age, tumor stage, OS, and the proportion of complete gross resection (R0 resection) achieved following ICS. Patients in cluster 3 were younger compared to those in other clusters and had the highest rate of R0 resection following ICS, reaching 65.3%. In contrast, cluster 4 had a higher proportion of advanced tumor stage cases. Cluster 2 exhibited the lowest rate of R0 resection after ICS (53.5 %) and the lowest proportion of well-differentiated tumors.

Differences between clusters in genomic data

The distribution of gene sequencing results across different clusters is shown in Fig. 3A. We then compared the differences in ovarian cancer-related genes between the clusters with better prognosis (cluster 3, cluster 4) and those with worse prognosis (cluster 1, cluster 2). The results showed that the clusters with better prognosis had a higher mutation-positive rate for homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status, Sig3, CDK12, CCNE1, and TP53 compared to the clusters with worse prognosis. Notably, the mutation-positive rate for BRCA1 showed a significant difference between the two classification groups (11.7% vs. 24.5%, p = 0.032; Fig. 3B). Stage III patients demonstrated a higher mutation-positive rate for BRCA2 compared to stage IV patients (16.0% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.043; Fig. S5A). However, among patients with complete resection of disease after ICS, no significant differences were observed in HRD status, Sig3, or mutation-positive rates for certain genes compared to those with incomplete resection (Fig. S5B).

Distribution of clusters in transcriptomic sequencing data

We further analyzed the classification results of the TCGA-OV samples in conjunction with their corresponding transcriptome sequencing data. Differential gene expression among the clusters was first evaluated using GSEA (Table S6). Genes upregulated in cluster 2 compared to cluster 1 were mainly enriched in pathways related to metabolism, MYC target genes, and DNA repair (Fig. 3C). When comparing the poor-prognosis cluster 2 with the favorable-prognosis cluster 3, upregulated genes in cluster 2 were predominantly enriched in epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), inflammatory response, metabolism, and several classical cancer-related pathways (Fig. 3D). Similarly, compared to cluster 4 (which also had relatively favorable prognosis), genes upregulated in cluster 2 were enriched in EMT and angiogenesis pathways, while downregulated genes were associated with immune-related pathways (Fig. 3E). Additionally, we performed immune cell infiltration analysis across clusters using ssGSEA (Fig. 3F–G). The results showed that cluster 4 had a lower proportion of central memory CD8+ T cells compared to the other three clusters. In contrast, cluster 1 exhibited a lower proportion of gamma delta T cells relative to clusters 3 and 4.

Drug sensitivity of different clusters on TCGA-OV dataset

We selected a panel of chemotherapy and targeted therapy drugs relevant to the treatment of ovarian cancer and analyzed the sensitivity (IC50 values) of different clusters to these drugs. Compared to cluster 1, cluster 4 showed significantly higher sensitivity to several chemotherapeutic drugs, including paclitaxel, docetaxel, gemcitabine, vinorelbine, and cyclophosphamide, as well as to targeted therapies such as olaparib and talazoparib (Fig. 4A). Similarly, when comparing cluster 2 vs. cluster 4 (Fig. 4B) and cluster 3 vs. cluster 4 (Fig. 4C), cluster 4 consistently demonstrated greater sensitivity to various chemotherapy and targeted therapies, suggesting that cluster 4 may represent a high drug-sensitivity subgroup.

Since commonly used immunotherapeutic drugs for ovarian cancer were not included in the drug sensitivity analysis, we employed the TIDE score to predict the immunotherapy response of each cluster. Interestingly, although the differences were not statistically significant, cluster 4 exhibited higher TIDE scores compared to the other clusters, suggesting a potentially poorer response to immunotherapy (Fig. S7).

WGCNA analysis on TCGA-OV dataset

Further, we performed WGCNA analysis on the transcriptomic data. After data preprocessing, we determined the soft threshold (Fig. 5A, B) and constructed a co-expression network, categorizing genes into different modules. A correlation heatmap was then generated to examine the relationship between each module and sample classification. The genes were divided into seven modules, with clusters 1 and 2 (poor prognosis) grouped together, and clusters 3 and 4 (better prognosis) grouped separately. The black module was found to be significantly associated with the poor prognosis clusters 1 and 2 (p = 0.02; Fig. 5C). GO enrichment analysis of the genes in the black module revealed enrichment in pathways closely related to chromosome structure and genome stability (Fig. 5D). The hub genes of the black module were extracted and displayed (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the RNA signature score of the prognosis-poor clusters (cluster 1 and cluster 2) was significantly higher than that of the prognosis-better clusters (cluster 3 and cluster 4) (p < 0.05; Fig. 5F).

Clinically interpretable labels of radiomic clusters

Based on integrated analyses of clinical characteristics, prognostic outcomes, and molecular features, we assigned clinically interpretable labels to the four radiomic clusters. Cluster 1 – “Poor Prognosis”: associated with worse outcomes and the lowest proportion of γδ T cells. Cluster 2 – “Aggressive–Treatment-Resistant”: defined by the poorest prognosis, lowest R0 resection rate, worst tumor differentiation, and enrichment of EMT and other classical oncogenic pathways, consistent with a highly invasive phenotype. Cluster 3 – “Resectable–Favorable Prognosis”: exhibited the best prognosis and the highest rate of complete gross resection following ICS. Cluster 4 – “Drug-Sensitive”: although enriched with more advanced-stage cases, patients demonstrated higher sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, suggesting a favorable therapeutic response profile.

Discussion

In this study, we performed consensus clustering based on radiomic data from 686 ovarian cancer patients prior to NACT, and constructed five classifiers for classifying subsequent enrolled patients. Training supervised classifiers on clustering-derived labels provides practical advantages, including prediction of subgroup membership in external cohorts, selection of the classifier with the best performance, and enabling assignment of new patients to radiomic subgroups in clinical practice. We also analyzed the differences in clinical pathology, genomics, and transcriptomics between the clustering groups generated from the non-invasive radiomics data. Currently, short-term efficacy indicators for evaluating the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in ovarian cancer include the achievement of R0 resection during ICS, while long-term outcomes are primarily assessed by OS [3, 23]. Our analysis revealed that cluster 3 exhibited the highest short-term efficacy, as indicated by the surgical R0 rate, and the longest long-term efficacy, as reflected by OS time, compared to the other clusters. In contrast, cluster 2 showed the poorest short-term and long-term efficacy metrics, highlighting that the radiomics-based classification can effectively predict the prognosis of different patients.

Ovarian cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease, and patients who do not respond to NACT may require alternative therapeutic strategies. In recent years, targeted therapies and immunotherapies have been integrated into NACT regimens to address this issue [24, 25]. By analyzing the relationship between radiomic clustering results and biological behavior, we observed significant differences in gene expression and transcriptomic profiles across the clusters. Moreover, patients in different clusters exhibited varying sensitivities to chemotherapeutic and targeted therapeutic drugs. These findings further support the notion that radiomics can reflect the biological behavior of tumors and provide a potential basis for the development of personalized treatment strategies. Notably, genes upregulated in the poor-prognosis cluster 2 were significantly enriched in pathways related to EMT and several classical cancer-related pathways. Additionally, WGCNA identified modules associated with the poor-prognosis clusters 1 and 2. Gene enrichment analysis revealed that these modules were significantly enriched in pathways closely related to chromosomal structure and genomic stability, further highlighting the biological characteristics of these clusters.

Previous studies have mostly focused on additional sequencing data, such as RNA-seq, to categorize ovarian cancer patients into molecular subtypes with different prognoses [26, 27], or used clinical pathology data [28] and blood-based test results, such as circulating-tumor DNA and CA125 levels [29, 30], to predict the prognosis of ovarian cancer patients. This study, however, focuses on ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT and uses non-invasive imaging data for clustering. Compared to merely using radiomic data for disease diagnosis and prognosis prediction [31–33], we also explore the correlation between radiomics and biological behavior. This integration aims to bridge the gap in current research by providing insights into how imaging-derived phenotypes reflect the molecular and clinical pathology characteristics of the disease. Contrast-enhanced CT was chosen in this study because it is routinely performed in patients with advanced ovarian cancer, ensuring a sufficient sample size and reproducibility for radiomics analyses. While MRI and PET offer complementary advantages—such as superior soft tissue contrast or metabolic information—they were available only in limited subsets, restricting their use in this multicenter cohort. Future prospective studies integrating MRI and PET with standardized protocols may further enhance radiomics-based stratification and provide additional biological insights.

In terms of methodology, we adopted a consensus clustering strategy to enhance the stability and reliability of the results [34]. Previous studies have primarily applied consensus clustering to data analysis of proteomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, etc. [35–38], and our study extends this robust approach to non-invasive imaging data. Consensus clustering aggregates multiple clustering outputs to form a robust final classification, thereby reducing the impact of inherent random variation and noise in the data. This method has significant advantages over deep learning methods, which typically require large amounts of labeled data, high computational resources, and are prone to overfitting and interference from noisy data [39, 40]. By utilizing consensus clustering, our study mitigates these limitations and ensures that the identified imaging subtypes are both reproducible and clinically meaningful.

We evaluated the association between radiomic clusters and immunotherapy response using TIDE scores, but no statistically significant differences were observed. Several factors may explain this, including the focus of our study on NACT patients, where immunotherapy is rarely used, making the link between radiomic phenotypes and immunotherapy sensitivity more indirect. Additionally, the complex and heterogeneous immune microenvironment of ovarian cancer may not be fully captured by TIDE scores or radiomic features alone. Importantly, the lack of statistical significance does not rule out an association but rather highlights the need for further validation in ovarian cancer-specific immunotherapy cohorts. With the increasing use of immune checkpoint inhibitors, future studies integrating radiomics with immune-related biomarkers (e.g., tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, PD-L1 expression) will be essential to strengthen the link between imaging phenotypes and immunotherapy response.

This study has several limitations. First, we conducted a retrospective analysis using multi-center data, and lacked a prospective cohort for validation. Second, due to the lack of transcriptomic data for ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT in TCGA, we enlisted experienced gynecological oncologists to assess the limited clinical data and CT images available for patients prior to treatment. Based on their evaluations, we identified the subset of patients who were eligible for NACT. As a result, the sample size of transcriptomic data included in the study was limited. We hope that these findings can be further validated in future prospective cohorts that simultaneously include pre-NACT RNA-seq data and contrast-enhanced CT images. Additionally, the DNA sequencing data used in this study were derived from the MSKCC and TCGA-OV cohorts. Since the original MSKCC data are not publicly available, we only analyzed results related to markers such as HRD status. We hope to validate our findings in larger, multi-center datasets in the future and integrate other omics data (e.g., proteomics, metabolomics) to further explore the underlying tumor biology behind radiomics.

Conclusions

This study focused on ovarian cancer patients undergoing NACT and used non-invasive radiomic data collected before treatment to perform consensus clustering for patient stratification. This approach identified subgroups of patients who are more likely to respond well to NACT and have favorable survival outcomes, thereby aiding treatment decision-making. Furthermore, we found that the clustering patterns derived from radiomics exhibited significant differences in clinicopathological data, as well as in genomic and transcriptomic profiles, revealing a correlation between radiomics and tumor biological behavior. These findings contribute to advancing precision medicine for ovarian cancer patients.
