Risk factors for anthrax in livestock: findings from one health outbreak investigations in Bangladesh
Authors: Muhammad Belal Hossain, Najmul Haider, Md Saiful Islam, Sukanta Chowdhury, Ahmad Raihan Sharif, M Salim Uzzaman, Mohammad Mostafizur Rahman, Mahbubur Rahman, Mahmudur Rahman, Farhana Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnthrax remains endemic in Bangladesh, causing recurring outbreaks in livestock and humans despite vaccination programs. Although environmental persistence of Bacillus anthracis is recognized, evidence on modifiable animal- and farm-level practices that contribute to livestock anthrax is limited. This study aimed to identify animal-level and farm-level risk factors associated with anthrax in livestock in endemic areas of Bangladesh, addressing gaps in understanding how husbandry practices and farm management contribute to disease transmission.MethodsGeographically matched animal case–control studies of ruminants were conducted in Bangladeshi subdistricts reporting human cutaneous anthrax cases from October 2012 to December 2016. A total of 103 cattle, goats, and buffalo were recruited from 81 case farms, while 1,098 ruminants were recruited from 553 control farms. Data on animal characteristics, feeding and grazing practices, vaccination status, and farm management behaviors were collected using structured questionnaires and analyzed using conditional logistic regression.ResultsAt the animal level, communal grazing (adjusted matched odds ratio [mOR]: 2.67, 95% CI: 1.48–4.82), feeding freshly harvested hybrid grass (cultivated fodder) within three days of illness onset (adjusted mOR: 6.07, 95% CI: 1.71–21.42), and failure to vaccinate livestock (adjusted mOR: 0.06, 95% CI: 0.02–0.14) were strongly associated with infection. At the farm level, practices that disturbed or imported potentially contaminated soil, including scraping topsoil from animal sheds (adjusted mOR: 10.8, 95% CI: 3.02–38.63), routine collection of soil from cultivable land for shed maintenance (adjusted mOR: 6.49, 95% CI: 1.73–24.33), and on-farm slaughtering of animals (adjusted mOR: 3.09, 95% CI: 1.30–7.16), significantly increased anthrax risk. These findings highlight behavioral and management practices that facilitate exposure to B. anthracis spores.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that environmental disturbances, animal husbandry and farm management practices, alongside low vaccine coverage, intersect to sustain anthrax transmission in livestock in Bangladesh. Strengthening immunity through large-scale vaccination programs, coupled with improved animal grazing, soil husbandry, farm management, and slaughter practices, could significantly reduce animal anthrax and prevent human spillover in endemic settings such as Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42522-026-00210-x.Keywords: Anthrax, Animal, Risk factors, Bangladesh

Introduction

Anthrax, a globally distributed zoonotic disease caused by the spore-forming, Gram-positive bacterium Bacillus anthracis, poses a significant threat to livestock, wildlife, and humans [1–3]. This disease is particularly prevalent in countries with agricultural economies where close human-animal interactions and inadequate veterinary infrastructure increase the risk of outbreaks [4]. Anthrax remains endemic in many regions, including Bangladesh, where outbreaks occur annually both in animals and humans [5, 6]. The first documented anthrax outbreak in cattle and humans in Bangladesh occurred in 1986 [7]. However, following the initial report of human anthrax in the 1980s, no anthrax cases were reported until June 2009 [8, 9], most likely due to the absence of active surveillance and monitoring programs. Because of the agrarian economy, high human and animal population densities, and extensive livestock farming, anthrax poses a substantial threat to both animal and human health in Bangladesh [6, 10, 11]. Despite control efforts through livestock vaccination campaigns and public health interventions, anthrax outbreaks continue to emerge, leading to economic losses and public health emergencies [6, 8, 12]. Since 2009, Bangladesh has reported recurrent anthrax outbreaks affecting livestock and humans annually, with repeated clusters of human cutaneous anthrax and substantial numbers of animal cases documented in endemic districts [6]. Approximately 4000 suspected human cutaneous anthrax cases have been reported between 2009 and 2020 [5, 8]. These outbreaks impose significant economic burdens through livestock mortality, reduced agricultural productivity, healthcare costs, and disruption of local and regional trade [13, 14].

Anthrax control in Bangladesh has evolved. to 2009, detection relied on passive surveillance with intermittent vaccination and logistic constraints, while major outbreaks prompted a coordinated One Health surveillance program between 2012 and 2016. Following this period, active surveillance was not sustained, although outbreak response and vaccination have continued at variable scales.

Environmental factors play a critical role in the transmission of anthrax, with the ability of the bacteria to persist in soil [15, 16]. Bacillus anthracis spores can survive for decades under favorable conditions, and regions with alkaline soils and high organic content have been shown to support spore persistence, thereby potentially increasing the risk of infection among grazing livestock [17–19]. Climate variables, including temperature, humidity, and rainfall, also influence spore viability with seasonal changes affecting anthrax dynamics [16]. In Bangladesh, the monsoon season typically spans from June through September and is characterized by heavy rainfall, periodic flooding, and widespread soil disturbance. For instance, periods of heavy rainfall followed by drought can expose buried spores, increasing the likelihood that livestock will encounter them in grazing fields [20]. Moreover, animal husbandry practices further impact anthrax transmission within livestock populations [21]. Overcrowding, poor sanitation, and low vaccination rates facilitate the risk of anthrax occurrence among domestic animals by increasing shared exposure to contaminated environments, rather than through direct animal-to-animal transmission. In rural areas, traditional farming methods, including communal grazing and shared feed and water sources, can increase contact with soil-and vegetation-borne spores, particularly where veterinary services and biosecurity measures are insufficient [6]. Together these factors coupled with environmental dynamics, underscore the complex interplay between biological, ecological, and human factors in the persistence and spread of anthrax [5, 13].

Previous studies undertaken in the country of Georgia and in the United States of America (North Dakota) indicate that ingestion of spores while grazing on contaminated pastures and consumption of contaminated water or harvested grass are the primary routes of infection for ruminants [21, 22]. Earlier studies from Bangladesh identified factors that likely increased the risk of anthrax outbreaks in animals, such as feeding uprooted and unwashed grass to cattle, providing water hyacinth, history of heavy rainfall, disposing of dead carcass nearby water bodies, and environmental contamination within farms [9, 10, 12]. The presence of sick animals on farms likely reflects shared exposure to contaminated environments or feeds rather than direct animal-to-animal transmission, as anthrax infection in livestock primarily occurs through ingestion of soil-borne spores. Additionally, a couple of studies suggested a possible link between anthrax outbreaks and contaminated livestock feed [9, 23]. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of feed source and seasonal exposure. In Bangladesh, concentrates and stored feed are used year-round, whereas anthrax outbreaks peak during the monsoon season, suggesting that environmentally contaminated fresh fodder and soil-associated vegetation, rather than processed feed alone, may play a more prominent role in anthrax transmission [5, 6]. Despite several epidemiologic studies conducted during anthrax outbreaks in Bangladesh, a critical gap remains in systematically quantifying livestock risk factors using integrated One Health field investigations. To date, no studies have formally evaluated farm-level risk factors for anthrax outbreaks in livestock, and existing literature of animal-level risk factors has been limited by small sample sizes [10, 12, 13]. Most prior work has focused on human exposure, outbreak characterization, or risk practices rather than analytic assessment of livestock management and environmental determinants. In Bangladesh, humans primarily contract cutaneous anthrax through direct contact with infected animals, carcasses, hides, meat, or contaminated animal by-products, particularly when cuts or abrasions are present on the skin [5]. Outbreak investigations and case control studies are valuable tools for understanding the dynamics of anthrax transmission in domestic livestock, as they enable researchers to identify specific risk factors—such as environmental conditions, animal husbandry practices, and socioeconomic determinants that heighten the likelihood of outbreaks [5, 10, 12, 24–27]. The present study addresses this gap by embedding a matched case–control design within real-time outbreak investigations to identify animal- and farm-level risk factors directly relevant to anthrax transmission in livestock. Based on prior investigations in Bangladesh and other anthrax-endemic regions, we hypothesized that livestock exposure to contaminated environments would increase the risk of anthrax infection. Specifically, we expected that communal grazing, exposure to soil-associated fodder sources, and farm practices involving soil disturbance or carcass handling would be associated with increased risk of anthrax among ruminants. Conversely, we hypothesized that vaccination against anthrax would be protective.

Addressing anthrax outbreaks in Bangladesh necessitates a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that integrates veterinary science, public health, and socio-economic interventions. Identifying specific risk factors at both individual animal and farm levels is crucial for developing context-appropriate interventions to prevent animal anthrax and to better inform potential pathways of human exposure. This study aims to identify individual animal and farm-level risk factors for anthrax outbreaks in domestic livestock, thereby informing the understanding of factors relevant to effective prevention and control strategies. By combining outbreak investigations and case-control studies, this research seeks to provide a deeper understanding of the risk factors driving anthrax outbreaks in domestic livestock in Bangladesh. The findings may contribute to improved understanding of prevention and control measures and support efforts to reduce the burden of anthrax in livestock and associated public health risk.

Methodology

Identification of animal anthrax cases linked to cutaneous anthrax outbreaks in humans

The Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR), the national public health and research institute of the Government of Bangladesh with formal authority for disease surveillance and outbreak response, conducted a nationwide One Health anthrax surveillance between 2012 and 2016. Between October 2012 and December 2016, IEDCR identified cases of human cutaneous anthrax through multiple healthcare channels, which subsequently triggered investigations to identify epidemiologically linked anthrax cases in domestic livestock. These channels included reports made directly to district and sub-district healthcare managers, hotline reports, detections through mass media surveillance, defined as the systematic monitoring of national and local newspaper and outlets for reports of unusual disease events, and web-based integrated disease surveillance conducted by IEDCR. Following notification of human anthrax cases, a multi-disciplinary One Health team consisting of trained epidemiologists, physicians, veterinarians, social scientists, anthropologists, trained phlebotomists, and field data collectors from the IEDCR, and the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b) visited each outbreak community to conduct epidemiological, laboratory, anthropological, environmental, and animal investigations. To identify the implicated livestock exposure, given that domestic ruminants were presumed to be the source of infection for human anthrax cases, trained field data collectors systematically searched outbreak communities reporting at least one confirmed human anthraxcase to identify suspected animal anthrax cases and livestock exposures. Animal investigations included clinical assessment of livestock, documentation of exposure and husbandry practices, specimen collection where feasible, and epidemiologic linkage of suspected animal cases to confirmed human cutaneous anthrax cases.

Definition of animal anthrax cases and case farms

An animal was defined as an anthrax case if any dead or slaughtered ruminant had a clear epidemiological link to a human case and/or demonstrated direct microbiological evidence of Bacillus anthracis via M’Fadyean reaction, and/or exhibited non-hemolytic medusa head colony morphology in samples cultured on blood agar. We considered a case farm as an anthrax-affected farm if we identified at least one ruminant in the herd meeting the case definition of animal anthrax.

Definition of control animals and farms

We defined an animal as a control that had no recognized illness in the three days preceding the onset of the corresponding animal case outbreak, and that remained clinically healthy at the time of investigation and enrollment. Control animals were reared on one of the 7–10 farms nearest to the case farm and were selected during the same investigation period, typically within the same field visit or within a few days of outbreak detection. Animals that developed clinical signs or symptoms compatible with anthrax after outbreak identification were not enrolled as control animals. We considered a farm as a control when that farm had no recognized sick or dead animals in the three days to the onset of anthrax cases or outbreaks in animals within the corresponding community. In outbreaks involving more than one case farm within the same community, control farms were selected based on geographic proximity to each case farm to ensure comparable environmental, ecological, and husbandry conditions.

Because anthrax in livestock often presents as a peracute disease with sudden death and minimal or no preceding clinical signs, control animals were also initially permitted to be selected from the same farm as a case animal if they were clinically healthy at the time of investigation and had no recognized illness in the three days preceding enrollment. These animals were actively followed during the investigation period, and any animal that subsequently developed signs compatible with anthrax was excluded from the control group. Importantly, farms were classified as case farms if at least one animal met the case definition, regardless of the presence of control animals on the same farm. However, animals from farms where a case animal had been identified were later excluded from the final animal-level analysis to avoid potential exposure misclassification.

The higher control-to-case ratio was intentionally adopted to enhance statistical efficiency and improve precision of exposure estimates, given the limited number of anthrax cases available during outbreak investigations. Although statistical gains diminish beyond a 4:1 control-case ratio, including larger control pools (such as 10:1) still contribute to improved precision of effect estimates even though gains in power become minimal [28, 29]. Outbreak-driven field constraints and the clustered nature of livestock farming therefore necessitated enrolling additional controls to adequately capture exposure variability at both animal and farm levels. Importantly, because logistic regression estimates odds ratios based on relative exposure distributions rather than absolute sample sizes, the inclusion of additional controls does not violate model assumptions but primarily affects the precision of estimates [30]. Conditional logistic regression inherently accounted for matching, and sensitivity analyses using reduced control subsets (approximately 1:4 case–control ratio) were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.

Enrollment of case animals and case farms

We enrolled animals from the same community where human anthrax cases were identified. Upon arriving at the outbreak site, we identified the owners of animals associated with human cutaneous anthrax cases. Subsequently, we compiled a list of farmers who owned domestic ruminants (cattle, goats, and buffalo) that had been slaughtered or had died within 14 days of the onset of symptoms of human cases. Upon obtaining informed written consent from the primary animal owner or head of household, we conducted interviews and collected specimens from available deceased or slaughtered animals.

Enrollment of control animals and control farms

Healthy ruminants with no documented illness in the three days preceding the outbreak and that were clinically healthy at the time of outbreak investigation and enrollment were included in the study as controls. These animals were selected from the 7–10 farms nearest to the case farm during the corresponding outbreak investigations. Upon written consent, interviews were conducted with animal owners by administering a questionnaire similar to that used for case animals. For each case animal, we enrolled 5–10 control animals, and for each case farm, we selected 4–8 control farms. Animals that developed signs of illness following outbreak identification were not eligible for inclusion as controls.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was administered to both the owners of case and control animals, focusing on individual animal and farm-level management practices. This questionnaire encompassed various aspects, including individual animal-level feeding system practices (such as whether ruminants were exclusively confined in stables with no access to grazing in the field - intensive, or allowed occasional grazing in the field - semi-intensive), communal grazing practices, duration of grazing periods, and anthrax vaccine received in the past year. Farm-level factors, such as herd size, history of animal mortality on the farm in the previous two years, and the practice of collecting soil from agricultural land to add layers in animal sheds, were also included in the survey. Demographic information, including species, age, sex, breed, grazing practices, feeding habits as well as clinical history such as illness onset dates, clinical signs, and clinical outcomes of affected animals were also recorded.

Specimen collection from case animals and laboratory diagnosis

Specimens were obtained from deceased animals, comprising blood or swabs from the bone marrow of the mandible, along with nasal turbinate whenever accessible. Subsequently, all samples were transferred to nutrient broth media and promptly transported to the laboratories of IEDCR and Bangladesh Livestock Research Institute (BLRI) for examination and further testing. Gram stain and M’Fadyean reaction analyses were conducted on all samples collected from case animals. Additionally, bacterial culture was performed on samples collected in nutrient broth to ascertain colony morphology.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the case and control animals included in the study. Univariable conditional logistic regression was employed to assess the associations between individual animal-level and farm-level risk factors and anthrax infection. Matched odds ratios (mOR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each significant variable to estimate the likelihood of anthrax associated with respective risk factors, with matching implemented at the community level. As expected, effect estimates are based on discordant case–control sets within matched strata, while concordant sets do not contribute to the conditional likelihood. To avoid premature exclusion of potentially important covariates, variables with a p-value < 0.25 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable analysis [31]. Multivariable conditional logistic models were then constructed using a stepwise backward elimination approach. Model selection was guided by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which are widely used information criteria for comparing competing regression models while balancing model fit and model parsimony, rather than by statistical significance alone [32, 33]. Variables are retained in the final model when their inclusion improved overall model fit, as indicated by lower AIC and BIC values. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was subsequently used for interpretation of predictors retained in the final model, rather than as the sole criterion for variable selection. Collinearity and multicollinearity among predictors were evaluated using correlation matrices (supplementary materials) and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) [34], with no significant issues identified. Matched odds ratio (mORs) and their respective 95% CIs were reported for each risk factor identified. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

Demographic and clinical information

We enrolled 103 ruminants as anthrax cases from 81 farms and 1,212 control ruminants from 553 farms. After excluding 114 control animals originating from farms where anthrax cases had occurred, the final animal-level dataset included 103 case animals and 1,098 control animals from unaffected farms. Among the 103 case animals, 18 (17.5%) were confirmed through laboratory testing while 85 (82.5%) were classified based on epidemiological linkage to confirmed human anthrax cases. Among these 103 cases, 57 (55%) were cattle, 45 (44%) were goats, and 1 (1%) was a buffalo. Among the enrolled cases, 76 (74%) were slaughtered after the onset of illness, while 27 (26%) died. The mean age of cattle was 35 months (range: 12–90 months), and for goats was 22 months (range: 5-108 months). Case animals, on average, either died within 6.5 h of symptom onset (range: 12 –72 h) or were slaughtered within 7.03 h of the onset of clinical illness (range: 30 –83 h) (Table 1). Animal anthrax cases were most frequently identified between April and August, with the highest number of cases detected in July (Fig. 1).

Animal-level risk factors

Compared to control animals, anthrax cases were more frequently reared in a semi-intensive farming system (71% vs. 54%), had grazed in communal grazing lands before the onset of illness (40% vs. 22%), and had grazed for longer periods before the onset of illness (2.9 vs. 1.3 h). Feeding hybrid grass (defined as cultivated, high-yield fodder grass primarily Napier variety which is distinguished from naturally occurring pasture or wild grasses) within three days before symptom onset was more common among cases (14% vs. 2.5%), and they received anthrax vaccination in the past year (9% vs. 53%) (Table 2). Upon multivariable analysis, factors independently associated with anthrax infections in ruminants at the individual level included history of grazing in communal lands (Adjusted matched Odds Ratio [Adjusted mOR]: 2.67, CI: 1.48–4.82), feeding hybrid grass within past 3 days of onset of symptoms (Adjusted mOR: 6.07, CI: 1.71–21.42), and absence of anthrax vaccination within the past years (Adjusted mOR: 0.08, CI: 0.02–0.14) (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses using randomly subsampled controls (1:4 case–control ratio) produced effect estimates comparable to those observed in the primary analysis, with no meaningful changes in the direction or statistical significance of identified risk factors (Supplementary Tables S1-S2).

Farm-level risk factors

Among the animals enrolled from 81 case farms and 553 control farms, the majority (93% case farms and 97% control farms) were reared in a backyard system, with herd sizes ranging from 1 to 10 cattle per farm (96%, 608/634), while few were in small-scale commercial operations (3.2%, 20/634), and only three were within larger commercial operations 3/634, 0.5%) (Table 1). As highlighted in Table 4, compared to control farms, owners of case-farms more frequently scraped earth off or removed surface soil from the floor of the animal shed (17% vs. 4%), collected soil from nearby agricultural lands to add layers within cattle sheds (16% vs. 5%), had a history of animal slaughter within the farm premises (22% vs. 14%), buried carcasses in nearby soil pits (25% vs. 11%), and conducted earthwork within the shed (15% vs. 11%). Compared to control farms, the case farms had higher mean animal death in the past two years (0.7 vs. 0.3), more skinning of dead animals before disposal in the past two years (10% vs. 3%), and less presence of flies (70% vs. 75%) (Table 4). Upon multivariable analysis (Table 5), factors independently associated with anthrax illness in ruminants at farm level included scraping topsoil in sheds (Adjusted mOR: 10.8, CI: 3.02–38.63), routine collection of soil from cultivable land for shed maintenance or earthwork (Adjusted mOR: 6.49, CI: 1.73–24.33), and slaughtering animals within the shed or household premises (Adjusted mOR: 3.09, CI: 1.30–7.16).

Discussion

The results of this study provide critical insights into animal-level and farm-level risk factors associated with anthrax outbreaks in Bangladesh, where the disease continues to cause significant morbidity and mortality among livestock [5, 13]. A total of 103 ruminant anthrax cases were identified from 81 farms, including 57 cattle (55%), 45 goats (44%), and 1 buffalo (1%), mirroring patterns observed in prior studies from Bangladesh and other endemic regions where animals are primarily affected due to their grazing habits and exposure to contaminated environments [35]. The rapid progression of anthrax in this study with animals either dying within hours of symptom onset or being slaughtered aligns with similar observations from Zambia, Tanzania, and United States, where livestock succumb quickly to the disease once symptoms manifest [36–38]. Furthermore, the seasonality of outbreaks, predominantly occurring between April and August, supports previous findings that anthrax outbreaks are often associated with environmental factors such as the monsoon season, flood, and subsequent soil disturbances, which facilitate the resurfacing of B. anthracis spores [16, 39, 40].

Individual animals faced a heightened risk of anthrax if they grazed for extended periods tethered by a pole in a communal grazing land. This finding is consistent with studies from endemic areas such as northern Tanzania and West Texas, where animals grazing in communal areas face increased exposure to contaminated soil and water [35, 38, 41]. Feeding hybrid grass within three days of symptom onset was found to significantly increase the risk of anthrax. This practice likely exposes livestock to contaminated feed, especially if the grass is sourced from pastures or areas which are more likely to harbor anthrax spores. Previous research has indicated that grass and other vegetation can serve as vehicles for the transmission of anthrax, particularly when grazed by animals in close proximity to infected areas [15, 42]. Importantly, the risk is not attributed to the vegetation itself, but to soil-borne anthrax spores adhering to grasses, aquatic plants, or root materials when fodder is harvested with attached sole form contaminated environments. Ingestion of contaminated grass or fodder can contribute directly to infection, highlighting the importance of proper grazing practices and harvesting livestock feed during high-risk periods to prevent exposure.

In our study, vaccination of animals against anthrax in the past year was the most crucial protective measure against anthrax. Specifically, vaccinating animals with the Sterne vaccine, a live attenuated Bacillus anthracis spore vaccine, in the past year was found to be a protective factor. However, despite statistical evidence of vaccine effectiveness, vaccine coverage within the outbreak areas remained low (below 10%) which is consistent with previous reports [6, 11]. Insufficient supply of anthrax vaccine, lack of staffing to implement vaccination programs on national and subnational levels, and ineffective vaccine strategies that fail to target the high-risk areas, rendered livestock susceptible to anthrax infection [43]. In addition, limited farmer awareness regarding vaccination benefits, appropriate timing, and scientific farm management practices likely contributes to suboptimal vaccine update and sustained risk of anthrax transmission. Nonetheless, a post outbreak ring-vaccination strategy is often implemented at high-risk areas in response to the outbreaks. Department of Livestock Services (DLS) under the Ministry of Fisheries and Livestock (MoFL) of Bangladesh, utilizes live attenuated Sterne vaccine produced locally in the country [6]. The majority of livestock vaccines in use all over the world today for immunization against anthrax are derived from the live spore vaccine formulated by Sterne in 1937, with the use of descendant strains 34F2 [23]. This vaccine be an effective control strategy in many countries, with considerable reduction, and sometimes complete elimination of the disease in animals [23]. However, the live-attenuated vaccine can only protect animals effectively for six to nine months leaving vaccinated cattle susceptible again in the subsequent year [44]. Although anthrax vaccination efforts exists in Bangladesh, it is often outbreak driven and inconsistently implemented, limited travel allowances hinder livestock officers from reaching high-risk, low-income, and remote rural communities [6]. In addition, farmers in Bangladesh often avoid vaccinating animals when the animals are pregnant, or when animals recently gave birth. Qualitative studies further suggest that financial constraints prevent many farmers affording vaccination [21]. These combined factors likely led to the low rates of vaccination against anthrax thereby increasing susceptibility of animals to anthrax. Addressing low vaccination coverage requires structural interventions, including adequate travel allowances for livestock vaccinators or livestock officials, mobile vaccination campaigns targeting high-risk areas, and pre-monsoon scheduling. Evidence from Bangladesh suggests that inflated delivery costs and limited field incentives significantly reduce vaccine uptake, underscoring the need for policy-level investment to sustain coverage in endemic zones. Since the 2012–2016 study period, Bangladesh has made incremental improvements in anthrax surveillance, outbreak investigation coordination, and risk communication. However, vaccination coverage remains variable, and challenges related to vaccine access, logistical constraints, and safe carcass disposal persist in many endemic rural areas [13, 14]. Consequently, the animal- and farm-level risk factors identified in this study remain directly relevant to contemporary anthrax prevention and control efforts.

We identified three risk factors associated with exposure to soil: (1) animals allowed to graze on communal grazing lands, (2) scrap off earth or removal of topsoil layer of the stable floor, and (3) collections of soil from nearby agricultural lands to add a layer of soil within animal sheds. In the rural areas of Bangladesh, only a limited number of animals are allowed to graze on a rotating schedule during specific times of the day on small plots of land. The first set of animals usually consumes the top layers of the grass while the next set of animals grazing on the same lands consumes the root stock of the grass containing soil. This soil might have been contaminated with anthrax spores. In endemic regions, when an anthrax infected animal is slaughtered or dies naturally, the vegetative cells of anthrax are introduced to soil nutrients within these microenvironments and form spores [16]. These spores can persist in the soil for several decades as they are resistant to a number of chemical agents [16]. The anthrax spores are usually exposed after rainfall, and we found most of the anthrax cases occurred during the monsoon season in Bangladesh, which provides a favorable environment for anthrax spore exposure [10].

The practice of scraping off the top layer of stable soil and collecting soil from agricultural lands for adding layers within cattle sheds significantly increased the risk of anthrax outbreaks. In Bangladesh, it is a cultural practice to add layers of soil in cattle sheds to avoid dusting and to enhance the appearance of the sheds. Farmers repeat this process at least once a month throughout the year. To perform the work, they collect soil from nearby agricultural lands. Since spores released from dead carcasses can resist prolonged exposures to chemical disinfectants including agricultural lime and remain in the soil for several decades. When farmers bring in the soil contaminated with B. anthracis spores, cattle can lick the soil if the soil is stored in the cattle shed or after adding a fresh layer of soil in sheds. Farmers usually collect soil once or twice a year and pile it up in the cattle shed. These soils are usually collected after harvesting of agricultural crops and there are two major crop seasons in Bangladesh: one of which ends before the monsoon season, the other ends during winter. Since significant outbreaks were not observed during winter season, we assumed that most farmers collected soil before the monsoon to be piled in cattle sheds. However, further investigation is required to fully understand this particular risk factor.

The history of animal slaughter within the animal shed or household premises was identified as a significant risk factor for anthrax infection. This practice may facilitate the spread of B. anthracis spores, particularly if carcasses are not properly disposed of or if contaminated materials are left in the environment. In regions with inadequate veterinary services, the slaughtering of moribund animals and handling of carcasses within livestock shed or household premises can increase the risk of environmental contamination [45]. The practice of slaughtering a moribund domestic ruminant is common in Bangladesh to avoid financial losses, where butcher-waste products are often discarded in nearby rivers, ditches, bamboo bushes or agricultural land [46]. Therefore, the spores shed during slaughter or post-slaughter practices can persist in the soil, building materials, and bedding of the shed, which may subsequently infect rearing livestock or animals in close proximity. Moreover, the absence of effective bio-security and sanitation measures during slaughter or handling butcher by-products are absent [46], which can lead to a cycle of contamination in the environment, making it difficult to control the spread of the disease. This finding underscores the importance of implementing rationale and safe slaughtering practices, and improving carcass disposal methods, particularly in such rural and resource-limited settings. Farms with a history of animal slaughter on the premises were found to face an increased risk of anthrax, reiterating findings from other studies where improper disposal of infected carcasses has been identified as a key source of environmental contamination [8, 46, 47].

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the delays in reporting of human anthrax cases and subsequent delays in investigation of animal anthrax outbreaks. The delays in animal investigation often resulted in a lack of available animal samples for laboratory testing. Consequently, most of our animal cases were defined based on clinical and/or epidemiological links to human anthrax cases, which may have introduced some misclassification bias. In addition, we did not perform serological testing to confirm disease-free status of control animals; therefore, animals in the incubation period may not have been identified. Because outbreak investigations were conducted at the community level, some control animals were selected from the same farms or from geographically closest farms. Given the peracute nature of anthrax in livestock, where animals often die suddenly with minimal prodromal signs, controls were carefully followed at enrollment to minimize misclassification. Nevertheless, shared environmental exposures and residual spatial dependence cannot be fully excluded. Although a matched study design and conditional logistic regression were used to reduce bias associated with within-community correlation, complete independence of observations cannot be assumed in outbreak-response field investigations in resource-constraint settings. Moreover, respondents may not accurately recall all past events including animal exposure, behaviors, and husbandry practices since we administered the interviews after 7–21 days of animal death or slaughter, which may have introduced recall bias. However, all data were collected within one month of the incident. Socio-demographic characteristics of livestock owners were not systematically collected due to the rapid response nature of outbreak investigations. As owner behavior strongly influences livestock exposure and disease transmission, future studies should integrate socio-economic variables to better inform targeted prevention strategies. Also, we have not conducted soil investigation as part of the study. Future investigations should apply standardized spore detection protocols to better characterize the environmental contamination. Although formal key informant interviews were not conducted in our study, questionnaire development was informed by prior outbreak investigation experience and routine consultations with field epidemiologists and veterinary officers involved in anthrax surveillance. Finally, our animal samples were not confirmed by PCR results due to the lack of suitable laboratory facilities; therefore, confirmatory PCR technique could be undertaken to confirm bacterial identification in the future. Nonetheless, our study provides significant insights into anthrax infection in livestock, offering valuable contributions to the development of effective control and prevention strategies.

Conclusions

These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to control anthrax outbreaks in Bangladesh. Animal grazing on communal grazing land for extended period is a key source of anthrax transmission to ruminants. The practice of using soil from agricultural lands in cattle sheds increases the risk of anthrax infection and potential outbreaks among livestock. Vaccination is a critical protective measure, recognized globally as the most effective method for preventing anthrax in animals and reducing subsequent spillover to humans. However, vaccination coverage has historically been low in Bangladesh. We recommend increasing vaccination efforts, particularly through affordable nationwide campaigns with a focus on pre-monsoon periods. Moreover, the slaughtering of potentially infected or moribund animals on farm or household premises should be strictly avoided, as anthrax spores can persist in contaminated soils, posing a prolonged risk to non-immune animals. Raising farmer awareness about the risks of using contaminated soil in cattle sheds is essential. While communal grazing of livestock and reducing grazing duration may not be practicable in Bangladesh, enhancing vaccine coverage, especially in areas with recurrent anthrax outbreaks, and implementing proper carcass disposal remain crucial strategies for anthrax prevention. Farmers should be educated on safe fodder harvesting practices, including avoiding uprooted grasses and rootstock collection in areas where carcasses may have been disposed. Further research is also needed to clarify species-specific roles of cattle, goats, and buffalo in anthrax transmission during active outbreaks.
