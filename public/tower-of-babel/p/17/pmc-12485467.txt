Pain, poverty, and the politics of health: a global call for justice-oriented pain management
Authors: Jose Eric Mella Lacsa
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR

The groundbreaking study by Lyu et al. [1], “Causal association of obesity and chronic pain mediated by educational attainment and smoking: a mediation Mendelian randomization study,”underscores a critical yet often overlooked truth: chronic pain is not merely a clinical condition—it is a social consequence. By revealing that educational attainment and smoking behaviors mediate the genetic relationship between obesity and chronic pain, the study opens new avenues for understanding and addressing pain through systemic lenses.

In the Philippine context, this research carries profound implications. The country faces a growing obesity epidemic, driven not only by changing diets and sedentary lifestyles but also by socio-economic disparities and limited access to education and healthcare [2]. At the same time, chronic pain remains underdiagnosed and undertreated, especially among low-income populations. Traditional approaches to pain management often focus narrowly on pharmacological or procedural solutions. However, the findings of this study demand a broader view—one that incorporates social determinants of health.

In urban poor communities across Metro Manila, for example, high body mass index is often the byproduct of food insecurity, leading families to consume calorie-dense but nutrient-poor diets [3]. Smoking prevalence remains high among the youth and labor sectors, frequently tied to stress and lack of health education. These behavioral patterns, compounded by low educational attainment, form a vicious cycle of chronic disease, pain, and poverty.

Globally, the study’s implications are just as urgent. It reinforces the call for integrative pain management policies that do not isolate biology from biography. Chronic pain cannot be fully understood apart from the structural inequities that shape health behaviors and limit life choices [4]. The identified mediators—education and smoking—are proxies for deeper issues: social exclusion, economic instability, and intergenerational disadvantage.

Thus, prevention and management of chronic pain must move beyond the clinic and into classrooms, policy halls, and community centers. Public health strategies should include school-based nutrition programs, anti-smoking campaigns that go beyond awareness to address root causes, and equitable access to quality education [5]. Pain specialists and primary care providers alike must be trained to recognize and address these broader contributors to pain.

The research of Lyu et al. [1] does more than confirm a causal pathway; it highlights a moral imperative. If we are to alleviate the global burden of chronic pain, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like the Philippines, we must adopt a justice-oriented approach—where managing pain is inseparable from dismantling the inequities that cause it.
