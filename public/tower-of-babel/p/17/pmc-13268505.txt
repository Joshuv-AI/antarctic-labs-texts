Association of heavy metal exposure, C-reactive protein, and stroke risk in US adults: A cross-sectional analysis (NHANES 2005–2008)
Authors: Yanli Cui, Juan Gao, Jing Zhang, Jing Li, Ziwen Wang, Xin Zhang, Yujing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke occupies an important position in the global disease burden, but the combined effect of heavy metal exposure and the inflammatory marker C-reactive protein (CRP) on stroke remains unclear. Based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, this study explores the association between blood lead, blood cadmium, blood mercury, CRP, and the risk of stroke. A total of 8297 American adults aged 20 years and older were included in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Standardized questionnaires were used to collect stroke history and covariates such as age, gender, race, education, income poverty ratio, body mass index, smoking, drinking, hypertension, and diabetes; whole blood cadmium, lead, mercury, and serum CRP were measured. Weighted multivariate logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between heavy metal exposure and CRP outcomes; receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed to compare model performance; and a 3D interaction graph was used to evaluate the synergistic effect of plumbum and CRP. Baseline characteristics: the stroke group’s median age (69 years) was significantly higher than the non-stroke group’s (33 years). Stroke patients had higher blood lead, CRP, hypertension/diabetes rates, and body mass index, but lower blood mercury, education, income levels (P < .05). After adjustment, each unit increase in blood lead and CRP increased the risk of stroke by 7% (odds ratio [OR] = 1.07, 95% confidence interval: 1.00–1.14) and 19% (OR = 1.19, 95% confidence interval: 1.07–1.32), respectively; blood cadmium and mercury had no significant association. The Receiver Operating Characteristic curves showed model 2 (area under the curve = 0.80) outperformed model 1 (area under the curve = 0.64). In age subgroups, cadmium affected the 40- to 60-year age group (OR = 1.39); lead was positively and mercury negatively correlated with the ≥60 group; CRP rose in both groups. The 3D graph indicated that stroke risk increased when both blood lead and CRP levels were high. Blood lead and CRP are independent stroke risk factors. Each unit rise in blood lead raises stroke risk by 7%, and each unit increase in CRP raises it by 19%. When both are high, stroke risk may further rise, especially in the elderly. Clinically, regular monitoring of these factors aids in stroke risk assessment. For high-risk groups, including these in routine screening enables early risk identification.

1. Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide and the leading cause of disability among adults. Its high incidence rate, high recurrence rate, and heavy disease burden have become major challenges in the field of public health.[1] According to the 2022 report by the World Health Organization, approximately 15 million people suffer from stroke each year, among whom 5.5 million die from complications related to stroke, and this number is still on the rise.[2,3] In the United States, although the mortality rate of stroke has decreased over the past 3 decades, the incidence rate has been on the rise among the younger population (under 55 years old), suggesting that in addition to traditional risk factors, new environmental and metabolic factors may be reshaping the disease profile.[4] Previous studies have clearly demonstrated the contribution of traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and obesity to the occurrence of stroke.[5,6] However, with widespread exposure to environmental pollutants and the deepening of the theory of chronic inflammation, the role of heavy metal (especially lead) exposure in conjunction with systemic inflammation in the etiology of stroke has gradually gained attention.[7,8]

We selected the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005 to 2008 cycle for this study for several important reasons. First, this cycle represents the most recent period for which publicly available data exist that link stroke outcome measures to relevant biomarkers. This linkage is crucial, as it allows us to directly investigate the associations between blood lead levels, systemic inflammation (as indicated by C-reactive protein [CRP]), and stroke risk. Without such linked data, our ability to draw meaningful conclusions about these relationships would be severely limited.

Second, the time frame of 2005 to 2008 is still relevant in the context of studying long-term health effects. The biological processes involved in the development of stroke, such as atherosclerosis and the cumulative effects of environmental exposures, often take years or even decades to manifest. By using data from this period, we can capture a snapshot of exposure levels and health outcomes that may still be reflective of current trends, especially considering that environmental exposures and lifestyle factors do not change abruptly over short periods.

Third, the NHANES data from this cycle has undergone rigorous quality control and standardization procedures. This ensures the reliability and validity of the data, which is essential for conducting robust epidemiological research. Using well-validated data enhances the credibility of our findings and allows for more accurate comparisons with other studies in the field.

Data from the NHANES 2005 to 2008 cycle (the most recent cycle with publicly available stroke outcome data linked to biomarkers) demonstrate that even low-dose blood lead levels in US adults are linearly and positively associated with cardiovascular disease mortality.[9,10] Lead can promote atherosclerosis and thrombosis through mechanisms such as inducing oxidative stress, inhibiting the activity of nitric oxide synthase, and damaging vascular endothelial function.[11,12] Furthermore, lead exposure can also affect the expression of vascular-related genes through epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation).[13] It is worth noting that the toxic effects of lead are not limited to a single organ but involve interactions among multiple systems. In particular, there is a synergistic effect with systemic inflammation, which provides a new explanatory path for the complex causes of stroke.[14]

The role of systemic inflammation in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and stroke has been well established through numerous experimental and epidemiological studies.[15] CRP, as an acute-phase response protein, is a sensitive indicator of systemic inflammation. Prospective studies have shown that individuals with elevated baseline CRP levels have a 1.5 to 2.0 times higher risk of developing ischemic stroke in the future.[16] CRP not only reflects the inflammatory state but also can directly participate in the process of atherosclerosis through mechanisms such as activating the complement system, promoting the phagocytosis of low-density lipoprotein by macrophages, and inducing the expression of adhesion molecules by vascular endothelial cells.[17] Furthermore, there may be a synergistic effect between inflammatory response and lead exposure: animal experiments have shown that lead exposure can activate the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells signaling pathway and upregulate the expression of inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6.[18] But causal inference studies in large human populations remain lacking.

Demographic factors such as age, race, and socioeconomic status may modulate lead and inflammation effects. For instance, the non-Hispanic Black population in the United States has significantly higher blood lead levels due to differences in living environment and occupational exposure compared with the White population, and the levels of inflammatory markers in the low socioeconomic status population are also generally higher.[19] Furthermore, the NHANES data show that although Mexican Americans have higher levels of lead exposure, their incidence of stroke is lower than that of non-Hispanic whites, suggesting that racial-specific genetic background or lifestyle factors may play a protective role.[20] Therefore, exploring the interaction between blood lead and CRP in different subpopulations is of great significance for formulating precise prevention strategies for stroke.

This study leverages NHANES 2005 to 2008 data to systematically evaluate associations between blood lead, CRP, and stroke risk, addressing 3 key questions: Are blood lead and CRP independent risk factors for stroke?; Does blood lead mediate its effects via CRP?; and Do associations vary by age, race, or socioeconomic status? Using weighted logistic regression, mediation analysis, and interaction models, we aim to provide novel epidemiological evidence for environmental-inflammation interactions in stroke and offer theoretical foundations for early high-risk population identification. Innovations include: the first large-sample assessment of blood lead and CRP’s independent/combined effects in NHANES; A “receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC)-subgroup-mediation-3D interaction” framework to optimize risk prediction, explore age heterogeneity, test causal paths, and visualize synergistic effects; methodological generalizability, revealing a significant synergistic amplification of stroke risk by blood lead and CRP (3D interaction graph), enabling precise “dual-risk” stratification. Critically, we refute the “Pb→CRP→Stroke” mediating pathway (no significant indirect effect) in a large population, proposing independent/collaborative (rather than causal chain) mechanisms, thereby challenging and supplementing prior animal-based inflammatory hypotheses at the population level.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Clinical data

The data for this study were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) of the United States. It was a cross-sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States. The purpose of NHANES was to provide nationally representative data on the civilian population of the United States. The National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board approved the data collection protocol, and all survey participants gave informed consent before undergoing interviews and examinations.

In this study, 2 cycles (2005–2006, 2007–2008) of NHANES response public data files were used to construct a dataset. The study population included all NHANES respondents, and all participants signed written informed consent forms. The data for this study were sourced from the NHANES 2005 to 2008 database. Initially, 20,497 participants were included. After systematic data screening, participants with missing stroke diagnosis information (n = 9610), missing key demographic variables (n = 811), missing heavy metal exposure indicators (n = 900), missing CRP test values (n = 45), and missing covariate information, such as smoking and drinking (n = 832), were sequentially excluded, leaving 8299 participants. Subsequently, 3 extreme outliers for CRP or heavy metal exposure values were further removed using boxplots or Z-score normalization, resulting in a final cohort of 8297 participants eligible for analysis (Fig. 1). It should be noted that excluding patients with missing data on confounding variables is a limitation as it may lead to potential selection bias, as well as a reduction in sample size and loss of representativeness. After excluding patients with missing data on confounding variables such as stroke, demographic factors, heavy metal exposure, CRP, smoking, and drinking, a total of 8297 interview data points were left, including 7993 non-patients and 304 patients.

2.2. Data collection

This study aims to explore the risk factors of stroke while considering a series of potential covariates. These variables are based on previous literature and substantive reasoning and are believed to have a potential association with stroke. The study employed the NHANES standardized questionnaire to collect the following indicators of association: CRP (mg/L), blood cadmium, blood lead, and blood mercury. Covariates: age, gender, race, education, income poverty ratio, body mass index (BMI), smoking, drinking, hypertension, and diabetes.

Whether it is a stroke is determined through a series of self-reported questionnaires. Based on affirmative answers to the multiple-choice questions (MCQ160F) of the NHANES: “Have you been told that you have had a stroke?” However, relying on self-reported data for stroke diagnosis may introduce reporting bias.

Through a comprehensive analysis of these data, this study aims to analyze the role of CRP and heavy metal exposure in stroke. In determining the sample size for this study, we comprehensively considered effect sizes from previous similar studies, set the statistical test power (≥0.8) and significance level (0.05), and used specialized software to calculate the initial sample size. After considering a dropout rate of 10% to 15%, the sample size was appropriately increased, ultimately determined to be 8297 participants (7993 non-patients and 304 patients), balancing both efficiency and feasibility. During data collection, there were some missing data, and different methods were applied depending on the type of missingness. For completely random missing data (MCAR) with a small proportion, simple imputation was used; specifically, the mean for continuous variables and random sampling according to the distribution for categorical variables. For random missing data (MAR), multiple imputation was applied, using models appropriate for the data type to generate multiple complete datasets for integrated analysis. For nonrandom missing data (MNAR), a sensitivity analysis was first conducted; if the impact was small, the results were considered robust, and if the impact was large, measures were explored to address it, or the results were interpreted cautiously with limitations explained, ensuring research transparency and result reliability.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses followed the guidelines of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, using sampling weights to generate estimates of the prevalence of disease in the noninstitutionalized US population that are nationally representative. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as number and percentage; quantitative data with a normal distribution are described as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), with comparisons between 2 groups conducted using t tests; non-normally distributed data are described using the median and interquartile range M (P25, P75). Comparisons between 2 groups were conducted using t tests (for continuous variables) or chi-square tests (for categorical variables). A weighted logistic regression model was used to explore the impact of CRP and heavy metal factors on the risk of stroke. All exposure variables (blood cadmium, blood lead, blood mercury, CRP) were included simultaneously in the multivariate logistic regression model in both Model 1 and Model 2 using the forced entry method (enter method) rather than stepwise regression. This approach avoids bias caused by variable selection strategies and ensures a direct assessment of the independent effects of each exposure. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore population heterogeneity. Mediation effect tests were performed to analyze the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the relationship between heavy metal exposure, CRP, and stroke. Finally, interaction effect analyses were conducted to verify the combined effect of heavy metal exposure and CRP.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

A total of 8297 participants were included in this study, among which 7993 were in the non-stroke group, and 304 were in the stroke group. By comparing the baseline characteristics of the 2 groups, the results showed significant differences in multiple features. There was no significant difference in gender distribution between the 2 groups (P = .515). The median age of the stroke group was 69 years, much higher than that of the non-stroke group (47 years), indicating that increasing age is an important risk factor for stroke. The racial distribution showed a significant difference: the proportion of non-Hispanic Whites was lower in the stroke group than in the non-stroke group (50.1% vs 59.9%), while the proportions of Mexican Americans (18.7% vs 8.9%) and other Hispanics (7.2% vs 3.3%) were higher in the stroke group. The P-value < .001 suggested that race might be a risk factor. The association between education level and economic status was significant: the proportion of “university or above” education in the stroke group was lower (35.9% vs 48.4%), and the median income poverty ratio was lower than the median value (1.75 vs 2.33), with P-values < .001 indicating that low education level and low income groups had a higher risk of stroke. Smoking history had a significant impact: the proportion of “former smokers” in the stroke group was 42.4%, much higher than that in the non-stroke group (25%), while the proportion of “never smokers” was lower (35.2% vs 52.7%), with a P-value < .001, indicating that smoking history was related to stroke. The drinking pattern difference was weak: the proportion of “occasional drinking” was similar (50% vs 48%) in the stroke group, but the proportion of “heavy drinking” was slightly lower (12.8% vs 14%), with a P-value of .012 suggesting a weak influence of drinking. Heavy metal exposure is worthy of attention: the concentration of lead (Pb) in the stroke group was higher (1.99 vs 1.44), with a P-value < .001, while the concentration of mercury (Hg) was lower (0.78 vs 0.90), with a P-value of .010. The direction of the mercury association needs further verification. Inflammation and chronic diseases are key: the median value of CRP (an inflammatory marker) in the stroke group was higher (0.35 vs 0.20), and the proportions of hypertension (77% vs 31.9%) and diabetes (34.9% vs 10.8%) were significantly higher, with P-values < .001, indicating that inflammation and chronic diseases are risk factors. The median value of BMI in the stroke group was higher (29.44 vs 27.79), with a P-value of .001 indicating that obesity is related to the risk of stroke (Table 1).

3.2. The increase in blood lead and CRP levels raises the risk of stroke: based on multivariate logistic regression analysis

In both the basic model (Model 1) and the extended model adjusted for confounding factors (Model 2), lead (Pb) and CRP showed significant associations (P < .05), indicating a clear statistical link with stroke risk. For each 1 μg/dL increase in lead exposure, stroke risk increased significantly by 7%. For each 1 mg/L increase in inflammation level (CRP), stroke risk increased significantly by 19%. However, no significant associations were found between blood cadmium (Cd) and blood mercury (Hg; P > .05). Comparing the basic model (Model 1) and the extended model adjusted for confounders (Model 2) confirmed the stability of lead and CRP as independent risk factors for stroke (Table 2).

4. Discussion

This study, based on nationally representative NHANES data, for the first time tentatively explored the association between blood lead and CRP and the risk of stroke in a large sample population and systematically analyzed the modifying effects of key factors such as age and race. It should be noted that this is an exploratory study, and further research is required to confirm the results. The main findings suggest: elevated blood lead and CRP levels significantly increase the risk of stroke, and this association remains robust even after adjusting for confounding factors; the mediation analysis failed to confirm that CRP plays a mediating role in the association between lead and stroke, suggesting that the pathogenic mechanisms of the 2 may be relatively independent; the 3D interaction plot visually shows that blood lead and CRP have a synergistic effect on the risk of stroke; that is, when both increase simultaneously, the risk significantly accumulates.

The positive correlation between blood lead and stroke is consistent with previous studies. Relevant studies have found that for every 10-fold increase in blood lead, the risk of cardiovascular death increases by 44% (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.05–1.98).[21] This study further focused on the endpoint of stroke and found that for every 1-unit increase in blood lead level, the risk of stroke appears to increase by 7%. Moreover, the effect seemed to be stronger in the elderly population (≥60 years old; OR = 1.09). The pathogenic mechanism of lead may involve multiple pathways: first, lead can replace calcium ions and bind with calmodulin, causing abnormal contraction of vascular smooth muscle cells and elevated blood pressure[22]; secondly, lead inhibits the activity of δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase, thereby increasing the production of reactive oxygen species and promoting oxidative damage to vascular endothelium. Furthermore, lead exposure can also affect the expression of vascular-related genes through epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation).[23]

The association between elevated systemic inflammatory markers such as CRP and stroke has been confirmed by numerous studies. In the Physician’s Health Study, it was found that the healthy male participants in the highest quartile of baseline CRP levels had a 1.9 times higher risk of future stroke compared with those in the lowest quartile.[24] This study further revealed that for every 1-unit increase in CRP, the risk of stroke increased by 19%, and this effect was most significant in the middle-aged population (aged 40–60; OR = 1.23). CRP may promote stroke through the following mechanisms: first, CRP can activate the classical complement pathway, forming membrane attack complexes, which leads to the dissolution of vascular endothelial cells[25]; second, CRP increases the expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, thereby inhibiting the activity of the fibrinolytic system and promoting thrombosis[25]; third, CRP stimulates the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells, accelerating the progression of atherosclerotic plaques.[25]

However, the mediation analysis of this study failed to confirm that CRP mediates the association between lead and stroke. Instead, it was found that lead has a weak negative effect on CRP (β = −0.0118). This contradictory result may be related to the dual inflammatory effect of lead: low-dose lead may activate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 antioxidant pathway and inhibit the inflammatory response mediated by nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells, while high-dose lead exacerbates inflammation through oxidative stress.[26] Further research is needed to explore the inflammatory response patterns under different doses of lead exposure in the future. It should also be noted that there may be residual confounding factors. For example, dietary factors such as fish consumption, which is rich in omega-3 fatty acids and has anti-inflammatory properties, could potentially influence the associations between blood lead, CRP, and stroke. Socioeconomic status may also play a role, as it can affect access to healthcare, lifestyle factors, and exposure to environmental toxins. Additionally, metal clearance mechanisms in the body could confound the relationships between cadmium, mercury, and stroke risk.

The subgroup analysis revealed the age-specific effects of blood lead and CRP. In the elderly population (≥60 years old), the effect of blood lead was stronger (OR = 1.09), which might be related to the reduced lead clearance due to the decline in physical function in this age group.[27] Furthermore, the effect of CRP is stronger in the elderly population (OR = 1.16), which may be related to the chronic low-level inflammatory state caused by immune aging.

The interaction analysis revealed that when blood lead and CRP both increase, the risk of stroke significantly increases, suggesting that there may be a synergistic pathogenic mechanism between the 2. On one hand, lead exposure can exacerbate vascular endothelial dysfunction, providing “soil” for the inflammatory damage mediated by CRP; on the other hand, the pro-thrombotic effect of elevated CRP may amplify the hypercoagulable state induced by lead.[28] This discovery could potentially have great significance for clinical practice: for individuals with high blood lead levels (such as those exposed to occupational hazards), even if CRP is slightly elevated (such as hs-CRP > 3 mg/L), intensified primary prevention measures for stroke (such as blood pressure reduction and antiplatelet therapy) could potentially be considered.

The limitations of this study need to be noted. First, NHANES is a cross-sectional survey, which cannot establish the temporal sequence between blood lead and CRP exposure and stroke. Future prospective cohort studies are needed to verify the causal relationship. Second, the stroke outcome is based on self-reporting, which may have recall bias. Although NHANES reviewed some cases through medical records, it may still underestimate the underdiagnosis rate. A single blood lead and CRP test may not reflect the long-term exposure level. Finally, although multiple confounding factors were adjusted, residual confounding (such as dietary patterns and genetic susceptibility) may still affect the results.

5. Conclusion

This study, based on a large sample population from the NHANES, tentatively suggests that blood lead and CRP are independent risk factors for stroke and that they may have a synergistic pathogenic effect; however, no association between lead and CRP mediated by CRP was found. In the future, further verification of these associations through prospective studies is needed, and early intervention strategies targeting lead exposure and systemic inflammation should be explored to potentially reduce the disease burden of stroke.
