Psychometric properties of the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) screening tool in people living with HIV: the Afaan Oromoo version
Authors: Fufa Olana, Mubarek Abera, Liyew Agenagnew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of the study was to assess the psychometric properties of the Afaan Oromoo version of Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale among people living with HIV.DesignA hospital-based cross-sectional study.SettingThe study was conducted at a tertiary-level antiretroviral therapy clinic.ParticipantsThe study included 163 people living with HIV.Primary outcome measuresThe GAD-7 scale was evaluated against the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition diagnostic criteria for GAD as the gold standard.ResultA total of 163 participants were enrolled (response rate: 98.2%). The Afaan Oromoo GAD-7 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.92). A cut-off score of ≥9 provided the optimal balance between sensitivity (89.19%) and specificity (85.75%), with an area under curve of 0.91, a positive predictive value of 64.7% and negative predictive value of 96.4%. Confirmatory factor analysis supported a one-factor structure.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the Afaan Oromoo version of the GAD-7 is a valid and reliable tool for screening GAD among adults receiving HIV care. Routine use of the GAD-7 in antiretroviral therapy clinics is recommended to support early identification of anxiety symptoms.

Introduction

Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is characterised by persistent feelings of anxiety and worries about multiple things, in which the worry is usually out of proportion to the real circumstances and is difficult to control.1 Among anxiety disorders, GAD is one of the most prevalent mental disorders and contributes significantly to the global burden of disease, and it is the most common form of anxiety disorder, which accounts for approximately 110 million disability days every year.2

It is one of the most underdiagnosed anxiety disorders, and it is difficult to diagnose consistently. A study showed that only 34% of people with GAD are diagnosed correctly by general practitioners.3

HIV/AIDS may raise the risk for developing anxiety symptoms and disorders, and similarly, anxiety disorder may influence the severity of HIV/AIDS.4 HIV-related symptoms can intensify anxiety, while anxiety may negatively impact disease management, creating a cycle that worsens both conditions.5 Among people living with HIV (PLHIV), the prevalence of GAD is approximately 30% in primary care settings of high-income countries6 and about 23% in low- and middle-income countries.4 In the USA, 19% of PLHIV experience symptoms of GAD,7 while in sub-Saharan Africa, around 21.7% of PLHIV had anxiety disorder and around 5.3% suffer from mixed anxiety depressive disorder.8 On the other hand, the prevalence of GAD among people living with HIV/AIDS varies based on the screening instrument used, which may lead to overestimation or underestimation. Overdiagnosis of GAD can place a financial strain on the healthcare system in resource-constrained communities, and false-positive cases may be referred for treatment unnecessarily. In one study, using the International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) as an assessment tool, the prevalence of GAD among people living with HIV/AIDS was 13.3%,9 while GAD was shown to be just 0.4% in another study that used the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) as an assessment tool.10 In another study, the prevalence of GAD was 25%, using GAD-7 as an assessment tool.11

Although the GAD-7 has been validated in several African settings and across different population groups, evidence remains limited among PLHIV. To date, only a small number of studies have specifically focused on this population, including one conducted in Kilifi, Kenya,12 and another in Zimbabwe.13 Beyond PLHIV populations, validation work has been carried out in other clinical and non-clinical groups, such as patients with tuberculosis in South Africa,14 students in Ghana,15 the general population in Uganda16 and university students in Ethiopia.17 This indicates that, while the instrument has demonstrated cross-cultural applicability in Africa, important gaps persist in validating the tool among HIV-affected populations, particularly across diverse linguistic and clinical contexts.

A report on Ethiopia’s mental health strategy for 2020–2025 estimates that only 25% of hospitals in the country offer outpatient mental health services. There are only 843 psychiatric beds in Ethiopia, the majority of which are situated in Addis Ababa facilities such as Yeka Kotebe Hospital and Amanuel Specialized Mental Hospital. The Gefersa Rehab Center, which has 200 beds overall and serves patients with severe mental illness, is the only long-term rehabilitation facility in the country.18 As a result, validating a screening instrument that is brief, easy and can be used by any healthcare professionals based on societal culture and language is important for engaging general healthcare work in mental health screening. Non-psychiatric health professionals such as comprehensive nurses, public health officials, midwifery nurses and general practitioners can perform GAD-7 screenings in as little as 3–5 min. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the psychometric properties of GAD-7 have not been studied among people living with HIV/AIDS in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of GAD-7 among people living with HIV/AIDS visiting an antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinic at Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC) in Jimma, southwest Ethiopia, in the year 2021.

Methods and materials

Study design, period and area

JUMC, a major teaching and referral hospital in southwest Ethiopia, served as the study site. A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from August to October 2021. The data collection period was determined after completion of key preliminary activities such as language translation and ethical clearance approval.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

The sample size was determined by using anticipated sensitivity (89%) and anticipated specificity (73%) for the single validation test formula.13 Based on the anticipated sensitivity formula, the calculated sample size was 83, including non-response rate. Based on the formula of specificity including non-response rate, we got 166. To obtain the maximum required sample size, the estimate based on specificity was used. A consecutive sampling technique was used to select study participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

PLHIV who were attending ART at JUMC and were aged 18 years and older were included in this study. Individuals who were critically ill during the data collection period and who were unable to understand the Afaan Oromoo language were excluded from the study.

Procedures and measures

All questionnaires were translated into Afaan Oromoo with translation and back-translation procedures. To ensure good semantic validity, the original English version of the questionnaires was forward translated into Afaan Oromoo. The translation was done by two bilingual professionals in both languages. The forward translated questionnaires were back-translated into English by another two bilingual college instructors with MSc degrees who have experience in translating research questionnaires. The original forward and back translated versions of the questionnaires were reviewed and discussed among the team of the translator involved, the principal investigator and among senior psychiatry professionals. The discrepancies were resolved after discussion with the involved professionals. Data were collected by two BSc nurses and two BSc psychiatry professionals through face-to-face interviews after they received practical training on the procedure of data collection and about tools on data collection instrument. Data were collected using participants’ sociodemographic characteristics’ questionnaire, Generalised Anxiety 7 scale (GAD-7) and Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria for GAD (see online supplemental file 1).

Sociodemographic

Participant’s sociodemographic questionnaire like sex, age, educational status, religion, marital status, residency and employment were assessed.

Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7

GAD 7 (GAD-7) is a seven-item anxiety screening tool developed by Spitzer et al in the USA in 2006 for use in primary care and is now used in a variety of settings. It asks how often participants have been bothered by anxiety symptoms in the past 2 weeks. Its items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale indicating symptom frequency; response categories are: not at all, several days, more than half the days and nearly every day, scored as 0, 1, 2 and 3, respectively. Total scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating the presence of GAD. The overall score can be interpreted as suggesting no/minimal anxiety (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14) or severe (15–21), with a reasonable cut-off value of 10 points for detecting cases with GAD, according to the original authors. In the original validation study performed in the primary care clinics, the cut-off score of 10 or higher (recommended cut-off score) provides a sensitivity of 89% and a specificity of 82% with Cronbach’s alpha α 0.92.19

Since its development, GAD-7 has been validated in a range of demographics and cultures, as well as in a variety of populations, including people living with HIV/AIDS,13 people with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,20 people with multiple sclerosis,21 pregnant women,22 people with migraine headache23 and people with cancer.24

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the validity of GAD-7’s among people living with HIV/AIDS has only been studied in two developing countries. The DSM-IV was employed as the gold standard to test the criterion validity in a study conducted in Zimbabwe among people living with HIV/AIDS.13 The reliability and structural validity of GAD-7 were investigated in another study conducted in Kilifi, Kenya, among people living with HIV/AIDS.12 The GAD-7 was administered through face-to-face interviewer administration for all participants, regardless of literacy level. To ensure standardised administration and minimise the influence of literacy status on responses, the interviewers read each item verbatim in Afaan Oromoo and recorded participants’ responses without interpretation or modification.

DSM-5 criteria for GAD

The diagnosis of GAD was established using DSM-5 diagnostic criteria through a clinician-administered structured clinical assessment. The assessment was conducted by two BSc psychiatry professionals who received intensive training from a senior mental health professional prior to data collection. Training included review of DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, mock interviews and supervised pilot assessments.

The diagnostic interview evaluated all DSM-5 criteria (A–F), including duration (≥6 months), excessive worry, difficulty controlling worry, associated symptoms, functional impairment and exclusion of substance-induced or other mental disorders (see table 1). The GAD-7 items mainly reflect DSM–5–TR Criterion C symptoms such as restlessness, irritability, difficulty relaxing. The assessment followed DSM-5 diagnostic guidelines and did not employ an additional disability scale; impairment was determined clinically according to DSM-5 Criterion D. The diagnostic assessor was blinded to the GAD-7 results to avoid assessment bias.25

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into EPI Data V.4.6 and was exported to SPSS V.25 and Analysis of Moment Structures V.26 (AMOS) for statistical analysis. AMOS was used for analysis of confirmatory factor analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated for sociodemographic and item characteristics. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to measure internal consistency and determine reliability. The Kappa coefficient was used for analysis of inter-rater reliability.

Criterion validity was determined using the area under curve (AUC) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to estimate the diagnosis accuracy of GAD-7 and the cut-off score, which provides high sensitivity and specificity with corresponding 95% CI. Positive and negative predictive values and likelihood ratios were also calculated for the yielding cut-off score. Youden’s J index (sensitivity and specificity −1) was used to determine the optimal cut-off score with the best balance of sensitivity and specificity.

The structural validity of GAD-7 was determined using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Different evaluations of statistic measurement, such as absolute fit measurement, incremental fit measurement and parsimonious fit measurement were performed against the standard using the AMOS V.26 for CFA.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or publics were not involved in the design, conducting, reporting or dissemination plan of the study.

Result

A total of 163 patients took part in the study, with a response rate of 98.19%. The participants’ ages ranged from 19 to 84 years, with the mean age of 39.9 (SD±13.9) years. Nearly two-thirds of the participants were female 62% (101), nearly half of the respondents were married 47.9% (78) and 53.4% (87) were Muslim. Out of all participants, 60.1% (98) were from rural areas, 36.2% (59) of the participants had attended primary school and nearly one-third 30.7% (50) were farmers by occupation (see table 2).

Structural validity of GAD-7

Confirmatory factor analysis

CFA was done based on previously hypothesised one model fitness and two model fitness of GAD-7. Model fit indices were used to determine the acceptable model fit for one and two component solutions. The one-factor model of the GAD-7 scale approached, but did not meet, acceptable fit criteria. For one model fitness, another measurement of fitness was satisfactory but the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was not, due to the value being not less than or equal to 0.08 (RMSEA=0.120). Separating items 4–6 as a separated factor (somatic) and 1, 2, 3 and 7 (cognitive-affective) (see figure 2) as another separated factor was used to test the GAD-7’s two-factor solution, separation to somatic and cognitive-affective was done based on previous study results.26 The model fit was, however, worse than a single factor solution (χ²=192.016, df=13, Comparative Fit Index (CFI)=0.865; Goodness of Fit Index (GFI)=0.773; RMSEA=0.292).

Discussion

The aim of this research was to investigate the psychometric properties of the Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 among adults living with HIV at JUMC. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first Ethiopian study to evaluate the psychometric properties of GAD-7 among PLWHA. The Afaan Oromoo version of the GAD-7 had good internal consistency, which was 0.92, acceptable semantic and content validity, and satisfactory criterion validity as evidenced by its correlation with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. All items loaded well on a one-factor solution, with factor loadings ranging from 0.60 to 0.99. The Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 retained a one-dimensional factor structure.

The Cronbach’s alpha of the Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 was 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency between items. Internal consistency of rating scales ranges from poor to excellent, with values ranging from 0.50 to 0.9.27 The current study result had a higher Cronbach’s alpha than a previous study conducted in Kenya among HIV patients (α=0.82),12 and a study conducted in Zimbabwe among patients living with HIV in primary care (α=0.87),13 and Washington among patients with multiple sclerosis (α=0.75),21 but this study’s findings were consistent with the original study done among primary care patients (α=0.92),19 the study done among patients in primary healthcare in Pakistan (α=0.92),28 the study done among psychiatric patients in England (α=0.91),29 and the study done among migraine headache patients (α=0.915).23 However, the current study’s findings were lower than those of a study conducted in Pune, India, among PLHIV (α=0.96),11 and the Spanish version of GAD-7 (α=0.936),30 both of which were slightly higher than the current study. These differences may be attributable to linguistic and cultural variations across study populations. The study conducted in Kenya among HIV patients was validated in Swahili. The other possible reason may be differences among study participants. The study done at Washington was among multiple sclerosis patients, and the study done in Pakistan was among patients in primary care. Sample size could also be a possible variation for Cronbach’s alpha. The study done in Pakistan was conducted among 285 patients, while the present study was done among 163 study participants.

The optimal cut-off score was determined using the ROC curve and the DSM-5 as the gold standard. This study found that a cut-off score of 9 with an AUC of 0.910 and a Youden’s J index of 0.74 balanced sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity and specificity were 89.19% and 85.71%, respectively, at cut-off score 9, area under the curve 0.910, and Youden’s J index was 0.74. This suggests that the Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 is a useful screening tool for PLWHA with a cut-off score of 9 or higher. The relatively modest positive predictive value may be due to high prevalence of somatic symptoms related to HIV infection and ART adverse effects, which overlap with GAD-7 somatic items (fatigue, restlessness, sleep disturbance). This may inflate screening positives while maintaining high negative predictive value. Additionally, major depressive symptoms are common among PLHIV and have overlapping symptoms with GAD, especially with symptoms including sleep disturbance, fatigue and impaired concentration. This similarity may result in increased GAD-7 scores and possible misclassification, thereby affecting the specificity of the GAD-7 screening tool.

Compared with the GAD-7 version conducted in Zimbabwe among PLHIV, using DSM-IV as the gold standard, the present study had similar results in sensitivity and AUC, which were 89% and 0.90, respectively.13 The sensitivity of the present study and the study conducted in the USA among primary care patients using DSM-IV as the gold standard was similar (89%),19 and also the study done in America among chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients showed similar results, 89%.20

The cut-off point was slightly lower than the study conducted in Zimbabwe among HIV patients using DSM-IV as the gold standard,13 the study conducted in the USA among primary care patients using DSM-IV as the gold standard,19 the study conducted at Boston University among patients with anxiety and mood disorders, using DSM-IV as the gold standard,31 and the study conducted in Spanish primary care patients using the CIDI as the gold standard.32 These findings revealed that a cut-off value of 10 had the optimal balance of sensitivity and specificity, and that this cut-off point was suitable for screening GAD.

In contrast, the cut-off value of the current study result was higher than that of other studies, including a study done in Jakarta among epileptic patients using MINI as a gold standard that showed a cut-off point of 6 maximised sensitivity and specificity,33 the Malaysian version of GAD-7 done among women attending primary care using CIDI as a gold standard that showed a cut-off value of 8,29 and a study from Kyungpook National University that showed a cut-off point of 6 maximised sensitivity and specificity.23 The GAD-7 cut-off value was 7 in a study done in Finland among primary care patients using the MINI as gold standard,34 and the cut-off value was 7 in a study done in Germany among patients with cancer using the CIDI for Oncology (CIDI-O) as gold standard.24 The variation in this cut-off score could be due to differences in the gold standard tool used. Validation of GAD-7 in Germany used the Composite CIDI-O as the gold standard, in Jakarta used MINI as a gold standard, Malaysia used CIDI as gold, and at Boston University used DSM-IV as the gold standard; however, the present study used DSM-5 as the gold standard reference.

Several modification indices, as well as one- and two-component models, were investigated during CFA. The indices research allowed for the co-variation of the error terms of items 5 and 7, as well as 2 and 3, to improve model fitness for a one-factor model. Allowing the error terms of items 2 and 3, 5 and 7 into the model improved the model fit. In the Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 from CFA, the one-factor solution was retained. All elements loaded well onto a one-factor solution with greater than the minimal standard factor loading of 0.5, with factor loadings ranging from 0.60 to 0.99 and outstanding GFIs. This result was consistent across studies conducted in different settings and countries. A Swahili version of GAD-7 conducted among HIV patients in Kenya also showed similar results;12 a study conducted by Spitzter et al19 supported a one-dimensional factor structure of GAD-7,19 an Urdu version of GAD-735 and Spanish version of GAD-7 also supported a one-dimensional factor structure.30 Other study results, in contrast to this one-dimensional model factor, suggest a two-dimensional model factor by splitting factors into two components: somatic (items 1, 2, 3 and 7) and cognitive-affective components of anxiety disorders (factors 4 to 6). A study done in England among acute psychiatric patients,36 a study done by Moreno et al among primary care also supported a two-factor model fit,26 and a study done by Beard and Björgvinsson among heterogeneous psychiatric patients indicated a two-dimensional factor model was a good fit.37 This model fitness variance might be due to different types of factor analysis used and differences in the assessment of modification indices. Despite this being the first validation study conducted among PLHIV and the third study in Africa, our study has limitations such as, the GAD-7 assesses a 2-week symptom period, which does not align with the 6-month DSM-5 diagnostic requirement, potentially reducing its positive predictive value. Conducting the study at a single ART clinic limits the generalisability of the findings. Even though, the interviewer administration support participation among individuals with low literacy, oral administration does not fully solve literacy related challenges. Understanding of participants about psychological concepts may still vary according to educational background, potentially influencing responses. Besides to this, interviewer admiration may have increased social desirability bias. Additionally, other anxiety disorders and major depressive disorder were not evaluated using structured diagnostic interviews. Given the considerable symptom overlap between GAD, other anxiety disorders and depression, certain participants may have been misclassified, possibly influencing the estimated diagnostic performance of the GAD-7. Significant HIV-related clinical features, including CD4 count, viral load, length of HIV infection, WHO clinical stage, opportunistic infections and ART regime, were not included. These factors may influence both somatic and psychological symptoms and hence could affect GAD-7 scores and diagnostic classification. Even though rough forward backward translation processes and expert review were conducted, some linguistic and cultural nuances may not have been fully captured, which could have affected participants’ interpretation of certain GAD-7 items.

Conclusions

These findings suggest that Afaan Oromoo version of GAD-7 is one-dimensional scale with excellent psychometric properties among adults living with HIV from Jimma, Ethiopia. The cut-off score of 9 and above with sensitivity 89.19%, specificity 85.71% and area under the curve of 0.910 is suitable for screening GAD among adults living with HIV. As a result, at this cut-off score, GAD-7 can be used to screen GAD among PLHIV.

Recommendation

We recommend that healthcare providers use this tool to screen anxiety symptoms among people living with HIV/AIDS. In addition to this, researchers interested in these topics are encouraged to use the findings of this study for future research. Further research is needed to better understand the psychometric and case-finding capabilities of the GAD-7 screening tool, not only in PLHIV, but also in other patients. We did not include HIV clinical indicators such as viral load, CD4 count or ART regimen, and future studies should incorporate these variables to better understand their influence on GAD-7 performance.
