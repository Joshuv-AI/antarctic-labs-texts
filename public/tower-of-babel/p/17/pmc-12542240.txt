The relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement among middle school students: the chain mediating role of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude
Authors: Yousong Hu, Yuqian Weng, Ping Ning, Li Han, Youxue Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement among middle school students. It further explores the chain mediating role of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude in this relationship.MethodsA cross-sectional survey, conducted in 2024, examined the relationships among psychological capital, adolescent internalizing problems, life attitude, and subjective academic achievement in a convenience sample of 1,019 middle school students from Chengdu, China. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and a chain mediation model.ResultsPsychological capital significantly predicted subjective academic achievement, in which adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude served as mediators. Specifically, higher psychological capital was associated with fewer adolescent internalizing problems, more positive life attitude, and, ultimately, improved subjective academic achievement.ConclusionThese findings offer novel theoretical insights, emphasizing the pivotal role of psychological capital and positive life attitude in educational interventions aimed at fostering student academic achievement.Keywords: Psychological capital, Subjective academic achievement, Middle school students, Adolescent internalizing problems, Life attitude

Introduction

In recent years, the rise of positive psychology has brought increased research attention to psychological capital (PsyCap) as a core construct for understanding individual development and well-being. PsyCap is defined as a positive psychological state of development characterized by four components: self-efficacy, optimism, hope, and resilience [1]. As an emerging psychological resource, PsyCap has demonstrated significant associations with mental health, learning motivation, and personal achievement across various educational settings [2, 3]. Prior research has also shown that psychological capital can buffer the effects of academic stress in adolescents and promote positive academic outcomes through enhanced self-efficacy and resilience [4]. Furthermore, studies have shown that a supportive school climate can enhance students’ psychological resources and academic outcomes, as evidenced in both Western and non-Western contexts [5].

Middle school adolescence constitutes a pivotal developmental juncture marked by accelerated emotional, cognitive, and social transformations. This period renders students particularly vulnerable to academic stressors while concurrently fostering the dynamic formation of their academic self-concepts [6, 7]. Subjective academic achievement, referring to students’ self-evaluations of their learning abilities and performance, is essential for shaping motivation, well-being, and long-term academic trajectories [8, 9]. In the Chinese educational context, where students face high levels of academic pressure and competitive tracking, understanding the psychological mechanisms that influence subjective academic achievement is of paramount and immediate importance.

Although prior studies have demonstrated that psychological capital is positively associated with academic outcomes [10, 11], most have examined either adolescent internalizing problems (e.g., anxiety, depression) [12, 13] or life attitude [14] as separate mediators in the PsyCap–achievement relationship. The chain mediating mechanism involving both adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude remains untested, particularly in the context of Chinese middle school students facing intense academic pressures [15]. This omission limits our understanding of the sequential psychological processes through which PsyCap may influence subjective academic achievement and constrains the design of integrated intervention strategies.

Hence, this study addresses the research gap by focusing on the interplay of these variables in a Chinese adolescent population, offering novel insights into educational and mental health interventions. Specifically, drawing upon Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory, self-determination theory, and Bandura’s social learning theory, this study proposes a chain mediation model, seeking to examine the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement, specifically exploring the mediating roles of adolescent internalizing problems (anxiety and depression) and life attitude.

Literature review

The relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement

Subjective academic achievement refers to students’ self-assessment and subjective perceptions of their academic performance. Unlike objective academic achievement, subjective academic achievement emphasizes students’ subjective cognition and emotional responses to their learning abilities and academic outcomes. This self-assessment includes not only academic success or failure but also encompasses students’ overall perception and satisfaction with their academic abilities [16]. The various dimensions of psychological capital significantly enhance subjective academic achievement by fostering students’ academic self-confidence and motivation [17]. For example, high self-efficacy strengthens students’ belief in their ability to complete academic tasks, thereby enhancing their academic performance. Resilience enables students to maintain a positive coping attitude when encountering academic challenges [18, 19]. Hope and optimism instill motivation and confidence in students, empowering them to achieve their academic goals [1].

Furthermore, psychological capital acts as a mediator between positive emotions and academic performance, facilitating the translation of positive emotional states into improved academic outcomes [20]. Additionally, psychological capital plays a pivotal role for students with learning disabilities by enhancing their subjective well-being and facilitating academic adjustment, thereby helping them overcome unique challenges in their learning environments [21]. These studies suggest that students with higher psychological capital are more likely to employ positive coping strategies when encountering academic challenges, thereby achieving better academic outcomes. The overarching role of psychological capital in facilitating academic adjustment among university students underscores its importance as a critical resource for enhancing their success and adaptability in academic settings [22].

The role of adolescent internalizing problems

Previous studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between elevated psychological capital and academic achievement [20, 23]. Additionally, psychological capital is inversely correlated with behavioral problems, including internalizing issues [24]. Internalizing problems are emotional and affective disturbances that can manifest as anxiety, depression, and withdrawal [25]. Adolescents struggling to cope with academic, interpersonal, and family challenges are more likely to develop internalizing problems [12], with those possessing lower psychological capital being particularly vulnerable. Moreover, psychological capital plays a pivotal role in enhancing mental health by buffering against emotional distress through mechanisms like enhanced social support and subjective well-being [26, 27]. For instance, adolescents with elevated psychological capital derive stronger online social support and an enhanced sense of belonging, collectively mitigating internalizing issues [26, 27].

Furthermore, studies indicate that elevated psychological capital can mitigate fear [28]. Psychological distress and depressive symptoms during adolescence exert a significant influence on educational achievement [29, 30]. Specifically, separation anxiety in childhood can have a sustained negative impact on academic outcomes, manifesting in internalizing behaviors, reduced academic achievement, and adverse physical health effects [31]. Additionally, internalizing and externalizing mental health issues among adolescents significantly influence educational outcomes for both boys and girls, with mental health problems strongly associated with levels of educational attainment [32]. The effect of stress on psychological capital is particularly significant, as chronic stress undermines mental health and depletes psychological resources, thereby impairing academic performance [33, 34]. However, it is also important to note that moderate levels of stress, when perceived as challenge stressors, may serve as motivational forces that encourage students to engage in goal-directed efforts. Recent research has shown that short-term stress, under specific conditions, can enhance academic persistence and focus [33].

Finally, depression significantly predicts lower academic achievement among rural children [35]. As a form of internalizing problem in adolescents, depression may also diminish subjective academic achievement by undermining psychological capital. The role of social support is crucial, as it moderates the relationship between psychological capital and academic success by enhancing academic adjustment and promoting emotional stability [36].

The impact of life attitude

According to Yuan et al. life attitude refers to an individual’s overall perspective on life’s meaning, goals, and values, along with the psychological and emotional responses exhibited while pursuing these goals [37]. It reflects how individuals perceive and manage their life experiences and may directly may contribute to their life satisfaction and well-being [38]. Research indicates that the positive traits within psychological capital, such as hope and optimism, encourage individuals to confront life challenges with a positive mindset and believe in their ability to achieve life goals [1]. Similarly, studies suggest that applying psychological capital across diverse environments helps individuals adapt and thrive, reinforcing its critical role in shaping life attitude [39].

Resilience enables individuals to sustain positive emotional responses during adversity, thereby cultivating a more optimistic life attitude [40]. Therefore, psychological capital directly enhances psychological adaptability and indirectly improves quality of life and subjective well-being by cultivating a positive life attitude. Furthermore, recent research suggests that a positive life attitude, characterized by a strong sense of meaning in life and psychological resilience, plays a crucial role in buffering the effects of academic stress and school burnout among adolescents, ultimately contributing to their academic adaptation and success [41].

Self-determination theory emphasizes the importance of autonomy and fulfilling autonomous needs, proposing that individuals integrate various aspects of the self to form a coherent whole within society [42]. This theory posits that students shape their life attitude through participation in decision-making, goal setting, and self-directed learning. Positive psychological capital shapes subjective academic achievement by cultivating a positive mindset. According to triadic reciprocal determinism within social learning theory, behavior, cognition, and environment are interdependent and mutually influential. Environmental factors supply positive psychological resources that shape students’ cognitive processes, thereby affecting their assessment of subjective academic achievement [43].

Mediating effect of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude

Lower psychological capital, by influencing adolescents’ internalizing problems, can increase their susceptibility to internal emotional issues such as anxiety and depression when encountering stress and challenges [13]. These internalizing problems may trigger negative emotional responses in academics, family, and interpersonal relationships, thereby diminishing students’ ability to cope with challenges [30]. Additionally, stress and interpersonal conflicts exacerbate these negative emotional states, further amplifying their impact on psychological health and academic outcomes [34]. These emotional responses directly affect students’ mental health and profoundly influence their life attitude, often causing feelings of helplessness and defeat when encountering life’s difficulties [35]. This negative emotional state and attitude further shape students’ perceptions of life goals and meaning, resulting in diminished motivation to set and achieve academic goals, which ultimately affects their subjective academic achievement.

Based on existing research, the effect of psychological capital on subjective academic achievement may be indirect, primarily mediated by internalizing problems and life attitude. Specifically, higher levels of psychological capital—which encompasses self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism—enhance adolescents’ emotional regulation and perceived social support, thereby reducing internalizing symptoms such as anxiety, depression, and withdrawal [26, 27]. This reduction in internalizing problems facilitates the development of a more positive life attitude, characterized by optimism, self-worth, and a sense of purpose, which in turn contributes to greater subjective academic achievement by reinforcing students’ confidence and motivation toward academic goals [44, 45].

Furthermore, human, social, and psychological capital collectively promote academic success by enhancing students’ capacity to cope with challenges and mobilize supportive resources [46]. This mechanism is consistent with the triadic reciprocal model of social learning theory, which posits that behavior, cognition, and environment interact dynamically to shape adolescents’ academic and emotional development [13, 47]. Within this theoretical framework, psychological capital functions as a core cognitive resource, facilitating adaptive cognitive appraisals and mitigating the effects of academic stress and peer pressure through future-oriented thinking and resilience. Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in an integrated framework that draws on three complementary theories.

First, Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions posits that positive psychological resources—such as self-efficacy, hope, and optimism—expand individuals’ thought–action repertoires and build enduring personal resources [48]. In the school context, these resources enable students to cope with academic challenges, regulate emotions, and envision achievable learning goals.

Second, Self-Determination Theory explains how psychological capital fulfills basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, thereby strengthening intrinsic motivation and fostering a purposeful life attitude [42]. Students with higher psychological capital are more likely to engage in self-directed learning, goal setting, and adaptive coping, which contribute to positive perceptions of their academic abilities.

Third, Bandura’s Social Learning Theory, particularly the triadic reciprocal determinism model, highlights the dynamic interactions between personal factors (e.g., psychological capital), behaviors (e.g., academic engagement), and environmental conditions (e.g., supportive school climate) [13, 47]. Within this framework, psychological capital shapes how students interpret stressors, reduces vulnerability to internalizing problems such as anxiety and depression, and promotes adaptive cognitive appraisals.

Integrating these perspectives, we propose a sequential mediation model/framework: psychological capital reduces adolescent internalizing problems, which in turn fosters a more positive life attitude, ultimately enhancing subjective academic achievement. This pathway reflects a motivational–emotional–cognitive process in which emotional regulation precedes and supports the development of enduring life perspectives that are conducive to academic success.

Given the cross-sectional design of this study, the proposed mediation model is exploratory and based on theoretical assumptions rather than causal inference. While our theoretical framework suggests a directional process, the relationships identified should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal pathways, warranting confirmation in longitudinal or experimental research.

Hypotheses of the study

Based on the research gap, this study aims to investigate the impact of psychological capital on subjective academic achievement, as well as the mediating roles of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude. The study proposes the following four hypotheses (see Fig. 1):

Hypothesis 1

Psychological capital is positively associated with subjective academic achievement among middle school students.

Hypothesis 2

Adolescent internalizing problems is expected to serve as a potential mediator in the association between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement.

Hypothesis 3

Life attitude is expected to serve as a potential mediator in the association between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement.

Hypothesis 4

Psychological capital may be related to subjective academic achievement through a potential chain mediation pathway involving adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude.

Method

Participants

A convenience sample of 1,044 students (Grades 7–9) was recruited from three public schools in Chengdu, China. These schools, with approximate enrollments of 2,000, 1,200, and 3,000 students respectively, were selected through pre-existing academic partnerships with local education authorities. Of five schools invited via official correspondence facilitated by the municipal education bureau, three consented to participate, while two declined due to scheduling constraints. Data collection took place over two weeks in May 2024 during the regular school term to minimize disruption and reflect typical academic conditions.

Participants were selected based on availability and willingness, with assistance from school administrators who identified intact classes from each grade level for group-based testing during self-study periods. Standardized instructions were provided, and each class served as a testing unit. After excluding responses with over 10% missing data or patterned answers indicating inattentiveness, 1,019 valid questionnaires were retained (response rate = 97.6%). The final sample consisted of 52.6% male and 47.4% female students, with a mean age of 14.16 years (SD = 0.89; age range: 13–16). The grade distribution was as follows: Grade 7(20.80%), Grade 8( 44.70%), and Grade 9 (34.40%). Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and conducted in compliance with institutional ethical guidelines for research involving minors. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Academic Ethics Review Committee of Chengdu Normal University (CDNU2024016).

Measures

Positive Psycap questionnaire

The Positive Psycap Questionnaire (PPQ) is an instrument utilized to assess psychological capital among Chinese adolescents [49]. Participants rate items on a 7-point Likert scale, where higher scores reflected greater positive psychological capacities. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four subscales—self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism—were 0.86, 0.70, 0.80, and 0.83, respectively, revealing varying levels of reliability. The overall Cronbach’s α for the entire questionnaire was 0.91, reflecting high internal consistency. Although the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the resilience subscale (α = 0.70) was slightly below the conventional threshold, item-total correlation analysis indicated that removing any individual item would not substantially improve the overall reliability. Therefore, the original structure of the subscale was retained in alignment with the validated version of the questionnaire used in prior studies.

Life attitude questionnaire for middle school students

The Life Attitude Questionnaire for Chinese College Students, originally developed by Yuan et al. [37], was adapted for use with adolescents in this study. To ensure age-appropriateness and content clarity, several items were revised in wording and contextual examples to better reflect the cognitive, emotional, and developmental characteristics of adolescents. These revisions were made based on expert consensus from three specialists in developmental and educational psychology. A pilot test involving 98 middle school students was conducted to evaluate the clarity, readability, and relevance of the adapted items.

The final version of the revised Life Attitude Scale consists of 17 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 6 = “strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating a more positive life attitude. The scale includes three subdimensions: life belief (Items A1–A6), life experience (A7–A12), and life will (A13–A17). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using Mplus 8.0 supported the three-factor structure, showing acceptable fit: χ²(116) = 585.19, df = 116, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.063 (90% CI [0.058, 0.068]) and SRMR = 0.048. Although the chi-square was significant, other indices indicated a good model fit. The scale also demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.90 (life belief), 0.83 (life experience), 0.75 (life motivation), and 0.92 for the total scale, supporting its reliability and construct validity among middle school students.

Adolescent internalizing problems questionnaire

This study employed the Chinese version of the Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scale (DASS) to measure internalizing problems in adolescents. The scale includes three sub-dimensions—stress, anxiety, and depression—and consists of 21 items. It has demonstrated robust psychometric properties in Chinese adolescent populations [25]. Participants rated their experiences over the past week on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “never,” 1 = “sometimes,” 2 = “often,” 3 = “always”). In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the stress, anxiety, and depression subscales were 0.85, 0.85, and 0.87, respectively, indicating high reliability. The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.95, reflecting excellent internal consistency.

Subjective academic achievement questionnaire

In this study, subjective academic achievement was assessed through students’ self-evaluation of their performance in three core subjects: Chinese, Mathematics, and English. Participants rated statements such as “I believe my performance in Mathematics is good” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “very poor” to 5 = “very good”). The average of the three items was calculated, with higher scores indicating more positive self-perceived academic performance.

The questionnaire was adapted from Sticca et al. [50], who found a robust correlations between self-reported and actual grades, supporting the validity of self-evaluations despite minor over-reporting. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α of the questionnaire was 0.72, indicating moderate internal consistency. Chinese, Mathematics, and English were selected due to their core status in the national curriculum and significant weighting in high-stakes assessments. Other subjects were excluded to mitigate participant burden, representing a limitation noted for future research.

Covariates

In addition to gender and age, three background variables were included as covariates in the mediation analyses to control for potential confounding effects. Parental Highest Educational Attainment (PHE) was assessed using a 6-point ordinal scale: 1 = primary school or below, 2 = junior middle school, 3 = senior high school, 4 = junior college (associate degree), 5 = bachelor’s degree, and 6 = master’s degree or above. Household Income (HHI) was measured as the average monthly household income (in RMB) on a 6-point scale: 1 = < ¥5,000, 2 = ¥5,001–8,000, 3 = ¥8,001–12,000, 4 = ¥12,001–15,000, 5 = ¥15,001–20,000, and 6 = > ¥20,000. Prior Academic Achievement (PAA) was computed as the mean of last-semester final exam grades in Chinese, Mathematics, and English, with higher scores indicating better prior performance. These covariates were selected because prior research has shown that parental education, household income, and previous academic achievement are associated with both students’ life attitude and academic performance [51, 52].

Data processing and analysis

Data were processed using SPSS 26.0 and the SPSS macro program PROCESS 3.4.1 developed by Hayes [53], and analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics were first calculated. Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. The Process macro (Model 6) was subsequently used to examine the independent mediating effects of life attitude and adolescent internalizing problems on the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement, as well as the chain mediation effect. Before model testing, all research variables were standardized.

Results

Common method bias test

Given all data in this study were self-reported, potential common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test [54]. Results revealed 11 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 under unrotated conditions, with the first factor accounting for 26.98% of the variance. This result falls below the 40% threshold, indicating that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study.

In addition to the statistical test, procedural controls were implemented to reduce the potential for common method bias. All participants completed the questionnaires anonymously and were informed that there were no right or wrong answers. They were encouraged to respond honestly and independently. Furthermore, the order of items across different constructs was randomized to minimize response consistency bias.

Mediating effect analysis of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude

The correlation analysis results satisfied the statistical prerequisites for conducting further mediating effect tests on adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude [55]. This study utilized the PROCESS macro in SPSS to perform a chain mediation effect analysis. For the mediation effect analysis, Model 6 of the chain mediation model was applied with 5000 bootstrap samples. Psychological capital served as the independent variable, subjective academic achievement as the dependent variable, and age, gender, parental highest educational attainment (PHE), household income (HHI), and prior academic achievement (last-semester final exam average) as control variables. Adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude were designated as the first and second mediating variables in the chain mediation path, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 2, within the chain mediation model, psychological capital is positively associated with subjective academic achievement (β = 0.18, t = 4.85, p < 0.001), negatively predicts adolescent internalizing problems (β = -0.38, t = -12.12, p < 0.001), and positively predicts life attitude (β = 0.65, t = 27.16, p < 0.001). Adolescent internalizing problems was not significantly related to subjective academic achievement (β = 0.001, t = 0.03, p = 0.980) but was negatively associated with life attitude (β = −0.10, t = − 4.46, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study examined the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement, aiming to uncover the chain mediating role of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude in this relationship. The findings suggested that psychological capital not only directly predicts subjective academic achievement but also exerts an indirect influence via the chain mediation of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude. Psychological capital, adolescent internalizing problems, and life attitude are significant factors in shaping the subjective academic achievement of middle school students. Therefore, it is vital to enhance psychological capital, mitigate adolescent internalizing problems, and foster a positive life attitude in adolescents, which could lead to their improved subjective academic achievement. The discussion was as follows.

Psychological capital and subjective academic achievement

The results support Research Hypothesis 1, indicating that psychological capital is positively associated with subjective academic achievement among middle school students. This finding aligns with prior research, which identifies psychological capital—comprising self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism—as a key resource for maintaining positive mental states and coping with academic challenges [1]. Self-efficacy enhances confidence, resilience promotes persistence, and hope and optimism provide emotional support in academic contexts.

Psychological capital also mediates the link between positive emotions and academic performance [20], and plays a vital role in the academic adjustment of students, including those with learning disabilities [21]. It contributes to academic adaptability and interacts with human and social capital to influence academic and career outcomes [22, 46]. Moreover, it helps mitigate negative emotions such as anxiety and depression [56], and strengthens social support that facilitates academic success [36]. Overall, psychological capital serves as a critical factor in enhancing students’ academic performance and should be fostered through educational interventions.

The mediating role of adolescent internalizing problems

Although previous studies have suggested that adolescent internalizing problems—such as anxiety, depression, and emotional withdrawal—may impair academic performance by reducing students’ motivation and concentration [25], the present study did not find a statistically significant mediating effect of adolescent internalizing problems in the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement. This result indicates that while psychological capital is negatively associated with internalizing symptoms [34], the pathway from internalizing problems to academic self-evaluation may be more complex or context-dependent.

One possible explanation is that the buffering effects of psychological capital, such as enhanced resilience, self-efficacy, and optimism, may not directly translate into improved academic self-perceptions through internalizing symptoms alone [3, 35]. Additionally, while psychological capital has been found to support emotional adjustment and reduce internalizing distress in adolescents [26, 27], these improvements in emotional well-being may require a longer period or additional contextual supports (e.g., family or school climate) to influence academic outcomes meaningfully.

Therefore, the findings do not support Hypothesis 2 but instead suggest that the influence of psychological capital on subjective academic achievement is more likely to operate through other psychological pathways—such as life attitude—or potentially through interactive effects not captured in the current model. Future studies may consider using longitudinal or multilevel approaches to examine whether internalizing problems exert delayed or context-specific effects on academic self-perceptions.

The mediating role of life attitude

The results of this study confirm Research Hypothesis 3, which suggests that life attitude may mediate the relationship between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement. The findings suggest that psychological capital directly enhances psychological adaptability and indirectly improves adolescents’ academic performance by fostering a positive life attitude. Life attitude, reflecting an individual’s overall perspective on life’s meaning, goals, and values, shapes how one copes with challenges and stress [37]. Positive traits within psychological capital, such as self-efficacy, hope, and optimism, enable adolescents to develop a more positive outlook on life, fostering greater resilience and adaptability when confronting academic pressures [42, 57].

Moreover, research highlights how psychological capital interventions enhance mental well-being and cultivate positive attitudes, playing a vital role in managing academic stress and promoting success [39]. These effects are particularly pronounced in contexts where life attitude, shaped by environmental factors like school climate and collective self-esteem, interact with psychological capital to enhance academic outcomes [2]. Adolescents with a positive life attitude are more likely to set clear academic goals, sustain motivation, and believe in their ability to achieve these goals.

In conclusion, Research Hypothesis 3 is validated, underscoring that life attitude is a key mediator between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement. By integrating psychological traits with resilience and environmental factors, as observed in extreme sports participants, life attitude can significantly enhance academic performance through improved focus and adaptability [58]. These findings highlight the importance of fostering positive life attitude through educational interventions to advance academic success. Moreover, previous studies have shown that interventions aimed at enhancing psychological capital can significantly improve mental well-being and foster a positive life attitude, even under highly stressful conditions such as the COVID-19 pandemic [57]. These findings provide additional support for the integration of psychological capital development into school-based mental health and academic support programs.

The chain mediation model of adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude

The results of this study confirm Hypothesis 4, which posits that life attitude and adolescent internalizing problems serve as chain mediators between psychological capital and subjective academic achievement. Psychological capital, encompassing self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism, significantly enhances adolescents’ mental health and behavioral outcomes by mitigating internalizing problems and fostering a positive life attitude [1, 45]. Internalizing problems, such as anxiety and depression, disrupt adolescents’ ability to set and pursue academic goals; however, psychological capital mitigates these effects by enhancing self-regulation and alleviating emotional distress [44]. This dual pathway illustrates how psychological capital fosters resilience, alleviates stress, and cultivates a goal-oriented life attitude, ultimately enhancing subjective academic achievement.

Moreover, this study corroborates prior findings that internalizing problems negatively affect life attitude. Internalizing problems, such as depressive symptoms, provoke negative emotional reactions and cognitive distortions, undermining adolescents’ confidence and obscuring their perception of life goals [35]. This study extends prior research by demonstrating that mitigating internalizing problems through psychological capital improves life attitude and enhances academic outcomes. Environmental factors, such as stress, further complicate these relationships, underscoring the importance of addressing both psychological and situational contributors to academic success [34].

In conclusion, this study integrates classical theories and contemporary research, demonstrating that psychological capital mitigates internalizing problems and cultivates a positive life attitude, which collectively enhance subjective academic achievement. These findings align with the triadic reciprocal determinism model, which emphasizes the interaction of behavior, cognition, and environment in shaping adolescents’ academic outcomes [58]. This chain mediation model underscores the pivotal role of psychological capital in advancing academic success across multiple dimensions.

Limitations

Although this study offers valuable insights into the relationships among psychological capital, adolescent internalizing problems, life attitude, and subjective academic achievement, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which prevents the establishment of temporal ordering and limits causal inference. Consequently, the proposed chain mediation model should be interpreted as describing associations rather than definitive causal pathways. Secondly, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from only three public schools in one single city of China, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions and educational contexts in China. Thirdly, several important covariates—such as family socioeconomic status (SES), parental education, prior academic achievement, and school-level factors—were not collected, which may have introduced unmeasured confounding and biased the estimated associations.

Despite these limitations, this study offers a theoretical basis and empirical evidence for understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying subjective academic achievement during early adolescence. Future research could extend the current model to other student populations, such as those in vocational education or rural settings, and explore the impact of contextual or institutional factors.

Conclusion

This study examined the mechanisms underpinning subjective academic achievement among middle school students, highlighting the pivotal role of psychological capital. The findings revealed that psychological capital may positively contribute to academic success both directly and indirectly and particularly alleviates adolescent internalizing problems, such as anxiety and depression. In addition, psychological capital is correlated with students’ positive life attitude, which enhances psychological adaptability and academic motivation. More importantly, the study identifies adolescent internalizing problems and life attitude as key mediating factors in this relationship. These results suggest that schools, educators, and mental health practitioners should endeavour to cultivate students’ psychological capital, addressing internalizing problems and promoting a positive life attitude, to ultimately enhance adolescents’ academic performance and overall well-being.

Implications for future research

While this study provides valuable insights, future research can further advance the field in several ways. First, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to establish causal relationships between psychological capital, adolescent internalizing problems, life attitude, and academic outcomes. Second, expanding the sample to include students from different regions, age groups, or educational systems could improve generalizability. Third, future studies may explore additional mediating or moderating variables (e.g., school climate, parental support, socioeconomic status) that influence the link between psychological capital and academic achievement. These efforts would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological mechanisms underpinning academic success.

Given the high academic pressure and competitive tracking that characterize Chinese middle schools, our findings suggest several actionable recommendations tailored to this context. Schools could embed psychological capital training programs—targeting self-efficacy, resilience, hope, and optimism—into moral education classes, class meetings, or school-based mental health courses, with particular attention to Grade 9 students preparing for the high-stakes secondary school entrance examination through targeted group counseling and stress management initiatives. At the teacher level, homeroom and subject teachers can employ positive feedback, role modeling, and differentiated instruction to strengthen students’ academic self-confidence and positive life attitude, supported by training in the early identification and intervention of internalizing symptoms. At the family level, strengthening school–family collaboration through regular parent workshops can help caregivers recognize signs of internalizing distress and foster a positive outlook on life at home, particularly during exam preparation periods. At the policy level, educational authorities could incorporate the development of psychological capital and life attitude into school-based curricula, student well-being monitoring systems, and evaluation frameworks, ensuring these competencies are valued alongside academic performance and contributing to a more balanced and supportive educational environment for adolescent development in China.
