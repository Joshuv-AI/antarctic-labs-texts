Exploring Overlooked Mediators Linking Myocardial Infarction to Hematologic Malignancies
Authors: Parth Aphale, Shashank Dokania, Himanshu Shekhar
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Yoon et al,1 which demonstrated an increased risk of hematologic malignancies among patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) using a robust nationwide cohort. Although the study significantly advances our understanding of the cardio-oncology interface, certain overlooked areas merit further consideration to refine the biological plausibility and translational impact of these findings.

First, the role of cardiometabolic medications commonly prescribed post-AMI (eg, statins, beta-blockers, and antiplatelet agents) was not examined. Statins, for instance, have demonstrated both protective and adverse associations with hematologic malignancies. A Swedish registry study involving over 40,000 individuals found long-term statin use associated with a reduced risk of multiple myeloma but an increased risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma.2 Accounting for pharmacotherapy could provide mechanistic clarity beyond shared risk factors.

Second, the authors briefly referenced clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) but were unable to assess it directly. CHIP prevalence in individuals over 60 years can reach 10% to 20%, and it markedly increases risks of both cardiovascular events and hematologic cancers.3 Large-scale genomic datasets such as UK Biobank could complement claims data to disentangle CHIP-mediated overlap between AMI and malignancy.

Third, environmental and occupational exposures, important contributors in East Asia, were not evaluated. For example, benzene exposure is a well-established risk factor for acute myeloid leukemia, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been independently linked to both cardiovascular disease and hematologic malignancies.4 Inclusion of geospatial or occupational exposure variables could strengthen causal inference. Also, survivorship bias deserves attention. Patients with AMI who survive long enough for cancer to manifest may represent a biologically distinct subset. Contemporary registries show improved AMI survival rates exceeding 80% at 7 years post-event in older adults,5 which reshapes long-term cancer risk estimates. Analytical strategies adjusting for competing cardiovascular mortality could refine outcome interpretation.

In conclusion, Yoon et al1 provide valuable evidence supporting a bidirectional relationship between AMI and hematologic malignancies. Future studies integrating pharmacotherapy data, CHIP genotyping, and environmental exposures will be essential to establish mechanisms and guide targeted surveillance strategies.
