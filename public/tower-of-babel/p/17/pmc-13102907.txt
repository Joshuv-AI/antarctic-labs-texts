SYN-OCT:A synthetic dataset of ocular optical coherence tomography images from healthy and glaucoma eyes
Authors: Damon Wong, Ashish Jith Sreejith Kumar, Rachel S Chong, Monisha E Nongpiur, Rahat Husain, Tina Wong, Shamira Perera, Tin Aung, Bingyao Tan, Ching-Yu Cheng, Eranga N Vithana, Jacqueline Chua, Alina Popa Cherecheanu, Leopold Schmetterer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe SYN-OCT dataset contains a total of 200,000 synthetic cross-sectional circumpapillary optical coherence tomography (OCT) images, comprising of 100,000 images from a generative image model for glaucoma eyes and 100,000 from a generative image model for healthy normal eyes. The generative image models were developed using real OCT imaging data acquired from study participants at the Singapore Eye Research Institute, and were trained with the respective real images from glaucoma or healthy eyes. Structural characteristics of the synthetic data were validated and found to be comparable with the real data using an automated segmentation approach. These measurements are provided together with the synthetic images. We envision this dataset to be useful for the development or validation of deep learning applications for glaucoma analysis and detection, and as a dataset to study the synthetic generation of medical images and their usability. This dataset is publicly available.Subject terms: Medical imaging, Eye diseases

Background & Summary

With the increasing use of artificial intelligence, machine learning and other data-driven approaches in healthcare1,2, there is a rising need for data that can be used to develop, train and validate these models3. Similar to other domains, these approaches benefit from greater data availability that allows better models to be trained on more diverse data. More critically, enhancing data availability enables an evaluation of the generalizability of data-driven algorithms in different settings. However, due to privacy concerns and associated regulatory frameworks, there is limited accessibility and availability of healthcare data compared to other domains4. Traditional approaches of healthcare data sharing necessitate the removal of sensitive personal data, often a manual and time-consuming process that is not practically scalable with the need for larger datasets. Several studies have been conducted on federated learning5, which allows model development through distributed learning without transfer of data. However, this can require significant infrastructural investments and synchronization of data access networks across healthcare sites and institutions.

Recent advances in generative modelling has raised the prospect of these approaches as a potential way to address the limited availability of data, particularly in sensitive domains such as healthcare5,6 and finance7. Generative models are trained on real data, and can output synthetic data that embodies characteristics and structures of the originating data, while excluding sensitive information8. Several studies have evaluated the potential of tabular synthetic data, including their use as a proxy for real data in clinical trials9,10. With the rapid development of deep generative image models that are able output highly realistic images11, the adaptation of these models for the generation of medical images containing clinically relevant characteristics is increasingly being explored12. In particular, the generation of synthetic medical images without identifiable personal features may help to address privacy barriers in data sharing, enabling new paradigms of collaborative research. Although several studies have evaluated the use of images from deep generative models in healthcare13, including ophthalmology14–16, the availability of synthetic medical images for the research community is still limited.

In this work, we present a dataset of 200,000 circumpapillary cross-sectional ophthalmic OCT images that were synthetically generated using real images acquired from human participants. The dataset is equally divided into a subset of 100,000 images from a generative glaucoma model, and a subset of 100,000 images from a generative normal control model. Each model was separately developed using the real images from the respective glaucoma or healthy participants. To evaluate the structural characteristics of the synthetic images, we applied a deep-learning based segmentation model to extract the retinal nerve fiber layer thickness profile and compared the obtained measurements with the measurements from the real images. This dataset of synthetic ophthalmic OCT images represents a significant advance in the availability of synthetic data embodying normal and pathological characteristics, particularly in ophthalmology.

Methods

Participants

Images from real participants were retrospectively acquired from studies conducted at the Singapore Eye Research Institute from 2012 to 2021. Normal controls without ocular conditions such as glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration or diabetic retinopathy were included from the Singapore Chinese Eye Study (SCES) cohort17 in the Singapore Epidemiology of Eye Disease (SEED) study, a population-based study of adults aged 40 or older conducted in Singapore. Participants with glaucoma were enrolled from four separate studies18,19, and were included if they had a clinical diagnosis of glaucoma, based on the presence of glaucomatous optic neuropathy (defined as loss of neuroretinal rim with a vertical cup-to-disc ratio of >0.7 or an inter-eye asymmetry of > 0.2 and/or notching attributable to glaucoma) with compatible visual field (VF) loss, open angles on gonioscopy, and absence of secondary causes of glaucomatous optic neuropathy. Written informed consent was obtained for participation in the originating studies18,19. For inclusion into this study, all identifiable information was removed and the data was rendered non-identifiable. The study received institutional approval from the Singapore Eye Research Institute for the use of non-identifiable data in this project, following organizational guidelines for projects not requiring ethics review.

To minimize the effect of ethnic differences20, only participants of Chinese ethnicity were included. The research was approved by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (2025-0193) and performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, which included imaging of the optic nerve head using a commercial spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) system (Cirrus HD-OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditech, USA) with an operating central wavelength of 840 nm. Scan were excluded if the signal strength was below 6 or contained substantial movement artefacts. From each acquired 6 mm × 6 mm OCT scan, a circumpapillary cross-sectional image was obtained using a 3.46 mm circle centered on the optic nerve head.

Synthetic image generation

An adversarial training methodology was used for model development. In generative adversarial networks (GAN), which are based on game theory, two networks are trained in parallel, with a generator network focused on generating realistic images, and a discriminator network which tries to determine or classify if an image is real or synthetic. As both models are updated and improved in tandem, increasingly realistic images are generated. We used a progressive generative adversarial network (PGAN) architecture, which initiates image generation from a lower resolution and progressively increases image resolution as the training progresses, allowing a more stable and efficient image generation process. In the PGAN methodology, compared to other conventional GAN frameworks, convolutional layers are added to the generator and discriminator sub-networks during training. This enables the inclusion of coarse image structure to finer details as training progresses, and was first demonstrated for the generation of high quality photo-realistic faces. Further details of the PGAN methodology can be found the report by Karras et al.21. For our study, we used the implementation of PGAN from the publicly accessible pytorch GAN_zoo repository. (https://github.com/facebookresearch/pytorch_GAN_zoo). Two generative models were separately developed for the generation of circumpapillary cross-section OCT images, one for normal eyes and one for glaucoma eyes, using the normal and glaucoma images. The dimensions of each image are 188 pixels × 256 pixels (width × height), corresponding to approximately 10.86 mm × 2.00 mm. The models were developed on a Nvidia DGX Workstation equipped with 4 V100 GPUs. Both models used the Adam optimizer with WGAN-GP loss, at a learning rate set at 0.001 and batch size of 16. Training was run for approximately 624,000 iterations.

Technical Validation

In total, 990 eyes from 693 healthy normal controls and 862 eyes from 618 glaucoma patients were included in the dataset. Demographic characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Compared to the normal controls, participants with glaucoma were older and had more positive refractive errors, and had a higher proportion of males.

We generated a total of 200,000 synthetic circumpapillary OCT images using the trained generative models—100,000 from the model trained on healthy eyes, and 100,000 from the model trained on glaucoma eyes. Images from each generative model were consecutively generated. The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), which is a standard metric to evaluate the quality of synthetic images23, was calculated for the synthetic images for each model against the real training data, and FID scores of 15.84 for the normal model and 12.02 for the glaucoma model were obtained. Human level evaluation of the synthetic images was independently performed by two senior clinicians on a total of 200 image samples, comprised of 50 real healthy, 50 real glaucoma, 50 synthetic healthy and 50 synthetic glaucoma images, which were randomly shuffled. When evaluated for clinical image quality, the human assessors agreed on 178 of the image samples, with 93 of the synthetic images assessed to be of sufficient quality by both experts. Further, when tasked to identify if an image was real or synthetic, the average accuracy between the two experts was 51.6%, suggesting that the generated images had a high degree of realism with real images. Detailed results of the human evaluation can be found in a prior publication14.

In addition, as characteristics of the circumpapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) from the cross-sectional OCT scans are routinely used in clinic for glaucoma assessment18, the RNFL thickness from the real and synthetic data were compared. We applied a pre-trained RNFL segmentation model based on the established U-Net architecture24 to extract the RNFL thickness profiles from both real and synthetic images14,25. This segmentation model was previously reported to have achieved a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.90 with manually labelled segmented OCT data26. No re-training or fine-tuning of the segmentation model was performed with the synthetic images. Distributions of the RNFL profiles for both real and synthetic datasets are presented in Fig. 2. Differences between the mean RNFL profiles of the real and synthetic datasets were not significant for the normal (P = 0.764) and glaucoma (P = 0.242) images.

Distribution of retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (RNFL) profiles in the glaucoma (blue) and normal (red) images in the (a) real and (b) synthetic datasets. RNFL positions are presented in a TSNIT sequence (T = Temporal, S = Superior, N = Nasal, I = Inferior) with respect to the optic nerve head.

Global RNFL thicknesses were obtained by calculating the mean of the RNFL thickness profile for each image. Distributions of the normal and glaucoma images for the real and synthetic datasets are shown in Fig. 3. For the images obtained from participants, the global RNFL thickness was significantly different (independent t-test, P < 0.001) between glaucoma (76.6 ± 14.5 µm) and normal (103.8 ± 11.5 µm). In the synthetic images, the normal dataset had a mean RNFL thickness of 104.7 ± 11.6 µm, while the glaucoma dataset had mean RNFL thickness of 78.2 ± 13.4 µm. RNFL thicknesses between the synthetic normal and synthetic glaucoma eyes were also significantly different (independent t-test, P < .001).

Distribution of the global retinal nerve fiber layer thicknesses in the glaucoma (blue) and normal (red) images in the (a) real and (b) synthetic datasets.

We also compared the discrimination between normal and glaucoma images in the datasets using area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis via the RNFL measurements. The ROC curves are presented in Fig. 4. In addition, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was also calculated. For the data from the real images dataset, the AUC was 0.927 (95% CI: 0.915–0.934). In the synthetic dataset, the AUC was 0.930 (95% CI: 0.929–0.931). The difference between the AUCs for the real and synthetic images was not found to be significant (DeLong’s test, P = 0.571).

Receiver operating characteristic curves for the detection of glaucoma images in the real and synthetic image datasets. Differences in AUC between the two curves were not significant (P = 0.571).

To evaluate the diversity of the generated synthetic images, we used the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), a metric that has been widely used to evaluate the similarity between two images27. SSIM values range from 0 to 1, with a maximum value of 1 indicating equivalence between a test image and a reference image and a minimum value of 0 indicating no similarity. Using the synthetic images generated from each model, we calculated the SSIM value for each synthetic image against the real training images used during model development. Distributions of the mean SSIM values are presented in Fig. 5. The median values were 0.280 [Inter-quartile range (IQR): 0.259–0.300] and 0.275 [IQR: 0.255–0.295] for the synthetic normal and synthetic glaucoma images respectively, demonstrating that the generated synthetic images were not highly similar to the acquired real OCT images.

Distribution of the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) for the synthetic (a) normal and (b) synthetic glaucoma images, with respect to the real data for the respective datasets.

To further evaluate the usability of the synthetic images for developing data-driven detection models, two deep-learning models were trained to classify OCT images as healthy or glaucoma. One glaucoma detection model was trained using only real images from the generative model training data (train-real). In the other, only synthetic images were used model (train-synthetic). The two models were evaluated using an internal test set of 70 glaucoma images and 70 healthy images from the Singapore Eye Research Institute, and an external test set of 150 healthy images and 150 glaucoma images from the Carol Davila University of Medicine and Pharmacy in Romania, Bucharest. The same RNFL segmentation model was applied to the real internal and external test set images and an average global RNFL thickness was used as a clinical baseline for glaucoma detection, which was assessed AUC analysis. For the RNFL measurements, the AUC for glaucoma detection was 0.92 and 0.83 in the internal and external test sets respectively. With the train-real model, the AUC was 0.96 for the internal test set and 0.84 for the external test set. When the train-synthetic model was applied, the AUC was 0.97 in the internal test set and 0.90 for the external test set. Using Delong’s test, differences in AUC between the train-real and train-synthetic models for the internal test set were not significant (P = 0.49) but were significant for the external test set (P < 0.05). Further details, including experimental settings and model parameters are described in a prior publication14.
