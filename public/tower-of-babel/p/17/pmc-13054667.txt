Non‐Dipping Blood Pressure and Reduced Heart Rate Deceleration Capacities Are Associated With Malnutrition in Patients With Gastrointestinal Cancer
Authors: Congshui Wang, Yushan Chen, Di Zhang, Yangying Feng, Chun Gui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbnormal blood pressure rhythm and decreased heart rate variability are both associated with poor prognosis. However, there are few studies on the relationship between these factors and malnutrition. This study aimed to investigate the characteristics of blood pressure and heart rate variability in malnourished individuals with gastrointestinal cancer. We collected ambulatory blood pressure and heart rate variability data from 662 patients with gastrointestinal tumors at Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital in 2023. The Controlling Nutritional Status index (CONUT) was used as a malnutrition indicator, and CONUT < 2 was defined as malnutrition. The characteristics of blood pressure and heart rate variability and their relationship with malnutrition were analyzed. A significant association was found between malnutrition and blood pressure dipping patterns (p = 0.003). Deceleration capacity (DC) [OR (95% CI): 0.724 (0.628–0.835), p < 0.001] and NPDR [OR (95% CI): 0.951 (0.904–1.001), p = 0.054] was negatively correlated with malnutrition, nighttime average heart rate (nighttime‐HR) [OR (95% CI): 1.089 (1.031–1.151), p = 0.002] was positively correlated with malnutrition. DC achieved the highest AUC for malnutrition prediction in the ROC analysis (0.706). Our research indicates that Patients with malnourished gastrointestinal tumors exhibited abnormal circadian rhythms, including blunted nocturnal blood pressure dipping, attenuated heart rate deceleration, and elevated nighttime heart rate. This pattern reflects autonomic dysfunction characterized by impaired vagal activity.

Introduction

Patients with gastrointestinal tumors have the highest incidence of malnutrition among all cancers. According to a survey report on the nutritional status of patients with common malignant tumors in China [1], all gastrointestinal tumors have the poorest nutritional status. Nutritional status plays a critical role in determining postoperative recovery, treatment response, and survival [2, 3, 4].

Changes in dipping patterns and decreases in heart rate variability are important indicators that affect the overall prognosis of patients [5]. Blood pressure variability is regulated by the autonomic nervous system [6], and heart rate variability is an important non‐invasive index of autonomic nervous system function [7]. Malnutrition is associated with lower blood pressure in both the elderly and healthy adolescents [8, 9]. A study on hypotension and cisplatin chemotherapy toxicity [10] indicates that there is a significant correlation between hypotension and low albumin levels. The incidence of hypotension is higher in the group with low albuminemia. Hypotension can increase mortality in patients [11]. Hypotension is as harmful as hypertension, but it is easily ignored in clinical practice. For accurate diagnosis and to avoid misdiagnosis, 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring is the recommended standard [12]. Malnutrition affects the characteristics of blood pressure dipping patterns [13]. Hypertension with non‐dipper blood pressure patterns is also associated with reduced heart rate variability [14, 15]. However, the overall cardiovascular characteristics of both blood pressure and heart rate changes in patients with gastrointestinal tumors are not clear, and the internal relationship between them and nutritional status needs to be revealed.

The CONUT score is a nutritional status score. The CONUT score has been shown to be a more responsive prognostic indicator for colorectal cancer patients than the PNI and other nutritional scores [16]. This study aims to explore the correlation between malnutrition and alterations in blood pressure and heart rate variability by integrating and analyzing clinical data from gastrointestinal cancer patients treated in 2023. These findings are expected to provide information for the study of the potential mechanisms underlying the impact of malnutrition on the prognosis of cancer patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We enrolled 971 patients from the Gastrointestinal Oncology Department between January and December 2023. All patients underwent 24‐h ambulatory monitoring of blood pressure and electrocardiogram. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital (Approval No KY2025656). We excluded patients for the following reasons: repeated measurements (n = 255), incomplete or inadequate data (n = 17), other tumor types (n = 11), non‐tumor diagnoses (n = 19), and missing biochemical data (n = 7). Finally, 662 patients were included in the study (Figure 1).

Flowchart of the participants.

Malnutrition Evaluation

The CONUT index was calculated as the sum of scores for serum albumin, total cholesterol, and peripheral lymphocyte count. According to the CONUT index, the patients were divided into a malnutrition group (CONUT ≥ 2) and a non‐malnutrition group (CONUT < 2).

Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring

Ambulatory blood pressure was monitored using a device and analysis system (Diem Co.). The cuff was placed on the non‐dominant arm, with cuff size selected appropriately for each patient. At least 80% of the expected 24‐h readings should be valid, and unqualified ambulatory blood pressure data should be deleted. The measurement should be performed every 30 min during the day (8:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m.) and every 60 min at night (10:00 p.m. to 8:00 a.m.). Blood pressure and heart rate data were obtained. The 24‐h mean systolic blood pressure (24h‐SBP), 24‐h mean diastolic blood pressure (24h‐DBP), 24‐h mean pulse pressure (24h‐MBP), daytime mean systolic blood pressure(daytime‐SBP), daytime mean diastolic blood pressure (daytime‐DBP), daytime mean pulse pressure (daytime‐MBP), nighttime mean systolic blood pressure (nighttime‐SBP), nighttime mean diastolic blood pressure (nighttime‐DBP), nighttime mean pulse pressure (nighttime‐MBP), 24‐h mean heart rate (24h‐HR), daytime mean heart rate (daytime‐HR), nighttime mean heart rate (nighttime‐HR), morning blood pressure surge (MBPS) were calculated by the software. Nocturnal SBP decline = (daytime mean SBP − nighttime mean SBP) × 100%. According to the Nocturnal SBP decline, the blood pressure pattern was classified into the dipper group (nocturnal SBP decline between 10% and 20%), the non‐dipper group (Nocturnal SBP decline <10% and ≥0%), and the reverse dipper group (Nocturnal SBP decline <0%).

24‐h Holter

A 12‐lead dynamic electrocardiogram recorder (Dim Company) was used for continuous 24‐h ECG monitoring. After monitoring, the data were imported into the supporting analysis system for automated analysis, then manually checked and confirmed by two physicians who were unaware of the subjects' grouping. Subsequently, time‐domain heart rate variability analysis was performed on 24‐h dynamic electrocardiogram recordings. All analyses were based on the normal‐to‐normal (NN) interval sequence generated by sinus rhythm. Calculate the following time domain indicators: Standard Deviation of Normal‐to‐Normal intervals (SDNN); Standard Deviation of the Average NN intervals (SDANN); Mean of the Standard Deviations of NN intervals (SDNN Index); Root Mean Square of Successive Differences (rMSSD); deceleration capacity (DC) [17].

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Multivariate linear regression was used to examine the relationships among malnutrition indicators, ambulatory blood pressure indicators, and heart rate variability indicators. Binary Logistic regression was used to analyze the correlation between indicators and malnutrition, and meaningful indicators were included in the ROC curve to estimate their AUC values. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.4.2.

Compared to the non‐malnutrition group, the malnutrition group was significantly older and had a higher prevalence of pain (p < 0.05). Significant reductions were also observed in multiple biochemical markers, such as serum creatinine, uric acid, and blood lipid levels. There was no significant difference in gender, history of hypertension, or history of diabetes between the two groups.

Discussion

Malnutrition is associated with decreased blood pressure. We found that both the 24‐h and daytime average diastolic blood pressures were lower in the malnutrition group. After accounting for the confounding effects of age, hypertension, diabetes, and pain, it was found that the 24‐h average systolic blood pressure, 24‐h average diastolic blood pressure, daytime average systolic blood pressure, and daytime average diastolic blood pressure in the malnutrition group were significantly lower. A study [10] of hypotension and cisplatin chemotherapy toxicity found that albumin in the hypotension group was significantly lower than that in the normal blood pressure group, and the incidence of hypotension in the low protein group was also significantly increased. Another study found that, in patients with early gastric cancer, blood pressure decreased significantly after surgery, and that the positive impact of gastrectomy on blood pressure may extend beyond weight loss itself [18]. Studies have found that malnourished children have lower diastolic blood pressure [19]. Kocyigit et al. [8] found that malnutrition and even malnutrition risk are associated with orthostatic hypotension during systole. Improving nutritional status can improve the overall cognition and gait balance function of elderly patients with orthostatic hypotension and prevent falls [20]. In this study, malnutrition was associated with lower blood pressure in patients with gastrointestinal tumors, consistent with the conclusions of the above study. However, some studies have suggested that hypoalbuminemia is associated with increased hypertension. In a longitudinal cohort study in South Korea, hypoalbuminemia is an important predictor of early hypertension progression [21]. The controversial relationship between malnutrition and blood pressure may be related to the different populations included in the study.

This study found that in malnourished patients, 24‐h heart rate, daytime average heart rate, and nighttime average heart rate were significantly increased, and heart rate variability indicators, including SDNN and DC, were statistically lower. After correcting the interference factors, there was still statistical significance. Logistic regression showed that the average heart rate at night (OR = 1.089, 95% CI 1.031–1.151, p < 0.002), heart rate deceleration (OR = 0.724, 95% CI 0.628–0.835, p < 0.001), and DC achieved the highest AUC for malnutrition prediction in the ROC analysis (AUC = 0.706). Heart rate variability is a non‐invasive tool for assessing autonomic nervous function [22]. Compared with traditional heart rate variability indicators such as SDNN, DC is a better indicator of autonomic nerve function and has greater predictive value for prognosis [17]. Park et al. [23] found that albumin, prealbumin, and transferrin were used as nutritional indicators, and that heart rate variability in malnourished people was lower, suggesting that these nutritional indicators were associated with autonomic dysfunction. Studies have also shown that high resting heart rate is associated with cardiovascular adverse events [24]. In this study, the overall heart rate of malnourished patients increased; the increase was most significant at night, and heart rate deceleration decreased, suggesting that malnourished patients had autonomic dysfunction.

Blood pressure dipper changes and decreased heart rate deceleration rate coexist. The normal blood pressure showed a dipper pattern. Tekce et al. [13] found that in hemodialysis patients, the circadian blood pressure rhythm deteriorated in those with high malnutrition scores, whereas in non‐dippers and reverse‐dippers, malnutrition was more common and severe. Another study [25] found that patients with reverse dippers had lower serum albumin in hypertensive patients without diabetes. Impaired autonomic nervous function plays an important role in nocturnal blood pressure elevation [26]. The study found that a decrease in nocturnal blood pressure drop rate was associated with a decrease in heart rate deceleration capacity [14, 27]. We have, for the first time, discovered this phenomenon in malnourished patients with gastrointestinal tumors. There was a decrease in nocturnal blood pressure drop rate and heart rate deceleration capacity in malnourished patients with gastrointestinal tumors, suggesting that malnourished patients with tumors have impaired autonomic nervous function.

Several studies have shown that changes in blood pressure dippers and decreased heart rate deceleration are closely related to cardiovascular adverse events and tumor prognosis. Non‐dipper type and reverse dipper type are closely related to cardiovascular adverse events [28, 29, 30]. The Ohasama study included 1542 subjects and followed up for 9.2 years. It was found that, even when subjects had normal 24‐h blood pressure, their nocturnal blood pressure reduction rate remained an independent predictor of cardiovascular adverse events [31]. Changes in blood pressure rhythm also increase the incidence of tumors and accelerate tumor progression [32]. Deceleration capacity is associated with the overall survival rate of non‐small cell lung cancer and is considered to be an effective tool for predicting the prognosis of non‐small cell lung cancer [5]. Feng et al. also found that deceleration capacity was associated with overall survival of breast cancer [33]. Malnutrition is closely related to the prognosis and all‐cause mortality of cancer patients [34, 35, 36]. This study found that malnourished patients had both a dipper pattern in blood pressure and a decreased heart rate deceleration rate. The deterioration of blood pressure rhythm and the decrease in heart rate deceleration rate may be one of the factors that increase the deterioration of the outcome of malnourished tumor patients.

There are some deficiencies in this study. The study is a single‐center, retrospective study, but the population is large; therefore, the conclusion has some reference significance. In addition, the study did not consider the effects of tumor drugs and treatments on blood pressure and heart rate variability. Although the included population was the first treatment population, the long‐term effects of tumor drugs were, in a sense, excluded. It would be valuable to conduct long‐term follow‐up studies on the overall survival of malnourished tumor patients, assessing how reduced blood pressure and blunted heart rate deceleration affect their outcomes.

Conclusions

Our research discovered that patients with malnourished gastrointestinal tumors exhibit abnormal circadian rhythms, including blunted nocturnal blood pressure dipping, attenuated heart rate deceleration, and elevated nighttime heart rate. These findings are expected to provide information for the study of the potential mechanisms underlying the impact of malnutrition on the prognosis of cancer patients.
