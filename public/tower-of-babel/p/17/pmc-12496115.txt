Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading global cause of death, causing an estimated 17.9 million deaths per year and accounting for 32% of all global deaths.1 CVD incidence is still rising due to populations becoming older, the increased prevalence of modifiable risk factors and lifestyle factors.2 Traditional CVD risk prediction equations, such as the Framingham Risk Score and the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease risk estimator, primarily use demographic (eg, sex and age) and biochemical markers (eg, cholesterol, blood pressure and diabetes) to calculate an individual’s likelihood of developing CVD over a certain time frame.

Traditional CVD risk prediction models have been useful in clinical practice; however, they often fail to incorporate behavioural and psychological determinants, potentially leading to misestimation of risk in diverse populations.3 Growing evidence suggests that behavioural factors, such as smoking, diet, alcohol and exercise, and psychological factors, such as depression, stress and anxiety, play an important role in the development of CVD.4 Evidence shows that depression and chronic stress are associated with a 1.5-fold to 2-fold higher risk of myocardial infarction and stroke.5 Furthermore, low physical activity levels have been estimated to contribute to an estimated 9% of premature deaths worldwide.6 Given these known relations, the most widely used CVD risk prediction models do not account specifically for these variables, and therefore there could be lost opportunities for early intervention.7

More recent studies have sought to improve CVD risk prediction by including behavioural and psychological risk factors in conventional models. One is the INTERHEART study, which included 29,972 persons from 52 countries and concluded that modifiable risk factors (stress and depression being included) accounted for over 90% of first myocardial infarction risk.8 Another 2023 study from the UK Biobank dataset concluded that the inclusion of behavioural risk factors (smoking, physical activity and diet quality, to mention a few) added 8%–12% to CVD risk prediction compared with conventional models.9

Despite advances in health prediction models, there are significant research gaps. In particular, we do not yet know which behavioural and psychological factors are most critical to include in disease risk models, including CVD prediction. Current models focus almost exclusively on physiological biomarkers, with psychological ageing, stress and lifestyle factors—despite exerting an enormous impact on healthspan—rarely included in risk assessments. Emerging evidence suggests that anywhere from 70%–90% of health outcomes are influenced by lifestyle and environmental exposures, and yet these factors are often not included in high-tech medical interventions. Bridging this gap requires a more integrated approach that combines behavioural and psychological data with traditional biomarkers, leveraging new technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) and wearable devices to enable enhanced predictive accuracy.10 Second, differences in methodology between studies hinder direct comparison and standardisation. Third, while machine learning-based prediction models have been shown to have the potential to encompass a multifactorial risk profile, clinical utility remains hampered by concerns regarding interpretability and validation.11

Methods and design

This protocol describes a planned systematic review, with the literature search anticipated to commence in October 2024.

Protocol and registration

This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines.12 The conduct of the systematic review will follow the methodological guidance outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The protocol has been prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251014218).

Discussion

This protocol outlines a systematic review that aims to evaluate the extent to which behavioural and psychological factors have been incorporated into CVD risk prediction models, and how such integration may improve model design and clinical applicability.

Although traditional CVD risk models primarily rely on demographic and physiological indicators, a growing body of research has highlighted the relevance of modifiable psychosocial and behavioural factors. However, how these factors are operationalised, measured and interpreted in prediction models remains inconsistent. This systematic review seeks to address this gap by identifying which variables have been used, how they have been defined and what methodological approaches have been used in validating their predictive value.

This study has several methodological strengths. It will use a rigorous and transparent literature search across multiple databases, guided by the PRISMA-P reporting framework. Study screening and data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers, and the quality and risk of bias will be appraised using established tools such as the NOS and the QUIPS tool. These steps aim to minimise bias and enhance the reliability of findings.

Nonetheless, several challenges may arise. Variability in definitions of behavioural and psychological constructs may limit comparability across studies. Differences in statistical modelling methods and outcome measures may introduce heterogeneity, which could complicate synthesis and meta-analysis. Additionally, exclusion of non-English articles may lead to language bias, and many studies may lack external validation or generalisability.

Despite these challenges, the anticipated findings of this review could inform future risk model development by identifying promising behavioural and psychological predictors and highlighting methodological best practices. This, in turn, may facilitate the design of more holistic, equitable and precise CVD prevention strategies.

Implications and future directions

The findings of this review support the growing consensus that behavioural and psychological factors should be systematically integrated into CVD risk models to enhance their predictive capabilities. However, several challenges remain.

Standardisation of behavioural and psychological measures

Current models vary in how they define and measure behavioural and psychological factors (eg, self-reported stress vs clinically diagnosed depression). Future studies should work towards harmonising these definitions to improve comparability across different risk models.

Longitudinal validation

Most studies included in this review used cross-sectional or retrospective data to assess predictive accuracy. There is a need for prospective cohort studies that track changes in behavioural and psychological risk factors over time to determine their true predictive value for future CVD events.

Integration of machine learning approaches

While machine learning-based predictive models offer potential for handling multifactorial risk profiles, their clinical applicability remains limited due to challenges in interpretability and validation.16 17 Future work should focus on developing transparent, explainable AI models that can be seamlessly integrated into clinical practice.

Personalised and sex-specific risk prediction

Evidence from the sex-specific acute myocardial infarction studies highlights the different impact of depression and smoking on men and women. Future CVD risk models should consider sex-based and personalised risk stratification approaches, particularly for psychological health factors.

Policy and clinical implementation

Even if enhanced risk models incorporating behavioural and psychological factors prove to be superior, translating these findings into clinical guidelines remains a challenge. Effective implementation will require training healthcare providers to use these models effectively and adapting public health policies to emphasize modifiable lifestyle factors in CVD prevention strategies.
