Metal–organic framework glass enables durable sodium-ion storage for hard carbon negative electrodes
Authors: Jingwen Zhao, Wujie Yang, Yanpeng Fan, Zhi Chang, Haoshen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hard carbon negative electrodes, owing to their abundant precursors and low operating potential, are promising candidates for sodium-ion batteries. However, sluggish desolvation kinetics and unstable electrode–electrolyte interfaces hinder their practical deployment. Here we report a melt-coating strategy to encapsulate hard carbon particles with a metal–organic framework glass. The resulting glass coating contains sub-nanometer channels that lower the desolvation activation energy, accelerate sodium-ion transport, and induce a thin, anion-derived solid electrolyte interphase rich in NaF during cycling. The optimized interface promotes the formation of quasi-metallic sodium clusters within the closed pores of hard carbon, activating a high-capacity sodium storage mechanism. Consequently, the glass-coated hard carbon delivers a reversible capacity of 462.2 mAh g−1, cycling stability of 89.1% capacity retention after 4000 cycles at 1 A g−1 and 95.3% capacity retention after 8000 cycles at 2 A g−1, and high-rate capability. This work offers insights into interfacial design strategies for durable sodium-ion storage in hard carbon negative electrodes.

Introduction

The highly ordered layered structure of graphite has established a classical lithium storage paradigm based on the reversible insertion/extraction of Li+ (0.76 Å) within its two-dimensional interlayer spacing (3.35 Å)1–3. For sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), however, graphite fails to serve as a viable negative electrode because Na+ intercalation into the graphene interlayers is thermodynamically unstable, in sharp contrast to the stable LiC6 formed in lithium-ion batteries4,5. As a non-graphitized carbon material, hard carbon (HC) features low cost and abundant raw materials and has emerged as a leading negative electrode candidate for SIBs targeting large-scale energy storage6–9. The desolvation process of HC negative electrodes determines the Na+ diffusion kinetics and influences the evolution of the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)10,11. However, the wide pore size distribution leads to two key bottlenecks in achieving efficient desolvation12,13. Firstly, the ambiguous pore structure hinders the regulation of Na+ desolvation behavior: large micropores (>5 Å) allow solvent molecules to co-intercalate with Na+, while ultra-micropores (<2 Å) completely block the Na+ transport14,15. This duality triggers sluggish Na+ desolvation kinetics16. Secondly, the numerous surface defects on HC cause continuous electrolyte decomposition to form a thick and unstable SEI layer rich in organic17–19. Notably, the occurrence of intense interfacial side reactions induces substantial irreversible capacity loss, thereby giving rise to a typically low initial Coulombic efficiency20,21. Therefore, constructing size-adaptive Na+ storage spaces within graphite-like channels is a key strategy for achieving high-performance HC negative electrodes.

Regarding Na+ storage mechanisms in HC, micropore storage demonstrates the metalloid-like aggregation characteristics of sodium atoms in confined environments22,23. Gotoh et al. established approximately 3.5 Å as the so-called “golden micropore size range,” providing a mechanistic basis for subsequent structural design24. Most recently, Yang’s team proposed a “pore size effect”, demonstrating that 2–5 Å pore size can forcibly desolvate Na+ through spatial sieving25,26. Hu’s team successfully attained a high specific capacity of 416.1 mAh g−1 by regulating semi-closed and fully closed pore structures27. Notably, prior studies have reported that narrowing the pore entrances of HC can promote Na+ desolvation, facilitating the further formation of quasi-metallic Na clusters within the nanopores25,28. Conventional modification strategies have yet to reconcile efficient Na+ desolvation with stable SEI formation, a shortcoming that limits the reversible formation of quasi-metallic Na clusters and ultimately compromises the sodium storage performance of HC29.

In this context, metal-organic framework (MOF) glass has emerged as a promising class of functional coating materials. Distinct from our previous work on inert bilayer SEIs in lithium-based batteries5,30, this study reports the application of a MOF glass coating to HC negative electrodes, yielding a HC@g‑ZnPIm architecture with a sub‑nanometer pore sieving effect. This structure enables Na+ pre‑desolvation and the formation of a stable SEI (Fig. 1a), while also allowing the reversible generation of quasi‑metallic Na clusters within the closed pores. Consequently, the HC@g-ZnPIm negative electrode achieves a reversible capacity of 462.2 mAh g−1 at 0.02 A g−1, maintains 89.1% initial capacity after 4000 cycles at 1 A g−1, and exhibits 95.3% capacity retention after 8000 cycles at 2 A g−1. Results

Rational design and structural characterizations of MOF-glass-coated HC negative electrode

The efficiency of the desolvation process is directly determined by pore size and relies on its precise matching with solvated sodium clusters, which is key to achieving rapid ion transport31. Notably, traditional solid-state MOF powders and coating materials are constrained by solid particle diffusion barriers that prevent penetration into their intricate internal pores, resulting in overall inefficient desolvation32–34. To achieve effective desolvation of Na+ in HC, we selected a low-temperature meltable MOF, ZnPIm, as the porosity-regulating medium (Supplementary Fig. 1). ZnPIm features one-dimensional nanoscale channels and undergoes a well-defined solid-to-liquid transition at 158 °C, substantially below the thermal degradation temperature of HC, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the carbon substrate35. Upon melting, ZnPIm forms a low-viscosity liquid that infiltrates the complex surface of HC particles; upon rapid cooling, it solidifies into a continuous, conformal, and thickness-tunable glass coating. Unlike conventional amorphous glasses, ZnPIm retains a sub-nanometer porous framework after vitrification. This pore size lies between the diameters of solvated Na+ clusters and bare Na+, enabling the coating to function as a molecular sieve that promotes pre-desolvation and selectively blocks solvent co-intercalation. These combined attributes, low-temperature melt processability and retention of functional microporosity, render ZnPIm an ideal candidate for constructing an ion-sieving and interface-stabilizing coating. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals sharp crystalline peaks for ZnPIm and broad peaks for g-ZnPIm, confirming the transformation from crystalline MOF powder to amorphous MOF glass (Supplementary Fig. 2). Positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) confirms that ZnPIm retains approximately 0.35 nm pores after undergoing glass transition (Supplementary Fig. 3). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements show a clear endothermic peak in ZnPIm powder at approximately 158 °C (Tm) prior to decomposition. The subsequent cyclic scan of the prepared g-ZnPIm reveals a glass transition temperature (Tg) of approximately 30 °C, demonstrating the successful preparation of the MOF glass (Supplementary Fig. 4). To further characterize the porous nature and thermal stability of the MOF glass, CO2 adsorption isotherms were measured at 273 K, 308 K, and 323 K (Supplementary Fig. 5). The CO2 uptake gradually decreases with increasing temperature, following the typical exothermic behavior of physisorption in microporous materials. Notably, obvious CO2 adsorption is maintained at 308 K and 323 K, confirming that the microporous channels remain intact and accessible even in the rubbery state above Tg. This structural stability ensures continuous Na+ transport and pre-desolvation during cycling, which is critical for the enhanced interfacial kinetics. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals that ZnPIm powder consists of irregular particles, while g-ZnPIm exhibits a crack-free film-like structure, with a uniform elemental distribution observed in the corresponding mapping (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). Relying on the distinct properties of MOF glass, the melting process allows it to infiltrate the complex surface and internal pores of HC, significantly constricting the pore openings and creating a molecular sieve-like architecture. Subsequent rapid cooling induces coordination rearrangement between metal nodes and organic ligands, locking the structure and forming tight interfacial encapsulation with pore modification, thereby constructing a strongly bonded composite sieve-pore carbon structure. (HC@g-ZnPIm) (Fig. 1b). This melt coating strategy is effective to traditional solution or high‑temperature methods, as it avoids solvent effects and structural destruction, achieving dense, continuous, and defect‑free surface encapsulation. In addition, XRD patterns show that the characteristic broadened (002) diffraction peak of HC is preserved in HC@g-ZnPIm, indicating that the introduction of the MOF-glass coating layer has not altered the intrinsic structure (Supplementary Fig. 8). SEM images depict that the particle size of HC@g-ZnPIm coated with MOF-glass exhibits no significant difference compared to the original HC, with the smoother surface resulting from the MOF-glass coating (Supplementary Fig. 9).

The surface chemistry of pristine HC and HC@g-ZnPIm was investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Raman spectroscopy was further employed to analyze the structural features of the samples (Supplementary Fig. 11). The ID/IG ratio of HC@g-ZnPIm decreases to 1.56 relative to HC, corresponding to a relative reduction in defect density, likely due to the protective effect of the coating against surface side reactions. Additionally, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results reveal a thin continuous amorphous layer of approximately 3 nm thickness on the surface of HC@g-ZnPIm particles, which is absent in pristine HC (Fig. 1c, d), indicating the formation of a uniform MOF-glass coating. Corresponding elemental mapping shows a homogeneous distribution of Zn, P, N, and O, verifying that HC is completely covered by the MOF glass without cracks (Fig. 1e). To systematically investigate the regulation of the pore structure by the MOF coating, low-temperature CO2 adsorption-desorption isotherm measurements were employed36. As displayed in Fig. 1f, h, the BET surface areas (SBET) values of HC and HC@g-ZnPIm calculated based on CO2 adsorption are 18.3 m2 g−1 and 12.7 m2 g−1, respectively. This indicates that the molten MOF constructs ultra-micropores in HC@g-ZnPIm, which can reduce the interfacial contact between the inner carbon surface and the electrolyte, thereby minimizing side reactions.

To better understand the pore structure and the infiltration behavior of the MOF-glass coating, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and He pycnometry measurements were performed. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, the SAXS profiles of both HC and HC@g-ZnPIm exhibit a distinct scattering peak in the low-q region (q ~ 0.49 Å−1 for HC and q ~ 0.62 Å−1 for HC@g-ZnPIm). The pore bulk distribution (PBD) derived from SAXS fitting reveals that the primary pore diameter shifts from 1.28 nm for HC to 1.02 nm for HC@g-ZnPIm (calculated using d=2π /qd=2π/q). The slight decrease in pore size after MOF-glass coating suggests partial infiltration or surface modification of accessible pores by g-ZnPIm, while the preserved scattering feature indicates that the overall porous framework of HC remains intact. Correspondingly, the true density decreases from 1.69 g cm−3 for HC to 1.63 g cm−3 for HC@g-ZnPIm, as determined by He pycnometry (Supplementary Fig. 13). This is consistent with the introduction of the MOF-glass material and the partial narrowing of surface pores observed by SAXS. Combined with the decreased BET surface area from CO2 adsorption, these results collectively demonstrate that the MOF-glass coating primarily modifies the surface pore structure without disrupting the internal pore structure.

Interfacial Na+ desolvation behavior

To investigate the evolution of the coordination environment of the electrolyte on different electrode surfaces, we performed in-situ Raman during the discharge process. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the HC electrode exhibits distinct spectral changes throughout discharge. Specifically, the Raman peak corresponding to the PF6− A1g mode undergoes a redshift, indicating strong adsorption and uncontrollable decomposition reaction of the anions on the electrode surface. This provides direct evidence for the formation of a thick and structurally unstable organic-rich SEI film and intensified interfacial side reactions. Meanwhile, the peak intensity associated with the C–O–C wagging vibration of the diglyme (G2) solvent (–CH2) weakens, confirming the co-intercalation of solvent molecules with Na+ into the carbon layers, which further promotes interfacial degradation37. It is noteworthy that the PF6− A1g Raman peak in HC@g-ZnPIm presents a free state and remains stable at 745.1 cm−1 and the peak position remains stable throughout the entire discharge process (Fig. 2b). This demonstrates that the ~0.35 nm confined channels in the MOF-glass coating effectively function as molecular sieves, blocking the direct contact between anions and the carbon surface. The C–O–C characteristic peak also persists throughout the discharge process, suggesting that the co-intercalation behavior of the solvent is significantly suppressed38. Based on the Arrhenius equation39,40, the activation energies for Na+ desolvation (Fig. 2c) and charge transfer (Fig. 2d) processes were further calculated (Supplementary Fig. 14). The HC@g-ZnPIm electrode demonstrates low Na+ desolvation activation energy (ESEI = 6.13 kJ mol−1) and transport activation energy (Ect = 6.57 kJ mol−1) compared with HC electrode (ESEI = 8.01 kJ mol−1) and (Ect = 8.09 kJ mol−1). This reduction in activation energy indicates that the MOF-glass coating effectively lowers the energy barrier required for Na+ to be released from the solvation shell, which is consistent with the expected desolvation effect. As illustrated in the solvation structure schematic (Fig. 2f), the MOF-glass coating weakens the interactions between cations and solvents as well as between cations and anions by regulating the pore structure41. It not only provides low-resistance ion transport channels but also reduces the energy barrier for Na⁺ to detachment from the solvation shell through pre-desolvation42, thereby accelerating Na⁺ transport and optimizing the ion transport environment at the electrode-electrolyte interface. The MOF-glass coating promotes pre-desolvation through its sub-nanometer pores (~0.35 nm), which lie between the diameters of solvated Na+ clusters and bare Na+, partially stripping the solvent shell via steric hindrance. This reduces the charge transfer activation energy and promotes the formation of an inorganic-rich, NaF-dominated SEI, collectively accelerating interfacial Na+ transport. Furthermore, the ²³Na solid-state NMR spectra reveal the distribution characteristics of Na+ in the electrode region during the initial discharge (1.2 V). Compared with the HC electrode, the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode exhibits a significantly enhanced sodium signal near ~0 ppm, with the resonance peak shifting toward higher field (Fig. 2e). This change in chemical shift reflects an evolution in the Na+ local coordination environment, consistent with the role of the MOF-glass confined pores in modulating the solvation structure and facilitating the pre-desolvation process. Collectively, these multi-scale characterization results demonstrate the role of the MOF-glass coating in optimizing the Na+ transport kinetics.

Dynamic interfacial resistance and Na+ transport kinetics during cycling

To investigate the differences in ion diffusion kinetics on different electrode surfaces, Nyquist plots at different states of charge (SOC) and depth of discharge (DOD) during the first cycle are presented in Fig. 3a, d. The interfacial film resistance and charge transfer resistance for HC@g-ZnPIm show a substantial reduction relative to HC, which stems from the tailored porous structure of g-ZnPIm. To intuitively verify the stability of ion and electron transport at the interface, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) was analyzed (Fig. 3b, c). For the HC electrode, the ττ distribution exhibits broader, more dispersed peaks, reflecting heterogeneous relaxation processes associated with sluggish ion transport and inefficient charge transfer. In contrast, the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode displays sharper, more concentrated ττpeaks with shorter characteristic relaxation times, indicating more uniform and rapid ion transport/charge transfer dynamics (Fig. 3e, f). This convergence of relaxation behavior underscores the role of g-ZnPIm in homogenizing ion pathways and reducing kinetic heterogeneity. The variations of the SEI film resistance (RSEI) and charge transfer resistance (Rct) of HC and HC@g-ZnPIm at the SOC and DOD state are shown in Fig. 3g, h. The bulk transport kinetics of Na+ were further analyzed by the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) curves in Fig. 3i, j. The HC@g-ZnPIm electrode exhibits a more uniform charge–discharge plateau and a higher Na+ diffusion coefficient, indicating that HC@g-ZnPIm preserves the structural integrity of both the interface and the bulk. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves recorded at various scan rates show greater overlap for the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode, revealing improved electrochemical reversibility (Supplementary Fig. 15). As depicted in the schematic diagram of Na+ desolvation and interfacial transport (Fig. 3k), the pore structure provided by the MOF-glass coating fundamentally reshapes the physical and chemical environment of the electrode–electrolyte interface. The ~0.35 nm pores exert a strong spatial confinement effect on solvated Na+, enabling the ions to complete the initial desolvation and comprehensively accelerating the interfacial reaction kinetics. This fundamentally eliminates the structural damage and irreversible capacity loss caused by solvent molecules entering the carbon layers through the larger pores of pristine HC.

Enhanced rate capability and long-term cycling durability

To evaluate the electrochemical advantages of the g-ZnPIm coating, the electrochemical performances of the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode were evaluated. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, HC@g-ZnPIm electrodes with different coating amounts (0.5 wt.%, 1.0 wt.% and 2.0 wt.%) were prepared. The sample with 1.0 wt.% coating exhibited the optimal rate performance, and this proportion was adopted for all subsequent investigations. The galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves depicted in Fig. 4a reveal that the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode achieves a substantially elevated Coulombic efficiency (CE = 86.1%), compared to the bare HC electrode (CE = 79.5%). The enhancement can be deemed to stem from the g-ZnPIm coated on the negative electrode acting as an insulating barrier, which robustly suppresses parasitic side reactions between electrolyte and electrode, thereby mitigating irreversible capacity losses associated with electrolyte decomposition and unregulated SEI layer formation. The rate performance in Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 17, shows that the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode delivers a higher specific capacity than the HC electrode across a range of specific current from 0.02 A g−1 to 2.0 A g−1, and the capacity recovers well upon returning to 0.02 A g−1. The contribution ratios of plateau and slope capacities in Fig. 4c further illustrate that the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode features a higher proportion of plateau region capacity at different specific currents, suggesting that Na+ is more readily intercalated into the ordered structure of HC after the desolvation process. The cycling stability of both electrodes at 0.2 A g−1 is shown in Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 18. HC@g-ZnPIm exhibits a clear improvement in discharge capacity and cycling stability relative to the unmodified HC counterpart. After an initial activation process at 0.02 A g−1, the HC negative electrode fails at the 200th cycle. In contrast, the optimized HC@g-ZnPIm electrode achieves a meaningful enhancement in discharge capacity and retains a specific capacity of 409.3 mAh g−1 after 250 cycles. To assess the practical potential, the electrodes were also tested in a carbonate-based electrolyte of 1 M NaPF6 in ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate (EC/DEC) at 0.2 A g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 19). Notably, the HC@g-ZnPIm maintains a capacity retention of 89.3% after 300 cycles, whereas the pristine HC only retains 51.8% of its initial capacity. This contrast highlights the critical role of the MOF-glass coating in stabilizing the electrode-electrolyte interface and mitigating capacity decay, even in conventional carbonate electrolytes. When tested at an elevated specific current of 0.5 A g−1 (Fig. 4e), the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode exhibits long cycling stability of 900 cycles while with a stable discharge specific capacity of 382.3 mAh g−1. Compared with the pristine HC electrode, which failed after fewer than 450 cycles, g-ZnPIm demonstrates a unique advantage by leveraging its sieving effect to accelerate the desolvation process, thereby improving Na+ diffusion kinetics and interfacial stability. The GCD curves of the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode exhibit a higher degree of overlap than that of the HC negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 20), which indicates that the coating of g-ZnPIm has enhanced the reversibility of Na+ insertion and extraction. Furthermore, the long-cycle capability at higher specific current is demonstrated in Fig. 4f, g. The HC@g-ZnPIm electrode delivers 371.1 mAh g−1 specific capacity after 4000 cycles at 1 A g−1, and 331.5 mAh g−1 specific capacity after 8000 cycles at 2 A g−1. In contrast, the HC electrode decays to near-zero capacity after a relatively short number of cycles. These electrochemical results verify that g-ZnPIm substantially improves the long-cycle performance of HC by accelerating the desolvation process and stabilizing the electrode structure. The radar chart (Fig. 4h) compares the performances of the HC negative electrode reported in this work with that in other literatures from multiple dimensions to intuitively demonstrate the superiority in modifying HC negative electrode32,43–47. The literature data sources are summarized in Table S2 of the Supplementary Information. The HC@g-ZnPIm electrode shows the advantages in key indicators for coulombic efficiency, specific capacity, and capacity retention, underscoring the effectiveness and advancement of the molten-MOF-glass layer on the HC negative electrode.

Dual interface and bulk structural stabilization induced by ion-sieving

To further investigate the interfacial and structural stabilizing effects of MOF-glass coating, TEM images of the HC and HC@g-ZnPIm electrodes after 50 cycles are presented in Fig. 5a, c, respectively. The SEI exhibits markedly different morphologies on the two cycled electrodes. The pristine HC electrode shows an uneven, irregular SEI layer with 7.5 nm in thickness. In contrast, the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode forms a thin, uniformly distributed SEI layer in 2.8 nm. The thinner SEI film facilitates faster Na+ diffusion. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, P 2p XPS spectra confirm stable phosphorus chemical states. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) results show negligible Zn dissolution (below 0.1 ppm), demonstrating excellent structural and chemical stability of the coating (Table S1). Furthermore, TEM characterization of the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode after 120 cycles verifies that the MOF-glass coating remains stable and intact during long-term cycling (Supplementary Fig. 22). Such a robust and compact interface effectively restrains persistent electrolyte decomposition and underpins the exceptional long-cycle stability of the electrode. Meanwhile, XPS depth profiling further reveals interfacial differences. For the HC electrode, F-containing species-diagnostic of electrolyte decomposition products display extensive distribution and high signal intensity with increasing etching time (C–F, Na–F)17,48,49, signaling severe parasitic reactions and the development of a thick, structurally disordered SEI (Fig. 5b). By contrast, the Na-F signal in the HC@g-ZnPIm electrode demonstrates the MOF-glass coating eloquently attests to suppressed electrolyte degradation and the formation of an ultrathin, chemically robust SEI. Interface chemistry and Na+ storage mechanism.

a, c Ex situ TEM images of HC (a) and HC@g-ZnPIm (c) electrodes after 50 cycles at 0.2 A g−1 (25 ± 2 °C), fully discharged (0.01 V vs Na+/Na) before disassembly. b, d Ex situ XPS depth profiles (F 1s) of HC (b) and HC@g-ZnPIm (d) after 50 cycles under the same conditions. e–h Ex situ XPS depth profiles of C 1s and Na 1s for cycled HC and HC@g-ZnPIm. i, j In situ Raman contour maps collected during cycling at 0.2 A g−1 and 25 ± 2 °C of HC (i) and HC@g-ZnPIm (j). k Low-frequency Raman spectra of HC@g-ZnPIm at open circuit voltage (OCV) and different depths of discharge (DOD). Inset: schematic of Na cluster. l Schematic of Na+ storage mechanism: large-sized solvated Na+ enter the open pores of HC, while in HC@g-ZnPIm coated with MOF-glass, selective transport is achieved through the confined channels with size sieving effect, effectively promoting the nucleation and growth of Na clusters, thereby realizing efficient and stable sodium storage.

In this context, the electronically insulating MOF-glass layer can be regarded as an artificial solid electrolyte interphase that physically isolates the HC surface from the liquid electrolyte. Unlike a conventional SEI that forms solely from electrolyte decomposition, the MOF-glass coating and the electrochemically formed SEI together constitute a hybrid interface; the MOF-glass layer provides structural integrity and ion‑sieving functionality, while the NaF-rich SEI ensures high ionic conductivity. This synergistic combination results in a thin, mechanically robust, and ion-conductive composite interface that suppresses parasitic reactions, reduces interfacial resistance, and maintains structural integrity during prolonged cycling, directly contributing to the exceptional long‑term stability of the HC@g‑ZnPIm electrode. The C 1s (Fig. 5e, f) and Na 1s (Fig. 5g, h) XPS spectra provide deeper insight into SEI composition. In the C 1s spectrum of cycled HC, the main peak exhibits a significant shift toward lower binding energies (Fig. 5e), originating from the formation of an organic SEI film on the surface as a result of solvent decomposition14. By contrast, HC@g-ZnPIm coated with molten MOF exhibits only a slight peak shift, further confirming a thinner SEI with reduced organic content (Fig. 5f), consistent with TEM results. Supplementary Fig. 23 presents the C 1s XPS spectra of HC and HC@g-ZnPIm electrodes after 200 s of etching. For the pristine HC electrode, the C 1s signal can be deconvoluted into four components attributed to C–C/C=C, C–O, C=O, and O=C–O bonding, with binding energies at 284.8, 286, 288, and 291 eV, respectively38. In comparison to HC electrodes, HC@g-ZnPIm exhibits an oxygen-containing functional group ratio of 34.1%, lower than that of HC (40.9%). This reduction mitigates capacity decay caused by side reactions and enhances the electrode’s cycling stability. For the Na 1s spectrum, HC@g-ZnPIm exhibits a sharp and symmetric characteristic peak at ~1072.4 eV, with both peak position and intensity remaining highly stable throughout etching. This thin, inorganic-dominated SEI effectively explains the reduced activation energy for Na+ transport and significantly enhanced rate performance. Overall, the optimized composition effectively reduces ion transport resistance and improves interfacial stability, establishing a fundamental basis for long-term electrochemical durability.

The structural evolution of the HC and HC@g-ZnPIm electrode during cyclingwas further investigated by utilizing in-situ Raman spectroscopy50, as shown in Fig. 5i, j, Supplementary Figs. 24, 25. In the HC electrode, both the D and G bands exhibit irreversible red shifts, stemming from the inability of the carbon layer to contract after excessive expansion and from disordered defects triggering side reactions. By contrast, in HC@g-ZnPIm, where the pore size is precisely tuned to approximately 3.5 Å, matching the insertion dimensions required for Na+ while preventing excessive swelling. Upon discharge to 0.01 V, the G band undergoes a red shift from 1580 cm−1 to 1574 cm−1 and is completely restored to its original peak position upon charging to 2.0 V. This demonstrates that the uniform pore structure provides a directional insertion/extraction pathway for Na+, effectively suppressing irreversible stacking or collapse of the carbon layers51. The vibrational environment within the graphitic domains remains stable throughout cycling. Additionally, the appearance of a reversible new peak in the D band during cycling confirms the in-situ formation and decomposition of sodium-carbon compounds NaₓCᵧ51,52. This indicates that the MOF coating synergistically enhances the sodium storage reversibility of HC at the structural level through pore size regulation and defect functionalization, providing evidence for their outstanding electrochemical performance. The formation of Na clusters was detected by low-frequency Raman spectroscopy53. Figure 5k shows the Raman signals at open circuit voltage (OCV) and different DOD. At OCV, no distinct Na cluster characteristic peaks are observed, indicating that Na+ exists in a dispersed or solvated form. As the depth of discharge progresses, Na+ begins to aggregate. The most prominent peaks are located at 112 and 151 cm−1, close to the strongest signals reported for the magic Na8 cluster isolated at 15 K in an Ar matrix53. When the discharge reaches 100% DOD, the characteristic peak intensity is markedly enhanced, indicating the generation of Na clusters. For the HC electrode, a similar but significantly weaker Na cluster Raman signal was detected under identical conditions, consistent with previous reports (Supplementary Fig. 26). The MOF-glass coating forms a sieve-like structure that allows only de-solvated Na+ to pass, while inducing small, controllable Na clusters within the closed pores (Fig. 5l). These clusters rapidly enter the HC via an optimized path, avoiding kinetic sluggishness from large cluster formation and achieving superior Na+ storage kinetics.

In this work, we report a top-down pore engineering strategy mediated by molten MOF-glass. By constructing a MOF-glass coating with ~0.35 nm channels on the surface of HC particles, an ion screening interface is achieved that promotes the desolvation of Na+, significantly lowers the desolvation energy barrier, and induces the formation of a thin, inorganic-rich, and highly stable solid electrolyte interphase. Importantly, the MOF-glass coating facilitates the generation of quasi-metallic Na clusters within the confined pores, activating a high-capacity storage mechanism. The as-prepared HC@g-ZnPIm electrode delivers a high reversible capacity of 462.2 mAh g−1, retains 89.1% and 95.3% of its capacity over 4000 and 8000 cycles at 1 A g−1 and 2 A g−1, respectively, coupled with high-rate capability. Overall, this work proposes a new paradigm of interfacial ion sieving and presents a feasible strategy for developing high-energy-density and long-cycle-life sodium-ion batteries.

Methods

Materials

Zinc oxide (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%), imidazole (Aladdin, 99%), phosphoric acid (Aladdin, 85%), dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%, anhydrous), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Solef5130, Solvay, Mw ≈ 500,000 g mol−1), acetylene black (MTI Corporation, 99.9%), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%) were used as received. The electrolyte (1 M NaPF6 in DME) was purchased from Duoduo Chemical Co., Ltd. Copper foil (99.9% purity, 10 μm thickness, battery grade) was obtained from Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium lumps (99.9% purity) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The glass fiber membrane separator (Whatman GF/A) had a thickness of 260 µm, a diameter of 16 mm, a porosity of approximately 86%, and an average pore size of 1.6 µm. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](ImH2)2 powder (ZnPIm)

Zinc oxide (81 mg, 1 mmol), imidazole (136 mg, 2 mmol), ethanol (500 μL), and phosphoric acid (205 μL, 3 mmol) were combined in a mortar and ground for 30 min. The resulting powder was then evacuated at 100 °C overnight to obtain a dry compound in its pure phase.

Preparation of [Zn(HPO4)(H2PO4)2](ImH2)2 glass (g-ZnPIm)

The as-prepared ZnPIm MOF powder was heated at 158 °C under vacuum for 30 min before fast cooling to room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). During this low-temperature heating process, the ZnPIm MOF powder transformed into MOF liquid and underwent rapid vitrification to form MOF glass (g-ZnPIm). The as-prepared MOF glass was then used for further physical/chemical measurements.

Preparation of HC@g-ZnPIm

The HC powder was provided by BTR New Material Group Co., Ltd. To prepare the MOF glass-coated negative electrode, ZnPIm MOF powder was mixed with the HC negative electrode. MOF powders with different weight ratios of 0.5 wt.%, 1.0 wt.% and 2.0 wt.% were used for comparative experiments. Firstly, the mixed materials were ground in an argon-filled glovebox (both oxygen and water contents below 0.1 ppm) for 15 min and then collected (named HC@ZnPIm). Subsequently, the HC@ZnPIm powder was vacuum heated at 158 °C for 30 min, and then rapidly cooled to room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Thus, the MOF-glass coated negative electrode materials were successfully obtained and defined as HC@0.5 wt.% g-ZnPIm, HC@1.0 wt.% g-ZnPIm and HC@2.0 wt.% g-ZnPIm, respectively. The 1 wt.% coated HC negative electrode was finally determined to be the optimal sample and was uniformly denoted as HC@g-ZnPIm.

Electrochemical characterization

The negative electrode slurry was prepared by mixing the active material (HC or HC@g-ZnPIm), acetylene black (MTI Corporation, 99.9%) as the electron conductive additive, and PVDF (Solef5130, Solvay, Mw ≈ 500,000 g mol−1) as the binder in a weight ratio of 8:1:1. N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma‑Aldrich, 99.5%) was used as the solvent. The slurry was thoroughly mixed and then cast onto a copper foil current collector (Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd., 99.9% purity, 10 µm thickness, battery grade) using a manual film applicator (doctor blade) with a wet-film thickness of 100 μm. The copper foil was used as received without any surface pre-treatment. The coated electrode was dried at 100 °C for 12 h under vacuum to remove residual solvent. For coin-cell assembly, the electrode sheets were then cut into disks of 12 mm diameter using a manual disc cutter (MSK‑T10, MTI) with an active material mass loading of approximately 2.0 mg cm−2. Sodium lumps (99.9% purity, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) were mechanically scraped to remove the surface oxide layer, then rolled into 500 μm-thick foils using a manual hand-held stainless steel roller inside an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm, 25 ± 2 °C). The rolling pressure was applied manually and controlled by the final foil thickness, with no precise pressure value recorded. No chemical cleaning was performed. The foils were subsequently punched into 12 mm-diameter disks immediately before cell assembly, with no intermediate storage. The 2032 coin-type cells were assembled in the same Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm), employing the as-prepared sodium metal disks as the counter electrode, one layer of glass fiber separator (Whatman GF/A, Cytiva) as the separator, and 1 M NaPF6 in dimethoxyethane (DME) as the electrolyte. The separator was punched into disks with a diameter of 16 mm, with a nominal thickness of 260 μm, a nominal average pore size of 1.6 μm, and a nominal porosity of approximately 86%, as specified by the manufacturer’s datasheet. The amount of electrolyte used for a coin cell is 100 μL. For in-situ Raman spectroscopy measurements (to monitor the dynamic evolution of solvation structure during the cycle), the assembled cells adopted the same configuration as the coin cells, except that the electrolyte was replaced with a G2 solvent-based electrolyte (1 M NaPF6 in G2). This electrolyte was selected for its distinct and stable C-O-C vibrational peak, which facilitates accurate tracking of solvent molecule behavior. The Na+ storage performance was evaluated on the LAND battery test system (CT2000A), and the voltage range of Na| |HC half-cell can be set to 0.01–2.0 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were employed on an electrochemical workstation (VSP300) at different sweep rates (0.2–2.0 mV s–1). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted on an electrochemical workstation in the potentiostatic mode, with a sinusoidal perturbation amplitude of 5 mV. The tests were performed over a frequency range of 106 Hz to 10−2 Hz, with 10 data points per frequency decade. Prior to each EIS measurement, the cells were rested at open-circuit potential for 2 h to reach a quasi-stationary state, and all measurements were carried out at 25 ± 2 °C. Distribution relaxation times (DRT) were derived from EIS data using MATLAB R2021 with a toolbox of DRT-TOOLS. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) used a unique GCD mode. The test was conducted after the first three cycles, and its duty cycle includes two stages: a 5-min charge-discharge process at 0.02 A g−1, followed by a 0.5-h pause. All electrochemical measurements were carried out at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C).

Ex situ sample preparation for post-cycling characterization

Post-cycling electrode samples were harvested and processed following characterization-specific protocols, all performed inside an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm, 25 ± 2 °C). After disassembly of the coin cells, the electrodes were gently removed and rinsed three times with anhydrous dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma‑Aldrich, 99.5%) to remove residual electrolyte and soluble by-products. For transport to external characterization instruments, the dried samples were sealed in vacuum‑sealed containers to prevent any exposure to ambient air. The transfer was performed at 25 ± 2 °C, and the total time between disassembly and measurement was kept within 1 h for air‑sensitive analyses (XPS, TEM).

Characterizations

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) characterizations

The morphological features of the samples were investigated using a TOPCN DS-720 scanning electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, which enabled the observation of microscale surface topography, particle agglomeration, and overall morphological uniformity. For nanoscale structural analysis, a Tecnai F20 transmission electron microscope (FEI Company) equipped with a field-emission gun was utilized at 200 kV. For pristine powder samples, the materials were dispersed in anhydrous ethanol via mild ultrasonic treatment and deposited onto copper grids. For ex situ characterization of cycled electrodes, cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm, 25 ± 2 °C). The electrode disks were rinsed three times with anhydrous DME and dried in the glovebox, then gently scraped from the copper current collector, dispersed in anhydrous DME via gentle sonication, and drop-cast onto copper grids inside the glovebox. All grids were sealed in an argon-filled transfer holder before loading into the microscope to prevent air exposure.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) characterization

Crystalline phase identification and structural analysis were performed using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 5–30° in MOF powder and glass, as well as 5–80° in HC and HC@g-ZnPIm with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 2° min−1. The resulting diffraction patterns were analyzed using Bruker DIFFRAC.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) characterization

Thermal behavior, including phase transitions and thermal stability, was assessed using a DSC-TA Discovery 214 differential scanning calorimeter. Samples (~5 mg) were hermetically sealed in aluminum pans and subjected to heating/cooling cycles at a rate of 10 °C min−1 within a temperature range of 0 to 200 °C under a high-purity N2 atmosphere. The results clarify the thermal properties related to the operational stability of the material.

CO2 adsorption-desorption characterization

The specific surface area and pore structure were evaluated via CO2 adsorption-desorption measurements at 273 K using a Tristar II Plus 3030 analyzer. Prior to measurements, samples were degassed at 100 °C for 12 h under vacuum to remove adsorbed impurities. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was employed to calculate specific surface areas, while pore size distributions were derived from the adsorption branches using the quenched solid density functional theory (QSDFT) model, offering insights into the porous architecture.

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements

The experiment was conducted using the Xeuss 2.0 instrument (Xenocs) and with copper Kα radiation (wavelength λ = 1.5418 Å) in the transmission mode. The scattering intensity was recorded within the q range of 0.01–1.0 Å−1, where q = 4πsinθ/λ. The scattering curves were fitted with the spherical pore model using the SasView software, and the pore size distribution was obtained from the SAXS data. The characteristic pore diameter was estimated from the peak position using the Bragg equation d = 2π/q.

True density measurements

The specific gravity of the samples was accurately measured using a helium gas densitometer (product of MicrotracBEL Company). Before the analysis, the sample was degassed at 100 °C in a vacuum environment for 12 h to remove any adsorbed moisture or gases. The measurement was conducted at 25 ± 2 °C with a pumping time of 5 min, and 10 measurement cycles were performed. The reported accurate specific gravity value is the average of five independent tests.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) characterization

The chemical composition and bonding states of the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) were characterized using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS system equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV). For ex situ XPS characterization, cycled cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm, 25 ± 2 °C). The harvested electrodes were rinsed three times with anhydrous DME (1 mL per rinse) to remove residual electrolyte salts, followed by natural drying in the glovebox for 30 min to evaporate residual solvent. The prepared samples were sealed in an argon-tight transfer vessel and transported to the XPS instrument without air exposure. The depth XPS etching time ranges from 0 to 750 s.

Raman characterization

Structural information at the molecular level was obtained using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer equipped with a 633 nm laser excitation source. Measurements were conducted over a spectral range of 100–2000 cm−1 with a resolution of 0.5 cm−1. For data processing, the raw Raman spectra were first subjected to baseline correction using a linear or polynomial fitting method to eliminate the influence of fluorescence background and instrumental noise. Subsequently, the characteristic D-band (~1350 cm−1) and G-band (~1580 cm−1) were deconvoluted and fitted using a Lorentzian function to extract the precise peak positions, full width at half maximum (FWHM), and integrated intensities. The goodness of fit (R²) for all fittings was greater than 0.995, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the derived parameters (e.g., the intensity ratio of D-band to G-band, ID/IG) for evaluating the structural order and defect density of the carbon materials.

Data analysis and visualization

Electron microscopy images were acquired using the native acquisition software of the corresponding instrumentation. X-ray diffraction patterns, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy spectra and electrochemical performance curves were processed and plotted using OriginPro 2023b (OriginLab, USA). All figure panels were assembled and formatted using Adobe Illustrator CC 2023 (Adobe Inc., USA).
