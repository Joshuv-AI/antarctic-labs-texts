Emergency Heart Transplantation for Recurrent Infective Endocarditis With Irreparable Damage

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a life-threatening condition characterized by infection of the heart valves or endocardial surface. Traditionally, IE is managed with long courses of antibiotics, often followed by surgery for valve repair or replacement in cases of severe damage or complications such as heart failure, abscess formation, or persistent infection. However, in some cases, even these interventions may not be able to treat the disease.

The advent of heart transplantation (HTx) as a treatment for advanced heart failure has shifted the paradigm for cases where conventional therapies are inadequate. In cases of severe IE with irreversible valvular damage, perivalvular abscesses, and refractory heart failure, HTx may be the only viable option for survival. Despite its potential benefits, this approach is often underused, as guidelines typically prioritize surgical valve replacement or repair.

We describe a dramatic case of recurrent prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) with extensive perivalvular destruction that was successfully managed with HTx. This case not only illustrates the complexities of managing severe IE, but also provides a paradigm for considering transplantation in similarly challenging cases. This case highlights the critical role of HTx in the management of severe IE with extensive anatomic destruction and discusses the implications for current clinical practice and future guideline revisions. Although transplantation represents an extreme solution, it may be the only remaining option when anatomic destruction precludes conventional repair.

History of Presentation

A 61-year-old man presented with a 10-day history of chills and rigors, which worsened over the past day with fever (maximum temperature 38.5 °C), exertional dyspnea, and chest tightness. He also reported fatigue, headache, right calf myalgia, and right foot swelling and pain for the past 3 days. Initial laboratory tests revealed elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (164.88 mg/L), leukocytosis (white blood cell count 21.0 × 109/L), neutrophilia (84.9%), and lymphopenia (7.9%). He was admitted for further evaluation and management.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus for more than 2 years, initially treated with oral hypoglycemic agents for 6 months before self-discontinuation. Recent glucose levels were suboptimally controlled. Additional comorbidities included hepatitis B virus carrier status and aortic atherosclerosis. Notably, 2 years prior, he underwent 23-mm bovine pericardial bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement for IE on a bicuspid valve with severe stenosis, complicated by valve perforation and abscess formation, followed by a 6-week course of postoperative antibiotics.

Investigations

Blood cultures obtained on admission identified Staphylococcus epidermidis, confirming bloodstream infection. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed prosthetic aortic valve vegetations (29 × 9 mm, mobile with marked oscillation during the cardiac cycle) (Figure 1), severe paravalvular leak, valve dehiscence, and extensive perivalvular abscess formation. The aortic root was dilated with prominent bulging of the noncoronary sinus and a fistula between the right coronary sinus and right atrium. Additional findings included severe mitral regurgitation, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and vegetations adherent to the tricuspid annulus. Coronary imaging revealed a perivalvular abscess in the right coronary sinus (Figure 2A) accompanied by the formation of a right coronary sinus–to–right atrial fistula (Figure 2B). Coronary imaging demonstrated massive abscess cavities beneath the right coronary artery (RCA) and noncoronary sinuses, compressing the left atrium and eroding the right fibrous trigone (Figure 3). Vegetations invaded the proximal RCA, causing chronic occlusion, with embolic debris observed in the mid-RCA (Figure 4). High-sensitivity troponin T was elevated to 2090 ng/mL, consistent with myocardial injury secondary to coronary embolism. These findings collectively indicated extensive anatomic destruction secondary to IE, involving both valvular and perivalvular structures.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient's presentation of fever, chills, elevated inflammatory markers, and evidence of valvular and perivalvular destruction on imaging raised several potential diagnoses. The primary consideration was PVE due to S. epidermidis, supported by blood culture results, large mobile vegetations on the prosthetic aortic valve, and extensive perivalvular abscess formation. The presence of severe paravalvular leak, valve dehiscence, and fistulous communication between the RCA sinus and right atrium further corroborated this diagnosis.

The patient's acute chest pain, elevated high-sensitivity troponin T level, and electrocardiographic changes raised the possibility of acute myocardial infarction. Coronary imaging revealed chronic occlusion of the proximal RCA due to vegetation invasion, with embolic debris observed in the mid-RCA, confirming myocardial injury secondary to coronary embolism as a complication of PVE. This differentiated the condition from primary acute myocardial infarction secondary to atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, as the underlying mechanism was clearly related to septic embolization from IE.

Management

On admission, the patient was initiated on broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and supportive therapies to stabilize hemodynamics and manage heart failure. Surgical intervention was planned owing to the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction and perivalvular complications. However, during preoperative preparation, the patient's infection remained uncontrolled, with persistent fever and elevated inflammatory markers. Two days after transfer to the cardiac surgery department, he developed acute chest pain, profuse sweating, and hypotension (blood pressure 79/49 mm Hg). High-sensitivity troponin T was elevated to 2090 ng/mL, and coronary imaging confirmed chronic occlusion of the proximal RCA due to vegetation invasion, with embolic debris in the mid-RCA, leading to a diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction and cardiogenic shock.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for advanced management, where he experienced recurrent episodes of heart failure and septic shock. A multidisciplinary team, including cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and infectious disease specialists, reviewed the case. Given the extensive anatomic destruction, including severe paravalvular abscesses, a fistula between the RCA sinus and right atrium, and vegetations on both prosthetic aortic and tricuspid valves, conventional surgical repair was deemed insufficient. The presence of a large abscess cavity compressing the left atrium and eroding the right fibrous trigone, combined with chronic RCA occlusion due to vegetation invasion, posed significant risks of recurrent infection and severe myocardial ischemia postoperatively.

After thorough discussion, HTx was identified as the only viable treatment option. The patient underwent emergency orthotopic HTx. The intraoperative exploration demonstrated severe postsurgical adhesions and profound cardiac structural damage, featuring: 1) extensive vegetations on the aortic valve prosthesis (Figure 5A); 2) significant sinus dilation with circumferential perivalvular abscess formation (Figure 5B); 3) a well-developed sinus-to-atrial fistula; and 4) vegetations with severe mitral regurgitation (Figure 5C). The procedure was successfully completed (Figure 5D), and the patient was transferred back to the general ward after a period of stabilization in the intensive care unit.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperatively, the patient received a 6-week course of intravenous antibiotics to eradicate residual infection. He demonstrated gradual improvement in cardiac function and infection control. At 4-month follow-up, he remained clinically stable with no signs of recurrent infection, heart failure, or arrhythmias. He continued a standard immunosuppressive regimen without evidence of graft rejection.

Discussion

This dramatic case of recurrent PVE with catastrophic structural destruction underscores the limitations of conventional surgical strategies and illustrates the role of HTx as a salvage therapy for end-stage IE. Whereas current guidelines prioritize valve repair or replacement in IE, our patient's extensive perivalvular abscesses, fistulous tracts, and coronary involvement rendered traditional approaches futile. PVE caused by S. epidermidis is associated with a high mortality rate despite aggressive therapy, which is largely due to biofilm formation and resistance to host defenses, as evidenced by our patient's persistent bacteremia and vegetation progression despite broad-spectrum antibiotics. The development of acute myocardial infarction secondary to coronary embolization further exemplifies the systemic complications of IE, particularly when combined with hemodynamic instability. Notably, the coexistence of chronic RCA occlusion owing to direct vegetation invasion and acute mid-RCA embolism created a unique challenge, as revascularization was precluded by unresectable septic foci.

Current guidelines cautiously endorse HTx for IE, primarily in healed cases or cases with limited surgical alternatives1; however, our case challenges this conservative stance. The patient's recurrent PVE, compounded by aortic root destruction, tricuspid valve involvement, and coronary septic emboli, exceeded the reparative capacity of conventional surgery. Notably, even in the setting of uncontrolled infection, HTx can achieve successful outcomes, with a growing number of cases reported in recent literature.2 Importantly, HTx remains constrained by organ availability and institutional expertise, making case selection and timing critically dependent on local resources.

The transition from valve-centric surgery to HTx required rigorous multidisciplinary collaboration, balancing infection control (achieving bactericidal antibiotic levels despite active bacteremia), anatomic unsalvageability (erosion of the right fibrous trigone and left atrial compression by abscesses), and immunosuppression risks (tailored tacrolimus trough levels and prolonged antibiotics). This case exposes critical gaps in current IE management algorithms, as the 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines lack criteria for HTx timing or patient selection.1 We propose a framework for HTx consideration in IE, including anatomic criteria (eg, perivalvular abscesses involving >50% of the annulus, fistulae, or coronary involvement with irreversible ischemia), microbiological criteria (susceptible pathogens and ≥72 hours of effective antibiotics), and hemodynamic criteria (refractory cardiogenic shock).

The Asia-Pacific region faces particular challenges in IE management,3,4 where delayed presentation remains common. Our case underscores the need for both public health initiatives to improve recognition of persistent fever in prosthetic valve recipients and physician education about the rapid destructive potential of staphylococcal PVE.

The absence of recurrent infection at 4-month follow-up challenges the dogma that active IE contraindicates HTx, likely due to a 6-week antibiotic course overlapping with calcineurin inhibitor initiation and individualized immunosuppression. This case redefines the boundaries of surgical management in IE, demonstrating that HTx can achieve durable survival in patients with anatomically destructive, recurrent PVE when conventional repair is untenable. It calls for guideline revisions to incorporate HTx as a prioritized—not last-resort—option in end-stage IE with high-risk features. Future research should standardize selection criteria and optimize post-HTx protocols in this population.

Conclusions

When encountering irreparable IE with no conventional options, carefully selected patients may achieve survival through HTx as a last-resort measure, provided that multidisciplinary expertise and timely organ availability align. This case underscores that post-transplant immunosuppression did not compromise infection control despite active preoperative infection, challenging traditional contraindications. These findings may inform future guideline revisions to expand therapeutic options for end-stage IE.
