pH-responsive microgels containing multifunctional ginsenoside Rh2-based liposomes remodel intestinal environment to enhance celastrol therapy for ulcerative colitis
Authors: Peihong Lin, Zhouru Wang, Wenjing Yang, Xuelian Yang, Mengdie Yu, Jie Fang, Zhen Ma, Aizhen Zhou, Wenying Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease closely associated with intestinal mucosal barrier damage, abnormal immune responses, and gut microbiota dysbiosis. Celastrol (Cel), a natural triterpenoid compound, has shown great potential in modulating macrophage polarization and inhibiting inflammation. However, its clinical translation is hampered by poor solubility and nonspecific biodistribution. To address these issues, we developed a novel drug delivery system. Initially, functional liposomes (Rh2-LPs@Cel) were constructed by substituting cholesterol with ginsenoside Rh2. This strategy not only reduced drug leakage and enhanced the formulation stability, but also imparted intrinsic bioactivity to the carrier. Subsequently, these liposomes were coated with chitosan-sodium alginate to form a pH-responsive microgel (M/Rh2-LPs@Cel) for colon-targeted delivery. Experimental results showed that this microgel could precisely deliver drugs to the colon after oral administration. It effectively promoted the polarization of macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype, reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory factors, reshaped the diversity of the gut microbiota, elevated short-chain fatty acid levels, and repaired the intestinal epithelial barrier, thereby alleviating the colonic pathological damage induced by dextran sulfate sodium in mice. It is worth noting that M/Rh2-LPs@Cel did not show significant toxicity in vitro or in vivo. In conclusion, this work presents a promising approach for UC that combines efficient drug delivery, targeted release, and multiple therapeutic effects. Our findings provide a valuable foundation for the clinical application of natural bioactive compounds in inflammatory bowel disease management.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic relapsing inflammatory bowel disease with a continuously rising global incidence, posing a significant challenge to public health systems worldwide [1,2]. In current clinical practice, mild-to-moderate UC patients are primarily treated with first-line medications such as mesalazine, yet their non-specific anti-inflammatory mechanisms are often accompanied by adverse effects [3,4]. Although moderate-to-severe patients may be administered JAK inhibitors or biologics, these treatments face multiple limitations, including high costs, the risk of drug resistance, and long-term safety concerns [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In this context, the development of novel therapeutic strategies that combine high efficacy with an improved safety profile has become particularly urgent.

Research has demonstrated that the pathogenesis of UC is closely associated with immune homeostasis dysregulation, among which macrophage polarization imbalance serves as a critical driver of disease progression [9]. Within the UC intestinal microenvironment, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages are excessively activated, exacerbating tissue damage through massive release of inflammatory factors, while the polarization of repair-capable M2 macrophages is impaired, hindering inflammation resolution mechanisms [10]. This polarization imbalance not only perpetuates mucosal inflammation but also disrupts epithelial barrier integrity and disturbs microbial balance, forming a vicious cycle of “inflammation-barrier damage-dysbiosis” [11]. Therefore, modulating macrophage polarization to simultaneously achieve anti-inflammatory effects, barrier repair, and microbiota reconstruction represents a promising strategy for interrupting UC progression.

The natural product celastrol (Cel) has garnered considerable attention due to its unique immunomodulatory capabilities. Studies indicate that Cel not only promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway but also enhances intestinal barrier function by modulating short-chain fatty acid metabolism and suppresses pathogenic bacterial overgrowth, thereby multi-directionally restoring intestinal homeostasis [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, its extremely poor aqueous solubility and oral bioavailability (<20%), coupled with potential toxicity from non-targeted distribution, severely limit its clinical application [[16], [17], [18]]. To overcome these limitations, developing targeted delivery systems responsive to the colonic inflammatory microenvironment to achieve precise drug accumulation at lesion sites has become crucial for advancing Cel's clinical translation.

Liposomes (LPs) are widely utilized as efficient delivery vehicles for poorly soluble drugs; however, their conventional component, cholesterol (CHO), has raised safety concerns regarding potential cardiovascular side effects in long-term applications [19]. Guided by a “structure-function” substitution strategy, this study proposes utilizing Ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2) to replace CHO in the construction of novel functionalized LPs (Rh2-LPs). The rationale for this substitution extends beyond mere structural similarity; Rh2, a steroidal compound with intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties, functions as a “functional pharmaceutical excipient”. Crucially, Rh2 possesses a glycosyl moiety that exhibits high affinity for glucose transporter-1 (GLUT-1), a receptor significantly overexpressed on the surface of activated macrophages within inflamed tissues [[19], [20], [21]]. By integrating Rh2 into the lipid bilayer, we engineered multifunctional Rh2-LPs@Cel. This design not only circumvents the potential risks associated with inert CHO but also facilitates enhanced uptake by pro-inflammatory macrophages via Rh2-GLUT-1 interactions, thereby promoting intracellular drug accumulation. Nevertheless, as a nano-formulation, Rh2-LPs@Cel remains challenged by gastric acid degradation and inadequate accumulation at the lesion site during oral administration, necessitating an external protective carrier to ensure its targeted delivery to the colonic lesion site.

Natural polysaccharides, such as chitosan (CS) and sodium alginate (SA), by virtue of their superior biocompatibility, serve as ideal materials for constructing such a colon-specific delivery system. Through electrostatic cross-linking, the two polymers form pH-responsive microgels that maintain structural integrity in the acidic gastric environment, effectively preventing premature drug leakage in non-target regions. Conversely, upon exposure to the neutral to weakly alkaline environment of the colon, the electrostatic attraction between CS and SA is attenuated; simultaneously, the -COO- groups generated via SA deprotonation induce strong inter-chain repulsion. This phenomenon promotes rapid swelling and disintegration of the microgels, thereby realizing targeted drug release in the colon [22,23].

Based on this, the present study constructed a novel “nano-in-micro” cascade delivery system (Scheme 1). Specifically, a pH-responsive protective shell was fabricated via an electrospraying technique to encapsulate the Rh2-LPs@Cel, yielding the final construct designated as M/Rh2-LPs@Cel. This system synergistically integrates the pH-triggered release capability of the microgel with the cellular targeting specificity of the Rh2-functionalized LPs. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that this delivery system can effectively regulate macrophage polarization, promote intestinal barrier repair, and rebuild microbiota balance, significantly alleviating colitis progression while demonstrating good biocompatibility. Materials and methods

Materials

The following reagents were used in this study: Cel (purity ≥98%) was obtained from Shanghai D&B Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Rh2 (purity ≥98%) was acquired from Shanghai Bidepharm Technology Co., Ltd. Soybean lecithin and cholesterol (CHO) were sourced from AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. Chitosan (CS) (MW: 30000; degree of deacetylation ≥85%; Viscosity: 20-100 mPa s; Moisture: ≤10%) was purchased from Tuopu Technology (China) Co., Ltd. Sodium alginate (SA) (Purity ≥90%; Mannuronate: Glucuronate = 1:2; Viscosity: 200-550 mPa s) was obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Mouse ELISA kits for TNF-α and IL-10 were purchased from MultiSciences (Lianke) Biotech Co., Ltd. The myeloperoxidase (MPO) assay kit was obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS) (MW: 36,000–50,000) was procured from Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai). The SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit, Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Premix Kit, and SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix qPCR Kit were purchased from Changsha Ecorui Biology.

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from Hangzhou Jcellm Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Male C57BL/6 mice (20 ± 2 g) were provided by the Animal Center of Hangzhou Medical College. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility maintained at a relative humidity of 55 ± 10%, a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Welfare Committee of Hangzhou Medical College (Approval No. 2024-185).

Preparation of Rh2-LPs@Cel

LPs loaded with Cel were prepared using the ethanol injection method [24]. Initially, the drug and lipid materials were precisely weighed according to the mass ratio (phospholipid: Rh2: Cel = 10: 2.5: 1) and dissolved in anhydrous ethanol. The organic phase was uniformly and slowly injected into deionized water maintained at a constant temperature of 47 °C while continuous stirring was performed at 1500 rpm using a magnetic stirrer. The anhydrous ethanol was then evaporated under reduced pressure to obtain Rh2-LPs@Cel. Under identical conditions, CHO-LPs@Cel were prepared by substituting Rh2 with CHO.

Characterization of Rh2-LPs@Cel

The particle size and ζ-potential of the LPs were characterized using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer. Additionally, the morphology of the LPs was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with an HT7700 instrument (Hitachi, Japan). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of the LPs were assessed using low-speed centrifugation [19]. The samples were diluted with methanol and analyzed using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system.

Rh2-LPs@Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel were stored at 4 °C for one week to determine the stability. The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and EE were periodically assessed to provide an initial evaluation of the formulations' stability.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of Rh2-LPs@Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Free Cel was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.3 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight. Subsequently, Rh2-LPs@Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Free Cel were diluted to various Cel concentrations (50, 75, 100, 200, 300, and 400 ng/mL) and added to the wells for co-incubation with the cells for 24 h. Blank and control groups were also established. After 24 h of incubation, the CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance was obtained at 450 nm.

Cellular uptake and internalized mechanism

To assess the cellular internalization efficiency of different formulations by RAW264.7 cells, the fluorescent dye FITC was used as a surrogate for Cel to prepare fluorescently labeled LPs for cellular uptake studies. RAW264.7 cells were activated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h to establish an inflammatory cell model. Subsequently, the cells were treated with Rh2-LPs@FITC or CHO-LPs@FITC for 4 h. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and DAPI was applied for nuclear staining. Cellular internalization was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). For quantitative analysis, identically treated cells were analyzed for fluorescence intensity by flow cytometry, collecting 10,000 events per sample.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying cellular uptake, LPS-induced macrophages were pretreated overnight with the specific GLUT-1 inhibitor STF-31 (10 μM), following protocols adapted from previous studies [20]. For clarity, we designate the inhibitor-treated group as “MI”. Cellular uptake was subsequently assessed via CLSM and quantified by flow cytometry. To further assess alternative endocytic pathways, LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells were pretreated for 1 h with chlorpromazine (CPZ, 5 μg/mL, clathrin inhibitor), indomethacin (IND, 3 μg/mL, caveolin inhibitor), or amiloride (AMI, 8 μg/mL, macropinocytosis inhibitor). A parallel group was kept at 4 °C to assess energy dependence, and untreated cells at 37 °C served as blank controls. CHO-LPs@FITC or Rh2-LPs@FITC was then incubated for 4 h, after which intracellular fluorescence was quantified by flow cytometry and normalized to the control group.

Molecular docking

The GLUT1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 6THA) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank to investigate the binding interactions of Rh2 and CHO with GLUT1. Molecular docking simulations were performed using Maestro 11.8 to assess the binding affinities of Rh2 and CHO to GLUT1. The optimized GLUT1 structure was individually docked with Rh2 and CHO, and their respective binding energies were calculated. The binding poses and interaction patterns of the docked complexes were visualized using Discovery Studio 2016 and PyMOL 3.0.3.

Immunofluorescence and ELISA

Immunofluorescence was employed to assess the effect of drugs on the polarization of RAW264.7 cells [21,22]. The fluorescence intensity and distribution of CD86 and CD206 proteins were measured to quantitatively analyze cell polarization. The cytokine levels in RAW264.7 cell culture supernatant was determined by an ELISA kit.

Preparation of microgels

M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was prepared using the electrospray technique [23]. To ensure the robustness and reproducibility of the microgel fabrication, key process parameters were strictly controlled.First, sodium alginate (SA) was dissolved in deionized water to prepare a 1% (w/v) solution. Separately, a mixed solution A was prepared by dissolving chitosan (CS, 0.1% w/v) and CaCl2 (0.5% w/v) in 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid. The pH of both the SA solution and mixed solution A was adjusted to 5.5 - 6.0. Rh2-LPs@Cel was added to the SA solution and stirred thoroughly to obtain a homogeneous mixture B. This mixture was then injected into mixed solution An under a constant high-voltage electrostatic field of 10-12 kV. The flow rate was maintained at 4 mL/min, and the distance between the needle tip and the surface of solution A was fixed at 10 cm. Under these optimized conditions, the electrostatic force overcomes the surface tension, generating uniform droplets that undergo instant ionic gelation and electrostatic cross-linking upon contacting solution A, thereby forming spherical M/Rh2-LPs@Cel microgels. After standing for 5 min, the microgels were collected, rapidly rinsed three times with deionized water, lyophilized, and stored at 4 °C. Blank microgels were prepared following the same procedure except for the omission of Cel.

Characterization of microgels

To assess batch-to-batch consistency, samples of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel were randomly collected from three independent preparation batches. An appropriate amount of microgels was photographed under the inverted microscope, and the diameters of no fewer than 1000 randomly selected microgels per batch were measured and analyzed using the inverted microscope. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the detailed surface morphology of the samples.

M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) to evaluate intermolecular interactions, thermal stability, and crystallinity. Its EE and DL were determined by HPLC.

Swelling behavior and in vitro release profile

The swelling behavior of lyophilized M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was investigated by measuring the weight change of pre-weighed samples (M0) after immersion in buffers of pH 1.2, 6.8, and 7.4 at 37 °C over 12 h; the swelling ratio was calculated from the final weight (M1). The in vitro release profiles of Rh2-LPs@Cel and M/Rh2-LPs@Cel were studied using a dialysis method [25]. Samples equivalent to 1.0 mg of Cel were sealed in pre-soaked dialysis bags (MWCO 12–14 kDa) and immersed in 50 mL of release medium containing 15% (v/v) ethanol to maintain sink conditions. To simulate gastrointestinal transit, the bags were sequentially incubated in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, 0.1 M HCl, pH 1.2) for 2 h, simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, phosphate buffer, pH 6.8) for 2 h, and simulated colonic fluid (SCF, phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for the remaining 8 h at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. At 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h, 1.0 mL aliquots were withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. The samples were analyzed by HPLC to determine the cumulative release of Cel.

Biodistribution of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel

Acute colitis was induced in male C57BL/6 mice by administering 3% (w/v) DSS in their drinking water ad libitum for 7 days. This dosage and duration were chosen based on established protocols to ensure the induction of consistent and moderate-to-severe intestinal inflammation with a predictable clinical course [26]. The fluorescent probe DiR (final concentration of 20 μg/mL) was utilized as a surrogate for Cel to label the LPs and microgels. Subsequently, mice were randomly assigned into two groups (n = 12) and administered treatments of Rh2-LPs@DiR and M/Rh2-LPs@DiR, respectively. At predetermined time points (1, 4, 8, and 12 h), mice were euthanized, and the entire gastrointestinal tract was excised. A multifunctional in vivo imaging system was employed to evaluate the distribution of the probes within the mice and to perform semi-quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity in the isolated tissues.

In vivo efficacy

Mice were randomly assigned to one of the following six groups (n = 6): Control group (administered drinking water), UC model group (administered 3% DSS), Free Cel group (administered 3% DSS + Free Cel), CHO-LPs@Cel group (administered 3% DSS + CHO-LPs@Cel), Rh2-LPs@Cel group (administered 3% DSS + Rh2-LPs@Cel), and M/Rh2-LPs@Cel group (administered 3% DSS + M/Rh2-LPs@Cel). Drug administration was performed by gavage in the intervention groups for 7 consecutive days, commencing on the 4th day of DSS-induced UC. Each mouse received a volume of 200 μL, with a Cel dose of 2 mg/kg [27]. Mice in the Control and UC model groups were gavaged with an equivalent volume of saline. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was recorded throughout the experiment according to the standard protocol, which serves as a critical metric for assessing the severity of colitis in mice.

At the conclusion of the administration period, mice were humanely euthanized, and blood samples were collected for the measurement of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) levels to evaluate liver and kidney function. Additionally, colons were harvested to measure their lengths and were photographed for macroscopic assessment. To investigate pathological alterations, colons and five major organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histopathological scoring of the colon tissues was independently performed by two researchers blinded to the group assignments. As previously described [28], the total histological score (range 0–11) was calculated based on the sum of three parameters: inflammation severity (0, rare cells; 1, increased granulocytes; 2, extension to submucosa; 3, transmural infiltration); crypt damage (0, intact; 1, basal 1/3 loss; 2, basal 2/3 loss; 3, entire crypt loss; 4, epithelial erosion; 5, confluent erosion); and ulceration (0, none; 1, 1–2 foci; 2, 3–4 foci; 3, extensive). The final score for each sample was determined as the average of the scores from the two independent observers. The remaining colon tissues were utilized for qPCR assays to quantify the cytokine levels, as well as MPO levels.

To evaluate the functional integrity of the intestinal barrier, the expression levels of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in colon tissues were examined via immunofluorescence staining [29]. Additionally, macrophage polarization was evaluated via double immunofluorescence staining to detect the co-expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). For quantitative analysis, three random non-overlapping fields were selected from each tissue section. The relative fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software, and the average value was calculated for each sample.

16S rRNA sequencing of intestinal microbiota

On day 10, following euthanasia, fecal samples were collected from the mice for 16S rRNA sequencing analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from the fecal samples using a standardized protocol. Subsequently, the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified using specific primers. The amplified products were subjected to high-throughput sequencing to elucidate the composition and structure of the microbial community across different experimental groups.

Analysis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in cecal contents

Cecal contents (30 mg) were homogenized using a tissue grinder. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and derivatized. The target metabolites were subsequently detected and analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) using a Waters I-Class system coupled to an AB Sciex QTrap 5500 mass spectrometer.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).

Results

Characterization of Rh2-LPs@Cel

Rh2-LPs@Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel were successfully prepared via the ethanol injection method (Fig. 1A). TEM images revealed that both Rh2-LPs@Cel (Fig. 1B) and CHO-LPs@Cel (Fig. 1C) exhibited a similar spherical morphology with a more uniform particle size distribution. DLS analysis further demonstrated that the average particle size of Rh2-LPs@Cel (153.9 ± 3.4 nm) was significantly smaller than that of CHO-LPs@Cel (167.7 ± 1.9 nm). This observation is in agreement with previous report [29,30], indicating that the substitution of CHO with Rh2 effectively reduces the particle size of LPs. Additionally, zeta potential measurements (Fig. 1D) revealed that Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited a higher negative surface charge potential (−23.57 ± 0.33 mV) compared to CHO-LPs@Cel (−18.10 ± 0.17 mV). The significantly enhanced zeta potential of Rh2-LPs@Cel nanoparticles indicates a stronger electrostatic repulsion, which predicts a substantial increase in their colloidal stability. Over a one-week period, the particle size and PDI of Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited minimal changes (Fig. 1E), whereas CHO-LPs@Cel showed a more pronounced increase in particle size (Fig. 1F), with the size growing from 167.7 nm to 182 nm. HPLC results showed that the EE of Rh2-LPs@Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel were 91.37 ± 0.52% and 90.17 ± 0.64%, respectively, with corresponding DL values of 6.86 ± 0.03% and 6.84 ± 0.04%, respectively. Although both formulations exhibited high EE and DL, the drug leakage rate of Rh2-LPs@Cel remained below 5% during storage, whereas CHO-LPs@Cel demonstrated a drug leakage rate exceeding 10% within one week (Fig. 1G). These findings suggest that Rh2-LPs@Cel possesses superior stability compared to CHO-LPs@Cel. Cytotoxicity of Rh2-LPs@Cel

As illustrated in Fig. 1H, when the Cel concentration was below 100 ng/mL, the cell viability of three treatment groups (Free Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Rh2-LPs@Cel) exceeded 80%, indicating minimal cytotoxicity within this concentration range. However, as the Cel concentration increased, cell viability decreased to varying extents across all groups. Notably, the Free Cel group showed a sharp inhibitory effect, while the CHO-LPs@Cel and Rh2-LPs@Cel groups exhibited greater tolerance to the drug. Specifically, at a Cel concentration of 200 ng/mL, the cell viability of the Free Cel group dropped to 68%, whereas that of the Rh2-LPs@Cel group remained at approximately 87%. This finding suggests that encapsulation of Cel within Rh2-LPs significantly mitigates its cytotoxicity.

Based on these cytotoxicity results, to ensure that subsequent experiments were not confounded by significant cytotoxic effects, a Cel concentration of 100 ng/mL was selected for the assessment of anti-inflammatory activity. This concentration was chosen to balance the therapeutic potential of Cel while maintaining cell viability above 80%, thereby providing a suitable condition for evaluating the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the different formulations.

Cellular uptake and internalized mechanism

The cellular internalization efficiency of different formulations by macrophages under different physiological states was evaluated using CLSM and flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 2A, RAW264.7 cells in the inactivated state (designated as M0) exhibited minimal uptake of both Rh2-LPs@FITC and CHO-LPs@FITC, as evidenced by weak green fluorescence. However, following LPS stimulation to induce the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, a significant enhancement in uptake was observed for both preparations. Notably, the Rh2-LPs@FITC group displayed markedly stronger fluorescence intensity compared to the CHO-LPs@FITC group. Quantitative analysis by flow cytometry (Fig. 2B and C) confirmed these observations, revealing that the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the Rh2-LPs@FITC group was 5 times higher than that in the CHO-LPs@FITC group. This indicates that activated macrophages possess a significantly stronger capacity to internalize Rh2-LPs in the inflammatory microenvironment. This phenomenon may be attributed to the glycosidic moieties within the Rh2 structure and the upregulation of GLUT-1 in activated macrophages [31,32]. To investigate if GLUT-1 mediates this uptake, since it is upregulated in M1 macrophages, we pretreated LPS-stimulated macrophages with STF-31 (designated as MI). Fig. 2A shows that the Rh2-LPs@FITC group had a large drop in fluorescence intensity after inhibitor treatment. In contrast, the CHO-LPs@FITC group showed little reduction in fluorescence intensity. Flow cytometry analysis supported these findings (Fig. 2B and C). M1 cells pretreated with STF-31 showed about a 29% reduction in Rh2-LPs@FITC uptake, while CHO-LPs@FITC uptake decreased by only 10%. The significant disparity in inhibition rates indicates that the efficient uptake of Rh2-LPs by M1 macrophages is partially dependent on GLUT-1-mediated recognition pathways.

Molecular docking simulations were performed to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying this interaction. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the glycosidic part of Rh2 forms hydrogen bonds with key amino acids in GLUT-1, specifically GLN282, GLN283, GLN288, and ASN411. This binding pattern matches the critical residues for glucose binding reported in previous studies [33]. The docking data showed that Rh2 binds more strongly than CHO. The docking score for the Rh2-GLUT-1 complex was −9.979, which is significantly lower than that of the CHO-GLUT-1 complex (−7.737), indicating stronger affinity. Similarly, the Glide energy for Rh2 (−46.254 kcal/mol) was much lower than that for CHO (−34.787 kcal/mol) (Fig. 2F and G). These results confirm that Rh2 interacts with GLUT-1 more stably and strongly than CHO. This provides a structural reason for the efficient uptake of Rh2-LPs via GLUT-1.

Beyond GLUT-1-mediated internalization, we further investigated the contribution of alternative endocytic pathways to the uptake of CHO-LPs@FITC and Rh2-LPs@FITC by activated macrophages. As shown in Fig. S1, intracellular fluorescence was markedly reduced at 4 °C for both formulations, indicating an energy-dependent uptake process. Pretreatment with CPZ and AMI significantly inhibited cellular uptake, whereas IND showed only a limited inhibitory effect. These findings suggest that, although Rh2-LPs@FITC uptake is partially dependent on GLUT-1, multiple endocytic pathways, particularly clathrin-mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis, also contribute to cellular internalization.

Rh2-LPs@Cel regulated macrophage polarization

To evaluate the regulatory effects of different preparations on macrophage polarization, we examined the expression levels of CD86 (an M1-type marker) and CD206 (an M2-type marker) using immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 2H, compared with the unstimulated group, the LPS-stimulated model group exhibited typical M1-type polarization characteristics, as evidenced by significantly enhanced expression of CD86 (red fluorescence) and almost no expression of CD206 (green fluorescence), confirming the successful establishment of the pro-inflammatory macrophage model. After interventions with free Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Rh2-LPs@Cel, the fluorescence signal intensities of CD86 and CD206 were altered to varying extents. Among them, the Rh2-LPs@Cel group showed the most significant changes, manifested as a significant attenuation of red fluorescence and a significant enhancement of green fluorescence. This observation was further corroborated by quantitative analysis (Fig. 2I and J). Compared with the free Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel groups, the intensity of red fluorescence (CD86) in the Rh2-LPs@Cel group decreased by 1.8-fold and 1.4-fold, respectively, while the intensity of green fluorescence (CD206) increased by 1.9-fold and 1.3-fold, respectively. The superior ability of Rh2-LPs@Cel to inhibit CD86 and promote CD206 expression is likely attributed to the Rh2-mediated enhanced cellular uptake, which ensures higher intracellular drug concentrations within M1 macrophages, thereby efficiently reprogramming them toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.

In vitro anti-inflammatory activity

In this study, the secretion levels of TNF-α and IL-10 in macrophage supernatants were measured using ELISA. Compared with the unstimulated control group, TNF-α levels in the LPS-induced Model group were significantly increased (Fig. 2I). After intervention with different preparations, TNF-α secretion was significantly attenuated in all administered groups. Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect, with a 56% reduction in TNF-α secretion level compared to the Model group (34% and 13% reduction in the Free Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel groups, respectively). Additionally, the level of IL-10 in the Rh2-LPs@Cel group was significantly elevated, being 2.4 times and 1.6 times higher than that in the Free Cel and CHO-LPs@Cel groups, respectively (Fig. 2L). The significantly stronger in vitro anti-inflammatory activity observed with Rh2-LPs@Cel is consistent with its high cellular internalization efficiency. By efficiently entering macrophages via the Rh2-GLUT-1 interaction, the formulation can more effectively regulate the polarization state and inhibit the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines while promoting the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines.

Construction and characterization of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel

M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was successfully fabricated by electrospraying. Strict control over the electrostatic parameters (voltage, flow rate, and collecting distance) ensured high operational robustness. The microgel was uniformly spherical under the inverted microscope (Fig. 3A). The particle size analysis revealed a distribution mainly in the range of 250-450 μm (Fig. 3B), which is consistent with findings from previous studies [30]. Notably, this particle size distribution exhibited high reproducibility across three independent batches, confirming the stability of the fabrication process. After lyophilization, M/Rh2-LPs@Cel displayed a three-dimensional polygonal structure with flat wrinkles and collapses under SEM (Fig. 3C). In addition, M/Rh2-LPs@Cel had excellent drug-carrying properties, with an EE and DL of 92.07 ± 1.10% and 4.32 ± 1.03%, respectively. FT-IR results showed (Fig. 3D) that the characteristic absorption peaks of CS were located at 3437 cm−1, 1652 cm−1, 1603 cm−1, and 1066 cm−1, which are attributed to -OH/-NH, -C=O, -NH, and -C-O stretching or bending vibrations, respectively [23]. While the characteristic absorption peaks of SA were located at 3448 cm−1, 1614 cm−1, 1420 cm−1, and 1030 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of -OH, symmetric stretching vibration of -C=O, asymmetric stretching vibration of -C=O, and stretching vibration of -CN, respectively [23]. The lipid-like bilayer interface of Rh2-LPs@Cel can be characterized by absorption peaks located at 3437 cm−1, 1635 cm−1, and 1051 cm−1 attributed to -OH, -C=O and -P=O stretching vibrations [28]. In the blank microgel, the stretching vibration peak of -NH in CS showed a significant shift from the characteristic peak of -C=O in SA. This phenomenon can be attributed to the electrostatic interaction between -COO- in SA and -NH3+ in CS [34]. In contrast, the FT-IR profile of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was similar to that of the blank microgel, proving that the incorporation of Rh2-LPs@Cel did not affect the gel-forming structure of the microgel. The XRD results (Fig. 3E) demonstrated hump-shaped characteristic peaks at 2θ = 20.1°and 2θ = 25.5° for CS and SA, respectively, suggesting that both belong to the amorphous or semicrystalline state [31]. However, the XRD patterns of blank microgels showed strong and narrow multi-peak features indicating the formation of crystalline states [31,32]. This suggests that CS and SA undergo chemical reactions in the solution state, and the formation of crystalline states further confirms the stability of the system structure [28]. Meanwhile, the XRD pattern of Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited an amorphous hump feature. In contrast, the XRD pattern of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel showed that although the intensity of the peaks was weakened, the sharp crystalline peaks still existed. This phenomenon indicated that the microgel successfully encapsulated Rh2-LPs@Cel within its structure. DSC analysis (Fig. 3F) showed that the characteristic peaks of CS were at 103 °C (absorptive dehydration peak) and 317 °C (exothermic degradation peak) [35]. The characteristic peaks of SA were located at 96 °C (absorptive dehydration peak) and 254 °C (exothermic degradation peak) [32]. When CS and SA were physically mixed, the apparent double degradation peaks at 264 °C and 315 °C indicated no specific reaction between CS and SA. However, the exothermic peaks of CS and SA disappeared in the DSC profiles of the blank microgels, which might be due to the chemical cross-linking reaction between the -NH3+ in CS and the -COO- in SA, rather than mere physical mixing. Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited characteristic exothermic peaks at 339 °C [36], but these peaks disappeared in M/Rh2-LPs@Cel, suggesting that the three-dimensional network structure of the microgel effectively encapsulated Rh2-LPs@Cel. The thermodynamic behaviors of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel were highly consistent with those of the blank microgels, further confirming the successful encapsulation of Rh2-LPs@Cel within the microgel network.

Swelling and in vitro release behavior

Fig. 3G presents the swelling curves of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel in different pH media in vitro. It was found that the swelling behavior of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel is characterized by initial water absorption and swelling, followed by a tendency toward equilibrium. In the acidic environment with pH = 1.2, the swelling ratio of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel eventually stabilized at 144%. However, in the medium with pH = 6.8, the swelling ratio increased significantly to 1374%. Furthermore, in the medium with pH = 7.4, the peak swelling rate of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel increased by approximately 34-fold compared to that in the acidic environment (pH = 1.2) and eventually stabilized at 4844%, which was significantly higher than the swelling rates observed in the other two groups. The significant swelling at pH 7.4 indicates that the microgel network expands rapidly in the colonic environment, facilitating drug release. These results confirmed that M/Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibits remarkable pH-responsive swelling behavior. To further evaluate its release performance, we analyzed the drug release characteristics of the system under different pH conditions through in vitro drug release experiments (Fig. 3H). Rh2-LPs@Cel showed significant drug release in SGF (pH = 1.2) and SIF (pH = 6.8), with cumulative release rates of approximately 25% and 42%, respectively. In SCF (pH = 7.4), the release rate of Rh2-LPs@Cel remained relatively high, with a cumulative release of about 77% at 12 h. In contrast, M/Rh2-LPs@Cel demonstrated prominent pH sensitivity. In SGF, M/Rh2-LPs@Cel exhibited minimal drug release. However, drug release increased in SIF, with a cumulative release rate of about 11%. The drug release rate of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel was significantly accelerated in SCF, with a cumulative release of about 64% at 12 h. These results indicate that M/Rh2-LPs@Cel can effectively avoid premature drug release in simulated gastric juice and achieve rapid drug release in simulated colon fluid, thereby exhibiting potential colon-targeting characteristics.

Biodistribution of M/Rh2-LPs@DiR

The in vivo targeting distribution of M/Rh2-LPs@DiR was evaluated using small animal in vivo imaging. Over time, the Rh2-LPs@DiR group exhibited persistent fluorescent signals in the stomach at 4 h, which significantly weakened by 8 h and nearly disappeared by 12 h. In contrast, the M/Rh2-LPs@DiR group showed a gradual increase in fluorescence signal intensity at 4 h, which peaked at 8 h and remained strong at 12 h, with signals primarily concentrated in the intestinal region. Fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis of the isolated digestive system further confirmed these observations (Fig. 4B and C). At 4 and 8 h post-administration, the average fluorescence intensity in the colon tissue of the M/Rh2-LPs@DiR group was 1.4 and 2.5 times higher, respectively, than that observed in the Rh2-LPs@DiR group. The rapid disappearance of Rh2-LPs@DiR may be associated with structural instability and subsequent clearance in the acidic gastric environment [37]. MPO is a heme peroxidase predominantly found in neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages. Elevated MPO activity is typically associated with the exacerbation of inflammatory responses and is closely correlated with the severity of inflammation. As such, MPO activity serves as a valuable biomarker for evaluating the severity of colitis. As depicted in Fig. 5H, the MPO activity in the colonic tissues of the Model group was significantly elevated, reaching approximately 4 times higher than that in the Control group. Each treatment group exhibited varying degrees of MPO activity inhibition, with MPO activity in the M/Rh2-LPs@Cel group decreased to 30% of that in the Model group. These findings indicate that M/Rh2-LPs@Cel exerts a significant protective effect against DSS-induced colitis in mice, effectively mitigating inflammation and tissue damage.

M/Rh2-LPs@Cel promoted repair of intestinal barrier damage

In this study, the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Occludin) in colonic tissues was evaluated by immunofluorescence techniques. As shown in Fig. 6G, the Control group exhibited robust ZO-1 (red) and Occludin (green) fluorescent signals, indicative of an intact intestinal barrier structure. Conversely, in the DSS-induced UC model group, the fluorescent signal intensity of both ZO-1 and Occludin was significantly diminished, reflecting severe impairment of intestinal barrier function. After drug intervention, all treatment groups demonstrated varying degrees of barrier repair. Among the treatment groups, Free Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Rh2-LPs@Cel were able to partially restore the expression of ZO-1 and Occludin. However, the M/Rh2-LPs@Cel group exhibited the most remarkable restoration of fluorescence signal intensity, with an expression pattern that closely resembled that of the Control group. The superior barrier repair observed with M/Rh2-LPs@Cel is likely a downstream consequence of its potent anti-inflammatory and macrophage-reprogramming effects, which reduce the inflammatory assault on the epithelium and create a favorable microenvironment for barrier restoration. These data demonstrate that M/Rh2-LPs@Cel can significantly upregulate the expression of tight junction proteins, thereby effectively repairing the damaged intestinal barrier function.

Biosafety assessment

To ensure the clinical translational potential of the constructed nano delivery systems, we comprehensively evaluated their biosafety both in vitro and in vivo. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, even at a high blank vector concentration of 500 μg/mL, the viability of RAW264.7 macrophages remained above 80% for both Rh2-LPs and CHO-LPs. This finding indicates that these nano delivery systems exhibited negligible cytotoxicity within the concentration range of 25-500 μg/mL. This excellent biocompatibility may be attributed to the use of FDA-approved pharmaceutical excipients (such as SPC and CHO) in the formulation, combined with the strict control of the working concentration of Rh2 within a safe range [46]. For in vivo safety evaluation, we monitored key indices of liver and kidney function in mice throughout the treatment period (Fig. 8B–E). Compared with the Control group, the serum levels of AST, ALT, and CREA in the Model, Free Cel, CHO-LPs@Cel, and Rh2-LPs@Cel groups exhibited varying degrees of change. However, from a physiological perspective, these fluctuations remained within the normal range of variation for liver and kidney function in mice. Meanwhile, none of the indices in the M/Rh2-LPs@Cel group showed significant differences compared to the Control group. Therefore, it can be concluded that the studied formulation was safe for liver and kidney function in mice during administration. This safety profile was further corroborated by histological analysis via H&E staining (Fig. 8F), which revealed clear and intact histological structures in the major organs of the Control group, with no evident signs of damage. In contrast, the spleens of mice in the Model group exhibited marked dilation of the red pulp, along with blurring of the red pulp–white pulp junction (marginal zone) structure. This pathological change may be associated with gastrointestinal bleeding induced by chronic colitis due to DSS administration, which in turn triggers compensatory activation of the spleen's extramedullary hematopoietic function [47]. Among the treatment groups, only the Free Cel group exhibited mild congestion, while no pathological damage to the heart, liver, lungs, kidneys, or other vital organs was observed in any group. Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we developed a novel therapeutic strategy for UC by constructing functionalized LPs loaded with Cel and further encapsulating them within a polysaccharide-based microgel, forming an M/Rh2-LPs@Cel cascade delivery system. Although pH-responsive microgels have been widely explored for colon-targeted delivery, most existing systems remain primarily limited to macroscopic physical barrier construction and pH-triggered site-specific release [48]. Following release in the colon, drugs often undergo non-specific passive diffusion, making it difficult for them to be selectively recognized and efficiently internalized by key pathological cells, such as pro-inflammatory macrophages. This cellular-level delivery barrier results in limited intracellular bioavailability even when the drug is enriched in colonic tissues, often necessitating higher doses to achieve therapeutic concentrations.

To overcome these limitations, we replaced the inert excipient CHO in the liposomal membrane with the bioactive component Rh2, thereby conferring functional properties to the liposomal bilayer while maintaining its structural stability. In this design, the glycosyl moiety of Rh2 may facilitate the uptake and intracellular accumulation of LPs via interactions with activated macrophages, thus enabling a shift from a conventional “inert excipient” to a “functional pharmaceutical excipient”. Meanwhile, the outer polysaccharide-based microgel effectively overcomes the biological barriers associated with oral delivery, providing gastric protection and enabling colon-specific pH-triggered drug release. Although the Rh2 dose used in this study (5 mg/kg) was significantly lower than those previously reported for Rh2 monotherapy in DSS-induced colitis models (typically ≥50 mg/kg) [49,50], the targeted enrichment of the cascade system within the colonic mucosa may result in elevated local concentrations of Rh2. Consequently, we cannot exclude the possibility that Rh2 may contribute locally to the overall efficacy, either through its own mild pharmacological activity or via a synergistic effect with Cel in the inflamed colon. Therefore, future studies will include a blank Rh2-LPs control group or pharmacologically inert Rh2 analogs to further clarify the role of Rh2 in this delivery system.

Based on the above design strategy, M/Rh2-LPs@Cel demonstrated marked therapeutic efficacy in the DSS-induced colitis model by promoting anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine release, restoring gut microbiota balance, and increasing SCFA levels, thereby promoting intestinal barrier repair. Compared with similar oral delivery systems reported in recent years (Table S1), this delivery system combines the pH-responsive drug release behavior of polysaccharide-based microgels with the intrinsic advantages of natural product-derived functional carriers, which not only promotes drug accumulation in the colon but also enhances the uptake of the formulation by activated macrophages without the need for exogenous targeting ligands [[51], [52], [53]]. Furthermore, the therapeutic regimen also demonstrated favorable biosafety, suggesting its potential for clinical translation as an oral delivery platform for UC treatment.

In summary, the M/Rh2-LPs@Cel developed in this study shows considerable potential for the oral delivery of Cel in UC therapy. However, its clinical translation still faces several key challenges. First, while the electrospraying technique enables precise control over the particle size and morphology of M/Rh2-LPs@Cel, its relatively low yield limits large-scale application. Future translational research needs to evaluate high-efficiency alternatives to achieve the production efficiency required for clinical use. Second, the human gastrointestinal environment is complex and variable, characterized by dynamic pH gradients, transit times, and enzyme activities; therefore, validation of microgel stability using physiologically relevant models (e.g., dynamic digestion models) is necessary. Third, comprehensive long-term toxicological assessments must be conducted to analyze potential bioaccumulation and confirm the biosafety of the system. Addressing these challenges are crucial for the future successful clinical application of this delivery system.
