Cost-effective lanthanum-promoted iron catalyst on coal gangue-derived mesoporous γ-Al2O3 for SO2 catalytic reduction
Authors: Mohammad Sepehrian, Mansoor Anbia, Fatemeh Yazdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A highly active and stable SO2 reduction catalyst, 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3, was successfully synthesized using γ-Al2O3 derived from coal gangue. The structural properties of the synthesized catalyst were analyzed using XPS, SEM, TEM, EDS, BET, XRD, and H2-TPR. Characterization studies revealed a high BET specific surface area of 288.55 m2/g for the coal gangue-derived mesoporous γ-Al2O3. Furthermore, lanthanum doping inhibited iron crystallite growth, enhancing dispersion on the support and contributing to superior catalytic performance. Under optimized conditions (380 °C, 6000 h−1 GHSV, and a CO/SO2 ratio of 2), the catalyst achieved 98.92 ± 1.3% SO2 conversion and 99.43 ± 0.7% selectivity to elemental sulfur. This high performance remained stable for over 50 h, demonstrating the catalyst’s potential for industrial application. The utilization of coal gangue-derived γ-Al2O3 offers both economic and environmental benefits by providing a cost-effective support material and addressing coal gangue disposal challenges. This work presents a promising strategy for sustainable SO2 abatement and resource utilization.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of various industries, particularly steel and power generation, coupled with the rising reliance on fossil fuels for example oil and coal, has caused a sharp rise in annual sulfur dioxide (SO2) emissions1–3. For instance, SO2 emissions from coal combustion alone can reach up to 20% by weight. SO2 is a pungent, colorless gas and a major air pollutant. Mammalian exposure to SO2 can cause various health problems, including DNA damage, respiratory illnesses, oxidative organ damage, and genetic mutations. Furthermore, SO2 aids in the formation of photochemical smog and acid rain, causing damage to soil and vegetation, thereby impacting ecosystems and raising significant public health concerns4,5. Economic damage resulting from deterioration of infrastructure and buildings is also a considerable consequence. So, the WHO and the EPA have established emission standards to mitigate the detrimental effects of SO2 pollution. The EPA modified its National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for the primary one-hour SO2 standard from 140 ppb to 75 ppb in 20121. Similarly, European Union policymakers, through Regulation (EU) 2016/2284, adhere to this global trend, mandating a 70% reduction in SO2 emissions by 20296.

Wet and dry scrubbing, traditional gas desulfurization techniques, utilize aqueous solutions of alkali and alkaline earth metals, and dry alkaline earth metal salts for example limestone slurry and calcium hydroxide. While these techniques can achieve removal efficiencies exceeding 90%, their high energy consumption, substantial equipment costs, extensive water usage, and the generation of low-value byproducts pose significant economic challenges. Furthermore, the production of large volumes of wastewater and the accumulation of substantial amounts of unrecoverable desulfurization gypsum and ash, which can cause secondary pollution, create significant environmental concerns7–10.

Therefore, the development of green and cost-effective SO2 removal methods is crucial. Catalytic SO2 removal has gained significant attention due to its lower cost, reduced environmental impact, high efficiency, and potential for byproduct recovery1,3,11,12. A particularly attractive feature is the production of valuable byproducts and the absence of waste generation. This method can employ either catalytic oxidation or catalytic reduction:

1. In catalytic oxidation, SO2 is converted to SO3 on a catalyst in the presence of O2, which can subsequently react with H2O to form H2SO4 (Eqs. (1) and (2))13–15.

While sulfuric acid is cheap and has possessing minimal added value, its transportation costs are high, and overproduction leads to market saturation. Conversely, the demand for elemental sulfur is increasing across various industries. Elemental sulfur is attractive due to its ease of storage, convenient transportation, and high monetary value, making it a desirable product from SO2 conversion. Therefore, converting SO2 to elemental sulfur presents an efficient approach for resource recovery and preventing secondary pollution1,20,21.

It is noteworthy that in industrial processes, SO2 is typically present in the tail gas alongside various other gaseous compounds. Prior to conducting catalytic SO2 removal processes, it is necessary to address the issues caused by these species. Typically, incineration units in refineries and similar industries convert different sulfur-containing compounds such as H2S and CS2 into sulfur dioxide, thereby simplifying and enhancing the efficiency of the SO2 removal process. Additionally, to improve the removal efficiency and mitigate the effects of residual interfering species, the use of supplementary methods such as filtration and advanced separation systems is recommended.

A comparison of various reducing agents reveals that methane requires a temperature approximately 200 °C higher than other reducing agents for catalytic reduction, and produces a broader range of byproducts22,23. Hydrogen (H2), despite its high yield and selectivity, is expensive and its storage and transportation are difficult and hazardous, hindering its industrial applicability20,24,25. Carbon monoxide (CO), however, offers both high yield and selectivity, similar to H2. Furthermore, CO is readily available and cost-effective as it is a byproduct in various industrial processes, including blast furnace gas, yellow phosphorus production tail gas, converters in iron and steel industries, and synthetic ammonia feedstock gas. Consequently, CO presents promising prospects for industrial applications26–29.

Various metals, including Co, Ni, Fe, Zr, Ca, Ru, Mo, Ce, and Ba, have been employed as catalysts for SO2 reduction. These metal catalysts can be used in their promoted or bimetallic forms1,16,17,20,30–33. Recently, bimetallic catalysts and their synergistic effects have attracted considerable interest due to their tunable oxidation states. One metal element can alter the catalytic properties of another through electronic and structural modifications, leading to improved water resistance, enhanced adsorption/desorption, increased redox ability, and the creation of more acidic sites, ultimately boosting catalytic activity and selectivity. Research indicates that rare earth elements are particularly active components, enhancing the performance of other metal catalysts in converting SO2 to S. This enhancement stems from their abundant of oxygen vacancies and facile oxygen mobility, facilitating the reaction. However, the efficiency of this process is highly dependent on the catalyst type and composition, the reducing agent used, operating conditions, and other factors2,16,17,27,31,32.

Supports play a crucial role in enhancing catalyst performance. A suitable support provides a high surface area, preventing the formation of crystalline aggregates or sintering of the active components, while simultaneously stabilizing the dispersion and chemical stability of the active phase. The support can also enhance mass transfer by providing space for catalytic reactions and can act as a ligand or promoter1,34,35. Since most SO2 catalytic reduction mechanisms follow Langmuir-Hinshelwood or Eley-Riedel kinetics, which require chemisorption of SO2, γ-alumina (γ-Al2O3), with its high chemical stability and abundant Lewis’s acid sites, appears to be an ideal support for adsorbing electron-rich SO236,37.

Currently, the synthesis of mesoporous γ-Al2O3 is commonly carried out using expensive aluminum precursors such as Al(NO3)3·9H2O, aluminum isopropoxide, and other similar compounds. This significantly increases the final production cost and poses limitations for the large-scale development of industrial applications38–40. In this context, the utilization of industrial wastes such as coal gangue (CG)—a byproduct of coal mining generated in enormous quantities with an annual global production of approximately one billion tons—has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable and low-cost source of aluminum. This approach not only drastically reduces the cost of raw materials and the overall price of γ-Al2O3, but also offers significant environmental benefits, as it helps to decrease the volume of waste and the associated disposal costs, thus mitigating environmental pollution. Moreover, this process enables the simultaneous production of high-purity silica as a valuable byproduct, thereby considerably increasing the overall economic value of the process. Therefore, exploiting free and sustainable aluminum resources not only represents an effective step toward reducing alumina production costs, but is also considered a promising and environmentally sustainable solution41–43.

This study presents a novel and sustainable approach to SO2 catalytic reduction by synthesizing a high-purity, mesoporous γ-Al2O3 support directly from coal gangue, an abundant industrial waste. Unlike conventional catalysts that rely on costly commercial alumina, this valorization of coal gangue not only significantly reduces material costs but also addresses environmental challenges associated with waste disposal. The mesoporous structure derived from coal gangue provides a high surface area and enhanced mass transfer, which are critical for catalytic performance. Furthermore, the catalyst system employs iron (Fe) as the active metal, promoted by lanthanum (La), a rare earth element known for its ability to create abundant oxygen vacancies and facilitate oxygen mobility. This promotion enhances SO2 adsorption and redox reactions, leading to improved catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability compared to previously reported Fe-based catalysts on commercial supports. Comprehensive characterization and systematic optimization of reaction parameters demonstrate the synergistic effect between the coal gangue-derived support and lanthanum promoter, resulting in superior CO-assisted SO2 reduction efficiency. This integrated strategy not only advances catalyst design by combining waste valorization with enhanced catalytic function but also offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly solution for industrial SO2 emission control.

In this study, high-purity, high-surface-area mesoporous γ-Al2O3 was extracted from CG and used as a catalyst support. Iron and lanthanum oxides were then dispersed onto the γ-alumina as active components using an impregnation method. These compounds were subsequently operated for the catalytic reduction of SO2 using CO as the reducing agent. The synthesized catalysts were characterized utilizing XRD, BET, XPS, SEM, H2-TPR, EDS and TEM techniques. Furthermore, to verify the optimal conditions, the effects of numerous factors such as reaction temperature, molar ratio of SO2 to CO, iron and lanthanum percentages, gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), and catalyst stability were investigated.

Catalyst preparation

Coal gangue activation process

The alumina in CG is not easily soluble in mineral acids, like HCl, due to the fact that approximately 90% of it is in a vitreous phase and therefore in equilibrium38. However, thermal activation with Na2CO3 can decompose the stable, insoluble quartz and kaolinite phases, converting them into sodium aluminate, sodium aluminosilicate, sodium silicate, and nepheline phases. For this purpose, to activate the CG, 10 g of CG and 10 g of Na2CO3 were thoroughly combined. This combination was then heated (10 °C/min) for two hours at 800 °C in a muffle furnace.

Preparation of mesoporous γ-Al2O3

Mesoporous γ-Al2O3 was prepared according to our previous research38. For the hydrochloric acid leaching experiment of CG, thermometer, a three-necked flask, magnetic stirrer, and condenser were used. The weighed CG was added to the flask. Then, 6 M HCl was added at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:15 g/mL. The combination was stirred at 90 °C and 500 rpm for 150 min. The resulting suspension was filtered, separating the leached residues from the leach liquor. To produce high-purity alumina and remove impurities such as calcium, magnesium, potassium, and titanium, 5 M NaOH was slowly inserted to the leach liquor until the pH reached approximately 12–13. With increasing pH, Al(OH)₃ changed to (Al(OH)4)−, and the hydroxide precipitates of other metals readily formed and were separated from the leach liquor by filtration. In the next step, aluminum hydroxide gel appeared upon the addition of hydrochloric acid to the residual solution from the filtration. Hot deionized water was used to wash the filtered gel multiple times, removing any remaining sodium chloride. The obtained precipitate was dried at 110 °C. Mesoporous alumina was obtained by calcining dried aluminum hydroxide at 750 °C for 6 h in an electric furnace, using a heating rate of 1 °C/min. Ultimately, CG was used to produce mesoporous alumina without the usage of either hard or soft templates.

Active components immobilization onto mesoporous γ-Al2O3

All of the catalysts used in this investigation were synthesized using the incipient-wetness impregnation approach. The water-absorbing ability of γ-Al2O3 was evaluated prior to catalyst preparation. Then, in the appropriate volume of deionized water, La(NO3)3·6H2O and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O were dissolved. Once fully dissolved, the support was supplemented with known concentrations of metal salts in deionized water. After that, the precursor was left undisturbed for two hours after impregnation. The sample was calcined in a muffle furnace at 800 °C for 4 h after being dried for 24 h at 120 °C. After that, the synthesized catalyst was presulfided for two hours in a 10% v/v H2S/H2 flow and at 420 °C.

Catalyst evaluation

Catalyst activity for SO2 reduction to S was evaluated in a fixed-bed stainless steel tube reactor. The reactor was a stainless-steel pipe with an 8 mm diameter. In the center of the fixed-bed reactor was around 1.5 mL of synthesized catalyst (30–50 mesh). Prior to heating, the reactor was purged with N2, and then a gas mixture of 1% SO2, 2% CO, and 97% N2 was fed once the target temperature was reached. To remove water and collect sulfur, two traps were installed downstream. A SCD-equipped Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph (GC) was used to examine the outlet gases, including SO2 and COS. The GC system was calibrated with certified standard gases, ensuring a detection limit down to 1 ppm. To maintain measurement accuracy and prevent detector saturation, samples were diluted tenfold via a split injection method prior to GC analysis. All data were recorded after at least 60 min of steady-state operation to guarantee data reliability. External standard calibration procedures were employed to quantify SO2 and COS concentrations. Characterization

The crystalline phases of the catalysts were identified by XRD using a Bruker D8-Advance diffractometer (Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). The measurements were conducted at room temperature (298 K) with a step size of 0.05 ° and a scanning speed of 2θ = 5 °·min⁻¹. TEM analyses of the as-prepared catalysts were performed using a JEM-1400 instrument (JEOL Ltd., Japan) to investigate their fine structural features. SEM-EDS analysis, and elemental mapping were conducted on the as-prepared catalysts using a Philips XL30 FEG SEM (Philips/FEI, Netherlands) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Prior to SEM imaging, the powder samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance surface conductivity. The chemical composition of the samples was determined by XRF analysis using a PHILIPS PW1480 analyzer (Netherlands). Surface area, pore size distribution, and pore volume measurements were conducted through nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer (United States). Before each measurement, samples were outgassed under vacuum at 300 °C. The specific surface areas were calculated by the BET method. The reducibility of the samples was evaluated by H2-TPR using a NanoSORD Iran apparatus equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). For each measurement, 100 mg of calcined sample was initially outgassed at 250 °C for 1 h, then reduced with 5% H2/Ar gas mixture while heating from 100 °C to 900 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min⁻¹. XPS was employed to investigate the elemental composition and surface chemical states of the synthesized catalysts using a BesTek (Germany) instrument with an Al Kα radiation source (hν = 1486.6 eV).

Result and discussion

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

Variations in the phases and composition of CG can significantly influence the dissolution of Al2O3. Figure 1 illustrates the phase changes in coal gangue after calcination with the addition of Na2CO3. As observed in the XRD pattern, after alkaline fusion, quartz diffraction almost completely disappears, while diffractions corresponding to sodium aluminate, sodium aluminum silicate, sodium silicate, and nepheline emerge. These results indicate that the addition of Na2CO3 to coal gangue facilitates the decomposition of quartz through reaction with Na2CO3, thereby promoting the conversion of aluminosilicates into more reactive phases38.

Digital images and XRD patterns of CG and calcined CG.

Also, the XRD of γ-Al2O3, Fe/γ-Al2O3, and La-Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalysts are exhibited in Fig. 2. The XRD pattern of the γ-Al2O3 shows strong peaks at 2θ = 37.2°, 39.6°, 45.8°, and 66.9°, corresponding to the (311), (222), (400), and (440) planes (JCPDS Card 29–0063), demonstrating well-crystallized γ-Al2O3 particles with a face-centered cubic structure. These peaks are present in all catalyst samples. Upon loading varying percentages of Fe onto the surface of γ-Al2O3, additional peaks appeared at 2θ = 24.2°, 33.1°, 35.7°, 41.0°, 49.6°, 54.2°, 57.5°, 62.5°, and 64.1°. These peaks are assigned to the diffraction of the (012), (104), (110), (113), (024), (116), (018), (214), and (300) planes of Fe2O3 (JCPDS card 79–0007), respectively. As observed, increasing the Fe percentage increases the intensity of the Fe2O3 diffraction peaks in the XRD patterns, indicating the presence of larger Fe2O3 particles. With the addition of La, given its low loading percentage, only very weak peaks appear at 2θ = 31.5° and 40.6°, matching to the (400) and (431) planes of La (JCPDS card 22–0369), respectively. However, it is clearly evident that adding La to the Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst significantly reduces the intensity of the diffraction peaks associated with Fe2O3 crystals. Furthermore, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) values for the strongest peak of the 15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 and 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 samples were measured as 0.157 and 0.314, respectively. The Scherrer equation states that the FWHM and average grain size are inversely related44. Therefore, the XRD results indicate that La loading inhibits catalyst particle growth and material crystallization, potentially leading to improved surface dispersion of the active components on γ-Al2O3.

Nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis

Figure 3 illustrates the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of γ-Al2O3 and γ-Al2O3 doped with varying percentages of active components, while Fig. 4 presents the corresponding BJH pore size distribution curves. The isotherms of all synthesized catalysts, according to the IUPAC classification, belong to type IV and all exhibit H3 hysteresis loops, confirming their mesoporous nature38. This mesoporous structure can enhance mass transfer in catalytic reactions and facilitate the reaction. Table 2 exhibits the total pore volume, BET surface area, and average pore size of the catalysts. The surface area of γ-Al2O3 alone is 288.55 m²/g. With the loading of Fe onto the support, the surface area of the catalyst decreased, and this decline was more pronounced with rising Fe weight%. This is attributed to Fe crystals occupying and blocking some of the γ-alumina channels, which also resulted in a decrease in the total pore volume and average pore size of the catalysts. However, it is noteworthy that the addition of La as a second component increased both the total pore volume and average pore size of the 15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 sample. This is consistent with the XRD analysis results, which presented that the addition of La can reduces Fe particle size and improve dispersion, thus leading to less pore blockage.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

Figure 6 displays the XPS spectra of the 3%La15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst. The primary components of the catalyst were Al, O, Fe, La, and C, as observed in Fig. 6a. The external contamination was the source of the C element. Figure 6b, depicting the O 1s region, reveals three distinct peaks, providing insights into the oxygen’s chemical environment within the catalyst. Peaks at 529 eV and 530 eV correspond to oxygen in metal oxides, primarily associated with the Fe2O3 and La2O3 promoters, potentially reflecting subtle differences in bonding configurations or the existence of additional metal oxide species46. The peak at 531 eV is assigned to lattice oxygen in the Al2O3 support material, confirming its presence and interaction with the promoters46. Figure 6c illustrates the binding energies of the Fe 2p orbitals. The Fe 2p3/2 peak was found at 711.62 eV, while the Fe 2p1/2 peak was located at 725.53 eV. These values are in agreement with the prior published values of 710.8 and 724.8 eV for Fe2O347. Figure 6d displays the La 3d high-resolution spectrum, revealing characteristic peaks at 835.52 eV and 852.18 eV, corresponding to La 3d5/2 and La 3d3/2, respectively. Accompanying shake-up satellite peaks are observed at 838.60 eV and 855.23 eV, further confirming the presence of lanthanum in the sample48. Additionally, the La 3d3/2 and 3d5/2 states have an energy difference of around 17 eV, which is in good agreement with the La2O3 values for characters47.

Scanning electron microscope images (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray (EDS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

EDS, TEM, and SEM were employed to examine the distribution of the active components on the γ-Al2O3 support. As depicted in the EDS spectrum (Fig. 7), the main elements identified are oxygen (O), aluminum (Al), iron (Fe), and lanthanum (La), with weight percentages of 50.82%, 31.12%, 14.89%, and 3.17%, respectively. These values closely correspond to the nominal loadings of Fe (15%) and La (3%), confirming the successful incorporation of the target elements into the catalyst structure. Furthermore, EDS spectral imaging and elemental mapping analysis (Fig. 8a) confirm the presence of Fe and La as the active components, demonstrating their successful and uniform distribution across the support surface. Overall, the EDS results verify that the synthesized catalyst possesses the intended chemical composition and a homogeneous dispersion of the active phases. TEM analysis (Fig. 8b) corroborates these findings, revealing the presence of active component nanoparticles (identified as small, dark particles) deposited on the γ-alumina crystals, with particle sizes ranging from 13.3 to 17.1 nm. Effect of Fe loading

To examine the influence of the active component on catalyst performance, different percentages of Fe (0 to 20 wt%) were loaded onto a γ-Al2O3 support. The results concerning the impact of Fe loading on catalytic performance are shown in Fig. 9. The reactions were carried out at 380 °C, a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 6000 h−1, and a molar ratio of CO/SO2 = 2. Initially, with increasing Fe weight%, the catalyst activity improved due to an increase in active sites. Upon reaching 15% Fe loading, a rapid increase in both selectivity and conversion was observed, correlating with a rise in the number of active phases. Specifically, conversion increased from 29.72 ± 1.8% to 96.05 ± 1.3%, while selectivity rose from 41.27 ± 2.2% to 96.87 ± 1.6%. However, with further increase in the active component, both selectivity and SO2 conversion showed a downward trend. This could be because an excessive amount of the active component may lead to the formation of larger particles, blocking the catalyst pores, and overlapping active sites. Therefore, the 15 wt% Fe/γ-Al2O3 demonstrated the best catalytic performance and was chosen as the identified catalyst for the loading of the second active component.

Effect of La loading

The effect of La as a second active component, at loadings of 1, 2, 3, and 4 wt%, was investigated at 380 °C, a GHSV of 6000 h⁻¹, and a molar ratio of CO/SO2 = 2 (Fig. 10). Results indicated a significant enhancement in catalytic activity with the addition of a specific amount of La. This enhancement was beneficial up to a 3 wt% La loading, increasing conversion and selectivity to 98.92 ± 1.3% and 99.43 ± 0.7%, respectively. However, further increases in the second active component loading resulted in a decrease in catalytic activity of approximately 2%. While the addition of La enhances SO2 adsorption on the catalyst surface and improves catalytic activity, excessive La loading can lead to the formation of large La2O3 crystalline phases, accumulating around the active component crystallites. This effectively covers the active catalyst surface area, resulting in a reduction in catalytic activity. Consequently, the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst was selected as the optimal catalyst.

Effect of temperature

To investigate the effect of reaction temperature, the catalytic behavior of the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst was examined under reaction conditions of GHSV = 6000 h⁻¹ and a molar ratio of CO/SO2 = 2. The changes in conversion and selectivity of the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst with respect to temperature changes are presented in Fig. 11.

At a reaction temperature of 320 °C, the catalyst exhibited very low catalytic activity, showing that the catalyst activation energy had not been reached. However, with increasing temperature, the catalyst began to show catalytic activity, and both conversion and selectivity raised. Upon reaching 380 °C, the reaction saw a significant rise in SO2 conversion and selectivity Further increases in temperature had a negligible effect on both conversion and selectivity. This suggests that increasing the temperature to a particular point provides the necessary activation energy for the target reaction, enabling it to proceed rapidly and efficiently. Therefore, 380 °C was determined as the optimal reaction temperature.

Effect of GHSV

Figure 12 shows the effect of GHSV on the catalytic performance of 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3. For this purpose, the molar ratio of CO/SO2 and the reaction temperature were kept constant at 2 and 380 °C, respectively, while the GHSV was changed in the range of 2000 to 10,000 h−1. When the GHSV raised from 2000 to 6000 h−1, the catalyst selectivity showed a slight increase. This could be attributed to a lower GHSV increasing the gas residence time in the reactor, potentially leading to reverse reactions and the conversion of the produced sulfur to COS. However, a further rise in GHSV from 6000 to 10,000 h⁻¹ resulted in a decline in both SO2 conversion and catalyst selectivity. This is likely due to the reduced contact duration between the reactants and the catalyst surface.

Reaction mechanism

Figure 14 shows the XRD patterns of the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst before and after reaction. As clearly shown in the image, the XRD analysis of the fresh catalyst revealed Fe2O3 peaks, indicating the oxidized state of the active components supported on γ- Al2O3. However, in the XRD pattern of the used catalyst, peaks of FeS2 appeared, showing that the active component has transformed after use49. No diffraction peaks for the lanthanum component are observed, which is attributed to the low amount of lanthanum in the catalyst structure.

Also, the proposed catalytic reaction mechanism is shown in Eqs. (6–8) and Fig. 15. This mechanism includes surface reactions among SO2 and COS on Lewis and Brønsted acid sites. COS can be formed either through a gas-phase reaction between CO and elemental sulfur or via a gas-solid reaction among CO and a metal sulfide. Subsequently, a sulfur vacancy forms on the surface of the metal sulfide, which is then filled by the produced S. It has been determined that COS is a crucial intermediate in this mechanism, playing a significant role and acting as a more efficient reducing agent than CO for the reduction of sulfur dioxide to S.

Comparison with other studies

Compared to other catalysts evaluated for SO2 reduction (Table 4), the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst demonstrates a compelling combination of performance metrics and operational efficiency. Most notably, it achieves a superior sulfur selectivity of 99.43 ± 0.7% alongside a high SO2 conversion of 98.92 ± 1.3%, surpassing several catalysts like 14wt%Fe–2wt%Co/γ-Al2O3 and 2La8Ni/γ-Al2O3 in selectivity while maintaining comparable conversion rates. While some catalysts achieve slightly higher conversions (e.g., Fe-Mn(Al) and 15%La-Ce-Ox@ZrO2 at 100%), the 3%La15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst presents a more favorable balance, particularly considering its operation at a moderate temperature of 380 °C and a GHSV of 6000 h−1, offering a good balance between activity and throughput. This is advantageous compared to catalysts requiring higher temperatures, such as Fe-Mn(Al), which operates at 700°C. Furthermore, the high surface area of 235.96 m2/g is attributed to the use of gamma-alumina derived from coal gangue as the catalyst support, facilitating abundant active sites for the reaction and distinguishing it from catalysts with lower surface areas. This surface area is considerably higher than those of catalysts such as SnO2-ZrO2 and 15%La-Ce-Ox@ZrO2, suggesting that the catalyst’s structure is beneficial for the reaction. The enhanced selectivity and balanced operating conditions of the 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3 catalyst make it a promising candidate for practical applications in SO2 reduction.

Conclusion

This study successfully synthesized a highly efficient and stable SO₂ reduction catalyst, 3%La-15%Fe/γ-Al2O3, utilizing γ-Al2O3 derived from coal gangue. The optimized catalyst, operating at 380 °C, a GHSV of 6000 h−1, and a CO/SO2 = 2, exhibited exceptional activity (98.92 ± 1.3%) and selectivity (99.43 ± 0.7%) for the conversion of SO2 to S. Characterization revealed that the addition of lanthanum effectively inhibited the growth of iron crystallites, promoting their dispersion on the support and to the enhanced catalytic performance. This observation underscores the critical role of lanthanum as a promoter in optimizing the catalyst’s active sites. The catalyst’s robust performance, maintained over a 50-hour stability test, further strengthens its potential for long-term industrial application in SO2 abatement. This sustained activity suggests resistance to common deactivation mechanisms, such as sintering or poisoning, which are crucial factors for practical implementation. Moreover, the utilization of coal gangue-derived γ-Al2O3 not only provides a sustainable and economical alternative to commercially available supports but also addresses the environmental challenge of coal gangue disposal. This dual benefit positions the developed catalyst as a promising solution for both SO2 emission control and resource valorization, contributing to a more circular and sustainable chemical industry.
