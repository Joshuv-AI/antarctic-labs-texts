Inflammatory Markers Associated With Radiation-Induced Lung Injury, Metastasis and Mortality After Breast Cancer Treatment
Authors: Jarle Karlsen, Guro Giskeødegård, Torgrim Tandstad, Øyvind Salvesen, Torgeir Wethal, Randi Johansen Reidunsdatter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeBreast cancer (BC) radiation therapy (RT) prevents local recurrence and improves survival. However, it can induce acute and late effects on organs at risk, such as the lung. In some patients, radiation activates inflammatory markers, which promote inflammation in the lung leading to radiation pneumonitis and fibrosis (RF). Moreover, inflammatory markers may be associated with metastatic disease and survival. This study examines the relationships between inflammatory markers and clinical radiation pneumonitis and RF and explores their potential to predict BC metastasis or overall survival.Methods and MaterialsPatients prescribed postoperative BC RT were included in this prospective longitudinal cohort study with assessments before RT (baseline) and after 3, 6, and 12 months and at a long-term follow-up 7 to 12 years after RT. Inflammatory markers from each assessment were analyzed. BC metastasis and overall survival were registered up to 16 years after RT.ResultsMonocyte chemoattractant protein 1 at 3 months emerged as a predictor for clinical RF at 12 months. Tumor necrosis factor alpha at baseline and the change in fibroblast growth factor basic, neutrophil lymphocyte ratio, C-reactive protein, and interleukin (IL) 1b (IL-1b) from baseline to 3 months predicted BC metastasis. IL-6 at 3 and 12 months, IL-8 at 12 months, and an increase in fibroblast growth factor basic and IL-6 from baseline to 3 months were associated with all-cause mortality.ConclusionsWe found monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 linked to radiation-induced lung injury. Inflammatory markers before and after RT were associated with BC metastasis and all-cause mortality. Our findings indicate that prolonged inflammation following RT may be linked with BC metastasis and overall survival. However, further research is needed to understand their signaling pathways and their role in predicting BC outcomes.

Introduction

Radiation therapy (RT) for breast cancer (BC) is effective in reducing local recurrence and enhancing survival rates.1 However, RT can also induce cardiopulmonary side effects, particularly affecting the lungs and heart.2 The most prevalent radiation-induced lung injuries are radiation pneumonitis (RP) and radiation fibrosis (RF). RP typically manifests within 2 to 3 months post-RT, whereas RF has a delayed onset, peaking approximately 12 months post-RT and potentially persisting for several years.3 The risk of lung toxicity mainly depends on radiation dose and volume to the ipsilateral lung. High-dose-volume parameters, such as V20 (the percent of lung tissue receiving 20 Gy or more), and mean lung dose are the main predisposing factors, but also low-dose-volume parameters have been related to lung injury.4 Patients with BC treated with anthracyclines or taxanes in addition to RT have an increased risk of RP, whereas endocrine treatment with tamoxifen is associated with RF.5,6 Personally related factors associated with radiation-induced lung injury are older age, body mass index, smoking, and lung comorbidity such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and interstitial lung disease.7,8

Both chemotherapy and RT induce systemic inflammation that may persist for months after the end of therapy.9 Radiation-induced cell damage triggers the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including fibroblast growth factor (FGF) basic, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-1ra, IL-6, IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α). These cytokines recruit monocytes, macrophages, and fibroblasts, potentially leading to RP and RF.10, 11, 12 FGF basic, a signal protein produced by macrophages, has been associated with radiation-induced toxicity in vitro.12 However, there is a scarcity of studies examining the role of basic FGF in radiation-induced toxicity in humans.

Chemokines, such as IL-8 and monocyte chemotactic protein (MPC)-1, are small proteins involved in leucocyte migration and homeostasis during inflammation. Although pretreatment values of these chemokines have been linked to radiation-induced lung injury in patients with lung cancer,13 their association with lung injury in patients with BC remains unclear. C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute-phase inflammatory protein and is elevated after injury, infection, and inflammation. Elevated CRP is a potential predictor for RP in patients with lung cancer.14 A slight increase in CRP level at 12 and 25 weeks after RT was observed in patients with BC with RP, but there is a lack of studies reporting CRP as a predictor for lung injury in patients with BC.15 Identifying patients at elevated risk of lung toxicity is important when planning RT. Optimizing dose to target patients with increased risk of BC recurrence must be balanced against acceptable doses to organs at risk, such as lungs and heart. Currently, there are no reliable predictors for radiation-induced lung injury following BC treatment in clinical practice, which highlights the need for applicable biomarkers.

Inflammatory markers may not only mediate short-term tissue toxicity but also serve as predictors for cancer recurrence and overall survival. For instance, these markers have been linked to reduced chemotherapy response and poorer cancer-specific survival in colorectal and patients with BC.16,17 In patients with BC, elevated CRP levels measured before treatment have been reported as a predictor of reduced disease-free and overall survival.18 Additionally, increased CRP levels measured 2.5 years after BC diagnosis have been associated with reduced overall survival, indicating a persistent inflammatory state that impacts survival. However, the findings have not yet been implemented in clinical practice, for example, identifying patients at high risk of recurrence. Neutrophil lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is an important biomarker of inflammatory status and disease exacerbation. A high NLR ratio (>3) has been associated with BC recurrence and may therefore serve as a predictor of worse prognosis.19 Nevertheless, the clinical meaningful threshold may vary among different cancer patient subgroups, and the use of NLR as a prognostic biomarker requires further evaluation.20

The primary aim of this study was to demonstrate the associations between various inflammatory markers measured during the first year after RT and radiation-induced lung injuries, specifically clinical RP and clinical RF. The secondary aim was to explore whether these proinflammatory markers could predict BC metastasis or mortality.

The following hypotheses were stated to target our aims: (1) inflammatory markers before RT predicts clinical RP at 3 months and clinical RF at 12 months after RT, (2) inflammatory markers at 3 months predict clinical RF at 12 months and at long-term follow-up, (3) inflammatory markers before RT, at 3 months or at 12 months predict BC metastasis or all-cause death, and (4) a change in inflammatory markers from before RT to 3 months predicts BC metastasis or all-cause death.

Methods and Materials

Patients with BC scheduled for postoperative RT in the period February 2007 to October 2008 at 1 single institution were consecutively invited to participate in this prospective longitudinal cohort study. Exclusion criteria were metastatic disease or inability to understand the Norwegian language.

Treatment

Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 103 patients and ended 3 to 4 weeks before RT except for 16 patients with locally advanced BC who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Patients received computed tomography (CT) dose-planned RT with 2 6-MV photon tangential fields, supplemented by low-weighted field segments. A total dose of 50 Gy in 2 Gy/fraction, 5 fractions per week was administered to the breast after breast-conserving surgery or to the thoracic wall after mastectomy. Patients with node-positive disease received 46 Gy to the axilla and the fossa supraclavicular region. In accordance with national guidelines (nbcg.no), none of the patients received RT to the internal mammary nodes. Hormone therapy was administered to 137 patients. Among the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positive patients eligible for chemotherapy (n = 34), trastuzumab was given concomitant with docetaxel and continued for 1 year. The time-treatment schedule is illustrated in Figure E1.

Assessments

The patients took part in an extended follow-up program with assessments before RT (baseline), after 3, 6, and 12 months, and a long-term follow-up taking place 7 to 12 years after RT; see flowchart, Figure 1. At each assessment, patients underwent CT examinations of the lungs, blood sample collections, and clinical controls with symptoms registrations. RP and RF were documented according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 3.0. Blood samples—storage and analyses

Serum CRP, lymphocytes, and neutrophiles were analyzed at the Department of Medical Biochemistry. Blood samples for measuring inflammatory markers were centrifuged, and serum was extracted and immediately frozen at –80°C before further analysis. The levels of the inflammatory markers IL-1b, IL-1ra, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, FGF basic, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), and TNF-α at each timepoint were measured using the array reader Bio-Plex 200 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc, Norway). Samples with centrifugated serum were mixed with washed antibody-linked magnetic beads coded for each inflammatory marker and thereafter incubated with biotinylated detector antibodies. Streptavidin phycoerythrin, a conjugate that detects antibodies with a high affinity for biotin, was added, and unbound streptavidin was washed away before the analysis.

The Bio-Plex 200 reader has a 532-nm Nd-Yag laser system used to excite streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. When the suspended beads pass rapidly, the laser excites the internal dye in the bead, identifying the specific analyte (the specific inflammatory marker). A second laser of 635 nm quantifies the analyte based on fluorescent reporter signals. Using Bio-Plex Manager Software data analysis for different assays was performed.

Cytokine values were adjusted for plate variations based on quality control samples run on each plate. Adjustment results in equal mean values of the quality control samples as described by Browne et al.21 Values below detection limits were imputed using an expectation-maximization algorithm from the zCompositions package in R.22 Batch adjustments and imputations were done in R version 4.3.

IL-10 was excluded from further analysis because 56% of the values were below the lower detection limit.

Statistical analysis

Median values with an interquartile range of inflammatory markers at different time points were calculated. The inflammatory markers were log-transformed to obtain normal distribution before further analysis.

To explore variations in inflammatory markers over time between patients with and without clinical RP at 3 months and clinical RF at 12 months, we used a linear mixed model. Linear mixed model was performed with inflammatory markers as response variables, time, clinical group, and the interaction time*clinical group as fixed effects, and patient ID as random effect. Clinical group was defined as patients with and without clinical RP at 3 months, and with and without clinical RF at 12 months.

The analyses of the association between inflammatory markers and clinical RP and RF were guided by directed acyclic graphs in Figure E2. The association between baseline inflammatory markers and clinical RP at 3 months was analyzed by multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, smoking (yes/no), comorbidity (yes/no), and chemotherapy (yes/no). Similarly, age, comorbidity, body mass index, and endocrine treatment (yes/no) were used for the analysis of inflammatory markers and clinical RF at 12 months. Multivariable logistic regression was also applied to test the association between inflammatory markers at 3 months and clinical RF at 12 months or at long-term follow-up, adjusted for age, comorbidity, hormone therapy (yes/no), and V20.

Cox survival analysis was performed to explore if inflammatory markers at baseline, 3 months, 12 months, or changes in markers from baseline to 3 months, predicted BC metastasis or overall death. The survival analyses were adjusted for age, comorbidity, chemotherapy, V20, hormone therapy, and tumor stage according to directed acyclic graphs in Figure E3. Survival status and the cause of death were obtained from the electronic patient journal. BC metastasis was verified from the histopathological report. The time at risk for BC metastasis was defined from the start date of RT to the first date of metastasis, death, or April 30, 2024, encompassing a median follow-up duration of 16 years. Time at risk for all-cause death was defined from the date of start of RT to the date of death or April 30, 2024. In the survival analysis, no patients were lost to follow-up, but survival data were censored for 2 emigrants.

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 28 and R version 3.6.3.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics, and the patients gave their informed consent before the start of RT.

Patient attendance was consistently high across all assessment points, as shown in Figure 1. Notably, 172 patients met at the long-term follow-up of 7 to 12 years after RT, of which 141 patients provided blood samples. Missing blood samples were primarily because of patients forgetting to have their blood samples taken or physicians neglecting to order the blood tests.

RP and fibrosis during follow-up

Among the 231 patients who underwent CT examinations, 179 (77%) patients developed RP 3 months following RT, whereas 131 (57%) had RP grade 1 and 48 (21%) grade 2. After 12 months, 200 (90%) had developed fibrosis in the ipsilateral lung, and 38 (17%) had clinical RF. At long-term follow-up, 144 (91%) demonstrated RF, and 34 (22%) had clinical RF (Table E1).

BC recurrence, metastasis, and all-cause mortality

After a median of 16 years of follow-up, 47 (19%) patients had BC recurrence, of which 13 had local recurrence only and 34 had metastatic disease. Thirteen patients received a diagnosis of new cancer, of whom 8 had lung cancer. Discussion

Biomarkers may identify patients at higher risk of side effects or recurrence, which is important in tailoring BC treatment. When planning BC RT, reducing the clinical target volume may be necessary for patients at elevated risk of lung injury. However, this must be balanced with ensuring adequate dosage to the breast or regional lymph nodes. This study aimed to investigate the relationships between inflammatory markers and radiation-induced lung injury by analyzing 9 markers at 5 different time points. Additionally, we examined the associations between inflammatory markers, or changes in these markers before and after RT and BC metastasis or all-cause mortality.

Inflammatory markers and clinical RP and RF

Our study suggests that MCP-1 could contribute to the development of radiation-induced lung fibrosis. MCP-1 is a chemokine with a vital role in the inflammatory process, regulating the migration of monocytes and macrophages and enhancing the expression of inflammatory factors.23 MCP is activated minutes to hours after RT, and its concentration may remain high for months. In a study with irradiated mice, pulmonary fibrosis observed 26 weeks after RT was linked to elevated chemokine levels, including MCP-1, suggesting that RT induces persistent inflammation.24 However, the mechanism for radiation-induced injury in humans is not well documented. In a study involving 24 patients who underwent intrathoracic radiation, IL-1 alpha and IL-6, but not FGF basic and MCP-1, were associated with RP.25 This study included patients with lung cancer and thymoma, but none with BC. Similar to our findings, MCP-1 levels declined from the start of RT up to 8 weeks afterward. However, we observed that MCP-1 levels peaked 12 months post-RT, coinciding with the highest incidence of clinical RF. This suggests that MCP-1 activation by radiation could be a predictor of RF.

No other inflammatory markers were associated with clinical RP or RF. However, the extent of RP at 3 months and RF at 12 months was modest, with these conditions affecting only a limited portion of the ipsilateral lung. These conditions were diagnosed as National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade 1 or 2, indicating only modest inflammation in the lung tissue. Additionally, other inflammatory markers not included in our analysis may significantly contribute to the development of radiation-induced lung injury. For instance, IL-4, IL-13, and TNF-β are central to macrophage activation and cytokine signaling in the inflammatory response following RT.4 Moreover, our study is an explorative study, assuming the inflammatory markers measured at different timepoints are confounded by personal and treatment-related confounders. In a previous study, we identified personal and treatment-related factors as age, V20, V30, and tamoxifen use predicting RP and RF.26 A multivariable risk model that integrates treatment, personal, and biological variables could more accurately predict lung toxicity risk.

Inflammatory markers and BC metastasis

Increasing levels of inflammation markers have been linked to the progression of BC.27 High pretreatment levels of TNF-α were predictive of a negative response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in a study with locally advanced patients with BC.28 Our findings support this, demonstrating that baseline TNF-α levels are associated with later recurrence and metastatic disease. The reason why pretreatment values of TNF-α in our study are linked to metastatic disease remains unclear. However, TNF-α is a major mediator of inflammation and can induce both cancer cell death and promote tumor growth.29 Elevated inflammation levels may be because of previous surgery or chemotherapy. Moreover, the high TNF-α levels may represent micrometastasis. Additionally, high TNF-α levels could be because of lifestyle factors such as obesity and smoking, increasing the risk of BC recurrence.30,31

We found that an increase in NLR from baseline to 3 months after RT was associated with BC metastasis. High NLR has been associated with poor cancer outcome and reported as a prognostic factor in BC survival in several studies.19 Furthermore, in a longitudinal prospective study with 1519 neoadjuvant-treated patients with BC, NLR > 2 was associated with adverse overall survival in triple-negative BC, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 positive and in patients with residual disease after neoadjuvant treatment, representing patients with BC with higher risk of recurrence.32 In contrast to our study, 90% of the patients had locally advanced BC, and blood samples were measured only before treatment. This could explain the difference in our results, where we found associations with BC metastasis but not with overall survival.

FGF basic is a regulator for angiogenesis and in patients with BC increasing levels have been linked with relapse and progressive disease.33 Unexpectedly, the increase in basic FGF from baseline to 3 months after RT in our study was inversely associated with BC metastasis. Basic FGF may rise under systemic treatment, but the impact of RT on FGF basic levels in humans is less documented.33 The observed elevation of basic FGF from baseline might be a response to RT, with immune cells increasing FGF production to protect lung tissue.34 Additionally, low levels of FGF basic have been reported as a predictor for BC recurrence.35 However, the FGF signaling pathway is complex and therapeutic approach to target FGF receptor in BC is challenging partly because of the heterogeneity of the tumor.36

Contrary to our expectations, a modest decrease in IL-1b and CRP from baseline to 3 months after RT was associated with BC metastasis. Although IL-1b induces IL-6 production, the reason why a decrease in both cytokines is associated with BC metastasis remains unclear. However, the levels of IL-1 and CRP at 3 months may still indicate an elevated state, either habitually, because of persistent inflammation or represent micrometastases. Moreover, IL-1b has both negative and positive effects on tumor expression and cancer treatment. Serum levels of IL-1b have been linked to BC progression, and the expression of IL-1b in primary tumors may predict bone metastasis.36 On the other hand, IL-1b can inhibit primary tumor growth in BC and consequently, the IL-1b receptor has emerged as a key target in BC clinical trials.37 Increased CRP has been associated with poor survival in patients with metastatic BC, but studies reporting CRP as a prognostic biomarker in early BC are sparse.38

However, the role of TNF-α, NLR, CRP and IL-1b in BC diagnostics and treatment remains unclear, necessitating further research to explore their potential as biomarkers for metastatic disease.

Inflammatory markers and survival

In our study, we found an association between IL-6 levels at 3 months and IL-6 and IL-8 at 12 months after RT and reduced overall survival. IL-6 measured before treatment has been associated with all-cause mortality in patients with BC.37 Even though the level of IL-6 was highest at baseline, perhaps because of recent chemotherapy and surgery, we found no significant association between baseline IL-6 levels and survival. However, a continuous low-grade inflammation after RT may impact all-cause survival. IL-6 is secreted from various cell types involved in inflammation and tumor cells regulating tumor growth and differentiation.39 By modulating the IL-6 pathway, tumors may develop resistance to drug treatments40 as shown in patients with ovarian cancer where increased levels of IL-6 were associated with resistance to apoptosis induced by paclitaxel.41 Moreover, IL-6 may both promote and reduce production of anti-inflammatory cytokines and is affected by physiological factors such as diet, exercise, stress, and estrogens.40

IL-8 measured 12 months after RT was associated with all-cause death. However, we found no associations between IL-8 and BC metastasis. IL-8 may be induced by proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1b, and by environmental changes such as hypoxia.42 IL-8 has proangiogenic properties, which contribute to tumor progression and metastasis, and has been associated with poor prognosis in BC possibly mediated by estradiol.43

Inflammatory markers, metastasis, and survival

Our findings suggest that RT induces inflammation, which subsequently impacts BC metastasis and overall survival. Cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α play crucial roles in this process. These cytokines are not only associated with inflammation but also with BC metastasis and overall survival.39,43,44 Elevated levels of these cytokines in our study may represent micrometastasis. However, understanding the relationship between RT-induced inflammation and these cytokines can help in developing more effective treatment strategies. The exact mechanism by which inflammation after BC treatment impact metastasis and mortality is still not fully understood. RT is highly effective in preventing local recurrence and reducing BC deaths. However, its impact on mortality from non-BC causes is less well documented.1 Nonetheless, genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors may contribute to this outcome.45 Weight gain and continuing smoking after diagnosis may increase the risk of BC recurrence.44 Obesity is associated with a low-grade chronic state of inflammation, where adipose tissue produces TNF-α and IL-6 where both cytokines contribute to insulin resistance. Moreover, TNF-α has been linked to colon, skin, and hepatocellular cancer.30 and elevated TNF-α levels may increase the risk of coronary heart disease.46 which, alongside cancer, was a leading cause of death in our study (Fig. 3).

The inflammatory markers we identified could be instrumental in predicting the risk of recurrence. This insight can guide personalized treatment plans and potentially enhance survival rates. Targeting receptors for inflammatory markers such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, or inhibiting their signaling pathways, might represent a promising strategy in BC therapy.39,43,45 However, given the number of markers and timepoints analyzed, the possibility of false-positive associations cannot be excluded.

Long-term side effects

BC RT can increase the risk of heart disease and lung cancer.2 In our study, we identified 9 (4%) patients with lung cancer, of whom 8 received diagnosis after BC radiation. The estimated risk of lung cancer for a 50-year-old smoker 10 years or more after BC radiation was in a meta-analysis estimated to 4% for smokers and 0.3% for nonsmokers, and the risk of lung cancer mortality for the smokers before the age of 80 years was 14%.2 These estimates are in accordance with our observations where 6 (13% of the smokers at baseline) died of lung cancer with a median age of 57 years. Unfortunately, recommending smoking cessation before RT was not mandatory at the time of inclusion in this study. In our material, 6 (2.4%) patients had died of cardiovascular disease, which included coronary ischemic heart disease, heart failure, and stroke after 16 years of follow-up. Cardiac disease is the leading cause of noncancer mortality after BC RT. Estimated absolute risk for death because of coronary heart disease after BC RT has been reported to 1% for smokers and 0.3% for nonsmokers but this study included retrospective data with new and older RT techniques and a mean dose 4.4 Gy for whole heart.2 However, the dose to the heart and lungs remains a concern because of potential late effects. Modern radiation techniques, such as volume-modulated arc therapy, deep inspiration breath-holding, and proton therapy, may help reduce the dose to these organs.47, 48, 49

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of our study is its prospective longitudinal design, combined with an extensive follow-up program involving a relatively large cohort of patients who maintained high compliance over a period of up to 12 years. The comprehensive data collection included biological data, imaging diagnostics, and physician-reported outcomes. The study was initially designed to capture late effects following RT; therefore, our baseline assessments were conducted before starting RT. Inflammatory markers measured prior to surgery and chemotherapy would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the inflammation processes and their associations with treatment-related late effects. In our survival analysis, the presence of deaths of 15 patients from unknown causes may have introduced uncertainty and affected the accuracy of the results. Additionally, the absence of an external, age-matched validation cohort limits the generalizability of our findings. Although such validation would have strengthened the robustness of our conclusions, identifying a comparable population with similar treatment protocols and patient characteristics proved challenging. Furthermore, the analysis involved multiple inflammatory markers assessed at several timepoints, which increases the risk of false-positive findings because of multiple hypothesis testing. Although we did not apply formal corrections such as Bonferroni adjustments, we aimed to mitigate this risk by using multivariable models and focusing on consistent patterns across markers and timepoints. However, we acknowledge that the absence of correction for multiple comparisons is a limitation and that our findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger cohorts and external validation are needed to confirm these associations.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the inflammatory marker MCP-1 may serve as a predictor of radiation-induced lung injury, whereas TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 are linked to BC metastasis and survival. Changes in inflammatory marker levels from before to 3 months after RT were associated with metastatic disease and overall mortality. However, the underlying mechanisms of this influence remain unclear. Further research is necessary to evaluate the clinical implications of these findings and to investigate the role of inflammatory markers in predicting BC recurrence and mortality.
