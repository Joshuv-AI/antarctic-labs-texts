Comparison of effect of massage or chicory bath on bilirubin levels in term newborns undergoing phototherapy: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Malihe Nourollahpour Shiadeh, Hajar Pasha, Ommolbanin Zare, Alireza Yahyaei Shahandashti, Fatemeh Haghshenas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal hyperbilirubinemia is a frequent metabolic complication in newborns. The available treatment methods may bring about adverse consequences; therefore, finding new modalities to decrease the duration of treatment is a major concern.The aim of this study was to compare the effect of massage or chicory bath on bilirubin levels in term newborns undergoing phototherapy.MethodsThis randomized clinical trial study was conducted on 90 newborns with hyperbilirubinemia at Imam Ali Hospital of Amol on October 26, 2024 to February 6, 2025. Two intervention groups received chicory bath or massage as an adjuvant intervention in addition to standard phototherapy treatment, and the control group only received phototherapy. A custom-designed data collection form was used in this study. All newborns who met the inclusion criteria were selected and informed consent was obtained from their parents. Then, using a computerized random number table, the newborns were randomly assigned to three groups. Sampling continued until the intended number was reached. Descriptive and analytical statistical tests including analysis of variance, chi-square, Tukey's post hoc test, and repeated measures ANOVA were used to analyze the data using SPSS software (version 26). A significance level of 0.05 was considered acceptable.ResultsThere was no significant difference between the three groups in terms of neonatal age, gender, birth weight, gestational age, and type of delivery. There was no significant difference between the three groups regarding average bilirubin levels on the second, third, and fourth days of hospitalization. The change in bilirubin levels over time was significant regardless of the group (P=0.000), and the trend of bilirubin change over time was not significant among the three groups. There was no significant difference in the rate of complications including skin rashes, drug sensitivity, and phototherapy sensitivity among the three groups. There was a significant difference between the three groups in terms of the frequency of defecation on the third and fourth days (P=0.02), which was higher in the massage group. There was a statistically significant difference in the number of hospitalization days among the chicory (3 ±0. 91) (Length of stay: 2-5), massage (2.77±0.73) (Length of stay: 2-4), and control groups (3.37±0.93) (Length of stay: 2-5) (p=0.03). This significant difference was observed between massage and control groups (P=0.02) concerning the rate of hospitalization, and the newborns in the massage group were discharged earlier.ConclusionBased on the results of the present study, Field massage combined with phototherapy was more effective in increasing the frequency of defecation and reducing the length of hospitalization in infants with hyperbilirubinemia compared to those in the chicory bath group and the control group who received phototherapy only.Trial registrationThis trial was registered on https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir (trial ID 77302).Keywords: Chicory, Bath, Massage, Jaundice, Newborn

Background

Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia (NHB) is a frequent metabolic complication in newborns, affecting more than 60% of term infants and about 80% of preterm infants in their first days of life [1]. Hyperbilirubinemia is the most common cause of neonatal hospitalization [2], and although most infants with hyperbilirubinemia have a good prognosis [3], approximately 8–11% of them have elevated bilirubin levels that may lead to severe complications, including lifelong disabilities such as growth retardation, encephalopathy, autism, and hearing impairment [4, 5].

Phototherapy and exchange transfusion are the mainstays for the treatment of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, and a subset of patients also respond to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [6]. A longer duration of phototherapy and increased oxidative stress could be associated with higher mortality rates [7]. Accumulating evidence in recent years has shown that phototherapy may cause a variety of adverse reactions, including DNA damage, cancer risk, and mortality in very low birth weight (VLBW) infants [8–10]. Although phototherapy is considered safe, evidence in recent years has shown that this treatment modality could not be free of side effects as well as short- and long-term unfavorable outcomes. These are usually mild or rare, but should be decreased or avoided if possible. Many useful complementary measures and treatments have been described that could reduce the duration of exposure to phototherapy. Further studies are needed to clarify the preferred complementary treatments shortening the duration of exposure to phototherapy without impairing its effectiveness [11]. Finding new modalities to decrease the duration of treatment is a major concern because any of the available treatment methods may lead to adverse consequences [12].

Massage is a type of therapy to stimulate the organ system for better function. Field massage method on infants focuses on stimulating the chest and abdomen, which can also improve the activity of the digestive organs and the swallowing process of infants, increasing the body’s metabolic system in a way that the toxins in the body are easily excreted through feces and urine [13].

In addition, medicinal plants have also attracted attention for reducing hyperbilirubinemia. Chicory sweat, also known as Grysis, has been used to treat neonatal jaundice for a long time. Chicory has different species, of which three are known: berry chicory, known as tansy, which does not grow in Iran. Yellow chicory, namely dandelion, which was used in most parts of the country in the past, and common chicory. Chicory is a perennial plant with blue or purple flowers, which is originally native to the Old World and was later introduced to the Americas as a wild plant by the roadside. The origin of chicory is Central Europe, Western and Central Asia and North Africa, and it is widely distributed in different parts of Iran, especially in the cities of Eqlid, Azerbaijan and mountainous regions of Khorasan. Several studies have evaluated the effect of common chicory on serum indirect bilirubin levels in infants. Chicory sweat is a stomach tonic, purgative, choleretic, and hepatotonic, which lowers blood pressure [14]. A range of biological activities including antidiabetic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and/or immunomodulatory effects have also been reported with the consumption of chicory leaves, roots, seeds and sweats [15]. This herbal medicine is able to accelerate bilirubin excretion due to its high content of flavonoids (such as apigenin, cortisone luteolin) [16]. A randomized, single-blind clinical trial evaluated the effect of maternal chicory consumption on the severity of neonatal jaundice. The study results showed that chicory consumption by healthy pregnant women did not have a significant effect on reducing serum bilirubin in their infants [17]. However, evaluation of serum bilirubin in hyperbilirubinemic infants after chicory baths before and 24 h after phototherapy showed that chicory baths reduced the severity of neonatal jaundice [18].

In other study, bilirubin levels in blood samples were measured before and after the addition of chicory essence or distilled water. Totally, the chicory essence had no effect on reducing the serum bilirubin level in preterm infants [19].

In a rat model, common chicory extract was prescribed to mothers in order to measure the effects of chicory essence secretion through the milk on rat bilirubin. The results indicated a significant decrease in total and direct bilirubin levels in the neonates with hyperbilirubinemia following the administration of chicory to the mother [20].

There is limited high-quality clinical evidence about chicory’s impact on serum indirect bilirubin in infants. While massage and chicory sweat are each effective to some extent in reducing serum bilirubin levels in newborns, no comparison has been made between these two methods to determine which one is more effective in reducing serum hyperbilirubinemia in newborns. The present study is a randomized controlled clinical trial aimed at comparing the effect of massage or chicory bath on bilirubin levels in term newborns undergoing phototherapy.

Methods

Study design and participants

A three-arm parallel, randomized controlled trial was undertaken at Imam Ali Hospital of Amol, Mazandaran Province in northern Iran from October 26, 2024 to February 6, 2025. The study was conducted on 90 newborns who were hospitalized only for indirect hyperbilirubinemia. Thus, a total of 30 newborns were studied in each of the three groups (massage, Chicory bath and control).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: infants with a gestational age of 37–42 weeks and a weight of 2500–4000 g, Apgar score between 7 and 10 in the first and fifth minutes after birth, jaundice in the first week after birth (2–7 days), indirect hyperbilirubinemia 24 h or later after birth, bilirubin level 12–20 mg/dL, breastfeeding, absence of congenital anomalies and infections, non-hemolytic jaundice, lack of contraindications to massage (wounds, cuts, burns, infectious rashes, dislocations, hyperthermia and swollen joints), no restrictions on bathing and massaging the infant, no medication consumption by the mother, absence of gastrointestinal diseases such as liver enzyme disorders, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), infections (pneumonia, gastroenteritis, sepsis, bronchitis). Exclusion criteria were as follows: G6PD deficiency and positive Coombs test, jaundice requiring blood exchange, treatment with intravenous fluids, antibiotics or oxygen therapy, receiving medications such as milk thistle drops or clofibrate capsules to reduce bilirubin, hypothermia with an axillary temperature of < 36.5o C, smoking, alcohol or drug use by the mother during pregnancy and after delivery, a history of skin allergies in family members or any allergy to chicory sweat in the infant, increasing bilirubin levels to > 20 mg/dL during hospitalization despite treatment, parental unwillingness to continue, discharge or death of the infant.

Randomization and blinding

Simple random sampling was applied in this study. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to three groups via a computerized random number table. Opaque and sealed envelopes were sequentially numbered. A ward nurse outside the research team who was blinded to the study assigned the intervention using the next randomization envelope. Parents were informed to which group they had been assigned. The sampler in this study was another nurse from the neonatal ward who had received training in massage and chicory baths for newborns and did not participate in research design and statistics. The ward nurse, sampler, statisticians and laboratory personnel remained blinded until the final analysis. The parents could not be blinded in this study.

Sample size

based on previous studies [18, 21], the sample size was calculated as 17 subjects in each group, with accuracy = 3.7, confidence interval (CI) = 95%, approximate standard deviation (SD1 = 3.08) and (SD2 = 3.22) and power = 90% for each group. Using the corrected sample size formula (n’=√Kn, k = 2) [22], and considering 20% dropout, a total of 95 eligible newborns were selected through computer-aided randomization in 3 groups (31 subjects in each group). A total of 95 newborns were randomly allotted in a 1:1:1 ratio to three equal groups as follows: (i) Chicory bath, (ii) Massage, and (iii) Control (Fig 0.1).

Data collection

A custom-designed data collection form was used in this study, which was developed in three parts after extensive review of relevant literature review [18, 23]. The first part of this form recorded demographic information of the mother and newborn, and the variables included the age of the newborn, gender, birth weight, gestational age, and type of delivery. The second part of this form included information related to disease such as total bilirubin level at admission, and the third part of the form contained information related to the results of interventions in the three groups (chicory bath group, massage group, and control group), including daily bilirubin test, meconium excretion rate, complications of the intervention, and length of hospital stay.

Validity and reliability of instrument

The design, procedure and writing of this study meet the CONSORT guidelines for randomized controlled trials. The validity of the tool, which was estimated at 94%, was tested by measuring its contents validity index by 10 faculty members of Nasibeh School of Nursing and Midwifery, Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences, as well as neonatologists and nurses in the neonatal ward of Imam Ali Amol Hospital. The reliability of the tool was calculated statistically by Cronbach’s alpha test (r = 0.82). The primary outcomes of the study were bilirubin levels and frequency of defecation, and the secondary outcome included complications of the intervention and the number of hospitalization days, which were measured by the custom-designed data collection form during the study. At least one meeting per month was held to discuss the obstacles and progress during the implementation of the research.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by Ethics Committee of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences, and written informed consent with assent was obtained from each legal guardian before enrollment. The researchers followed ethical principles of beneficence, respect for human dignity and justice. All the personal information was used anonymously to preserve respondents’ privacy and no names but unique codes were recorded during this study. All the participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Data analysis

Data were processed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for Windows (version 26.0) with statistical significance set to p < 0.05. Analysis of variance was used to compare mean bilirubin levels and length of hospital stay, and chi-square test was employed to compare meconium excretion and complications of the intervention in the three groups. ANOVA with repeated measures was used to compare mean bilirubin levels at different times in the three groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was utilized to compare the number of hospital stay days among the groups.

Intervention

The procedure of this study was as follows. Hard copies of informed consent were given to all parents. Eligible newborns were randomly assigned into three groups: massage, chicory bath, and control. Before starting phototherapy, all infants were tested for total and direct bilirubin, G6PD, Coombs, maternal and neonatal blood group and Rhesus factor (Rh). The results were recorded in the patient registration form.

It is worth noting that newborns in all three groups received conventional treatment, namely phototherapy, to control jaundice and prevent harm to them. For newborns, blue light with a radiation of 6–12µw/cm2/nm was used in single and double phototherapy, and intense phototherapy with a radiation of 25µw/cm2/nm was applied based on bilirubin levels.

The sampler who performed the sampling had more than 5 years of experience in the neonatal ward. Phototherapy lamps were checked and adjusted at a height of 30 cm above the infants who were kept in bed with their eyes closed and their reproductive organs protected. After every 3 h of treatment, a 30-minute rest period was considered, in which the infants were breastfed with their eyes open. Hyperbilirubinemia was treated in the selected hospital with single, double, and intensive phototherapy according to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines for the treatment of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. The intervention was performed under supervision of a neonatologist.

The first group received both phototherapy and chicory sweat bath, the second group received both phototherapy and massage and the third group (control group) received only phototherapy according to American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines for the treatment of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. New borns in the chicory bath group were bathed with chicory sweat at the beginning of hospitalization using a sterile cotton before starting phototherapy and then once a day for 4 days after feeding at noon when phototherapy was interrupted for health care and rest. The infant’s body was washed with chicory sweat using sterile cotton by the sampler in the neonatal ward in the presence of infant’s mother. The room temperature was adjusted to 24–28 °C. The chicory sweat was heated to 37.5 °C. Afterward, the infant was washed for 5 to 7 min with a cotton soaked in 100 ml of chicory sweat. The chicory sweat was prepared from Sahar Daru Company with an essential oil concentration of 1.1 to 1.5 mg per 100 ml.

Before the intervention, body temperature was measured using a mercury thermometer (Meheconova). Before intervention, the hands were thoroughly washed, starting from the face to the neck, especially the area behind the ears, chest and upper limbs, abdomen, lower limbs and back. Finally, the body temperature was measured again. If the infant showed allergy to chicory, would be excluded from the study and treated.

In the massage group, massage was performed according to massage therapy method of Field et al. [24] Each infant in the massage group was massaged twice a day (one hour after waking up in the morning and at afternoon) for 15 min (starting approximately 30 min after the first morning feed). The sampler massaged the face using two thumbs to gently rub the forehead and cheek area, and then the hands moved over the chest, the two sampler hands sliding from the lower edge of the chest to the opposite upper edge alternately. Afterward, on the abdomen, in accordance with the structure of the colon, the practitioner gently pressed in a semicircle, then the hands were placed on the limbs, the practitioner used moderate hand pressure from the outer side of the upper and lower limbs, and finally on the back, the practitioner slid both hands from both sides of the cervical vertebra to the buttocks. The massage intervention was performed in the intervention group in the morning and afternoon every day.

During the treatment, serum bilirubin was measured every day at 6 AM by photometry (Spectrophotometer, Cecil Model, Italy) at a reliable laboratory in the same hospital. Bilirubin level, meconium excretion rate, complications of the intervention, and length of hospital stay were recorded by the sampler on patient registration form.

Results

Figure 1 shows the CONSORT flow diagram, depicting the participant flow of this study. A total of 120 newborns were admitted to the neonatal ward from October 26, 2024 to February 6, 2025. After excluding 25 newborns, namely infants with positive Coombs (n = 10) and G6PD deficiency (n = 15), 95 newborns remained eligible. All the participants were randomly allocated to one of the three groups, including Chicory bath (n = 32), Massage group (n = 32), and control group (n = 31). Five newborns were dropped out due to the following reasons: (1) parents were unwilling to continue the study (n = 2, 1 in the Chicory bath group, 1 in the Massage group); (2) newborns required intravenous fluids and medication (n = 3, 1 in the Chicory bath group, 1 in the Massage group and 1 in the control group). A total of 30 newborns were studied in each of the chicory bath, massage, and control groups (Fig. 1).

There was no significant difference in the frequency of defecation, namely another primary outcome of the study, among the three groups on the days of hospitalization and the second day, respectively (p = 0.81, p = 0.28). However, there was a significant difference between the three groups in terms of frequency of defecation on the third and fourth days (p = 0.02), and the frequency of defecation was higher in the massage group.

The findings regarding secondary outcomes, namely the number of hospitalization days and the rate of complications, are as follows.

There was a statistically significant difference in the number of hospitalization days among the chicory (3 ± 0. 91) (Length of stay: 2–5), massage (2.77 ± 0.73) (Length of stay: 2–4), and control groups (3.37 ± 0.93) (Length of stay: 2–5) (p = 0.03). A post hoc Tukey test showed a significant difference in the number of hospitalization days between the massage and control groups (p = 0.02), and the infants in the massage group were discharged earlier.

There was no significant difference in the rate of complications among the three groups on the second (p = 0.36), third (p = 0.48), and fourth (p = 0.66) days of hospitalization. There were no adverse effects such as drug sensitivity for infants who used chicory baths as the intervention. Skin rashes were observed in one infant in the massage group as well as two infants in the control group who were referred to a neonatologist and diagnosed with phototherapy sensitivity.

Discussion

Based on the findings, no significant difference was observed in mean bilirubin levels of the chicory bath, massage, and control groups on the first, second, third, and fourth days. Although the results of the present study are consistent with the study of Dehghani et al. [23], the methodology of the two studies was different. In the present study, chicory bath was performed once a day for up to 4 days, but in Dehghani’s study, the infant was bathed only once immediately after birth. In addition, giving medications such as Cotoneaster drop or clofibrate capsule to newborns by mothers, as well as the newborns’ type of feeding, which was beyond the researcher’s control might have affected the newborn’s bilirubin level. In the study of Mohammadi Pirkashani et al. [21], chicory bath was performed for 2 days with 500 ml of chicory sweat, and the mean bilirubin level of the chicory bath group at 24 and 48 h after the intervention was lower than that of the control group. This study reported that chicory extract in a chicory extract bath was absorbed through the skin and reduced bilirubin levels in infants, but the mechanism of chicory’s effect and the rate of meconium excretion were not reported. Moreover, in this study, serum therapy was used for infants if needed, which was part of the exclusion criteria in the present study, which could have affected the outcome of the intervention. In the present study, the bath was performed with a cotton soaked in chicory sweat, while in the study of Mohammadi Pirkashani, the bath was performed with 500 ml of chicory sweat prepared by hydroalcoholic method in a bathtub. However, different bathing methods in various studies may have affected the results.

In the present study, massage was not significantly different from other groups in reducing bilirubin within the four days of intervention, but the rate of excretion in the massage group was higher than in the chicory bath and control groups on the third and fourth days. In the study conducted by Chen et al. [25], it was reported that meconium excretion in infants who received Field massage was higher on the first and second days than in the control group. Of course, Chen et al. studied all term infants from birth, while the present study only included infants hospitalized with jaundice. Furthermore, in the study of Abdolhamid Zaki and Mohammad Sabet [26], an increase in the frequency of excretion in the Field massage group was reported, which is similar to the present study. Seyed Rasouli et al. found in their study that Field massage may have a positive effect on bilirubin levels, provided that it is performed over a period of more than 4 days [27]. In the study conducted by Boskabadi et al. [28], performing massage twice for 20 min from the first day after delivery to the fourth day led to an increase in bowel movements and a significant difference in bilirubin levels between the control and treatment groups. Study results may vary depending on the type of massage, the techniques used during the massage, the experience of the persons administering the massage, as well as massage duration and frequency [29].

Of course, the difference between massage using the Field method and massage in general is in the area that is stimulated. The massage using Field method provides more stimulation to the face, stomach and chest area. It aims to activate the vagus nerve to increase metabolism such that the digestive organs can work better. Field massage as adjuvant therapy can increase the excretion of bilirubin that has been broken down through phototherapy mechanisms [30].

The reduction of jaundice with massage therapy may be related to the stimulation of bowel movements; increased stool output enables infants to pass more bilirubin-containing meconium [25]. However, this idea was not confirmed in a meta-analysis study, and the reasons were cited as sample size, massage therapy methods, and different incidence rates of jaundice [31]. Another study also reported that the effectiveness of massage therapy increased significantly with increasing frequency and duration [32]. In the present study, the findings showed that the three groups were similar in terms of complications, which is in line with the study of Mohammadi Pirkashani et al. [21] Therefore, chicory bath and massage did not have more side effects than phototherapy.

There was a significant difference in the number of hospitalization days among chicory, massage and control groups. Thus, infants in the massage group were discharged earlier. This finding is consistent with the studies conducted by Lin et al. [33], Dag and Yayan [18] and Ahmadipour et al. [34]on massage.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although we successfully blinded the ward nurse, the statistician and laboratory personnel, the parents could not be blinded in spite of our efforts. Another limitation of this study was that the volume of the newborn’s stool was not measured. Other limitations included discharge of most infants on the fifth day and the lack of follow-up after discharge in terms of bilirubin reduction and meconium excretion, because studies have reported that Field massage may have a positive effect on bilirubin levels provided that it is performed over a period of more than 4 days [27].Additionally, this research was conducted in term infants with jaundice, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Further studies are recommended to discuss the relationship between the effect of Field massage on serum bilirubin levels and frequency of defecation with longer follow-up periods.

Conclusion

Based on the results of the present study, Field massage combined with phototherapy was more effective in increasing the frequency of defecation and reducing the length of hospitalization in newborns with hyperbilirubinemia compared to infants in the chicory bath group and the control group who received phototherapy only.
