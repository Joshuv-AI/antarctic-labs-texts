Sudden Release of a Fixed Contracture as a Clue to Occult Humeral Fracture in a Neonate With Arthrogryposis Multiplex Congenita
Authors: Daisuke Watanabe, Yohei Hasebe, Yuki Maebayashi, Nobuyuki Katsumata, Atsushi Nemoto, Atsushi Naitoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A neonate with multiple congenital contractures consistent with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita was found to have an initially clinically occult humeral shaft fracture immediately after axillary cleansing. The first abnormal sign was the sudden release of a previously fixed upper-limb posture, which initially resembled improvement in contracture, before swelling, erythema, or deformity became apparent. Radiography confirmed a spiral fracture of the midshaft of the left humerus, which healed with conservative treatment. This case highlights that an abrupt apparent improvement in a fixed contracture after handling should prompt consideration of occult long-bone fracture and targeted radiographic assessment. Careful support of the entire long bone and avoidance of rotational force across stiff joints may help reduce the risk of a handling-related injury during routine neonatal care.

Introduction

Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC) refers to a heterogeneous group of conditions characterized by multiple, congenital, nonprogressive joint contractures [1]. Reduced fetal movement is the common pathogenic pathway and may lead to contractures accompanied by secondary abnormalities of the muscles and periarticular tissues. Fractures have been reported in infants with AMC, particularly during the perinatal period [2]. Bathing and cleansing are routine components of neonatal skin care [3]. However, practical descriptions of the earliest fracture signs during routine care in neonates with AMC are limited. Here, we present a neonate with multiple congenital contractures consistent with AMC who developed a clinically occult humeral shaft fracture immediately after axillary cleansing. The sudden release of a previously fixed contracture was the first clinical clue and prompted radiography before swelling, erythema, or deformity became apparent.

Case presentation

A male infant was born at 40 weeks and 4 days of gestation with a birth weight of 4,110 g. Apgar scores were 7 and 9 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. There was no family history of congenital contractures, recurrent fractures, or known skeletal dysplasia. Contractures were present at birth in the elbows, hips, knees, and fingers (Figure 1A). Initial radiographs showed no fractures (Figure 1B). The infant had multiple congenital contractures consistent with AMC. The phenotype was clinically suggestive of amyoplasia, although no etiologic diagnosis was established.

Clinical and radiographic findings

(A) Contractures of the upper and lower limbs at birth; (B) Initial radiograph showing no evidence of fracture; (C) Radiograph obtained after sudden release of the left arm contracture on day 6 showing a spiral fracture of the midshaft of the left humerus (arrow); (D) Follow-up radiograph on day 20 showing callus formation after splintage.

At six days of age, axillary cleansing was performed during bathing due to retained debris. Immediately after this procedure, the previously fixed posture of the left upper limb suddenly released, giving the appearance of improved contracture. There was no swelling, erythema, or deformity. Because the abrupt change in limb posture was unexpected, radiography was performed and revealed a spiral fracture of the midshaft of the left humerus (Figure 1C). A splint was applied. Fourteen days after fracture recognition, follow-up radiography at 20 days of age showed callus formation (Figure 1D). The fracture healed with conservative treatment. No additional fractures occurred during the remaining follow-up after handling precautions were introduced, and the congenital contractures gradually improved with conservative management.

Following this event, the incident was reported to the hospital’s patient safety committee. The parents were informed of the situation, and consent was obtained to modify routine hygiene care. During axillary cleansing, the entire humerus was supported. External rotation of the shoulder was avoided. The axilla was opened only by gentle abduction synchronized with exhalation. If a gentle opening was difficult due to crying or increased muscle tone, cleanliness was maintained using gauze or a cotton swab rather than forcing the shoulder position. No recurrent fracture was observed during the remaining follow-up after handling precautions were introduced. No postnatal findings emerged that supported a progressive neuromuscular disorder, a syndromic diagnosis, or another bone fragility disorder.

Discussion

Perinatal and early-infantile fractures have been reported in AMC, demonstrating that long-bone fractures can occur in affected infants. AMC encompasses a heterogeneous group of conditions associated with a substantial musculoskeletal and orthopedic burden [2,4]. However, previous reports have mainly focused on the occurrence of fractures and have paid less attention to how they first become apparent during routine neonatal care. In this regard, the present case offers a practical bedside observation: an occult humeral shaft fracture was identified immediately after routine axillary cleansing in the neonatal period, before swelling or erythema became apparent.

The main clinical significance of this case lies in its mode of presentation. The earliest abnormal finding was not local inflammation or visible deformity, but rather the sudden release of a previously fixed contracture. This apparent release should raise suspicion for occult long-bone fracture in neonates with severe contractures, especially when it occurs immediately after handling. The finding can be mistaken for an improvement in range of motion. A handling-related torsional load may have contributed to the fracture when the rigid upper limb was opened for axillary cleansing. In a contracted limb, even gentle opening of the axilla may transmit rotational stress to the humeral shaft when motion across the shoulder and elbow is restricted. This interpretation is consistent with the temporal relationship observed at the bedside, the absence of fracture on initial radiographs, and the lack of recurrent fracture after handling was modified. However, the proposed handling-related mechanism is based on clinical observation and cannot be confirmed from a single case. Thus, the clinical value of this case lies not in the fracture pattern itself but in highlighting an early, easily overlooked clue to occult fracture during routine care.

This case suggests several practical considerations for bedside care. In neonates with severe contractures, supporting the entire humerus, minimizing rotational force across stiff joints, and using alternative cleaning methods when gentle axillary opening is difficult may reduce handling-related stress. Fractures in nonambulatory infants also warrant careful evaluation for bone fragility disorders and non-accidental trauma [5]. In this context, a sudden apparent improvement in a pre-existing contracture may justify targeted examination and radiographic assessment rather than reassurance. Recognition of this sign may facilitate earlier diagnosis of an occult fracture and inform follow-up and safer handling during subsequent care.

Conclusions

In neonates with severe congenital contractures, sudden release of a previously fixed limb posture may be an early sign of an occult long-bone fracture rather than true improvement in range of motion. When this finding occurs immediately after handling, targeted examination and radiographic assessment may be considered before swelling, erythema, or deformity becomes apparent. During bathing or axillary cleansing, supporting the entire humerus, minimizing rotational force across stiff joints, and using alternative cleaning methods when gentle opening is difficult may help reduce handling-related stress. Recognition of this sign may facilitate earlier diagnosis of an occult fracture and inform safer subsequent bedside care.
