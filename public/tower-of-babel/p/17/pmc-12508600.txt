Concurrent incomplete atrioventricular septal defect and supravalvular mitral stenosis causing heart failure in a 13-year-old cat: a case report
Authors: Kazuki HARADA, Chisato AKITA, Hinako HARADA, Hiroki KUBOTA, Yuji SUNDEN
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 13-year-old female Persian cat with a heart murmur presented with dyspnea. Thoracic radiography revealed pleural effusion and dilation of peripheral pulmonary vessels. Echocardiography showed significant enlargement of the right atrium and ventricle and left atrial appendage; an atrial septal defect (ASD) near the atrioventricular valves; and a septum-like structure below the left atrial appendage. Left-to-right shunting through the ASD was noted, with a pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 2.72. The cat was diagnosed with incomplete atrioventricular septal defect (IAVSD) and supravalvular mitral stenosis (SVMS) and subsequently medically managed for 7 months before death. Necropsy confirmed IAVSD with a mitral valve cleft and SVMS. To our knowledge, this is the longest reported survival in a cat with both anomalies.Keywords: cat, heart failure, incomplete atrioventricular septal defect, supravalvular mitral stenosis

Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD), also known as endocardial cushion defect, is a rare congenital heart disease in cats [3, 10, 11]. AVSD is categorized into complete and incomplete types, with one of the incomplete types involving an ostium primum atrial septal defect (ASD) accompanied by an intact ventricular septum and an abnormal mitral valve [10]. Supravalvular mitral stenosis (SVMS) is another congenital heart anomaly characterized by a fibrous or fibromuscular septum—known as a supravalvular mitral stenotic ring—within the left atrium. This condition can be differentiated from cor triatriatum sinister (CTS) based on the location of the septum [1, 2]. Concurrent incomplete AVSD (IAVSD) and SVMS is exceptionally rare in cats, with only four feline reported cases, to our knowledge [2]. All of these cases were diagnosed before 2 years of age, with three resulting in death or euthanasia shortly thereafter [2]. Here, we report a feline case of IAVSD and SVMS that showed no clinical signs until the onset of heart failure at 13 years of age.

The patient was a neutered female Persian cat weighing 2.6 kg (body condition score, 2/5). Although a heart murmur had been auscultated from an early age, no detailed cardiac evaluation had been conducted. At 13 years of age, she presented with dyspnea and was suspected of cardiac involvement based on physical examination. She was referred to Tottori University Veterinary Medical Center on Jun 13, 2023 (Day 1) for further assessment. Written informed consent for this study was obtained from the owner.

Physical examination revealed an elevated respiratory rate (60 breaths/min) with exertional dyspnea, regular heartbeat rhythm (150 beats/min) with a Levine 3/6 systolic murmur, which was loudest over the left parasternal area. Blood tests showed a mild to moderate decrease in hemoglobin (9.2 g/dL, reference interval [RI]: 9.8–15.4 g/dL), packed cell volume (27.8%, RI: 30–45%), and platelet count (146 × 103/µL, RI: 300–800 × 103/µL), as well as a mild increase in blood nitrogen urea (45 mg/dL, RI: 19–34 mg/dL). Electrocardiography based on standard limb lead II revealed a normal sinus rhythm with deep S waves. Thoracic radiography showed pleural effusion with obscuration of the cardiac silhouette, dilatation of the peripheral pulmonary arteries and veins, and an alveolar lung pattern in both cranial lung lobes (Fig. 1A and 1B). Echocardiography revealed severe enlargement of the right atrium, right ventricle, and left atrial appendage, along with a large ASD (defect diameter: 9.6 mm) proximal to the atrioventricular valves. A septum-like structure was observed within the left atrium and was located inferior to the left atrial appendage (Fig. 2A and 2B). A left-to-right continuous shunting blood flow (a peak velocity of 2.3 m/sec during atrial systole) was observed from this septum-like structure, through the ASD, and into the right atrium (Fig. 2A), with a high estimated pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 2.72. Mitral valve regurgitation (MR), flowing into distal left atrium (DLA) and accompanied by shunting blood flow into the right atrium, was also observed during systole (Fig. 2B), with a velocity of 5.3 m/sec measured in the left apical four-chamber view. Although a standardized protocol for the diagnosis of feline pulmonary hypertension (PH) has not yet been established [7], echocardiographic findings consistent with canine PH [8] were also observed. These included ventricular septal flattening during both diastole and systole (Fig. 3), pulmonary artery (PA) enlargement (PA to aorta ratio: 1.28), shortening of acceleration time (AT) of pulmonary arterial flow velocity (AT to ejection time ratio: 0.22), dilatation of the hepatic veins and caudal vena cava, and mild ascites. Pulmonary ultrasonography detected B-lines in both cranial lung lobes.

The results supported a diagnosis of IAVSD (left-to-right shunt) complicated by a left atrial septal formation (consistent with either CTS or SVMS), leading to congestive heart failure and suspected PH. On Day 1, medical treatment with beraprost sodium was initiated (10 µg/kg q 12 hr), torasemide (0.14 mg/kg q 24 hr), and spironolactone (2.4 mg/kg q 24 hr). Regular thoracocentesis (100–150 mL per session) was also performed. Following these treatments, the patient’s respiratory symptoms exhibited a fluctuating course, characterized by recurrent episodes of deterioration followed by improvement, predominantly attributable to thoracocentesis. Despite recommendations for escalation of therapy, the owner declined further intensification of the medical regimen due to difficulties and burden associated with administering medication to the patient. Imaging findings remained largely unchanged, including left-to-right shunting by IAVSD. The patient experienced sudden death on Day 227.

A necropsy was performed with the owner’s consent. Significant blood-like pleural effusion was confirmed within the thoracic cavity, with minimal ascites present. Gross heart pathology revealed marked dilation of both the atrial appendage and right ventricle, along with partial thinning of the ventricular wall (Fig. 4). A large defect in the inferior portion of the atrial septum was confirmed, with thickened bilateral atrioventricular valves and a cleft-like indentation in the septal leaflet of the mitral valve (Fig. 5A). A membranous septum structure that was continuous with the mitral valve (Fig. 5B) was observed within the left atrium and located inferior to the left atrial appendage (Fig. 5C). Histologically, the septum-like structure in the left atrium was composed of fibrous tissue continuous with the mitral valve (Fig. 6). Atrophy, loss of myocardial fibers, and fibrosis were observed in the right ventricular wall, with notable grossly thinning lesions (Fig. 4). Furthermore, pulmonary edema and pleural thickening were histopathologically identified in the lungs.

In this study, our patient was diagnosed with IAVSD based on the large ASD located proximally to the atrioventricular valves together with an intact ventricular septum. Schrope [10] found that 8 of 26 cats with AVSD were diagnosed with IAVSD with atrial communication, the prevalence of which was higher than that of complete AVSD (n=4) but lower than that of IAVSD with ventricular communication (n=14). The author also reported double-outlet right atrium (DORA) in 3 of the 8 cats with IAVSD with atrial communication. However, DORA was not identified in our case because none of the characteristic features were noted, including an extreme leftward deviation of the lower portion of the interatrial septum [4, 5, 9].

A septum located inside the left atrium, which was observed in our case, has been implicated in two congenital heart diseases (i.e., SVMS and CTS), which can be differentiated based on the position of the septum. In SVMS, the septum is located below the fossa ovalis and divides the left atrium into a proximal chamber, which encompasses the left auricle and the outlet of the pulmonary veins, and a distal chamber adjacent to the mitral valve leaflets [1, 2]. In comparison, in CTS, the septum is located above the fossa ovalis and causes formation of a distal chamber that encompasses the left auricle [1, 2]. The septal formation within the left atrium in our case initially raised a suspicion for CTS; however, re-evaluation of echocardiographic images and necropsy findings ultimately established a diagnosis of SVMS. Our findings underline the importance of accurately differentiating between CTS and SVMS based on the location of the left atrial septum. A mitral valve anomaly involving a continuity with the abnormal left atrial septum strongly indicates that both IAVSD and SVMS are congenital abnormalities resulting from endocardial cushion malformation. This hypothesis is supported by the findings of Campbell and Thomas [2] that IAVSD is the most frequent concurrent cardiac abnormality in cats with SVMS. Ultimately, we diagnosed our patient with multiple congenital heart defects, specifically concurrent IAVSD and SVMS, although a case of concurrent IAVSD and CTS had also been previously reported [6].

Considering the pathophysiology of multiple congenital heart diseases, both left- and right-sided heart failure was expected in our feline case. In the present case, left-sided congestive heart failure was confirmed by the presence of pulmonary edema and pleural effusion, which may have been caused by increased blood pressure within the left atrium, especially the proximal chamber, resulting from SVMS, as indicated by Alessandro et al. [1]. Right-sided heart failure, which is characterized by right atrial and ventricular enlargement and thinning and fibrosis of the right ventricular wall, is highly likely due to volume overload in the right heart due to IAVSD with left-to-right shunting [10]. This condition is likely further exacerbated by MR flow associated with a cleft-like mitral valve. Furthermore, increased left atrial pressure due to SVMS may induce post-capillary PH, as indicated by several related findings [7, 8], thereby contributing to increased pressure load in the right heart.

The patient in this case first showed signs of heart failure at the advanced age of 13 years and survived for 7 months after its onset. To our knowledge, this cat has survived the longest among previously reported feline cases with concurrent IAVSD and SVMS. Although we could not identify the precise reason for the long-term survival in this case, one possible reason may be that the elevated pressure in DLA may have impeded the inflow of venous blood from the right atrium, thereby potentially suppressing the development of cyanosis due to right-to-left shunting (i.e., Eisenmenger’s syndrome), in addition to the lack of severe concurrent diseases in other organs. Further investigation into prognostic factors for feline cases of IAVSD and/or SVMS is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and thus enhance the management of such diseases.
