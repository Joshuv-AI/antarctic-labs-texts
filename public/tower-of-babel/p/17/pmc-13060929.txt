Motor-cognitive aging: The role of motor cortex and its pathways
Authors: Jiaqi Wen, Zifei Liang, Chenyang Li, Huize Pang, Li Jiang, Jiayi Li, Xiaojun Guan, Jiangyang Zhang, Henry Rusinek, Xiaojun Xu, Yulin Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Motor and cognitive decline are hallmark features of aging. In the primary motor cortex (M1), pyramidal neurons project to the corticospinal tract (CST), a well-established motor pathway, and send collaterals to the ipsilateral striatum, forming the corticostriatal tract (CStrT). While the CST has been extensively studied, the role of the CStrT in motor and cognitive aging remains poorly understood.Methods:We analyzed T1- and T2-weighted MRI, multi-delay arterial spin labeling, and multi-shell diffusion MRI data from 339 right-handed healthy adults (aged 36–90 years) in the Human Connectome Project–Aging dataset. Age-related trajectories of M1 structure and hemodynamics, as well as CST and CStrT microstructure, were assessed. Segment-wise along-tract analyses were conducted to identify localized tract degeneration. Mediation analyses were performed to examine whether tract integrity linked M1 atrophy to motor and cognitive performance.Results:With age, M1 exhibited reduced volume and hemodynamics, altered T1/T2 ratio, and increased cortical curvature, reflecting structural and hemodynamic alterations. Along-tract analyses revealed localized microstructural degeneration in the CST adjacent to M1, whereas the CStrT showed more extensive degeneration along its trajectory. These tract changes were associated with structural and hemodynamic alterations in M1. Furthermore, integrity of the dominant (left) CST and CStrT mediated the relationship between ipsilateral M1 atrophy and motor decline. Notably, CStrT integrity also mediated the association between M1 atrophy and motor cognition decline.Conclusion:These findings establish age-related structural and functional degeneration of M1 and its pathways, highlighting the CStrT as a critical mediator between motor cortical atrophy and both motor and cognitive decline. These normative imaging markers of healthy aging may help inform the early detection of neurodegenerative diseases.Keywords: Aging, Human connectome project, Primary motor cortex, White matter tracts, Structure, Hemodynamics, Motor Cognition

Introduction

Healthy aging is accompanied by declines in both motor and cognitive function, which can compromise quality of life and increase the risk of disability in older adults (Beard et al., 2016). The primary motor cortex (M1), located in the precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe (Ribas, 2010), plays a crucial role in voluntary movement and exhibits a somatotopic organization (Schott, 1993). Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have demonstrated age-related cortical thinning in M1 as early as midlife (Salat et al., 2004). Quantitative MRI studies have shown increased iron accumulation with age in this region (Hirai et al., 1996). Moreover, the motor cortex appears vulnerable to age-related cerebrovascular changes (Schreiber et al., 2023), which have been linked to both functional reorganization of the aging brain and cognitive decline (Li et al., 2022; Staffaroni et al., 2019). While prior studies have documented age-related alterations in M1 (Wang et al., 2025; Wulff-Abramsson et al., 2025), little is known about how these changes extend to its connected white matter tracts.

The corticospinal tract (CST), a primary motor pathway descending from M1, exhibits age-related demyelination and reductions in fiber density (Sala et al., 2012; Kelley et al., 2021). These changes are thought to contribute to the decline in motor performance commonly observed in older adults (Coon et al., 2022). Anatomically, CST axons descend through the internal capsule and brainstem, with most fibers decussating at the pyramids of the medulla to project contralaterally (Welniarz et al., 2017). This pattern of lateralization introduces individual variability in the expression of structural aging effects across hemispheres.

Beyond the CST, pyramidal neurons from M1 also project to the ipsilateral striatum, forming the corticostriatal tract (CStrT) (McColgan et al., 2020). While striatal degeneration has been associated with both motor and cognitive aging (Volkow et al., 1998), the structural integrity of the M1–striatal connection via the CStrT has been largely neglected in the aging literature. As such, the CStrT may represent a crucial but underexplored link between cortical degeneration and broader motor-cognitive aging. Furthermore, few studies have examined how microstructural changes vary along the CST and CStrT, making it difficult to identify specific patterns of white matter degeneration.

This study aims to systematically examine age-related structural and functional changes in the M1 and its two principal output tracts – the CST and CStrT, and to evaluate how these alterations relate to motor and cognitive performance. We leveraged the Human Connectome Project-Aging (HCP-Aging) dataset (Bookheimer et al., 2019; Harms et al., 2018), which offers a comprehensive multiparametric neuroimaging protocol from a large lifespan cohort. This dataset includes high-resolution T1- and T2-weighted MRI, multiple post-labeling delay (PLD) pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL), multi-shell diffusion MRI (dMRI), and extensive behavioral assessments. Only right-handed participants were included to minimize variability associated with CST decussation and potential effects of handedness.

Methodologically, compared to memory-related regions, M1 can be segmented more reliably and consistently across individuals. Similarly, the CST and CStrT are among the most robustly identifiable pathways, enabling precise and reproducible tractography. Furthermore, we applied along-tract analysis to detect localized microstructural variations along fiber pathways.

We hypothesized that: (1) M1 would undergo both structural and hemodynamic alterations with aging; (2) the CST and CStrT would exhibit tract-specific patterns of age-related microstructural changes along their trajectories; (3) age-related tract changes would be linked to M1 structure and hemodynamics; and (4) the CST would be associated with motor function, whereas the CStrT may play an important role in both motor and cognitive decline during aging. This study offers a framework for investigating the normative aging trajectories of the M1 and its pathways, offering insights into the interconnected decline of motor and cognitive functions related to aging. Such understanding may support future research on age-related degeneration of the brain’s motor system, serving as key references for the study of neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia.

Materials and methods

Participants from the HCP-Aging dataset

This study included 339 participants from the publicly available HCP-Aging database, Lifespan 2.0 release (https://www.humanconnectome.org/study/hcp-lifespan-aging/document/hcp-aging-20-release). All participants provided written informed consent, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study protocol as part of a cross-sectional research initiative. The HCP-Aging cohort comprises healthy volunteers that represent normative aging across the adult lifespan. Participants were free of known pathological conditions associated with cognitive decline, such as stroke, brain tumors, or clinically diagnosed dementia (Bookheimer et al., 2019). Only right-handed individuals were included in our study, as determined by an Edinburgh Handedness Inventory score of ≥ 50 (range: −100 indicating full left-handedness to 100 indicating full right-handedness) (Oldfield, 1971; Indovina et al., 2020). Participants over the age of 90 were excluded from our study due to potential inaccuracies in self-reported age. Detailed demographic descriptions and inclusion/exclusion criteria for the HCP-Aging dataset have been provided in prior publications (Bookheimer et al., 2019). For analyses of age-related differences, participants were stratified into two age groups using a 65-year cutoff, consistent with epidemiological definitions and prior aging research (Beard et al., 2016; Rubenstein, 2006): younger adults (36–65 years) and older adults (66–90 years).

Motor function and motor cognition (i.e., executive function) were assessed to investigate age-related alterations in motor and cognitive performance. Motor function was assessed using three domains: endurance, measured by the 2-minute walk test; strength (dominant hand), measured by grip strength dynamometry test; and locomotion, evaluated using the 4-meter walk test. Motor cognition was assessed using a battery of tasks targeting: cognitive flexibility via the Dimensional Change Card Sort (DCCS) test; inhibitory control via the Flanker task; and task switching via the Trail Making Test Parts A and B. All motor and cognitive function measures were analyzed using normative scores, with higher scores indicating better performance, except for the Trail Making Test, which was measured in seconds, where longer completion times reflect poorer performance.

All participants underwent a comprehensive imaging protocol, which included T1-weighted (T1w) imaging, T2-weighted (T2w) imaging, multiple PLD pCASL, and multi-shell dMRI, allowing for the evaluation of brain structure, cerebral hemodynamics, and white matter integrity.

MRI acquisition protocol

MRI data for the HCP-Aging participants were acquired across four sites using Siemens Prisma 3 Tesla scanners equipped with 32-channel head coils, all running the same software version (E11C). A harmonized, state-of-the-art imaging protocol was implemented across sites, with standardized procedures, centralized training, and ongoing quality control analyses ensuring high inter-site consistency (Harms et al., 2018). A T1w anatomical scan was obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, echo times (TE) = 1.8/3.6/5.4/7.2 ms, repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1000 ms, flip angle = 8°, number of echoes = 4. T2-weighted images were acquired using a variable-flip-angle turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence (Siemens SPACE) with the following parameters: TR = 3200 ms, TE = 564 ms, turbo factor = 314, voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3.

The pCASL data were acquired without background suppression, as described in previous studies (Li et al., 2018; Li et al., 2015). The labeling duration was 1500 ms, with five PLDs: 200 ms (6 control/label pairs), 700 ms (6 pairs), 1200 ms (6 pairs), 1700 ms (10 pairs), and 2200 ms (15 pairs). Signal readout was conducted using multi-band 2D gradient echo-planar imaging (EPI) with the following parameters: TR = 3580 ms, TE = 19 ms, partial Fourier = 6/8, simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) acceleration factor = 6, total slices = 60, voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm3, total acquisition time = 5.5 min. Two equilibrium magnetization (M₀) scans were acquired at the end of the session to calibrate cerebral blood flow (CBF) quantification. For the correction of field inhomogeneity-related distortions, a pair of spin echo EPI scans with opposite phase encoding directions was acquired: spatial resolution = 2.5 mm isotropic, TR = 8000 ms, TE = 40 ms.

The dMRI was performed using a multiband 2D spin echo EPI sequence with the following parameters: TR = 3230 ms, TE = 89.20 ms, voxel size = 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm3, multiband acceleration factor = 4, b-values = 1500 and 3000 s/mm2 (92~93 directions per shell) with 28 b-value = 0 s/mm2 images. Two spin-echo EPI scans were acquired with opposite anterior-posterior phase-encoding directions for distortion correction. The total acquisition time for the dMRI protocol was approximately 21 min.

MRI processing

T1w image processing

T1w images were processed using the “recon-all” command from the FreeSurfer (version 6.0) pipeline (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). This procedure performs a full cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation workflow, including the following steps (Fischl et al., 2002; Fischl, 2012): correction for magnetic field inhomogeneities via non-uniform intensity normalization; computation of the Talairach transform; skull stripping; brain-extracted intensity normalization to improve tissue classification and surface reconstruction; subcortical and white matter segmentation; tessellation of the gray–white matter boundary; automatic topology correction; generation of the pial and gray–white matter surfaces; and cortical parcellation based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Following these procedures, regional brain volumes and the estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) were computed. All segmentations were visually inspected for anatomical accuracy and manually corrected as needed, following the FreeSurfer guidelines (http://freesurfer.net/fswiki/FsTutorial/TroubleshootingData).

Cortical volume and morphologic measurements

The volume and mean cortical curvature of the precentral gyrus, corresponding to the M1, were extracted based on anatomical definitions provided by the Desikan–Killiany atlas, which parcellates the cerebral cortex into 34 regions per hemisphere (Desikan et al., 2006). FreeSurfer-derived M1 vol measurements have shown generally high test–retest reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients typically exceeding 0.90 across repeated scans (Liem et al., 2015). To account for inter-individual differences in head size, precentral gyrus volume was normalized by the eTIV.

Mean cortical curvature of M1 was calculated as the integrated rectified mean curvature across each surface label (Pienaar et al., 2008). Rectification involves converting negative curvature values, which correspond to concave folds, into their absolute values. This produces a direction-independent measure of local surface bending (Koenderink and Van Doorn, 1992). Mean curvature H is defined as the average of the maximum (k1) and minimum (k2) principal curvatures (inverse radii):

H is given in units of mm−1. Higher values indicate more pronounced cortical folds. Mean curvature provides a reliable estimate of gyral shape patterns that complements volumetric measures (Jessen et al., 2019).

T1/T2 ratio map reconstruction

The T1/T2 ratio map was generated for each subject by performing voxel-wise division of the T1w image by the corresponding T2w image (Du et al., 2019). Since the T1w and T2w images were acquired during the same scanning session with identical spatial resolution (0.8 mm isotropic), and all images were visually inspected to confirm the absence of substantial head motion. These steps were taken to ensure spatial alignment between the two modalities and enable accurate voxel-wise ratio computation. The mean T1/T2 ratio within the precentral gyrus was extracted for further analysis.

ASL processing

The multi-band pCASL data (acquired with 10 slice bands) were preprocessed using the hcp-asl minimal processing pipeline (McConnell et al., 2022) (https://github.com/physimals/hcp-asl) to generate CBF and arterial transit time (ATT) maps. First, the label and control images were corrected for linear intensity variation (banding artifacts) observed within each 10-slice band, which are attributed to magnetization transfer (MT) effects. Then, slice-wise signal fluctuations caused by simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) acquisition were corrected by fitting a saturation recovery curve. Motion parameters were estimated iteratively in three steps: the first two iterations progressively refined the alignment to improve saturation recovery fitting, while the third iteration aligned the de-banded ASL image series to the final target space for subsequent analysis. Susceptibility distortion correction was carried out using TOPUP with phase encoding-reversed spin-echo images (Andersson et al., 2003). Subsequently, dynamic ASL image series were aligned with the pre-processed T1w images using the HCP structural minimal preprocessing pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013).

Hemodynamic analysis was performed on the 2.5 mm isotropic ASL voxel grid. A general linear model was employed for label-control subtraction, aiding in the rescaling of the perfusion-weighted signal and its separation from through-slice motion variations (Suzuki et al., 2019). Computation of CBF and ATT was executed using the Bayesian Inference for Arterial Spin Labeling (BASIL) toolkit within FSL (version 6.0.5, Oxford, UK), with partial volume correction derived using Toblerone on the FreeSurfer-derived cortical surface and FreeSurfer segmentations for subcortical structures (Kirk et al., 2020; Chappell et al., 2009; Chappell et al., 2011). This approach independently estimates tissue and macrovascular compartments (Parkes and Tofts, 2002).

To compute CBF and ATT in M1, the low-resolution T1w images of the ASL grid generated by the ASL pipeline were registered to the native high-resolution T1w images. The resulting transformation was applied to the CBF and ATT maps, and the resulting hemodynamic images were averaged over the precentral gyrus.

dMRI preprocessing

The dMRI data were preprocessed using MRtrix3 (version 3.0.3) (Tournier et al., 2019), following procedures informed by the DESIGNER pipeline (Ades-Aron et al., 2018). The preprocessing pipeline included denoising, Gibbs ringing artifact removal, motion and eddy current correction, and susceptibility-induced distortion correction using FSL’s topup and eddy software. Bias field correction was also applied to improve spatial intensity homogeneity. The diffusion tensor was estimated using weighted least-squares based on the preprocessed diffusion-weighted images (Basser et al., 1994; Veraart et al., 2013). Voxel-wise diffusion tensors were fitted using the dwi2tensor function. This process subsequently yielded fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps, computed using the tensor2metric function. For tractography, white matter response functions were estimated and used in constrained spherical deconvolution to compute fiber orientation distributions (FOD) (Jeurissen et al., 2014).

Tractography

For each participant, probabilistic tractography was conducted in MRtrix3 using the iFOD2 algorithm with dynamic seeding. Anatomically Constrained Tractography (ACT) was applied to improve biological accuracy. Seed and target regions were defined using the Johns Hopkins University white matter and subcortical atlases (Hua et al., 2008).

For CST reconstruction, the precentral gyrus was used as the seed region and the ipsilateral cerebral peduncle as the target. For CStrT, the precentral gyrus was used as the seed, and the ipsilateral striatum (putamen, caudate, and globus pallidus) as the target. To restrict tracking to ipsilateral pathways, the contralateral hemisphere mask was applied as an exclusion.

A total of 5000 streamlines were generated per tract, following prior methodologies in motor-system tractography (Muller et al., 2022). Streamline length was constrained to [3 mm, 200 mm] range. All other tracking parameters (e.g., step size, curvature threshold) were kept at MRtrix3 default settings to ensure reproducibility and consistency. FA and MD values were averaged over tract voxels.

Tract segmentation and along-tract parcellation

To enable along-tract analysis of diffusion metrics, binary masks of the CST and CStrT were first generated for each subject. Each tract mask was then subdivided into equidistant segments in native space using voxel coordinate-based slicing along the tract direction: the CST into 40 segments and the CStrT into 10 segments (Fig. 1D). The segmentations were visually inspected, and their orientations were adjusted to align with each tract’s spatial trajectory. Diffusion metrics were averaged within each segment to obtain segment-wise mean values along the tract. Left and right tracts were processed separately. All processing was performed using in-house Python scripts (Python v3.8) with NumPy and NiBabel. The pattern of FA along the resulting CST is similar to that of the CST in the MGH diffusion MRI tractography atlas (Maffei et al., 2021) (https://dmri.mgh.harvard.edu/tract-atlas/#lh.cst), supporting the validity, reproducibility, and reliability of our approach.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and behavioral data analysis

Data normality within each age group was assessed prior to statistical comparisons. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for datasets with sample sizes ≤50, while the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied for larger samples. Group differences in age and Edinburgh Handedness Inventory scores were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences in eTIV between groups were examined using independent samples t-tests. Gender distribution across groups was compared using the chi-square test. Group differences in motor and motor cognition measures were assessed using a general linear model, with gender included as a covariate.

Along-Tract FA and MD analysis

Group differences in FA and MD were examined for each tract segment derived from the along-tract parcellation. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), with a significance threshold set at q < 0.05, corresponding to FDR-corrected P < 0.05.

Compared with voxel-wise analyses performed in standard space, such as tract-based spatial statistics or voxel-based analysis, our segment-wise along-tract analysis was conducted in individual space. Skeletonization, template registration, and FA-based projection in voxel-wise analyses can reduce anatomical accuracy and discard the orientation information in the diffusion data, particularly in regions with crossing fiber bundles (Bach et al., 2014). In contrast, our segment-wise along-tract analysis preserves tract-specific anatomy and directional information, and thus enhances sensitivity to localized microstructural changes along the tracts.

Correlations among age, M1, white matter tract metrics, and clinical data

To determine the most appropriate model for analyzing age-related changes, an extra sum-of-squares F test was conducted to determine whether the second-order polynomial (quadratic) parameter (B2) significantly differed from the null hypothesis. A quadratic model (Y = B0 + B1 × Age+ B2 × Age2 + B3 × Gender) was selected if the p-value was <0.05, and the coefficient of determination (R2) was reported as the effect size. Otherwise, a linear model was used to assess associations between age and imaging or clinical measures, and the correlation coefficient (r) was reported as the effect size.

We performed partial correlation analyses to assess the associations between M1 metrics (volume, mean curvature, T1/T2 ratio, ATT, and CBF) and white matter tract integrity (FA and MD of CST and CStrT), controlling for gender as a covariate of no interest. P-values were adjusted using FDR correction, with the significance threshold set at FDR-corrected P < 0.05.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was conducted separately for the left and right tracts to examine whether white matter integrity (mediating variable) mediated the relationship between M1 degeneration (independent variable) and motor or motor cognition (outcome variable). Gender was included as a covariate. Statistical analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Version 2.16.3, http://www.afhayes.com/index.html). The indirect effect was estimated using a nonparametric bootstrapping procedure with 5000 resamples as recommended in previous studies (Preacher and Hayes, 2008). The standardized indirect effect (SIE) was reported as the effect size for the mediation analysis, providing a standardized estimate of the magnitude of the indirect effect (Cheung, 2009; Preacher and Kelley, 2011). The SIE ranges from −1 to +1. In line with Cohen’s guidelines, absolute SIE values of approximately 0.02, 0.13, and 0.26 were considered to represent small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively (Cohen, 1988). An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the p-value was <0.05.

Along-tract FA and MD alterations of CST and CStrT

Along-tract analyses, corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR, revealed segment-wise microstructural differences between younger (36–65 years) and older (66–90 years) adults. In the bilateral CST, older adults showed significantly lower FA in segments 1–10, located adjacent to the M1, with corresponding physical distances of 26.89 mm (right CST) and 26.81 mm (left CST) (P < 0.05, pink background in Fig. 4B, C). In contrast, segments 15–17 (8.07 mm in the right CST; 8.04 mm in the left CST) exhibited significantly higher FA in the older group (P < 0.05, green background in Fig. 4B, C). For MD, significantly higher values were observed in the older group across right CST segments 1–26, 28, and 34–37 (totaling 83.37 mm), and left CST segments 1–25 (67.02 mm) (P < 0.05, pink background in Fig. 4E, F). Conversely, segments 27–28 of the left CST (5.36 mm) showed significantly lower MD in older adults (P < 0.05, green background in Fig. 4F). In the bilateral CStrT, older adults consistently demonstrated significantly reduced FA across segments 1–6 (28.53 mm in the right CStrT; 29.47 mm in the left CStrT; pink background in Fig. 4H, I) and significantly increased MD across all segments (47.55 mm in the right CStrT; 49.12 mm in the left CStrT; pink background in Fig. 4K, L), suggesting more extensive age-related degeneration along this pathway (P < 0.05).

Relationship between metrics of white matter tracts and M1

Mean FA and MD values of tract segments exhibiting age-related degeneration, as identified through along-tract analysis, were significantly associated with both structural and hemodynamic characteristics of M1 (Table 2). FA values in the bilateral CST and CStrT were correlated with M1 vol (P < 0.001), ATT (P < 0.05), and mean curvature (P < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we used multiparametric MRI to characterize age-related structural and functional changes in the M1 and its connected CST and CStrT, and to determine how these alterations relate to motor and cognitive performance in a large right-handed adult lifespan cohort. The key findings are as follows: (1) M1 exhibited age-related reduction in volume, increased cortical curvature, and hemodynamic decline, with the T1/T2 ratio showing an initial increase followed by a subsequent decline. (2) Older adults exhibited localized microstructural degeneration in the CST adjacent to M1, whereas the CStrT showed more extensive degeneration along its trajectory. (3) The structural integrity of these white matter pathways was associated with M1 structural and hemodynamic measurements. (4) Notably, the integrity of the motor-dominant (left) CST mediated the relationship between ipsilateral M1 atrophy and motor function decline. The CStrT integrity mediated the impact of M1 atrophy on both motor and motor cognition function decline, highlighting its involvement in motor as well as cognitive decline.

Structural and vascular aging in the M1

This study revealed significant age-related alterations in M1, characterized by cortical atrophy, increased curvature, impaired hemodynamics, and a nonlinear trajectory of T1/T2 ratios—initially elevated with age, followed by a subsequent decline. These findings suggest concurrent structural remodeling and hemodynamic decline throughout the adult lifespan. Macrostructural degeneration of the motor cortex, including reductions in cortical volume and thickness, is well-documented in aging (Salat et al., 2004; Tisserand et al., 2002). Here, we observed significant M1 vol loss accompanied by increased mean curvature, which likely represents cortical thinning with sulcal widening—a hallmark of brain aging (Kochunov et al., 2005; Lin et al., 2021).

M1 receives blood supply from both the anterior and middle cerebral arteries (Ugur et al., 2005). Our findings of decreased CBF and prolonged ATT indicate compromised vascular function, potentially due to increased vessel tortuosity, microvascular rarefaction, and arteriole stiffening and narrowing (Sun et al., 2024). These changes reflect diminished perfusion efficiency, which may exacerbate cortical degeneration with age.

Although the T1/T2 ratio is often considered a surrogate marker of cortical myelin content (Margoni et al., 2022; Glasser and Van Essen, 2011), its interpretation in aging brains, particularly in iron-rich regions like the M1, requires careful consideration of both iron and myelin dynamics (Kakeda and al., 2008). The superparamagnetic properties of iron reduce T2 relaxation times and may slightly affect T1 values (Fig. 2A). We observed an inverted U-shaped trajectory of the T1/T2 ratio with age in bilateral M1—characterized by an initial increase followed by a decline in older age. This nonlinear pattern likely reflects a dynamic interplay between iron accumulation and myelin integrity across the adult lifespan. In early to mid-adulthood, progressive iron deposition may dominate and contribute to the elevation of the T1/T2 ratio, even if myelin content remains stable or decreases modestly. However, in later life, neurodegenerative processes such as demyelination or axonal loss may begin to outweigh iron-related effects, leading to a decline in the T1/T2 ratio despite persistent or even increasing iron levels. This is supported by studies showing that iron accumulation can confound MRI-based myelin estimates, as increased iron may mimic high myelin content in imaging signals (Birkl et al., 2019). Importantly, iron and myelin are biologically interconnected. Oligodendrocytes rely on iron for myelin synthesis, suggesting that moderate iron levels support healthy white matter maintenance (Khattar et al., 2021). However, an overload of iron, particularly when unbound to storage proteins, may trigger oxidative stress and accelerate myelin breakdown, thereby fostering a self-perpetuating cycle of neurodegeneration (Ward et al., 2014). These age-related changes in the T1/T2 ratio in M1 may indicate a complex, nonlinear balance between iron accumulation and myelin degeneration.

Together, these structural and hemodynamic changes suggest that M1 is vulnerable to aging, potentially underlying motor function decline in older adults.

Age-Related microstructural degeneration of the CST and CStrT

Significant age-related degeneration was observed in both the CST and CStrT, as evidenced by decreased FA and increased MD. These changes likely reflect underlying demyelination and axonal loss associated with normal aging. Notably, FA reductions were most prominent in CST segments adjacent to the M1, consistent with prior evidence that subcortical white matter is more vulnerable to age-related decline than deeper regions (Sullivan et al., 2010). These superficial segments contain abundant dendrites and short-range U-fibers (Xie et al., 2016), which play a critical role in local cortical networks. Our findings suggest that they may be particularly susceptible to age-related microstructural degeneration. Previous studies have linked CST degeneration in aging to ischemic insults, including small infarcts and cortical microinfarcts, which are often localized to juxtacortical regions and may contribute to demyelination and motor function decline (Kubota et al., 2019; Ferro et al., 2020). Conversely, some CST segments near the brain’s center exhibited elevated FA and lower MD in older adults, potentially reflecting region-specific compensatory mechanisms such as remyelination in response to chronic degeneration. This may be supported by residual oligodendrocyte precursor cells and influenced by altered neural activity (Barres and Raff, 1993; Young et al., 2013). Histopathological studies also support that periventricular white matter exhibits greater microglial activation and proliferation than deep subcortical regions, creating a more permissive environment for repair (J.E. Simpson et al., 2007; J.E. Simpson et al., 2007).

Similarly, the CStrT, which also lies near the cortex, showed bilateral FA decreases and MD increases, supporting the notion that cortical-adjacent tracts are particularly susceptible to neurodegenerative processes in aging. Notably, the CST and CStrT are not entirely independent, as both originate from M1, with the CStrT representing a proximal branch of the CST (McColgan et al., 2020). Overlap analysis performed in individual diffusion space revealed that the two tracts share a moderate portion of their proximal segments near M1. Specifically, the overlap ratio ranged from 35 to 45 % for each tract, and the Dice coefficient was approximately 38 % bilaterally (for details, see Supplementary Material S1). These findings indicate partial convergence near M1, while more distal portions of each tract remain largely distinct. The shared proximal region likely contributes to the similar age-related microstructural changes observed in both tracts near M1. Crucially, we found that microstructural integrity of both CST and CStrT was significantly associated with morphological and functional properties of M1, including cortical volume, curvature, T1/T2 ratio, and hemodynamics. These associations suggest a coupled degeneration pattern, in which white matter and cortical gray matter decline in a negative synergy.

The divergent spatial patterns of FA and MD—localized FA versus widespread MD increases, highlight their differential sensitivity to specific microstructural alterations. While FA reductions likely reflect focal demyelination and axonal degradation, MD increases may signify broader changes in extracellular space and cellular density (Pierpaoli et al., 1996). This spatial dissociation underscored the complementary value of FA and MD in capturing white matter degeneration associated with aging.

Handedness effect on the CST degeneration

Along-tract analyses revealed subtle hemispheric differences in age-related CST degeneration among right-handed adults. Specifically, the right CST exhibited a slightly broader spatial extent of MD elevation in older adults compared to the left, whereas the left CST showed localized MD reductions in a few segments near the brain’s midline. This pattern likely reflects functional dominance and usage-dependent microstructural plasticity. Developmental studies have shown higher FA in the left CST of right-handed adolescents, suggesting microstructural lateralization (Angstmann et al., 2016). This asymmetry likely results from experience-dependent maturation, as the left CST supports more frequent and fine motor activity of the dominant hand (Fuelscher et al., 2021). Such specialization enhances myelination and fiber organization, contributing to greater microstructural integrity early in life. Consequently, in adulthood, the dominant hemisphere (typically the left) may better preserve myelin integrity and axonal structure, conferring relative resilience to age-related decline. Conversely, the non-dominant right CST, subjected to less frequent activation, may be more susceptible to diffuse microstructural deterioration with aging. These findings suggest that handedness exerts a modulatory effect on white matter aging, warranting consideration in future studies of bilateral motor pathways.

CST and CStrT mediate M1 atrophy–related motor and cognitive decline

Our findings revealed that the integrity of the motor dominant (left) CST specifically mediated the relationship between ipsilateral M1 atrophy and motor decline, consistent with its primary role in motor control. In the meanwhile, the CStrT mediated the effects of M1 atrophy on both motor and executive function decline, suggesting its integrative contribution to both motor and cognitive functions.

These distinct yet interconnected roles may be rooted in functional evolution and organization of the motor cortex. In the M1, pyramidal neurons project directly to the CST, while also sending collaterals to the ipsilateral striatum, giving rise to the CStrT (Fig. 1B) (McColgan et al., 2020). This anatomical branching enables simultaneous engagement of brainstem/spinal outputs for motor function and basal ganglia circuits for higher-order motor planning and cognitive regulation. The dual projections from M1 thus provide a structural substrate for coordinated motor and cognitive control. Neurobiological evidence further supports this model. Age-related reductions in striatal D2-like dopamine receptor availability have been associated with decline in both motor performance and executive functions (Volkow et al., 1998; Bäckman et al., 2000). Degeneration of corticostriatal pathways may disrupt the integration of motor execution with cognitive control, contributing to functional decline in both domains. Motor and cognitive decline frequently co-occur in aging and are increasingly understood as mechanistically intertwined (Hill and Ekstrom, 2025). The concept of motoric cognitive risk syndrome—defined by the co-occurrence of gait slowing and cognitive complaints—has been shown to predict dementia risk more strongly than either condition alone (Collyer et al., 2022). Moreover, slower gait in cognitively unimpaired older adults has been linked to increased β-amyloid burden (Nadkarni et al., 2017), reinforcing the notion of shared neuropathological pathways of motor and cognition dysfunction.

Together, these findings support a model in which M1 atrophy contributes to age-related motor and cognitive decline via separable but interconnected white matter pathways. While the CST primarily supports motor function, the CStrT links M1 to the striatum, facilitating motor-cognitive integration. Degeneration of both tracts disrupts distinct aspects of behavior but may share a common cortical origin. These results highlight the importance of targeting shared neural pathways in aging research and interventions aimed at preserving both motor and motor cognition function.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this multiparametric, lifespan-spanning study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to infer causal relationships or temporal dynamics of age-related degeneration. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm the progressive trajectory of structural and functional decline in the M1 and its connected white matter tracts. Second, although ASL imaging with 2.5 mm isotropic resolution is an improvement over conventional 4 mm protocols, partial volume effects may still impact the accuracy of CBF and ATT quantification, particularly in regions with cortical atrophy. While partial volume correction was applied using the BASIL toolkit, future work should consider advanced acquisitions or reconstruction methods to further mitigate the partial volume effects and enhance perfusion estimates. Third, due to the fan-shaped dispersion of the tracts near cortical terminations, our method did not subdivide segments along the curved surface in this region, which may introduce minor spatial averaging. Nevertheless, the pattern of FA along the resulting CST closely resembled that of the canonical CST in the MGH diffusion MRI tractography atlas, supporting the validity and reproducibility of our approach. Fourth, data on physical activity and cardiovascular indicators (e.g., blood pressure, glycemic status, fitness) were lacking, which may influence M1 hemodynamics, tract microstructure, and confound the observed associations. However, as our cohort consisted of healthy adults, these measures are expected to be within normal ranges. Future studies should assess and control for these factors. Finally, although we controlled for striatal volume when examining the effects of the CStrT on motor and cognitive functions—and the results remained largely unchanged—the striatum, as a key subcortical hub involved in both motor and cognitive control, may exert broader influence. Given the current study’s focus on the motor cortex and its connected pathways, the functional role of the striatum warrants more comprehensive investigation in future research.

Neuroscience perspectives, clinical implications and future directions

Our results highlight the critical role of the M1 and its dual projection pathways—the CST and CStrT—in age-related motor and cognitive decline. Neuroscientific evidence reveals that M1 pyramidal neurons not only drive voluntary movement via the CST but also send collaterals to the striatum through the CStrT, integrating motor execution with basal ganglia circuits involved in motor planning and executive control (McColgan et al., 2020).

The observed microstructural degeneration of the CStrT alongside CST suggests that aging disrupts both direct motor outputs and motor-cognitive integration pathways. Such disruption may underlie the common co-occurrence of motor slowing and executive function decline in older adults, consistent with the motoric cognitive risk syndrome. Additionally, iron accumulation and reduced vascular function within M1 may exacerbate microcircuit vulnerability by promoting oxidative stress and myelin breakdown.

Clinically, these findings advocate for assessing both CST and CStrT integrity when evaluating motor and cognitive aging, as targeting corticostriatal circuits might improve interventions aimed at preserving mobility and executive function. Future longitudinal studies with high-resolution multimodal imaging are essential to elucidate the temporal dynamics of M1 microcircuit and pathway degeneration, and to identify biomarkers predictive of motor-cognitive decline. Comparative analyses across cortical hubs will further clarify whether these mechanisms reflect unique vulnerabilities of M1 or broader cortical aging patterns.

Conclusion

Our study revealed age-related degeneration in the M1 and its two major output pathways using multiparametric neuroimaging in a large adult lifespan sample. Structural and hemodynamic degeneration in M1 were accompanied by microstructural declines in both CST and CStrT, with CStrT showing more widespread vulnerability. Notably, CStrT integrity mediated the relationship between M1 atrophy and declines in both motor and executive functions, highlighting its pivotal role in motor-cognitive aging. These findings offer normative imaging markers of healthy aging and may support early detection of movement and cognitive disorders in the elderly.
