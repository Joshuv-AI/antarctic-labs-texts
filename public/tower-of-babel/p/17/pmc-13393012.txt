Frailty status, clinical and functional outcomes in cardiac amyloidosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Haowen Jiang, Daniel J. Lim, Chun Yuan Khoo, Sarah A.M. Cuddy, Raymond Wong, Ru-San Tan, Fei Gao, Yucheng Chen, Sharmila Dorbala, Angela S. Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn parallel with diagnostic advances and awareness of cardiac amyloidosis (CA) in aging populations, frailty is increasingly observed in CA. However, the relationship between the frailty and CA remains poorly characterized owing to limited studies that have defined frailty a priori. We performed a meta-analysis to characterize the impact of frailty on clinical and functional patient outcomes in CA.MethodsSystematic review of four electronic databases was performed for all studies reporting frailty prevalence, functional and clinical endpoints in patients with CA from January 2015 to April 2026 (CRD420251152212). Frailty was classified based on individual study scoring systems. The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality, expressed as pooled hazard (HR) or risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using inverse-variance random-effects models. Other outcomes included hospitalizations, quality of life, functional status (6-min walk distance [6MWD]), and prescription of disease modifying treatment. Heterogeneity was summarized using the I2 statistic; study quality was rated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale; and certainty of evidence was graded using the GRADE framework. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and visual funnel plots were performed where applicable.FindingsFifteen studies with a total of 5048 patients, predominantly (>95%) with transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM), were included. The prevalence of frailty ranged between 6.7% and 75% across studies with a pooled mean of 33.9% (826/2435 frail). Pooling 2141 patients with outcomes available, both pre-frailty (RR 2.22, 95% CI 1.37–3.60, p < 0.01) and frailty (RR 3.93, 95% CI 2.09–7.40, p < 0.01) significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality after adjustment for age and frailty assessment tool. 6MWD, a functional surrogate of frailty, predicted poorer outcomes both using baseline 6MWD (<350/300 m: HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.15–4.31, p = 0.02) and 1-year interval change in 6MWD (>35 reduction: HR 1.80, 95% CI 1.52–2.14; >5% reduction: HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.60–2.23; both p < 0.01). Beyond mortality, frailty also predicted increased risk of functional decline, poorer quality of life, and reduced prescription of disease-modifying treatment.InterpretationPatients with ATTR-CM and frailty have higher mortality risk, poorer functional outcomes and reduced prescription of disease-modifying treatment compared to those without frailty. Findings were consistent across frailty instruments and sensitivity analyses, supporting the incorporation of routine frailty assessment and 6MWD into clinical management of ATTR amyloidosis.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

A search was done on four electronic databases (Pubmed, EMBASE, SCOPUS, CINAHL) for relevant studies related to the incidence and prognostic significance of frailty in cardiac amyloidosis. The incidence of CA, especially ATTR-CM, has been increasing over the past two decades due to increased disease awareness and advancements in cardiac imaging. At the same time, an increasing proportion of patients with CA were found to have concomitant frailty, a marker of reduced physiological reserve. However, the data on the prevalence and prognostic significance of frailty in patients with CA was heterogenous and hence there is a need to aggregate the available evidence to characterize the impact of frailty on clinical and functional outcomes in patients with CA.

Added value of this study

By pooling together data from 5048 patients across 15 studies, an estimated 1 in 3 patients with CA were estimated to have concomitant frailty, as was as high as 75% in some studies. Both pre-frailty and frailty were found to have prognostic significance in predicting all-cause mortality in patients with cardiac amyloidosis. Both baseline and interval changes in 6MWD, a functional surrogate of frailty, was also found to be prognostically significant. Beyond mortality, frailty also predicted increased risk of functional decline, poorer quality of life, and reduced prescription of disease-modifying treatment. Emerging longitudinal data suggest that frailty status may fluctuate over the natural course of ATTR-CM, supporting serial rather than one-time assessment.

Implications of all the available evidence

Frailty should be routinely assessed in all patients with ATTR-CM as it is associated with increased mortality, poorer functional outcomes and reduced prescription of disease-modifying treatment compared to those without frailty. Based on the available data, we suggest standardized frailty testing with Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and 6MWD, integrated alongside National Amyloidosis Centre staging in the contemporary care pathway of ATTR-CM.

Introduction

Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) is caused by deposits of misfolded proteins in the myocardium, leading to a restrictive cardiomyopathy and significant morbidity and mortality.1 The pathological amyloid protein is either produced by clonally proliferating plasma cells (light-chain amyloidosis [AL]) or by the liver in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM). The prevalence and incidence of CA, especially ATTR-CM, have burgeoned in the past two decades due to increased disease awareness and use of diagnostic imaging such as bone scintigraphy and cardiovascular magnetic resonance.2, 3, 4, 5 CA is associated with high morbidity and mortality, with reported hospitalization rates of 61–78% at one year and median survival at diagnosis of 3–5 years.2,6 Both AL amyloidosis and ATTR amyloidosis have a predilection to affect older adults,1 especially the latter, which rises steeply with age.7 ATTR-CM is present in approximately 25% of autopsies of adults ≥80 years old,8 and 11–13% of elderly adults with HFpEF9,10 or severe aortic stenosis11 on 99mTc-scintigraphy. ATTR-CM is often diagnosed late7—mean delays of >4 and >5 years for wild-type (ATTRwt) and hereditary forms (ATTRv),12 respectively—with over a third of cases presenting with advanced symptoms and end-organ damage.7 Whereas the diagnosis of CA had previously connoted imminent death, recent advances in therapeutic management of AL-CA13 and ATTR-CM14 have extended survival significantly, underscoring the need for early detection and disease-specific intervention.

Frailty—a marker of physical decline characterized by inability to maintain homeostasis and adapt to stressors—captures systemic vulnerability1 not reflected by cardiac tests alone. It often co-exists with, and prognosticates poorer outcomes, in diverse cardiovascular diseases.15,16 Patients with CA can develop frailty over the course of the disease due to aging or CA and its associated comorbidities.17 Frailty assessment plays a role in defining disease severity and serves as a touchpoint for rationalizing intervention.1,14 Despite its clinical importance, the prevalence and effect of frailty in patients with CA have not been well characterized and are limited to a few observational studies.18, 19, 20 With emerging promising treatment options for CA that can improve function and prolong survival,14 the potential for frailty reversal1 obligates early recognition of frailty, in order to avail patients opportunities for multidisciplinary interventions targeted against frailty.21 Therefore, it is important to understand the effects of frailty on outcomes in patients with CA.21 Frailty assessment not only stratifies risks but also identifies modifiable vulnerabilities, both of which can assist in guiding disease-as well as frailty-targeted interventions.

In this study, we performed a meta-analysis to characterize the impact of frailty on clinical and functional outcomes in patients with CA.

Methods

This review has been registered at International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/) with the registration No. CRD420251152212. This study was conducted in line with recommendations from Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.22

Search strategy and selection criteria

Two investigators (H.J. and D.J.L.) independently performed a literature search in four electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, SCOPUS and the Cochrane Library). An initial search was first performed in October 2025, with an updated search in April 2026 for eligible studies published between January 2015 and the date of last search. The start date of January 2015 was chosen because it approximates the period in which non-invasive bone-scintigraphy diagnosis of ATTR-CM23 and the first disease-modifying therapies entered routine clinical practice,24 literature before that date would under-represent contemporary diagnostic and treatment pathways and introduce era-related heterogeneity. Only English-language articles were considered; the use of a translation tool was not used for non-English language articles. The detailed search strategy for all four databases can be found in Supplementary Table S1. Both ATTR-CM and AL-CM were considered for inclusion. Frailty must have been pre-defined with validated tools, including Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), Fried frailty phenotype (FFP), or other toolsets that quantify an individual's frailty status. Additionally, studies utilizing measures that served as physical or functional surrogates for frailty (e.g., hand grip strength, gait speed, 6-min walk distance [6MWD]) were included as part of an extended analysis. Studies examining age alone were excluded. All study types, including research letters, were considered if of sufficient quality and detail. Conference abstracts were excluded. Title and abstract screening and full-text review were performed independently by the two investigators (H.J, D.J.L); discrepancies at any stage were adjudicated by a third senior investigator (A.S.K.). In addition, relevant citations in the articles were manually identified and reviewed for inclusion in the analysis.

Data collection and assessment of quality

Two investigators (H.J. & D.J.L.) independently collected data pertaining to study characteristics, patient population and outcome measures with cross-verification of data collected. The same 2 investigators also independently performed quality assessments for all papers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Form25 for cohort studies and modified version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment form for cross-sectional studies (Supplementary Table S2) based on previous systematic reviews of cross sectional studies.26 In cases where no agreement could be reached, the final decision was made by a third senior investigator (A.S.K.).

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were (1) frailty prevalence across toolsets; and (2) all-cause mortality stratified by frailty subgroups as defined by individual studies. If a fixed definition was not provided within the study, we classified patients into “Robust” vs “Frail” if there were 2 subgroups; “Robust” vs “Pre-frail” vs “Frail”, if 3 subgroups; and “Robust” vs “Pre-frail” vs “Frail” vs “Very Frail”, if 4 subgroups. For studies which categorized frailty using CFS, categories of frailty were most commonly defined by the following ranges; 1–3 (not frail), 4–5 or 4–6 (pre-frail), 6–9 or 7–9 (frail), and 8–9 (very frail). For studies that used more than one frailty assessment method, CFS was prioritized for the purposes of primary assessment of outcomes. Secondary outcomes included changes in functional status (6MWD, VO2max), quality of life indices, and prescription of disease-modifying therapy.

Statistical analysis

Prevalence rates of frailty were stratified by frailty toolset and qualitatively examined via bubble plots. Hazard ratios (HR) were pooled with their 95% CI for clinical outcomes via an inverse variance random-effects model, where available. Where HR was not reported, event rates from relevant studies were pooled with HR and displayed as risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using an inverse variance random-effects model. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q-test27 and I2 statistic.28 We defined levels of heterogeneity as mild, moderate, substantial and considerable when I2 values were in ranges of 0–29%, 30–49%, 50–74% and 75–100%, respectively, which have been modifed from Deek et al.28 We modeled a dose-response relationship using a two-stage random-effects meta-regression with restricted cubic splines, anchoring at CFS score of 2 (midpoint from 1 to 3) Study-reported RRs were assigned midpoints of CFS 4.5 and 65 based on groupings and compared to a common reference of CFS 1–3. The resulting spline curve with 95% CIs captured the continuous potentially nonlinear association between frailty severity and mortality risk.

To address the heterogeneity observed across studies, we performed the following pre-specified subgroup analyses: (i) restricted to CFS-only studies; (ii) leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for the all-cause mortality pool; (iii) excluding studies rated as satisfactory quality on the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Because fewer than ten studies contributed to the mortality pool, formal tests for publication bias (Egger, Begg) were not performed in line with Cochrane Handbook recommendations; however, we present a visual funnel plot.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) and Python (version 3.11). All p values were from 2-sided tests, and the results were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05, unless stated otherwise.

Role of funding source

The funding source is used for payment of any publication fee(s) and had no influence on the methodology or results of the present study.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 2237 studies screened, 16 studies comprising a total of 5565 patients were included. These consisted of 10 studies with 2435 CA patients in the primary analysis on 1) prevalence and 2) all-cause mortality based on objective frailty scales; and an additional 6 studies with 3130 CA patients included as part of an extended analysis based on physical or functional surrogates for frailty. Prevalence of frailty

For the primary analysis, there were 826/2435 frail patients (33.9%). The reported prevalence of frailty ranged from 6.7%40 to 75%31 across studies, which had used different assessment tools and cut-offs. There was no correlation between frailty assessment tool and frailty prevalence on visual assessment of bubble plots (Supplementary Figure S1).

For studies that had used CFS, scores variably stratified into ranges of 1–3, 4–5 or 4–6, and 6/7 or more were most commonly used to categorize frailty status18,19,36,40; in one study, CFS score of 8–9 was used to define the very frail.18 One study categorized patients into CFS of 1–3 vs 4–9.37 Among 1311 patients across 4 studies,18,19,36,40 the prevalence of patients with CFS scores between 4–5/6 and > 6/7 were 39.7% (n = 521) and 16.2% (n = 212), respectively. Among 360 patients across 3 studies19,20,30 that used FFP, the prevalence of pre-frailty and frailty were 38.3% (n = 138) and 37.2% (n = 134), respectively. Among studies not using the above assessment tools, frailty prevalence of 14.5%, 75% and 52.4% were observed using cumulative deficit model,29 G8 score,31 and ECOG-PS,41 respectively.

All-cause mortality

Among the 10 studies in the primary analysis, 6 studies18,19,36,37,40,41 with a total of 2141 patients reported on outcomes of all-cause mortality. Pooling univariable outcomes, both pre-frailty (RR 2.22, 95% CI 1.37–3.60, p < 0.01; I2 = 49.9%) and frailty (RR 3.93, 95% CI 2.09–7.40, p < 0.01; I2 = 82.7%) were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality compared with non-frail status (Fig. 2). Pooling 2 studies18,37 with 1019 patients, frailty remains a significant predictor of all-cause mortality (RR 5.60, 95% CI 2.09–15.02, p < 0.01, I2 = 42.4%) after adjustment for age, gender, NAC stage, and use of disease-modifying treatment (Supplementary Figure S2). Including only studies that used CFS as frailty classification,18,19,36,40 similar results were obtained, with both pre-frailty (RR 2.22, 95% CI 1.37–3.60, p < 0.01; I2 = 49.9%) and frailty (RR 4.06, 95% CI 2.08–7.94, p < 0.01; I2 = 76.3%) remaining associated with significantly increased mortality risks (Supplementary Figure S3). Visual assessment of predicted mortality risk by frailty toolset used showed no obvious correlation even after factoring in use of disease-modifying treatment (Fig. 3). The pooled RR remained within the range 2.49–3.62 (all p < 0.01) when any single study was omitted, confirming robustness (all p < 0.01; Supplementary Figure S4). Sensitivity analysis excluding the two satisfactory-quality studies yielded a consistent pooled RR of 3.88 (95% CI 1.95–7.71). A dose–response meta-regression treating frailty as a continuous variable (using CFS) demonstrated a monotonic trend of increasing mortality risk with higher frailty scores. On linear regression, the pooled analysis estimated an 18% increase in mortality per 1-point increase in CFS (RR 1.18 per point, 95% CI 0.80–1.73; p = 0.44), which did not achieve significance due to wide confidence intervals and inter-study heterogeneity. A spline curve of this relationship demonstrated a steadily rising risk across the frailty spectrum without a clear threshold effect (Supplementary Figure S5).

Effect of frailty on other outcomes

Only one study evaluated other clinical endpoints, which included cardiovascular mortality and cardiovascular hospitalization.37 CFS score ≥4 was associated with significantly increased risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR 10.63, 95% CI 2.71–41.66, p = 0.001) but not cardiovascular hospitalizations (HR 1.57, 95% CI 0.81–3.04, p = 0.184). Adjusted for significant clinical co-variates, CFS score ≥4 remained significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality (HR 8.87, 95% CI 2.67–29.42, p < 0.001).

Other outcomes evaluated included quality of life using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ)29; functional reserve, 6MWD; and VO2 max assessed using cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET),31 and prescription of disease-modifying agents.19,35 Increase in frailty with a cumulative deficit model was significantly associated with worsening KCCQ-assessed quality of life (β −60.40, p < 0.001).29 Frail status stratified defined by G8 score ≤14 was found to be associated neither with 6MWD (frail: 283 ± 101 m, non-frail: 305 ± 187 m; p = 0.615) nor VO2max (Frail: 13 ± 3 ml/kg/min, non-frail: 16 ± 2 ml/kg/min; p = 0.107).31 Frail status was significantly associated with reduced prescription of disease-modifying agents, whether defined by FFP (OR 0.182, 95% CI 0.047–0.881, p = 0.033), CFS score 6–9 (OR 0.208, 95% CI 0.081–0.603, p = 0.004), or ≥4 cumulative deficits (OR 0.219, 95% CI 0.064–0.781, p = 0.019).19 In a multicentre cohort study, the most common reason for not prescribing tafamidis (47%) and for stopping tafamidis in patients already on it (61%) was due to perceived frailty, poor mobility or a poor 6MWD.35

Extended analysis of other parameters of frailty

Among 5 other studies that investigated physical or functional surrogates for frailty, 4 studies used functional measures (6MWD33,34,39 and gait parameters32 in 3 and 1 studies, respectively); and 1 study, chest computed tomography–derived skeletal muscle index (SMI) and adiposity.38

Three studies with a pooled total of 2425 patients33,34,39 examined the effects of 6MWD, a marker of physical frailty, on outcomes in patients with CA. At baseline, 6MWD below cutoff value (<350 m33,34 or <300 m39 depending on study) was significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.15–4.31, p = 0.02) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 57.2%, p = 0.10). Pooling 2 studies with 1-year follow-up data on 1308 patients,33,34 both absolute reductions in 6MWD of >35 m (HR 1.80, 95% CI 1.52–2.14, p < 0.01, I2 = 0%) and relative reductions of >5% (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.60–2.23, p < 0.01, I2 = 0%) were associated with significantly increased mortality (Fig. 4). Publication bias

A funnel plot was performed for studies with data on mortality outcomes (n = 6). Visual analysis of the forest plot suggests possible publication bias right rightward-leaning pattern, suggesting a possible overestimation of the true effect. Nonetheless, this is limited by the small number of studies a lack of formal Egger/Begg tests given k < 10 (Supplementary Figure S6).

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 15 studies encompassing 5048 CA patients, including 2435 with objective frailty assessment, frailty emerged as a prevalent and prognostically important feature of ATTR-CM. Approximately one-third of patients were frail. A clear gradient of risk was observed: pre-frail and frail patients had 2.22-fold (95% CI 1.37–3.60) and 3.93-fold (95% CI 2.09–7.40) higher all-cause mortality than non-frail patients, while those with severe frailty (CFS≥8) had over eightfold higher mortality (RR 8.04, 95% CI 3.01–21.48) (Fig. 5). This graded relationship persisted after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and cardiac biomarkers, confirming frailty as an independent determinant of outcomes over chronological aging in patients with ATTR-CM. Consistent with this continuum of risk, a dose-response analysis modeling frailty on a continuous scale showed a monotonic increase in mortality risk with rising frailty severity. However, the pooled linear slope (RR 1.18 per 1-point increment in CFS) was not statistically significant (95% CI 0.80–1.73; p = 0.44), likely due to coarse frailty categories and inter-study heterogeneity. Our work provides novel data that distinguishes from prior work in this area. A recent meta-analysis by Finazzi et al. (2026) pooled a smaller group of six studies with 1422 patients and used CFS as the sole frailty metric.42 Across 15 studies, we studied a larger sample of 5048 patients, and pooled prevalence across five validated instruments. Furthermore, the present study pooled mortality and functional outcomes, with an extended analysis of the impact of physical and functional surrogates as well (6MWD, gait, SMI), which were not quantitatively synthesized in Finazzi.

Compared to other chronic cardiovascular diseases, a substantially higher proportion of patients with ATTR-CM are classified as frail. Post-hoc analysis of large heart failure trials including DELIVER,43 DAPA-HF,44 and PARAGON45 suggest a proportion of frail patients ranging from 11.7 to 23.8%. In patients with chronic coronary syndromes, post-hoc analysis of the CANTOS46 and EXTEND-DAPT47 trials suggest a frailty prevalence of 12–13.3%. In a pooled analysis of 2 trials and one prospective registry, patients with aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter or surgical aortic valve interventions had a pooled frailty prevalence of 6.1%.48 Comparatively, a significantly higher proportion of patients with AT`TR-CM in the present are classified as frail at 33.8%, further emphasizing the importance of routine and deliberate frailty assessment.

Prior cohort studies report hospitalization rates of 61–78% at one year and median survival at diagnosis of 3–5 years in patients with ATTR-CM.2,5 These real-world rates are consistent with what is observed in the present study and substantially higher than what is reported in contemporary trials: in the placebo arm of ATTRibute-CM, a composite of all-cause mortality or first cardiovascular-related hospitalization occurred in 50.5% of participants over 30 months49; in the placebo arm of HELIOS-B, approximately 37.5% of patients experienced ≥1 cardiovascular event and 49.1% experienced outpatient worsening heart failure over 36 months.50 Whilst these trials did not formally assess frailty, the mean age of participants in both trials (77 years) is substantially lower than the mean age of patients classified as frail in the present study (83–86 years).19,31,36 The lower hospitalization and mortality rates reported in contemporary trials likely reflect stricter trial eligibility and potentially underrepresenting older, more frail patients.

Emerging data reinforce that frailty in ATTR-CM reflects systemic physiological vulnerability rather than isolated cardiac dysfunction and may be a useful prognostic marker independent of existing staging systems.19 Although only observational data was available, the prognostic signal of frailty remained consistent across studies with a moderate GRADE certainty contributed by consistent directionality and large magnitude of effect sizes, as well as across various instruments, supporting the robustness of frailty as a prognostic construct in ATTR-CA independent of the tool used. Among these, CFS remains the most practical and discriminative, with moderate-to-severe frailty (CFS ≥6) conferring over fourfold increased mortality risk (RR 4.06, 95% CI 2.08–7.94). Standardizing frailty assessment using established tools such as the CFS could facilitate its integration into prognostic models and care pathways, in line with contemporary expert consensus advocating routine frailty screening in CA management.1,21

Objective physical performance markers, including gait speed and 6MWD, consistently forecast worse outcomes.32, 33, 34 In our study, 6MWD demonstrates potential as an adjunct to frailty with both baseline 6MWD <300/350 m (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.15–4.31) as well as interval reductions in distance at 1-year follow-up (>35 m reduction: HR 1.80, 95% CI 1.52–2.14; >5% reduction: HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.60–2.23) prognosticating poorer outcomes. Decline in VO2max and ventilatory efficiency (as assessed by ratio of minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production) over serial testing further mirrors progressive physiological deterioration with increases over the natural course of ATTR-CM (ΔVE/VCO2 slope 8.6, 95% CI 6–11; p < 0.01).51 Separately, imaging-derived measures of body composition have demonstrated that lower skeletal muscle and fat mass predict mortality up to 10 years independent of cardiac indices in CA (Low SMI: HR 3.72, 95% CI 1.74–7.97, p = 0.001),38 which underscore the importance of adequate nutrition. Indeed, there is cross-talk between the latter and frailty. In a recent study, frail patients exhibited higher rates of malnutrition and elevated cardiac biomarkers, embodying an intertwined cardio-metabolic phenotype.52 Collectively, these observations underscore frailty as a multisystem construct that incorporates functional and metabolic dimensions like sarcopenia, reduced exercise tolerance, and diminished reserve capacity beyond traditional cardiac biomarkers in ATTR-CM, and support the utility of objective frailty assessment in risk stratification of ATTR-CM patients.

Routine frailty assessment could be incorporated into clinical care for patients with ATTR-CM, as a prognostic marker complementary to NAC staging. CFS could be used as an initial rapid screening tool to stratify patients into non-frail (CFS 1–3), pre-frail (4–5) and frail (>5) categories. Patients in the very frail category (CFS >7) are at the highest risk spectrum.18 Concurrently, for the quantification of physical reserve, 6MWD at baseline and follow-up are useful as changes in 6MWD has been shown to prognosticate outcomes. This forms a practical strategy for routine practice.

Frailty also influences therapeutic decision-making. Frail patients are substantially less likely to receive tafamidis or disease-modifying therapy—approximately 80% lower odds even after adjustment for clinical status18,19—despite real-world evidence suggesting that functionally impaired older patients might still derive symptomatic benefit.15,53 Recent cohort data on tafamidis in octogenarians53 and the open-label extension of ATTRibute-CM54 reinforce that benefit extends into older, more-vulnerable groups. This underscores the need for individualized, rather than exclusionary, treatment decisions in the presence of frailty. This also emphasizes the need for objective assessments of frailty rather than on perceived subjective frailty. Future work should test whether targeted interventions, such as structured exercise, nutritional optimization, or anti-sarcopenic therapies, can alter the frailty trajectory and improve survival or quality of life outcomes.

Taken together, our study findings provide support for the centrality of frailty as a multidimensional determinant of outcomes in ATTR-CM, capturing systemic vulnerability and diminished resilience not reflected by conventional biomarkers.

Our study has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity arose from the diverse frailty assessment methods in the analyzed studies. At least five instruments were used; with the widely applied CFS, cut-offs for “frail” vs “non-frail” varied, likely contributing to the statistical heterogeneity observed (I2∼50–70%). The varied frailty assessment tools used and small number of studies assessing each tool made it difficult to perform sub-analysis stratified by frailty score. Future studies should look to assess a variety of frailty assessment tools where possible. Second, the analysis included relatively few studies, mostly observational cohorts with inherent selection biases from referral populations and residual confounding; only 6 studies reported mortality. We did not formally assess publication bias as there were less than 10 studies in the primary analysis; selective reporting remains possible. Third, the small number of AL amyloidosis patients (58 patients across a single study) precluded definitive analysis; as such, our primary findings pertain specifically to ATTR amyloidosis (ATTR-CM) and should not be extrapolated to AL amyloidosis. While AL also predominantly affects older adults, the patients are typically younger than ATTR-CM. We believe that the associated oncological treatment toxicity and multiple comorbidities in AL also warrants frailty assessment equally, if not more, relevant. This is an area for future investigation. Fourth, data on outcomes other than mortality were sparse and heterogeneous, which precluded pooled analyses of functional capacity, hospitalization, or quality of life. Subgroup analysis by detailed ATTR genotype (ATTRwt vs ATTRv, and by ATTRv variant) was also not feasible as studies did not distinguish outcomes based on the two groups. Fifth, the included cohorts were male-predominant (87.3%), consistent with the known epidemiology of ATTR-CM but limiting generalizability to women; female-specific effects of frailty on outcomes therefore remain under-characterized. Sixth, the year of diagnosis and time of assessment are well known to affect prognosis in patients with cardiac amyloidosis given developments in contemporary treatment. Whilst we have tried to address this by limiting the inclusion to the past decade, further subgroup analysis based on year of publication and time of clinical assessment was not feasible due to the small number of studies. Finally, frailty was assessed cross-sectionally in most studies, limiting analyses of temporal changes and the effects of interventions on frailty status.

Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis contains the largest multinational cohort to date of ATTR-CM patients with frailty evaluation, comprising more than 5000 individuals including over 2400 with frailty assessment. Subgroup analyses across frailty definitions demonstrated consistent findings across the analyzed cohorts. These attributes lend robustness to the conclusion that frailty is a major and independent prognostic factor in ATTR-CM.

Patients with ATTR-CM and frailty have higher burdens of mortality, cardiovascular hospitalizations, poorer functional outcomes and reduced prescription of disease-modifying treatment compared to those without frailty. The prognostic signal was consistent across observational studies included (moderate GRADE certainty). Standardized frailty assessment (CFS ± 6MWD), integrated with NAC staging, should be considered in the contemporary care pathway of ATTR-CM.
