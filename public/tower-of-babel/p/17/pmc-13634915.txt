Integrated continuous berth allocation with time-invariant specific quay crane assignment using a mixed-integer model and greedy genetic algorithm
Authors: Yan Zhou, Xu Cheng, Yu Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient coordination of berth and quay crane resources is essential for improving the operational performance of container terminals under increasing vessel traffic and limited shoreline capacity. This paper studies an integrated optimization problem that combines continuous berth allocation with time-invariant specific quay crane assignment. Unlike studies that determine only the number of quay cranes assigned to each vessel, this work explicitly determines the identities of assigned quay cranes while considering practical operational constraints, including continuous berth positions, vessel-specific crane quantity limits, contiguous crane assignment, vessel non-overlap, crane capacity, and crane non-crossing requirements. A mixed-integer programming model is formulated to minimize the total time that vessels spend in port. To solve medium- and large-scale instances efficiently, a greedy genetic algorithm is developed by combining greedy initialization, evolutionary search, decoding-based feasibility checking, and repair operations for infeasible offspring. The algorithm is designed to preserve useful inherited information while restoring feasibility with respect to berth-space and crane-resource constraints. Computational experiments are conducted using real operational data from a container port in Liaoning, China, together with synthetic instances of different scales. The results show that the proposed method can obtain high-quality feasible schedules within practical computation times. Additional ablation experiments demonstrate that both greedy initialization and repair contribute to performance improvement, with the repair mechanism providing the most evident gain in average solution quality and stability. These findings indicate that the proposed approach is suitable for integrated seaside scheduling problems requiring explicit crane identity decisions and fast feasible-schedule generation.

Introduction

Container terminals are critical interfaces in global maritime logistics, and their operational efficiency directly affects vessel turnaround time, port congestion, and supply chain reliability1,2. As vessel arrivals and cargo volumes continue to increase, terminal operators are under growing pressure to coordinate limited shoreline and equipment resources more effectively. In this context, efficient berth and quay crane scheduling has become a key operational issue in modern container terminals3,4.

Among seaside operational decisions, berth allocation and quay crane assignment are strongly interdependent. Berth allocation determines the service start time and berthing position of each vessel, whereas quay crane assignment determines the handling resources allocated during the service period. In practice, these decisions cannot be optimized independently. The berthing position of a vessel affects the set of quay cranes that can feasibly serve it, while crane availability, interference, and movement restrictions may in turn constrain berth allocation decisions5–7. Therefore, integrated optimization of berth allocation and quay crane operations is important for reducing vessel delay, shortening port time, and improving terminal resource utilization8–10.

The berth allocation problem has been extensively studied in both discrete and continuous settings, under static and dynamic arrival assumptions1,7,11. Early studies mainly focused on berth allocation or quay crane scheduling separately. With the increasing complexity of terminal operations, subsequent research gradually shifted toward integrated optimization frameworks that jointly consider berth allocation and quay crane assignment or scheduling5,6,8–10,12. These studies have employed mixed-integer programming, heuristic procedures, robust optimization, and hybrid metaheuristics to address the resulting computational complexity8,10,13–15.

Despite this progress, several limitations remain. First, many studies determine only the number of quay cranes assigned to each vessel rather than the identities of the assigned cranes. However, in actual terminal operations, determining specific cranes is important because quay cranes share tracks and are subject to contiguity and non-crossing restrictions5,8,15. Second, the integrated optimization of continuous berth allocation and specific quay crane assignment is computationally challenging because of the strong coupling between spatial and temporal decisions and the large number of feasibility constraints8,10,15. Third, from a practical perspective, terminal operators often require high-quality feasible schedules within short computation times, which limits the direct applicability of exact optimization methods to medium- and large-scale instances6,8,14.

In addition to deterministic formulations, recent studies have increasingly incorporated uncertainty in vessel arrivals and operational conditions into integrated berth-crane planning16–19. These studies highlight the practical importance of robust and rescheduling-oriented models, but they also further increase computational complexity. As a result, efficient heuristic and metaheuristic methods remain highly relevant for practical terminal scheduling.

Metaheuristic algorithms, especially genetic algorithms, have been widely used in berth-related scheduling problems because of their flexibility and scalability11,13,15. However, a standard genetic algorithm may generate many infeasible offspring when operational constraints are tight. Greedy construction strategies can rapidly generate feasible solutions by exploiting problem structure, while repair procedures can preserve useful inherited information during evolutionary search. This observation motivates the development of a hybrid approach that combines fast feasible-solution construction with the global search capability of a genetic algorithm.

Motivated by the above considerations, this paper studies an integrated continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific quay crane assignment problem in container terminals. The problem explicitly determines not only the berthing time and position of each vessel, but also the identities of the quay cranes assigned to it. Practical constraints such as vessel arrival times, shoreline boundaries, ship-specific crane quantity limits, contiguous crane assignment, crane capacity, and crane non-crossing are taken into account. The objective is to minimize the total time that vessels spend in port.

To address this problem, we first formulate a mixed-integer programming model. Since exact optimization becomes computationally expensive for larger instances, we further develop a greedy genetic algorithm. The proposed method combines greedy initialization, evolutionary search, decoding-based feasibility checking, and repair operations. The greedy procedure is used to generate feasible and promising initial solutions, while the repair mechanism restores infeasible offspring generated during crossover and mutation. In this way, the proposed framework aims to improve both solution quality and feasibility maintenance under practical terminal constraints.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

First, an integrated mixed-integer programming model is developed for continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific quay crane assignment under practical crane-related constraints. The model explicitly considers specific crane identities, contiguous crane intervals, crane non-crossing, vessel non-overlap, and vessel-specific crane quantity limits.

Second, a feasibility-oriented greedy genetic algorithm is proposed by combining greedy initialization, evolutionary search, decoding, and repair operations. The algorithm provides detailed procedures for constructing initial chromosomes, decoding berth-crane assignments, searching earliest feasible time-position combinations, and repairing infeasible offspring.

Third, computational experiments are conducted using both real operational data and synthetic instances of different scales. In addition to comparison with a standard genetic algorithm and a commercial solver, ablation experiments are introduced to isolate the effects of greedy initialization and repair. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm and identify the repair mechanism as the most influential component for improving average solution quality and stability.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature. Section 3 describes the problem and presents the mathematical formulation. Section 4 introduces the proposed algorithm. Section 5 reports the computational experiments and discusses the results. Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

Literature review

Berth allocation problem

The berth allocation problem has long been recognized as a fundamental seaside planning problem in container terminals. Imai et al.1 investigated the dynamic berth allocation problem and established one of the early foundations of this research stream. Kim and Moon7 studied berth scheduling using simulated annealing, while subsequent works further analyzed continuous berth allocation under more realistic operating assumptions11. In recent years, scholars have improved continuous berth allocation models by considering practical uncertain terminal operating conditions. Zheng et al.23 built a disruption-oriented continuous berth allocation model to handle uncertain vessel arrival deviations, which enhances the anti-interference performance of scheduling schemes. Targeting another common port uncertainty, time-varying water depth, Liu et al.27 developed a fuzzy programming berth allocation model minimized total vessel waiting time, and solved the model via genetic algorithm and simulated annealing. Their sensitivity analysis on confidence levels verified the robustness of fuzzy optimization methods for uncertain berth scheduling. In berth allocation problems, vessels may be assigned either to discrete berth segments or to continuous berthing positions along the quay. Continuous berth allocation is generally more flexible and closer to real terminal operations, but it also introduces additional spatial complexity because berth positions become continuous decision variables.

Research on berth allocation has developed from basic static models to dynamic, stochastic, robust, and integrated formulations. Static models assume that all vessel information is known at the beginning of the planning horizon, whereas dynamic models consider vessels arriving over time. Compared with discrete models, continuous berth allocation models can better capture the spatial flexibility of berth planning, but they require more complex non-overlap constraints in the berth-time plane. These characteristics make continuous berth allocation an important but challenging research topic.

Integrated berth allocation and quay crane assignment

As terminal operations became more complex, researchers increasingly recognized that berth allocation decisions are tightly coupled with quay crane assignment and scheduling decisions. Li and Jin3 studied the joint optimization of continuous berth scheduling and quay crane allocation. Türkoğulları et al.5 proposed an optimization model for integrated berth allocation and time-invariant quay crane assignment. Rodríguez-Molins et al.6 introduced robustness considerations into integrated berth allocation and quay crane assignment. More comprehensive integrated formulations that jointly consider berth allocation, quay crane assignment, and scheduling have subsequently been reported in the literature8–10. Latest studies further expand the application scenarios of integrated berth-crane optimization. Considering multi-terminal collaborative operation requirements, DU, Bo, et al.24 established a mixed-integer linear integrated optimization model for cross-terminal berth and quay crane resource allocation, verifying that joint optimization can effectively reduce overall terminal operation costs and vessel waiting time. In addition, Li et al.25 focused on parallel berthing scenarios in modern automated terminals and constructed an integrated model adapted to simultaneous berthing of multiple small vessels, enriching the research boundary of berth-crane joint optimization under diversified docking modes.

Integrated berth-crane optimization improves the consistency between vessel berthing decisions and handling-resource allocation. However, integration also significantly increases computational difficulty because the service time of a vessel depends on the number and availability of assigned quay cranes, and quay crane feasibility depends on vessel positions and overlapping service intervals. Therefore, integrated models usually require a trade-off between modeling realism and computational tractability.

Specific quay crane assignment and practical constraints

Although many integrated studies determine the number of quay cranes assigned to each vessel, relatively fewer works explicitly determine which specific cranes are assigned. In actual terminal operations, explicit crane identification is important because quay cranes operate on the same rail track and are subject to contiguity and non-crossing restrictions. If a model determines only the number of assigned cranes without identifying the cranes themselves, the resulting plan may require additional post-processing before implementation.

Li et al.13 and Yu et al.15 considered specific crane assignment in continuous berth-related settings. These studies suggest that explicit crane identification is important for generating implementable schedules, but it also substantially increases model complexity. In particular, crane intervals must remain contiguous, two simultaneous vessels cannot use the same crane, and the relative order of crane intervals should be consistent with the relative order of vessel berthing positions to avoid crane crossing. These practical restrictions motivate the need for modeling and solution methods that can jointly handle continuous berth positions and specific crane identities.

Exact methods and computational complexity

Mixed-integer programming provides a rigorous modeling framework for berth and quay crane optimization. Exact solvers can obtain optimal or near-optimal solutions for small instances and are useful for benchmarking heuristic methods. However, the computational burden increases rapidly as the number of vessels and quay cranes grows. This is especially evident in integrated models with continuous berth positions, pairwise non-overlap relations, binary crane assignment variables, and time-space resource conflicts. A systematic review conducted by Makhado, Ndifelani, et al.26 in 2025 summarized the computational complexity of existing continuous berth-crane integrated models, and pointed out that exact algorithms are only applicable to small-scale instances within 15 vessels; for large-scale actual terminal scheduling cases, metaheuristic algorithms are indispensable to balance solution accuracy and computational efficiency.

For practical container terminals, schedules often need to be generated or updated within limited decision time. Although exact optimization remains valuable for small instances and model validation, heuristic and metaheuristic methods are usually required for medium- and large-scale operational applications.

Metaheuristics and hybrid solution methods

Given the computational difficulty of integrated seaside scheduling problems, researchers have proposed a variety of heuristic and metaheuristic approaches, including simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, fuzzy optimization, and hybrid heuristics7,10,12–15. Genetic algorithms are attractive because they can handle complex solution structures and explore a large search space without requiring differentiability or convexity. They are also flexible enough to incorporate customized decoding and repair operators.

However, a standard genetic algorithm may perform poorly when constraints are tight. Crossover and mutation can easily destroy feasibility, especially when berth positions, service times, and crane intervals are strongly coupled. Hybrid methods that combine constructive rules and evolutionary search can be more effective in handling such feasibility requirements. Greedy initialization helps construct feasible and relatively good initial solutions, whereas repair procedures help retain inherited information while restoring feasibility after genetic operations.

Uncertainty-aware integrated planning and advanced optimization

Recent studies have extended integrated berth-crane planning to uncertainty-aware settings. Xi and Li16 proposed an almost robust optimization model for integrated berth allocation and quay crane assignment. Nourmohammadzadeh and Vo17 investigated a robust multiobjective model for integrated berth and quay crane scheduling. Zhang et al.18 studied integrated rescheduling optimization under uncertainty. Rodrigues and Agra19 reviewed berth allocation and quay crane assignment or scheduling problems under uncertainty. These studies indicate that robust and uncertainty-aware extensions are increasingly relevant for practical terminal operations.

Beyond berth-crane optimization, advanced optimization approaches such as self-adaptive algorithms, island algorithms, polyploid evolutionary algorithms, and hyperheuristics have been applied in many challenging domains, including scheduling, transportation, online learning, multi-objective optimization, medicine, data classification, and logistics planning20–22. These methods are valuable because they can improve diversity, adapt parameters during the search, or combine multiple low-level heuristics. For integrated port scheduling, such advanced approaches may provide stronger scalability and robustness in future research.

Research gap

Although existing studies have made important progress, three issues remain relevant. First, many studies determine only the number of quay cranes assigned to each vessel rather than their specific identities. Second, the integrated optimization of continuous berth allocation and specific quay crane assignment under contiguity and non-crossing constraints remains computationally challenging. Third, terminal operators require methods that can produce high-quality feasible schedules within short computation times. Motivated by these issues, this study develops an integrated mixed-integer programming model and a feasibility-oriented greedy genetic algorithm for continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific quay crane assignment. In addition, ablation experiments are conducted to clarify the individual contributions of greedy initialization and repair.

Problem description and mathematical formulation

Problem description

A container terminal with a continuous shoreline of length L and a set Q of quay cranes is considered. The quay cranes are installed on the same rail track and are indexed from left to right. A set V of vessels arrives dynamically during the planning horizon. For each vessel, the estimated arrival time, vessel length, handling workload, and lower and upper bounds on the number of simultaneously assigned quay cranes are known in advance.

The decision problem is to determine, for each vessel, the berthing start time, berthing position, service completion time, and the specific quay cranes assigned to it. The assignment must satisfy shoreline capacity, vessel non-overlap, quay crane quantity limits, crane contiguity, crane capacity, and crane non-crossing constraints. The objective is to minimize the total time spent by all vessels in port.

In this study, the specific set of quay cranes assigned to each vessel is assumed to remain unchanged during the vessel’s service period. Therefore, the problem can be characterized as an integrated continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific quay crane assignment problem.

Assumptions

The model is based on the following assumptions.

1) Vessels can berth at any feasible position along the continuous shoreline.

2) Draft and water-depth restrictions are not explicitly considered.

3) For each vessel, the minimum and maximum numbers of quay cranes are known in advance.

4) The set and number of quay cranes assigned to a vessel remain unchanged during its service period.

5) All assigned quay cranes work simultaneously on the same vessel.

6) Quay cranes are homogeneous and have identical handling productivity.

7) Quay crane preemption is not allowed.

8) Safety distance is incorporated into the effective berth length of each vessel.

9) Vessel information used in the deterministic planning model is assumed to be available before scheduling. This assumption is commonly adopted in deterministic berth allocation and quay crane assignment studies and provides a basis for evaluating the proposed integrated model before considering uncertainty extensions.

Model interpretation

The objective function minimizes the total time spent by vessels in port. Constraints (2)–(4) define the basic temporal and spatial feasibility of vessel service. Constraint (2) ensures that service cannot start before vessel arrival. Constraint (3) ensures that the entire vessel remains within the shoreline boundary. Constraint (4) defines the departure time as the sum of the service start time and service duration.

Constraints (5)–(8) define pairwise ordering relationships between vessels in the berth-time plane. The variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{ik}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{z}_{ik}$$\end{document} are introduced to describe relative spatial and temporal orders. For a pair of vessels, if their berthing positions would overlap, then their service intervals must be separated in time. Conversely, if their service intervals overlap, then they must be separated along the shoreline. These constraints jointly prevent two vessels from occupying overlapping berth segments during overlapping service periods.

Constraint (9) enforces vessel-specific lower and upper bounds on the number of assigned quay cranes. These bounds reflect practical handling policies and physical limits. Constraint (10) ensures that the total work completed by all cranes assigned to a vessel is sufficient to process its workload. Constraints (11)–(13) connect the auxiliary service variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{ij}$$\end{document} with the service duration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{i}$$\end{document}and assignment variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{ij}$$\end{document}. The auxiliary-variable representation is retained because it makes the link between crane assignment, service duration, and workload feasibility explicit and facilitates algorithmic decoding and repair.

Constraints (14)–(17) determine the specific and contiguous assignment of quay cranes. The variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{i}$$\end{document} represent the first and last crane assigned to vessel i. Constraint (15) links the interval length to the number of assigned cranes, while Constraints (16) and (17) ensure that selected cranes lie within this interval. Together, these constraints enforce contiguous crane assignment.

Constraints (18) and (19) prevent a quay crane from being assigned to two vessels whose service intervals overlap. Constraints (20) and (21) prevent crane crossing by preserving the relative order between berth positions and crane intervals when vessels operate simultaneously. If vessel i is located to the left of vessel k, then the crane interval assigned to i should also be located to the left of the crane interval assigned to k during overlapping service. This logic is essential because quay cranes share the same rail track and cannot pass through each other.

The integrated model is computationally challenging because it combines continuous berth decisions, binary specific crane assignment decisions, service-duration determination, and multiple time-space feasibility relationships.

Greedy genetic algorithm

Algorithm motivation

The integrated problem considered in this study is difficult to solve exactly for medium- and large-scale instances because of the strong coupling between berth allocation and specific quay crane assignment. A standard genetic algorithm can explore a large search space, but it may generate many infeasible offspring when practical operational constraints are tight. To address this issue, a greedy genetic algorithm is developed by combining greedy initialization, genetic evolution, decoding-based feasibility checking, and repair.

The main idea is to use greedy construction to provide feasible and promising initial individuals, and then use genetic operations to explore different vessel sequences, berthing positions, and crane intervals. Because crossover and mutation may destroy feasibility, a repair mechanism is applied to restore feasibility before fitness evaluation. This design allows the algorithm to balance exploration capability and feasibility maintenance.

Solution representation

Each chromosome represents a complete berth-crane assignment scheme. For each vessel, the chromosome records four main components: berthing priority, tentative berthing position, assigned crane quantity, and tentative first crane index. The last crane index is derived from the first crane index and the number of assigned cranes. The service duration and departure time are then calculated during decoding according to the assigned crane quantity and vessel workload.

Decoding procedure

The decoding procedure transforms a chromosome into a complete berth-crane schedule. The vessels are first sorted according to their priority values. Then, the algorithm decodes vessels one by one following this order. For each vessel, the tentative crane quantity and crane interval are adjusted to satisfy crane-number bounds and contiguity. The service duration is calculated based on the vessel workload, quay crane productivity, and number of assigned cranes.

The decoding procedure contains the following steps.

Step 1: Sort vessels according to their priority values in the chromosome.

Step 2: For the current vessel, adjust its assigned crane quantity to satisfy the lower and upper bounds.

Step 3: Rebuild a contiguous crane interval according to the adjusted crane quantity.

Step 4: Calculate the service duration using the workload and total productivity of the assigned cranes.

Step 5: Search for the earliest feasible service start time and berth position that satisfy arrival-time, shoreline-boundary, vessel non-overlap, crane-capacity, crane-contiguity, and crane non-crossing constraints.

Step 6: Fix the vessel assignment and update the occupied berth-time and crane-time resources.

Step 7: Repeat Steps 2–6 until all vessels are decoded.

Greedy initialization

The quality of the initial population has a strong influence on the performance of a genetic algorithm. Instead of generating all individuals purely at random, the proposed algorithm constructs part of the initial population using a greedy procedure. The remaining individuals are randomly perturbed to maintain population diversity.

For each vessel, the greedy procedure first considers its earliest possible service start time, which is no earlier than its arrival time. Candidate crane quantities are generated within the vessel-specific feasible range. For each crane quantity, possible contiguous crane intervals are enumerated. For each crane interval, candidate berth positions are examined along the shoreline. The algorithm then checks whether the vessel can be inserted without violating berth boundary, vessel non-overlap, crane capacity, crane contiguity, and crane non-crossing constraints.

The greedy rule gives priority to the earliest feasible service start time. If multiple feasible combinations are available at the same time, the combination that produces the earliest completion time is selected. If there is still a tie, the combination with smaller berth-position deviation and smaller crane-index deviation is preferred. If no feasible assignment is available at the current time, the service start time is postponed to the next candidate time generated by existing vessel departure times and the vessel arrival time. This process continues until a feasible assignment is found.

This initialization procedure provides feasible individuals with relatively good objective values, while random perturbation of priorities, berth positions, and crane intervals helps preserve diversity in the initial population.

Earliest feasible time-position search

For each vessel during decoding or repair, the earliest feasible time-position combination is found using an enumeration-and-check procedure. The candidate start-time set includes the vessel arrival time and the departure times of already scheduled vessels. These time points represent moments at which berth or crane resources may become available. For each candidate start time, the algorithm searches candidate berth positions generated from the left boundary, the right endpoints of already scheduled vessels, and feasible shifted positions within the shoreline. For each candidate berth position, candidate contiguous crane intervals are checked.

Repair mechanism

Because crossover and mutation may generate infeasible offspring, a repair procedure is applied before fitness evaluation. The repair mechanism is designed to preserve as much inherited information as possible while restoring feasibility.

The repair procedure follows these priorities.

First, the number of assigned quay cranes is projected onto the allowable interval . If the inherited crane quantity is below the minimum, it is increased to the lower bound. If it exceeds the maximum, it is reduced to the upper bound.

Second, the crane assignment is reconstructed as a contiguous crane block. If the inherited first crane index leads to an interval outside the crane-index range, it is shifted to the nearest feasible position. The last crane index is then recalculated according to the first crane index and crane quantity.

Third, the berth position is adjusted to satisfy the shoreline boundary. If the inherited berth position exceeds the allowable range, it is projected into the interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:[0,L-{l}_{i}]$$\end{document}.

Fourth, the vessel is inserted into the partial schedule according to the decoding order. If the inherited time-position-crane combination causes vessel overlap, crane conflict, or crane crossing, the vessel is shifted to the earliest feasible time-position combination using the search procedure described in Sect. 4.5.

Fifth, after all vessels are repaired, a final feasibility check is performed. If any conflict remains, the affected vessel is removed and reinserted using the earliest feasible insertion rule.

Through these steps, the repair mechanism handles berth-capacity constraints, crane-capacity constraints, contiguous crane assignment, vessel non-overlap, and crane non-crossing requirements. It also reduces the number of infeasible individuals entering the fitness evaluation stage, thereby improving the stability of the evolutionary search.

Algorithm framework

The proposed greedy genetic algorithm can be summarized as follows.

Step 1: Generate an initial population using greedy initialization and random perturbation.

Step 2: Decode each chromosome and repair infeasible assignments if necessary.

Step 3: Evaluate the fitness of all individuals.

Step 4: Select parent individuals using roulette wheel selection.

Step 5: Perform arithmetic crossover with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{c}$$\end{document}.

Step 6: Perform mutation with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{m}$$\end{document}.

Step 7: Repair infeasible offspring according to crane quantity, crane interval, berth boundary, vessel non-overlap, crane conflict, and crane non-crossing constraints.

Step 8: Evaluate the offspring and update the population using elitism.

Step 9: Update the best solution.

Step 10: Repeat Steps 4–9 until the stopping criterion is reached.

The main originality of the algorithm lies in the integration of problem-specific greedy initialization, decoding-based earliest feasible insertion, and feasibility repair for specific quay crane assignment. These components are tailored to the continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific crane assignment problem.

Computational experiments

Experimental setting

The computational experiments were carried out on a workstation with an Intel Core i7-10700 K CPU at 3.80 GHz and 32 GB RAM. The proposed greedy genetic algorithm was compared with a standard genetic algorithm and the Gurobi solver. The handling productivity of each quay crane was set to 35 TEU/h. Unless otherwise specified, the main algorithm parameters were set as follows: population size = 50, crossover probability = 0.85, mutation probability = 0.20, and maximum number of generations = 100.

Each stochastic algorithm was independently executed 10 times for each instance. For each method, the best objective value, average objective value, standard deviation, feasibility rate, and computational time were recorded when applicable. These statistics were used to evaluate both solution quality and stability.

Gurobi was used as the exact benchmark solver with a preset time limit for each instance group. Unless otherwise stated, the default parameter settings were adopted. For instances terminated by the time limit, the reported Gurobi value corresponds to the best incumbent solution found within the allowed runtime.

Two groups of experiments were conducted. The first used real operational data obtained from a container port in Liaoning, China. The second used synthetic instances of different scales to evaluate algorithm scalability. In addition, an ablation experiment was conducted to isolate the contribution of greedy initialization and repair.

Data source and instance generation

The real-data instance was based on operational information collected from a container terminal in Liaoning, China. The terminal has a 1400-meter shoreline and 11 quay cranes. The planning horizon covered three consecutive days. The real-data instance includes vessel arrival times, vessel lengths, handling workloads, and lower and upper bounds on assigned quay cranes. These data reflect typical berth and crane planning information available to terminal operators before schedule generation.

Synthetic instances were generated to evaluate scalability under controlled terminal configurations. Vessel lengths, workloads, and crane quantity bounds were generated according to vessel types. Small, medium, and large vessels were considered with different length and workload ranges. The proportions of these vessel types were set to reflect mixed vessel traffic patterns. Synthetic instances allow the number of vessels, shoreline length, and number of quay cranes to be varied systematically.

The use of both real and synthetic data provides a more comprehensive evaluation. The real-data instance demonstrates practical applicability, while the synthetic instances test the behavior of the proposed method under different problem scales.

4) GGA: complete greedy genetic algorithm combining greedy initialization and repair/guided strategy.

This confirms that the combination of greedy initialization and repair is effective. However, in this particular ablation test, GA-RP achieves the lowest mean objective value and the smallest standard deviation. This suggests that the repair mechanism is the most influential component for this instance, because it directly restores feasibility after genetic operations and prevents the search process from wasting evaluations on infeasible or poor-quality offspring.

The runtime of the improved variants is higher than that of the standard GA because greedy construction, feasibility checking, and repair require additional computation. Nevertheless, the increased runtime is accompanied by improved solution quality and stability. For practical terminal scheduling, this trade-off can be acceptable when higher-quality feasible schedules are required.

Discussion

The computational results reveal four main findings.

First, exact optimization is competitive for small instances but becomes increasingly time-consuming as problem size grows. This is consistent with the structural complexity of the integrated model, which combines continuous berth decisions, specific crane identities, and multiple resource-coupling constraints.

Second, although the standard GA remains computationally efficient, its average solution quality deteriorates as the instance size increases, indicating limited robustness under tight feasibility requirements. The main reason is that standard genetic operations may generate infeasible or weakly feasible solutions when berth positions and crane intervals are strongly coupled.

Third, the proposed GGA consistently achieves better average objective values and improved stability than the standard GA. This suggests that the combination of greedy initialization and feasibility repair effectively guides the search toward promising feasible regions of the solution space.

Fourth, the ablation experiment confirms that the performance improvement is not caused by a single unexplained factor. Greedy initialization improves the quality of the initial population, while repair improves feasibility maintenance and robustness after crossover and mutation. Among the tested components, the repair procedure contributes most strongly to average objective improvement and stability.

The experimental tables and figures jointly support these conclusions. Tables 4 and 5 show that the proposed method remains close to exact solutions for small-scale cases. Tables 6 and 7; Figs. 5 and 6 show that GGA outperforms GA for medium-scale cases. Table 8; Fig. 7 demonstrate the scalability advantage of GGA under a limited computation time. Table 9 further explains the algorithmic source of this improvement through ablation analysis.

At the same time, the proposed method has limitations. First, the model assumes homogeneous quay cranes and deterministic vessel information. Second, the crane assignment is time-invariant during vessel service, whereas practical terminals may sometimes reassign cranes dynamically. Third, the current algorithm is compared mainly with GA and Gurobi; future work should compare it with more advanced metaheuristics and hyperheuristics. These limitations provide directions for further research.

Conclusions

This paper studied an integrated continuous berth allocation and time-invariant specific quay crane assignment problem in container terminals. A mixed-integer programming model was developed to minimize the total time spent by vessels in port while explicitly considering practical operational constraints, including vessel arrival times, continuous berth positions, crane quantity limits, contiguous crane assignment, crane capacity, and non-crossing crane operations.

To address the computational difficulty of the problem, a greedy genetic algorithm was proposed. The algorithm combines greedy initialization, evolutionary search, decoding-based feasibility checking, and a repair mechanism for infeasible offspring. In this way, the proposed method is able to maintain feasibility more effectively than a standard genetic algorithm while preserving global search capability.

Computational experiments based on real data from a container port in Liaoning, China, and synthetic instances of different scales showed that the proposed method provides better average solution quality and higher stability than a standard GA under comparable computational effort. The advantage of the proposed method becomes more evident as the instance size increases, which suggests that it is suitable for medium- and large-scale integrated seaside scheduling problems.

An ablation experiment was further conducted to evaluate the effects of greedy initialization and repair. The results show that both components improve the performance of the standard GA. In particular, the repair procedure provides the largest improvement in average objective value and the smallest standard deviation, indicating that feasibility restoration is a key factor for improving solution quality and robustness.

Several directions remain for future research. First, uncertainty in vessel arrivals, handling workloads, and crane productivity can be incorporated into robust or stochastic optimization models. Second, heterogeneous quay cranes, crane maintenance, and dynamic crane reassignment can be considered to improve modeling realism. Third, multiple objectives such as emissions, energy consumption, service fairness, and berth utilization can be integrated. Fourth, more advanced optimization approaches, including self-adaptive algorithms, island algorithms, polyploid evolutionary algorithms, hyper heuristics, and learning-assisted metaheuristics, can be explored for this decision problem. Such methods have shown potential in scheduling, transportation, online learning, multi-objective optimization, medicine, data classification, and other complex domains, and may further improve scalability and robustness for integrated port scheduling. Finally, future studies may integrate seaside planning with yard-side and gate-side operations to support more comprehensive terminal decision-making.
