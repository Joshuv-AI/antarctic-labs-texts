Right ventricular-pulmonary arterial uncoupling: an underrecognized predictor of in-hospital outcomes in acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Sibel Çatalkaya, Eda Özcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight ventricular (RV) function is an underrecognized determinant of outcomes in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and is not routinely incorporated into risk stratification. Beyond primary RV involvement, interventricular interdependence and ventriculoarterial coupling suggest a more substantial role of the RV in acute hemodynamic adaptation than previously appreciated. The TAPSE/sPAP ratio, a non-invasive index of RV-pulmonary arterial (RV-PA) coupling reflecting the interaction between RV function and afterload, may capture this interaction, but its prognostic role in AMI remains insufficiently defined.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 290 consecutive AMI patients (138 STEMI, 152 NSTEMI). The primary endpoint was in-hospital major adverse events (MAE), defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction, acute decompensated heart failure, acute kidney injury, stroke, or major bleeding. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the prognostic value of TAPSE/sPAP. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as the composite of in-hospital events excluding major bleeding.ResultsMAE occurred in 53 patients (18.3%). In multivariable analysis, TAPSE/sPAP was an independent predictor of MAE (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.03–0.73, p = 0.017). Chronic kidney disease and Shock Index also remained significant predictors, whereas LVEF was not independently associated. Sensitivity analysis using MACE confirmed the prognostic value of TAPSE/sPAP (OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.62, p = 0.011). ROC analysis identified a TAPSE/sPAP threshold of 0.47 mm/mmHg, with 83.3% sensitivity and 85.6% specificity for predicting MAE.ConclusionsThe TAPSE/sPAP ratio is a non-invasive independent predictor of in-hospital complications in AMI and remains associated with adverse outcomes after adjustment for clinical risk factors. Early assessment of RV-pulmonary arterial coupling may improve identification of high-risk patients and guide targeted clinical management.

Background

Acute Myocardial Infarction, including ST-segment elevation (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation (NSTEMI), remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Despite advances in reperfusion therapy and evidence-based pharmacological management, substantial interindividual variability in outcomes persists, underscoring the continued need for early and reliable risk stratification to guide personalized therapeutic strategies [2–4].

Traditionally, AMI risk assessment has relied on clinical, biochemical, electrocardiographic, and angiographic parameters [2–9]. Further, rapid bedside tools, including the Shock Index and early echocardiography enable immediate evaluation of hemodynamic compromise [2, 3, 10]. Although LVEF is commonly used for risk stratification, as a load-dependent measure it does not fully capture intrinsic myocardial contractility or RV-LV interactions [11–13].

In physiological conditions, right ventricular contractility is matched to pulmonary vascular load to optimize energy transfer efficiency. In AMI, ischemia may disrupt RV-PA coupling through increased afterload due to elevated pulmonary artery occlusion pressure secondary to left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, as well as reduced RV contractility related to myocardial ischemia. In addition, ventricular interdependence due to the anatomical and functional coupling of the right and left ventricles may further contribute to hemodynamic impairment [14–20].

The TAPSE/sPAP (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to systolic pulmonary artery pressure) ratio, a non-invasive measure of RV-PA coupling, integrates RV function and pulmonary load into a single physiologically relevant parameter [16, 21]. While extensively studied in chronic cardiopulmonary diseases, its prognostic significance in AMI remains insufficiently defined. 14–16,21−24

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of TAPSE/sPAP in a contemporary AMI cohort and determine whether it is associated with in-hospital outcomes beyond established clinical risk factors.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective single-center cohort study included consecutive patients ≥ 18 years admitted for AMI between January and June 2025. Patients were classified as STEMI or NSTEMI based on initial ECG findings and cardiac biomarkers, in accordance with current ESC guidelines.²,³ STEMI patients underwent immediate primary PCI (percutaneous coronary intervention), whereas NSTEMI patients received early invasive management within 24–48 h. Exclusion criteria were known pre-existing pulmonary hypertension, myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), advanced valvular disease (> grade 2), end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis, and active malignancy. A history of heart failure and pre-existing coronary artery disease were not considered exclusion criteria, as they represent common comorbidities in ACS patients and reflect routine clinical practice. Non-dialysis-dependent CKD (chronic kidney disease) patients were also included to maintain a representative real-world cohort. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was in-hospital major adverse events (MAE), defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction, acute decompensated heart failure, stroke, acute kidney injury (KDIGO [Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes] criteria), and major bleeding (BARC [Bleeding Academic Research Consortium] grade > 3 or requiring transfusion).

Acute decompensated heart failure was defined as clinical heart failure requiring either inotropic or mechanical circulatory support and/or non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation due to acute cardiogenic pulmonary congestion.

The secondary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as MAE excluding major bleeding. Patients with multiple events were counted once for each composite endpoint.

Clinical, angiographic, and echocardiographic assessment

Hemodynamic status at admission was assessed using the Shock Index (SI, heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure). Coronary artery disease extent was classified from invasive angiography as one-, two-, or three-vessel disease. Post-PCI, TIMI (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction) flow grade 3 was achieved in all patients.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at admission by experienced cardiologists using either GE Vivid S5 or Philips EPIQ 7 ultrasound systems. LVEF was calculated using the biplane Simpson method in apical four- and two-chamber views. Diastolic function was assessed using the transmitral E/A ratio measured by pulsed-wave Doppler. RV function was evaluated by TAPSE in M-mode from the apical four-chamber view. Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) was estimated using the Bernoulli equation: sPAP = 4×(TR velocity)² + RAP (right atrial pressure), with RAP (3–15 mmHg) estimated from the diameter and respiratory collapsibility of the inferior vena cava. sPAP was successfully assessed in 251 of 290 patients (feasibility 86.6%). In 39 patients, sPAP could not be measured due to insufficient or absent tricuspid regurgitation Doppler signal quality. The TAPSE/sPAP ratio was calculated as a noninvasive index of RV-PA coupling (Fig. 1) [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Independent predictors of in-hospital MAE were identified using multivariable logistic regression models. Variables were first assessed in univariate analysis, and those with a p-value < 0.10 were considered for further evaluation. From this set, variables with established clinical relevance and prior evidence in the literature were selected for inclusion. All selected variables were simultaneously entered into the multivariable models. This approach reflects a clinically guided model-building strategy while limiting the number of variables relative to the number of events to reduce the risk of overfitting.

To assess potential effect modification, interaction terms between TAPSE/sPAP and AMI type (STEMI vs. NSTEMI) were tested in sensitivity analyses. Model stability was assessed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations), and multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Discriminative ability was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [27, 28].

Results

Baseline and angiographic characteristics

A total of 290 patients were included, comprising 138 with STEMI and 152 with NSTEMI. The cohort was predominantly male, with a higher proportion of men in the STEMI group (p = 0.012). NSTEMI patients were older and exhibited a greater burden of cardiovascular comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and prior coronary artery disease (all p < 0.05). Regarding coronary anatomy, NSTEMI patients more frequently presented with multivessel disease, whereas STEMI patients predominantly exhibited single-vessel involvement (p < 0.001).

Hemodynamic and echocardiographic findings

At admission, STEMI patients exhibited greater hemodynamic compromise, as reflected by a higher Shock Index (0.66 [0.60–0.74] vs. 0.59 [0.54–0.65], p < 0.001). Left ventricular systolic function was modestly reduced in STEMI compared with NSTEMI patients (median LVEF 45% [37.5–50] vs. 48% [38–55], p = 0.007). In contrast, left atrial and left ventricular diameter were significantly larger in NSTEMI patients. RV function and pulmonary pressure parameters were comparable between groups; neither TAPSE, sPAP nor the TAPSE/sPAP ratio differed significantly.

Laboratory parameters

NSTEMI patients exhibited a laboratory profile consistent with a higher chronic cardiovascular burden, characterized by markedly elevated BNP levels (1835 vs. 207 pg/mL, p < 0.001) and impaired renal function, reflected by higher creatinine levels and lower eGFR (both p < 0.001). In contrast, STEMI patients demonstrated a more pronounced acute inflammatory response, as indicated by higher white blood cell counts (12.5 vs. 10.6 × 10³/µL, p < 0.001). In-hospital clinical outcomes

Overall, 53 patients (18.3%) experienced in-hospital MAE, with no significant difference between STEMI and NSTEMI patients (15.2% vs. 21.1%, p = 0.199). In-hospital death occurred in 5 patients (1.7%). The most frequent complication was acute decompensated heart failure, occurring in 36 patients (12.4%), of whom 8 required inotropic support and 9 required invasive ventilation. Acute kidney injury and major bleeding affected 3.1% and 2.8% of patients, respectively. Stroke and recurrent myocardial infarction were less frequent, each occurring in 4 patients (1.4%). Multivariable predictors of in-hospital MAE

To evaluate independent predictors of in-hospital MAE, three sequential multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, comparing established clinical risk factors with echocardiographic parameters. Of the 53 patients with MAE, two had missing sPAP values and were therefore excluded from TAPSE/sPAP-based analyses. The TAPSE/sPAP × AMI type (STEMI vs. NSTEMI) interaction was tested and found not to be significant; therefore, AMI type was excluded from the final models. Similarly, the extent of coronary artery disease was not associated with the primary endpoint (p > 0.10) and was also excluded. Model 1 included age, gender, CKD, and Shock Index. Model 2 added LVEF < 40%. Model 3 comprised Model 1 variables plus TAPSE/sPAP ratio. Across models, CKD and shock index remained independent predictors, whereas gender was not statistically significant; age lost statistical significance in Model 3. Model 3 showed a statistically significant improvement in model fit compared with Model 1 (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.009). However, the improvement in discriminative performance was modest, as reflected by minimal changes in AUC across models (Table 3; Fig. 2). TAPSE/sPAP remained an independent predictor in Model 3 (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.03–0.73, p = 0.017).

A sensitivity analysis was performed using the prespecified secondary endpoint of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). In this analysis, the TAPSE/sPAP ratio remained independently associated with in-hospital MACE after adjustment for age, chronic kidney disease, and Shock Index (OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.62, p = 0.011). The model showed good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI 0.734–0.857, p < 0.001).

To determine the optimal prognostic threshold for the TAPSE/sPAP ratio, the Youden Index was calculated and identified 0.47 mm/mmHg (Sensitivity 83.3%, Specificity 85.6%) as the ideal cut-off for predicting in-hospital MAE.

Discussion

This study identifies the TAPSE/sPAP ratio, a noninvasive measure of right ventricular-pulmonary arterial coupling, as an independent predictor of in-hospital MAE and MACE in a contemporary ACS cohort. ROC analysis revealed a threshold of 0.47 mm/mmHg. Its predictive value remained significant after adjustment for established clinical risk factors and showed good discriminative ability, including patients with CKD (prevalence 9.3%), a population often underrepresented in trials where acute-on-chronic renal impairment contributes substantially to in-hospital MAE [7–10].

Initially applied for risk stratification in pulmonary hypertension (cutoffs < 0.30–0.40 mm/mmHg), [15, 22, 29, 30] the TAPSE/sPAP ratio has more recently emerged as a significant prognostic marker across various cardiovascular pathologies, including heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and valvular interventions. Across these diverse populations, lower TAPSE/sPAP values have been associated with adverse outcomes, reflecting the detrimental impact of impaired RV-PA coupling [16, 21–24, 31–35].

The physiological rationale for the prognostic utility of the TAPSE/sPAP ratio lies in the unique structure and function of the RV. Its thin-walled, crescent-shaped architecture renders it particularly vulnerable to increases in afterload compared with the more robust left ventricle.15,17,36 The RV is naturally adapted to function within a low-pressure pulmonary circuit, but this makes it highly sensitive to disruptions in the coupling between its contractile performance and pulmonary vascular resistance.15,17 This mechanical sensitivity is particularly relevant in acute myocardial infarction, where acute left ventricular dysfunction may rapidly increase pulmonary pressures and contribute to RV-pulmonary arterial uncoupling through afterload mismatch. In addition, ventricular interaction may contribute to reduced right ventricular contractility, which can be further exacerbated by concomitant right ventricular ischemia.19,20.

Data on the TAPSE/sPAP ratio in ACS remain scarce and have yielded partially conflicting results [37–39]. Fauvel et al. [37] first described a significant association between reduced TAPSE/sPAP values and MACE in a subgroup of 481 patients from the ADDICT-ICCU trial, demonstrating its independent prognostic value and proposing thresholds of < 0.55 mm/mmHg for STEMI and < 0.48 mm/mmHg for NSTEMI. In contrast, a recent study by Piras et al. [39] involving 152 patients found that while a ratio < 0.55 mm/mmHg was associated with MACE and prolonged hospitalization in univariate analysis, it lacked independent prognostic power in their multivariable model.

In our cohort, the TAPSE/sPAP ratio emerged as an independent predictor of both in-hospital MAE and MACE, with a cutoff of 0.47 mm/mmHg. This association persisted after multivariable adjustment for established risk markers, including age, CKD, and Shock Index. While Fauvel et al. [37] reported findings from a subgroup analysis of a multicenter trial, our study investigated a consecutive real-world AMI cohort with predefined in-hospital endpoints and echocardiographic assessment performed according to standardized clinical protocols, providing independent validation in a routine clinical setting. Discrepancies compared with the study by Piras et al. [39] may be related to differences in adjustment strategies, variable selection, and cohort characteristics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability, particularly as patients with severe comorbidities were excluded.

Second, endpoint adjudication was based on predefined clinical criteria; however, independent blinded event adjudication was not performed, which may introduce potential classification bias in the composite MAE endpoint.

Third, although echocardiographic measurements were performed by experienced operators using standardized protocols, inter-observer variability was not assessed due to the lack of access to raw echocardiographic data for re-analysis in this retrospective study. In addition, sPAP could not be obtained in all patients due to absent or insufficient tricuspid regurgitation Doppler signal quality, resulting in incomplete measurements in a subset of the cohort. This may limit the reproducibility and generalizability of the study findings in routine clinical practice, where image quality and measurement variability may differ.

Fourth, TAPSE provides only a surrogate of longitudinal right ventricular function, and more comprehensive assessment using speckle-tracking-derived strain imaging or cardiac magnetic resonance was not available. Structural right ventricular parameters such as RV dimensions were not systematically assessed, limiting detailed characterization of right ventricular morphology. Furthermore, pre-infarction echocardiographic data were not available, precluding assessment of acute changes in RV-pulmonary arterial coupling induced by the index event.

Fifth, the multivariable models were constructed in a relatively modest sample with a limited number of events, which may contribute to wide confidence intervals for individual predictors including TAPSE/sPAP. Although bootstrap resampling and collinearity diagnostics were applied, a residual risk of overfitting cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, residual confounding inherent to the observational study design cannot be excluded, including potential effects of baseline clinical characteristics and comorbidities, and causal inference remains limited. Prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes should validate these findings and directly compare the prognostic utility of TAPSE/sPAP with established risk scores such as GRACE and TIMI.

Conclusion

The TAPSE/sPAP ratio is an echocardiographic marker of RV-PA coupling that is independently associated with in-hospital MAE and MACE in patients with acute coronary syndrome. A proposed cutoff of 0.47 mm/mmHg identifies patients at higher risk. Incorporation of this readily obtainable measure into early bedside risk assessment may support improved identification of high-risk patients in addition to established clinical parameters and conventional left ventricular assessment. Prospective studies are warranted to validate this cutoff and to further investigate the clinical utility of RV-PA assessment in acute coronary syndromes.
