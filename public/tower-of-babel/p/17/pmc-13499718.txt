Real-World Adult Height Outcomes in Girls with Central Precocious Puberty Receiving GnRHa Monotherapy or Combined with Growth Hormone: A Cohort Study in China
Authors: Fei Wang, Yan Li, Yu Yang, Wei Gu, Xuan Xu, Haiying Wu, Guimei Li, Xiaoqin Yin, Shasha Zhou, Pin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs (GnRHa) are the standard treatment for central precocious puberty (CPP), the efficacy of GnRHa monotherapy in older children (around 8 years) and that of combination therapy with recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) both remain unclear. This study aims to evaluate the real-world efficacy of each regimen.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled Chinese girls with CPP. Two groups were formed: GnRHa monotherapy and GnRHa + rhGH combination therapy. Outcomes included adult height gain [AHG, final adult height (FAH) standard deviation score (SDS)– predicted adult height (PAH) SDS]，genetic height gain [GHG, FAH SDS–target height (Tht) SDS], and change in height (FAH SDS – baseline height SDS).ResultsIn the GnRHa group, the mean age at treatment initiation was 8.87 ± 0.87 years. After 23.02 months of GnRHa treatment and 14.02 months of rhGH treatment, the group showed significantly greater AHG [1.72 SDS (1.24, 2.59)].ConclusionsGnRHa monotherapy initiated after age 8 may still provide meaningful height gains, and adding rhGH is associated with improved FAH in patients with severe baseline height impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03662-9.Keywords: Central precocious puberty, Gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs, Recombinant human growth hormone, Adult height gain

Introduction

Central precocious puberty (CPP) is defined as the early development of secondary sexual characteristics due to the premature activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, manifesting before the age of 8 in girls and 9 in boys [1]. CPP exhibits regional variations in incidence, with the annual incidence among girls in Spain being 1.1 per 100,000 [2], whereas the 20-year average annual incidence is 92 per 100,000 among native Danish girls [3]. Currently, no incidence data specific to CPP are available in China. However, a multi-regional cross-sectional study demonstrated that the overall prevalence of precocious puberty, including both central and peripheral subtypes, among children aged 6–18 years was 0.43% (430 per 100,000) [4]. CPP disrupts normal puberty timing, potentially causing impaired final adult height (FAH) and psychosocial problems [5].

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogs (GnRHa) represent the standard treatment for CPP. They suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis to decelerate skeletal maturation, thereby preserving FAH potential rather than directly accelerating growth velocity (GV). As noted in a narrative review [6], earlier studies on early GnRHa treatment have focused on children with CPP who have a young age of onset (mean approximately 6 years) and experience rapidly progressing puberty, and the efficacy in this group is well established. In recent years, the incidence of breast development, the initial presentation of precocious puberty in girls, has increased annually among girls aged 7–8 years. The application of GnRHa has therefore been expanded to girls aged 7–9 years. However, whether this population achieves clinical benefits from such intervention remains inconclusive. In particular, studies using 8 years of age as the cutoff for treatment initiation remain highly controversial. Several studies have detected no height benefit in girls treated after age 8, and some even indicate a potential negative impact on height outcome associated with treatment [7–9]. Given these clinical controversies, we conducted a retrospective real-world analysis. After data screening and enrollment, the study population consisted mainly of children who started treatment at a relatively late age, aligning with the target population. Therefore, this part of the study aimed to investigate the clinical efficacy of GnRHa treatment in this patient group.

The use of GnRHa combined with rhGH dates back to the 1990s [10]. Nevertheless, combination therapy has remained a subject of debate, and current clinical guidelines on CPP management rarely address recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) combination therapy, largely because the available evidence remains limited and inconclusive. One exception is the Korean guideline, which provides a conditional recommendation: combination rhGH therapy may be considered only in children with CPP who experience a marked decline in GV during GnRHa treatment or have a poor predicted adult height (PAH) [11]. This recommendation is supported by two key studies. A meta-analysis involving 1268 patients showed that the combination group gained an additional 1 cm in FAH compared with the GnRHa monotherapy group, and 3.3 cm compared with untreated patients [12]. Another study of 166 children with CPP (mean age at treatment initiation, 7.89 years) confirmed that adding rhGH to GnRHa treatment resulted in additional height benefits over GnRHa monotherapy [13]. Meanwhile, the Chinese CPP consensus suggests that combination therapy provides greater height benefits in children with more severe height impairment at the start of treatment, although it is not routinely recommended [5].

In summary, two clinical controversies in CPP management are: whether GnRHa monotherapy is effective in girls aged 7–9 years with mild early puberty, and which patients might benefit from adding rhGH to GnRHa therapy. To address these gaps, this retrospective real-world study examined the use of GnRHa monotherapy and combination therapy in children who started treatment at a relatively older age, and explored factors influencing height gain from combination therapy to guide future prospective studies.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at six medical centers in China from November 2023 to October 2024. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and anonymized data analysis with no patient involvement. The study was approved by the ethics committees of all participating centers: Shanghai Children’s Hospital (2023-R092F01), Jiangxi Provincial Children’s Hospital (IIITPJ2024-002), Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (2024-030121), Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (2024-001), Shandong Provincial Hospital (2023-641), and Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (2023-198), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

We enrolled girls first diagnosed with CPP between September 2013 and November 2021 at six hospitals in China. The index date was defined as the date of initial CPP diagnosis, and data were collected from electronic health records (EHR) or paper medical records.

Inclusion criteria: eligible patients had achieved FAH following either GnRHa monotherapy (≥ 12 months), or combined GnRHa + rhGH therapy (GnRHa ≥ 12 months; rhGH ≥ 6 months). FAH was defined as GV < 1 cm/year, bone age (BA) ≥ 15 years, chronological age (CA) ≥ 18 years, or epiphyseal closure on BA radiograph.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: growth failure/short stature etiologies (growth hormone deficiency, small for gestational age, Turner syndrome, idiopathic short stature, hypothyroidism); secondary CPP (intracranial tumor or history of radiotherapy/chemotherapy); systemic comorbidities affecting treatment efficacy (severe chronic diseases including cardiac, hepatic/renal insufficiency, inflammatory bowel disease); peripheral precocious puberty progressing to CPP due to syndromes (McCune–Albright syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia); diagnosed but no treatment received; treatment duration below the required threshold and could not be determined; or missing or incomplete baseline key data [BA, CA (age at treatment initiation)].

Exposures and Study Groups

GnRHa monotherapy group: defined as participants receiving GnRHa monotherapy for at least 1 year, with continuous medication use (i.e., no treatment interruption lasting ≥ 1 month). The 1-year minimum was based on clinical experience, as shorter treatment durations are unlikely to produce meaningful effects on FAH.

GnRHa + rhGH group: defined as participants who received GnRHa for over 1 year and combined rhGH for at least 6 months, either sequentially or concurrently. Concurrent therapy was defined as overlapping treatment periods > 3 months, while sequential therapy was defined as initiating rhGH after GnRHa start with an interval < 6 months between treatments. The 6-month minimum rhGH exposure criterion was applied because all patients received at least 6 months of treatment. Medication duration was determined based on drug prescription records, and medication continuity was comprehensively assessed according to prescription dosage and the interval between consecutive prescriptions.

Data Collection

Data were retrospectively collected from EHR or paper medical records, as EHR were not widely available in China in the early years. Patient data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality. Baseline data were collected within a ± 90-day window from the date of treatment initiation, defined as the first administration date of either GnRHa (monotherapy group) or the first use of either GnRHa or rhGH (combination therapy group). In cases where multiple records were available, the record closest to the treatment initiation date was used.

Primary assessment indicators included three key growth metrics: adult height gain [AHG, FAH standard deviation score (SDS)–PAH SDS], genetic height gain [GHG, FAH SDS–target height (Tht) SDS], and change in height [ΔHt SDS, FAH SDS–baseline height (Ht) SDS]. The loss in height potential was defined as PAH SDS–Tht SDS.

Radiographs for BA assessments were retrospectively collected and analyzed at a single center using the Greulich–Pyle method [14]. Ht SDS and body mass index (BMI) SDS were calculated based on the reference values established in China [15, 16]. Tht was determined using the formula: (father’s height + mother’s height−13)/2 cm. PAH was calculated using the Bayley–Pinneau method [17].

Statistical Analysis

Two analytical cohorts were constructed: the GnRHa monotherapy cohort and the GnRHa + rhGH cohort. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed variables were described as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Welch’s t test, while non-normally distributed variables were described as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Given the imbalances in key baseline indicators reflecting growth potential and skeletal maturation, such as BA and BA minus CA (BA–CA), as well as in baseline height-related parameters, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to adjust for confounding factors in the intergroup comparison of efficacy (Table S1). A matched cohort was constructed using nearest-neighbor matching with a 1:4 ratio and a caliper of 1.5. Paired-sample tests were conducted to assess baseline balance between the groups after matching. No missing data were present in the matched dataset. Subgroup analyses based on CA at treatment initiation (< 8 years vs. ≥ 8 years) were conducted in the GnRHa monotherapy cohort. To assess the independent contributions of baseline characteristics and treatment factors on AHG in the combination group, a multiple linear regression model was constructed with AHG as the dependent variable. Key covariates, including baseline age, BA–CA, and baseline height variables, were included in the model. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2). Hypothesis tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Subjects Treated with GnRHa Monotherapy and Height Outcomes at FAH

A total of 207 patients were enrolled in the GnRHa monotherapy group (Figure S1). The mean age at diagnosis was 8.70 ± 0.78 years. At treatment initiation, the CA was 8.86 ± 0.65 years, and height was 135.00 (131.05, 138.40) cm. BA was 10.00 (10.00, 10.50) years, with a BA–CA difference of 1.36 ± 0.70 years, and BMI SDS was 0.92 ± 1.07. Baseline Tht was 159.06 ± 3.59 cm, and PAH was 153.91 (149.63, 157.02) cm. The duration of GnRHa treatment was 17.87 (14.92, 22.54) months, and the age at treatment cessation was 10.43 ± 0.58 years. FAH was 161.00 (159.00, 163.00) cm. The FAH–PAH gain was 7.09 (4.39, 10.70) cm with an AHG of 1.33 SDS (0.82, 2.00), and the FAH–Tht gain was 2.00 (− 0.50, 4.50) cm with a GHG of 0.37 SDS (− 0.09, 0.84) (Table 1).

In the GnRHa group, patients were stratified into two subgroups based on their age at treatment initiation: Subgroup 1 (onset of symptoms and both treatment initiation before 8 years of age) and Subgroup 2 (onset of symptoms before 8 years of age, but treatment initiation after 8 years of age). Compared with Subgroup 2, Subgroup 1 exhibited more advanced BA and higher Ht SDS, but lower baseline height. Tht and PAH were comparable between the two subgroups. Subgroup 1 had a longer treatment duration compared to Subgroup 2. FAH was similar between the two groups. In Subgroup 2, the FAH–PAH was 7.05 (4.43, 10.35) cm, with an AHG of 1.32 SDS (0.83, 1.93); the FAH–Tht was 2.00 (− 0.88, 4.50) cm, with a GHG of 0.37 SDS (− 0.16, 0.84) (Table 1).

Characteristics of Subjects Treated With GnRHa + rhGH and Height Outcomes at FAH

A total of 26 patients were enrolled in the GnRHa + rhGH combination therapy group. Among them, 25 patients started with GnRHa treatment (Figure S2), and all received concurrent rhGH therapy for at least 6 months. Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The mean age at treatment initiation was 9.01 ± 0.80 years. Baseline height was 128.00 (124.63, 131.63) cm, Tht was 154.43 ± 4.27 cm, and PAH was 148.21 (144.87, 150.46) cm; all these measurements were significantly lower compared to those in the GnRHa monotherapy group (Table 1). BA was 10.00 (9.00, 10.00) years, and the BA–CA difference was 0.89 ± 0.90 years, indicating a less advanced BA compared to the monotherapy group. The median duration of GnRHa treatment was 23.02 months, with treatment discontinued at a mean age of 10.98 ± 0.63 years. RhGH was administered daily at a starting dose of 0.05 ± 0.01 mg/kg/day, with a median treatment duration of 14.02 (9.61, 23.94) months. The GnRHa + rhGH group showed substantial height improvements: the increase in FAH–PAH of 9.21 (6.63, 13.87) cm, an AHG of 1.72 SDS (1.24, 2.59), a GHG of 0.75 SDS (0.37, 1.50), and a ΔHt SDS of 0.15 (− 0.39, 0.35).

Linear regression models were used to assess the relationship between baseline characteristics and treatment variables and growth outcomes (AHG) in the GnRHa + rhGH group (Table 2). Ht SDS, BMI SDS, PAH SDS, and PAH SDS–Tht SDS were significantly associated with AHG, with coefficients of − 0.774, 0.685, − 0.949, and − 0.796, respectively (all P < 0.05). No statistically significant associations were observed between growth outcomes and treatment duration, the BA–CA difference, or age at treatment initiation.

Between-Group Comparison of GnRHa Monotherapy vs. GnRHa + rhGH Combination Therapy: An Exploratory Analysis

After PSM, the study included 25 patients in the GnRHa + rhGH group and 97 patients in the GnRHa monotherapy group (Table 3). After balancing BA and the BA–CA, which are key indicators of growth potential, no significant between-group differences were observed in these variables (BA: P = 0.500; BA–CA: P = 0.397). However, significant differences remained in baseline height-related parameters: the GnRHa + rhGH group had a lower baseline height (128.00 vs. 134.50 cm), Ht SDS (− 0.71 vs. 0.41), Tht (155.25 vs. 159.00 cm), and PAH (148.37 vs. 154.70 cm) than the GnRHa monotherapy group (all P < 0.001). After matching, BA was approximately 10 years, with mild advancement (BA–CA ≤ 1 year) in both groups. Analysis of FAH in the matched cohort showed that although the FAH in the GnRHa + rhGH group remained lower than that in the GnRHa monotherapy group (158.50 vs. 161.00 cm, P < 0.001), the FAH–PAH (8.57 vs. 6.53 cm, between-group difference: 2.04 cm, P = 0.020) and FAH–Tht (4.25 vs. 2.00 cm, P = 0.031) were both significantly greater in the combination group (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

Efficacy and Controversy of GnRHa Monotherapy in Girls with CPP Aged Over 8 Years

In this retrospective cohort study of Chinese girls diagnosed with CPP who initiated GnRHa treatment at an older age (mean age > 8 years), GnRHa monotherapy improved AHG (an increase of approximately 1.33 SDS, 7.09 cm). Subgroup analyses using 8 years as the cutoff value yielded largely consistent findings. These findings provide new evidence contributing to the ongoing debate about the efficacy of GnRHa initiation after 8 years. One study focusing on this population, with a median treatment initiation age of 7.92 years, reported no significant difference in FAH (cm) between the GnRHa-treated and untreated groups, concluding that the height benefit of initiating GnRHa at around age 8 years is minimal [18]. In contrast, another study compared GnRHa efficacy between girls younger than 8 years and those aged ≥ 8 years, and the results indicated that all treated patients demonstrated slowed BA advancement, improved PAH, and significant height gain both at treatment completion and near FAH [19]. Furthermore, another large-sample study with a mean treatment age of 8.5 ± 0.5 years and treatment duration ≥ 2 years reported continuous improvement in Ht SDS during follow-up, confirming short-term efficacy. However, this study lacked long-term outcome evidence as patients were not tracked to FAH [20]. Our findings are consistent with the latter two studies [19, 20], showing that GnRHa monotherapy yields significant height benefits in girls with a mean treatment initiation age of 8.86 ± 0.65 years. These discrepancies across studies may be related to differences in patient characteristics and outcome measures. Unlike previous studies, the present study traced FAH, thereby enhancing its clinical relevance. A previous study suggested that using only measures at treatment completion to evaluate efficacy may have limitations. Despite similar BA, CA, and pubertal stage at treatment completion, patients who initiated GnRHa between 6 and 8 years of age had lower residual growth potential and FAH than those who started before 6 years, likely due to their more mature growth plates [7]. This finding indicates that, even after treatment completion, height growth potential may still be independently influenced by baseline characteristics such as age at onset and the rate of bone maturation. Therefore, PAH at treatment completion does not accurately reflect true FAH. Based on this rationale, the present study focuses on actual achieved FAH-related indicators to evaluate the long-term height benefits of initiating GnRHa after 8 years of age.

Achieving FAH above Tht is one of the core objectives of treatment. In the present study, patients achieved a median FAH increase of 2 cm above Tht (73.53% of patients exceeded Tht). In contrast, a previous meta-analysis reported that patients with CPP had a mean FAH 1 cm below Tht [21]. This variation in outcomes may be attributed to patient characteristics. Guidelines identify significantly advanced BA as a core indicator of GnRHa height benefit [22]. Patients in the present study had an advanced BA (BA–CA ≥ 1 year), which may explain the more favorable outcomes. To further clarify discrepancies across studies, future research should include larger sample sizes, populations with diverse baseline characteristics, and standardized study designs.

(2) Efficacy and Influencing Factors in the Combination Therapy Group

In this study, GnRHa + rhGH therapy improved FAH. Although guidelines suggest that select patients (e.g., those with severely impaired PAH) may benefit from combination therapy [23], the retrospective design and small sample size limit our conclusions to this specific population: age at initiation 8.89 ± 1.05 years, baseline PAH SDS − 2.31 (− 2.93, − 1.89), GnRHa duration approximately 2 years, and rhGH duration 14.02 (9.61, 23.94) months.

Multivariate regression analysis with AHG as the dependent variable revealed that baseline Ht SDS, PAH SDS, and GHG are negative factors influencing the efficacy of the combination therapy. This finding is consistent with the 2015 China consensus, which recommends considering combined rhGH therapy for children with severe PAH impairment [23]. One study confirms that children with severely impaired PAH achieve greater height gain with combined therapy compared to GnRHa monotherapy (9.22 ± 6.03 cm vs. 4.72 ± 5.01 cm, P < 0.001) [24]. Furthermore, children with severe baseline height impairment also showed significantly greater improvements in PAH SDS and height gain SDS with combination therapy (both P < 0.05) [13]. Although Tht SDS is generally recognized as a critical determinant of FAH, it did not show statistical significance in this study. Similarly, the BA–CA difference and duration of rhGH therapy were not significant predictors of efficacy. Several potential explanations may explain these observations. First, the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect the effects of these factors. Second, the duration of rhGH treatment showed substantial interindividual variability (all ≥ 6 months; median 14.02 months), and the unavailability of dosage data may have further attenuated the capacity of treatment duration to explain variations in clinical outcomes. Finally, it is also possible that, under the combined treatment regimen used in this study, the degree of BA advancement and treatment duration are not critical factors influencing efficacy. These findings warrant further validation in studies with larger sample sizes.

(3) Is Combination Therapy Associated with Greater Benefits Compared to Monotherapy?

This study also investigated whether GnRHa + rhGH yields superior outcomes compared with GnRHa monotherapy, and the difference in FAH–PAH between groups was 2.04 cm, which falls within the range reported in previous studies. A prior meta-analysis reported a random-effects mean difference (MD) for height gain of 3.81 cm (95% CI 2.77–4.84; P < 0.0001; I2 = 78.8%) based on 13 studies, and an MD of 1.99 cm (95% CI 0.84–3.13; P = 0.0007; I2 = 77.2%) for FAH–Tht in 12 studies. The authors concluded that adding rhGH to GnRHa significantly improves growth outcomes in children with CPP, with optimal results observed for GnRHa duration ≥ 2 years and rhGH dose ≥ 0.3 mg/kg/week [25]. However, the meta-analysis noted high heterogeneity and did not report baseline differences between groups. Clinically, more severe height deficits may prompt the prescription of combination therapy, so observed differences could partly or entirely reflect patient characteristics rather than true treatment effects. In the present study, the treatment initiation CA and BA were comparable between groups. Despite exhibiting more severe baseline height deficits, the combination therapy group achieved significantly greater height gain. This finding indicates that additional rhGH treatment may confer additional height benefits to children with CPP receiving GnRHa treatment.

Our study represents the real-world outcomes of two treatment regimens for CPP in China. Unlike the previous study by Fu et al. [26], we specifically excluded patients diagnosed with early and fast puberty, who typically develop puberty after the age threshold (e.g., 8 years) but exhibit accelerated sexual maturation and rapid BA advancement. This often presents a narrower therapeutic window and a more complex intervention timeline, which falls beyond the scope of the current comparative effectiveness analysis and aims to avoid potential confounding factors.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective cohort study, it is subject to indication bias, which is difficult to overcome in observational comparative effectiveness research [27]. The exploratory comparative analysis between the two groups showed significant baseline differences in height-related parameters, indicating selection bias in treatment indications, which led to imbalance in key prognostic factors affecting adult height, such as baseline height and the degree of advanced BA. Although confounding factors should be adjusted using appropriate statistical methods, due to sample size constraints, this study adjusted only for BA and BA–CA, which still failed to eliminate between-group differences in baseline height. Only a limited number of covariates could be included for matching when using PSM, which was insufficient to control for all known potential confounders; thus, residual confounding may still exist.

Second, the main conclusions are based on real-world efficacy data for each treatment group. Although the data reflect real-world clinical practice, retrospective studies are inherently subject to selection bias, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The loss to follow-up rate in this study exceeded the recommended threshold of 20%, which made it impossible to use sensitivity analyses (e.g., worst-case scenario, multiple imputation) to assess the robustness of the results. Additionally, incomplete baseline data in EHR precluded analysis of missing data patterns or comparison between included and excluded participants; thus, the analyzed sample may not fully represent the target population. Furthermore, as shown in Figure S1, the timing and duration of combination therapy varied considerably. While this reflects real-world prescribing heterogeneity, it may have affected treatment outcome assessment.

Third, this study relied on EHR data and could not verify whether prescribed medications were actually administered as scheduled. Unrecorded missed doses or poor treatment adherence may affect the accuracy of exposure classification and outcome assessment. Despite these methodological limitations, the data in this study were derived from a real-world clinical population, objectively reflecting treatment patterns and height gain in a subset of children with CPP in routine clinical care. The findings can provide individualized treatment references for children with similar clinical characteristics and offer implications for population selection in future research. Further large-scale, prospective studies with standardized data collection and active follow-up are warranted to validate the present results and provide higher-level evidence for clinical decision-making in CPP.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence from a Chinese multicenter real-world cohort. In clinical practice, GnRHa monotherapy and GnRHa combined with rhGH therapy are used for distinct patient populations, with a trend toward clinical benefit observed in eligible population. GnRHa monotherapy shows potential efficacy even in children starting treatment at 8 years or older, while combination therapy suggests the possibility of further improving adult height in patients with severe baseline height impairment. However, given the methodological limitations and potential biases inherent in the study design, the above conclusions should be interpreted with caution. In clinical practice, the decision whether to add rhGH to GnRHa therapy should be individualized, considering each patient’s clinical characteristics, the economic feasibility of treatment, and higher-quality evidence.
