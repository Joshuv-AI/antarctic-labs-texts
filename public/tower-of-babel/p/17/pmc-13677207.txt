Functional glucocorticoid system in bovine oviduct epithelial cells attenuates lipopolysaccharide induced inflammatory responses
Authors: Omowumi Funmilayo ADEMOLA, Sameera PREMARATNE, Yuki MURANISHI, Hiroyuki WATANABE, Masafumi TETSUKA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bovine oviduct supports early reproductive events that require a balance between immune defense and tolerance. Glucocorticoids are well-known immune modulators; however, their presence, regulation, and functional relevance in the bovine oviduct remain poorly understood. This study investigated the presence and role of the corticosteroid system in the bovine oviduct and cultured bovine oviduct epithelial cells (BOECs). Bovine oviducts were collected during estrous cycle, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed for immunohistochemistry. BOECs were harvested from the ampulla and isthmus and cultured under sequential progesterone and estradiol treatment to mimic estrous cycles. On day 8, which equivalent to post‑ovulation day 1 in vivo, cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/ml), cortisol (100 nM), cortisone (100 nM), or their combinations for 24 h. After culture, cells were harvested for gene analysis by RT-PCR, while spent media were collected for cortisol measurement by ELISA. Immunohistochemistry revealed predominant epithelial localization of NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 in both ampulla and isthmus. During 7-day preculture, expression of NR3C1 and HSD11B1 persisted, whereas that of NR3C2 and HSD11B2 declined sharply. LPS treatment on day 8 sharply upregulated TLR2, TNF, IL1B, IL6, CXCL8 and HSD11B1, whereas the concomitant treatment with cortisol suppressed all LPS-induced cytokines. BOECs were capable to convert added cortisone to cortisol, and LPS doubled this through up-regulating HSD11B1. Locally produced cortisol also suppressed LPS-induced cytokines. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that BOECs possess a functional glucocorticoid system, capable of producing cortisol from cortisone, which in turn suppresses LPS-induced inflammation.Keywords: Bovine oviduct, Cortisol production, Glucocorticoid, Inflammation, Lipopolysaccharide

Low reproductive efficiency remains a major challenge in cattle production, with approximately 30–40% of fertilized embryos failing to establish pregnancy [1]. Infectious diseases, endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and physiological stress all contribute significantly to early embryonic loss [2]. These factors activate inflammatory pathways and elevate pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [3, 4], which can impair normal cellular functions and disrupt the oviduct microenvironment [5,6,7,8]. Notably, several physiological processes, including ovulation and sperm–oviduct interactions, exhibit features similar to inflammatory responses [9]. Therefore, the bovine oviduct, which supports gamete transport, fertilization, and early embryonic development, must maintain a delicate balance between immune defense against pathogens and immune tolerance toward sperm and embryos [10,11,12].

Emerging evidence suggests the presence of a local immunoregulatory system within the female reproductive tract that contributes to maintaining this immune balance [13, 14]. Among these systems, the corticosteroid system has gained increasing attention because of its role in stress response and immune modulation. Cortisol, the biologically active glucocorticoid, is a potent anti-inflammatory agent that suppresses cytokine production and inflammatory signaling [3, 8, 15, 16]. Its biological activity and availability are tightly regulated by key components of the corticosteroid system, including the glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1), mineralocorticoid receptor (NR3C2), and its interconverting enzymes 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (HSD11B1), which converts inactive cortisone to active cortisol, and type 2 (HSD11B2), which inactivates cortisol to cortisone [17].

Components of the corticosteroid system have been identified in several reproductive tissues, including the bovine oviductal ampulla, endometrium, and uterine wall [18,19,20], and their expression changes in response to different stimuli [18, 21, 22]. However, their expression patterns, regulatory mechanisms, and functional relevance within the bovine oviduct remain unclear. It also remains unclear whether the oviduct can respond to corticosteroid signaling, locally produce cortisol in a manner similar to the uterus [23], or modulate inflammatory responses during physiological or microbial challenges.

Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for understanding how the oviduct, as the first maternal environment encountered by the oocytes, spermatozoa, and newly formed embryo [10], maintains immune homeostasis while protecting gametes and early embryos from inflammation-induced damages. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: 1) determine the tissue localization of corticosteroid system components in the bovine oviduct; 2) characterize their temporal expression in cultured bovine oviduct epithelial cells (BOECs); and 3) investigate their functional relevance during LPS-induced inflammation.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (Approval No. 24-13).

Collection of bovine oviducts

For immunohistochemistry, oviduct samples (ampulla and isthmus) from stages I, III, and IV were obtained at a local abattoir and processed to visualize the localization patterns of NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2. The reproductive stage was determined based on corpus luteum morphology as previously described [24]. They were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) overnight at 4ºC, and stored in 0.4% (v/v) PFA at 4ºC. Prior to embedding, oviduct samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70–100%; Muto Pure Chemicals, Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), cleared in Tissue-Clear (Sakura Finetek Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and embedded in paraffin (FUJIFILM Wako).

For cell culture study, pairs of healthy mid‑luteal phase oviducts (stage III) were collected from cows (n = 3–4 at a time). Immediately after collection, the oviducts were rinsed in sterile saline supplemented with penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml) (Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), placed on ice, and transported to the laboratory. Upon arrival, the oviducts were washed twice with saline, and briefly rinsed with 70% ethanol before further processing.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin embedded oviduct samples were sectioned at 5 µm. Sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase, followed by antigen retrieval using target retrieval solution (Dakocytomation S169984-2, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in an autoclave (LSX-300; TOMY SEIKO CO., LTD, Tokyo, Japan) at 121°C for 15 min. After blocking with 5% goat serum (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) for 1 h, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 (rabbit polyclonal; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA; 24050-1-AP, 21854-1-AP, 10928-1-AP, 14192-1-AP; dilutions 1:300, 1:300, 1:300, and 1:50, respectively). Negative controls were incubated without primary antibodies; positive controls consisted of mouse liver and kidney. Sections were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (NB7160, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA), visualized with DAB substrate (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan), counterstained with hematoxylin (FUJIFILM Wako), rehydrated, and mounted. Images were captured using a Nikon DS-Fi1 digital camera attached to a Nikon Optiphot‑2 microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at × 40 and × 400 magnification under identical acquisition setting. Using ImageJ Fiji (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), images were uniformly adjusted for brightness. DAB staining intensity was quantified by extracting DAB optical density values in QuPath [25], while mean pixel intensity was obtained with ImageJ Fiji and converted to optical density using the formula log10(I0/I1), where I0 = 255 represented background intensity and I1 was the measured mean intensity [26]. Ten representative regions were analyzed per animal (n = 3) per stage.

Primary oviductal cell isolation and culture

After removing surrounding connective tissue, oviducts were separated into the ampulla and isthmus. Epithelial cells were gently stripped using tweezers into Ca2+/Mg2+‑free PBS (D‑PBS; Sigma‑Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Suspensions were collected and centrifuged (365 g, 5 min). Cells were digested with collagenase (0.2 mg/ml; FUJIFILM Wako) at 37°C for 30 min with gentle agitation. The supernatant was removed and cells were washed three times with PBS. Viability and cell number were assessed using Trypan Blue (FUJIFILM Wako) and cell counting chamber (Watson Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) respectively. Cells were resuspended in 10 ml D‑MEM/Ham’s F‑12 medium (FUJIFILM Wako), supplemented with 2 mML‑glutamine, penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 µg/ml; FUJIFILM Wako), Amphotericin B (250 µg/ml; FUJIFILM Wako), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FetalClone III; HyClone, Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA). Cells (3–6 × 105) were seeded into 50 ml culture flasks (Wuxi Nest Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) and incubated at 37.5°C with 5% CO2.

Hormonal treatment of cultured BOECs

To mimic the in vivo hormonal environment of the bovine estrous cycle, cells were cultured under sequential progesterone (P4) and estradiol (E2) supplementation for seven days, following hormone concentrations reported for bovine oviductal fluid [27]. Cells received P4 (100 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) on days 1–4; a combination of P4 (50 ng/ml) and E2 (50 pg/ml) on day 5; and E2 (100 pg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) on day 6. On day 7, cells were detached with Accumax (Innovative Cell Technologies, San Diego, CA, USA) and reseeded at 2 × 105 cells/500 µl /well in 24‑well plates (Nunc; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, NY, USA) in steroid‑free medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h. On day 8, equivalent to post‑ovulation day 1 in vivo, cells were subjected to the designated experimental treatments.

Experimental design

Temporal mRNA expression of corticosteroid related genes in BOECs during 7 days of culture: The expression profiles of NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2, were examined in BOECs during the culture period. Cells were harvested on days 0, 4, 5, 6, and 7 for gene expression analysis using RT-PCR.

Effect of added cortisol on LPS-induced inflammatory genes expression in BOECs: On day 8, BOECs were treated with LPS (1 µg/ml; Escherichia coli 026: B6; Cat No. 00-4976-93; Thermo Fisher Scientific), cortisol (100 nM; Sigma‑Aldrich), or their combination for 24 h. The group without LPS or cortisol served as the control. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were collected for further analysis. Concentrations of LPS and cortisol were selected based on those reported in vivo [27,28,29,30] and those commonly used in vitro study [3, 8, 15, 16].

Effect of added cortisone on LPS-induced inflammatory genes expression in BOECs and HSD11B1 activity: On day 8, BOECs were treated with LPS (1 µg/ml), cortisone (100 nM; Sigma‑Aldrich), or their combination for 24 h. Untreated cells served as controls. Following treatment, cells were harvested for gene expression analysis and spent media were collected for cortisol production assay. To assess whether BOECs have the enzymatic capacity to synthesize cortisol from upstream substrates such as progesterone, we examined the expression of two key steroidogenic enzymes required for this pathway, CYP11B1, and CYP21A2.

cDNA synthesis and real-time PCR: Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The primer-BLAST tool of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) was used to design primers as shown in Supplementary Table 1. The PCR conditions were as follows: activation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by thermal denaturation at 95°C for 20 sec, annealing at 60°C for 20 sec, and extension at 72°C for 20 sec, for a total of 45 cycles. Cycle quantification (Cq) values were transformed, and the relative abundance of the target genes was normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Oviductal fluid collection: Oviduct fluids were collected from stages I, III and IV oviducts as previously described [27]. Briefly, after the oviducts (n = 3) were trimmed of surrounding tissues and separated (ampulla and isthmus), the epithelial cells were gently stripped out and collected into a tube without the addition of PBS. The oviductal fluid was separated from the epithelial cells by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again under the same conditions to remove any residual cell debris. The clarified fluid samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Cortisol measurement in spent culture media and oviductal fluid by ELISA: Cortisol concentrations in spent media were measured using a commercial cortisol ELISA kit (Cayman Chemical Co., Ann Arbor, MI, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Before the assay, the samples were diluted 1:10 in the assay buffer. Blank medium was also assayed, and the values were subtracted from the assay results, where appropriate. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were below 10%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software [31]. Data were log-transformed before analysis when not normally distributed. DAB quantification data were analyzed using one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Temporal gene expression data were analyzed using one‑way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. The effects of LPS, cortisol and cortisone were evaluated using two‑way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM; n = 3 biological replicates), and differences between groups were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Tissue localization of the corticosteroid system components in bovine oviduct

NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 were predominantly localized to the epithelial compartment of both the ampulla and isthmus in stages I, III, IV oviducts similarly (Figs. 1 and 2 for stage I, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 for stages III and IV, respectively). NR3C1 (Figs. 1A, B) exhibited nuclear localization in epithelial cells, NR3C2 also displayed nuclear localization in both regions (Figs. 2A, B). HSD11B1 and HSD11B2 were predominantly detected around the apical region of the epithelium facing the lumen (Figs. 1C, D; Figs. 2C, D). The negative controls showed no positive staining (Figs. 1E, F; Figs. 2E, F).

Quantitative analysis of DAB immunostaining for NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1 and HSD11B2

In the ampulla (black bars), QuPath analysis shows that NR3C1 was higher in stages I and IV compared with stage III (Supplementary Fig. 3A), NR3C2 was higher in stage I than stage III and IV (Supplementary Fig. 3B), while HSD11B1 and HSD11B2 were significant across all stages, with the highest level observed in stage I and IV respectively (Supplementary Figs. 3C, D). In the isthmus (white bars), NR3C1 and HSD11B1 levels were significant across all stages (Supplementary Figs. 3A, C), while NR3C2 and HSD11B2 were significantly lower in stage III (Supplementary Figs. 3B, D). Quantification using ImageJ Fiji shows that in the ampulla, NR3C1, NR3C2 and HSD11B1 (Supplementary Figs. 3E–G) were significantly higher in stages I and IV compared to stage III, while HSD11B2 (Supplementary Fig. 3H) was significant across all stages with the highest expression at stage IV. In the isthmus, NR3C2, HSD11B1 and HSD11B2 (Supplementary Figs. 3F–H) levels varied significantly across all estrous stages, whereas NR3C1 (Supplementary Fig. 3E) showed a significant decrease in stage III.

Cortisol concentration in oviductal fluid

Cortisol was detected in oviductal fluid from all stages. Cortisol concentration was significantly higher (P < 0.05) in stages III and IV in the ampulla and isthmus compared to stage I (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Temporal expression of NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 in cultured BOECs

Figure. 3 shows the relative mRNA expression profiles of NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 over a 7-day culture period. NR3C1 expression gradually declined for 5 and 6 days in the ampulla and isthmus, respectively (Figs. 3A, E), then resurged toward the end of the culture period. HSD11B1 expression declined during the early culture period in both the ampulla and isthmus, then began to recover from day 4 onward toward the end of the culture period (Figs. 3C, G). In contrast, NR3C2 and HSD11B2 expression progressively declined without recovery in both regions (Figs. 3B, F, D, H). Effect of added cortisol on LPS-induced inflammatory genes expression in BOECs

LPS treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of TLR2, TNF, IL1B, IL6, CXCL8, and HSD11B1 in both ampulla and isthmus BOECs (P < 0.001). Cortisol treatment alone suppressed HSD11B1 in the ampulla and NR3C1, IL1B, IL6, and CXCL8 in both regions. Co‑treatment with LPS and cortisol suppressed the expression of LPS-induced cytokines in both ampulla and isthmus (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The bovine oviduct plays a central role in early reproductive events requiring a tightly controlled balance between immune surveillance against pathogens and tolerance towards spermatozoa and developing embryos [11, 12, 22, 32]. The present study provides comprehensive evidence that the bovine oviduct possesses an innate corticosteroid regulatory system, and that BOECs harbor a functional glucocorticoid signaling machinery capable of modulating inflammatory responses through local cortisol production. Importantly, this cortisol is produced from cortisone via the metabolic activity of HSD11B1, as there was no detectable expression of CYP21A2 and CYP11B1, key enzymes that catalyze cortisol production from progesterone.

Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated that key corticosteroid system components NR3C1, NR3C2, HSD11B1, and HSD11B2 are primarily localized within the oviductal epithelium of both the ampulla and isthmus. This distribution indicates that the bovine oviduct possesses the structural and enzymatic framework required for corticosteroid signaling and action. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the expression of these proteins in the oviduct is regulated in an estrous cycle stage-dependent manner, with higher staining intensity observed at stages I and IV compared with stage III, indicating a clear cyclical variation. A similar estrous cycle stage–dependent pattern has previously been reported for the mRNA expression of corticosteroid related genes in bovine ampulla tissue [18].

The nuclear localization of NR3C1 supports its role as a ligand-activated transcription factor [33, 34]. The presence of HSD11B1 indicates the capacity for local production of biologically active cortisol from cortisone, thereby enabling the tissue-level regulation of glucocorticoid availability [17, 22]. NR3C2 regulates epithelial ion transport and luminal fluid composition [35]. These epithelial processes are essential for establishing the oviductal environment conducive for gamete transport, sperm reservoir formation, fertilization, and early embryo development [11]. HSD11B2 converts cortisol to its inactive form, cortisone, thereby protecting NR3C2 from glucocorticoid activation, and conferring ligand specificity to mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone [36]. The co-localization of NR3C2 with HSD11B2 indicates that the mineralocorticoid system contributes to maintaining the ionic and osmotic microenvironment required for successful reproductive events while preserving signaling specificity between mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid pathways [37].

Despite the relatively high expression of HSD11B1 in stage I, the cortisol concentrations in oviductal fluid at this stage were lower than those at other stages. This discrepancy may be explained, at least in part, by a dilution effect resulting from increased oviductal fluid volume. Oviductal fluid volume changes markedly across the estrous cycle and is elevated during the peri ovulatory period under the influence of estradiol (E2) [38, 39]. In this context, a potential role for mineralocorticoids in regulating fluid and ion transport during this stage warrants further investigation [35]. Taken together, the local glucocorticoid milieu within the oviduct appears to be regulated through a complex interplay between systemic and local cortisol availability as well as dynamic changes in fluid balance.

Temporal expression analysis during 7 days of primary culture revealed dynamic changes in corticosteroid-related gene expression. The initial decline and recovery in expression of NR3C1 and HSD11B1, likely reflects the transient loss and subsequent acquisition of its epithelial features as primary epithelial cells adapt to in vitro conditions [12, 40, 41]. In contrast, NR3C2 and HSD11B2 expression progressively declined without recovery in vitro despite its clear in vivo expression. This disparity suggests that mineralocorticoid signaling is highly dependent on the in vivo oviductal microenvironment and structural support [42]. Furthermore, downregulation of HSD11B2in vitro may functionally favor glucocorticoid activity by reducing cortisol inactivation, thereby enhancing NR3C1-mediated anti-inflammatory responses [36]. Overall, the maintained expression of NR3C1 and HSD11B1 implies that glucocorticoid mediated immunoregulation is more cell-autonomous, whereas NR3C2 signaling is more tightly linked to physiological epithelial transport functions.

The functional relevance of the glucocorticoid system was investigated in this study by treating BOECs with LPS to induce inflammation in vitro. Exposure of BOECs to LPS (1 μg/ml) elicited a strong upregulation in expression of pro-inflammatory markers, including TLR2, TNF, IL1B, IL6, and CXCL8. This confirms the high sensitivity of BOECs to endotoxin stimulation, consistent with previous reports [4, 43, 44]. Although TLR4 mRNA expression was not upregulated by LPS in our study, basal level of TLR4 is sufficient to detect LPS and induce cytokine production [45, 46]. Cytokines play essential roles in reproductive processes and in regulating the oviduct microenvironment [47, 48]. Low levels of cytokines are beneficial, as they coordinate communication among gametes, embryo, and the oviduct [49], and contribute to immune surveillance [50]. However, excessive cytokine production can impair normal cellular functions via oxidative stress and apoptosis [7, 47,48,49,50,51].

LPS also increased HSD11B1 expression and cortisol production in BOECs which in turn suppressed LPS induced cytokine expression. This demonstrates that inflammatory stimuli can upregulate HSD11B1 [52, 53], and enhance cortisol production [54] to constrain excessive inflammatory responses. Although only 10% of added cortisone was converted to cortisol, this was sufficient for suppressing LPS effect on cytokine production. The cortisol concentrations found in the spent media (0.7–2 ng/ml) were comparable to the concentration range in the oviductal fluid in this study (4–10 ng/ml), or even lower than previously reported values (35–58 ng/ml) [27], indicating that the bovine oviduct contains sufficient levels of cortisol for suppressing LPS effect at any stage in vivo.

In conclusion, this study shows that BOECs possess an active glucocorticoid regulatory system capable of producing cortisol locally via HSD11B1 and effectively suppressing inflammatory response. The locally inducible cortisol provides an autocrine/paracrine negative-feedback mechanism, and together with systemically delivered cortisol, enables the oviduct to mount a finely tuned autonomous immune response that defends against pathogens while maintaining tolerance towards gametes and early embryos. Collectively, our findings offer a mechanistic framework for how the oviduct maintains immune balance and protects its microenvironment from inflammatory insults, ultimately supporting reproductive success.
