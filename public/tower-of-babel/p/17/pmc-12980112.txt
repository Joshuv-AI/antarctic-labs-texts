A High‐Resolution Transcriptomic Atlas of Cell Types in the Ventral Visual Thalamus
Authors: Katelyn Stebbins, Maira Jalil, Parsa Khaksar, Addison N Webster, John Campbell, Michael A Fox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAxons from retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) convey visual information from the retina to distinct regions within the mammalian brain, including several nuclei within the visual thalamus. In rodents, the visual thalamus contains three primary retinorecipient regions: the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN), the ventral LGN (vLGN), and the intergeniculate leaflet (IGL). While principal retinorecipient neurons in dLGN are excitatory thalamocortical relay cells (TRCs), most neurons in vLGN are GABAergic, including GABAergic projection neurons. Previous studies suggest that both dLGN and vLGN neurons may be specified into parallel visual pathways for processing and transmitting to select cortical and subcortical brain regions, respectively. While dLGN neurons have been studied extensively, prior studies suggest that additional cell types remain to be identified and characterized in vLGN. Here, we used transcriptomic profiling to generate the first comprehensive and high‐resolution atlas for cell types in the mouse vLGN. This atlas was generated based on a single nucleus‐sequencing (snRNA‐Seq) dataset that contained data from over 16 500 nuclei. We systematically analyzed neuronal and non‐neuronal cell types across vLGN based on gene expression. The results revealed the identity of 20 potential types of neurons in the mouse vLGN, including at least two excitatory neuronal types. This comprehensive transcriptomic atlas of mouse vLGN establishes a benchmark reference atlas and a foundational resource for deep and integrative investigations of cell type and circuit function, development, and evolution of an essential nucleus of the mammalian visual system. Keywords: GABAergic neuron, retinal ganglion cell, single‐nucleus transcriptomics, subcortical visual system, thalamus, ventral lateral geniculate nucleus

Introduction

When light enters the eye and falls upon photoreceptors, phototransduction occurs, converting light‐derived signals into electrical signals that can be transmitted in neural tissue. This neural activity is transmitted from photoreceptors to bipolar cells and then to retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), the only projection neurons in the retina. Axons from these RGCs convey these neural signals from the retina to dozens of retinorecipient brain areas, including three nuclei of the mouse visual thalamus (Monavarfeshani et al. 2017): the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN), the intergeniculate leaflet (IGL), and the ventral LGN (vLGN) (Kolb et al. 1995; Monavarfeshani et al. 2017).

Decades of anatomical and functional studies have revealed that neurons in the mouse dLGN are incorporated into neural circuits that are critical for processing and transferring image‐forming visual information from the retina to the primary visual cortex (Guido 2018; Huberman et al. 2008). Principal retinorecipient neurons of mouse dLGN are thalamocortical relay cells (TRCs) (Guido 2018). These excitatory neurons make up the majority (80%–90%) of neurons in dLGN and can be divided into three morphologically distinct types: W‐like TRCs which are hemispheric in shape, X‐like TRCs which are biconical in shape, and Y‐like TRCs which are radially symmetrical in shape (Krahe et al. 2011). While axons from distinct types of RGCs appear to project into a “hidden” laminar organization within mouse dLGN (Martin 1986; Reese 1988; Hong and Chen 2011), the three types of TRCs do not demonstrate clear laminar distribution. Instead, W‐like cells occupy the shell region of dLGN, while X‐ and Y‐like cells occupy the core of the mouse dLGN (Krahe et al. 2011). Finally, the other resident retinorecipient neuronal type in dLGN is the GABAergic (inhibitory) interneuron. Interneurons comprise 10%–20% of the total neuron population and are evenly distributed throughout dLGN (Jaubert‐Miazza et al. 2005; Seabrook et al. 2013; Charalambakis et al. 2019). GABAergic interneurons in dLGN appear relatively homogeneous in terms of their distribution, morphology, and gene expression, with just a single study to date suggesting that more than one subtype of interneuron may reside in mouse dLGN (Leist et al. 2016; but see Müllner and Roska 2024).

In contrast to dLGN, far less is known about neuronal cell types in mouse vLGN. What is known is that circuits and cells in vLGN are different than those in their dorsal counterpart: the majority of neurons in vLGN are GABAergic, not glutamatergic; the vLGN is composed of two main divisions—the external vLGN (vLGNe) which is retinorecipient and the internal vLGN (vLGNi) which is not retinorecipient; vLGN neurons (in either vLGNe or vLGNi) do not project axons to neocortex; visual circuits associated with neurons in vLGN are primarily associated with non‐image‐forming visual functions (Monavarfeshani et al. 2017; Sabbagh et al. 2021). Emerging optogenetic and behavioral studies are now beginning to elucidate some of the functional roles for circuits in vLGN. This includes functions in light‐mediated programming of circadian cycles, mood disorders, eye and head movement in response to changes in the visual environment, and innate fear responses (Harrington 1997; Huang et al. 2019, 2021; Fratzl et al. 2021; Salay and Huberman 2021). However, fully understanding how visual information is processed and transmitted in vLGN and vLGN‐associated circuits requires a detailed characterization of the full cohort of neurons in this retinorecipient brain region.

Recently, our lab set about identifying and characterizing GABAergic neuronal types in vLGN (Sabbagh et al. 2021). Not only could we identify distinct transcriptional profiles of neuron types in vLGNe and vLGNi, but we also demonstrated that four types of neurons in vLGN were organized into distinct layers in vLGNe. These subtype‐specific neuronal layers were composed of molecularly and functionally distinct neurons, several of which receive direct monosynaptic input from the retina (Sabbagh et al. 2021). However, the data suggested that we had not yet identified the complete set of vLGN neuron types. Moreover, the data raised questions about whether any excitatory neurons were present in vLGN. The lack of a complete catalog of the types of neurons in vLGN is making it difficult to understand the circuits underlying these potential functional behaviors. This current gap has also precluded the field from utilizing molecular and transgenic tools used to study dLGN to probe vLGN function. Identifying the structure and function of neural circuits related to non‐image‐forming vision is crucial for understanding vLGN function and the presence of potentially parallel pathways. Here, we sought to create the first atlas of cell types in mouse vLGN. Using single‐nuclei transcriptomics, we identified at least 20 neuronal types in vLGN that expressed different constellations of gene transcripts. We then used transsynaptic viral tracing to test which types are retinorecipient. We discovered that at least 13 of the 20 identified neuronal types in vLGN receive direct input from the retina. We also characterized distinct types of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in this dataset. Taken together, this study provides a comprehensive catalog of transcriptomically defined cells in vLGN that will open doors to study subcortical visual circuits and gain a better understanding of how vLGN contributes to visual behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Wild‐type C57BL/6 and H2b‐TRAP mice (strain #029789; RRID:IMSR_JAX:029789) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Mice were maintained under temperature‐controlled conditions on a 12 h light/dark cycle, housed in groups of 3–5 per cage, with free access to food and water. Experiments included both male and female animals. All procedures were conducted in compliance with regulations approved by the Virginia Tech Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #21–130). Unless otherwise specified, n denotes the number of animals, with a minimum of three age‐ and genotype‐matched mice used for each experiment. The ages of animals used are indicated for each experiment.

Intraocular Injection of Adeno‐Associated Virus

Monosynaptic labeling of retinorecipient neurons in the vLGN was achieved using bilateral intravitreal injections of pENN.AAV.hSyn.Cre.WPRE.hGH (2.5 × 1013 GC/mL; Addgene viral prep #105553‐AAV1; RRID:Addgene_105 553; referred to here as AAV1‐Cre). Mice were anesthetized with 2%–4% isoflurane delivered by inhalation in an induction chamber. Once animals reached a deep plane of anesthesia, confirmed by the absence of reflexes and lack of response to toe or tail pinch, a Picospritzer fitted with a fine glass micropipette was used to deliver 1–2 μL of AAV1‐Cre into each eye at approximately a 45° angle relative to the optic axis of P14 H2B‐TRAP mice. Because the injections were brief, minimally invasive, and non‐surgical, additional analgesia was not required. Bilateral injections were performed to increase the likelihood of retinal ganglion cell infection and subsequent trans‐synaptic labeling in the vLGN. After injection, animals were observed in a recovery cage for 45 min to assess signs of discomfort or eye irritation before being returned to the colony. No animals displayed acute distress following intraocular injections. Injection sites and overall health—including signs such as lethargy, hunched posture, or poor grooming—were assessed daily until euthanasia, with no adverse effects noted. Animals were then allowed to recover in their home cages for 2–3 weeks to permit viral spread. A total of 28 mice received intraocular injections, and no animals required euthanasia due to procedural complications.

Preparation of Tissue and Immunohistochemistry

As described previously (Sabbagh et al. 2021), mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal administration of tribromoethanol (Avertin) prepared at a stock concentration of 12.5 μg/mL. Avertin was made by dissolving 2.5 g tribromoethanol in 5 mL 2‐methyl‐2‐butanol and diluting the solution with 200 mL distilled water adjusted to pH 7.4. The solution was passed through a 0.2‐μm Millipore filter, stored protected from light at 4°C, and used within 2 weeks of preparation. Animals received a lethal dose of Avertin (400 mg/kg, i.p.), after which they were transcardially perfused with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, pH 7.4) (Su et al. 2010). Brains were post‐fixed overnight at 4°C in 4% PFA and subsequently cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS for a minimum of 48 h. Tissue was embedded in Tissue Freezing Medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences), and 16‐μm coronal sections were collected using a Leica CM1850 cryostat. After air‐drying for 30 min, sections were incubated for 30 min in immunohistochemistry blocking solution containing 2.5% bovine serum albumin, 5% normal goat serum, and 0.1% Triton‐X in PBS. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody against GFAP (1:1000; catalog #Z0334, Dako; RRID:AB_10013382) diluted in blocking buffer at 4°C for > 18 h. Following PBS washes, fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. After additional washes, sections were counterstained with DAPI (1:5000 in water) and mounted using Vectashield (Vector Laboratories; RRID:AB_2336789).

Riboprobe Production

Riboprobes targeting Lypd1 (Lypd1‐F: AAGGGAGTCTTTTTGTTCCCTC; Lypd1‐R: TACAACGTGTCCTCTCAGCAGT; NM_145100.4, nucleotides 849–1522; NM_007492.4, nucleotides 1830–2729), Ecel1 (Ecel1‐F: CGCGCTCTTCTCGCTTAC; Ecel1‐R: GGAGGAGCCACGAGGATT; NM_001277925.1, nucleotides 942–1895), Penk (Penk‐F: TTCCTGAGGCTTTGCACC; Penk‐R: TCACTGCTGGAAAAGGGC; NM_001002927.3, nucleotides 312–1111), and Arx (Arx‐F: CTGAGGCTCAAGGCTAAGGAG; Arx‐R: GGTTTCCGAAGCCTCTACAGTT; NM_007492.4, nucleotides 1830–2729) were produced in‐house. cDNAs were generated using the SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (catalog #18064014, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol and amplified by PCR with primers corresponding to the indicated gene regions. PCR products were gel purified and cloned into the pGEM‐T Easy Vector using the pGEM‐T Easy Vector System (catalog #A1360, Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Antisense riboprobes were generated from 5 μg of linearized plasmid DNA using digoxigenin‐ or fluorescein‐labeled UTPs (catalog #11685619910; #11277073910, Roche) and the MAXIscript In Vitro Transcription Kit (catalog #AM1312, Ambion). To reduce probe length, 5 μg of synthesized riboprobe (20 μL) was hydrolyzed to approximately 0.5 kb fragments by incubation at 60°C in a reaction containing 80 μL water, 4 μL 1 M NaHCO₃, and 6 μL 1 M Na2CO3. The resulting RNA fragments were ethanol precipitated and resuspended in RNase‐free water.

In Situ Hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) was performed on 16‐μm‐thin cryosections (Su et al. 2010; Monavarfeshani et al. 2018). Sections were first allowed to air‐dry for 1 h at room temperature and washed with PBS for 5 min to remove freezing media. They were then fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min, washed with PBS for 15 min, incubated in proteinase K solution for 10 min, washed with PBS for 5 min, incubated in 4% PFA for 5 min, washed with PBS for 15 min, incubated in acetylation solution for 10 min, washed with PBS for 10 min, incubated in PBS‐diluted 0.1% triton for 30 min, washed with PBS for 40 min, incubated in 0.3% H2O2 for 30 min, washed with PBS for 10 min, prehybridized with hybridization solution for 1 h, and then hybridized with heat‐denatured riboprobes at 62.5°C overnight. The sections were then washed five times in 0.2× SSC buffer at 65°C. Slides were then washed with TBS, blocked, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐DIG (catalog #11207733910, Roche, RRID:AB_514500) or anti‐fluorescent antibodies (#11426346910, Roche, RRID:AB_840257) overnight at 4°C. Finally, the bound riboprobes were detected using a tyramide signal amplification system (catalog #NEL753001KT, PerkinElmer).

Single‐Nuclei RNA‐Sequencing

As previously reported (Schwalbe et al. 2024), mice were rapidly decapitated and brains were immediately removed, briefly chilled in ice‐cold PBS, and coronally sectioned at 300 μm intervals spanning the full rostral–caudal extent of the vLGN (Bregma −2.0 to −2.9 mm). Sections were submerged in ice‐cold RNAlater (Ambion, catalog #AM7020) to stabilize RNA during subsequent microdissection and storage. Following a minimum incubation of 30 min in RNAlater, the vLGN was identified under a dissection stereomicroscope (Leica S6 E Greenough) and microdissected into pre‐chilled microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were stored at −80°C for no longer than 2 weeks.

After collection of all samples, tissue was dounce homogenized and processed into a single‐nucleus suspension using density‐gradient centrifugation, as described previously (Habib et al. 2016; Todd et al. 2020). Nuclei were sorted by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) using an 80 μm nozzle to isolate GFP+/mCherry+ nuclei into 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Requiring GFP co‐expression during sorting increased confidence that collected nuclei originated from live, actively translating neurons rather than autofluorescent debris or dead cells. Libraries were prepared using the 10X Genomics Chromium Next GEM Automated Single Cell 3′ cDNA Kit v3.1 following established protocols (Zheng et al. 2017). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 550 using high‐output 75‐cycle v2.5 kits. Reads were demultiplexed with bcl2fastq2 v2.20.0 (Illumina) and aligned to the mm10‐2020‐A mouse genome using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v5.0.0, 10X Genomics).

Gene expression matrices generated by Cell Ranger were analyzed in Seurat v5.1.0 using R (v4.4.1). Genes detected in fewer than three cells, cells containing fewer than 200 genes, and cells with greater than 1% mitochondrial read content were excluded (Figure S1). Downstream processing included log normalization, identification of the 2000 most variable genes using FindVariableFeatures(), data scaling, and dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis (PCA). Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm based on Euclidean distances in PCA space defined by the first 20 principal components, with a resolution of 0.5. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for two‐dimensional visualization (Armstrong et al. 2021). PCA and resolution parameters for each regional dataset are provided in Figure S1. Relationships among clusters were visualized using dendrograms generated with the BuildClusterTree() function in Seurat.

Differential gene expression was assessed using Seurat's FindAllMarkers() function, which ranks genes based on expression levels and the proportion of nuclei expressing each gene within (pct.1) and outside (pct.2) the cluster of interest. Cluster specificity was defined by high pct.1 values (approximately ≥ 0.8) combined with low pct.2 values (approximately ≤ 0.2), reflecting genes broadly expressed within a cluster but minimally expressed elsewhere. Notably, lower expression in pct.2 was more predictive of strong cluster specificity than higher pct.1 values. Adjusted P values from the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were not used for marker selection, as the top‐ranked genes consistently exhibited very low adjusted P values that did not necessarily correlate with marker performance. Instead, genes were ranked based on the difference between pct.1 and pct.2 to identify candidate cluster‐enriched markers.

Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) provides several advantages over alternative sequencing approaches, including the ability to isolate nuclei from brain tissue without protease‐based dissociation that may perturb mRNA expression (Lacar et al. 2016; Saunders et al. 2018), reduced cell number requirements for subtype identification (approximately 50–100 nuclei per cell type), and scalability to thousands of nuclei. Additionally, the use of AAV1‐Cre offers benefits relative to other synaptic viral strategies, including low toxicity, robust Cre expression across retinal cell types (Martersteck et al. 2017), and reliable monosynaptic anterograde transsynaptic spread (Zingg et al. 2022).

Imaging

All imaging for quantification was performed on a confocal Zeiss LSM 700 microscope at 20× magnification and 0.5 digital zoom. Each representative image in the figure is a maximum‐intensity projection. Colocalization was confirmed using single‐plane images. Cell counts were obtained from the three sections with the largest overall area within the middle of vLGN, using the hippocampus pyramidal cell layer as a guide for consistency between animals.

Results

snRNA‐Seq Identifies Distinct Neuronal and Glial Cell Types in vLGN

Our goal was to create a comprehensive catalog of transcriptomically‐defined cells in vLGN, including identifying which of those cells were directly innervated by retinal axons. To identify vLGN cells that receive direct input from the retina, we binocularly injected an anterograde transsynaptic viral tracing tool (AAV1‐Cre) in which Cre recombinase (Cre) is selectively transferred through one synaptic connection (Figure 1A). We injected AAV‐Cre into transgenic reporter mice (H2B‐TRAP mice) whose cells will express mCherry in the presence of Cre (Figure 1A,B). We then isolated mCherry+ cell nuclei (Figure 1B) from dissected vLGN tissue samples, using flow cytometry to filter out likely cell doublets (Figure 1C).

To molecularly identify all vLGN neurons and those labeled following intraocular AAV injection, we performed high‐throughput single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐Seq). We chose to profile nuclei instead of whole cells because it causes less stress on the cells during the isolation process, which can lead to more accurate gene expression data (Wu et al. 2019; Denisenko et al. 2020). Importantly, a cell's nuclear RNA profile is sufficient to identify its molecular type (Habib et al. 2016; Lake et al. 2017; Bakken et al. 2021).

To capture sufficient cell nuclei to identify all cell types in the ventral visual thalamus, we micro‐dissected the vLGN from 28 mice (aged between postnatal day [P] 30–40). After extracting the cell nuclei from the tissue by dounce homogenization, we used an Optiprep density gradient to purify nuclei and FACS to collect unlabeled nuclei into a collection tube. Using the 10X Genomics Single Cell Controller for Single Cell 3′ Gene Expression Profiling, we generated cDNA libraries and sequenced as previously described (Schwalbe et al. 2024; Webster et al. 2024). Altogether, we recovered transcriptomes from 16 500 cell nuclei from vLGN. Filtering out likely cell doublets and low‐quality transcriptomes left 10 266 nuclei. Grouping these cells based on high‐variance transcripts yielded 22 unique clusters, shown in a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) representation in Figure 1D. The number of each cluster represents the rank order of the cluster size based on the number of nuclei sequenced (cluster 0 has the most nuclei; cluster 21 has the fewest).

To understand what each of these clusters of cells represents, we explored the expression of genes known to be associated with specific cell types in the mammalian brain. We assessed the expression of (1) pan‐neuronal genes Syn1 and Rbfox3 (which encodes NeuN); (2) inhibitory neuron genes Gad1 and Gad2 (which encode glutamic acid decarboxylase [GAD] 67 and GAD65, respectively); (3) excitatory neuron genes Slc17a6 and Slc17a7 (which encode vesicular glutamate transporter (VGLUT) 2 and VGLUT1, respectively); (4) the oligodendrocyte gene Olig1 (which encodes the transcription factor Olig1); (5) the pericyte gene Vtn (which encodes vitronectin); (6) the macrophage gene Cx3cr1 (which encodes the C‐X3‐C motif chemokine receptor 1, a receptor for fractalkine); (7) the microglia gene Mrc1 (which encodes mannose receptor C‐type (MRC) 1); (8) the astrocyte gene Aldoc (which encodes aldolase C); and (9) the endothelial cell gene Cldn5 (which encodes claudin‐5) (Figure 1E). Importantly, each of these marker genes was restricted to specific clusters and expressed by most if not all cells within those clusters (Figure 1F). In total, we identified two clusters as astrocytes, three as oligodendrocytes, one each as endothelial cells, macrophages, and microglia, thirteen as neurons, and one ambiguous cluster containing cells that did not uniformly express marker genes for one of the previously named cell types (Figure 1G). This raised the possibility of distinct types of oligodendrocytes and astrocytes in vLGN, which has been suggested but not clearly demonstrated. It also indicated that at least 13 different types of neurons exist in vLGN, far more than have been estimated by prior studies (Sabbagh et al. 2021).

Glial Diversity in vLGN

Among non‐neuronal cell populations, oligodendrocytes were the most abundant class profiled. We further explored the molecular heterogeneity of vLGN oligodendrocytes by separating the 4036 nuclei identified by expression of Olig1 from the overall dataset and re‐clustering them apart from non‐oligodendrocytes. This resulted in six unique clusters of vLGN oligodendrocytes (Figure 2A). By separating and re‐analyzing these clusters, we gained additional power to tease apart transcriptionally distinct cell types. We found that some known oligodendrocyte genes were expressed by nuclei in all six clusters (i.e., Olig1) whereas some were uniquely expressed by only a subset of clusters (i.e., Pdgfra) (Figure 2B).

Both during development and in the adult mouse brain, oligodendrocytes can be found in various developmental stages that have been well characterized based on transcriptional and functional differences, including (1) oligodendrocyte precursor (OPC) genes, such as Pdgfra and Mmp15; (2) newly‐formed oligodendrocyte (NFO) genes, such as Fyn and Nfasc; and (3) myelinating oligodendrocyte (MO) genes, such as Mog and Mal (Zhang et al. 2014; Kalish et al. 2018). Two closely related clusters, clusters 2 and 5, appeared to correspond to OPCs and NFOs, respectively. These clusters were transcriptionally distinct from clusters 0, 1, 3, and 4, which demonstrated high expression of MO marker genes (Figure 2C). The similarity in the spatial distribution of Pdgfra + oligodendrocytes (likely OPCs) compared to Tmem163 + oligodendrocytes (likely NFOs; Figure 2D) further supports the continued proliferation and differentiation of oligodendrocytes throughout life (Sams 2021). Together, these results suggest that the separate clustering of oligodendrocytes is at least partly driven by transcriptional differences present during differential stages of development, not separate cell types.

We then applied the same approach to astrocytes. We explored the molecular heterogeneity of vLGN astrocytes by separating the 849 nuclei identified by expression of Aldoc from the overall dataset and re‐clustering them apart from non‐astrocytes. This resulted in four clusters of vLGN astrocytes (Figure 2E). Similar to oligodendrocytes, we found that some known astrocyte genes were expressed by nuclei in all four clusters (i.e., Fgfr3) whereas some were uniquely expressed in one or a subset of the clusters (Figure 2F). Interestingly, only cluster 3 demonstrated high expression of Gfap, a well‐studied marker for mature astrocytes (Figure 2G). Unlike oligodendrocytes, these known genetic markers have not been associated with developmental changes in astrocytes. This unique clustering, combined with the unique spatial distribution of Fgfr3+ astrocytes compared to GFAP+ astrocytes (Figure 2H), suggests molecular heterogeneity of astrocytes in vLGN, potentially indicating distinct functional roles or developmental origins for these astrocyte types within the nucleus. Taken together, these results suggest the presence of transcriptionally distinct types of astrocytes in mouse vLGN.

snRNA‐Seq Predicts Over 20 Neuronal Types in vLGN

A previous study that used a candidate‐based approach to characterize vLGN neurons identified at least four transcriptomically‐distinct cells that were distributed in different lamina of vLGN (Sabbagh et al. 2021). These studies, while informative, revealed that a number of cell types in vLGN likely remain to be identified. Moreover, they raised questions about whether any excitatory neurons were present in vLGN. To characterize, in an unbiased manner, the full cohort of neurons in vLGN, we separated the 4489 nuclei identified by expression of Syn1, Rbfox3, Slc17a6, Slc17a7, Gad1, and/or Gad2 from the overall dataset and re‐clustered them apart from non‐neurons. This yielded 20 unique clusters of vLGN neurons (Figure 3A). As expected, only a few of these clusters showed enriched expression of excitatory neuron marker genes (i.e., Slc17a6) while most clusters showed enriched expression of inhibitory neuron marker genes (i.e., Gad2) (Figure 3B). We then checked these 20 clusters for the presence of those genes identified in transcriptomically‐distinct sublaminae in vLGN (Sabbagh et al. 2021) and found that, while these markers may have been enriched in one cluster, they were largely not confined to a single cluster (Figure 3C). Taken together, our snRNA‐Seq identifies two excitatory clusters (clusters 12 and 13) and 18 inhibitory clusters of vLGN neurons.

To identify top candidate genes that are uniquely generated by a single neuron subtype (or a small subset of types), we identified positive and negative gene markers of a single cluster, compared to all other cells, using differential expression analysis (Figure 4A). This list of candidate genes did include some marker genes identified by previous studies, such as Ecel1 and Penk (Sabbagh et al. 2021). We further refined the list of candidate genes by identifying marker genes with gene profiles uniquely expressed in one or a subset of the clusters (Figure 4B). Our results reveal the characterization of candidate genes that could be used to identify as many as 20 types of neurons in vLGN. It is important to note that some candidate gene markers are unique to a single cluster, while other genes are not. However, the combined expression of multiple genes for each cluster was unique, suggesting that a single candidate gene marker is not inherently a single cell type.

Most Neuronal Types in vLGN Are Retinorecipient

Previous studies of neuronal types in vLGN confirmed that at least four of the suggested half‐dozen types were retinorecipient (Sabbagh et al. 2021). However, they failed to identify whether the remaining types were retinorecipient or if there were any retinorecipient types that had yet to be characterized. As described above, to identify vLGN cell types that receive direct input from the retina, we applied anterograde transsynaptic viral tracing (AAV1‐Cre) combined with single nucleus transcriptomics. After filtering out doublets (Figure 1C), we isolated 89 mCherry+ vLGN nuclei from retinorecipient neurons for snRNA‐Seq analysis.

We then mapped the transcriptomes from these 89 nuclei onto the original neuronal dataset (Figure 5A). Of note, these retinorecipient cells were evenly distributed among clusters and included clusters positive for excitatory markers (clusters 12 and 13). To further characterize retinorecipient cells by cluster, we first evaluated each cluster for mCherry expression in the context of inhibitory marker genes (Gad1 and Gad2) and excitatory marker genes (Slc17a6 and Slc17a7), separating by original identity. Of the original 20 neuronal clusters, only seven inhibitory clusters (clusters 6, 9, 11, 16, 17, 18, and 19) demonstrated no mCherry+ cells (Figure 5B). We then evaluated each cluster for mCherry expression in the context of Nxph1, which encodes the α‐Nrxn ligand Neurexophilin‐1 and whose expression in vLGN is restricted to cells in the retinorecipient vLGNe (Sabbagh et al. 2021). As expected, nearly all clusters with cells that expressed Nxph1 were identified as inhibitory clusters (due to Gad1 and/or Gad2 expression) (Figure 5C). Of note, not all mCherry+ cells were identified by expression of Nxph1 (Figure 5D), suggesting that Nxph1 expression may not identify all retinorecipient neurons in vLGN. In total, 13 clusters were identified as retinorecipient; however, we were only able to isolate a small number of Cre‐labeled cells so we cannot exclude the possibility that the remaining 7 cell types may also be retinorecipient.

Discussion

Here, we employed single‐nucleus sequencing to identify vLGN cells based on their molecular features. Transcriptomic profiling and clustering analysis of 10 266 vLGN cell nuclei identified at least three distinct developmental stages of oligodendrocytes, at least two distinct types of astrocytes, and support cells such as microglia, macrophages, and endothelial cells. Moreover, we characterized 20 molecularly distinct types of neurons, including previously undetected populations of GABAergic neurons and at least two types of excitatory neurons in vLGN. These studies led us to investigate vLGN neurons directly innervated by retinal ganglion cells. While we were limited in the number of cells that were infected by low efficiency transsynaptic viral labeling, we discovered at least 13 retinorecipient neuron clusters in vLGN (which included the two clusters of excitatory cells) that are directly connected to the retina. Anatomical and functional studies on the projections that originate from vLGN have revealed a wide array of target nuclei within the thalamus, midbrain, and other subcortical structures (Swanson et al. 1974; Ribak and Peters 1975; Brauer and Schober 1982; Matute and Streit 1985; Taylor et al. 1986; Cadusseau and Roger 1991; Harrington 1997; Moore et al. 2000). Perhaps this is not surprising given the diversity of neuronal cell types that we identified here, although it remains unclear what percentage of these distinct cell types are projection neurons.

Previous studies have demonstrated that at least a portion of the inhibitory types in vLGN are projection neurons (Harrington 1997; Huang et al. 2019, 2021; Fratzl et al. 2021; Salay and Huberman 2021; Sabbagh et al. 2021), which raises interesting questions regarding the role of vLGN in visual processing. Perhaps the segregation of vLGN neurons into distinct sublaminae suggests they may be specified into parallel visual pathways for processing and transmitting to distinct brain regions. If these inhibitory neurons are primarily projection neurons, then the high inhibition in visual processing between nuclei (as opposed to local inhibition within a nucleus) may suggest roles for vLGN in modulating defensive behaviors and visuospatial processing. On the other hand, these long‐range inhibitory projections may serve to release their targets from inhibition, making overstimulation and unwanted excitation far less likely. This is particularly important given that many of the targets of vLGN neurons are not retinorecipient themselves.

In contrast to the inhibitory neurons, we identified at least two populations of excitatory neurons. Several functional studies have begun to elucidate the excitatory connections between vLGN and downstream targets, such as SC (Salay and Huberman 2021), though the characterization of these connections and whether their role in visual processing is heterogeneous remains unknown. Our results reveal significant cellular diversity in the vLGN, especially in terms of the unique gene combinations expressed by individual clusters. When comparing the neurons in the vLGN to those in the dLGN, we observed that the neuronal clusters in the vLGN are almost entirely distinct (Figure S2), suggesting that, although some neurons in both regions may share expression of certain genes, the specific combinations of genes they express are unique to each nucleus. In contrast, the glial populations in the vLGN and dLGN show much more similarity, as evidenced by their clustering patterns (Figure S2), indicating that glial cells across both regions may share more common molecular characteristics compared to the neurons.

We set out to identify unknown cell types that we assumed existed in the vLGN based on our previous studies. Part of this assumption was based on our inability to identify and label cells in a sublaminae of vLGN between the Ecel1+/Lypd1+ cells adjacent to the optic tract and the more medial Pvalb+/Spp1+ cells. Previous studies have also suggested a potential difference in the classification of Ecel1+/Lypd1+ cells in the external, retinorecipient vLGNe compared to internal, nonretinorecipient vLGNi. There is a notable disconnect between our identified clusters, which are defined by unique combinations of gene expressions, and the single‐gene layers described in Sabbagh et al. (2021). In addition, we were unable to identify the “Cell Type X” reported in their study based on spatial distribution of differentially expressed marker genes compared to known marker genes such as Ecel1 and Pvalb, highlighting potential differences in the gene expression profiles and classification criteria used in our respective approaches. This discrepancy may suggest that our approach, which incorporates a broader range of gene markers, provides a more nuanced resolution of cellular diversity compared to the single‐gene focus of previous studies.

The present study identified multiple clusters with cells that express Ecel1 and Lypd1 at various levels of expression, including some that also express Nxph1. Nxph1 has been shown at early ages to be expressed in the retinorecipient vLGNe with little to no expression in the nonretinorecipient vLGNi (Sabbagh et al. 2021). However, we found a disconnect between the clusters with mCherry+ cells and the clusters with Nxph1+ cells. In this study, we identified excitatory clusters that demonstrated mCherry expression (Figure 5C) but demonstrate little to no Nxph1 expression (Figure 5D). In addition to the excitatory clusters, we identified several inhibitory clusters (such as clusters 1 and 15) that are dense with mCherry expression (Figure 5C) but lack Nxph1 expression (Figure 5D). Based on our previous studies, Nxph1 appears to be at least a good early marker of vLGNe, but our present study demonstrates that not all Nxph1 clusters are retinorecipient and not all retinorecipient clusters are labeled by Nxph1. Taken together, this suggests that Nxph1 may not be the best marker for vLGNe in adulthood, or that dendrites from vLGNe may extend into vLGNi. While we did not provide an extensive assessment of the spatial localization of each of the cell clusters identified here, we did screen the Allen Brain Atlas database for the expression of most of the genes enriched specifically in these clusters. Our study provides a more comprehensive, unbiased characterization of the molecular types of vLGN cells than previously known. Our present study also provides a finer resolution of vLGN neuron types than previously known. For instance, Sabbagh et al. (2021) identified four types of inhibitory vLGN neurons, which were confirmed as distinct clusters by our integrated analysis. Studies have also identified excitatory glutamatergic neurons in vLGN (Harrington 1997; Monavarfeshani et al. 2017; Sabbagh et al. 2021) and begun to characterize their divergent outputs (Salay and Huberman 2021). Together, these findings expand our understanding of the cellular heterogeneity within the vLGN and lay the groundwork for future studies investigating how distinct neuron types contribute to parallel visual processing pathways.

While advances in sequencing have dramatically contributed to our understanding of the cells in the brain, current methodologies still lack the ability to answer the age‐old question of ‘what is a cell type’ (Cajal 1893). Traditionally, cell types have been classified based on specific molecular, structural, and functional characteristics. While these approaches categorize cells based on predefined criteria, single‐cell analysis offers an unbiased, computationally supported method to differentiate cell types (Macosko et al. 2015; Shekhar et al. 2016; Saunders et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2019; Krienen et al. 2020). However, defining cell types purely by molecular expression may not fully capture their identity; structural and physiological properties are also critical to understanding their role. It is important to remember that there may be missing relevant and distinguishing information by only looking at RNA expression in the nucleus as there may be important information in the cytoplasm (Lake et al. 2017; Eraslan et al. 2022) or dendrites (Farris et al. 2019). Additionally, our viral approach targets synaptically connected post‐synaptic neurons. This was consistent with previous reports that these viruses preferentially infect neurons, however, we cannot entirely exclude labeling of nearby astrocytes (based on the presence of their perisynaptic processes at synaptic sites) or neurons in regions with convergent synaptic structures (like in the dorsal LGN) or in regions with densely packed neuropil. Furthermore, expression of a genetic marker at the level of RNA does not always translate to protein. While sequencing analyses provide a result of separate, distinct clusters, these clusters do not inherently translate to distinct cell types. For example, the clusters we identified for vLGN oligodendrocytes appear to represent the different stages of development from OPCs to mature oligodendrocytes (Figure 2C). In contrast, neurons in the thalamus become post‐mitotic by embryonic Day 14.5 (E14.5) (Lund and Mustari 1977), and therefore clusters are unlikely to represent developmental stages; however, it remains possible that stochastic gene expression occurs in neurons, which could explain cell‐to‐cell variations in gene expression levels that leads to separate clustering. However, compared to previous approaches to characterizing vLGN cytoarchitecture in which cells were assigned identity based on expression of a single gene (Sabbagh et al. 2021), our present study evaluates the expression of multiple genes in each cluster, helping to bypass the issue of stochastic expression. Studies have demonstrated that the fragility, large size, and tight interconnections of neurons make them particularly well‐suited to single nucleus RNA sequencing compared to other sequencing methods (Krishnaswami et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2023). Nevertheless, such approaches may overlook phenotypic variation across different modalities, brain regions, or developmental stages. Ideally, a comprehensive classification would integrate morphological, functional, and neurochemical data (Zeng and Sanes 2017; Miller et al. 2020; Scala et al. 2021). The retina, with its completed cellular census, serves as an exemplary case where combining morphological, electrophysiological, and transcriptomic data could yield a more holistic understanding of ‘what is a cell type’ (Zeng and Sanes 2017; Yan et al. 2020). A long‐term goal is to apply a similar multidisciplinary approach to further characterize the cell types we identified here in vLGNe and vLGNi.
