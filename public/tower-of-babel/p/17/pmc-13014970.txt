PKA activation rescues myocardial injury elicited by silica nanoparticles through improving oxidative stress, mitochondrial health, and copper homeostasis
Authors: Caixia Guo, Xinying Zhao, Yawen Zhu, Miao Chen, Yanbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilica nanoparticles (SiNPs), as one of the most widely produced nanomaterials globally, have garnered widespread attention for their potential toxicity. Epidemiological and experimental evidence have illuminated that exposure to SiNPs can lead to cardiovascular toxicity, though the precise underlying mechanisms remain unclear. We previously identified PKA as a crucial regulator in SiNPs-elicited myocardial injury. Considering this, we further conducted validation in an animal model and also an in-depth investigation into the mechanisms involved. Our data manifested that the pharmacological activation of PKA via intraperitoneal administration of Forskolin greatly mitigated SiNPs-induced abnormalities in histology and function of the rat heart, along with lessening cardiac oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage. Concomitantly, copper overload was present in the rat heart and cardiomyocytes with SiNPs administration. Notably, PKA inhibition could worsen this phenomenon, whilst PKA activation alleviated it. The mechanistic investigation hinted at the PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling to modulate mitochondrial dysfunction and copper overload in cardiomyocytes and ultimately apoptosis caused by SiNPs.Keywords: Silica nanoparticle, PKA, Myocardial injury, Toxicity, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Copper overload

Introduction

Silica nanoparticles (SiNPs) are widely produced engineered nanomaterials owing to the favorable physicochemical properties and biocompatibility, making them highly versatile in food, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, etc. [[1], [2], [3]]. The Data Bridge Market Research states the global market size for SiNPs in 2023 is up to about USD 6.117 billion. Besides, silicon (Si) is one of the most abundant elements in airborne NPs [4]. Population studies revealed that silica exposure increased the risk of ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease [5], and also the contribution of inhaled airborne NPs to the increased heart failure and myocardial infarction [6]. With the increasing exposure to SiNPs in both occupational and environmental scenarios, human health concerns arise. Experimental studies have highlighted the toxicological impact of SiNPs on cardiovascular systems [[7], [8], [9]]. After inhalation, nanoscale silica particles can easily pass the air-blood barrier and distribute into the heart [10], contributing to myocardial injury and dysfunction [11]. Our work also revealed that the respiratory exposure to SiNPs disrupted myocardial mitochondria and activated ferroptosis in the rat heart [12,13]. Despite these advances, the impact of SiNPs on the heart and its possible mechanisms remain underexplored, and especially, the developing cardioprotective medications to counteract nanotoxicity remain elusive.

Protein kinase A (PKA), a vital intracellular serine/threonine protein kinase, is known as a pivotal regulator of cardiac function [14]. For instance, PKA modulates myocardial contraction and relaxation by phosphorylating L-type calcium channels, phosphorylase kinase, and phosphatase inhibitors, thereby enhancing myocardial contractility and conductivity. According to the literature, abnormal PKA activation or inactivation is involved in mitochondrial damage and myocardial dysfunction [15], thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of myocardial diseases. Hence, PKA is proposed as a promising target for cardioprotection [16], but contradictory findings exist [17,18]. In our previous work, PKA was screened out as a critical regulator in SiNPs-induced myocardial injury in rats [12], protecting cardiomyocytes from SiNPs-elicited mitochondrial injury and cell death in vitro [19]. Likewise, Wang et al. found that the activated PKA signaling can lower oxidative stress levels in cardiomyocytes, reduce mitochondrial fragmentation, inhibit apoptosis, and improve heart function [20]. Hu et al. revealed that the cardiac-derived meteorin-like (METRNL) protein activated SIRT1 via PKA signaling, which inhibited doxorubicin-elicited oxidative stress and apoptosis, and improved cardiac function [21]. Conversely, PKA inhibition could block METRNL's antioxidant and antiapoptotic impact on cardiomyocytes. Despite recent advances, the significance and regulatory mechanisms of PKA in the development of myopathy upon SiNPs stimuli warrant further investigation.

This work aimed to explore the role of PKA in the myocardial toxicity caused by SiNPs exposure and its mechanism. Consequently, our data suggested PKA activation exerting protection against SiNPs‐induced myocardial injury and dysfunction, probably through mitigating oxidative stress, and improving mitochondrial health and copper homeostasis. Mechanistically, PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling was a regulator for the SiNPs‐induced copper overload in cardiomyocytes, ultimately contributing to cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Collectively, our data shed new light on pharmacological approaches for the prevention of NPs‐induced cardiotoxicity and other cardiovascular‐related conditions.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

SiNPs in this work were prepared using the Stöber method and characterized [22]. In brief, the synthesized particles were approximately 60 nm in size, nearly spherical, and evenly distributed. Their average hydrodynamic size in distilled water was 80.38 ± 1.81 nm, and the Zeta potential was −30.10 ± 1.06 mV. PKA agonists Forskolin and 8-Br-cAMP, H-89 (a PKA inhibitor), and Elesclomol (a copper ion carrier) were obtained from MedChemExpress, USA, while Mdivi-1 (a mitochondrial fission inhibitor for selectively inhibiting DRP1, dynamin related protein 1) was got from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Kits for creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), malondialdehyde (MDA), and reduced/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) were acquired from Jiancheng, China, and the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Assay Kit from KeyGen, China. The kit for cardiac Troponin T (cTnT) was brought from Cloud-Clone, China, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) from Bestbio, China. Kits for mitochondrial respiratory complex I (NADH-CoQ Reductase), III (Coenzyme Q-Cytochrome C Reductase), V (F0F1-ATPase/ATP Synthase), ATP, and Cu2+ contents were all obtained from Elabscience, China. Primary antibodies for PKA, p-PKA, p-DRP1s637, SLC31A1, and ATP7B were purchased from ABclonal, China; FDX1 from Proteintech, USA; and DRP1 and GAPDH from CST, USA.

Animal studies

All animal procedures have been approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (No. AEEI-2019-177). Wistar rats (6-week-old, male) were supplied by Vital River Laboratory, China, acclimatized, and maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions. The rats were randomly divided into four groups, as follows: Control, SiNPs, SiNPs plus Forskolin (a PKA agonist), and SiNPs plus solvent. Rats received SiNPs suspension via intratracheal instillation (10 mg/kg·bw, once every three days, 10 times in total), while the control rats received saline instead. Rats in the SiNPs plus Forskolin/solvent group daily received an intraperitoneal injection of Forskolin (2.0 mg/kg·bw) or the corresponding solvent. The exposure dosage of SiNPs was in concerted with previous publishing [23], and the details have been introduced in our previous work [12]. In brief, it was set based on amorphous silica's occupational limit and adult rats' respiratory physiology. Forskolin was dissolved as manufacturer's instruction and its dosage used in this study was based on previous publishing [24]. The next day after the final particle instillation, echocardiography was performed. Afterward, all rats were anesthetized and euthanized, and blood and heart tissues were collected.

Echocardiographic assessment

Echocardiography was conducted using a Vevo 2100 imaging system (VisualSonics Inc., USA) with a 30 MHz phased array transducer. M-mode tracings of the left ventricle (LV) in the short-axis view were used to obtain digital measurements [25], which were averaged over five cardiac cycles. These parameters included LV internal diameter, anterior/posterior wall thickness during diastole and systole (shortened as LVIDd, LVIDs, LVAWd, LVAWs, LVPWd, and LVPWs), and also systolic function indicators, i.e., CO (cardiac output), EF (ejection fraction), SV (stroke volume), and FS (fractional shortening). Diastolic function of LV was also evaluated, focusing on the MV E/A (E-wave-to-A-wave ratio), IVRT (isovolumic relaxation time), and IVCT (isovolumic contraction time).

Histological examination

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining was performed in paraffin-embedded heart sections, and myocardial injury was evaluated and scored based on previous studies [26,27].

Ultrastructural analysis

The myocardial ultrastructural changes were observed with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) by investigators blinded to the study. Mitochondrial damage was evaluated and scored according to the criteria described by Flameng et al. [28]. At least 100 mitochondria (5 fields of 20 each) were examined and averaged for analysis.

Biochemical analysis

CK-MB and cTnT are commonly used as markers to indicate myocardial injury, and their serum levels were quantified using the corresponding ELISA kits following the product's manual.

Oxidative stress evaluation

The cardiac levels of ROS, MDA, and GSH/GSSG were analyzed in the supernatant obtained from heart samples after homogenization and centrifugation. The serum level of MDA was also detected following the kit's instructions.

Mitochondrial complex activity and ATP assays

The activities of mitochondrial Complex I (NADH-CoQ Reductase), III (Coenzyme Q-Cytochrome C Reductase), and V (F0F1-ATPase/ATP Synthase) in rat myocardial tissue, as well as ATP content, were measured following standard protocols.

Cell culture and treatment

AC16 (human-derived cardiomyocyte) cells were routinely cultured in DMEM/F12 medium with 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. When the cellular confluence reached 80%-85% confluency, cells were incubated with culture medium or SiNPs for 24 h. For mechanism investigations, the cells received a 2-h pretreatment with 8-Br-cAMP (100 μM), H-89 (10 μM), Mdivi-1 (2.5 μM), or Elesclomol (200 nM) before SiNPs treatment (100 μg/mL, 24 h). The doses for 8-Br-cAMP, H-89, Mdivi-1, and Elesclomol were set in accordance with our and other group's publishing [19,[29], [30], [31], [32]].

Mitochondrial function measurement

Mitochondrial function of live cardiomyocytes was assessed using an XFe24 Flux Analyzer (Agilent, USA) [19,33]. For comparative analysis, the final data were normalized to protein concentration.

Cell apoptosis assessment

Cell apoptosis was quantified in a flow cytometer (Becton-Dickinson, USA) using an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (KeyGen, China).

Cu2+ level measurement

The Cu2+ levels in rat myocardial tissue, blood, and AC16 cardiomyocytes were assessed following the kit's guidelines.

Western blot

The protein expressions of PKA, p-PKA, DRP1, p-DRP1s637, FDX1, SLC31A1, and ATP7B were analyzed using Western blot, with GAPDH serving as the loading control. In brief, proteins were isolated from the frozen myocardial tissue or fresh cultured cardiomyocytes using RIPA solution with protease inhibitor cocktails. After quantification, proteins (40 μg of each sample) were separated via SDA-PAGE gels, electrically transferred onto NC membranes, and incubated with specific primary and secondary antibodies. Finally, signals were captured and quantified with the Image Studio™ software.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as “mean ± SD”. Normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were evaluated using SPSS 26.0 software. For two-group comparison, Student's t-test was applied, while one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett (homogeneous data) or Dunnett T3 (heterogeneous data) for multiple-group comparison. Pearson correlation analysis was carried out, and structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted with AMOS 26.0 software. A P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

PKA attenuated SiNPs-induced myocardial injury and dysfunction in rats

As manifested in HE images (Fig. 1A) and score analysis (Fig. 1B), the increased myocardial interstitial spaces and inflammatory infiltration were seen in the heart tissue in response to SiNPs exposure, while Forskolin alleviated these histological alterations. In agreement with the histology analysis, the determinations of serum CK-MB and cTnT levels revealed the myocardial injury caused by SiNPs, whereas Forskolin reversed it (Fig. 1C–D). Moreover, the impacts on rat cardiac structure and function were assessed via echocardiography. Compared to the Control group, SiNPs exposure led to a significant thickening of LVPWd, LVPWs, and LVAWs, while significantly reducing LVIDd and LVIDs (Fig. 1E–F). In terms of systolic function, SiNPs exposure caused a compensatory increase in EF and FS, and for diastolic function, a significant reduction in MV E/A was observed (Fig. 1G–H). These findings indicated that SiNPs induced cardiac remodeling and dysfunction in Wistar rats. Notably, after intervention with the PKA agonist Forskolin, there was a considerable reduction in LVPWd, LVPWs, and LVAWs, along with an increase in LVIDs when compared to the SiNPs group (Fig. 1E–F). Forskolin also remarkably mitigated the compensatory increases in EF and FS triggered by SiNPs and alleviated the decline in MV E/A induced by SiNPs (Fig. 1G–H). These data indicated the cardioprotective effect of PKA in SiNPs-instilled rats, which greatly improved myocardial injury and dysfunction caused by SiNPs.

PKA alleviated SiNPs-activated oxidative stress in the rat myocardial tissues

As illustrated in Fig. 2, SiNPs prompted an increase in ROS (Fig. 2A) and MDA (Fig. 2B–a) levels in rat myocardial tissue, along with a significant decline in GSH/GSSG owing to the greatly reduced GSH while increased GSSG (Fig. 2C–a). Similarly, an elevation in MDA (Fig. 2B–b) and a decline in GSH level (Fig. 2C–b) were observed in serum samples upon the SiNPs exposure. These results indicated that SiNPs triggered cardiac and systemic oxidative stress in rats. Moreover, the PKA agonist could greatly alleviate these alterations of oxidative stress indicators caused by SiNPs, including suppressing MDA production and reversing GSH decline in rats.

(B) MDA levels in both the myocardial tissue (a) and serum (b) were measured. PKA improved SiNPs-induced mitochondrial damage and dysfunction

The myocardium is rich in mitochondria, making it highly dependent on efficient mitochondrial function for energy production. As shown in TEM analysis (Fig. 3A), SiNPs caused mitochondrial swelling, disrupted or missing cristae, and increased the Flameng score. In contrast, mitochondrial morphology in the Forskolin + SiNPs group was largely restored to the control level, with a notable reduction in the Flameng score. Damage to mitochondrial respiratory chain subunits disrupts this process, leading to increased oxidative stress, which subsequently contributes to myocardial injury. The mitochondrial respiratory assay showed that SiNPs significantly reduced mitochondrial complex activities (including complex I, III, and V) and inhibited ATP synthesis (Fig. 3B). Remarkably, PKA effectively alleviated the suppression of mitochondrial respiratory chain subunit expression caused by SiNPs and enhanced ATP synthesis in rat myocardium. To further assess whether PKA influenced this process, we conducted a mitochondrial stress assay in AC16 cells. In line with the in vivo data, SiNPs impaired mitochondrial respiration and ATP production, which was ameliorated by the PKA stimulator 8-Br-cAMP and exacerbated by the PKA inhibitor H-89 (Fig. 3C). These results suggested that PKA activation alleviated SiNPs-induced cardiac mitochondrial injury and dysfunction in vivo and in vitro.

PKA protected against copper overload triggered by SiNPs

Research indicates that mitochondrial damage can lead to disturbances in copper ion metabolism [34,35]. As depicted in Fig. 4A, exposure to SiNPs resulted in a remarkable increase in serum and myocardial Cu2+ levels in rats, which could be greatly alleviated by Forskolin. Consistently, a dose-dependent increase of Cu2+ level was detected in SiNPs-treated cardiomyocytes in vitro (Fig. 4B). Measuring pyruvate level is essential for understanding how fluctuations in Cu2+ concentration impact cellular metabolism and energy production. Following SiNPs exposure, the pyruvate level in AC16 cells was elevated, potentially exacerbating copper metabolic disturbances (Supplementary files, Fig. S1). Meanwhile, the intervention experiments evidenced that the PKA agonist could lower the copper overload condition in response to SiNPs exposure, whilst PKA inhibition aggravated it. Subsequently, we assessed key proteins related to copper metabolism, including FDX1, SLC31A1, and ATP7B. As shown in Fig. 4C, SiNPs exposure led to a remarkable reduction of ATP7B in AC16 cells. Treatment with 8-Br-cAMP substantially increased ATP7B expression compared to the SiNPs group, probably contributing to the observed decline in intracellular Cu2+ level. On the contrary, the opposite effect was observed with H-89 treatment. Also, the expression of ATP7B was measured in rat heart tissues (Fig. 4D). ATP7B was a copper-transporting ATPase responsible for exporting excess copper from cells to prevent accumulation [36]. These data indicated that the downregulated ATP7B, at least partially regulated by PKA, was responsible for the intracellular cardiac copper overload following SiNPs exposure.

Oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and copper overload as key biological processes in SiNPs-induced myocardial injury

To clarify the interconnection among oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and copper overload on myocardial injury, we developed a structural equation model (SEM). Generally speaking, a standardized factor loading value greater than 0.4 is considered indicative of a good predictor within the model. Our results indicated that out of 10 factors, 8 met this criterion (Fig. 5A). The path coefficients were calculated using univariate linear regression with the least squares method, and the findings revealed that oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage mutually influence each other, with mitochondrial damage subsequently affecting copper overload, ultimately leading to myocardial injury. Additionally, correlation analyses (Fig. 5B) showed strong associations between indicators of oxidative stress (cardiac ROS, MDA, and GSH/GSSG), mitochondrial damage (Mitochondrial Complex I, III, V, and ATP), and copper overload (Cu2+ content) with indicators of myocardial injury (CK-MB and cTnT). These results collectively demonstrated that oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and copper overload contribute synergistically to SiNPs-caused myocardial injury.

PKA/DRP1/ATP7B-mediated copper overload contributing to cardiomyocyte apoptosis triggered by SiNPs

Our previous work has well demonstrated that the perturbation of mitochondrial dynamics through PKA/DRP1 signaling is a crucial way contributing to SiNPs-elicited mitochondrial impairments and ensuing the activation of mitochondria-dependent apoptosis in myocardiocytes in vitro [19]. Here, the expression levels of DRP1 and its phosphorylation, p-DRP1(S637), were assessed in the rat heart tissues. As a result, SiNPs exposure increased DRP1 and decreased p-DRP1(S637) in myocardial tissue alongside with the decreased expression of p-PKA and PKA (Fig. 6A), indicating enhanced mitochondrial fission to contribute for the oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [31]. More importantly, the expression level of DRP1 was reversed following PKA agonist treatment, and that of p-PKA, PKA, and p-DRP1(S637) were elevated when compared to the SiNPs group (Fig. 6A). That may explain the improved myocardial mitochondrial morphology upon PKA activation (Fig. 3A). To further investigate the interconnection of mitochondrial damage and copper overload, we detected the effect of intracellular copper metabolism using a DRP1 inhibitor (Mdivi-1). To note, Mdivi-1 could greatly suppress copper overload through reversing the decline in ATP7B by SiNPs (Fig. 6B and C). Elesclomol is a potent copper ionophore, ignoring the ion concentration gradient. It is also a mitochondrion-targeting agent, wherein the elesclomol-Cu2+ complex shuttles copper into the cell, induces an increase of Cu2+ in mitochondria, activates mitochondrial oxidative stress, and elicits cell death [37,38]. Our data showed that Elesclomol did trigger a notable increase in intracellular Cu2+ level and ultimately promoted cardiomyocyte apoptosis triggered by SiNPs (Fig. 6D and E). In agree with the in vitro findings (Fig. 6E), the induction of apoptosis by SiNPs, at least partially PKA-dependent, was also determined in rat myocardial tissue (Supplementary files, Fig. S2). Taken together, these findings indicated the modulation of PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling as a crucial way to protect against SiNPs-triggered oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, copper overload, and the ultimate myocardial injury.

Discussion

NPs, owing to their unique physicochemical properties, have been widely employed in various industrial and biomedical applications. However, an increasing body of research suggests that they may pose potential risks to the cardiovascular system [39,40]. Studies have revealed that humans and animals exposed to nanoparticle-rich environments often exhibit abnormalities in cardiovascular function. Wherein, SiNPs exposure posed potential risks to cardiac health and warrants serious attention. For instance, Du et al. reported that SiNPs, upon entering the body via inhalation, can accumulate not only in the lungs but also translocate through the bloodstream to the heart and other vital organs, thereby inducing systemic inflammation and oxidative stress responses [11]. Qi et al. found that exposure to SiNPs significantly activated oxidative stress, mitochondrial anomaly, calcium homeostasis imbalance, ultimately leading to cardiac injury and dysfunction [27]. In concert with these findings, the onset of myocardial injury and cardiac dysfunction was clearly manifested in SiNPs-instilled rats (Fig. 1). More importantly, our work first evidenced that the pharmaceutical activation of PKA can protect the heart from SiNPs-induced myocardial injury and dysfunction by reversing mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and copper overload via PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling. These data would guide the development of intervention strategies against nanotoxicity.

Given cardiomyocytes with exceptionally high energy demands, mitochondria play a pivotal role in sustaining the cardiac structural and functional integrity [41], including the regulation of intracellular Ca2+, fatty acid oxidation, and ROS generation and scavenging. So far, a large body of experimental evidence has proven the crucial role of mitochondrial impairments in cardiotoxicity owing to SiNPs exposure [8,42,43]. Here, our data indicated the damage to mitochondrial respiratory complex activities (I, III, and V) and ATP production in myocardial tissue by SiNPs, as well as mitochondrial morphology disruption (Fig. 3), contributing to the cardiac systolic/diastolic dysfunction [44]. The disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis, including morphology and function anomalies, is implicated in the pathological process of cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, and heart failure [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. Conversely, the improvement of mitochondrial homeostasis could effectively mitigate cardiac damage and improve cardiac performance, thereby delaying the onset and progression of myocardial diseases [49].

PKA is a vital intracellular serine/threonine protein kinase, and its activity alterations may drive progression to cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Studies have well demonstrated the role of PKA in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and cardiac health [15,50]. PKA could reserve mitochondrial pathology and dysfunction through its modulation of oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial morphology, and quality control, thereby improving mitochondrial health [51]. Oppositely, PKA inhibition blocked preserved mitochondrial integrity and cell survival [52]. In our work, PKA activators (Forskolin, 8-Br-cAMP) could restore PKA activation and exert cardio- and mito-protective actions in in vivo or in vitro (Fig. 1, Fig. 3), including enhancing mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activities and ATP synthesis, ultimately improving cardiac contractility. Consistent with the concept that PKA maintains mitochondrial health upon SiNPs stimuli, PKA signaling is well-known to regulate cellular energy metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and dynamics [53]. For instance, one study demonstrated that PKA-mediated phosphorylation of NDUFS4, the 18 kDa subunit of mitochondrial complex I, led to enhancement of the mitochondrial respiratory complex activity [54]. By exogenous stimulation of PKA activity using Forskolin addition, Palorini et al. confirmed that PKA activation led to an enhancement in mitochondrial complex activity, favored mitochondrial fusion over fission, and decreased mitochondrial and cellular ROS levels [55]. Mitochondria are a source of ROS and a sensor of oxidative stress. Excessive ROS production induced oxidative stress, accelerated mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately cell death. Emerging evidence suggests PKA exerts a protective effect on ROS-mediated cardiac damage [56]. Indeed, alongside the improvements in mitochondrial health, the PKA agonist greatly abolished cardiac and systemic oxidative stress in response to SiNPs exposure (Fig. 2). Likewise, Liu et al. found that the PKA agonist reduced ROS to inhibit NLRP3-mediated retinal inflammation [57]. Hence, in this case, therapeutic interventions that enhance PKA signaling may be beneficial to protect against NPs-elicited myocardial impairments.

The dynamic balance of mitochondrial fission and fusion is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. Researches have demonstrated that the perturbations of mitochondrial dynamics lead to reduced ATP production, increased oxidative stress, and calcium dysregulation, thereby exacerbating cardiomyocyte damage [[58], [59], [60]]. ROS-mediated oxidative stress was also considered a contributing factor to mitochondrial dynamics imbalance [61]. Stabilizing mitochondrial dynamic balance can exert protection against NPs-induced cardiomyocyte structural alterations and cell death [62,63]. DRP1 expression and activity were significantly elevated in a myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury model, resulting in mitochondrial fragmentation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis [64]. In contrast, inhibiting DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission by Mdivi-1 significantly attenuated the damage caused by chronic hypoxia in acute myocardial infarction in mice [65]. Likewise, SiNPs-induced mitochondrial and cellular ROS levels were greatly declined by Mdivi, contributing to the inhibited activation of oxidative stress and an ultimate reduced cellular death [61]. In addition, Mdivi led to destabilization of mitochondrial complex I, resulting in increased ROS production and reduced ATP generation [66]. Notably, the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics by PKA signaling via DRP1 phosphorylation has been well established. PKA can phosphorylate DRP1 at the Ser637 residue, inhibiting its GTPase activity, preventing its mitochondrial translocation, and reducing mitochondrial fission [67]. PKA-mediated mitochondrial fusion exerted cytoprotection of cardiomyocytes against ischemia in vivo and in vitro [68]. PKA inhibitor (H-89) blocked doxorubicin-induced mitochondrial elongation and aggravated cell death in cardiomyocytes [69]. Our previous in vitro study illustrated enhanced mitochondrial fission in cardiomyocytes after SiNPs exposure, primarily via PKA-mediated up-regulation of DRP1 expression and phosphorylation modification [19]. Consistently, PKA-mediated DRP1 phosphorylation was noted in our in vivo model, and the underlying role in myocardial injury should be of concern.

Studies have demonstrated that copper dysregulation, either deficiency or overload, participates in the development of cardiac diseases, such as cardiomyopathy, myocardial ischemic-reperfusion injury, and heart failure [[70], [71], [72]]. Copper metabolism is essential in the maintenance of mitochondrial stability and cardiac function [73,74]. In mitochondria, copper acts as a cofactor for key enzymes in the respiratory chain, directly influencing ATP production and redox balance [75]. Disruptions in copper metabolism can impair mitochondrial function, leading to oxidative stress and related pathological processes [76]. Cardiomyocytes are highly sensitive to copper. In addition to disturb mitochondrial dynamics through up-regulating DRP1 for enhanced fission, existing data has demonstrated that copper overload could inhibit mitochondrial respiration, impaired GSH redox homeostasis, and promote mitochondrial ROS production, membrane damage and apoptosis [[77], [78], [79]]. Conversely, strategies by neutralizing excessive copper and rescuing mitochondrial health may serve as a potential therapeutic way in disease management [80,81]. As recently reported, copper can induce DRP1 activation and disrupt the mitochondria-lipid droplet (LD) contacts. In turn, DRP1 knockdown could efficiently promote mitochondria-LD contacts, alleviating copper-induced lipid metabolism disorders [82]. Xue et al. even reported a link between copper stress with GPX4 ubiquitination, contributing to GPX4 degradation and subsequent ferroptosis [83]. Interestingly, copper deficiency also led to heart damage, along with oxidative damage, mitochondrial dynamic disorder and dysfunction [74]. Likewise, the depletion of mitochondrial copper may promote cardiac fibrosis [84]. In our model, the disturbance of copper homeostasis by SiNPs was verified in vitro and in vivo, and copper overload aggravated SiNPs-induced apoptosis in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6). Moreover, the interconnection among PKA-mediated mitochondrial injury, oxidative stress, and copper overload in SiNPs-caused myocardial injury was clearly seen (Fig. 5), but the underlying regulatory molecules should be addressed.

The flux of copper is controlled by transporters and chaperone proteins, wherein copper transporter SLC31A1 (also known as Ctr1) mediates cellular copper ion absorption [85], and ATP7B is responsible for exporting Cu2+ from cells [36]. SLC31A1 was considered a diagnostic biomarker for acute myocardial infarction [86]. However, its expression was not remarkably altered by SiNPs or PKA intervention. It is well known that ATP7B mutation-mediated Wilson's disease is characterized by copper accumulation in organs like the liver, brain, and heart, resulting in multi-organ damage. Also, ATP7B deficiency-mediated copper accumulation led to decreased activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, declined ATP production, increased mitochondrial fragmentation, and impaired cardiac function [87]. In our model, reduced ATP7B may account for myocardial copper overload upon SiNPs exposure (Fig. 4). Encouragingly, PKA agonist and DRP1 inhibitor could restore copper homeostasis via regulating ATP7B, and PKA inhibition aggravates copper imbalance. PKA-mediated modulation of ATP7B and intracellular copper handling in the liver has been reported [88,89]. Given the role of mitochondria in maintaining ionic homeostasis, the restoration of mitochondrial health by PKA activation and subsequent DRP1s637 expression undoubtedly facilitated the recovery of cardiomyocytes from SiNPs-caused copper overload. Thereby, we considered the activation of PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling as a crucial mediator for the mitigation of SiNPs-elicited myocardial injury, primarily through facilitating the maintenance of mitochondrial heath and copper homeostasis. Existing researches have well illuminated the association of copper with multiple cell death pattern, such as apoptosis [90], ferroptosis [91], and pyroptosis [92]. Also, the induction of cuproptosis by copper dysregulation has been extensively investigated, with the Fe–S cluster protein Ferredoxin1 (FDX1) and lipoylation as key regulators [75]. In myocardial tissue of rats, SiNPs exposure up-regulated FDX1, but not PKA-mediated (Supplementary file, Fig. S2). Meanwhile, the insignificant alteration of FDX1 expression in SiNPs-treated AC16 cells may hint at the exclusion of cuproptosis (Fig. 4C). Hence, whether cuproptosis is involved in SiNPs-induced cardiomyopathy is still undetermined. Xia et al. proposed cellular cuproptosis synchronized with enhanced copper loading in the development of experimental silicosis in mice [93]. Anyway, our data provide guidance for further investigation into theraeutic strategies to manage mitochondrial health and copper homeostasis upon NPs exposure.

Several scenarios supported PKA-offered benefit against myocardial injury induced by SiNPs challenge. First and foremost, PKA activation efficiently alleviated SiNPs challenge-induced ROS generation and MDA production, lessened levels of GSH and ATP, impaired mitochondrial respiration, and copper overload, ultimately mitigating myocardial injury and dysfunction. These phenomena denote a modulatory role for improving oxidative stress, mitochondrial health, and copper homeostasis in PKA-activated benefits against SiNPs stimuli. Next, PKA appears to improve mitochondrial health, possibly through modulating DRP1 phosphorylation to facilitate the restoration of mitochondrial dynamic balance and ensuing copper homeostasis. Last but not least, PKA may stabilize ATP7B to facilitate copper export, leading to reduced copper within cardiomyocytes, ultimately mitigating apoptosis and myocardial injury, wherein PKA/DRP1/ATP7B signaling serves as a key regulator. It is also noteworthy that the existing limitations in our study. For instance, our data indicated the interconnection of myocardial oxidative stress, mitochondrial disorder, and copper overload following SiNPs exposure, but the precise interplay remains essentially elusive. Moreover, an interaction among PKA, DRP1, and ATP7B has been initially identified, but the exact protein-protein interaction governing mitochondrial health and copper homeostasis remains unknown. Another factor to consider when interpreting our findings is particle size. The myocardial toxicity arise from silica nanoparticle exposure is somewhat size-dependent [27,94]. Whether the cardioprotection of PKA could be still work in other size of SiNPs, not just 70 nm used, should be further explored.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that exposure to SiNPs posed a potential risk to cardiac health by disrupting mitochondrial morphology and function, copper metabolism, and activating oxidative stress. Specifically, SiNPs-induced mitochondrial dysfunction interfered with energy metabolism, induced oxidative stress, led to copper accumulation, and promoted apoptosis of cardiomyocytes, ultimately resulting in cardiac toxicity. PKA, functioning as a protective factor in the heart via modulating DRP1/ATP7B signaling to improve mitochondrial health and copper homeostasis, consequently mitigating SiNPs-caused cardiomyocyte apoptosis and ultimate cardiac damage. Our work provides valuable theoretical support for developing therapeutic strategies to address cardiac toxicity induced by nanoparticle exposure.
