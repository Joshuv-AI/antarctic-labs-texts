Dietary polyphenols from shredded, steam-exploded pine particles mitigate the adverse effects of heat stress in broiler chickens
Authors: Akshat Goel, Chris Major Ncho, Chae-Mi Jeong, Vaishali Gupta, Ji-Young Jung, Si-Young Ha, Jae-Kyung Yang, Yang-Ho Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current study investigated the impact of supplementing polyphenols extracted from shredded, steam-exploded pine particles (PSPP) on the performance, gene expression, and gut metagenome of broilers exposed to cyclic heat stress (CHS). A total of 216 chickens were distributed into a 2 (temperature) by 3 (diets) design, with each treatment consisting of six replicates of six chickens. Specifically, chickens were fed diets containing 0% PSPP, 0.5% PSPP, and 1% PSPP and exposed to two temperature conditions: CHS (31°C) and Thermoneutral (NT, 21°C). The CHS was conducted for 6 hours every day for 7 consecutive days. Final body weight, average daily gain, and average daily feed intake (ADFI) were decreased, while feed conversion ratio and rectal temperature were increased in heat-exposed chickens. Dietary PSPP supplementation enhanced ADFI. The weight of the liver, bursa, and length of the jejunum and ileum were decreased in heat-exposed chickens. Plasma cholesterol was increased, and triglycerides were decreased in heat-exposed chickens. After heat exposure, gene expression of ZO1, ZO2, GLP2, NOX1, SOD, GPX, HSP70, HSP90, NRF2, TLR2, and TLR4 increased in the jejunum. GLP2 gene expression was similar in 1%PSPP exposed to HS in comparison to the entire NT-exposed chickens. Concerning microbiota analysis, alpha diversity indices, such as Shannon and Gini-Simpson, were increased following CHS exposure. Beta diversity, measured through unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances, showed temperature, dose, and interaction effects. The relative abundance of the phylum Candidatus Melainabacteria was increased, while Tenericutes populations were decreased in heat-exposed chickens. Furthermore, a total of thirty genera were identified as microbial biomarkers of CHS. Interestingly, the relative abundance of five pathogenic bacterial genera was found to be decreased in the 0.5%PSPP treatment. Overall, CHS negatively influences growth performance, modulates the expression of the gut antioxidant-related genes, and favors the colonization of pathogenic bacteria. However, 0.5% PSPP may mitigate CHS by reducing pathogen colonization in the gut of broilers.

Introduction

Chicken, being the favorite source of animal protein, accounts for a major proportion of global meat production. Owing to the continuous increase in environmental temperature, poultry growers face detrimental repercussions every year (St-Pierre et al., 2003; Ncho et al., 2025a). Some of the consequences of heat stress (HS) in chickens are enhanced rectal temperature (RT) and compromised immunity, leading to pathogenic invasion resulting in impaired growth performance and gut health (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010; Awad et al., 2020; Ncho et al., 2022). Various strategies have been adopted to combat the adverse effects of HS in chickens. Dietary supplementation of feed additives is among those feasible methods (Greene et al., 2021; Goel et al., 2023). In search of economical substances, steam-exploded wood byproducts were explored as feed additives in chickens (Goel et al., 2021a; Ncho et al., 2023; Ncho et al., 2025b). The utilization of such byproducts in livestock feed may also reduce their accumulation. Furthermore, countries that are fully dependent on the import of animal feed constituents could become self-reliant.

Owing to the importance of microbiota in the gut, maintaining intestinal health is of the utmost importance. Genes such as zonula occludens 1 (ZO1), zonula occludens 2 (ZO2), claudin1 (CLDN), and occludin (OCLN) regulate the sealing of junctions and paracellular spaces in the intestine (Ulluwishewa et al., 2011). Similarly, mucin (MUC2) protects from the unwanted entry of pathogens (Forder et al., 2012). In continuation, toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4) recognize foreign entities to defend against them, and their gene expression depends on many factors under stress conditions (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2017; Goel et al., 2021c). Glucagon-like peptide 2 (GLP2) is another class of genes responsible for causing anorexia (Dalvi et al., 2012). Taken together, these genes help in understanding the gut health status of chickens. To reduce the use of antibiotic growth promoters in chickens' diet, the use of substances originating from plants, such as phytogenic feed additives containing polyphenols, has been encouraged (Murugesan et al., 2015). In challenged broilers, the addition of dietary phenolic compounds has improved the antioxidant status, resulting in improved growth performance and microbiota composition (Ahmed et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2018). Genes such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 1 and 4 (NOX1 and 4), nuclear factor erythroid-related factor 2 (NRF2), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), heat shock protein 70 and 90 (HSP70 and 90) are modulated under HS by a series of action (Kikusato et al., 2015; Chueh et al., 2019; Ncho et al., 2025a). Regulation of the expression of such genes can be beneficial for understanding the physiology of HS in chickens.

The effect of steam-exploded value-added byproducts has been explored to reduce the harmful effects of heat stress in chickens (Goel et al., 2021b; Goel et al., 2022). However, scanty information is available about the role of polyphenols from steam-exploded pine wood sources. The present study was conducted to evaluate the effect of dietary supplementation of polyphenols from shredded, steam-exploded pine particles (PSPP) on growth performance, gut health, antioxidant-related genes, and cecum microbiota in cyclic heat-stressed broiler chickens. We hypothesize that HS negatively influenced the performance parameters by modulating gut health, antioxidant-related genes, and cecum microbiota. Dietary supplementation of PSPP may influence the gut microbiota to relieve the harmful effects of heat exposure in chickens.

Materials and methods

This experiment was conducted at the research facility of Gyeongsang National University, Korea. All the experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Gyeongsang National University (GNU-200916-C0057).

Preparation of polyphenols from steam-exploded pine particles

The preparation of PSPP was carried out by following a series of steps that include exploding the pine wood chips (approximately 2 × 2 × 0.5 cm3) with steam (200°C; 11.5 min) as explained previously (Goel et al., 2022). The product thus produced was named shredded steam-exploded pine particles (SPP) and was used in our previous studies (Goel et al., 2021a; Goel et al., 2021b; Goel et al., 2022). The SPP was then further extracted in 74 % ethanol with an extract ratio of 1/46 (w/v) at 90°C for 240 min to obtain PSPP.

Experimental chickens and housing

A total of 216-day-old unsexed Indian River broiler chicks with similar body weight were distributed in three dietary treatment groups consisting of 12 replicates of 6 chicks each. All chickens were equally distributed in two climate-controlled rooms according to the treatment program. The room temperature was maintained as per the recommendation of the Indian River broiler guide until 4 weeks of age. Chickens were fed commercial starter, grower, and finisher feed containing 0, 0.5, and 1% PSPP throughout the experiment (Supplementary Table 1). The total polyphenol content was measured as 23.7 g per L of PSPP, and was 0 mg, 118 mg, and 235 mg per kg of feed in the 0% PSPP, 0.5% PSPP, and 1% PSPP groups, respectively. The cyclic heat stress (CHS) experiment started on the 29th day following the weighing of individual chickens. The Solver module of the Excel software (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) was employed to allocate the birds into treatment groups while ensuring comparable average body weights and SDs. The chickens were equally allocated to two identical rooms. The individual room thus received six treatments, each having six replicates of five chickens per cage. The temperature of the two rooms was either maintained at the recommended thermoneutral (NT, 21°C) or gradually increased by 3 ∼ 4°C per hour to reach 31°C within the first three hours and then maintained for another three hours (Fig. 1). The temperature was increased using radiators and heat blowers to allow steady increments and minimize variation throughout the CHS challenge. The heat treatment began at around 9:00 am every morning. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. The CHS experiment lasted for seven days, and thereafter, the body weight and feed consumption were recorded on the 35th day of age. Recording of rectal temperature

On the final day (the 35th day of age) of CHS, RT was measured before and after heat exposure using a digital thermometer (HI 91610, Hanna Instruments Inc., Padua, Italy). Two trained researchers were responsible for measuring RT. Specifically, while the first researcher restrained the birds by holding their wings close to their flanks, the second researcher inserted the probe 3 cm and angled it to allow direct contact with the internal walls of the rectum. To ensure accuracy, measurements were only recorded when the readings stabilized for at least 10 seconds following insertion.

Sample collection

After heat exposure on the final day (36th day of age), six chickens from each group were humanely euthanized. The blood samples were collected after euthanasia from heart puncture in a heparinized Vacutainer for plasma processing and stored at -20°C thereafter until processing. The internal organs (liver, gizzard, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius) were dissected, weighed, and expressed as absolute and relative to body weight. The length of the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum was measured and expressed as absolute and relative to body weight. The jejunum and whole cecum were snap-frozen immediately and stored at -80°C for further studies.

Blood biochemicals

After extracting the plasma from the blood, biochemical parameters such as glucose, total protein, triglycerides, and total cholesterol were analyzed. VetTest Chemistry Analyzer (IDEXX Co., Ltd., Westbrook, ME) with dry-slide technology was used for this purpose. The slides specifically for the above-mentioned parameters were obtained from IDEXX Co., Ltd., Westbrook, ME. After installing the slides, a total of 10 μL of plasma samples was loaded through an automated pipettor, and the results were then recorded.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

The extraction of total RNA from the jejunum samples was carried out in TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The spectrophotometric analysis for identifying the concentration and purity of the RNA samples was performed using Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). The cDNA synthesis was done using the Verso cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The expression of various genes (Supplementary Table 2) was performed using StepOnePlus real-time PCR systems (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). The total reaction volume for RT-PCR was 20 μL, containing Power SYBR green PCR master mix (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA), 10 pmol concentrations of forward and reverse primers for each gene, along with cDNA. The details of the PCR cycle are as follows: 10 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C and 1 min at 60°C. For normalization, the geometric mean of the housekeeping genes glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and beta-actin was employed (Supplementary Table 2). The 2−ΔΔct algorithm was used to represent the relative expression of each gene (Livak et al., 2001).

Metagenome investigation

The extraction of total genomic DNA from the cecum samples was performed using DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was quantified using Quant-IT PicoGreen (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The 16S metagenomic sequencing library was constructed using Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase Nextera XT Index Kit V2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and was then sequenced on the Illumina platform (Macrogen, Inc., Seoul, Korea). The fastp program was used for quality profiling, adapter trimming, and read filtering, followed by FLASH software (v1.2.11) (Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, USA) to assemble paired-end reads into one sequence (Magoc et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2018). Sequences within the range of 400–500 bp were used. The number of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with a 97% sequence identity cutoff was determined using the CD-HIT-EST program (Li et al., 2012). BLAST+ (v2.9.0) program (National Library of Medicine, Rockville Pike, Bethesda) was used to check the taxonomic similarity against the reference database (NCBI 16S Microbial) (Zhang et al., 2000). Identical coverage of less than 85% was determined as undefined. Metrics such as the number of observed OTUs, Chao1, Shannon, Good’s coverage, and Gini-Simpson indices were calculated to describe alpha diversity. Beta diversity metrics calculated were unweighted/weighted UniFrac distances.

Statistical analysis

In all subsequent analyses, a cage was considered an experimental unit. The IBM SPSS Statistics software package 25. 0 (IBM software, Chicago, IL, USA) was used to analyze the data of growth performance, gene expression, and blood biochemicals via a two-way ANOVA with temperature and PSPP level as main factors. All the data were expressed as means ± SEM. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Duncan's multiple range test was used as a post hoc test when significance existed. In terms of microbial analysis, alpha-diversity indices and microbial abundances (phylum and genus) were analyzed using the Scheirer-Ray-Hare test (non-parametric alternative to two-way ANOVA) with temperature and PSPP level as main factors, followed by the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. The Dunn test was used when significance was detected. The “Rcompanion” package of the R software version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, 2020) was employed for conducting this analysis. Beta diversity was visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), and differences among groups were assessed using a two-way PERMANOVA with temperature and PSPP levels as main factors. GraphPad Prism v8.0 software was used for preparing the graphs.

Discussion

HS has a detrimental effect on the performance of chickens (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010; Ncho et al., 2025a). In the present study, all performance parameters indicated a temperature effect, and heat exposure negatively influenced FBW, ADG, ADFI, and FCR in chickens. Our results are in correlation with previous studies (Awad et al., 2020; Salah et al., 2021). Many factors come into play when the chicken is under stressful conditions. For instance, HS increases respiration rates, leading to panting (Kang et al., 2020). Panting prevents the chicken from consuming feed, resulting in decreased ADFI, which ultimately negatively impacts FBW and ADG. The possibility of activating the anorexic system that reduces feed intake also exists (Furuse et al., 1997). In confirmation, reduced ADFI was accompanied by the enhanced expression of the GLP2 gene in the heat-exposed chickens of the current study. Additionally, among the heat-exposed broilers, PSPP-supplemented chickens had higher ADFI, indicating that they consumed more feed and experienced less stress than the control chickens during the heat exposure. In agreement, Salah and his group also reported higher feed intake in the chickens fed polyphenols such as curcumin during heat exposure (Salah et al., 2021). Concomitantly, enhanced ADFI was also accompanied by numerically higher ADG. Researchers have conflicting opinions regarding the effects of dietary polyphenol supplementation on growth performance. For instance, weight gain was decreased in chickens fed grape seed extract as a source of polyphenols (Viveros et al., 2011). In another study, dietary supplementation of grape seed showed a dose-dependent improvement in body weight gain, which decreases after a certain dose (40 g/kg) (Abu Hafsa et al., 2018).

RT has been previously known as an indicator of HS (Chen et al., 2013). The RT of heat-exposed chickens increases (Goel et al., 2021b; Goel et al., 2023). In correlation, the present study showed that RT and ΔT of heat-exposed chickens were higher than those of thermoneutral controls. This indicates that chickens were under stress when the temperature of the HS room was increased. Failure to exchange body heat with the surroundings could be the reason for the increase in the RT in heat-exposed birds (Deeb et al., 1999). The absence of a dose effect on RT suggests that PSPP exerts its beneficial effects through other mechanisms and plays only a minimal role in thermoregulation.

The liver in the chicken has a variety of metabolic and homeostatic roles. It is sometimes thought of as a biochemical factory that manages the majority of the body's synthesis, metabolism, excretion, and detoxification processes. Under stressful circumstances, the liver is a crucial organ for metabolism and hemostasis (Emami et al., 2020). Similarly, the bursa of Fabricius is an immune organ with an immune-conferring function. In the present study, the absolute and relative weight of the liver and bursa were decreased in heat-exposed chickens. This is in correlation with the previous studies where the weight of the liver was decreased when chickens were exposed to 14 days of HS (Liu et al., 2022b; Tang et al., 2022). A decrease in the weight of the liver could be due to liver injury resulting from sustained oxidative damage and impaired circulation (Fotouh et al., 2026). In contrast, some researchers reported that liver weight increased and bursa weight decreased in heat-exposed chickens (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2010; Chegini et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2019). Differences in the results might be due to the strain, temperature intensity, and duration of HS. Since the liver is one of the largest organs in the body, its weight loss affects the digestive system and productivity. In agreement, a decrease in the absolute length of the jejunum and ileum, followed by reduced FBW, ADG, and ADFI, was observed in the heat-exposed chickens of the current study.

Plasma biochemical parameters help in identifying the overall health condition of chickens. The enhanced temperature may have severe effects on the plasma biochemicals. In the present study, the cholesterol levels were increased in the heat-exposed chickens. This is consistent with previous studies showing that the level of cholesterol increased after HS (Xie et al., 2015). The levels of triglycerides were decreased in heat-exposed chickens compared to the control, which is in correlation with the results of the duck study in which ducks were exposed to 32°C for 8 h daily for 21 days (He et al., 2019). In general, the liver is the main site of lipogenesis in chickens. A decrease in liver weight may be a key factor in the lower triglycerides and higher cholesterol in heat-exposed chickens. During HS, blood circulation is redirected from central organs to the skin to facilitate heat exchange and reduce body temperature (Wolfenson et al., 1981). Concurrently, hepatic fat synthesis, particularly of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), which are major carriers of cholesterol, is exacerbated (Emami et al., 2020). Consequently, the impaired blood flow in the liver, combined with increased hepatic lipid accumulation, may account for the observed outcomes. While existing literature suggests a potential influence of HS on blood glucose levels (Wang et al., 2021b), the present study did not corroborate these findings. The absence of variability between treatments in our study may be attributed to the specific HS protocol employed. Notably, a prior study utilizing a CHS model as a heat challenge did not observe variations in blood glucose levels between thermal treatments (Sands et al., 2002). The CHS model alternates between thermoneutral and heat exposure periods, enabling the birds to adapt when temperatures are within a normal range, which could restore homeostasis to a limited extent.

Gut integrity is crucial for maintaining intestinal health. In general, modulation in the integrity of the junctions may result in enhanced pathogenic permeability. One of the initial physiological responses of the body to HS involves the regulation of gene expression through DNA methylation (Tzschentke et al., 2002). These variations in methylation levels target the regulation of the expression of tissue-specific genes. Given that the gut is among the organs most susceptible to HS, alterations in the gene-related structural composition of the gut are anticipated to occur during HS exposure (Perini et al., 2021). However, in terms of the expression of tight junction genes, researchers have contradictory opinions. In the current study, the jejunal expression of the ZO1 and ZO2 genes was increased, but no variation was observed in the expression of OCLN, CLDN, and MUC2 genes in heat-exposed chickens. A previous study showed similar results with enhanced expression of ZO1 but no variation in CLDN1 expression in HS chickens (Varasteh et al., 2015). Contrary to this, jejunal expression of ZO1, OCLN, CLDN, and MUC2 genes was significantly decreased in HS chickens (Liu et al., 2022a). Differences in the results might be attributed to the duration of HS, strain, and gender of broilers used in the study. The family of GLP-related genes is linked with anorexia, resulting in suppressed feed intake (Dalvi and Belsham, 2012). In the present study, HS resulted in an increased expression of the GLP2 gene, but a previous study reported that the expression of GLP-related genes decreased under HS (Wang et al., 2021a). Furthermore, chickens exhibit increased respiration rate under HS, leading to panting (Kassim et al., 1982; Kang et al., 2020). In the present study, we hypothesize that the upregulation of GLP2 gene expression may be associated with the chickens' immediate response to feed, which potentially diminishes their attraction to food that could be harmful due to an increased respiratory rate. However, given the lack of consistent findings in the existing literature regarding the relationship between GLP2 gene expression and feed intake in birds, further research is necessary. Overall, the absence of discernible effects of PSSP on the modulation of genes related to gut integrity suggests that its influence may not extend to the regulation of genes involved in thermal adaptation in broilers.

The role of TLRs has been discussed as protective agents against pathogen invasion during enhanced permeability (Goel et al., 2021c). In the present study, the expression of jejunal TLR2 and TLR4 genes was increased in heat-exposed chickens. This is partly in correlation with a previous study where the expression of TLR2 was not affected, but the expression of the TLR4 gene was increased in the jejunum of the HS chickens (Varasteh et al., 2015). An increase in the expression of the TLR4 gene might be due to bacterial penetration arising from intestinal barrier dysfunction. In correlation, a total of thirty genera showed a temperature effect, out of which the abundance of twenty-six genera was increased in HS chickens.

The expression of NOX family-related genes increases under HS and is associated with the production of superoxide and reactive oxygen species (ROS) during heat exposure (Katsuyama, 2010; Kikusato et al., 2015). In correlation, the results of the present study also showed higher NOX1 gene expression in HS chickens. This may be due to increased production of ROS under stress. ROS levels are regulated through a series of antioxidant enzyme actions. Enhanced NOX1 gene expression was followed by an increase in the expression of NRF2, SOD, GPX, HSP70, and HSP90 genes in heat-exposed chickens. This is in correlation with previous studies (Roushdy et al., 2018). NRF2 binds to the antioxidant response elements, triggering the upregulation of antioxidant genes and activating antioxidant defense systems (Chueh et al., 2019). SOD acts as a first line of defense by decomposing free radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which is then further broken down by CAT and GPX into water and molecular oxygen (Rimoldi et al., 2015; Roushdy et al., 2018). Similarly, expression of HSP family-related genes has also been associated with protection against oxidative stress (Favatier et al., 1997). Enhanced expression of antioxidant and HSP family-related genes might be protective, allowing chickens to survive exposure to high temperatures. A previous study suggested that polyphenol supplementation via water can help alleviate HS by modulating antioxidant responses (Yin et al., 2021). However, in the present study, no dose effects were observed in any of the antioxidant enzyme-related genes. The reason behind this could be related to the usage of lower dosages and the difference in the supplementation mode of PSPP in the chicken diet.

Owing to the modulatory properties of the gut microbiota, its role under stress cannot be ignored. The beneficial microbes help in digesting the gut nutrients, making them available to the chicken for various functions. Moreover, there is increasing evidence indicating that the gut microbiota can remodel host chromatin, induce differential splicing, and directly disrupt host signaling pathways (Nichols et al., 2021). Studies utilizing gnotobiotic animals have demonstrated that changes in the expression of histone deacetylase 3 are mediated by the gut microbiome (Alenghat et al., 2013). Additional research has shown the significant impact of probiotics on intestinal gene expression modulation using similar animal models (Selwyn et al., 2016). It is suggested that this close relationship between the gut physiology and its microbiome is partially attributable to the innate ability of microbes to produce short-chain fatty acids via fermentation, which the host cannot synthesize independently. Stress is among those factors that can influence the gut microbiota. For instance, HS tends to modify gut microbiota in chickens (Wang et al., 2018; Ncho, 2025). Hence, in the current study, both intestinal gene expression and gut microbiota dynamics were investigated.

Phylum analysis revealed that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the two most dominant phyla, and heat exposure negatively influenced the abundance of Tenericutes in the chicken cecum. This is consistent with previous studies (Shi et al., 2019). HS in chickens results in metabolic abnormalities (Lu et al., 2017), which lead to decreased abundance of Tenericutes in metabolically unhealthy populations (Yuan et al., 2021). The decreased abundance of Tenericutes in heat-exposed chickens in this study may be a result of metabolic rates. Candidatus Melainabacteria, a phylum related to Cyanobacteria, is known to be reduced in abundance in heat-exposed chickens (Shi et al., 2019; Goel et al., 2022). In contrast, the present study found an increased level of this substance in chickens exposed to HS. The exact reason behind this is not clear. However, the difference in the results could be due to the difference in strain and duration of HS. The abundance of the phylum Candidatus Melainabacteria was decreased in chickens fed 0.5% PSPP compared to the control (0% PSPP). Because of the lack of information on the inconsistent content of PSPP in diseased or healthy samples (Hu et al., 2022), further studies are needed to conclude the exact role of PSPP in PSPP-supplemented chickens exposed to different temperatures.

In the present study, about thirty genera were significantly modulated by heat exposure. Among the five most dominant bacterial genera, three (Ligilactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, and Mediterraneibacter) have shown a temperature effect. The genus Ligilactobacillus is the reclassification of Lactobacillus (Zheng et al., 2020). In the present study, L. araffinosus and L. salivarius were the two most abundant species identified from the genus Ligilactobacillus. The abundance of Ligilactobacillus was decreased in the heat-exposed chickens. L. araffinosus and L. salivarius from the genus Ligilactobacillus are lactic acid-producing bacteria detected in chickens that are known for their probiotic activity for promoting growth and preventing the colonization of infectious bacteria (Pascual et al., 1999; Mountzouris et al., 2007; Tohno et al., 2022). A decrease in the abundance of Ligilactobacillus indicates that heat exposure adversely affects the gut-beneficial bacteria and can be correlated with the lower performance parameters in the same treatment group of the current study. The genus Faecalibacterium is one of the key commensal bacteria that is actively involved in strengthening the intestinal mucosa (Wrzosek et al., 2013). Thus, it may be beneficial for the intestinal health of chickens. However, contradictory results were found regarding the abundance of Faecalibacterium in HS chickens. For instance, when female chickens were exposed to high temperatures (34-38°C) for 28 days, the abundance of Faecalibacterium decreased on the 1st and 7th day of HS (Shi et al., 2019). In contrast, when hens were exposed to 31°C, the abundance of Faecalibacterium increased on day 14 of HS (Wang et al., 2018). In the present study, the abundance of the genus Faecalibacterium was increased when chickens were exposed to 7 days of CHS at 31°C for 6 h daily. The exact reason for the increase in the abundance of the genus Faecalibacterium is not clear and requires further investigation. Mediterraneibacter is another bacterial genus that was increased in heat-exposed chickens. Mediterraneibacter was previously known as Ruminococcus and has been recently reclassified (Togo et al., 2018). Previous studies reported an increase in the abundance of Ruminococcus when birds were exposed to 7 days of HS at 31°C (Wang et al., 2021a).

The genus Vampirovibrio is a pathogenic bacterium found in chickens with infectious conditions that disrupt the normal gut microbiota (Yitbarek et al., 2018; Hovde et al., 2020). Caloramator is a less explored genus, with its precise role in chickens still unknown. However, feeding acidic water to mice was shown to increase Caloramator populations (Johnson et al., 2015). Similarly, the role of the genus Weissella is controversial, with some suggesting it may also have pathogenic properties (Abriouel et al., 2015). In the present study, the populations of all the above-mentioned genera were increased after heat exposure. Furthermore, their abundance in the 0.5% PSPP treatment decreased compared to the control group (0% PSPP). Additionally, the abundance of two more genera, namely Intestinimonas and Lutispora, was decreased by 0.5% PSPP compared to the control. The genus Intestinimonas belongs to favorable bacteria, but its abundance increases in rats fed a high-fat diet to induce obesity (Lin et al., 2019). In fact, the abundance of the genus Lutispora was more common in individuals with disease (Kwak et al., 2019). As HS can increase pathogenic penetrations in the gut, these findings indicate that PSPP at 0.5% may help suppress intestinal pathogens by reducing their abundance (Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2012). However, while the PSPP 0.5% treatment resulted in a reduction of pathogenic microbes, increasing the supplemental dose of PSPP did not produce consistent results. Ideally, to further confirm the effects of PSPP, one would expect either a more pronounced impact of the higher doses on pathogenic bacteria or at least a similar outcome to that observed with the lower dose. This inconsistency is likely attributable to the substantial variability in gut microbiota composition among hosts within a specific treatment group. Indeed, it is not uncommon to observe variability in the gut microbiota profiles of birds, as factors such as feed efficiency, and even the time of day can influence the microbiome (Liang et al., 2017; Kers et al., 2018). Therefore, these factors should be considered when interpreting the results of this trial.

As expected, the current study is associated with some limitations. Although it aimed to evaluate the potential impact of increasing doses of PSPP on various parameters in HS broilers, its effects on host physiology appeared to be minimal. Indeed, aside from feed intake and the expression of the GLP2 gene, no dose effect was observed, unlike the more noticeable effects of HS. This suggests that the effects of HS are more pronounced, and PSPP supplementation might contribute only marginally to restoring homeostasis in this setup. The HS conditions were indeed severe, characterized by a temperature-humidity index relatively high compared to similar previous experimental trials (Zhao et al., 2021). Additionally, the improved ADFI due to PSPP might have influenced the observed outcomes. While greater ADFI could be beneficial from a growth perspective, it might also lead to increased susceptibility to HS, as feed digestion and its utilization are an exothermic process (Lott, 1991). Higher metabolic heat production due to increased feed intake can exacerbate the detrimental effects of increased environmental temperature. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that the core body temperature of broilers increases as they start eating, and cyclic HS further duplicates this phenomenon (Ncho et al., 2026). Thus, while PSPP might have stimulated the growth of beneficial bacteria to combat HS, the resulting higher body weight of the host may have diminished its effects, leading to the observed outcomes. Furthermore, the non-linearity between the improvements associated with PSPP and its dose supplementation further supports this hypothesis. In regard to microbiota analysis, it is important to mention that the relative abundance changes observed following HS are not consistent across studies and are likely to be directed by the initial structure of the birds' microbiota before heat exposure (Ncho, 2025). Furthermore, although some of the aforementioned bacteria are generally either considered beneficial or pathogenic, it is very important to recognize that changes in the relative abundance of bacterial taxa associated with HS occur simultaneously. Hence, isolated population changes of specific taxa may not necessarily indicate significant alterations in microbiota functions (Lyte, 2026). Indeed, greater emphasis should be placed on the overall microbiota composition, and therefore it may be more appropriate to explain the macroscopic changes occurring within the treatment groups. Consequently, further research linking gut microbiota composition with functional pathways is warranted.

The results of this study indicate that higher supplemental doses of PSPP are not consistently beneficial for broiler performance. Furthermore, mitigation strategies for HS should prioritize the restoration of physiological homeostasis and survival over increased growth.
