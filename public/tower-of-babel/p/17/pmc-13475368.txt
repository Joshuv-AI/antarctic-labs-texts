Clinical Characteristics, Microbiology, and In-Hospital Outcomes of Infective Endocarditis at a Tertiary Cardiac Center in Saudi Arabia: A Retrospective Cohort Study

1. Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) remains a life-threatening infection with heterogeneous presentations and an in-hospital mortality that often ranges between 20% and 37% [1,2]. Despite advances in imaging, antimicrobial therapy, and surgery, outcomes remain poor, and the epidemiology continues to evolve [3,4].

In high-income settings, the decline of rheumatic heart disease (RHD) has been accompanied by an aging population and a rising burden of prosthetic valves, intracardiac devices, and healthcare-associated infection, with Staphylococcus aureus now the leading pathogen [7,10], a shift also reported in other contemporary hospital-based series [8].

In Saudi Arabia and the wider Gulf, RHD persists alongside a growing prosthetic-valve-at-risk population, producing a mixed substrate that has been described in regional series [5,6,11]. Contemporary single-center data describing the clinical profile, microbiology, and in-hospital outcomes of IE in this transitional setting nonetheless remain limited.

We therefore describe the demographic characteristics, comorbidities, cardiac substrate, microbiology, complications, and in-hospital outcomes of patients with IE managed at a tertiary cardiac center in Riyadh between October 2020 and December 2024, reported in accordance with the STROBE statement [21].

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at King Fahad Medical City (KFMC), a tertiary cardiac referral center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. We included patients diagnosed with IE between October 2020 and December 2024, identified from hospital diagnostic coding and microbiology records. IE was classified using the modified Duke criteria as definite or possible where documentation permitted [4,14]. The study was approved by the KFMC Institutional Review Board (IRB approval number: 00010471); the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design. The cohort comprises all eligible patients retrievable from the institutional electronic health record (EPIC) archive across this period; the realized date range reflects complete extraction of available cases.

2.2. Participants and eligibility

All patients with a clinical and/or microbiological diagnosis of IE during the study period were eligible. Patients with multiple IE episodes were entered as separate episodes linked to the same identifier. The final analytic cohort comprised 102 patients (Fig. 1).

2.3. Variables and data collection

Two investigators independently extracted demographics, comorbidities, prior cardiac interventions, prosthetic valve details, risk factors, echocardiographic findings, blood culture and serology results, causative organisms, complications (cardiac and neurological), and in-hospital outcomes (mortality, ICU admission, surgery, and length of stay). Outcomes were defined a priori: all-cause in-hospital mortality, IE-related in-hospital death, IE-related 30-day death, ICU admission, and cardiac surgery for IE.

2.4. Handling of missing data

Given the retrospective, record-based design, documentation was incomplete for several variables. We did not impute missing values. Instead, each variable was analyzed using the number of patients with available data as the denominator, and missing or undocumented observations are reported as an explicit category in every table and figure. The principal sources of missingness — including Duke classification (documented in 43 of 102), organism identification (48 of 102), and 30-day outcome (41 of 102) —are shown in the participant flow diagram (Fig. 1). Because missingness is likely non-random, we treat all estimates as descriptive (see Limitations).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive (R version 4. x). Continuous variables were assessed for distribution; normally distributed variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and non-normally distributed variables as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and proportions, each accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated using the Wilson method. Given substantial missingness in key exposures and outcomes, we did not perform inferential modelling of outcome predictors, as such analyses would be unreliable; this decision and its rationale are stated in the Limitations.

3.3. Microbiology

Blood cultures were documented in 92 patients (90.2%); results were positive in 52 (56.5%, 95% CI 46.3–66.2) and negative in 40 (43.5%, 95% CI 33.8–53.7), and not documented in 10. Among the 46 patients with an identified organism, Staphylococcus aureus was most common (30.4%), followed by Candida spp (15.2%), where Candida is first reported as the second most common organism, as fungal endocarditis carries distinct management implications [20]; the distribution is shown in Fig. 3 and Table 3. The high culture-negative rate is consistent with prior antibiotic exposure and with fastidious or atypical organisms: serology identified a patient with combined Coxiella and Brucella positivity. Duke classification, where documented (43 patients), was definite in 28 (65.1%, 95% CI 50.2–77.6) and possible in 15 (34.9%, 95% CI 22.4–49.8).

3.4. Complications

Among patients with cardiac complication data (n = 80), valve regurgitation occurred in 74 (92.5%) and heart failure in 28 (35.0%). Neurological complications across the cohort (N = 102) included embolic stroke in 18 (17.6%), intracerebral haemorrhage in 9 (8.8%), and brain abscess in 2 (2.0%). 3.5. In-hospital outcomes

All-cause in-hospital mortality was 24.5% (24/98; 95% CI 17.0–33.9), and IE-related in-hospital death occurred in 10 patients (9.8%, 95% CI 5.4–17.1). ICU admission was required in 49 of 87 patients (56.3%, 95% CI 45.9–66.3) and cardiac surgery for IE in 30 of 79 (38.0%, 95% CI 28.1–49.0). 3.6. Available follow-up data

Post-discharge information was incompletely recorded and is reported here with caution. Recurrent IE was documented in 80 patients, of whom 3 had a recurrence (3.8%, 95% CI 1.3–10.5). IE-related death during available follow-up was recorded in 3 patients and all-cause death during available follow-up in 7; follow-up status was undocumented in 22–34 patients depending on the variable. Because follow-up was neither systematic nor of standardized duration, these figures should be regarded as incomplete and are not used to estimate late mortality or recurrence rates (see Limitations).

4. Discussion

In this retrospective single-center cohort of 102 patients with IE managed between October 2020 and December 2024, the cardiac substrate was heterogeneous, with RHD and prosthetic valve disease each accounting for approximately one-third of cases; the culture-negative rate was high; and all-cause in-hospital mortality was 24.5%. These findings are broadly consistent with regional and international series [3,5,6,13], while the wide confidence intervals and substantial missingness temper their precision.

4.1. Cardiac substrate

The near-equal contribution of RHD and prosthetic valve disease distinguishes this cohort from Western registries, where degenerative and device-related disease predominate [3,13], and aligns with the transitional epidemiology reported regionally [5,6,9]. The mean age of 46.7 years is younger than in European cohorts, reflecting the persistent burden of RHD.

4.2. Microbiology and culture negativity

Staphylococcus aureus predominance is consistent with contemporary data [7,10,14], and the high culture-negative rate (43.5%) likely reflects prior antibiotic exposure and fastidious/atypical organisms; the observed Coxiella/Brucella positivity supports systematic serology and molecular (16 S rRNA PCR) testing in culture-negative cases, particularly in regions where these zoonoses are endemic [18,19].

4.3. Outcomes

The in-hospital mortality, ICU admission, and surgery rates are within the range of comparable hospital- and ICU-based series [12,13,15–17], and underscore disease severity in this referral population.

4.4. Limitations

This study has important limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design and incomplete, likely non-random documentation introduce selection and information bias that threaten the validity of all estimates; we have therefore reported missingness explicitly (Fig. 1) and present findings as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. Second, Duke classification was documented in fewer than half of patients, introducing uncertainty in case ascertainment. Third, because of this missingness we did not undertake inferential analysis of outcome predictors, which would be unreliable. Fourth, post-discharge follow-up was neither systematic nor complete, precluding reliable estimation of late mortality, recurrence, and reoperation. Fifth, the single-center referral setting limits generalizability to the broader Saudi population. Prospective, protocolized, multicenter data with standardized Duke classification and systematic longitudinal follow-up are required to address these gaps.

5. Conclusion

IE at this tertiary cardiac center presents across a heterogeneous cardiac substrate with substantial inhospital mortality. A high culture-negative rate and considerable missingness limit precision, and the findings should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. They support investment in microbiological diagnostics for culture-negative IE and, importantly, in prospective longitudinal follow-up to characterize the late course of survivors.
