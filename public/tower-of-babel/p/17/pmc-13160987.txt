Efficacy and safety of clipping prior to cyanoacrylate embolization in gastric varices: a single-arm systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Olivia Murga, Tamila Redzanova, Jariel Abreu Perez, Sally Ahmad, Alejandro Chen Liang, Martin Alexander Parra Salazar, Jashanpreet Gill, Muhammad Asfandyar Khan, Chinelo Okonkwo, Victor Sebastian Arruarana, Rashmi Advani, Ernesto Calderón Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric variceal bleeding is a life-threatening complication of portal hypertension. Cyanoacrylate injection is the standard endoscopic treatment that carries a significant risk of ectopic embolization, particularly in patients with gastrorenal shunts. Adding endoscopic clips before injection may enhance safety and efficacy, yet evidence remains limited. This study evaluates the safety and effectiveness of clip-assisted cyanoacrylate injection (clip-ECI).MethodologyA systematic search was conducted on PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library on October 9, 2025, following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251166002). The primary outcome was rebleeding. Secondary outcomes included technical success, adverse events, ectopic embolism, and procedure-related mortality. Meta-analysis was performed using R version 3.4.3 (R Core Team) with a random-effects model expressed as proportions and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSix studies involving 465 participants treated with clip-ECI were included, four in the meta-analysis due to overlapping populations. Most participants were middle-aged males with cirrhosis from hepatitis B, presenting with GOV2 or IGV1 gastric varices; 41.7–100% had gastrorenal shunts. The pooled technical success rate was 99% (95% CI 0.97–1.0; p = 0.97; I2 = 0%), rebleeding 11% (95% CI 0.08–0.16; p = 0.1; I2 = 50.4%), adverse events 20% (95% CI 0.15–0.26; p = 0.37; I2 = 0%), ectopic embolism 3% (95% CI 0.00–0.37; p = 0.01; I2 = 85.1%), deaths attributable to ectopic embolism 1% (95% CI 0.00–0.04; p = 0.67; I2 = 0%) and overall mortality 4% (95% CI 0.01–0.14; p = 0.01; I2 = 80.6%). Compared with conventional injection, clip-ECI showed fewer embolic complications and a non-significant trend toward lower rebleeding.ConclusionClip-ECI appears highly effective with low rebleeding and embolic complication rates, supporting its safety for gastric varices, particularly in patients with gastrorenal shunts. Large multicenter randomized trials are needed to strengthen these findings and establish clinical protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12667-y.

Gastric varices (GVs) develop as a serious complication of portal hypertension [1, 2]. The prevalence of GVs in patients with portal hypertension was reported to comprise 20% [3]. Effective management of gastric varices is critical due to the risk of massive bleeding, higher morbidity, and mortality [4]. GVs are categorized by the Sarin classification as gastroesophageal varices (GOV1, GOV2) and isolated gastric varices (IGV1, IGV2) [3]. Multiple treatment modalities are available for management of GVs, including endoscopic and endovascular approaches to prevent and manage bleeding gastric varices [2, 5]. Amongst endoscopic methods available, variceal ligation, gastric variceal sclerotherapy, and the gold standard—endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection (ECI)—are commonly used [6]. Most of these approaches have shown limited success in managing gastric varices with high rebleeding rates and serious complications, such as severe ectopic embolization due to cyanoacrylate migration into systemic circulation, particularly in patients with gastrorenal shunts (GRS) treated with ECI. This risk highlights the need for clipping prior to embolization.

The clip-assisted ECI (clip-ECI) is a newer technique specifically for GVs with GRS to reduce embolic complications while maintaining effective hemostasis. Early studies have reported promising results, including lower rebleeding rates and fewer embolic complications compared to conventional ECI [7]. However, the current evidence is limited to small, heterogeneous studies with variable outcomes. Consequently, the degree of effectiveness and safety of clip-ECI needs to be characterized.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the available evidence on clip-ECI to clarify the rates of technical success, hemostasis, rebleeding, embolic complications, and mortality to establish the overall therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of clip-ECI in the management of gastric varices, particularly with gastrorenal shunts.

Materials and methods

The present systematic review was conducted following the recommendations and criteria established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA 2020) reporting guidelines [8, 9]. The protocol was pre-registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Review (PROSPERO) with the identifier code CRD420251166002 [10].

Eligibility criteria

Types of study

This systematic review included studies published from the inception of each database up to October 9th, 2025, and was restricted to those available in English and Spanish. Eligible study designs consisted of randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and case–control studies as deemed appropriate to address the research question. Excluded studies comprised case series, cross-sectional studies, dissertations, book chapters, protocol articles, reviews, news articles, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, editorials, commentaries, and other forms of non-original research. Additionally, studies were excluded if they lacked sufficient methodological detail, presented duplicate data, or failed to provide the required information after attempts to contact the original authors.

Types of participants

The review included participants diagnosed with gastric varices who met the predefined inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria consisted of adult patients of 18 years or older with gastric varices. Gastric varices were defined as endoscopically diagnosed varices located on the posterior and/or greater curvature of the cardia (IGV1, GOV2) or on the lesser curvature of the cardia (GOV1). Exclusion criteria included patients < 18 years old, after transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), surgical splenectomy, or hepatocellular carcinoma not meeting the Milan criteria. These criteria are designed to ensure the population is homogeneous and relevant to the intervention or exposure under study.

Types of interventions/exposures and comparators

We included studies that evaluated clip-assisted cyanoacrylate injection (clip-ECI) for the endoscopic management of gastric varices (GOV1, GOV2, IGV1) in adults (18 years and older). Comparative studies were considered eligible if at least one arm received clip-ECI. When a study compared clip-ECI to an intervention outside the review scope (e.g., TIPS), we extracted data only from the clip-ECI arm and excluded the non-clip comparator arm from all analyses to maintain conceptual coherence and avoid unit-of-analysis errors. Non-Clip comparators were neither pooled nor interpreted for between-group effects.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this review was the rate of rebleeding following treatment. Secondary outcomes included ectopic embolism, encompassing incidents of cerebral, splenic, or pulmonary embolic events, as well as various procedure-related adverse events. Studies that did not present data on at least one of the defined outcomes were excluded from this review. Definitions of rebleeding and adverse events differed across studies in terms of outcome ascertainment and follow-up duration; detailed study-specific definitions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Searching methods

A systematic search was conducted on October 9, 2025, on PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Google Scholar, and Cochrane using the following terms: “gastric varices”, “cyanoacrylate” or “glue injection”, and “endoscopic clipping” or “hemostasis”. Search strategy details can be found in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables 1–6).

Selection of studies

All references were exported to Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) [11, 12], and duplicates were removed. Two authors independently (SA and TR) completed the eligibility assessment, first by title and abstract analysis and, subsequently, by full-text assessment. In disagreements between reviewers, a third reviewer (ECM) helped reach a consensus.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted the data (ACL and MAPS), and disagreements were resolved by consensus. When multiple overlapping reports from the same study were identified, the information from the one containing the most relevant information or the first published report was included. Extracted data included sample sizes, intervention types, and measured outcomes. Conventional methods were employed for data extraction, complemented by specialized tools such as WebPlotDigitizer (Automeris, Austin, TX, USA) for digitizing data from graphs, the Cochrane Calculator for statistical conversions, and StatsToDo for advanced calculations [13–15]. These outcomes comprised the cyanoacrylate dose from each group and the technique success percentage. Safety was assessed based on the occurrence of adverse events, including fever, nausea, abdominal discomfort, abdominal pain, and chest pain. Extracted complications included rebleeding, postinjection ulcer bleeding, portal hypertensive gastropathy bleeding, and ectopic embolism cases. Mortality and its causes were also documented. Additional extracted data included subgroup characteristics, such as the percentage of variceal types (IGV1, GOV2), gastrorenal shunt, lost to follow up individuals, and duration of follow up.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

To assess the quality of the studies included in the systematic review, according to the Cochrane guidelines we applied the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), ROBINS for Non-Randomized Controlled Trials for Cohort and cross-sectional studies [16–18]. Two independent reviewers (MAPS and MAK) evaluated the risk of bias in each study, considering the specific criteria and guidelines provided by the respective tools. Any reviewer's discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third, blinded reviewer (ECM).

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using R version 3.4.3 (R Core Team) with the meta and metafor packages [19]. The pooled effect of the outcomes was examined using a random-effects meta-analysis, the DerSimonian-Laird approach [20]. Whenever the number of studies reporting an outcome of interest was insufficient, only a qualitative analysis of the results was performed. Effect sizes were expressed as relative risk (RR), mean difference (MD), or proportions with a 95% confidence interval [21, 22]. The I2 statistic assessed heterogeneity, and the following cut-off values were used for interpretation: < 25%, 25–50%, and > 50% were considered small, medium, and large heterogeneity, respectively. For all outcomes, sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out method were performed to determine the influence of individual studies on the overall effect [23]. Egger's regression test was used to examine publication bias when 10 or more reports with the same outcome were available [24]. Prespecified subgroup analyses were undertaken, when feasible, according to risk of bias, study design, variceal type, and cyanoacrylate dose. Dose-based subgroup analyses were conducted at the study level using an operational definition of higher-dose protocols, defined as those reporting a mean dose > 3 mL or a median dose ≥ 3 mL.

Subgroup analysis according to the presence of a gastrorenal shunt was not feasible because this variable was uniformly reported across all included studies, precluding meaningful stratification. In addition, subgroup analysis by cirrhosis etiology could not be reliably performed, as the underlying causes of hepatitis leading to cirrhosis were inconsistently reported or insufficiently specified across studies, limiting the validity of subgroup classification.

Results

Study selection

In the initial search, a total of 703 records were identified across six databases: PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library. After removing 145 duplicates, 558 records remained for title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 535 records. Of the 25 full-text articles assessed for retrieval, three could not be retrieved, leaving 22 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Among these, six were excluded for inappropriate study design and ten for using the wrong intervention. Ultimately, six studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. Characteristics of the included studies

The cumulative sample size of the six articles was 465 participants treated with clip-assisted endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection, all drawn from a single geographic distribution in China (100%). The mean patient age across studies ranged from 50.3 ± 11.8 to 57.3 ± 11.2 years, with male predominance (69.2%). All studies included patients with cirrhosis and gastric varices, most commonly isolated gastric varices type 1 and gastroesophageal varices type 2. A subset of studies, Li H. et al. (2019) and Li J. et al. (2025) focused only on those with isolated gastric varices type 1 [25, 26]. Moreover, more than 41.7% of the patients included in all the studies had gastrorenal shunts, with Li J. et al. (2025) and Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) [27] including splenorenal shunts. Review of the study sites and timelines revealed partial overlap among the studies (Supplementary Table 8). Zhang M. et al. (2019) and Zhang M. et al. (2023) shared multiple centers and confirmed patient overlap [7, 28], while Li H. et al. (2019) involved some of the same sites at Anhui Geriatric Institute and Beijing Ditan Hospital with Zhang et al. (2023) but represented an earlier pilot phase with focus only on IGV1. Wang et al. (2025) [29] utilized a similar institutional network with Zhang et al. (2023), but the study took place later as part of a randomized controlled trial (RCT), ensuring no temporal overlap. By contrast, Li J. et al. (2025) and Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) were independent of all other datasets, both representing more recent investigations with distinct comparator interventions.

Most of the included studies were comparing the safety and efficacy of clip-assisted endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection (clip-ECI) and conventional endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection (con-ECI) in patients with gastric varices. However, two studies used different comparators: Li J. et al. (2025) compared clip-ECI with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), while Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) compared clip-ECI with EUS-guided ECI (EUS-ECI). Only one study, Zhang M. et al. (2019), evaluated clip-ECI alone, without a comparator arm. Regarding adverse events, all six studies reported on complications presented in Supplementary Table 7. Wang G. et al. (2025) found no increase in procedure-related adverse events, while Li H. et al. (2019) observed lower overall complication rates in clip-ECI (4.35% vs 18%) without statistical significance. Zhang M. et al. (2023) reported a higher incidence of adverse effects in the clip-ECI group, fever being most frequent. Zhang M. et al. (2019) mentioned no symptomatic adverse events without providing more detailed data. On the other hand, Li J. et al. (2025) and Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) documented comparable safety profiles with no clip-related complications. For nonlethal ectopic embolism, results also differed across studies. Wang G. et al. (2025) showed a significantly lower embolism rate in clip-ECI (11.4% vs 42.9%; p = 0.003), and Li H. et al. (2019) reported no embolism in clip-ECI versus one cerebral embolism in con-ECI. Li J et al. (2025) and Xiong YY et al. (2025) recorded no embolic events in either group, whereas Zhang M. et al. (2023) noted a non-significant increase in clip-ECI. Zhang M. et al. (2019) did not perform radiologic screening for embolism.

As for rebleeding, Li H. et al. (2019) showed a significantly lower rebleeding rate with clip-ECI (4.35% vs 18%; p < 0.007), whereas Zhang et al. (2023) found higher cumulative rebleeding in clip-ECI (88.6% vs 73.7%; p = 0.002). Wang G. et al. (2025) and Li J. et al. (2025) reported no significant differences, Zhang M. et al. (2019) observed a 22.8% rebleeding rate without comparator, and Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) found similar one-year rebleeding between clip-ECI and EUS-ECI (23.6% vs 19.4%; p = 0.623). Finally for mortality Wang G. et al. (2025), Zhang M. et al. (2023), Li J. et al. (2025), and Xiong Y.Y. et al. (2025) all reported no statistically significant survival difference between groups (e.g., Li J. et al. 2025: 15.8% vs 12.5%; p = 0.555). Zhang M. et al. (2019) did not report mortality, while Li H. et al. (2019) recorded no deaths.

Beyond clinical outcomes, economic aspects were also examined. Only Xiong (2025) assessed procedural efficiency and cost, showing that clip-ECI significantly shortened procedure time (24.0 ± 9.9 min vs 47.1 ± 21.0 min; p < 0.001) and reduced endoscopic therapy cost (7,523 ± 5,719 CNY vs 11,153 ± 7,679 CNY; p = 0.007) compared with endoscopic ultrasound–guided coil plus cyanoacrylate injection, while achieving comparable technical and clinical outcomes, suggesting potential economic advantages through improved procedural efficiency and reduced resource utilization. Although formal cost-effectiveness analyses were not performed, in comparison with conventional cyanoacrylate injection, the lower incidence of embolic complications observed with clip-ECI may further contribute to potential cost-effectiveness by decreasing downstream utilization related to diagnostic imaging, monitoring, and management of embolic events. Likewise, when compared with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), clip-ECI may represent a less resource-intensive upfront strategy, as TIPS is associated with higher procedural, hospitalization, and complication-related costs. No other studies included economic data, thereby limiting formal cost-effectiveness evaluation and precluding pooled cost analysis. Risk of bias

The risk of bias for the included studies was assessed by using appropriate tools based on the study design. Among the six included studies, two randomized controlled trials (RCT) were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2.0) tool, while the remaining four non-randomized clinical trials were assessed using ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions) tool. For the RCTs, the Cochrane “RoB 2.0 tool” assessed bias regarding process of randomization, measurement of outcomes, missing outcomes data and the reported results. Overall risk of bias in these studies was concluded to be low. For the four remaining non-randomized studies, the ROBINS-I tool assessed for potential bias regarding the selection of participants, how interventions were classified, missing data, the measurement of outcomes, and reported results. Meta analysis

Technical success

In this meta-analysis, we incorporated 4 studies encompassing a total of 331 participants. The pooled proportion of successful outcomes across all interventions was 0.99 with no observed heterogeneity (95% CI 0.97 to 1.0; p = 0.97; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3A). The prediction interval ranged from 0.94 to 1.0. The funnel plot revealed asymmetry suggestive of potential publication bias (Fig. 4A). Adverse events

In this meta-analysis, we incorporated 2 studies including a total of 183 participants. The pooled proportion of adverse events across all interventions was 0.2 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.26; p = 0.37; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3B). The prediction interval ranged from 0.02 to 0.73. The funnel plot revealed asymmetry suggestive of potential publication bias (Fig. 4B). Egger’s test was not performed due to the inclusion of fewer than ten studies per subgroup.

Rebleeding as a complication

In this meta-analysis, we incorporated 4 studies including a total of 331 participants. The pooled proportion of rebleeding events as complications across all interventions was 0.11 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.16; p = 0.10; I2 = 50.4%; Fig. 3E). The prediction interval ranged from 0.07 to 0.19. The funnel plot revealed asymmetry suggestive of potential publication bias (Fig. 4C). Egger’s test was not performed due to the inclusion of fewer than ten studies per subgroup. Subgroup analyses demonstrated a statistically significant difference by variceal type (p = 0.04). No statistically significant differences were identified across the other predefined subgroups (Supplementary Table 9). Influence analysis identified the studies by Wang G. et al. Discussion

In this analysis, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of clip-assisted cyanoacrylate injection in the management of gastric varices. Across two of the studies included in our review, 183 patients underwent the treatment with clip-ECI showing a pooled success rate of 99% (95% CI 97%-100%), demonstrating the feasibility and reliability of this procedure. Meanwhile, adverse events had a pooled incidence of 20% (95% CI 15%-26%), with a 3% (95% CI 0%-37%) occurrence rate of ectopic embolism. The rate of rebleeding within one year was 11% (95% CI 8%-15%), suggesting sustained hemostasis and long-term bleeding control. Procedure-related mortality was infrequent, reported in 4% (95% CI 1%-13%) of patients. This indicates that clip-ECI is a generally safe interventional choice.

Among the subgroup analyses, only variceal type was significantly associated with rebleeding, indicating that anatomical features of the varices may influence treatment durability. No significant effect modification was observed for cirrhosis etiology, presence of a gastrorenal shunt, or procedural factors such as glue dose or clip number, although these findings may be limited by small subgroup sizes. Further well-designed, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to better characterize gastric variceal recurrence and validate these subgroup observations.

The outcomes of our analysis align with existing knowledge in this field. For example, pre-existing data from Chandra et al. (2018), assessing standard ECI in a U.S. cohort, revealed a technical success of 96% but a higher incidence of rebleeding (19.5%) [30]. Another study form Brazil evaluated the odds for gastric variceal bleeding, with hemostasis rate of 92% and 21% of rebleeding [31]. In comparison to our pooled results, a higher rate of sustained hemostasis (99%) was demonstrated, likely due to the added mechanical support provided by clipping. This provides information into a new relievable approach that needs to be considered when treating gastric varices. In terms of safety, our analysis has shown markedly lower incidence of ectopic embolism (3%), in contrast to higher embolic rates in previous conventional ECI studies. The previously mentioned studies by Chandra et al. (2018) reported ectopic embolism in 17% of cases, whereas the cohort by Lôbo et al. (2019) reported a 25% rate of asymptomatic pulmonary embolism due to the coil procedure and 50% incidence in the conventional cyanoacrylate injection group. Both studies report higher incidence of ectopic embolism than in the clip-ECI population, suggesting that the addition of Clip to the procedure can reduce this adverse event. Nevertheless, a randomized controlled trial by Jhajharia et al. (2025), provide more information about how the procedures can be complemented and emphasized the importance of hybrid endoscopic procedures, particularly those enhancing the variceal occlusion and controlling injection flow, including coiling and glue showing reduced rate of rebleeding in contrast to using glue independently [32]. At 12 weeks, variceal reappearance in coil and CYA group was 12.5% vs 19.2% in the CYA only group. Finally, a cohort study by Chen et al. (2024) also contributed to prove the significance of hybrid EUS-coil methods, as proved to reduce the rate of rebleeding (4.8%) compared to conventional techniques (27.8%) [33]. With this, the use of EUS-coil methods also shows a significant reduction in embolic events, making it a viable alternative. However, due to the limited availability of equipment and advanced expertise, EUS-guided procedures may not be universally available. In this context, our findings provide supporting evidence of clip-ECI as a feasible, accessible, safe, and effective alternative.

From an economic perspective, evidence remains limited, with only one included study reporting cost data. Xiong (2025) [27] showed that clip-ECI significantly reduced procedure time and direct endoscopic therapy costs compared with endoscopic ultrasound–guided coil plus cyanoacrylate injection while achieving comparable clinical outcomes. Although formal cost-effectiveness analyses were not performed, reduced procedure time and fewer embolic complications relative to conventional cyanoacrylate injection may decrease downstream healthcare utilization. In addition, compared with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), clip-ECI may represent a less resource-intensive initial treatment strategy, given the higher procedural, hospitalization, and complication-related costs associated with TIPS.

Collectively, these external studies underscore how the clip-ECI integrates the simplicity of endoscopic injection with the enhanced safety of advanced interventions. Stabilization of the varix through clipping may limit glue dissemination, reducing the risk of embolism without jeopardizing the hemostatic effect of the procedure. Clip-ECI has a lower mortality rate (4%) compared to other modalities used for treatment of gastric varices. Although most studies in this review were from one country, it does provide a new perspective. It samples the population with different comorbidity, risk profile, intervention cause, and timing, that is not the same as previously published,—it adds more heterogeneity. Clipping has been used in other various non-variceal conditions such as Mallory-Weiss tears; Dielafoy lesions; angiodysplasias; peptic ulcer and post-operative anastomosis bleeding, post-polypectomy, mucosectomy, and sphincterotomy bleeding [34]. Clipping has been effective in these conditions by achieving higher success rates in the prevention of rebleeding study also aligns with this. Taken together, our findings reinforce that clip-ECI stands out as a reliable, safe and practical therapeutic option, bridging the gap between conventional ECI and hybrid procedures. The focus of future studies should be on maximizing safety and expanding the applicability of this approach across various clinical settings.

Limitations and future perspectives

A major strength of this work lies in its systematic methodology, which incorporated an extensive literature search, standardized data extraction, and rigorous quality assessment of the articles which were included for analysis. By integrating findings from multiple independent investigations, this analysis enhances the precision of effect estimates regarding both efficacy and safety outcomes. Another important strength is the inclusion of recent publications of up to 2025, reflecting contemporary endoscopic practice and advancements in techniques. The study also evaluated a broad range of clinically relevant outcomes including general complication rates, embolic events, and procedure-related adverse events to allow for a balanced assessment of therapeutic performance. The consistency of the results from multiple independent investigations further strengthens the reliability of the conclusions drawn from this analysis. Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations regarding this analysis. The first one is that it includes only four studies with relatively small sample sizes, which limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results of the studies to a whole population. Secondly, due to variations in the definitions of adverse events and rebleeding, a supplementary table was added to summarize the criteria used in each study presented in Supplementary Table 7. While a meta-analysis was performed, these outcomes were interpreted with caution due to definitional heterogeneity, which limits direct comparability. Additionally, the four included studies were conducted in China, which introduces potential regional and publication bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings for other populations. Finally, the variations of follow-up duration, the dosage of cyanoacrylate dose, and the procedural techniques used may have contributed to the differences in the outcomes. Furthermore, some studies lacked complete data on complications or control groups; this reduces our ability to draw strong and definitive conclusions. Funnel plot asymmetry was observed across all outcomes, but Egger’s test and subgroup analyses could not be performed due to fewer than ten studies per outcome. As a result, potential publication bias could not be formally assessed, which limits interpretation of the pooled results. Future large-scale, multicenter, multiregional and intercontinental randomized clinical trials with standardized protocols and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the clinical superiority and safe profile of the clip-ECI.

Conclusion

Our systematic review on clip-ECI demonstrated a very high technical success rate with low rebleeding and mortality rates, indicating that it is an effective and relatively safe intervention. Its main advantage is a significant reduction in embolism risk. However, heterogeneity, small sample size and possible population bias among studies limit the precision and strength of this finding, underscoring the need for larger, multicenter randomized trials with extended follow-up to confirm these results across diverse populations.
