Regulatory effects of molasses on microbial community and amino acid profiles during alfalfa high-temperature fermentation
Authors: Jingyi Liu, Zhijun Wang, Junfeng Hao, Muqier Zhao, Jiaqi Yu, Yushan Jia, Gentu Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The harvest period of alfalfa is highly concentrated in midsummer. To clarify the impact of high temperature on the fermentation process and the regulatory effect of fermentation methods on microbial community structure and amino acid metabolism, this experiment designed treatments involving fermenting alfalfa with molasses additives for 60 days under two temperature conditions: 35 °C and ambient temperature. High-temperature molasses fermentation promotes thermotolerant bacteria proliferation. Treated alfalfa's bacterial community differs sharply from fresh samples, with the most obvious changes at high temperatures. High temperature lowers Weissella's relative abundance, but it still reproduces in molasses-rich environments. It restricts glutamine-glutamic acid interconversion, reducing most amino acids. Molasses reshapes microbiota, regulates amino acids, and better inhibits degradation at high temperatures. In conclusion, temperature alters fermented alfalfa's microbial structure and amino acid expression; molasses aids amino acid retention under high heat.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is recognized as an important crop of global significance due to its high nutritional value and digestibility and is widely used as a protein source in diets (Liu, Zhao, et al., 2024). The fermentation process relies primarily on the activity of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) in a sealed, anaerobic environment, where LAB convert water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) into organic acids that lower pH and inhibit undesirable microorganisms. Temperature is a key factor in fermentation, directly affecting microbial activity and metabolic pathways (Ren et al., 2020). Given that alfalfa is typically harvested during midsummer, elevated ambient temperatures exacerbate storage challenges and reduce the effective shelf life of alfalfa. To mitigate these issues, optimizing fermentation conditions is crucial to regulating the microbial community, suppressing the activity of spoilage organisms such as Clostridium, and preventing unwanted biochemical reactions like butyric acid fermentation and protein degradation, thereby minimizing nutrient loss. In terms of economic benefits, after overcoming the problem of high temperature in production, the waste of alfalfa raw materials can be effectively avoided and the loss during high-temperature fermentation can be reduced. In addition, by optimizing the fermentation process using molasses additives, the resulting product can replace other high-cost protein raw materials, so as to achieve the goal of reducing costs.

Previous studies have shown that high temperatures also constrain lactic acid fermentation, particularly during the initial phase when residual oxygen supports aerobic respiration in plant tissues and microorganisms. This process releases heat, rapidly elevating the temperature of the fermenting mass, which in turn disrupts microbial dynamics and fermentation quality (Chen et al., 2013). Due to its high buffering capacity and relatively low WSC and dry matter content, alfalfa often undergoes suboptimal fermentation when ensiled alone. Additives are thus commonly used to improve fermentation quality. Sucrose has previously been used to support LAB growth and enhance lactic acid production via pyruvate metabolism (Li et al., 2023). Molasses, a cost-effective by-product of the sugar industry, contains abundant WSC and serves as a sustainable additive that reduces reliance on chemical preservatives (Gül, 2023; Xie et al., 2021). When added at 3–6 %, molasses provides sufficient energy to LAB and promotes efficient conversion of WSC into organic acids, particularly lactic acid.

Amino acids, which contain both amino and carboxyl groups, are the basic building blocks of proteins. Studying amino acid metabolism in fermented alfalfa is crucial for understanding the mechanisms of amino acid retention, degradation, and transformation. This provides valuable insights for optimizing the fermentation process and evaluating the fermentation quality of alfalfa. Despite its importance, research on amino acid metabolism in alfalfa fermentation remains limited. Fermentation is a dynamic microbial process marked by succession and interspecies interactions that shape metabolite profiles. In recent years, in fermentation processes, microbial communities have become a key indicator for evaluating fermentation quality; additionally, attention to metabolites has gradually increased. However, in previous studies, these two have always been analyzed separately. This study attempts to clarify the microbe-metabolism interaction mechanism in high-temperature alfalfa fermentation, reveal the combined regulatory rules of high temperature and molasses, establish key association targets through integrated microbiome-metabolome analysis, and thereby enhance the depth of mechanistic interpretation of the research. The third generation Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) single molecule, real-time sequencing technology (SMRT) generates long reads, which increases the sensitivity and accuracy of classification and allows for relatively high taxonomic resolution to the species level(Yan et al., 2019). Targeted amino acid metabolomics was conducted using MRM on the SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ LC-MS/MS platform, with quantification based on external calibration and isotope-labeled internal standards. This study focuses on the high-temperature fermentation of alfalfa with molasses, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms of microbial succession and metabolic pathways of differential amino acids under different temperatures.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and mini silos preparation

The alfalfa variety used in this study was Medicago sativa ‘WL168’, planted in June 2022 at the experimental farm of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University, located in the interior of the Eurasian continent (40°51′N, 111°38′E). The region experiences a temperate continental monsoon climate, characterized by distinct seasonal variation, with an average annual precipitation of approximately 400 mm and an altitude of about 1000 m.

Experimental procedures were conducted from June to August 2024 at the same base. Alfalfa was harvested at the early flowering stage. Freshly cut forage was collected into sterile centrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C. The remaining material was transferred to the laboratory, where fermentation was initiated once the moisture content fell below 60 %. The forage was chopped into 2–3 cm segments, thoroughly mixed, and supplemented with molasses at 2 % (w/w) while stirring continuously to ensure even distribution. Control samples received an equivalent volume of distilled water. In previous studies, it was found that when the content of molasses was added at 1.5 %, it was more conducive to the retention of nutrients compared to other treatment groups (Xie et al., 2021). In addition, when the addition ratio is 2.5 % and 5 %, the effect of 2.5 % on improving the fermentation quality is better than that of 5 % (Wang et al., 2017). Considering the comprehensive factors of improving fermentation quality and economic cost, we choose to add 2 % of molasses by fresh weight for auxiliary fermentation.

Each sample (300 g) was packed into polyethylene vacuum bags (300 mm × 400 mm, food-grade, provided by Shenyang Huasheng Plastic Packaging Co., Ltd.) and sealed using a vacuum packaging machine (Model DZ-500/2E, Hefei Hanjie Packaging Machinery Co., Ltd., China). Both the treatment group and the control group were evenly divided into two parts: one half was subjected to high-temperature fermentation at 35 °C, and the other half was fermented under indoor environmental conditions. Both groups of samples were treated with the same light-avoidance method, and the fermentation period lasted for 60 days.

Microbial community composition and amino acid metabolic pathways were analyzed for the following groups: molasses-treated at high temperature (GT), molasses-treated at ambient temperature (CT), high-temperature control (GCK), and ambient-temperature control (CCK).

Sequencing-based analysis of microbial diversity

Total microbial community DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The quality of the extracted DNA was evaluated by 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Using primers designed to target the V3-V4 region (338F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′; 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) of the 16S rDNA gene. The obtained raw fastq files underwent multiplexing, quality filtering using Trimmomatic, and merging using FLASH. Operational taxonomic units (ASVs) were clustered at a 97 % similarity threshold using UPARSE, and chimeric sequences were identified and eliminated using UCHIME. The classification of each 16S rRNA gene sequence was conducted using the RDP classifier algorithm and the Silva (SSU123) 16S rRNA database, with a confidence threshold set at 70 %.

Targeted metabolomic analysis of amino acids

50 mg solid sample were accurately weighed, and the metabolites were extracted using a 400 μL methanol: water (4:1, v/v) solution with 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanin as internal standard. The mixture was left to settle at −10 °C and then processed using a high-throughput tissue crusher Wonbio-96c (Shanghai Wanbo biotechnology co., LTD) at 50 Hz for 6 min, then followed by ultrasound at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5 °C. The samples were placed at −20 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 13,000g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant were carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS/MS analysis. The instrument platform for LC-MS analysis is UHPLC-Q Exactive system of Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Statistical analysis

Microbial community data analysis and targeted amino acid metabolic analysis were conducted using the online Majorbio I-Sanger Cloud Platform.

The fermentation process involves complex changes in microbial communities (Jiang et al., 2023). Results from microbial diversity indices indicated that only the GT group exhibited higher diversity than the Y group after fermentation, while most other treatment groups showed reduced diversity. This finding aligns with previous reports that bacterial diversity in fermented alfalfa is generally lower than in fresh material (Zhao et al., 2021), likely due to the rapid growth of fermentation-adapted bacteria that suppress the proliferation of others. In our study, the GT group demonstrated significantly greater diversity, whereas the CT group exhibited lower diversity indices than the Y group, with the exception of the Shannon index. Earlier research suggested that excessive microbial diversity may hinder fermentation success, as dominance by a few beneficial microbes is often associated with more stable fermentation outcomes (Ni et al., 2018). Thus, the use of molasses under high-temperature conditions may promote the growth of thermotolerant bacteria using abundant substrates, while suppressing beneficial fermentative bacteria. This interpretation is supported by previous findings that thermophilic microbes tend to proliferate during the later fermentation stages, contributing to increased microbial complexity (Guan et al., 2020). During the alfalfa silage process, excessively high temperatures can act as key environmental stress factors, disrupting the structural balance of bacterial communities and thus serving as one of the important factors affecting their stability. Meanwhile, high temperatures may stimulate the growth of thermophilic microorganisms that compete with lactic acid bacteria. Additionally, Yang and Wang (2018) reported that different additive pretreatment strategies significantly affect microbial community structure. To further explore these dynamics, we conducted β-diversity analysis. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) clearly showed that bacterial communities in the fermented samples were distinctly separated from those in fresh alfalfa, which showed minimal variation. Among all treatments, the GT group exhibited the most pronounced microbial community shift. Among control groups, the CCK group displayed more evident separation under ambient fermentation conditions.

Temperature is widely recognized as a critical factor influencing bacterial communities, and shifts in microbial composition may lead to variations in fermentation quality (Hu et al., 2024). In this study, Proteobacteria was the dominant phylum in fresh alfalfa but was replaced by Firmicutes after fermentation. This phylum typically dominates during fermentation, likely due to its ability to thrive under acidic and anaerobic conditions (Lu et al., 2021). Similar findings have been reported in Italian ryegrass, where Firmicutes became predominant following fermentation (Xia et al., 2023).

Taxonomic profiling at the genus and species levels revealed distinct community structures among treatments. In fresh alfalfa, Pantoea was the dominant genus, representing a non-lactic acid bacterium (non-LAB) commonly found in raw plant material. However, it was significantly suppressed after fermentation. In contrast, Weissella became the dominant genus in both the GT and CT groups. Its lower abundance in the GT group compared to the CT group suggests that elevated temperatures may partially inhibit its growth. Weissella is a Gram-positive, coccoid lactic acid bacterium typically involved in the initial stages of fermentation (Fusco et al., 2015).

Interestingly, Weissella remained dominant throughout the entire fermentation process in both GT and CT groups supplemented with molasses. This may be attributed to its strong capacity to utilize a wide range of sugars, enabling it to flourish in the nutrient-rich environment provided by molasses. Bai et al. (2021) similarly reported persistent dominance of Weissella cibaria in fermented alfalfa, indicating its robust adaptability and competitiveness. Previous studies also found Weissella to dominate in untreated grasses and oats during natural fermentation (Eikmeyer et al., 2013; Romero et al., 2017), although differences in material characteristics may account for the discrepancies with our results.

Moreover, Wu et al. (2024) reported that Weissella and Enterococcus dominated in fermented alfalfa, contributing to its unique sensory attributes. Under anaerobic conditions, Enterococcus and Lactobacillus are known to play a key role in initiating lactic acid fermentation (Kang et al., 2021). In agreement, our study found Enterococcus to be dominant in both molasses-treated and control groups. Additionally, Rhabdanaerobium, a thermotolerant genus (Liu et al., 2017), exhibited high relative abundance in the GCK group, suggesting its adaptation to elevated temperatures. However, its lower abundance in the GT group may reflect a limited ability to utilize molasses and weaker competitiveness in nutrient-rich conditions compared to Weissella and Enterococcus.

Samples in the unfermented Y group exhibited minimal separation, suggesting uniformity. In contrast, all fermented groups showed varying degrees of dispersion. Notably, the GT and GCK groups, which were fermented under high-temperature conditions, displayed greater intra-group separation compared to the CT and CCK groups fermented at room temperature. Moreover, the GT and CT groups exhibited more pronounced dispersion than their respective controls, highlighting the substantial impact of molasses supplementation on metabolic profiles. The GT group also demonstrated a greater separation from all other groups, suggesting a significant metabolic shift in amino acid composition driven by the combined influence of high temperature and molasses. This synergy likely accelerates molasses utilization and promotes amino acid biosynthesis.

Molasses acts as a readily fermentable substrate during ensiling, reshaping the microbial community by enriching dominant LAB and suppressing undesirable bacteria, thereby regulating the synthesis and degradation of amino acids. Previous research has shown that LAB can efficiently utilize amino acids from alfalfa to enhance fermented alfalfa quality and aroma (Liu, Du, et al., 2024). Additionally, amino acids may provide fermentative microbes with an auxiliary nitrogen source that facilitates lactic acid production (Sun et al., 2023).

PLS-DA analysis and model validation of alfalfa after 60 days of fermentation

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) is a supervised multivariate statistical method based on the classical partial least squares regression model. It requires the prior definition of sample groups, which introduces an implicit Y matrix to the model. This approach enhances discrimination among groups and facilitates the identification of key differences in metabolic patterns.

The PLS-DA score plots (Fig. 6) provide a clear visual representation of the classification efficiency, where greater separation between groups indicates more distinct classification. In the comparison between temperature treatments (GT vs. CT and GCK vs. CCK; Fig. 6A and C), the comparison results of PLS-DA between the GCK group and the CCK group showed that there was a significant separation between the two groups (no sample overlap) in the score graph. This result indicates that under the control group without molasses intervention, the regulatory effect of temperature difference on the amino acid metabolic profile is independent and significant, and high temperature can independently drive significant changes in amino acid metabolic characteristics. In the presence of molasses, each treatment group formed distinct and non-overlapping clusters, indicating relatively high classification specificity.Fig. 6Amino acid PLS-DA analysis and model validation of alfalfa after 60 days of fermentation. Model validation using permutation tests (Fig. 6B and D) confirmed the robustness of the models. For the GT vs. CT comparison, R2Y and Q2 values were 0.995 and 0.994, respectively; for GCK vs. CCK, the values were 0.981 and 0.968. Permutation tests for CT vs. CCK and GT vs. GCK yielded R2Y and Q2 values of 0.998 and 0.996, respectively, underscoring the strong discriminative capability and reliability of the PLS-DA models.

As observed in the heatmap, most amino acids in the Y group were downregulated compared to the fermented treatments, with the exceptions of l-glutamine and L-(+)-arginine, which were upregulated. Previous studies have reported that the levels of valine, isoleucine, and leucine are significantly higher in fermented feed compared to fresh forage (Guo et al., 2008), which aligns with our current findings.

Interestingly, l-glutamine was downregulated across all fermentation treatments (GT, CT, GCK, and CCK) compared to the Y group. l-glutamine is a multifunctional amino acid essential for nutrient metabolism and protein synthesis, and is known to interconvert with L-glutamic acid during microbial metabolism (Liu et al., 2025). Notably, L-glutamic acid levels increased in the GT and CCK groups but decreased in the CT and GCK groups, consistent with earlier findings (Winters et al., 2000). This may indicate that after the synthesis of L-glutamic acid, LAB participated in its subsequent metabolism. LAB can convert L-glutamic acid to γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) via glutamate decarboxylase, especially under acidic conditions (Fusco et al., 2015). In our study, the CT group exhibited higher relative abundance of Weissella than the GT group, suggesting a stronger involvement of Weissella in L-glutamic acid metabolism.

The decrease of L-glutamic acid in the GCK group suggests that high temperature, in the absence of regulatory interventions, may hinder the interconversion between l-glutamine and L-glutamic acid. This further highlights the role of molasses in modulating amino acid metabolism under high-temperature fermentation conditions.

Among all fermentation treatments, only the GT group exhibited upregulation of L-(−)-threonine, L-(+)-arginine, L-asparagine anhydrous, l-serine, and L-(−)-tyrosine. All other treatments showed downregulation of these amino acids. These results suggest that the high-temperature and high-sugar fermentation environment exerts a positive regulatory effect on the synthesis or preservation of these amino acids.

From an overall perspective, the GT group showed higher expression levels of most amino acids compared to other treatments, with amino acid profiles in the GT and CT groups being relatively similar, although the GT group retained more amino acids. The use of molasses effectively suppressed amino acid degradation during fermentation, and its effect was more pronounced at high temperature than at ambient temperature.

The GCK group showed downregulation in 14 amino acids, while the CCK group showed downregulation in 8 amino acids. This indicates that fermentation without molasses under elevated temperature conditions may negatively affect amino acid metabolism. A comparative analysis between GT and GCK revealed contrasting expression patterns in most amino acids, suggesting that the addition of molasses under high-temperature conditions positively regulates amino acid metabolism. However, this regulatory effect was less evident when comparing the CT and CCK groups, indicating that temperature may be a critical factor enhancing the effect of molasses.

The glycolysis-TCA (tricarboxylic acid) cycle pathway is essential for synthesizing fundamental metabolites such as amino acids and NADH (Liu et al., 2022). Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), a key intermediate in the glycolytic-TCA cycle, also serves as a precursor for amino acid biosynthesis (Li et al., 2021). In our study, molasses supplementation provided sufficient fermentable substrates to enhance glycolysis, thereby supplying essential precursors for amino acid biosynthesis.

Amino acid metabolism plays a vital role in the formation of metabolites during fermentation, as many amino acids are essential for the growth and protein synthesis of LAB (Fu et al., 2022). Previous reports have suggested that supplemental sugar during the early fermentation stage accelerates lactic acid production (Wang et al., 2020), rapidly lowers pH, and suppresses bacterial enzymatic activity, thereby reducing bacterial peptide utilization (Guo et al., 2007). Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that molasses supplementation effectively promoted the synthesis and retention of amino acids, especially in the GT group, where most amino acids were more abundant than in the other treatments. These results suggest that temperature should not be overlooked, as molasses addition under high-temperature conditions can significantly enhance amino acid synthesis during alfalfa fermentation.

Previous research has shown that L-alanine can be synthesized from L-aspartic acid via aspartate β-decarboxylase (ASD) (Cui et al., 2021), suggesting that a decrease in L-aspartic acid may contribute to reduced L-alanine synthesis. These findings imply that elevated temperature can exert an inhibitory effect on amino acid biosynthesis.

In contrast, following molasses supplementation, the GT group showed increased relative levels of most amino acids compared to the CT group. According to Bai et al. (2022), elevated temperatures enhance biological interactions and generally lead to more active metabolic processes and faster microbial growth. However, in the present study, we attribute the improved amino acid synthesis and retention primarily to the presence of molasses rather than temperature alone.

Specifically, the levels of L-(+)-lysine, L-asparagine anhydrous, L-(−)-threonine, l-serine, and L-glutamic acid were significantly higher in the GT group compared to the CT group. It is well established that protein hydrolysis during fermentation releases free amino acids, which can then be decarboxylated by spoilage bacteria to form.

biogenic amines (Mao et al., 2024). High-temperature fermentation conditions are known to inhibit the growth of such spoilage bacteria, thereby reducing amino acid decarboxylation and preserving amino acid levels.

Additionally, we compared amino acid profiles between treatments at the same temperature (Fig. 8C and D). Under 35 °C fermentation, amino acid levels differed significantly between treatments. In the GT group, the relative abundances of all amino acids were higher than those in the GCK group, except for L-alanine, L-tryptophan, and L-hydroxyproline, which showed slight decreases. These findings further confirm the regulatory effect of molasses on amino acid synthesis and preservation under high-temperature conditions. The minor reductions observed may be attributed to transamination reactions (Gauthankar et al., 2021).

Under ambient temperature conditions, the CT group showed significantly lower levels of L-alanine, L-tryptophan, glycine, L-isoleucine, L-glutamic acid, and L-(−)-threonine compared to the CCK group. Collectively, these results indicate that the impact of molasses on amino acid profiles is more pronounced under high-temperature fermentation, highlighting the importance of fermentation conditions in optimizing amino acid metabolism in fermented alfalfa.

Integrative analysis of microbiomics and targeted amino acid metabolomics

Microbial communities play a pivotal role in the fermentation of alfalfa. Different microorganisms possess distinct enzymatic systems capable of degrading plant proteins into amino acids or further metabolizing amino acids into other compounds. To explore the interplay between microbial dynamics and amino acid metabolism, we performed an integrated analysis combining microbiome profiling with targeted amino acid metabolomics.

To evaluate the congruency between these two omics datasets, we applied Procrustes analysis. As shown in Fig. 9, a clear alignment between microbial community composition and amino acid metabolite profiles was observed across the different treatment groups, indicating a significant correspondence in variation trends. Notably, L-alanine, L-tryptophan, and L-hydroxyproline were significantly negatively correlated with both Weissella and Lactiplantibacillus. These two genera were dominant in the GT and CT groups. It indicates that after adding molasses for fermentation, lactic acid bacteria may preferentially metabolize certain amino acids, resulting in a decrease in the availability of these amino acids. In contrast, the majority of amino acids showed positive correlations with Weissella, and their levels were higher in treatment groups where Weissella abundance was elevated compared to their respective controls. A previous study by Liu et al. (2023) reported that Enterococcus was significantly negatively correlated with L-phenylalanine, L-(+)-arginine, and L-glutamic acid (P < 0.05). In our study, Enterococcus was also significantly negatively correlated with L-phenylalanine and L-(+)-arginine, while its correlation with L-glutamic acid was positive but not statistically significant.

These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in plant material, fermentation temperature, and the use of additives, which could influence both microbial dynamics and metabolic pathways during fermentation. Since this study only selected the 60th day as the sole evaluation time point to analyze the variation patterns of microbial community structure and amino acids, and no dynamic sampling gradient data were used to characterize the specific metabolic processes of microorganisms and amino acids in the fermentation system, this resulted in the study's findings on molasses-mediated regulation of alfalfa fermentation under high-temperature conditions being mostly based on inferences drawn from the endpoint state, failing to fully cover the entire fermentation cycle.

Conclusion

Based on microbial sequencing results, fermentation at 35 °C with molasses supplementation promoted the proliferation of thermotolerant bacteria by providing sufficient substrates, and significantly altered the microbial community structure. Results from both GT and CT groups indicated that Weissella possesses a strong ability to utilize sugar sources. Amino acid metabolism analysis further revealed that temperature variations led to significant differences in amino acid profiles and restricted the interconversion between glutamine and glutamic acid. The addition of molasses under high-temperature fermentation conditions improved amino acid metabolism, with its effect being more pronounced at elevated temperatures compared to room temperature.

Collectively, based on the 60-day fermentation results, the findings of this study provide a new theoretical reference for optimizing the high-temperature fermentation process of alfalfa, and particularly highlight the regulatory potential of molasses addition on amino acid metabolism during high-temperature fermentation.
