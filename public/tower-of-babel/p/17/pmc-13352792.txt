The impact of hospital bed supply on medical insurance expenditure in China (2011–2024): evidence on Roemer’s Law and threshold effects
Authors: Jiacheng Zou, Jiazhen Yao, Lingqing Zhao, Tianming Zhao, Shaohua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid expansion of healthcare infrastructure may exert increasing pressure on the sustainability of social health insurance. China’s concurrent pursuit of universal coverage, coupled with extensive hospital construction, offers a valuable context for examining whether the patterns observed in China align with Roemer’s Law (1961), which is often summarized as ‘a hospital bed built is a bed filled’ within an insured population. This study investigates the relationship between hospital bed density and insurance expenditure to deepen our understanding of the factors associated with the growth of healthcare costs.MethodsWe compiled a panel dataset encompassing 31 Chinese provinces covering the period from 2011 to 2024. To strengthen identification and address potential endogeneity concerns, we employed two-way fixed effects models alongside instrumental variable (IV-2SLS) estimation. Mediation analysis was employed to investigate potential pathways, while panel threshold regression was utilized to examine nonlinear patterns in the relationship between supply and expenditure.ResultsThe baseline estimates indicated a positive association, suggesting that higher provincial bed density correlates with increased province-level insurance spending. Mediation analysis revealed that the Average Length of Stay may serve as a potential aggregate pathway, accounting for 17.8% of the estimated relationship. Furthermore, threshold regression analysis indicated a possible nonlinear pattern, with an estimated threshold of 7.271 beds per 1,000 population. Below this threshold, hospital bed density is positively related to insurance spending (β = 0.217); however, above this threshold, the association loses statistical significance. Regional analysis demonstrated that the positive association was most pronounced in the western region, while no statistically significant association was observed in the northeastern region.ConclusionOur findings support a conditional, province-level interpretation of Roemer’s Law within the Chinese context, indicating that the association between bed density and insurance expenditure varies across institutional and capacity settings. Specifically, higher provincial bed density is more strongly associated with a longer average length of stay at the provincial level than with increased admission volumes. Beyond the exploratory threshold estimate, the marginal association between additional bed supply and insurance expenditure appears to weaken. These results suggest that payment reform, length-of-stay management, and regulatory oversight should be prioritized alongside careful capacity planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00768-3.Keywords: Medical insurance fund, Hospital bed supply, Roemer’s Law, Threshold effect

Introduction

The financial sustainability of social health insurance systems has emerged as one of the most critical challenges in health policy during the 21st century [1]. It is increasingly strained by population aging [2], the epidemiological transition towards chronic diseases [3], and the escalating costs of medical technology [4]. These pressures are particularly acute in developing nations, where the simultaneous expansion of healthcare infrastructure and social health insurance systems imposes unique fiscal strain [5]. Such fiscal constraints jeopardize not only the operational stability of insurance funds but also the realization of Universal Health Coverage (UHC), a key Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) aimed at ensuring equitable access to quality services without financial hardship [6].

China provides an important example of this dual expansion. Since the 2009 health reform, China has achieved near-universal coverage, with over 1.3 billion enrollees (exceeding 95%) by 2024, thereby establishing the world’s largest basic health insurance network [7]. However, this achievement has also involved substantial fiscal costs. National basic health insurance expenditure surged from 2.1 trillion to 2.97 trillion yuan between 2020 and 2024, reflecting an average annual growth rate of 9.1% [8, 9]. Although the fund maintained a slight aggregate surplus in 2024 [10], regional disparities continued to persist [11]. Projections indicate that without intervention, the urban employee basic health insurance pooling fund will face imminent annual deficits, with cumulative deficits expected by 2033 [12].

This fiscal trajectory coincides with an unprecedented expansion in hospital infrastructure. Between 2009 and 2019, the total number of hospital beds more than doubled, increasing by 104% to 8.81 million. By 2024, the inventory was recorded at 10.29 million, a rate of expansion that outstrips both population growth and economic development [13]. Given that hospitals account for approximately 60% of total health expenditure, this rapid capacity expansion raises critical questions regarding its impact on insurance solvency and the mechanisms driving potential cost inflation.

Theoretically, this relationship can be interpreted through Roemer’s Law, which posits that “a built bed is a filled bed”, suggesting that bed supply creates its own demand via supplier-induced demand (SID) [14]. First articulated in 1961, this proposition asserts that under conditions of information asymmetry between physicians and patients, combined with moral hazard induced by insurance coverage, providers can influence utilization beyond clinical necessity [15]. While widely validated in Western contexts [16–18], the applicability of Roemer’s Law in China, where public hospitals dominate and payment systems are evolving, requires further empirical examination.

Despite the extensive literature on bed supply and utilization, significant gaps persist. First, prior studies predominantly focus on utilization metrics such as hospitalization rates and length of stay [19], rather than directly examining associations with health insurance expenditure, which is a more critical indicator of financial sustainability [20]. Second, the prevalence of linear models overlooks the potential for nonlinear dynamics and threshold effects. Furthermore, evidence regarding specific transmission mechanisms [21] and regional heterogeneity under varying resource-allocation patterns remains limited. To address these limitations, this study utilizes a provincial panel dataset from China and employs two-way fixed effects models, instrumental variable estimation, mediation analysis, and panel threshold regression to investigate the relationship between hospital bed supply and health insurance expenditure. The findings indicate a positive association at the province level between hospital bed density and medical insurance expenditure, partly mediated by a longer average length of stay at the aggregate level. Additionally, the results suggest a possible nonlinear threshold pattern and regional heterogeneity. Therefore, this study provides province-level evidence pertinent to a conditional interpretation of Roemer’s Law in China and contributes to ongoing discussions regarding healthcare resource allocation and the sustainability of insurance funds.

Methods

Data source and sample

We collected a balanced panel dataset encompassing all 31 provincial-level administrative divisions in mainland China, while excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan. This dataset, which spans from 2011 to 2024, includes a total of 434 observations. The data were sourced from the China’s Health Statistics Yearbook [22], China’s Labor Statistical Yearbook [23], and China’s Statistical Yearbook [24].

Conceptual framework

The analysis is grounded in Roemer’s Law and the theory of supplier-induced demand (SID). Roemer’s Law posits that the supply of hospital beds generates its own demand through two key mechanisms. First, the information asymmetry between physicians and patients allows providers to influence treatment decisions. Patients often defer to medical recommendations due to their limited clinical knowledge [25]. Second, health insurance mitigates out-of-pocket costs, rendering patients less price-sensitive and more amenable to additional services and extended stays [26]. As bed supply increases, hospitals experience pressure to maintain high occupancy rates to ensure financial sustainability. This incentive may influence provider behavior in various ways: physicians might postpone discharges, order additional tests, or admit patients who could otherwise be treated on an outpatient basis [27]. Collectively, these adjustments may be linked to increased medical insurance expenditures. We hypothesize that the relationship between bed supply and induced demand varies nonlinearly with bed density levels. At low densities, additional supply may primarily address unmet or genuine medical needs [27, 28]. At moderate levels, providers have a greater capacity to influence demand. However, this association may weaken beyond a certain capacity level, as additional capacity may no longer lead to proportional increases in utilization. Furthermore, regulatory constraints could further limit supply-driven expansion [29].

Variable measurement

Dependent variable

The per capita medical insurance fund (MIF) expenditure was the primary outcome, calculated as ln(total annual basic medical insurance fund expenditure / year-end resident population). This pooled measure captures the total provincial spending from China’s statutory basic medical insurance system. It was utilized because China’s insurance schemes underwent significant changes between 2011 and 2024: the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance and the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme were gradually integrated into the Urban-Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance, while the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance remained distinct. Therefore, total Medical Insurance Fund (MIF) expenditure offers a more consistent measure of the aggregate provincial fiscal burden than scheme-specific series; however, it should not be interpreted as a scheme-specific response. The logarithmic transformation was employed to reduce right-skewness [30] and to facilitate percentage-change interpretation of the outcome variable [31].

Independent variable

Hospital bed density served as the primary explanatory variable for examining the province-level patterns suggested by Roemer’s Law. We quantified this density as the ratio of hospital beds to the resident population, standardized per 1,000 residents. This standardization accounts for population differences across provinces. The bed counts encompass all medical institutions, including general hospitals, specialized hospitals, and traditional Chinese medicine facilities.

Mediating variables

All variables were operationalized according to standard definitions outlined in the China Health Statistics Yearbook. The specific measurements are as follows:

Hospitalization rates: Calculated as the total annual hospital admissions divided by the resident population, this metric captures the ‘extensive margin’ of demand induction. Increased bed availability may correlate with a rise in admissions, potentially due to broader admission criteria or enhanced patient recruitment efforts. Additionally, physicians may categorize cases as inpatient admissions that would typically suffice for outpatient care. As a result, this metric reflects a quantity-based expansion of healthcare services.

Control variables

Several categories of confounders were accounted for in the analysis. Economic factors included the natural logarithm of per capita GDP, average wages, and fiscal pressure, measured as the budget deficit as a share of GDP. Demographic variables captured population aging, represented by the percentage of individuals over 65 years, average years of schooling, and the urbanization rate. Characteristics of the insurance system included the urban employee enrollment rate and the government’s share of health spending. Healthcare supply factors encompassed physician density and the ratios of outpatient to inpatient services.

Statistical analysis

The baseline specification employs a two-way fixed effects model to estimate the relationship between bed supply and insurance expenditure.

The coefficient assesses whether the province-level association is consistent with the pattern implied by Roemer’s Law: A positive and statistically significant β would be consistent with the province-level pattern implied by Roemer’s Law, namely that higher bed supply is associated with higher insurance expenditure. Standard errors were clustered at the provincial level. To assess potential cross-sectional dependence, we employed the Pesaran CD test, the Breusch–Pagan LM test, and the Pesaran scaled LM test on the residuals of the baseline model. These tests operate under the null hypothesis of cross-sectional independence. Upon detection of cross-sectional dependence, we further estimated the two-way fixed effects model utilizing Driscoll–Kraay standard errors.

To assess potential endogeneity, we employed the IV-2SLS estimation method as a sensitivity analysis. The primary instrumental variable (IV) specification utilized the two-year lagged hospital bed density as an instrument for the current bed density. To mitigate the limitation of relying on a single instrument, we additionally incorporated both the two-year and three-year lagged bed density as instruments. Furthermore, we constructed a residualized policy-gap instrument by interacting the residualized 2013 bed-density gap, relative to the national 2020 planning target of six beds per 1,000 residents, with the 2015–2020 policy ramp. The signed 2013 gap was residualized using baseline provincial socioeconomic, demographic, fiscal, insurance, and healthcare-resource characteristics. The policy ramp was coded as 0 for the years 2011–2014, increased linearly from 1/6 in 2015 to 1 in 2020, and remained constant at 1 for the years 2021–2024. Instrument relevance was evaluated using first-stage F-statistics and Kleibergen–Paap statistics. Overidentification tests were reported for specifications that included more than one excluded instrument. Given that the exclusion restriction cannot be fully verified, the IV estimates were interpreted as supplementary sensitivity analyses rather than definitive causal estimates.

Mediation pathways were examined using Baron and Kenny’s framework. The procedure estimates (1) total effect (path c), (2) mediator effect (path a), and (3) direct effect (path c’) and mediator impact (path b). Indirect effects (a × b) were assessed using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 replications.

Finally, following Hansen, a panel threshold regression model was applied to investigate nonlinear effects. The specification allows the slope coefficient to vary across regimes defined by endogenously determined thresholds:

where represents the a priori unknown number of thresholds. Threshold parameters are estimated by grid search minimizing the residual sum of squares, and the number of thresholds is determined endogenously using sequential bootstrap-based likelihood ratio tests. Statistical inference is conducted using 5,000 bootstrap replications to account for non-standard asymptotic distributions. The threshold model utilizes hospital bed density, measured in the same manner as in the baseline specification, where beds per 1,000 population act as both the threshold variable and the regime-varying explanatory variable. As part of a sensitivity analysis, we further incorporated first-stage residuals from the IV specifications into the regime-specific regressions defined by the threshold groups. This approach aims to assess the robustness of the low- and high-density patterns against potential endogeneity in bed density. All analyses were conducted using R (v.4.4.2), with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents the summary statistics for the complete sample, stratified by region. The mean hospital bed density was 5.76 per 1,000 population (SD = 1.34), with the northeastern region exhibiting the highest density at 6.69 (SD = 1.29) and the eastern region displaying the lowest at 5.12 (SD = 1.03). Per capita MIF expenditure averaged 6.84 in logarithmic terms (SD = 0.79), ranging from 6.49 (SD = 0.78) in the central provinces to 7.22 (SD = 0.80) in the eastern provinces. The mechanism variables also varied by region. The hospitalization rate averaged 0.16 (SD = 0.04), with the central and western provinces reporting higher rates than the eastern provinces. The average length of stay was 9.46 days (SD = 0.99) nationally, with northeastern provinces recording the longest stays at 10.24 days (SD = 0.82). Bed occupancy averaged 81% (SD = 9%) and was lowest in the northeast at 74% (SD = 10%). The control variables also differed across regions. Per capita GDP was highest in the east and lowest in the west and northeast. Fiscal pressure varied markedly, with the western provinces facing nearly five times the pressure of the eastern provinces. Population aging was most pronounced in the northeast at 19%. Urbanization rates ranged from 53% in the west to 71% in the east. All variables exhibited statistically significant regional differences (p < 0.001).

Diagnostic tests

We conducted diagnostic tests to assess correlations, multicollinearity, and cross-sectional dependence prior to estimating the baseline models. The correlation matrix is depicted in Fig. 1 and detailed in Supplemental Table S1. Notably, hospital bed density exhibited a positive correlation with per capita MIF expenditure (r = 0.53), with no pairwise correlation exceeding 0.80. The variance inflation factor (VIF) results, presented in Supplemental Table S2, indicated that all VIF values were below 5, ranging from 1.168 to 2.960, thereby suggesting the absence of serious multicollinearity. Additionally, we tested for cross-sectional dependence utilizing the residuals from the baseline model. As illustrated in Table 2, the Pesaran CD test rejected the null hypothesis of cross-sectional independence (statistic = -2.331, p = 0.020). Furthermore, both the Breusch–Pagan LM test and the Pesaran scaled LM test yielded significant results, with statistics of 1,353.315 (p < 0.001) and 29.129 (p < 0.001), respectively. These findings indicate the presence of cross-sectional dependence among the provinces.

Instrumental variable sensitivity analyses

Table 4 presents the IV-2SLS estimates utilized as sensitivity analyses to address potential endogeneity. The original L2 specification produced a positive and statistically significant estimate (β = 0.149, SE = 0.072, p < 0.05). When both L2 and L3 bed density were employed as instruments, the coefficient remained positive and significant (β = 0.169, SE = 0.067, p < 0.05), and the overidentification test did not reject the overidentifying restrictions (p = 0.922). The residualized policy-gap specification also yielded a positive and significant estimate (β = 0.148, SE = 0.072, p < 0.05), with no rejection of the overidentifying restrictions (p = 0.887). Across IV specifications, the first-stage F-statistics and Kleibergen–Paap rk Wald F-statistics exceeded 10, indicating the statistical relevance of the instruments. The Durbin–Wu–Hausman tests did not provide statistical evidence against the exogeneity of hospital bed density. Given that the exclusion restriction cannot be fully verified, the IV estimates are interpreted as supplementary sensitivity analyses rather than definitive causal estimates. The Wooldridge test indicated the presence of serial correlation in the panel errors. Consequently, all IV specifications employed province-clustered robust standard errors to account for heteroskedasticity and arbitrary within-province serial correlation.

Regional heterogeneity tests

We conducted a regional analysis by performing separate regressions for the four major regions of China (see Fig. 2). The results indicate that the pattern of association varies across these regions. The western provinces demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between bed density and expenditure, with a coefficient of 0.179 (𝑝 < 0.01), aligning with the estimates observed in the overall sample. In contrast, the results for the other regions were not statistically significant (Supplemental Table S3). The eastern provinces displayed a small coefficient of 0.071 (𝑝 > 0.1), while the central provinces showed a similarly small and statistically insignificant coefficient of 0.042 (𝑝 > 0.1). The northeastern provinces showed a coefficient close to zero, with a coefficient of 0.007 (𝑝 > 0.1).

Exploratory mediation analysis

To explore potential pathways underlying the observed association, we conducted a mediation analysis that examined three candidate pathways: hospitalization rate (extensive margin), average length of stay (ALOS, intensive margin), and bed occupancy rate. Figure 3 illustrates the identified mediation pathway. At the provincial level, ALOS emerged as the only statistically significant aggregate mediator, exhibiting a notable indirect effect (coefficient = 0.030, 95% CI [0.009, 0.052]), which accounts for 17.8% of the estimated total association between bed density and insurance expenditure. Both path a (Hospital Bed Density → ALOS: β = 0.482, 𝑝 < 0.01) and path b (ALOS → expenditure: β = 0.063, 𝑝 < 0.01) are statistically significant, indicating a potential partial mediation pattern. In contrast, neither the hospitalization rate nor the bed occupancy rate demonstrated significant mediation effects, as their bootstrap confidence intervals include zero (Supplemental Table S4).

Discussion

This study analyzes the relationship between hospital bed supply and medical insurance expenditure using panel data from 31 Chinese provinces over the period from 2011 to 2024. The findings indicate that a higher provincial hospital bed density is positively associated with province-level insurance spending, a pattern that aligns broadly with a conditional interpretation of Roemer’s Law in China. The observed association appears to be more closely related to prolonged lengths of stay than to increased admission volumes. Furthermore, the threshold analysis suggests that this association may weaken at higher levels of bed density. Additionally, considerable regional heterogeneity is observed, with the positive supply–expenditure association predominantly concentrated in resource-scarce regions. Collectively, these results imply that the supply–expenditure relationship is both conditional and context-specific.

The conditional nature of Roemer’s Law

Our central estimate indicates that higher provincial bed density was associated with higher social health insurance spending, with a coefficient of 0.168. This finding contributes panel-data evidence that aligns broadly with Roemer’s Law within the institutional context of China. Notably, this coefficient is smaller than the 0.3–0.5 range reported in classic studies [16]. Three characteristics of China’s health system may explain this attenuation. First, despite achieving near-universal coverage, the cost-sharing for inpatient services remains relatively high (30–40%) [32], which likely preserves price sensitivity and limits demand inducement. Second, although fee-for-service payment structures create incentives for overtreatment, provincial pooling arrangements with global budget caps impose binding fiscal constraints [33], thereby restricting hospitals’ ability to convert induced utilization into proportionate spending growth. Third, the study period (2011–2024) encompasses the transition from fee-for-service to diagnosis-related group (DRG) payment systems, which may dampen supply-side incentives during the reform rollout. These results are consistent with recent evidence suggesting that bed supply is associated with utilization and costs across various settings [34]. The IV-2SLS estimates were slightly smaller than the baseline fixed-effects estimates while maintaining directionally consistent results. However, due to the inability to fully verify the exclusion restriction and the Durbin–Wu–Hausman tests not providing statistical evidence against exogeneity, we interpret the IV results as supplementary sensitivity analyses rather than definitive proof of bias in the baseline estimates. The negative and predominantly insignificant coefficient on population aging should also be interpreted with caution, as it represents a conditional association after adjusting for fixed effects and other socioeconomic, insurance, and healthcare resource covariates, rather than the overall effect of aging on health expenditure.

Exploratory mechanism: the role of average length of stay

Our exploratory mediation analysis reveals an asymmetric pattern: the province-level association between bed density and insurance spending is partially linked to a longer average length of stay (ALOS, the intensive margin), rather than to higher admission rates (the extensive margin) or bed occupancy rates (capacity utilization). ALOS was identified as the sole candidate mediator with a statistically significant indirect effect, accounting for 17.8% of the total association, while hospitalization rates and bed occupancy rates did not demonstrate statistically significant mediation. Consequently, ALOS should be interpreted as a potential partial aggregate pathway rather than as a confirmed or complete mechanism. Notably, approximately 82.2% of the association remains unexplained by the mediators tested, indicating that other factors, such as treatment intensity, case mix, payment rules, pricing, hospital behavior, and regional policy differences, may also play a role in the observed association. A plausible explanation for the ALOS pathway is the differential oversight across decision points. In China, admission decisions are subject to comparatively strict monitoring via pre-authorization requirements and real-time verification systems [35], while treatment intensity within an inpatient episode may be monitored less intensively. Consistent with this observation, recent evidence indicates that Chinese hospitals may respond to capacity pressures by extending stays rather than increasing admissions [36]. This pattern may also be reinforced by the ongoing rollout of Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payment systems. Although DRGs are intended to standardize episode payments, early-stage implementations may lack well-calibrated outlier adjustments [37], leaving room for strategic responses around length-of-stay thresholds.

Threshold patterns and attenuation

The threshold analysis indicates a potential nonlinear relationship, with an estimated threshold of 7.271 hospital beds per 1,000 population. This exploratory finding suggests that the association implied by Roemer’s Law may diminish at elevated levels of bed density. Below this threshold, bed density demonstrated a stronger positive correlation with expenditure than in the pooled model. Conversely, above this threshold, the association weakened and became statistically insignificant. Importantly, higher expenditure does not necessarily imply inefficiency or welfare loss. In certain contexts, it may indicate enhanced access to care, increased service availability, or improved treatment quality, particularly in previously under-resourced settings. Simultaneously, it may also signify supplier-induced demand or inefficient utilization of excess capacity. Consequently, our findings primarily address the fiscal implications of bed expansion for insurance funds, rather than offering a direct assessment of whether the additional expenditure is socially inefficient or health-enhancing. This exploratory threshold pattern is broadly consistent with the possibility that the marginal association between bed supply and expenditure diminishes at higher bed-density levels [38]. Several mechanisms may underlie this pattern. The prevalence and severity of morbidity impose an upper limit on the clinically appropriate demand for inpatient care. Additionally, professional norms and medical ethics constrain physicians’ willingness to induce clearly unnecessary treatments, even in the presence of excess capacity [39]. In addition, patients incur time costs and disutility from hospitalization, which can dampen demand once access constraints are relaxed [40]. Evidence from the regional heterogeneity analysis supports this interpretation. The supply–expenditure relationship is evident in the Western provinces, but it is not detectable in the Northeast or the East, where capacity levels are higher and regulatory oversight may be more stringent. Additionally, the control-function sensitivity analysis further indicates that the low-versus-high regime pattern persists even after accounting for potential endogeneity.

Regional heterogeneity

The regional variation in our estimates supports a conditional interpretation of Roemer’s Law rather than a universal one. Our analysis reveals a statistically significant association in the Western region; however, we found no statistically significant associations in the Northeast or East regions. These patterns may reflect differences in resource scarcity, market saturation, and regulatory capacity. The Western provinces, characterized by lower levels of economic development and baseline bed densities that remain below the exploratory threshold estimate, exhibit the most significant positive correlation between bed density and MIF expenditure. Recent studies have documented ongoing regional disparities in China’s healthcare infrastructure and utilization [41]. In contrast, the Northeastern region, which is characterized by the highest bed density, exhibits no discernible relationship between supply and expenditure. The Eastern provinces offer particularly valuable insights; despite maintaining moderate bed densities, they similarly demonstrate no detectable positive association between bed density and expenditure. One possible interpretation of this phenomenon is that stronger regulatory institutions may mitigate the association between capacity expansion and insurance expenditure. Notably, these provinces were among the early adopters of Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payment reforms and comprehensive hospital performance monitoring systems [42]. The findings align with recent evidence indicating that institutional quality and governance mechanisms play a moderating role in supply-induced demand [43].

Theoretical contributions

Our study offers three main theoretical implications. First, our findings suggest that the relationship articulated by Roemer’s Law may be bounded rather than absolute. Specifically, the province-level supply–expenditure association may diminish at higher levels of bed density, underscoring the need for more context-specific evidence regarding supply-side effects. Second, we illustrate that institutional design significantly influences the dominant inducement margin. Episode-based payment reforms require robust outlier policies and credible post-discharge auditing; otherwise, they may shift induced responses from the extensive margin to the intensive margin, rather than alleviating overall spending pressure. Third, our findings on heterogeneity indicate that healthcare markets are not homogeneous. This raises questions about the appropriateness of uniform capacity targets and advocates for context-specific planning, an approach increasingly reflected in global health policy discourse and recent WHO guidance [44, 45]. These implications extend beyond China. Many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) pursuing universal health coverage face similar trade-offs between infrastructure expansion and fiscal sustainability. This threshold-based perspective may provide valuable insights for more targeted capacity planning in these contexts.

Policy implications

Our findings have several policy implications. First, the expansion of hospital beds should be guided by local capacity levels rather than by uniform infrastructure targets. In regions with lower capacity, moderate expansion may still enhance access to inpatient care; however, such decisions should be informed by local demand, financial pressures, and service quality indicators, rather than relying solely on threshold estimates. Conversely, in high-density regions, further bed expansion may yield limited marginal access benefits while increasing pressure on insurance funds, necessitating a cautious approach. Second, policy attention should shift from merely increasing bed numbers to improving the efficiency of existing capacity. The province-level correlation between bed density and MIF expenditure appears to be partially linked to aggregate average length of stay (ALOS), suggesting that payment reforms and discharge management should be strengthened. In particular, implementing Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) or Diagnosis-Intervention Package (DIP)-based payment systems, monitoring length of stay, and establishing post-discharge review mechanisms may help reduce unnecessary inpatient days. Third, regulatory strategies should be tailored to specific regions. Western provinces may require ongoing capacity improvements alongside enhanced insurance supervision, whereas eastern and northeastern provinces should focus on optimizing capacity, assessing hospital performance, and preventing inefficient bed use. Finally, the sustainability of medical insurance funds should be integrated into health infrastructure planning. Decisions regarding hospital bed allocation should jointly consider population health needs, local fiscal capacity, insurance fund pressures, and the marginal benefits of additional beds.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while province and year fixed effects were included, residual time-varying confounding cannot be entirely ruled out; thus, the findings should be interpreted as robust associations rather than definitive causal effects. Second, the instrumental variable (IV) estimates are treated as supplementary sensitivity analyses. Although the overidentified lag-based and residualized policy-gap specifications did not reject the overidentifying restrictions, such tests cannot validate the exclusion restriction, and the instruments may still reflect persistent provincial healthcare conditions or unobserved trends. Third, the pooled MIF outcome captures the aggregate provincial fiscal burden but fails to distinguish scheme-specific responses across employee, resident, and earlier resident-insurance arrangements. Fourth, given that the analysis utilizes provincial-level aggregate data, the findings may be prone to ecological fallacy and should not be interpreted as effects at the individual, patient, or hospital levels. Fifth, the outcome measurement is per resident rather than per insured individual, which supports a population-level fiscal interpretation but may be influenced by provincial enrollment discrepancies. Sixth, the results regarding threshold and regional heterogeneity are exploratory and may be sensitive to model specification, potential endogeneity in the threshold variable, and limited subsample sizes. Finally, the mediation analysis was exploratory and based on a Baron–Kenny framework with bootstrapped confidence intervals, which is sensitive to model specification and cannot establish a causal mediation pathway using aggregate provincial data. While ALOS appeared to be a potential partial pathway, a substantial portion of the association remains unexplained. Future research utilizing scheme-specific, hospital-level, or claims-level data is necessary to more directly differentiate access expansion from inefficient utilization.

Conclusions

This study investigates the association between hospital bed supply and medical insurance fund expenditure in China, emphasizing potential mechanisms, regional heterogeneity, and threshold effects. Utilizing provincial panel data from 2011 to 2024, we employed two-way fixed effects models, supplemented with IV-2SLS sensitivity analyses, mediation analysis, regional analysis, and panel threshold regression. The findings indicate that provincial hospital bed density is positively correlated with province-level per capita medical insurance fund (MIF) expenditure, with this relationship partially mediated by aggregate average length of stay (ALOS). The threshold analysis reveals that this association may diminish at higher levels of bed density, while the regional analysis highlights that the relationship is most pronounced in western provinces. These results support a conditional, province-level interpretation of Roemer’s Law and suggest that the expansion of hospital beds should be aligned with payment reform, management of length of stay, and the sustainability of insurance funds.
