Shaping of microbial community structure and function by effluent-derived dissolved organic matter in a receiving river across seasons
Authors: Bo Shao, Zhengyu Wu, Haoran Peng, Qing Yang, Meiqi Huang, Qirui Wang, Yiwen Liu, Wei He, Yingxin Zhao, Xiaoyu Cui, Hongyang Cui, Yindong Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluent is a significant source of environmental impacts, introducing substantial dissolved organic matter (DOM) and nutrients into receiving waters. However, the specific role of effluent-derived DOM (EDOM) in shaping microbial community structure and key metabolic functions remains insufficiently understood. We systematically explored the effects of EDOM on the seasonal dynamics of bacterial communities in effluent-receiving waters in a typical water-scarce region of North China. Our results revealed that WWTP effluent input significantly altered DOM characteristics, shifting them toward greater humification, increased recalcitrance, lower molecular mass, and simpler structure, against a background of elevated nutrient levels. The interplay between transformed EDOM and resident microorganisms collectively restructured the bacterial community and its potential metabolic functions. These changes included (i) increased taxonomic richness and a more specialized community composition; (ii) enhanced microbial network complexity with a polycentric architecture dominated by DOM components; (iii) seasonal convergence of microbial network structure, as strong EDOM-driven interactions in the dry season were attenuated by wet season hydrology; and (iv) strengthened coupling between DOM molecular traits and biogeochemical cycling potential. Crucially, EDOM was identified as the key driver controlling microbial richness and core metabolic processes, with its influence exhibiting significant seasonal dynamics across the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles. While current Chinese wastewater discharge standards regulate nutrient inputs, they lack consideration of ecological risks posed by EDOM fractions and their dynamic interactions with microorganisms. Our findings highlight the need to integrate EDOM characteristics into effluent risk assessments to improve WWTP discharge standards.

Introduction

United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6 aims to ensure access to safe drinking water and sanitation for all, with a focus on the sustainable management of water resources, wastewater, and ecosystems [[1], [2], [3]]. This goal has driven the global expansion of wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), leading to significant progress in pollution control over the past two decades [4]. For example, by the end of 2022, more than 8000 WWTPs had been built in China. These facilities have a combined daily treatment capacity of 215 million m3, achieving a treatment rate of 97.9% [5,6]. Consequently, large volumes of WWTP effluent, from which contaminants have been partially removed through physical, biological, and/or chemical processes, are now discharged into rivers and have become a common and integral component of aquatic ecosystems [[7], [8], [9]]. A worldwide investigation has indicated that effluent discharge contributes more than 10% of the flow in over 72,000 km of rivers, primarily in areas with high population densities. These regions include Europe, the USA, China, India, and South Africa [7]. In Switzerland, WWTP effluent accounts for 10%–50% of the flow in small and medium-sized rivers [9]. Similarly, in China, research on the Yangtze River Basin revealed that one-third of river networks are potentially influenced by effluent discharge from local or upstream sources, raising concerns about unintended ecological and human health risks associated with persistent pollutants and nutrient loading [10]. While the expansion of WWTPs has substantially reduced the total amount of pollutants discharged into waters, WWTP effluent itself constitutes a new ecological challenge [11]. It continuously introduces nutrients, antibiotics, micropollutants, soluble and refractory organic matter, and various microbes into receiving waters [[12], [13], [14]]. These constituents act as persistent environmental and biological stressors, leading to potential ecological and human health risks [11,15].

WWTP effluent has both direct and indirect effects on the biogeochemical cycles of biogenic elements in receiving waters. First, it transports substantial amounts of dissolved organic carbon (DOC), along with nutrients such as nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), into these waters. This directly alters the stoichiometry and elemental composition of the receiving waters. Previous studies have shown that WWTP effluent typically contains high DOC concentrations (6.9−13 mg C/L), compared to the mean DOC concentration of 6 mg C/L in global rivers [[16], [17], [18]]. Total nitrogen (TN) concentrations in WWTP effluent typically range from 6 to 20 mg/L [19], whereas rivers classified as having good ecological status typically have TN concentrations ranging from 0.9 to 3.5 mg/L [20,21]. Second, these increased biogenic elements, as essential nutrients for organisms (e.g., microorganisms, algae), participate in the biogeochemical cycles of carbon (C), N, P, and sulfur (S) within receiving waters. Excessive N and P inputs can alter C cycling in rivers by promoting eutrophication and algal blooms, which in turn increase the production of autochthonous dissolved organic matter (DOM) [22]. However, high nutrient loadings may also accelerate organic carbon respiration and CO2 production in waters, counteracting labile carbon production [23]. Meanwhile, the release of N-rich effluent into rivers is expected to alter N cycling pathways [24]. Incomplete heterotrophic denitrification has been proven to be the main N transformation process in nitrate-rich waters [25], which are usually hotspots of N2O emissions. Furthermore, cyanobacterial blooms, driven by nutrient inputs, have been observed to cause pronounced seasonal variations in sulfate concentrations. Elevated sulfate levels not only enhance sulfate reduction but also inhibit methane emission-related carbon cycle processes [26].

Critically, these elemental cycles are ultimately governed by microorganisms, whose metabolic activities regulate energy and matter fluxes and drive key processes such as pollutant degradation and greenhouse gas emissions [11,27,28]. The responsiveness of microbial communities to WWTP effluent stressors remains an important research area, and these varied responses are shaped by different constraints imposed on species dispersal and interaction strengths [27]. Existing studies have focused on the effects of effluent discharge on water quality, microbial community structure, and microbial function in receiving water bodies [11,15,29,30]. However, few have examined how the composition and characteristics of effluent-derived DOM (EDOM) affect bacterial community dynamics in effluent-receiving rivers. Understanding how effluent-driven changes in the concentration and composition of DOM interact with microbial communities is crucial, as regulating key effluent components is essential for shaping eco-friendly policies related to effluent discharge [31,32]. To address this gap, we conducted a year-long seasonal field sampling campaign in a densely populated area in North China covering two types of waters: those receiving effluent as the only water source and background waters with no effluent input. Using spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, we characterized the concentration and composition of EDOM, as well as microbial community dynamics, in background waters and WWTP effluent-receiving waters. This study aimed to understand how EDOM variations influence the seasonal dynamics of bacterial communities in receiving waters. Specifically, we addressed three core questions: (i) How does effluent input alter the seasonal dynamics of nutrients and DOM? (ii) How do EDOM characteristics and composition shape the diversity, composition, and interaction networks of bacterial communities across seasons? (iii) Which specific EDOM components and characteristics are the key factors influencing changes in bacterial richness and metabolic functions related to C, N, and S cycling? By identifying the most influential EDOM component or characteristic in shaping microbial community dynamics, this research will inform the development of more targeted and effective policies for managing WWTP effluent discharge and mitigating its ecological impacts.

Materials and methods

Study area and field sampling

Dagu River (117°5′E−117°45′E, 38°55′N–39°10′N) is situated in the temperate eastern region of the Hai River Basin, China. As a river in a developed coastal region, it is typical of many effluent-receiving waterways worldwide and has been extensively influenced by human activities. The river receives high effluent loads (1,065,312 m3/d) from nine major WWTPs along its 70-km length [33], and is a good case for studying the ecology of effluent-receiving waters. Detailed information on the wastewater treatment processes and capacities of the surveyed WWTPs is provided in Text S1. To capture both immediate local impacts near outfalls and the integrated cumulative effects downstream, six sites (W1–W6; Fig. 1A) were established in the near-downstream reaches of WWTP outlets to represent effluent-receiving waters (EW). This approach frames the river as a continuous system receiving combined inputs from multiple point sources. In addition, clean waters without WWTP effluent inputs (C1–C5; Fig. 1A) were included in the study as background waters (BW) to highlight the impact of effluent discharge on river microbial ecology. Field samples were collected at each of the above-mentioned sites (n = 11) during the dry (December 2022, April 2023) and wet seasons (July 2023, September 2023) to encompass effluent dilution intensity variations under different climatic conditions (Total: n = 43; the December 2022 sample from site W1 was missed due to the COVID-19 lockdown). Briefly, 3 L surface-water samples were collected from each site using sterile bottles (BKMAM, China), stored at 4 °C, and brought to the laboratory within 6 h. In situ parameters were measured using the multiparameter sonde connected to the Hydrolab HL7 (OTT Hydromet Inc., Germany), including water temperature (Temp.), salinity (Sal.), and pH [34]. For bacterial community analysis, we set up three field replicates for each site by filtering 100–500 mL volume of water using a Millipore 0.2 μm hydrophilic polycarbonate membrane (Merck Millipore, Inc., USA). Membrane filters were placed in 5.0 mL sterile centrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. Unfiltered samples (about 200 mL) were analyzed for hydrochemical parameters like TN and total phosphorus (TP). The remaining water samples were filtered through a pre-combusted (450 °C, 4 h) 0.45-μm glass-fiber filter to remove particulates, and then stored at −20 °C in the dark for DOM analysis.

Nutrient concentration, DOM composition, and characteristic analysis

To characterize the spatial variations in nutrient levels between BW and EW areas, DOC concentrations in water were quantified using a total organic carbon analyzer (Shimadzu, Inc., Japan). TN and TP were measured following the standardized China EPA Test Method [35]. Detailed information regarding the analytical methodologies, method detection limit, and experimental procedures is provided in Text S2.

A combination of chemical analysis techniques, including UV-Vis (ultraviolet-visible absorption spectrum), 3D-EEMs (three-dimensional fluorescence spectrum), and UPLC-IM-QTOF MS (Ultra-performance Liquid Chromatography-Ion Mobility-Quadrupole Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry), was used to characterize changes in DOM compositions between the BW and EW areas. The UV-Vis absorption spectrum was measured using the UV-Vis 8453 spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA) at 200−800 nm [36]. 3D-EEMs were measured using a Hitachi F-2710 fluorometer with a 1 cm path quartz cuvette, and the fluorescence regional integration (FRI) was used to interpret the EEMs result [37]. Five fluorescence components were identified, including aromatic protein I (AP-I), aromatic protein II (AP-II), humic acid-like materials (HAs), fulvic acid-like materials (FAs), and soluble microbial byproduct-like materials (SMBs). Meanwhile, the fluorescence index (FI), the biological index (BIX), the humification index (HIX), and absorption spectral slope ratios (SR) were also calculated to reveal sources and contributions of DOM, and the measurements and calculations are detailed in Text S3.

UPLC-IM-QTOF MS (Waters, Inc., USA) was used to characterize DOM molecular compositions [i.e., formula, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and molecular abundance] in the BW and EW area, which can provide sufficiently high resolution [100,000 full width at half maximum (FWHM)] and accuracy <5 ppm [38]. Details of solid-phase extraction (SPE), instrumental condition, elemental formulas assignment, and classification boundaries of DOM classes are provided in the Texts S4-S5. The intensity-weighted average parameters, including molecular mass (MMwa), modified aromaticity index (AImod,wa), double-bond equivalent (DBEwa), and atomic ratios (N/Cwa, S/Cwa, P/Cwa, H/Cwa), were calculated by multiplying the key parameters of each molecular formula by its relative magnitude and summing the results. In addition, the molecular lability boundary (MLB; H/C = 1.5) was applied to divide DOM into more labile (MLBL; H/C > 1.5) and less labile (MLBR; H/C < 1.5) constituents [39]. Detailed methods for calculating the DOM molecular characteristics or compositions in the BW and EW areas are provided in Text S6.

DNA extraction, quantification, and sequence processing

Amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene was used to characterize the relative abundance, taxonomic composition, and diversity of pelagic microbial communities in field samples. Microbial DNA was extracted from the filters using the FastPure Soil DNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR with primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). Amplicons from three replicate PCRs per sample were pooled and purified using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, Inc., USA). The purified products were then ligated with sequencing adapters for library construction and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (PE 300). Raw sequencing data was processed using the pipeline described by Chen et al. [40]. Briefly, high-quality sequences were generated after quality filtering and paired-end read merging, followed by Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) clustering at 97% sequence similarity using UPARSE (version 7.0.1090) within the QIIME analysis workflow. Taxonomy was assigned to OTUs against the SILVA (Release 138) 16S rRNA database using the RDP classifier with a confidence threshold of 0.7. A total of 3,401,735 high-quality non-chimeric 16S rRNA gene sequences were obtained, with an average of 72,377 sequences per sample, ranging from 56,114 to 96,315. The sequencing was commissioned to Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and all bioinformatics data are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database, project reference PRJNA1412487.

Statistical analysis

Microbial community diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) and richness [Chao1 and abundance-based coverage estimator (ACE)] were calculated using “Mothur” (v.1.30.2), and inter-group difference was analyzed using the “boot” and “stats” packages. The UPGMA (Unweighted Pair-group Method with Arithmetic Mean) algorithm was used to construct a tree structure to visualize the differences in community composition in different environmental samples [41]. A genus-level species–environment correlation network was constructed based on Spearman correlation coefficients among OTUs with a total abundance rank ≥200 [42]. Significant relationships with |r| ≥ 0.5 and P < 0.05 were included in the network, and the co-occurrence network was visualized in Gephi 1.0. Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) was used to infer the putative biogeochemical functional composition (C, N, S cycling) from 16S rRNA data in both BW and EW areas [43,44]. We interpret these predictions as proxies for functional potential, acknowledging the inherent limitations of taxonomy-based inference. Differences among variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, t-test, or the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, depending on whether the data met the normality assumption, as assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Finally, to evaluate the direct and indirect drivers of microbial alpha-diversity, we employed partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) [45]. The model tested hypothesized causal pathways through which environmental variables (Env) and DOM characteristics influenced microbial richness. Separately, Mantel tests were used to explore the correlations between the environmental/DOM matrices and the abundance of predicted functional genes related to biogeochemical cycles. All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.4.3.

Results and discussion

WWTP effluent-driven restructuring of nutrients in receiving waters

Elevated nutrient loading was observed in receiving waters influenced by effluent discharge, with significantly higher concentrations of N and P. Nutrient levels (TN, TP) in the study area exhibited substantial spatiotemporal variation, with consistently higher concentrations in the EW area than in the BW area (Fig. 1B, C, and Table S3). For instance, the annual mean TN concentration was 6.14 mg/L in the EW area compared to 2.47 mg/L in the BW area (Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.0001), with seasonal increases of 73% (dry) and 40% (wet); TP showed a similar pattern, increasing by 64% (dry) and 17% (wet) in EW area relative to BW area. These findings are consistent with the established understanding that WWTP effluent discharge contributes to nutrient enrichment in receiving waters [46,47]. Beyond the pronounced differences between areas, the seasonal dynamics within the EW area itself were also significant. Specifically, TN concentrations in the EW area were significantly higher (38% increase) during the dry season (7.65 mg/L) than during the wet season (4.77 mg/L) (Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.006; Fig. 1B and Table S3). This pattern is consistent with reduced hydrological dilution during low-flow periods [48]. In contrast, DOC concentrations were consistently lower in the EW area across both seasons, with a significant reduction versus the BW area during the wet season (Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.025; Fig. 1D and Table S3). The concurrence of nutrient enrichment and specific DOC reduction in the wet season suggests a shift in the metabolic processes within the EW ecosystem. The significant decline in DOC during the wet season may be attributed to seasonal drivers, such as elevated temperature and altered hydrological conditions, which can enhance microbial activity and promote net heterotrophic carbon consumption in the receiving waters, thereby offsetting the direct carbon input from the effluent [49]. This implies that effluent-derived nutrients likely stimulate in situ carbon processing, an effect that appears particularly pronounced under favorable seasonal conditions.

WWTP effluent-driven restructuring of dissolved organic matter composition and characteristics in receiving waters

The sources and characteristics of DOM in the receiving waters were markedly different under the influence of continuous WWTP effluent discharge, as characterized by UV-Vis, 3D-EEMs, and UPLC-IM-QTOF MS analyses. Higher FI values were observed in the EW area than in the BW area during both the dry (EW: 2.75 vs. BW: 2.56; P = 0.008) and wet seasons (EW: 2.62 vs. BW: 2.44; P = 0.004) (Fig. 2A and Table S4). Additionally, high values of BIX (>1) were observed at all sites of the EW area (Fig. 2B and Table S4). These observations suggest that DOM in the EW area was primarily derived from autochthonous production and recently released microbe-derived sources, with strong biogenic signatures. Furthermore, the EW area also exhibited distinct DOM characteristics compared to the BW area, marked by elevated humification and recalcitrance, and reduced molecular mass with structural simplification (Figs. 2C−J and S1). Specifically, HIX values in the EW area were significantly higher than those in the BW area during both the dry (BW: 1.56 vs. EW: 3.03; P < 0.0001) and wet seasons (BW: 2.21 vs. EW: 3.09; P = 0.017) (Fig. 2C and Table S4). These differences were further supported by significantly higher MLBR, FAs, and highly unsaturated phenolic compounds (HUPCs), alongside lower MLBL in the EW area relative to the BW area (all P < 0.05, except for HUPCs in the wet season; Fig. 2 and Tables S4–S5), indicating selective accumulation of recalcitrant DOM fractions resistant to microbial degradation.

Concurrently, the MMwa decreased significantly in the EW area compared to the BW area during both the dry (BW: 487.61 Da vs. EW: 475.04 Da; P = 0.004) and wet seasons (BW: 500.52 Da vs. EW: 470.20 Da; P = 0.006) (Fig. 2F). This decline was accompanied by reduced SR values in both seasons (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D and Table S4), indicating enhanced long-wavelength absorption from persistent aromatic moieties within fragmented DOM. Structural simplification and reduced molecular aromaticity were further evidenced by reductions in the DBEwa (BW-Dry: 13.17 vs. EW-Dry: 12.77, P > 0.05; BW-Wet: 13.49 vs. EW-Wet: 12.72, P = 0.017) (Fig. 2E), alongside consistent reductions in Cwa, Hwa, and Owa (Fig. S1 and Table S5). Quantitatively, these transformations were reflected in elevated humification (indicated by HIX: 29%–48%) and recalcitrance (MLBR: 1%–2%; FAs: 31%–39%; HUPCs: 18%–24%), reduced molecular mass (MMwa: 3%–6%) with structural simplification (DBEwa: 3%–6%; Cwa: 3%–7%; Hwa: 3%–7%; Owa: 3%–6%) (Figs. 2 and S1; the range endpoints are the average changes observed in the dry and wet seasons).

Collectively, effluent-influenced DOM in receiving waters exhibited properties consistent with oxidation-driven fragmentation during advanced treatment [52], characterized by the breakdown of macromolecular substances and selective retention of low-molecular-weight compounds with persistent aromatic moieties (e.g., increased FAs and HUPCs in EW area during dry season, all P < 0.01; Figs. 2J, S1D and Table S5). These findings align with incubation experiments demonstrating microbial consumption of labile fractions produces recalcitrant materials characterized by decreased molecular weight and unsaturation but increased aromaticity [53], and are consistent with reported post-biotreatment increases in HIX and specific UV absorbance at 254 nm [54]. While the observed EDOM characteristics (lower molecular weight, elevated humification) are consistent with oxidation-driven fragmentation during advanced wastewater treatment [52], subsequent in-stream photochemical and microbial processing after discharge may further modify these properties [55], potentially reinforcing the trends observed in the receiving waters.

WWTP effluent-driven restructuring of microbial community composition and diversity in receiving waters

The 16S rRNA gene data from the field surveys revealed distinct differences in microbial community composition and diversity (alpha and beta) between the EW and BW areas (Fig. 3). Bacterial communities in the sampled waters were predominantly composed of Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Cyanobacteria, Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobiota, and Bacteroidota, collectively accounting for 94.8%–97.5% of the total microbial population (Fig. 3A). Proteobacteria abundance was elevated in the EW area (dry: 42.57%; wet: 34.97%) compared to the BW area (dry: 33.27%; wet: 19.50%). Conversely, Actinobacteriota and Cyanobacteria showed reduced abundances in the EW versus the BW area. The Venn diagram illustrates the presence of shared and unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among the sampled waters (Fig. 3B). The EW area harbored a significantly greater number of habitat-specific OTUs (3520) compared to the BW area (1359), indicating that effluent input enriched unique bacterial taxa. The unique phyla identified in the EW area were Zixibacteria (39.66%), Halanaerobiaeota (34.48%), DTB120 (17.24%), Fermentibacterota (5.17%), and LCP-89 (3.45%) (Fig. S2). Similarly, the unique genera identified in the EW area were Zoogloea (57.3%), Escherichia-Shigella (3.40%), Perlucidibaca (1.57%), Brachymonas (1.39%), and Atopobium (1.02%) (Fig. S3). Specific microbes detected in the EW area participate in wastewater treatment processes, performing functions such as nitrification-denitrification, sulfate reduction, or pollutant degradation, as detailed in Table S6 [[56], [57], [58]]. A comparison of alpha and beta diversity between microbial communities in the BW and EW areas is presented in Fig. 3D–F. The results showed that the bacterial community richness in the EW area was significantly higher than in the BW area in both seasons, as indicated by both the Chao1 and ACE indices (Mann–Whitney U test, all P < 0.05; Fig. 3D and Table S7). Specifically, during the dry season, the EW area exhibited significantly higher ACE (EW: 688.97 vs. BW: 462.00; P = 0.017) and Chao1 values (EW: 686.70 vs. BW: 461.59; P = 0.017). A similar significant pattern was observed in the wet season for both ACE (EW: 694.95 vs. BW: 466.99, P = 0.003) and Chao1 (EW: 685.29 vs. BW: 466.96; P = 0.006). These findings suggest that WWTP effluent discharge contributed to a higher number of microbial taxa observed in receiving water communities. However, this increased richness likely reflects a combination of both viable microbial cells and a substantial load of non-viable microbial DNA or dormant cells discharged from the WWTPs [59]. In contrast to the clear pattern in richness, the results for diversity indices (Shannon and Simpson) presented a different outcome. While the observed values were generally higher in the EW area, a statistically significant difference was only found for the Shannon index during the dry season (Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.017; Fig. 3E and Table S7). The differences for the Simpson index and for the Shannon index in the wet season were not significant (Mann–Whitney U test, P > 0.05; Fig. 3E). This implies that while effluent input significantly increased the number of microbial taxa (richness), it did not induce a consistent, significant change in the overall species diversity or evenness of the communities between the two ecosystems.

The distinct response patterns observed between community richness and diversity metrics warrant further interpretation. The significant increase in richness estimators (Chao1, ACE), without a parallel rise in diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson), indicates that WWTP effluent input primarily contributes a multitude of low-abundance (rare) taxa. This pattern is consistent with an ecological “inoculation” effect. The effluent introduces a wide spectrum of unique microbial taxa (as evidenced in Figs. 3B, S2, and S3) without substantially disrupting the evenness of the resident community, which is a key determinant of the Shannon and Simpson indices. Additionally, the effluent inoculum contains not only potentially viable cells but also non-viable microbial DNA [59]. The detection of this non-viable component may contribute to elevated taxon counts in richness estimates, a methodological nuance that further supports the interpretation that the introduced genetic signatures do not equate to established populations capable of altering community evenness.

Beta diversity analysis, based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index, further revealed significant clustering segregation between BW and EW microbial communities in both the dry (t-test, P = 0.003) and wet seasons (t-test, P = 0.046) (Fig. 3F and Table S7). Notably, significant differentiation in community composition was observed across seasonal periods, with a more pronounced distinction between the two areas during the dry season (t-test, P = 0.003; Fig. 3F). Additionally, hierarchical clustering based on UPGMA confirmed substantial differences in the microbial community structures between the BW and EW areas (Fig. S4).

EDOM drives the microbial interaction network to become more interconnected and seasonally homogenized

Co-occurrence network analysis revealed pronounced seasonal shifts in genus-level DOM-microbial interactions between the EW and BW areas (Fig. 4). In the dry season, the EW network was markedly more complex, featuring significantly more edges (1702 vs. 900), a higher average degree (16.13 vs. 8.65), and reduced modularity (0.25 vs. 0.41) than the BW network. These topological features suggest a pattern of tighter microbial-DOM coupling under conditions of persistent effluent influence. Conversely, these topological disparities diminished during the wet season. The EW network’s complexity decreased, while the BW network’s connectivity increased, leading to structural convergence between the two areas. Centrality analysis further elucidated the distinct topological organization patterns between the two aquatic systems during the dry season. In the EW area, the network exhibited a polycentric architecture with multiple DOM components serving as key hubs. DOC emerged as the primary network bridge with the highest betweenness centrality (0.1015), while CHOP% (proportion of DOM molecular formulas containing C, H, O, and P), MLBL, and MLBR demonstrated the most extensive connectivity with identical degree centrality values (0.4429). The strategic positioning of these DOM components was further evidenced by their high closeness centrality, particularly for MLBL and MLBR (both 0.5048), underscoring their efficiency in facilitating system-wide ecological influence. This multi-hub structure, characterized by CHOP%, labile and recalcitrant DOM fraction (MLBL and MLBR, respectively), aligns with the observed reduction in modularity and enhanced connectivity in the EW network, suggesting a tightly integrated microbial community shaped by EDOM.

Conversely, the BW area in the dry season displayed a fundamentally different organizational pattern dominated by basic physicochemical parameters. Salinity served as the paramount network bridge with a markedly higher betweenness centrality (0.1256) than any node in the EW area, complemented by FI (0.1193) and DOC (0.1144). These three parameters also exhibited the highest degree centrality values in the BW network (0.314, 0.285, and 0.2416, respectively), though these values were substantially lower than the maximum values observed in the EW area. This centralized yet less connected architecture corresponds to the higher modularity observed in the BW network. Specifically, a few nodes (e.g., salinity) played strong bridging roles, but direct connections among all nodes were fewer, and traditional water quality parameters maintained predominant bridging functions. The pronounced contrast in hub identity and connectivity patterns between the two areas reflects a fundamental difference in network organization, corresponding to a shift from a salinity-dominated natural system to a complex polycentric network structured around diverse DOM components.

Effluent-derived DOM variation increases microbial richness

PLS-PM was used to evaluate the influence of effluent-derived biogenic elements, environmental factors (Env), and EDOM characteristics (EDOM-C) on microbial richness during dry and wet seasons (Fig. 5). The outer models of latent variables are detailed in Tables S8–S9. The models for each season showed acceptable fit and explanatory power. In the dry season, the goodness-of-fit (GoF) index was 0.66, and the model explained 78% (R2 = 0.78) of the variance in microbial richness. In the wet season, the corresponding values were a GoF of 0.55 and an R2 of 0.42. The results revealed key drivers and distinct seasonal regulatory patterns influencing microbial richness variations between BW and EW areas. Specifically, in the dry season, N-related compounds exhibited the strongest total positive effect on richness (0.78), followed by Env (0.55) and P-related compounds (0.44). S-related compounds showed a significant direct negative effect (−0.45) (P = 0.012; Fig. 5A and B). A significant positive path linked N/P-related compounds to EDOM-C (0.67/0.38; P < 0.05), and EDOM-C to richness (1.16; P < 0.05), indicating that effluent-derived N and P drive DOM transformations (including elevated humification, recalcitrance, reduced molecular mass, and structural simplification). This transformed DOM directly promotes increased microbial richness during the dry season in effluent-receiving waters. In the wet season, N-related compounds also exerted the strongest total positive effect on richness (0.54), followed by Env (0.40), and C-related compounds (0.29). P-related compounds (−0.21) and S-related compounds (−0.22) showed total negative effects (Fig. 5C and D). Notably, the accumulation of oxidized, low-molecular-weight yet recalcitrant DOM components (e.g., HUPCs and FAs) in effluent-receiving waters is associated with specialized microbial communities equipped with specific enzymatic machinery for degradation [60]. This association suggests that such DOM components may select for or favor populations with a competitive advantage in utilizing this specific organic matter [61,62], which could contribute to a shift toward a more specialized community structure and increased richness. In contrast, previous studies have also reported reduced α-diversity in effluent-impacted waters, proposing that pollutant-induced stress can induce niche filtering that diminishes species richness [63]. We propose that in this system, the effluent acted not merely as a stressor but as a complex chemical resource matrix. The combined input of chemically diverse EDOM and elevated inorganic nutrients (N, P) likely facilitated resource-based niche differentiation, supporting a wider array of specialist taxa and thereby increasing overall richness. The microbial degradation of such a recalcitrant but diverse substrate mix likely requires a broader spectrum of specific enzymatic pathways and cooperative metabolic strategies. This scenario favors niche differentiation and functional complementarity within the microbial community, allowing more specialist taxa to coexist by partitioning different degradation steps or targeting specific compound classes. Importantly, this mechanism of complementarity primarily facilitates the proliferation of numerous low-abundance specialist taxa, which directly explains the observed increase in richness (e.g., Chao1) without a proportional shift in community evenness (as reflected in Shannon/Simpson indices). This hypothesis is also supported by our network analysis, which revealed a more complex and interconnected microbial association network in the effluent-impacted waters during the dry season. Together, these observations suggest that microbial responses to WWTP effluent are closely linked to the bioavailability and chemical nature of introduced substances, highlighting the complexity of these contrasting responses.

Differential impacts of effluent-derived DOM on biogeochemical cycling across seasons

Mantel test analysis revealed significant correlations between effluent-derived organic matter and the microbial functional potential of the receiving waters, which were particularly pronounced during the dry season. The results indicate a shift in the drivers of biogeochemical cycling between dry and wet seasons, from a DOM-dominated mechanism to a bulk-parameters-dominated mechanism (Fig. 6). During the dry season, when effluent dilution is minimal, the molecular composition of EDOM showed the strongest association with the inferred functional potential of the microbial community. The potential for C cycling, as predicted by FAPROTAX, exhibited the strongest and most diverse correlations with EDOM molecular composition (Fig. 6A). The significant associations with CHOS% (proportion of DOM molecular formulas containing C, H, O, and S; r = 0.64), CHO% (proportion of DOM molecular formulas only containing C, H, and O; r = 0.53), and aliphatic% (r = 0.50) suggest a tight link between microbial carbon processing and these specific molecular characteristics of EDOM. CHOS% and CHO% may represent carbon skeletons and energy sources that can be directly used or converted, while aliphatic compounds are usually easily degradable carbon sources. Furthermore, the strong correlations with aromatic and polyphenolic components, including HUPC% (0.57), lignin-like% (0.35), and polyphenols% (0.23), suggest that microbial communities under effluent stress are adept at degrading complex, recalcitrant aromatic structures, which are typically abundant in treated effluent.

Similarly, the potential for N cycling in the dry season was significantly associated with a suite of DOM characteristics and compositional metrics, such as FAs (0.32), aliphatic% (0.28), polyphenols% (0.27), S/Cwa (0.28), and N/Cwa (0.25) (Fig. 6B). This implies that nitrogen transformations are not only linked to nitrogenous and sulfur-containing substrate availability (as indicated by N/Cwa and S/Cwa) but may also be influenced by the redox-modulating role of phenolic and humic substances (such as those indicated by HUPC%, polyphenols%, FAs, and HAs) within the complex DOM pool. Notably, the potential for S cycling was primarily linked to DOC concentration (0.35) and pH (0.31) (Fig. 6C), suggesting that S cycling is less directly tied to specific DOM molecular classes and is more influenced by bulk DOC concentration and the ambient pH altered by effluent input. The sheer number and strength of significant correlations in the dry season paint a picture of a functionally specialized microbial community whose inferred metabolic repertoire is finely tuned to the complex and diverse molecular landscape of EDOM.

In contrast, the wet season is characterized by a dramatic simplification of these relationships. The previously observed associations between diverse DOM molecular classes and C-cycle functional potential largely vanished, with only the HIX (0.34) retaining a significant, albeit weaker, correlation (Fig. 6D). This pattern indicates that during periods of high hydrological dilution, the unique molecular signature of EDOM diminishes as a key correlate, and only the broadest indicator of DOM recalcitrance remains statistically linked to the C-cycle functional potential. A similar simplification in the significant link was also observed for the N and S cycles. During the wet season, the potential for N cycling became significantly correlated with HIX (0.30) and pH (0.27), mirroring the pattern observed for the C-cycle (Fig. 6D). Concurrently, the potential for S cycling showed stronger correlations with DOM stoichiometric ratios like P/Cwa (0.36) and S/Cwa (0.26). This simplification suggests that under strong dilution, the distinct associations between EDOM components and functional potential were attenuated, with inferred microbial functional profiles becoming primarily correlated with basic environmental parameters such as pH and broad DOM characteristics (e.g., HIX).

The seasonal differences in correlated factors may be attributed to the interplay between effluent discharge and hydrological dilution. The dry season allows for the accumulation of EDOM, creating a sustained, selective pressure that drives the microbial community to adapt its functional genes to utilize a wide spectrum of organic substrates, particularly those that are complex and recalcitrant. The wet season, acting as a hydrological “reset” event that dilutes the effluent signal, alleviates this pressure, allowing allochthonous inputs and natural hydrological conditions to override the effluent signal. This resulted in a shift where the key factors correlating with microbial functional potential in the wet season aligned with those commonly observed in natural water bodies, namely pH and broad DOM characteristics.

Toward ecology-informed water quality management

The seasonal dynamics and functional consequences of EDOM elucidated above indicate that the regulatory framework could be enhanced to better account for the long-term and seasonally mediated ecological footprint of effluent discharge, moving beyond conventional parameters. To bridge this gap, we propose that specific DOM property indicators (such as HIX, HAs, and FAs) be integrated as supplementary early-warning parameters into the environmental management of critical watersheds. These indicators are not only scientifically robust, directly reflecting the recalcitrance, chemical specificity, and structural simplification of EDOM that shape microbial communities, but also highly feasible for routine monitoring. They can be measured rapidly and cost-effectively using standard optical techniques, such as 3D-EEMs. A practical implementation pathway is envisaged: beginning with pilot monitoring to establish baseline values in sensitive waters, advancing to model development that quantitatively links DOM indicators to ecological health endpoints, and culminating in the gradual integration of these parameters into regulatory frameworks as early-warning tools. This approach translates mechanistic understanding into an actionable strategy, paving the way for water quality management that proactively considers ecological function and resilience.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that effluent discharge does not impose a static influence on the receiving ecosystem. Instead, it creates a dynamic functional landscape where the impact of EDOM on microbial biogeochemical cycling is profoundly mediated by seasonality. The dry season fosters a unique, EDOM-adapted functional community, while the wet season homogenizes the system, diminishing the distinctive functional footprint of effluent. This understanding is critical for predicting the functional resilience of aquatic ecosystems under persistent anthropogenic disturbance and for refining water quality management strategies under a changing climate.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that continuous WWTP effluent discharge reshapes the material composition, microbial community structure, and functional potential of receiving waters. Beyond nutrient enrichment by N and P, effluent discharge significantly alters the molecular characteristics and composition of DOM, shifting it toward a more humified, recalcitrant, and lower-molecular-weight state. This effluent-derived DOM is a key factor regulating microbial community structure and functional potential, thereby promoting the formation of specific microbial communities. Notably, the ecological effects of EDOM exhibit clear seasonal differences: during the dry season, the accumulation of specific EDOM components dominates microbial functional potential, fostering the development of more complex and tightly interconnected microbial interaction networks; during the wet season, such specific correlations weaken, and microbial functions become more closely associated with general environmental parameters such as pH and HIX. This seasonal dynamic highlights the central role of EDOM in regulating microbial community richness, composition, and functional potential related to C, N, and S cycling.

The above findings suggest that relying solely on traditional nutrient indicators and comprehensive DOM parameters, e.g., chemical oxygen demand, may be insufficient to fully assess the ecological risks posed by effluent discharge. To improve management foresight, we propose incorporating diagnostic DOM indicators, such as HIX and FAs, into water quality monitoring systems and establishing seasonally adaptive management frameworks. For example, dry-season measures could focus on controlling sources of recalcitrant organic compounds, while wet-season strategies could integrate the management of point-source and non-point-source pollution. Future research should clarify the long-term fate of EDOM and its ecosystem-scale functional impacts to support more resilient watershed management. Implementing such refined strategies is crucial for reducing environmental stress and maintaining the ecological integrity and sustainability of receiving waters.
