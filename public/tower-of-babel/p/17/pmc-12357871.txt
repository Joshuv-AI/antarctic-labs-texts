Declawing in Cat is associated with neuroplastic sensitization and long-term painful afflictions
Authors: Mathieu LaChance, Colombe Otis, Tristan Juette, Jérôme R. E. del Castillo, Aliénor Delsart, Maxim Moreau, Beatriz P. Monteiro, Aude Castel, Bertrand Lussier, Johanne Martel-Pelletier, Jean-Pierre Pelletier, Eric Troncy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Declawing of pet cats is widely believed to cause chronic pain and negatively impact animal welfare, leading to bans in many jurisdictions. However, little is known on how post-declaw pain develops and affects feline well-being. Existing data often fail to account for other sources of chronic pain, such as osteoarthritis (OA), which affects most aging cats. Here, the aim was to distinguish chronic post-declaw pain from OA-related pain. A secondary analysis of eight studies on feline OA was conducted, comparing somatosensory, biomechanical and functional assessments between healthy control cats, declawed OA (DOA) cats, and non-declawed OA (NDOA) cats. DOA cats exhibited significant somatosensory alterations (hyperalgesia and allodynia) and greater biomechanical (worse in heavier cats) and functional impairments, compared to NDOA cats. The alterations were not dependent on the number of declawed paws (two forelimbs vs. four paws). This alarming phenotype was associated with objective nervous conduction abnormalities indicative of worsened axonopathy in DOA cats. Our findings highlight the impact of chronic post-declaw pain and support the need to develop therapeutic strategies to alleviate chronic pain in DOA cats and highlights the pertinence of establishing a global ban of this elective procedure.

Introduction

Elective feline onychectomy, commonly known as declawing, involves the surgical amputation of the distal phalanx (P3) of all digits on a cat’s forelimbs, or on both forelimbs and hindlimbs. The procedure is most performed to prevent damage to household items and reduce the risk of injury to humans1,2. In a 1991 survey, only 2% of 276 owners of declawed cats expressed dissatisfaction due to guilt over the procedure3. However, ethical concerns about animal welfare have led to bans on onychectomy in many jurisdictions, including most of Canada and Western Europe4. Notably, the procedure remains legal in most of the United States and parts of Canada. In 2001, it was estimated that nearly 25% of American cats were declawed5 while a 2014 survey of veterinarians (with nearly 3,500 respondents) reported that 72.7% performed onychectomy at least occasionnally6. While some veterinarians consider onychectomy a last-resort alternative to relinquishment or euthanasia6,7 the purported benefits of the procedure for cats remain largely unsubstantiated.

Like other surgical procedures, onychectomy can lead to both short-term and long-term adverse effects. One early retrospective study reported a perioperative complication rate of 50% at a veterinary teaching institution8 with the most common complications being pain, lameness, infection and hemorrhage5,8. Acute postoperative lameness may persist for over 12 days despite opioid analgesia9.

The long-term complications of onychectomy are less understood. The most reported issue is the presence of remnant P3 bone fragments, detected radiographically in 52.5–62.8% of declawed cats and linked to back pain10,11. Additional long-term complications include persistent or intermittent lameness, pain on paw palpation, nail regrowth, P2 protrusion, reluctance to jump and behavioral changes such as aggression and house soiling (with occurrence of 1.7–32.6%)1,3,5,8,10–12. Onychectomy has also been implicated in flexor tendon contracture in four cats13,14 and in the development of osteosarcoma in a retained P3 fragment in one case15. Some complications, such as lameness, remnant P3 fragments, and nail regrowth, have been reported to be reversible with additional surgery8,16,17. However, much of evidence for chronic complications stems from surveys and retrospective studies, often lacking thorough, objective clinical evaluations, making causation difficult to establish.

One major challenge in assessing the long-term effects of onychectomy is the high prevalence of osteoarthritis (OA) in aging cats. In Western countries, OA affects 25.6%18 of adult cats19 rising to over 60% in cats aged six years or older20 and over 90% in those aged 12 years or older21. As a leading cause of chronic pain and mobility impairment in adult cats22–24 OA-related pain may confound the assessment of post-onychectomy pain. While one study reported increased back pain and behavioral issues in age-matched declawed/non-declawed cats – particularly when an inadequate surgical technique was used11 – interactions between OA and onychectomy have not been thoroughly investigated. In 2022, the American Veterinary Medical Association Animal Welfare Division issued a policy on declawing25 concluding that “conflicting scientific evidence exists regarding the implications of declawing”. Given the growing pet cat population in North America, (approximately 74 million in the U.S. in 202426 and 8.5 million in Canada in 202227), and their role as family members28 it is critical to understand the potential neuroplastic changes induced by onychectomy.

Recent advancements in pain transmission and nociceptive processing research, both in healthy and pathological states, have provided valuable insights. Over the past decade, the Groupe de recherche en pharmacologie animale du Québec (GREPAQ) has developed and validated several methods to study OA-related pain and impairment in cats through behavioral analysis and systems neurobiology. These include kinetic podobarometric gait analysis (PGA) using a sensitive walkway mattress22,29–32 a standardized functional assessment scale named the Montreal Instrument for Cat Arthritis Testing, for use by Veterinarians (MI-CAT(V))24,33–36 and quantitative sensory testing (QST) to assess sensory alterations22,23,30,31,37. These tools have been shown to differentiate OA-affected cats from healthy controls (specificity), detect responses to treatment (sensitivity), and produce reliable results (reproducibility and repeatability)18. Additionally, nerve conduction studies can provide objective evidence of distal nerve dysfunction following onychectomy38.

In this secondary analysis, we hypothesized that these validated methods could be used to assess long-term post-onychectomy pain, with declawed OA (DOA) cats exhibiting more severe dysfunction than non-declawed OA (NDOA) cats. The main objectives of this study were to compare three populations of cats, assessed over a decade, i.e., DOA, NDOA, and healthy, non-declawed control (HC) cats, based on biomechanical impairment (PGA), MI-CAT(V) scores, QST results, and nerve conduction abnormalities in a subgroup of cats.

Results

Animals

Data from eight studies were available for a total of 24 HC cats (n = 17 males), 125 NDOA cats (n = 63 males) and 39 DOA cats (n = 17 males). The sex distribution was not significantly different between groups (p = 0.099). The overall mean (SD) body weight (BW) was 5.1 (1.0) kg, with no significant differences between groups (p = 0.069). Not all outcome measures were available for every cat (Table 1).

Greater somatosensory sensitization in DOA cats compared to NDOA cats

Compared to the HC group, both OA groups exhibited peripheral (lower PWT) and spinal (lower number of tolerated stimulations in RMTS) sensitization (Fig. 1): paw withdrawal threshold (PWT; Fig. 1a), 166.3 (34.4) g (HC) vs. 130.2 (52.0) g (NDOA) and 101.5 (56.1) g (DOA) (p < 0.001); response to mechanical temporal summation (RMTS; Fig. 1b), 27.9 (2.5) vs. 13.3 (5.3) and 12.6 (3.8) stimulations, respectively (p < 0.001). DOA cats exhibited significantly greater peripheral sensitization (lower PWT) than NDOA cats (p < 0.001). In all groups, PWT was higher in the hindlimbs, 136.2 (5.4) g, than in the forelimbs, 122.1 (5.5) g (p < 0.001) and increased with BW (p < 0.001), with each additional kg of BW increasing PWT by 9.2 (2.1) g on average (~ 1% increase). However, no significant interaction was found between group and BW (p = 0.536) or between group and (fore or hind) limb (p = 0.583). Allodynia determination was specific with 36 NDOA (28.8%), 22 DOA (57.9%) and only one HC (4.3%) cats meeting the criterion for allodynia; it was also sensitive with a significant difference between NDOA and DOA proportion of allodynic cats (p = 0.003). Body weight had no significant effect on allodynia (p = 0.097) or RMTS (p = 0.692). A Kaplan-Meier analysis of RMTS (Fig. 2) revealed substantial differences between HC and both OA groups. Hazard ratios showed that DOA cats were 17.4 (95%CI = 8.1–37.6) times more likely than HC cats to respond before the set cut-off, while NDOA cats 12.6 (95%CI = 6.5–24.6) times more likely than HC cats.

Greater biomechanical and functional impairments, but not structural alterations, in DOA vs. NDOA cats

Biomechanics

When considering the raw PVF recorded simultaneously on the four limbs (i.e., expressed in kg), a significant group effect was observed (p = 0.003), but post-hoc comparisons were not significant, with only a trend toward reduced PVFkg in DOA compared to both HC (p = 0.052) and NDOA (p = 0.075). In all groups, PVFkg was greater in forelimbs, 2.7 (0.1) kg, than in hindlimbs, 2.2 (0.1) kg (p < 0.001) and increased with BW (p < 0.001), with each kg of BW increasing PVFkg by 660.4 (110.1) g on average (66% increase).

When looking at PVF values normalized by BW (i.e., expressed in %BW), and particularly for the most-affected limb (MAL), biomechanical impairments were present (p < 0.001) in both OA groups compared to HC (Fig. 3): PVFMAL (Fig. 3a), was 50.8 (5.9) %BW (HC) vs. 42.8 (7.8) %BW (NDOA; p = 0.009) and 41.5 (11.1) %BW (DOA; p = 0.002) without significant difference between both OA groups (p = 0.856).

Figure 4 illustrates the interaction of the ground reaction forces, PVFs, with BW: In all three groups, PVFkg was affected positively by BW with a slope (correlation estimate ± 95%CI) being slightly higher for the control (0.66 ± 0.11), than the NDOA (0.50 ± 0.05) and the DOA group (0.25 ± 0.11) (Fig. 4a). As expected, PVFMAL, normalized to BW, was not affected by BW for the HC and NDOA groups, with the latter presenting lower values than the HC group (Fig. 4b). However, BW affected negatively the BW-normalized PVFMAL in the DOA group (p = 0.049), indicating that the heavier a cat was, the worse the biomechanical alterations were in this group (Fig. 4b).

Mobility function

Compared to the control group, both OA groups exhibited significant functional alterations (p < 0.001) measured using MI-CAT(V) scores: 9.7 (5.1) % (HC) vs. 27.2 (15.0) % (NDOA; p < 0.001) and 33.3 (15.2) % (DOA; p < 0.001). DOA cats had significantly higher scores than NDOA cats (p = 0.033) (Fig. 3b). MI-CAT(V) scores increased with BW (p < 0.001), with each kg of BW increasing the score by 4.7 (1.3) % on average. However, the interaction between group and BW was not significant (p = 0.124).

Radiographic structure

Declawing was not associated with increased radiographic OA severity in the present population sample, as OA scores were 8.8 (4.4) in NDOA cats and 9.2 (4.9) in DOA cats (p = 0.296).

No differences between DOA subgroups

To further assess the impact of declawing, DOA cats were categorized into four subgroups based on the number of declawed limbs (DOA-2 or −4) and the presence of remnant P3 fragments (F vs. NF): DOA-2NF (n = 6), DOA-2 F (n = 1), DOA-4NF (n = 7) and DOA-4 F (n = 7). Information relating to P3 fragments was not available for the other 18 DOA cats (n = 11 DOA-2 and 7 DOA-4). Comparisons of radiographic OA scores, PWT, PVF and MI-CAT(V) scores (Table 2) revealed no significant differences between subgroups.

Similar findings were observed when comparing NDOA, DOA-2 and DOA-4 groups, indicating that having fewer declawed paws (DOA-2) did not reduce neuroplastic sensitization (PWT, RMTS, Allodynia), functional alterations (MI-CAT(V); p = 0.408) or biomechanical impairments (p > 0.441).

Hindlimb motor nerve conduction was altered in DOA cats

Motor nerve conduction in a subgroup of DOA-4 (n = 5) sex- and age-matched with NDOA (n = 5) cats revealed a ~ 45% decrease in compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude in DOA cats (p < 0.025) at all three stimulation points (Fig. 5). However, motor nerve conduction velocity did not differ between groups. Phenotype descriptive data of both subgroups indicated that DOA-4 cats were slightly more affected than NDOA cats for MI-CAT(V): 26.7 (15.6) % vs. 22.1 (10.4) %, and PWT: 81.5 (25.8) g vs. 93.7 (37.1) g, respectively.

Discussion

Declawing of pet cats has long been a controversial topic in veterinary medicine. Historically, long term complications of onychectomy have been poorly described5 potentially due to a lack of validated objective assessments and the confounding overlap with other pathologic conditions such as OA. The present study provides compelling evidence that declawing leads to chronic pain and impairment beyond what is typically associated with OA in cats.

A large sample of cats was recruited through Canadian shelters, and strict standardization of procedures ensured the selection of OA-affected cats without other comorbidities. Comprehensive assessments – including physical, paraclinical analyses, behavioral, neurological, orthopedic, and radiographic evaluations – were conducted blindly by board-certified specialists to enhance the validity of the findings. These methodological rigor and validated assessments22,31 add strength to the study’s conclusions.

Declawing-induced neuropathic pain and somatosensory sensitization

Somatosensory plasticity encodes environmental experiences through functional and structural reorganizational processes that shape memory, perceptual sensitivity and behavior39. However, pathological alterations in the sensory system’s nociceptive pathways can cause corresponding plasticity to become chronically maladaptive, in response to particularly intense or repetitive noxious triggers, and mediate pain chronification as a form of memory40. This is especially evident for the somatosensory system in specific types of neuropathic pain through potentiation of nociception, central sensitization and altered brain connectivity41,42. Central sensitization is known to be an important component of OA pain in people43,44 and in cats22,30,31,37 and may manifest as hyperalgesia and allodynia.

The findings demonstrate that DOA cats experience greater somatosensory sensitization compared to NDOA (and control) cats. The significantly lower PWT in DOA cats supports the presence of hyperalgesia, and the higher proportion of allodynic DOA cats further reinforces this conclusion. While one HC cat met the allodynia criterion, potentially indicating inter-individual variability in responsiveness to QST, the overall trend is clear: declawed cats exhibit heightened pain sensitivity.

Nevertheless, this allodynia classification has been useful for grading the severity of OA pain22 and PWT and RMTS have been validated as specific QST in OA cats22,23,31,37. Importantly, OA cats classified as allodynic have been shown to respond poorly to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) meloxicam22,30. However, these cats may still benefit from other adjunctive analgesics such as gabapentin24 and tramadol30,31. This raises significant concerns regarding the management of OA pain in declawed cats (as well as in other conditions), as NSAIDs remain a cornerstone of OA treatment18. The absence of significant difference in RMTS between DOA and NDOA cats (but both lower than HC cats) may be due to insufficient statistical power; however, Kaplan-Meier (Fig. 2) and hazard ratios analysis suggested increased sensitivity in DOA cats, warranting further investigation into the spinal cord mechanisms underlying this effect. Spinal cord excitability, particularly involved in the onset of central sensitization45,46 is indirectly assessed through RMTS, and the sensitivity of this outcome could be such that RMTS reaches a nadir level and not reflecting the depth of all neurobiological events.

Mechanisms underlying declawing-related pain

The physiopathology of chronic pain following onychectomy remains speculative, but our findings align with mechanisms observed in post-amputation pain syndromes in humans47,48 or cats49. Neuropathic pain following limb amputation is often attributed to maladaptive neuroplasticity, central sensitization, and sequelae of peripheral nerve damage47,50. Neuromas may arise from the inflammation and nerve regrowth that follow nerve transection47. While chronic pain in human amputees does not strictly correlate with the presence of neuromas51 neuroma ablation has been shown to alleviate pain in some patients52. The role of neuromas in post-onychectomy pain in cat remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

Chronic nerve injury in declawed cat was further supported by the nerve conduction study. The CMAP amplitude of motor tibial/sciatic nerve was lower in DOA than in NDOA cats, with a major effect size53 of at least 2.1 (power of 92%) at the hip level, reaching 2.7 (99%) at the distal hock. Decreased CMAP amplitude indicates loss of motor unit function (in the absence of severe muscle disease)54 and has been shown to occur with peripheral neuropathy55. Deep digital flexor muscle atrophy, as has been observed in declawed non-domestic felids56 may also account for the lower CMAP amplitude in DOA cats in this study. The mechanisms underlying that atrophy are not known, but might directly involve the loss of P3 and the tendon damage that occur during onychectomy56. Clearly, such a difference in CMAP amplitude, more pronounced distally, is indicative of axonal loss38. It may be associated with the nerve damages post-P3 amputation, and its phenotypical expression requires sensitive methods of assessment.

Declawing-related biomechanical and functional impairments

Declawed OA cats exhibited significant biomechanical (PVF) and functional (MI-CAT(V)) impairments compared to NDOA cats. These could be related to the onychectomy-induced somatosensory hypersensitivity and/or neuropathic abnormalities, contributing to altered paw biomechanics and muscle atrophy.

The PVF measurements indicated a clear difference between HC and both OA groups (Fig. 3a), though differences between DOA and NDOA groups were less pronounced. However, the significant interaction of BW and PGA outcomes normalized by BW, i.e. PVFMAL, in the DOA group (Fig. 4b) was unexpected and highlighted a key finding: heavier declawed cats exhibited greater biomechanical alterations, suggesting that weight exacerbates onychectomy-induced locomotor dysfunction by altering the ability to sustain the impact of normal ground reaction forces.

The PGA is a validated outcome to detect biomechanical impairment in cats57. It distinguished (specificity) between OA and normal cats22,29,31. and was sensitive to treatment of OA pain, either NSAID meloxicam22,30 and firocoxib32 or tramadol30,31. Over the last 13 years, the MI-CAT(V) scale has been developed and underwent many steps of concurrent (to objective outcomes) validation and refinement24,33,34. Recently were completed its concurrent validation with predictive validity, responsiveness to treatment with low placebo rate, reliability (reproducibility and repeatability) and discriminatory ability35,36. In the present study, the MI-CAT(V) scale was sensitive enough to detect worsening functional impairment from control to NDOA, to DOA cats (Fig. 3b). These findings align with previous research showing the scale’s responsiveness to OA pain treatment18,35,36. This supports the conclusion that declawed cats experience greater functional impairment, further compromising their overall well-being.

Alternative interpretations and potential confounders

A few studies have assessed PGA after onychectomy: a reduction in PVF was observed for a 12 days follow-up after unilateral forelimb onychectomy9 but not when assessed more than 6-months after bilateral forelimb onychectomy in young adult cats58. The endogenous-pain-healing system restores body tissue to a non-painful state after an injury leading to pain (such as onychectomy)59. The impairment of the endogenous pain modulatory system (altered cortical excitability and/or descending pain modulation60 would be related to the disruption of the pain healing process and its chronicity59. The authors acknowledge pain to be associated with plastic changes in the central nervous system that lead to an activation pattern of motor units that contributes to the individual’s adaptive sensorimotor behavior61. This activation pattern takes into account the biological, psychological and social influences on the musculoskeletal tissues involved in sensorimotor behavior and on the neuroplastic and glioplastic changes, as well as the experience of pain in that individual. This supports the individual variability in pain expression for a similar insult, and the pattern aims to minimize pain and any associated sensorimotor changes. The findings in the DOA group support that adverse biopsychosocial factors, such as declawing-, OA-associated neural damages, and their interactions, result in neuroplastic changes leading to less optimal, even maladaptive, sensorimotor changes producing motor unit activation patterns associated with the development of further chronic pain61.

It has been proposed that DOA cats are a heterogenous group: cats may be declawed on two or four limbs, and remnant P3 fragments may be present or absent. Recent studies have shown that P3 fragments were associated with a greater prevalence of unwanted behaviors and pain in declawed cats11,17. Our data could not confirm those findings, as all DOA subgroups showed similar signs of pain and impairment. Although our sample sizes were small, it should be noted that no variation of onychectomy (two or four paws) can be regarded as benign in the long term.

While our study strongly supports the conclusion that declawing exacerbates pain and dysfunction in OA cats, potential alternative explanations should be considered as potential limitations. Factors such as pre-existing subclinical neuropathy, individual variability in pain sensitivity, or subtle differences in activity levels between groups could contribute to the observed differences. Additionally, while efforts were made to exclude comorbidities, underlying conditions not detectable through standard diagnostics might have influenced results. Future studies could incorporate advanced imaging or molecular assessments to further explore neuroplastic changes in declawed cats.

Call to action: the case for a worldwide ban on declawing

This study provides robust evidence that declawing has chronic, deleterious effects on feline welfare and supports universal ban of this convenience-based surgery. Onychectomy-induced axonopathy exacerbates the neuropathic component of OA pain, leading to biomechanical dysfunction, maladaptive neuroplasticity, and impaired quality of life. Given that declawing is performed for owner convenience rather than as a medical necessity, these findings strongly support legislative efforts to ban the procedure.

Veterinary professionals must take an active role in educating cat owners about the severe long-term consequences of declawing and advocate for alternative strategies such as behavioral training, nail trimming, and the use of scratching posts. Indeed, other procedures such as flexor tenotomy/tendonectomy should be avoided as they alter the expression of natural feline behavior and could generate chronic pain similarly to declawing. Additionally, regulatory bodies should incorporate scientific evidence into policy decisions to protect feline welfare. As veterinary medicine continues to evolve toward evidence-based practice, it is imperative to recognize declawing not as a routine procedure, but as an ethically unacceptable intervention with lasting negative consequences for cats.

In conclusion, this study reinforces the urgent need to prohibit declawing and shift toward compassionate, welfare-focused veterinary care that prioritizes the well-being of feline patients.

Methods

Study design and animals

This study is a secondary analysis of data collected from eight studies conducted by GREPAQ between 2010 and 2023, and has been reported following the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. These studies aimed to discriminate cats with naturally occurring OA from non-OA cats, and to evaluate the effects of pharmacological treatments in OA cats. Each study received prior approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol numbers: 10/11-Rech-148222,29, A123-ART12F2413/14-Rech-175730,31, 16/19-Rech-183232,35, 21/23-Rech-211419). All procedures involving animal handling, housing and husbandry followed the guidelines outlined in the Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals published by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the U.S. National Institutes of Health.

Diagnosis of OA was established through independent and blinded orthopedic and radiographic evaluation by a North American board-certified veterinary surgeon (B.LU.). As the study population was sourced from Canadian shelters, the exact age at which onychectomy was performed was unknown for all DOA cats. However, verification procedures confirmed that declawing had been performed several years before the cats were recruited into the respective studies. To ensure data integrity, cats with comorbid conditions were non-included based on comprehensive physical and neurological examinations, along with routine bloodwork and urinalysis (inclusion and exclusion criteria described in each above-cited study publication). Individual-level data were extracted from the GREPAQ database, and cats were classified into three groups: healthy control (HC) cats – non-declawed, with no radiographic evidence of OA; non-declawed OA (NDOA) cats – diagnosed with OA but not declawed; and declawed OA (DOA) cats – diagnosed with OA and previously subjected to onychectomy. Comparative analyses were conducted across these groups using the following outcome measures, all evaluations being completed in blinded condition:

Additionally, a preliminary investigation of motor nerve conduction was performed in a subset of NDOA (n = 5) and DOA (n = 5) cats.

Radiographic assessment of structural alterations

Each cat underwent a comprehensive radiographic evaluation of the thoracic (carpus, elbow, shoulder) and pelvic (tarsus, stifle, hip) appendicular joints. Radiographic scoring was based on a composite system, combining a severity scale (ranging from 0 to 5, where 0 indicates no OA and 5 represents severe OA) across twelve joints, along with the total number of joint(s) affected by radiographic OA.

To qualify as an OA case, a cat had to exhibit radiographic alterations in at least one appendicular joint, characterized by the presence of osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, or cyst formation. Lesions such as meniscal mineralization or enthesiophytes were only considered clinically significant if they co-occurred with osteophytes or subchondral changes.

Where feasible, DOA cats also underwent radiographic screening for remnant P3 fragments to assess potential associations with long-term complications of onychectomy.

Quantitative sensory testing (QST)

Commonly used in human medicine, QST is a standardized and formalized set of clinical sensitivity tests, relying on semi-objective methods that require cooperation62. In veterinary applications, QST necessitates careful acclimation and training, as well as adaptation of calibrated stimuli to assess both sensory perception (dynamic QST) and pain threshold (static QST)37,63. This method evaluates both sensory (pain) loss (hypoesthesia, hypoalgesia) and sensory gain (hyperesthesia, hyperalgesia, allodynia)64 thereby testing nociceptive and non-nociceptive properties of different afferent nerve fibers and central pathways62.

Static QST – Punctate tactile sensitivity

Punctate tactile sensitivity was assessed using an electronic von Frey esthesiometer applied to the plantar/palmar paw surface. Paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) to ascending mechanical stimuli was recorded using a mechanical von Frey polypropylene probe (Rigid Tip 0.7 mm2 of surface 28G, IITC Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA, USA) fitted to a hand-held force transducer. While the cat stood in a specialized meshed cage designed for this evaluation, the probe tip was applied perpendicularly to the plantar/palmar surface of each paw while distracting the cat with treats or toys. The stimulus was gradually increased until the cat withdrew or elevated the paw, or displayed other avoidance behaviors such as vocalization, agitation, or jumping. A cut-off of 200 g was imposed to prevent injury.

Each paw was tested once per session, and each session included four PWT measurements conducted in duplicate (separated by a 4-minute interval) in a randomized order to prevent habituation or anticipation. Measurements below 2 g were considered unreliable and discarded. A predetermined allodynic threshold was established based on the first quartile of PWT values from a prior study on OA cats22. Cats were classified as allodynic if at least two of their eight total measurements (forelimb and hind limb) fell below this threshold.

Dynamic QST – Response to mechanical temporal summation (RMTS)

Mechanical temporal summation was assessed using a custom-made mechanical stimulator (TOP CAT, Bespoke Measurement Systems, Cambs, UK)23,30,31. The device applied repeated sub-threshold mechanical stimuli – each insufficient to elicit a pain response individually – at a fixed intensity (4 N), frequency (0.4 Hz) and duration (up to 30 stimulations over 72.5 s). A round-ended metallic pin (2.5 mm diameter, 10 mm length) mounted on a light rolling diaphragm actuator delivered the mechanical stimuli. The stimulator was secured within a band placed around the limb, positioned on the cranial aspect of either the right or left mid-antebrachium, with a ‘dummy’ device placed on the contralateral limb for control. During testing, cats were free to move within a meshed cage.

The evaluator terminated the stimulus application upon observing avoidance behaviors such as vocalization, agitation, striking at the band, or withdrawal of the limb. If no avoidance behavior occurred, stimulation automatically ceased after the maximum of 30 repetitions. Each assessment was conducted in duplicate, with a 4-minute interval between trials to ensure the cat remained in a positive, enriched environment.

The RMTS is associated with spinal wind-up, a phenomenon that amplifies nociceptive signals and facilitates pain sensitization. The RMTS score represents the total number of non-noxious mechanical stimuli tolerated before the cat exhibited an avoidance response, as previously validated23.

PGA – Biomechanical measure

Vertically oriented ground reaction forces were measured using a floor mat-based force measurement system (Walkway System WE4, Tekscan) in conjunction with the Walkway Research Software v.7.0 (Tekscan Inc., Boston, MA, USA). Calibration was performed prior to each measurement to ensure accuracy. The cats were trained to voluntarily trot across the Walkway in response to a specific auditory cue (clicker), receiving a treat or other reward at the end. Trot speed was maintained within predefined range of 0.8–2.0 m•s−1, calculated using the time and distance of each crossing57. Only the four-foot strikes from the first full stride of each trot were analyzed. Each session allowed the cat up to 10 trot attempts across the mat, with the goal of obtaining three valid sets of four-foot strikes for comparison. A valid attempt required that the cat trotted uninterrupted across the entire walkway; maintained a straight trajectory; and maintained a consistent speed throughout the trial. If three valid trots were not achieved within the allowed 10 attempts, the cat was released, and its data excluded from analysis.

Among the PGA parameters recorded, only peak vertical force (PVF) – the maximum vertical force applied by each limb during the stance phase – was analyzed22,29,65. Each limb PVF values (in kg) were collected, screened for validation (as described above) and used for for subsequent calculations. Based on the PVF normalized by BW (% BW), for each cat the limb (thoracic or pelvic) exhibiting the lowest PVF%BW was identified as the most affected limb (MAL). The outcome, PVFMAL, was calculated as the mean value of three valid trials for the MAL. PVFMAL is recognized as the most reliable kinetic indicator of weight-bearing asymmetry in OA assessment22,65.

MI-CAT(V) – A validated scale for OA-related functional alterations

Functional disabilities associated with OA were assessed using the MI-CAT(V) scale24,34–36 a validated observational tool designed to quantify mobility limitations in cats. The current version of MI-CAT(V) consists of four standardized sections: Body posture, Gait, Obstacles, and Global distance examination35,36.

While earlier iterations of the scale included slight variations in scoring criteria, the core structure of the first three sections remained unchanged. To account for discrepancies across versions used in different studies, the total MI-CAT(V) score was expressed as a percentage of functional alterations rather than absolute values. A higher MI-CAT(V) score indicates greater impairment in mobility and physical function.

Motor (tibial/sciatic) nerve conduction

A comprehensive neurological examination was performed on conscious cats by a North American board-certified veterinary neurologist (A.CA.) before conducting nerve conduction studies under standardized general anesthesia. The study included n = 5 DOA-4 (4 F, 1 M; 10.2 (2.9) years) n = 5 age- and sex-matched NDOA (10.4 (2.9) years) cats.

Anesthesia and monitoring

After an 8- to 10-hour fasting period, anesthesia was induced and maintained as follows: Premedication: Dexmedetomidine (5 µg/kg SC, G20 site) (DEXDOMITOR 0.5 mg/mL, Zoetis Canada, Kirkland, QC, Canada). Induction: Propofol (3 mg/kg IV) (PROPOFOL 10 mg/mL, Baxter Corporation, Mississauga, ON, Canada). Analgesia: Fentanyl bolus (3 µg/kg IV) (FENTANYL CITRATE IINJECTION USP 50 µg/mL, Sandoz Canada, Boucherville, QC, Canada) administered upon achieving a satisfactory depth of anesthesia (Stage 3 Plane II: regular respiration, stable cardiovascular function, constricted pupils, relaxed muscle tone, loss of eyelid and corneal reflexes). Maintenance: Propofol (0.26–0.28 mg/kg/min Syringe Pump DIGIPUMP SR31x, Digicare Biomedical Technology, Boynton Beach, FL, USA) continuous rate infusion (CRI). The cat was kept on enriched oxygen (1.0–1.5 L/min by mask) and fluid therapy (3 mL/kg/h, LACTATE RINGER’S INJECTION USP, Baxter Corporation), and eyes lubricated (OPTIXCARE eye lube, Aventix, Burlington, Ontario, Canada). The body temperature was maintained above 37.5 °C using a heated pad (VETERINARY HEAT PUMP PATCH/HTP-1500, Adroit Medical Systems, Loudon, TN, USA), blankets and warm bottles. Because hypothermia negatively impacts animal safety and the reliability of nerve conduction measurements, the procedure was discontinued if rectal temperature fell below 37 °C and did not improve within 10 min. Monitoring: Eye reflexes, capillary refill time and mucous membranes color were assessed manually every 5 min. Continuous monitoring (LW6000 LIFEWINDOW monitor, Digicare Biomedical Technology, Boynton Beach, FL, USA) included, heart rate, systemic arterial blood pressure measured by an oscillometric adapted cuff placed on the tail or on a limb, tongue pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, and rectal temperature. Recovery: At the middle of the procedure, the propofol CRI was decreased to 0.22–0.24 mg/kg/min before discontinuation at the end of the procedure. Dexmedetomidine was antagonized with atipamezole (25 µg/kg IM, lumbar muscle) (ANTISEDAN 5 mg/ml, Zoetis Canada, Kirkland, QC, Canada) and the fluid therapy stopped.

Nerve conduction measurement

Motor nerve conduction was assessed using the SIERRA II WEDGE/EP system (Caldwell Industries, Kennewick, WA, USA) on the right tibial/sciatic mixed nerve. The cat was positioned in left lateral recumbency, and subdermal needle electrodes were placed as previously described66,67. Stimulation and data collection: Stimulating electrodes (cathode and anode) were placed at three levels along the tibial/sciatic nerve: hock (tarsus), stifle, and hip. Recording electrodes were placed in the plantar interosseous muscles. Electric stimulation was applied until the maximal amplitude of the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) was reached and remained unchanged despite increasing stimulus intensity confirming that supramaximal stimulation had been reached. The distance between the different stimulation points was measured with a calibrated tape (in cm). Latency (ms) and amplitude (mV) of CMAP obtained at the different stimulation sites were recorded. Polyphasia (CMAP waveform crossing baseline five or more times) – a potential marker of nerve fiber regeneration – was not observed in this study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1 “Beagle Scouts”, The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Group comparisons: The distributions of sex and body weight (BW) within each group of cats were compared with a chi-square test, and a linear mixed model, respectively. The distribution of the groups across studies was compared with a multinomial model. Primary outcome analysis: Linear mixed models (LMMs) were used to evaluate the effects of group, BW, and the group * BW interaction on PWT, RMTS, MI-CAT(V) scores and ground reaction forces. Random subject effects were included to account for within-subject variability. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted when a significant group effect was detected. RMTS was also compared using a Kaplan-Meier and hazard ratios analysis. The effects of group and BW on allodynia were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) for a binomial distribution, incorporating random study effects.

Nerve conduction data: Both DOA-4 and NDOA subgroups were compared as two independent groups with non-parametric tests. Effect size and power were calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), based on group means, standard deviations (SD), and sample sizes68.

Model validation and adjustments: Residuals normality was confirmed with Shapiro-Wilks tests. Models were tested with likelihood ratios and Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons were applied, for an alpha threshold of 5%.
