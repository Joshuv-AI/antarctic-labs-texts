Impact of diabetes mellitus on one-year outcomes of bioresorbable versus durable polymer drug-eluting stents in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention following rotational atherectomy: Results from a large prospective registry☆
Authors: Matthew Siano, Rakhee Makhija, Neel Patel, Samantha Sartori, Serdar Farhan, David Power, Yihan Feng, Wen Chang, Anton Camaj, Frank Kalaba, Vishal Dhulipala, Keisuke Yasumura, Roja Thapi, Pooja Vijay, Nikito Gurung, Manish Vinayak, Amit Hooda, Parasuram Krishnamoorthy, Joseph Sweeny, George Dangas, Roxana Mehran, Annapoorna Kini, Samin Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with diabetes mellitus (DM) have worse outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), even with everolimus-eluting stents (EES). Limited data are available comparing outcomes in DM versus non-DM receiving various bioresorbable polymer (BP) or durable polymer (DP) EES after rotational atherectomy (RA). We aimed to study the impact of DM on outcomes in patients undergoing PCI with BP-EES and DP-EES after RA.MethodsConsecutive patients undergoing PCI with RA between 2014 and 2021 were included. The primary outcome was major adverse cardiac events (MACE), a composite of death, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke at 1 year, which was stratified according to DM status. Regression analysis was performed to adjust for confounders.ResultsAmong 2376 patients (mean age 70.3 ± 10.7, 49.5 % DM) that underwent RA, 552 received BP-EES and 1824 received DP-EES. Both groups had similar number of stents implanted and stent diameters; however, the BP-EES group had a higher SYNTAX score and was more likely to have multi-vessel disease and undergo PCI to the left anterior descending, circumflex, and right coronary artery. In both BP-EES and DP-EES groups, there was no significant difference in adjusted risk of MACE (BP-EES: 7.3 % vs. 7.1 %, HR 0.78, 95 % CI 0.35–1.78, P = 0.562 & DP-EES: 7.5 % vs. 3.7 %, HR 1.49, 95 % CI 0.89–2.50, P = 0.130) between diabetics and non-diabetics.ConclusionsDespite differences in baseline characteristics, DM was not associated with worse MACE at one year in patients with complex coronary artery disease undergoing RA and PCI with contemporary BP-EES or DP-EES.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Rotational atherectomy, Percutaneous coronary intervention, bioresorbable polymer, durable polymer, Drug-eluting stent

Introduction

Contemporary stents include durable polymer everolimus-eluting stents (DP-EES) commonly made of cobalt chromium (CoCr-DP-EES or Xience) or platinum chromium (PtCr-DP-EES or Promus) and bioresorbable polymer everolimus-eluting stents (BP-EES) made of platinum chromium (PtCr-BP-EES or Synergy). These stents are examples of second-generation drug-eluting stents (DES), which have been shown to have superior outcomes compared to their predecessor first-generation DES or bare metal stents [[1], [2], [3]]. Durable polymers may be associated with a late hypersensitivity reaction that can increase risk of stent thrombosis [4]. Stents featuring bioresorbable polymers were designed to reduce this theoretical risk by removing the polymer as a nidus for inflammation. Various trials have examined whether this theoretical advantage translates to improvements in outcomes. Most studies have found no difference between contemporary BP-DES and DP-DES [[5], [6], [7]]. However, several studies have shown BP-DES to be superior in certain patient populations [[8], [9], [10], [11]], while a few have demonstrated BP-DES to have worse outcomes than CoCr-EES [12,13].

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is associated with intimal calcification which necessitates advanced plaque modification techniques such as rotational atherectomy (RA) for optimal stent expansion prior to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [14]. Some, but not all [15,16], studies have shown diabetics have worse outcomes following PCI with [17,18] and without RA [[19], [20], [21]]. In some studies, it has been shown that PCI with RA and BP-DES have lower adverse event rates versus DP-DES [9,10]. Our study aimed to compare outcomes of patients undergoing PCI after RA utilizing contemporary BP-EES or DP-EES stratified by the presence or absence of DM.

Methods

Study population and design

Consecutive patients undergoing RA followed by PCI at Mount Sinai Hospital between 2014 and 2021 provided informed consent and were enrolled in the institutional catheterization registry and RA registry. Patients who received DP-EES (CoCr-DP-EES, Xience or PtCr-DP-EES, Promus) and BP-EES (PtCr-BP-EES, Synergy) following RA were included. Xience, manufactured by Abbott Vascular, is a CoCr platform with a poly-n-butyl methacrylate and poly-vinylidene difluoride co-hexafluoropropylene durable polymer circumferentially coated with everolimus featuring 81 μm strut thickness. Promus, developed by Boston Scientific, shares these characteristics with Xience but consists of PtCr stent material. Synergy, developed by Boston Scientific, contains PtCr with a poly-lactic-co-glycolic acid bioresorbable polymer albuminally coated with everolimus containing 74 μm strut thickness. The patients were divided into BP-EES or DP-EES groups based on stent implanted. Within each stent cohort, patients with diabetes were compared to those without diabetes. Patients less than 18 years of age were excluded. PCI was performed with standard technique. Choice of stent was according to treating physician preference. RA was performed with the Rotablator or RotaPro systems (Boston Scientific) using standard laboratory technique. The decision to perform RA was made at the operator's discretion based on lesion morphology. Burr speeds of 140,000–160,000 rpm were used, with individual runs limited to 15–20 s and a target burr-to-artery ratio of approximately 0.4–0.6. The Institutional Review Board at Mount Sinai Hospital approved the study. The data underlying this article may be shared upon request to the corresponding author.

Data collection

Informed consent was obtained for anonymous data collection and follow-up. Baseline clinical, laboratory, and procedural data was obtained from the electronic medical record or standardized forms on admission. Follow-up was completed through clinic visits, telephone interviews by trained personnel, and review of electronic medical records up to one-year post-procedure.

Definitions and endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was major adverse cardiac events (MACE), a composite of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke at one year. Secondary endpoints included the incidence of all-cause mortality, MI, stroke, ischemia-driven target vessel revascularization (TVR), and bleeding at one-year follow-up. The third universal definition of myocardial infarction was used to define MI [22]. TVR was defined as repeated vascularization of any portion proximal or distal to the target lesion within the major coronary vessel. Bleeding was defined as per the National Cardiovascular Data Registry CathPCI Registry (version 4.4), including any bleeding during the index hospitalization associated with a decrease in hemoglobin >3 g/dL, blood transfusion, or requiring procedural intervention or surgery at the site of bleeding.

Statistical analysis

Univariate analyses of baseline clinical and procedural characteristics were performed and compared between stent cohorts. Continuous variables are displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared with the Student's t-test or Mann-Whitney U test on the basis of normality. Categorical variables are presented as percent frequency and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression were performed to compare endpoints within each stent cohort according to diabetes status and are respectively reported as hazard ratios (HR) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI). Multivariate adjustment was performed to control for age, atrial fibrillation, anemia, chronic kidney disease, PCI presentation, multi-vessel disease, SYNTAX score, and total lesion length. The interaction test was performed within the DP-EES cohort to evaluate for differences in outcomes between CoCr-DP-EES and PtCr-DP-EES. A sensitivity analysis limited to patients receiving CoCr-DP-EES, excluding PtCr-DP-EES from the DP-EES cohort, was conducted. Cumulative incidence rates, stratified by stent cohort and diabetes status, were obtained using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences among the four groups were compared using the global log-rank test. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp). All P values are 2-tailed with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Among 2376 patients that underwent RA followed by PCI, 552 received BP-EES and 1824 received DP-EES. The baseline clinical characteristics of each stent cohort are reported in Table 1. The mean age of patients who received BP-EES was higher than that of DP-EES recipients. There were no differences in body mass index, female sex, and race or ethnicity between groups. Both groups were similar with respect to the prevalence of most comorbidities, though the BP-EES cohort had a higher prevalence of anemia, lung disease, atrial fibrillation, and chronic kidney disease (Table 1). There was no difference in the rate of insulin-dependent DM or hemoglobin A1c levels between patients receiving BP-EES and DP-EES. Patients receiving BP-EES were more likely to present with unstable angina or non-ST segment elevation myocardial infarction, but there was no difference between groups regarding presentation with ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (Table 1).

Angiographic and procedural characteristics

Procedural and angiographic data are displayed in Table 2. Overall, 73.3 % of patients had multivessel disease, and a mean of 2.3 ± 1.5 lesions were treated. Patients in the BP-EES group were more likely to have multivessel disease and undergo PCI to the left anterior descending, circumflex, and right coronary artery. Both groups had a similar prevalence of moderate or severe calcification, bifurcation disease, and chronic total occlusions. Although the BP-EES group had a higher number of lesions treated, both groups had a similar number of stents implanted and maximum stent diameter. However, the BP-EES cohort had greater total lesion length and total stent length implanted. While there was no difference in the frequency of B2/C lesions, patients receiving BP-EES had slightly higher SYNTAX scores and a greater proportion of complex PCI cases.

Discussion

In this study investigating outcomes of patients with and without DM undergoing PCI using BP-EES and DP-EES after plaque modification with RA, several findings emerged. BP-EES were more commonly used in patients with higher comorbidity and more complex coronary artery disease (CAD). Diabetic patients receiving DP-EES had a higher risk of MACE in unadjusted analyses. However, after adjusting for baseline differences, no significant outcome differences were found across BP-EES and DP-EES in patients with or without DM.

Previous autopsy studies have demonstrated delayed arterial healing and chronic inflammation associated with durable polymers in DES [23]. To mitigate these issues, BP-DES were developed, which metabolize after drug elution into water and carbon dioxide, resembling a bare metal stent. Results from studies comparing BP-DES to DP-DES with less complex CAD have been inconsistent [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Importantly, our study evaluated outcomes following PCI using BP-EES and DP-EES in patients with CAD requiring an atheroablative strategy. Interestingly, we observed both increased morbidity and increased severity of CAD in patients treated with BP-EES, which were not demonstrated in earlier studies. A study utilizing the RA in calcified lesions in Korea (ROCK) registry, as well as a smaller, single-center observational study, has made such a comparison and found similar baseline clinical characteristics between stent groups [9,10]. The relatively small sample sizes of these two studies may have contributed to a lack of statistical power to detect differences in baseline characteristics. The reasons for increased disease burden in our cohort of BP-EES recipients are unclear. Possible explanations include the smaller strut thickness of the PtCr-BP-EES platform which allows for easier deliverability in severely calcified and tortoise lesions, or its availability as a longer stent which can cover extensive lesions without requiring placement of two overlapping stents. Furthermore, operator preference cannot be ruled out from the present analysis. Dual antiplatelet therapy duration has been investigated with BP-EES, including in elderly patients at higher risk of bleeding, which might have contributed to the increased prevalence of anemia and atrial fibrillation in patients receiving BP-EES compared to DP-EES [24,25].

In Kaplan-Meier analyses, event rates differed across the four study subgroups, with visually lowest MACE and MI observed among non-diabetic patients treated with DP-EES. The aforementioned observational study using ROCK registry data compared outcomes between 272 patients treated with various DP-DES and 238 patients treated with various BP-DES, finding decreased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality at three years in the BP-DES group after both multivariate regression and propensity score matching [9]. Another observational study compared outcomes between 121 patients treated with bioresorbable polymer sirolimus-eluting stents (BP-SES) and 164 patients treated with DP-EES after RA [10]. There were comparable rates of target lesion failure at two years between groups in the overall population, but decreased target lesion failure in the ultra-thin (60 μm) BP-SES subgroup versus DP-EES, suggesting strut thickness – not polymer durability – was responsible for the difference in outcomes [10]. Multiple studies have compared BP-DES versus DP-DES in patients with DM and found no differences in outcomes according to stent implanted [[26], [27], [28]]. The available literature comparing polymer durability following RA mirrors the broader literature comparing bioresorbable to durable polymers in the absence of RA, which suggests comparable outcomes between bioresorbable and durable polymers but possibly improved outcomes with ultra-thin struts. The Kaplan-Meier analysis represents an unadjusted comparison among stent groups, without pairwise or adjusted analyses, and future studies could further explore these between-group differences.

In the present study, diabetics treated with DP-EES following RA had higher MACE and MI than non-diabetics, which was reduced to a nonsignificant trend upon adjustment, while no differences were observed in outcomes between patients with and without DM receiving BP-EES. Most studies have shown that patients with DM have worse outcomes compared to non-diabetics following PCI without RA, both in DP-DES [19] and in BP-DES [20,21]. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies examining the impact of DM on PCI outcomes following RA, suggesting RA attenuates the negative impact of DM [15,16].

The findings from this analysis carry several important clinical implications. First, both BP-EES and DP-EES appear to be safe for patients with or without DM undergoing PCI for complex CAD that necessitates atheroablative vessel preparation. Our results do not support the assumption that more calcified stenoses require stents with thicker struts to prevent recoil and stent failure and are in line with earlier studies using bioresorbable polymer ultra-thin DES [29,30]. Second, both BP-EES and DP-EES were effective in patients with DM, a population traditionally considered the Achilles' heel of PCI. However, further research from prospective studies is needed to confirm these findings.

Limitations of the present study include a single-center and observational design with associated weaknesses and biases. Selection bias is possible in view of dissimilar baseline characteristics between BP-EES and DP-EES groups. Lesion-level data regarding the number of lesions treated with rotational atherectomy were not available, which may limit more detailed procedural comparisons between groups. The relatively small sample size of the BP-EES cohort may be insufficiently powered to detect outcomes. The pooled DP-EES group limits head-to-head stent comparisons. Additionally, the duration and types of antiplatelet drugs used after PCI may have impacted the results obtained in the present analysis. Longer follow-up is also needed to assess late or very late events that may occur beyond one year and after completion of dual antiplatelet therapy.

Conclusion

In a cohort of more complex CAD requiring an atheroablative strategy for plaque modification, both DP-EES and BP-EES were associated with reassuring outcomes regardless of diabetes status. Prospective randomized studies are warranted to further elucidate the role of contemporary DES in patients with more advanced CAD undergoing PCI.
