“Menstrual hygiene perceptions and need for free sanitary products: a cross-sectional study in a UAE university setting”
Authors: Tehniyat Hammad, Maryam Alsaffar, Nusrat Taosin Ashin, Khadijah Rashid, Aaqila Gangat, Jayadevan Sreedharan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMenstrual health and hygiene play a vital role in the overall well-being and empowerment of women. Despite its importance, approximately 500 million women globally lack access to menstrual products and proper hygiene facilities, primarily due to financial constraints. Many resort to alternatives such as cloth, cotton, or tissue, which impacts their health, mental well-being, and academic or career progression due to absenteeism caused by the fear of leakage and embarrassment.ObjectiveTo assess the perception of both females and males on the need for freely available sanitary products in educational institutions, and examine the association between lack of access and its effects on mental, physical, and social health.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 400 participants in a university and affiliated hospital in Ajman, UAE. A validated questionnaire was used, and data were analyzed using SPSS version 28. Both descriptive and inferential statistics, including Chi-square tests, were applied.Results84% of female participants agreed on the need for free sanitary products in educational institutions. Over 65% reported missing academic or social events due to menstruation-related inconveniences. Around 73% had to borrow pads/tampons from others, and nearly 43% found the topic of menstruation embarrassing to discuss. More than 46% agreed that access to free products would help reduce stigma and improve health and participation.ConclusionThe majority of participants supported the availability of free sanitary products in educational institutions. Improving access can enhance the academic, social, and emotional well-being of female students, contributing to gender equity and empowerment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23929-0.Keywords: Sanitary pads, Menstrual hygiene, Period poverty, Women empowerment, Educational institutions, UAE

Background

Period poverty is the lack of access to sanitary products, menstrual hygiene education, toilets, handwashing facilities, or waste management. Period products are now considered to be as necessary as toilet paper in several nations, states, and localities around the world [1]. Menstruating woman need to have access to sanitary products and other hygiene related necessities in order to keep their body healthy. Lack of access to these products often causes woman to use substitutes which are unsafe to use for long period of times such as tissue, cloth, cotton, and even if they have sanitary products available due to inadequate access woman use these products for a long time, often longer than they should be, not changing it at regular intervals make them prone to getting infections such as UTI and bacterial vaginosis [1]. Untreated Reproductive tract infections can progress to Pelvic inflammatory disease which can affect their fertility.

Menstrual health and hygiene significantly contribute to the wellbeing and empowerment of women across all age groups. At any given time, over 300 million women worldwide are menstruating. However, approximately 500 million women lack access to sanitary products and adequate menstrual hygiene management facilities. In many developing countries, schools do not provide sanitary products in restrooms, leaving female students to borrow pads when needed. Estimates suggest that underprivileged girls may miss up to two consecutive school days each month due to menstruation, leading to considerable educational disruption and negatively affecting their academic performance. This results in an educational disparity when compared to their peers [2].

In April 2017, UNICEF addressed the issue of menstrual hygiene in developing countries, citing that many Pakistani girls miss school and job opportunities due to poor menstrual hygiene and the unavailability of sanitary products. Due to societal stigma and misconceptions surrounding menstruation, these girls are often prevented from attending school during their periods and may even drop out at menarche [3]. A survey at Rawalpindi Medical University revealed that 19.3% of girls miss at least two days of school monthly due to menstruation, and 14.8% avoid classes for fear of staining their clothes and facing embarrassment [3]. However, a study in Ghana found that girls missed significantly fewer school days after receiving free sanitary pads and puberty education over six months [4].

Due to limited access, many women prolong the use of sanitary products beyond the recommended duration. This practice increases the risk of vulvar irritation, vaginal discomfort, infections, and even life-threatening toxic shock syndrome (TSS), a severe bacterial infection associated with prolonged tampon use [5].

Providing free menstrual products in school restrooms can improve attendance, participation in sports and academics, and overall comfort during menstruation. It also helps normalize menstruation and reduce stigma.

Globally, many countries have launched initiatives to address these challenges. In 2017, Scotland began supplying free period products in schools and, by 2020, extended this to the general population [6, 7]. The USA passed the “Menstrual Equity for All Act” in 2021, offering federal grants for free menstrual products in schools and universities. New Zealand mandated free pads and tampons in schools, improving attendance and reducing period poverty [1, 7, 8]. Kenya, the first to eliminate the tampon tax, started providing free sanitary pads in public schools in 20187.

In China, the “End Women’s Poverty” campaign prompted over 250 campuses to set up “sanitary pad support boxes” following advocacy by the group Stand by Her [9, 10]. The UK government provided free menstrual cups, pads, and tampons in schools, addressing stigma and helping students stay active and confident [4]. Additionally, ‘The Pink Protest’ movement focused on period poverty and school absenteeism [11, 12]. In 2018, Australia announced free menstrual products in public schools to promote inclusivity and reduce stigma [13].

In France’s Île-de-France region, free organic sanitary products were distributed in high schools beginning in September 2020. By February 2021, universities were instructed to install tampon and pad dispensers [14]. Canada and South Africa removed the tampon tax in 2015 and 2018, respectively, and began distributing free products in schools [14].

A study conducted in a medical university in UAE, about menstrual hygiene, concluded that good knowledge and practices of MH were expected in a medical university. The study revealed differences in menstrual history and attitude among older and younger women. The attitude and beliefs vary among the participants, due to sociocultural factors and age differences. Physicians and healthcare providers play a vital role in providing appropriate evidence-based knowledge about healthy practices and overcoming taboos. There have been studies about Menstrual Hygiene in UAE but this study also takes opinion about the importance of freely available sanitary products and how it affects menstrual hygiene [15].

This study was conducted to assess the perception of males and females on the importance of freely available sanitary products in educational institutes in Ajman, UAE. Secondly, to assess the association of lack of sanitary products on mental health, as well as to compare the level of literacy towards menstruation among women and men with different nationality and education.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a Cross-sectional study.

Study population

The study population includes students and staff of one university, and visitors and staff of the university hospital.

Inclusion criteria

All participants were included, irrespective of their gender and ethnicity who were above 18 years of age.

Exclusion criteria

Those below the age of 18 years and those who were not willing to participate were excluded from the study.

Study setting

The research took place in Gulf Medical University and Thumbay University Hospital, Ajman.

Sample size calculation

For the calculation of sample size, the perception of women toward providing free sanitary napkins was taken as 50% a relative precision of 10%. Thus the mini um required sample needed was 400. A non-response rate of 10% was expected, thus the final sample size was 400 + 40 = 440 participants.

The inclusion of BMI categories aimed to explore possible associations with menstrual hygiene perception, though this was not a primary objective.

Study duration

This study took place over a period of 8 months.

Study instrument and validation procedure

A questionnaire was developed based on the research and it was approved by three experts in the field of the research. After approval, a pilot study was conducted on 10 participants before finalizing the questionnaire.

A total of 440 participants were included in the study, regardless of their gender or ethnicity. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Gulf Medical University prior to data collection. A total of 440 questionnaires were distributed, but only 400 complete responses were included in the analysis. The remaining 40 were excluded due to incomplete responses. A convenience sampling technique was utilized for participant recruitment.

A structured questionnaire was developed, consisting of both binary and multiple-choice questions. The purpose of designing the questionnaire in this way was to enable people to express their opinions more openly which helped us analyze the data on basis of different categorical questions. The tool was validated, and a pilot study was conducted to ensure clarity and reliability. The pilot study was conducted among 10 participants which included students and some staff members, they were given the printed questionnaire and were asked to review it, and give a feedback based on which any final changes would be made before starting data collection. We did only the content validity, did not calculate the reliability coefficient. The validated questionnaire was then distributed among the participants, who were requested to complete and return it. Each submitted questionnaire was checked for completeness. A total of 440 questionnaires were distributed, but only 400 complete responses were included in the analysis. The remaining 40 were excluded due to incomplete responses.

Ethical considerations

The proposal was approved by the institutional review board (IRB), during the first part of the study, before data collection. Thereafter, consent was obtained from Gulf Medical University and Thumbay University Hospital to enable the process of collecting data. Following this, written and informed consent was taken from each participant, ensuring that anonymity, privacy and confidentiality was maintained. Herewith, participants’ views were collected and they were made aware of the evaluation and aim of the study. Only elements that were relevant to the research were focused on. Participation in the research was completely voluntary and anyone hesitant to participate was not compelled to take part, thus no harm was done to participants.

Data analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to present the data in terms of percentages. Inferential statistics, specifically the Chi-square test, were employed to examine associations between variables. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Missing data were excluded pairwise during analysis. The Chi-square test of independence was used to examine the association between age groups and various perception-related variables. This statistical test was appropriate for determining whether there were significant differences in the distribution of responses across different age categories.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The study included a total of 400 participants, of which 266 (66.5%) were female and 134 (33.5%) were male. The mean age of the participants was 22.8 years (SD = 7), with a minimum of 18 years and a maximum of 56 years. Participants’ nationalities were classified into six WHO regions: the majority were from the Eastern Mediterranean Region (224, 56%), followed by South-East Asia (130, 32.5%). Smaller proportions represented the African Region (10, 2.5%), Region of the Americas (11, 2.8%), European Region (9, 2.3%), and the Western Pacific Region (16, 4.0%). A significant majority (369, 92.9%) were pursuing or had completed a bachelor’s degree. The minimum age for participant was 18y there was no maximum age for participants. Most participants were single (339, 85%), while 60 (15%) were married. In terms of body mass index (BMI), 250 participants (63.5%) fell within the normal range (BMI ≤ 24.9) (Fig. 1). The inclusion of BMI categories aimed to explore possible associations with menstrual hygiene perception, though this was not a primary objective.

Frequency distribution of BMI

Regarding occupational categories, most participants (328, 89.9%) belonged to pink-collar professions, typically associated with care-oriented roles while only (19, 5.2%) and (18, 4.9%) were from white-collar and grey-collar professions, respectively.

Perceptions on menstrual hygiene accessibility

The majority of participants supported the idea of providing free sanitary products in educational institutions. Specifically, (201 participants, 50.4%) strongly agreed that such access could reduce feelings of shame associated with menstruation, while only (11,2.8%) disagreed. Additionally, (182, 45.6%) strongly agreed that free access could enhance students’ self-esteem, with (6, 1.5%) disagreeing. Around (164, 41.1%) strongly believed that it could help reduce the stress and workload from missing school during menstruation, and (170, 42.6%) agreed that it could improve student participation in sports and social events during their periods.

A total of 173 (43.4%) strongly agreed that access to free menstrual products would help prevent infections like urinary tract infections (UTIs), while 11 (2.8%) disagreed. Another 186 (46.6%) strongly felt that it could contribute to reducing stigma around menstruation, although 13 (3.3%) disagreed. Moreover, 206 (51.6%) strongly agreed that such access could improve menstrual hygiene and reduce the risk of bleeding or accidental leakage in public. Nearly half 197 (49.5%) participants strongly agreed that free products would also help minimize unnecessary visits to school clinics.

Female participants’ perceptions and experiences

Among the 266 female participants, 238 (90.8%) reported using sanitary pads, followed by 16 (6.1%) using tampons, and 6 (2.3%) using menstrual cups as their primary menstrual hygiene product. Notably, 98 (37.3%) had never accessed free sanitary products, while 39 (14.8%) reported often being able to access them. Approximately 83 (31.6%) had never approached anyone in their organization for sanitary products, whereas 48 (18.3%) had done so frequently.

About 170 participants (65.1%) had missed academic or social events due to menstruation-related inconveniences, while 76 (29.1%) had not. A large majority 220 (84%) believed that sanitary products should be freely available in educational institutions, though 172 (65.9%) stated that they currently lacked such access. Nevertheless, 164 (82.4%) believed that availability of free products would benefit them.

Over half 156 (59.8%) of the participants reported difficulty accessing sanitary products on campus, and 108 (41.2%) could obtain sanitary products from staff in emergencies, while 103 (39.3%) could not. More than half 144 (55%) said that obtaining menstrual products had been a challenge, although 83 (31.7%) did not face such difficulties. Peer support was also evident, as 206 (78.6%) had lent a menstrual product to someone, and 179 (68.3%) had borrowed from a friend, coworker, or even a stranger.

Almost half 127 (49%) admitted feeling embarrassed discussing menstrual products publicly, while 88 (33.6%) disagreed. More than half 151 (57.9%) agreed that stigma surrounding menstruation impedes access to products. Additionally, 144 (55.2%) reported feeling anxious about leaving home during menstruation. A total of 112 (42.9%) agreed that discussing menstruation and period poverty could be embarrassing, yet 111 (42.5%) strongly agreed that today’s generation is more open about women’s health issues.

Male participants’ perceptions and experiences

A survey conducted among 134 male respondents assessed their awareness and perceptions related to menstruation and menstrual health. The majority demonstrated a high level of familiarity with key reproductive health terms: 91.8% were familiar with “menstruation,” 84.3% with “menopause,” and 70.9% with “menarche.” Similarly, 84.3% recognized the term “sanitary products,” and 72.4% were aware of “period poverty.”

Awareness regarding the health risks of prolonged use of sanitary products, such as urinary tract infections, was reported by 73.9% of participants. Most respondents (85.8%) believed sanitary products should be freely provided in educational institutions.

Regarding the impact of such provisions, a majority agreed (56, 41.8%) or strongly agreed (50, 37.3%) that it would reduce shame, (50, 37.3%) strongly agreed that it would improve self-esteem, and reduce school absenteeism, (60, 44.8%) agreed that this initiative would improve participation in sports, (59, 44%) agreed that it would help eliminate infections, combat stigma (58, 43.3%), and address gender issues (51, 38.1%).

Additionally, respondents supported and strongly agreed that free provision would improve cleanliness (55, 41%) and reduce unnecessary visits to school nurses (60, 44.8%). These findings indicate a positive shift in male awareness and support for menstrual health initiatives, suggesting increased receptivity to policies promoting menstrual equity and hygiene in educational settings.

As shown in Table 1, a significant proportion of participants across all age groups reported missing university, work, or social gatherings due to menstrual discomfort and lack of product access. Specifically, 69 (71.1%) of teens, 73 (62.9%) of young adults, and 28 (58.3%) of adults responded affirmatively. Across all age groups, over 220 (80%) agreed that educational institutions should provide free sanitary products; 84 (86.6%) among teens, 94 (80.3%) among young adults, and 42 (87.5%) among adults. However, less than 10% (total 42 participants across all age groups) responded neutrally or disagreed.

Despite strong support for institutional provision, most participants reported that their university or workplace did not currently offer this service. When asked if they would use such a service if available, the majority responded positively, with the highest agreement seen among young adults. Difficulty accessing products on campus was a common issue across all groups, and more than 79% had lent sanitary products to others. Borrowing behaviour was most prevalent among teens and adults, compared to young adults.

When asked if they would approach staff for menstrual products, teens and young adults responded affirmatively more often than adults. Still, all age groups reported challenges accessing these items in their academic or work environments. Notably, adults were less likely to attribute difficulty in obtaining menstrual products to financial issues, in contrast to teens and young adults.

In terms of whether periods posed financial stress for them or their families, most across all age groups disagreed, although teens had the highest rate of agreement. Regarding stigma, teens 61 (62.9%) most strongly agreed that menstruation was a stigmatized topic, while adults 18 (37.5%) had the highest rate of disagreement. Teens were also more likely to feel embarrassed or hide menstrual products compared to older groups. A large proportion of participants, especially teens and adults, reported feeling anxious during menstruation when leaving home.

Finally, a significant number of participants, particularly from the teenage and adult groups, agreed that women and girls feel embarrassed discussing menstruation and period poverty. Additionally, 24 (50%) of adults strongly believed that the current generation is more open about women’s health issues ( Table 2).

Discussion

Perceptions regarding menstrual products accessibility

Sanitary products are a basic necessity for women and should be readily available in public spaces to prevent accidents such as blood leakage, which can be distressing and embarrassing. It is therefore crucial to raise awareness about the importance of menstrual hygiene products. In the present study, approximately 91.5% of participants indicated familiarity with sanitary products. This level of awareness is encouraging when compared to a study by Gayoung Moon et al., which reported that 68.4% of women and 51% of men had good knowledge about menstrual products. Interestingly, the study also found that male participants developed a more positive perception of menstruation after gaining a better understanding of its significance in women’s lives [16].

Stigma surrounding menstruation

A Journal article named as the persistent power of stigma: A critical review of policy initiatives to break the menstrual silence and advance menstrual literacy explored menstrual stigma by analysing policy documents and taking in-depth interviews with policy makers, the article described Menstrual stigma as negative perception of menstruation. Its mentioned that there are policies formed to help address menstrual stigma but they don’t reach far enough [17]. It’s quite clear from the people perceptions that indeed individuals with proper knowledge about menstruation and those who are educated are against the stigma associated with menstruation. In our study, many female participants agreed that there is stigma regarding menstruation and sanitary products and they do feel shy or embarrassed when they have to ask someone for a sanitary product in times of need, about 57.9% agreed that menstrual stigma is one of the reasons why sometimes they aren’t able to access sanitary products. Borrowing or lending of sanitary products is quite common among girls in workplaces and educational institutions, this study also shows that 73.5% of young adults in this studied have lent a menstrual product at least to someone else and 60.7% have at least once borrowed a sanitary product from someone else. The high incidences show that that unavailability of sanitary products causes people to ask others which can be embarrassing for the woman menstruating.

Global comparison

Similarly, a study by Gizachew Abdissa Buldo et al., conducted among school-going adolescents in Ethiopia, found that 72.5% of participants had good knowledge about menstruation [18]. However, in many low-income countries, girls often begin menstruating without any prior knowledge or preparation. These findings underscore the direct relationship between menstrual health awareness and the sociodemographic characteristics of a population. As highlighted in a study by Laura Rossouw et al., period poverty in low-income settings manifests in various ways, including limited access to menstrual products and inadequate sanitation facilities, which are both essential for maintaining menstrual hygiene. The study concluded that the ability to afford and maintain menstrual hygiene is closely tied to the economic status of a country [19].

Further supporting this, research from rural schools in Zambia reported that a significant number of girls faced challenges managing menstruation due to lack of access to absorbent materials [20]. In another study conducted in five Junior High Schools (JHS) in the Northern Region of Ghana, schoolteachers reported that their schools did not provide sanitary materials, even in emergencies. Among the participants, 75.7% cited the high cost of menstrual care products as the primary barrier, which left nearly half (46%) of adolescents unable to afford sanitary pads [21].

Absenteeism due to menstrual related challenges

Our study revealed that a considerable number of participants had missed university, work, or social events due to menstrual-related challenges. This included 71.1% of teens, 62.9% of young adults, and 58.3% of adults. Similarly, a study conducted in three West African countries by Julie Hennegan et al. found that 17% of students aged 15–24 missed school due to menstruation [22]. These findings suggest that menstrual-related absenteeism is a widespread issue, cutting across different socioeconomic and demographic settings.

The majority of participants in our study, across all age groups, agreed that educational institutions should provide free sanitary products. This was echoed by 86.6% of teens, 80.3% of young adults, and 87.5% of adults. These findings are comparable to a Zambian study on incentivized menstrual hygiene product access, which showed that 84.6% of women aged 16–19 years and 79% of women aged 20–24 years accepted and used the incentives provided [23]. This reinforces the understanding that irrespective of a country’s economic or demographic status, there is a clear need for sanitary products and a shared recognition of their value. Furthermore, once access is granted, the uptake and usage of these products are high, demonstrating both the necessity and the willingness of women to manage their menstrual health when given adequate support.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the persistent challenges women face in accessing menstrual hygiene products within educational and workplace settings. Many reported having to borrow or lend sanitary items to peers due to limited availability, and such difficulties have contributed to missed academic activities and social events, including classes, university functions, and workplace gatherings. Importantly, a significant proportion of participants—regardless of gender—expressed strong support for the provision of free sanitary products in schools and universities. This consensus reflects a growing recognition that menstrual products are a basic necessity, not a luxury, and that ensuring their availability is essential to promoting gender equity and dignity.

Providing free period products across educational institutions would enable all students, especially those who menstruate, to participate fully in academic and extracurricular activities without the added burden of managing menstrual needs in inadequate conditions. By addressing this fundamental health requirement, institutions can contribute to a more inclusive, supportive, and equitable environment for learning and professional development.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted at a single university and its affiliated teaching hospital in Ajman, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of the United Arab Emirates. The responses reflect the perceptions and experiences of university students, faculty, and hospital visitors, and may not capture the views of individuals from other demographic or educational backgrounds. Furthermore, since the study population was primarily composed of individuals with access to higher education, it excludes perspectives from less privileged populations who may face even greater challenges in menstrual hygiene management. Future research should aim to include diverse population groups across various regions and educational levels to provide a more comprehensive understanding of menstrual health needs in the UAE.
