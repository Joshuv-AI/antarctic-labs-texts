Hiding in Plain Sight
Authors: Gargi Banerjee, David John Werring
Published: 2025

Rare or unusual disease variants can provide important mechanistic insights for their more common sister conditions. In the case of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), the sporadic form is a common age-related small vessel disease characterized by the cerebrovascular deposition of the amyloid-beta protein. CAA traditionally has 3 additional rare variants which differ in etiology, pathophysiology, or clinicoradiological presentation; these are the inherited, iatrogenic, and inflammatory subtypes. In their report, Storti and colleagues1 provide detailed clinical descriptions of symptomatic CAA-related inflammation (CAA-ri) in patients with the rare iatrogenic form of CAA and in so doing provide further evidence that shifts our perspective on the role of inflammation in CAA.

CAA-ri has long been considered a discrete variant of CAA. It has a distinct clinical presentation, typically with a subacute encephalopathy (in contrast to the predominantly acute haemorrhagic presentations of sporadic CAA) and characteristic neuroimaging features, in particular transient (or waxing and waning), often asymmetrical, confluent white matter hyperintensities, seen best on brain MRI.2 In addition, unlike other forms of CAA, CAA-ri can be treated, with a good response to early immunosuppression (mainly with corticosteroids) in many cases. However, this classical view of CAA-ri is changing. We now recognize that CAA-ri can exist radiologically in patients with mild or no symptoms3 and that the classical white matter changes of CAA-ri may not be prominent or can be preceded by more subtle imaging features, such as sulcal hyperintensities, gyral swelling, and leptomeningeal enhancement.4 These observations suggest that CAA-ri might be more common than originally thought, with milder or atypical clinicoradiological phenotypes not immediately recognized by treating clinicians. These observations align with the experience of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), a form of CAA-ri observed in patients with Alzheimer disease treated with anti-amyloid immunotherapies because ARIA is frequently observed radiologically—with findings suggesting an inflammatory response—but is usually clinically asymptomatic.5,6

In their report, Storti and colleagues1 systematically and retrospectively reviewed their large multicenter cohort of CAA patients, identifying 38 with likely iatrogenic CAA. Iatrogenic CAA is believed to occur after the inadvertent seeding of amyloid-beta during surgical procedures that took place decades earlier, many of which involved the use of cadaveric dura; it is of mechanistic interest because it suggests amyloid-beta can behave as a prion. On careful review of these iatrogenic cases, Storti and colleagues1 identified 6 patients with clinical and radiologic features consistent with CAA-ri. Some of these patients appeared to respond to immunosuppression, but many were challenging to manage, sometimes with medication-resistant seizures; interestingly, similar features were also observed in the only other published description of symptomatic CAA-ri in iatrogenic CAA to date.7 A recent international case series of iatrogenic CAA found radiologic evidence of inflammation in 28% of patients, including sulcal hyperintensities, cortical oedema, and parenchymal hyperintensities, but in nearly all cases this was clinically asymptomatic,8 suggesting a higher incidence than previously recognized. But why should inflammation be more frequent in iatrogenic CAA than other etiological subtypes (sporadic, inherited)? For now, we can only speculate; perhaps this represents a specific inflammatory response to exogenous amyloid-beta or to the kinetics of amyloid-beta seeding from external “inoculation” and its resulting pathology.

The presence of inflammation in iatrogenic CAA makes us reconsider the prevailing hypothesis that CAA-ri is a discrete “subtype”. Instead, it now seems that CAA-ri is a phenomenon which can occur with any CAA etiology, be it sporadic, inherited, or iatrogenic, as well as in Alzheimer disease (ARIA is observed not only in those treated with anti-amyloid immunotherapies but also in placebo groups5,6). Within this framework (Figure) and regardless of aetiology, CAA-ri could range from mild—for example, in those with minimal symptoms or more subtle imaging changes—to severe, in which the well-recognized “classical” clinicoradiological presentation of CAA-ri is observed. This shift moves us from thinking of CAA-ri as a final diagnosis, to instead considering it a pathophysiologic process with potential relevance for disease trajectory and prognosis across all CAA subtypes. The association of contrast enhancement (parenchymal, leptomeningeal, vessel wall) with unremitting transient focal neurologic episodes in sporadic CAA9 raises the possibilities that inflammation might cause neurologic symptoms or even precede haemorrhagic events in CAA and that episodes of inflammation are the basis for temporal and spatial clustering of CAA-associated haemorrhagic events.10,11 Whilst the directions of causality between intracranial hemorrhage, various forms of inflammation and symptoms remain to be elucidated, the suggestion that treating coexisting inflammation in CAA could reduce symptoms, subsequent haemorrhagic events, or both, is an exciting 1 that could be tested in clinical trials.

Alternative Framework for Inflammation in CAA

The frequent presence of inflammation in iatrogenic CAA suggests that, rather than being a distinct subtype, inflammation is instead a pathophysiologic process that can be present with any CAA etiology. In this alternative framework, the first step is defining why someone has CAA—is it sporadic, inherited, or acquired (iatrogenic)? Then, is inflammation present or absent? If inflammation is present, the final step is defining severity, on the basis of clinical presentation and radiologic appearances, and when available, histopathologic findings. Additional patient observations are needed to test the implications of this hypothesis-generating framework (for example, regarding the presence, pattern, and extent of inflammation in inherited CAA), but for now, this provides a classification system which accounts for existing typical and atypical reports of CAA-related inflammation. CAA = cerebral amyloid angiopathy.

This report by Storti and colleagues1 once again demonstrates that there is an important place for well-considered detailed clinical and radiologic case descriptions in contemporary research and that careful study of rare disease phenotypes can provide new insights and mechanistic hypotheses for conditions that, despite many scientific and technological advances, sadly remain untreatable. Newer imaging techniques, such as contrast-enhanced vessel wall imaging, should help us find CAA-ri in iatrogenic CAA but also other CAA etiologies, and if we do indeed find that CAA-ri is “hiding in plain sight” across all CAA subtypes, these observations could usefully frame how we investigate, understand, and treat CAA in future.
