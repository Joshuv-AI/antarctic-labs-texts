Anxious Depressive Attack (ADA) -its prevalence and clinical implication
Authors: Hisanobu Kaiya, Shota Noda, Satomi Matsumoto, Yojiro Sakai, Takeshi Otowa, Tsukasa Sasaki, Fusaka Minami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxious-Depressive Attack (ADA) is one of the three types of anxiety attacks originally described by Freud. ADA is characterized by the sudden onset of intense anxiety, immediately followed by intrusive ruminations. These intrusive thoughts, often centered on past regrets or future worries, cause significant psychological distress. Some individuals respond to these episodes with maladaptive or impulsive coping behaviors. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of ADA among new patients at an anxiety disorder specialty clinic and to examine the associated psychiatric and psychological characteristics.MethodsNew patients (N = 286) presenting to an anxiety disorder clinic in Tokyo were assessed for the presence of ADA independently by a psychiatrist and Certified Public Psychologists (Japan), based on predefined diagnostic criteria. Axis I and Axis II diagnoses were established using the Japanese version of the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis II Personality Disorders (SCID-II), respectively. Histories of early separation or abuse were assessed using structured questionnaires. Psychological assessments of anxiety, depression, social anxiety, stress, and rejection sensitivity (RS) were conducted. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed to examine relationships among these variables.ResultsThe lifetime prevalence of ADA was 21.68%. ADA was observed across 16 Axis I disorders and 6 Axis II personality disorders. Compared with patients without ADA, those with ADA exhibited significantly higher scores on all psychological measures. SEM demonstrated that both RS and depressive symptoms independently contributed to the occurrence of ADA.ConclusionADA was found across a wide range of psychiatric disorders characterized by heightened RS. These findings suggest that ADA may be associated with increased clinical complexity. Active screening for ADA in clinical practice may facilitate early identification and more appropriate intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13030-026-00355-1.

Introduction

While conducting research on various mental disorders in an outpatient clinic specializing in anxiety disorders, one of the authors identified a phenomenon termed anxious depressive attack (ADA), which may be conceptualized as a cognitive variant of panic attack (PA). Similar to PA, ADA begins with a sudden onset of intense negative emotions, followed by a cascade of overwhelming emotions, such as anxiety, agitation or loneliness. Concurrently, patients experience intrusive thoughts related to past regrets or future concerns, which can manifest as flashbacks. In some cases, patients engage in maladaptive behaviors, such as self-harm or overdose, as a means of coping with the distress associated with ADA [1–3].

A review of contemporary psychiatric literature revealed a lack of research explicitly addressing ADA. However, a passage in Freud’s work, The Justification for Disentangling Specific Syndromes from Nervous Breakdowns, alludes to the possible existence of this phenomenon. Freud [4], who himself experienced PA, wrote: ‘This [anxiety neurosis: Authors’ note] is not the only way in which apprehensiveness—which is not usually present in consciousness but is ever lying in wait—can express itself. It can, on the contrary, erupt suddenly into consciousness without being called forth by any train of thought, and thus bring about an anxiety attack. An anxiety attack of this kind either consists of a feeling of anxiety alone without any associated idea, or associated with the nearest interpretation, such as sudden death, a stroke, or approaching insanity; or else the feeling of anxiety is combined with paresthesias (similar to the hysterical aura); or finally, together with the feeling of anxiety, there is an accompanying disturbance of one or more bodily functions, such as respiration, heart action, vasomotor innervation, or glandular activity.’

From a modern psychiatric perspective, Freud’s description can be interpreted as follows: an anxiety attack represents a state in which latent anxiety suddenly becomes conscious, and it can be classified into three types: ①pure anxiety without associated ideas [3], ②anxiety accompanied by negative cognitions, or ③anxiety with physical symptoms. The third type corresponds to what is currently understood as a PA. This study focuses on the second type, which the authors have termed ‘anxious depressive attack’ (ADA) and which has been studied previously [1–3, 5–8].

ADA appears to be relatively common in psychiatric practice and may contribute to the complexity of cases that are difficult to treat; therefore, careful clinical identification of ADA is warranted. For this reason, this study investigates the prevalence of ADA and its clinical relevance in patients attending for the first time an outpatient clinic specializing in anxiety disorders.

Materials and method

Participants

The subjects of this study were selected from among the 4767 first-time patients who visited the Akasaka Clinic of the Warakukai Med. Corp. between July 2017 and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were men and women aged 16 years or older and under 80 years old who understood the purpose of the study and consented to be included in the study. Exclusion criteria were those at risk of suicide (a score of 10 or more on the Psychiatric Brief Structured Interview Suicide Risk Score), those with severe physical complications, and those with cognitive impairment. Of the 343 patients who consented to participate in the research, 54 patients with insufficient data and 3 patients with inappropriate age were excluded, leaving the data of 286 patients available for analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow chart of participant selection

Psychological batteries

Autism Questionnaire Japanese version (AQ-JV) [12]. This is a self-administered scale that tests for autism spectrum disorder tendencies. A score of 33 or higher is considered indicative of an autism spectrum disorder.

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (AD/HD) Self-Rating Scale (ASRS-v1.1) [13]. This is a self-administered scale for the screening of AD/HD in adults. A tendency toward AD/HD is indicated by checking four or more boxes.

Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale Japanese Version (HAMA) [14]. This is a 14-item questionnaire that allows clinicians to rate the severity of mental and physical anxiety.

Hamilton Depression Rating Scale Japanese Version (HAMD) [15]. This is a 17-item questionnaire that allows clinicians to assess the severity of depression.

Japanese version of the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptoms-C (QIDS-C) [16]. This is a 16-item self-administered scale that assesses the severity of depression.

Anxiety Depression Scale (ADS) [17]. This is a self-administered scale that scores the severity of atypical depression and ADA.

Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale Japanese Version (LSAS) [18]. This is a self-administered scale for social anxiety that evaluates the degree of fear/anxiety and avoidance behavior in 24 situations.

Interpersonal Stress Event Scale (ISES) [19] This is a self-administered 40-item scale that measures the intensity of interpersonal stress, which is composed of three factors: interpersonal conflict, interpersonal inferiority, and interpersonal exhaustion.

Interpersonal Sensitivity Measure (IPSM) [20]. This is a self-assessment scale consisting of 27 items across 5 factors, which assess RS.

Statistical analyses

First, the diagnostic concordance rate for ADA was presented as Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Second, chi-square, Cohen’s, and t-tests were used to compare patients with and without ADA. Third, using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was performed to determine how anxiety symptoms, social anxiety, and depressive symptoms mediate the association between RS and ADA. The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, the United States) was used to conduct the chi-square and t-tests, and Structural equation modeling was performed using Mplus 8 [21].

Ethical considerations

The design of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Corporation of Warakukai (Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare Research Ethics Review Committee Number 23,000,093). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.

Results

ADA prevalence

Of the 286 patients included in the study, 62 (49 female) had experienced an ADA and 46 had an inconsistent diagnosis (Cohen’s kappa = 0.32). Inconsistent cases were excluded from the study. Of the 62 patients 46 (35 female) had experienced an ADA within the previous two weeks, and 16 (14 female) had experienced an ADA before that time. As a result, the current prevalence of ADA was 16.08%, and the lifetime prevalence of ADA was 21.68% (Fig. 1).

Patients with ADA were significantly younger than those without ADA (31.18 ± 10.46 vs. 38.83 ± 12.88, t = 4.66, p = 0.00001). The age of onset of ADA ranged from 6 to 44 years, with a mean of 21.1 ± 9.2 years and a median of 20 years. The mean age of onset was younger in women than in men (19.7±8.4 vs. 25.7 ± 10.7), but this difference was not statistically significant. The mean time from ADA onset to first consultation was 95.1 ± 89.8 months.

Psychiatric disorders in which ADA was recognized

In a structured interview using the MINI interview, ADA was recognized in 14 Axis I disorders and 6 Axis II disorders (Table 2). In addition to the above diagnoses, ASD and AD/HD were also recognized in patients with ADA in the conventional DSM-5 diagnosis (Table 3).

The average number of diagnoses per patient based on the MINI interview was 3.5 in patients with ADA and 1.5 in patients without ADA, indicating a significantly higher level of comorbidity in the ADA group (t = 5.88, p < 0.01, Cohen’s w = 1.27). In addition, comorbidity of any affective disorder (such as major depressive disorder (MDD), dysthymia, and bipolar I or II disorder) with any anxiety disorder was significantly more common in patients with ADA than in those without ADA (55.9% vs 13.7%, χ2 = 42.68, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s w = 0.43).

Prevalence of panic disorder (PD)

The lifetime and current prevalence of PD was 23/59 (39.98%) and 15/59 (25.42%), respectively, in the patient group with ADA and 68/175 (38.85%) and 37/175 (21.14%), respectively, in the patient group without ADA. There was no significant difference in lifetime prevalence (χ2 = 0.0003, p = 0.9863) or in current prevalence (χ2 = 0.4678, p = 0.494) between patients with and without ADA.

Life history in childhood and adolescence

Significantly more patients with ADA reported having experienced abuse than did those without ADA (37.3% vs. 19.8%, p = 0.01). There was no significant difference in the history of early separation between the ADA group and the non-ADA group.

Psychological tests

A tendency toward AD/HD was significantly more frequent in the patients with ADA than those without ADA (45.8% vs. 12.6%, p = 0.01) on the ASRS-v1.1 test. An ASD tendency was significantly more frequent in the patients with ADA than in those without ADA (13.6% vs. 4.6%, p = 0.02) on the AQ-JV test.

All other psychological tests for depression (HAMD, QIDS-C), social anxiety (LSAS), stress (ISES), and RS (IPSM) showed significantly higher scores in the ADA group than in the non-ADA group (Table 4). The current ADA group showed higher scores than the past ADA group on all items except the LSAS, ISES, and IPSM.

SEM

SEM using MLR was done for the analysis of the data of 33 patients with current ADA and 169 patients without a history of ADA, all with no missing values in the psychological test scores. The results showed a significant association from IPSM to HAMA and to LSAS and no significant association from IPSM to HAMD; ADA was independently, significantly affected by HAMD and IPSM (Fig. 2). Because model fit indices are not provided for SEMs with a categorical outcome variable when using MLR, the model was also estimated using the weighted least squares mean- and variance-adjusted estimator (WLSMV) to evaluate model fit as a sensitivity analysis. Discussion

Prevalence of ADA

In the present study, the current (last two weeks) prevalence of ADA was found to be 16.08% and the lifetime prevalence was estimated to be 21.68%. This prevalence rate is quite high, indicating that ADA is not a rare symptom complex. With regard to patient selection, aside from recruitment on designated research days, no major sources of selection bias were identified. Because all participants were first-time visitors, prior knowledge of the researchers is unlikely to have influenced their responses, minimizing potential bias. However, it is important to note that this high prevalence of ADA was observed in an anxiety disorder specialty clinic. The inter-rater reliability, as measured by the kappa coefficient, was 0.32, indicating a reasonable level of agreement. This result is based on independent assessments conducted by a physician and certified public psychologists.

ADA and PA have been observed to occur alternately, and it has been proposed that they share a common underlying pathophysiology [2]. The findings of the current study further strengthen this hypothesis. Specifically, the one-month prevalence of PD was 25.42% among patients with ADA and 21.14% among those without ADA, figures that closely approximate the lifetime prevalence rate of ADA itself (21.68%). This study demonstrated that the lifetime prevalence of ADA is remarkably similar to the lifetime prevalence of PA (22.7%) observed in a large-scale epidemiological survey conducted in the United States [22].

Psychiatric disorders associated with ADA

Building on the findings of the present study and prior investigations [5, 7], it appears that depressive states and RS constitute the two principal factors underlying the onset of ADA.

In this study, affective patients with ADA exhibited a markedly higher rate of comorbid anxiety disorders compared to those without ADA (55.9% vs. 13.7%). This observation is consistent with previous findings suggesting that when anxiety disorders co-occur with depression, RS approximately doubles [23]. Such a relationship underscores the central role of RS in the psychopathology of ADA.

The role of RS in the emergence of ADA is further substantiated by findings from broader psychopathology. Salient experiences of ostracism, known to precipitate heightened RS, have been observed across a range of disorders, including Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD), MDD, Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD), ASD, alcohol use disorder, eating disorders, and schizophrenia [24]. Of these, the first six demonstrated ADA comorbidity in the present study, while ADA in schizophrenia was reported in a prior investigation [7].

Interestingly, Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) cases with co-occurring separation anxiety disorder - a developmental antecedent of RS [7] - are known to show increased treatment resistance and higher rates of comorbid anxiety disorders [25]. The OCD patients with ADA observed in the present study may belong to this difficult-to-treat subgroup, highlighting the role of RS even within neurotic spectra.

SAD, characterized by a core fear of negative evaluation, is inherently associated with elevated RS [26]. Large-scale epidemiological research in the United States has shown tetrachoric correlation of SAD, as follows; Agoraphobia (0.68), dysthymia (0.55), MDD (0.52), AD/HD (0.51), specific phobia (0.50), PD (0.48), General Anxiety Disorder (0.47), Hypomanic Manic Disorder (0.46), Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder(PTSD) (0.43), and alcohol dependence (0.31) [27]. In the present study, ADA was consistently identified among patients with these comorbidities, further strengthening the link between RS, SAD, and ADA.

Beyond mood and anxiety disorders, several personality disorders characterized by RS also demonstrated associations with ADA. Avoidant Personality Disorder [28] and the vulnerable/hypervigilant subtype of Narcissistic Personality Disorder [29] are both typified by hypersensitivity to perceived rejection, making the occurrence of ADA in these patients understandable. Similarly, BPD, where “fear of abandonment” constitutes a diagnostic hallmark [10], reflects extreme RS at its core. In dependent Personality Disorder, pervasive fear of rejection and abandonment [30] echoes similar vulnerabilities. Additionally, Antisocial Personality Disorder [31] and Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder [32] have shown high rates of comorbidity with SAD, suggesting broader transdiagnostic pathways involving RS.

Developmental disorders also merit attention. In AD/HD, heightened RS is believed to render individuals more susceptible to stress and trauma [33]. Similarly, in ASD, cognitive rigidity and persistent interests may predispose individuals to experiences of RS [34]. These findings extend the relevance of ADA into the realm of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Taken together, the present findings suggest that ADA constitutes a transdiagnostic phenomenon observed across a wide range of psychiatric conditions, mediated fundamentally by RS. ADA appears to traverse internalizing disorders, externalizing disorders, and psychotic disorders alike, bridging Axis I and Axis II domains (Fig. 3). Comorbidity and RS

In the present study, patients with ADA had a significantly higher number of diagnoses as assessed by the MINI compared to those without ADA (mean: 3.5 vs. 1.5). Moreover, the proportion of affective disorders comorbid with anxiety disorders was significantly greater in the ADA group than in the non-ADA group (55.9% vs. 13.7%). In an epidemiological study of the general population, Kessler et al. [27] reported that among individuals who experienced a mental disorder within a 12-month period, 55% received a single diagnosis, 22% received two diagnoses, and 23% received three or more. The study further demonstrated that a greater number of comorbid diagnoses was associated with increased clinical severity.

Caspi and Moffitt [36] proposed that the emergence of comorbidity in psychiatric disorders can be attributed to several factors: neuroticism and poor impulse control from a psychopathological perspective; shared genetic liability and gray matter atrophy from a neuroscientific standpoint; and exposure to psychological trauma as an environmental contributor.

The high rate of comorbidity observed in psychiatric disorders with ADA is presumed to result from the central role of RS—a fundamental and evolutionarily primitive form of anxiety—in the psychopathology of these conditions [8]. This core vulnerability may contribute to the increased clinical severity commonly seen in patients with ADA.

In conclusion, ADA represents a distinct symptom complex that does not conform to existing diagnostic categories.

Reports suggesting the existence of ADA

In reviewing the Western literature for references suggesting the possible existence of ADA, only a single pertinent source was identified. Liebowitz and Klein [41] described hysteroid dysphoria as “repeated episodes of abruptly depressed mood in response to feelings of rejection, usually lasting only hours to days.” This description is consistent with the contemporary psychiatric concept of depression with atypical features, as defined in the DSM. Our previous study showed that the incidence of ADA was highest among patients with atypical depression, reaching 78.3% [2]. Given that RS is a core psychopathology in both atypical depression [42] and ADA [5, 7], it is reasonable to infer that the “episodes of abruptly depressed mood” described by Liebowitz and Klein are indicative of ADA.

In addition to these findings, Okada [43] reported 10 cases of severe anxiety attacks without accompanying physical symptoms in Japan. Among these, seven cases exhibited features suggestive of atypical depression, while five demonstrated behaviors indicative of uncontrollable impulsivity. Based on these clinical observations, it is plausible that the cases described by Okada included instances of ADA as conceptualized by the authors.

Why has ADA been overlooked?

ADA has historically been underrecognized. Several factors contribute to this oversight. First, while physical symptoms such as those seen in PA are readily identifiable, psychological symptoms are often subtler and more challenging to detect. Second, ADA represents a highly subjective and ego-syntonic experience occurring during episodes of intense affective and cognitive disturbance, making it difficult for patients to objectively recognize and report these symptoms. Third, the relatively long duration of ADA episodes （from a few minutes to all day long, averaging approximately two hours) [2] complicates their characterization as discrete attacks.

Despite its under-recognition, it is reasonable to assume that ADA is not uncommon in Western countries. Atypical depression, in which RS plays a central psychopathological role [44] and that has the highest reported prevalence of ADA [2], is not a rare condition in the West. However, because ADA symptoms often remain concealed unless actively explored, their identification typically depends on the clinician’s careful and targeted inquiry during the clinical interview.

Limitations

The lifetime prevalence of ADA observed in this study (21.68%) appears relatively high. However, this estimate was derived from a specialized anxiety disorder clinic, and caution must be exercised in generalizing it.

In addition, this study employed SEM with a total sample of 202 participants, including 33 individuals with ADA. Although RS demonstrated a positive association with ADA in the proposed model, consistent with prior findings [5, 7], the modest sample size—particularly the limited number of ADA cases—may restrict the generalizability of the results and increase the risk of model overfitting. Replication studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to confirm the robustness and stability of these findings.

Conclusion

The core psychopathology underlying ADA is considered to involve depression and RS. ADA typically emerges between early childhood and middle adulthood and is frequently observed in psychiatric disorders characterized by heightened RS. Its lifetime prevalence in specialized clinics for anxiety disorders is 21.68%, indicating a substantial occurrence.

ADA may represent a transdiagnostic factor associated with increased clinical complexity and treatment resistance in disorders such as depression, PTSD, and BPD. Although its clinical relevance has received limited attention in psychiatric practice, systematic identification of ADA may offer valuable insights for understanding and managing difficult-to-treat psychiatric conditions.
