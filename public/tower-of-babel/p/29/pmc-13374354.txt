Percutaneous laparoscopic harvest and trans-vaginal implantation of autologous rectus fascia in the treatment of severe anterior vaginal prolapse
Authors: Yiqi Guan, Kun Zhang, Jinsong Han, Yiting Wang, Ying Yao, Junfang Yang, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo observe the efficacy of percutaneous laparoscopic harvest and trans-vaginal implantation of autologous rectus fascia (ARF) in the treatment of severe anterior vaginal prolapse (SAP).Materials and methodsAll women who underwent percutaneous laparoscopic harvest and trans-vaginal Implantation of ARF to treat SAP between December 2022 to December 2023 at a single center. Major measurements include operation time, blood loss, postoperative hospital stay, Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory-20 (PFDI-20), patient satisfaction, Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I), Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q). All patients were followed up at 3 months, 1 year and 2 years after the surgery.Results7 patients underwent pelvic floor repairing surgery using the described technique with an average age of 63.6 years, BMI 24.0 kg/m2, and median parity 2. The average preoperative PFDI-20 score was 67.2. The average operation duration was 163.2 min, intraoperative blood loss was 41.4 mL, and postoperative hospital stay was 4.6 days. No severe complications occurred in the perioperative period. No patients had developed recurrence of prolapse by the average 26 months postoperative telephone follow-up. The average PFDI-20 score was 12.8 and 12.2 at 12 and 24 months of follow-up, respectively. Patient satisfaction remained high and the PGI-I outcome remained “significantly improved” in all patients.ConclusionHarvesting ARF by percutaneous laparoscopy is feasible, and trans-vaginal implantation of ARF to treat SAP has good effects, high safety, and high postoperative satisfaction. More data and longer-term follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Severe anterior vaginal prolapse (SAP; POP-Q stage III/IV of the anterior vaginal wall) represents the most common form of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) requiring surgical intervention [1]. Its management remains challenging. Native tissue repair (NTR) alone carries a high recurrence rate. Over the past two decades, synthetic mesh has gained popularity due to its high anatomical success and low recurrence rates. However, complications such as mesh erosion and exposure occur frequently, with postoperative pain reported in up to 30% of patients [2]. Autologous fascia offers an alternative that avoids mesh-related complications and has been utilized in surgical treatment for urinary incontinence(UI). Sources of autologous fascial material include fascia lata, rectus fascia, hamstring tendon, semitendinosus tendon, and similar tissues [3]. Nevertheless, additional trauma at the harvest site may compromise patient acceptance and satisfaction. This study describes a minimally invasive percutaneous laparoscopic technique for harvesting autologous rectus fascia (ARF) and its subsequent trans-vaginal implantation for SAP treatment, with analysis of procedural safety and efficacy.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included patients admitted to the gynecology department of our center between December 2022 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria comprised: third- or fourth-degree anterior vaginal prolapse per the POP Quantification (POP-Q) System, with or without concomitant uterine or posterior vaginal prolapse; symptomatic disease requiring surgical intervention; desire for vaginal preservation; and patient refusal of mesh implantation. Outpatients meeting these criteria received counseling regarding mesh-free pelvic floor reconstruction options, including the study procedure alongside alternatives such as uterosacral ligament suspension, sacrospinous ligament fixation, and autologous fascia lata pelvic floor reconstruction. Final surgical approach selection occurred through internal family discussion. Exclusion criteria encompassed: recurrent POP; surgical scarring of the lower abdominal wall or abdominal hernia; severe comorbidities precluding laparoscopic surgery; and refusal to participate in follow-up.

Preoperative preparation

All patients underwent gynecological examination by an experienced urogynecologist (K.Z.) with POP-Q staging. Preoperative trans-vaginal ultrasound and cervical cancer screening were obtained. Patients with stress urinary incontinence (SUI) completed voiding diaries and underwent SUI provocation testing, cough testing, and urodynamic evaluation. Comorbidities were optimized to exclude surgical contraindications. Patients and families received comprehensive information regarding advantages and disadvantages of available treatment modalities, supplemented with POP educational materials. Written informed consent was obtained prior to surgery. Patients were instructed to perform daily sitz baths in dilute potassium permanganate solution for 3 days preoperatively and twice-daily vaginal douching with 5% chlorhexidine acetate solution for 2 days preoperatively.

Surgical procedure (supplementary material-video)

All operations were conducted by a single surgeon (Kun Z, MD, Chief Physician) to ensure surgical consistency. After satisfactory establishment of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lithotomy position and a urinary catheter was inserted. Laparoscopic surgery was performed first using the following four port positions: the lower edge of the umbilicus (10-mm diameter), the McBurney point in the lower right abdomen (5-mm diameter), 2 cm medial and superior to the anterior superior iliac spine in the lower left abdomen (5-mm diameter), and 1 cm lateral and superior to the midpoint of the first and third puncture sites (5 mm diameter). The uterus was anteverted to increase tension on the uterosacral ligaments (UL), facilitating their identification. The peritoneum was incised along the lateral edge of UL and slightly freed to a length of 5 cm, keeping it away from the ureter. Total laparoscopic hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy was then performed (all patients in this study were postmenopausal women who requested hysterectomy). By retracting trocar puncture sleeves to the subcutaneous fat layer of abdominal wall, lowering the CO₂ insufflation pressure to 8–10 mmHg, a subcutaneous working space was created. A 10-mm, 0-degree laparoscope was then withdrawn from the umbilical port into this subcutaneous cavity to enable visualization, while the fascial strip was harvested endoscopically through the original left lower quadrant trocar channels. The fat was separated to expose the anterior sheath of the rectus abdominis, and a 9- × 3-cm fascial strip was excised transversely with an ultrasonic scalpel and removed through the trocar (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 2, the rectus fascia was sutured continuously in two layers with 2 − 0 barbed absorbable suture (SXPP1A403; Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) (Fig. 3).

Switching to trans-vaginal surgery, a water cushion was placed on the anterior vaginal wall mucosa, which was then longitudinally incised along the midline at the level of the mid-urethra. The anterior vaginal wall was freed laterally, separating the vesicovaginal space superiorly to the vaginal fornix and inferiorly to the level of the internal urethral orifice. A portion of the anterior vaginal wall tissue was trimmed on both sides. This autologous fascial strip was then trimmed into two parts that were 0.5 and 2.5 cm wide, respectively (Fig. 4). The bilateral genital fold creases were marked at the level of the external urethral orifice as puncture points, and an incision was made in the skin. Using the fingers as a guide within the vesicovaginal space, the puncture device was passed through the vesicovaginal space to the medial edge of the obturator skin. The ends of the thin strip were tied, passed through the puncture tunnel, and fixed. The wide strip was longitudinally sutured into the vesicovaginal space, and the free end was extended into the pelvic cavity through the vaginal stump. The incision in the anterior vaginal wall was closed with absorbable suture. The vaginal wound was irrigated with saline and checked for active bleeding.

Returning to laparoscopy, the vaginal stump was closed in a continuous pattern with absorbable suture, and UL was sutured in a continuous pattern with non-absorbable sutures. The UL sutures on both sides were used to fix the free end of the fascial strips and were knotted separately, completing the high uterosacral ligament suspension (HUS) procedure (Fig. 5). Laparoscopic inspection confirmed no active bleeding at the fascial harvest site. A negative-pressure drainage tube was brought out through the lower abdominal trocar site and left in place. A postoperative vaginal examination was performed, and if necessary, posterior vaginal wall NTR was carried out.

Postoperative management

The urinary catheter was removed at 48 h. Intravenous antibiotics were administered, converting to oral agents 24 h postoperatively if afebrile. A 1-kg sandbag was applied to the lower abdomen with abdominal binder placement for 24 h. The subcutaneous drain was removed 2–4 days postoperatively when output fell below 20 mL daily.

Perioperative parameters

The following parameters were recorded from electronic medical records: concomitant procedures, operative time, estimated blood loss, operative cost, postoperative hospital stay, and perioperative complications (Clavien-Dindo classification).

Follow-up parameters

Outpatient follow-up at 3 months and 1 year postoperatively included POP-Q restaging, Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory-20 (PFDI-20), patient satisfaction assessment, Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I), and complication documentation. Telephone follow-up at 2 years assessed prolapse recurrence symptoms, PFDI-20 scores, patient satisfaction, PGI-I outcomes, and complications. Primary endpoints comprised vaginal prolapse symptoms, overall patient satisfaction, and PGI-I. The PGI-I is a patient-reported single-item scale assessing perceived improvement relative to preoperative baseline, utilizing a 7-point scoring system: 1 = very much improved, 2 = much improved, 3 = minimally improved, 4 = no change, 5 = minimally worse, 6 = much worse, 7 = very much worse.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The seven patients had mean age 63.6 years (range 57–69), mean BMI 24.0 kg/m² (21.5–28.4), and median parity 2 (range 1–4). Mean preoperative PFDI-20 score was 67.2 (28.1–106.3). Preoperative diagnoses included third-degree anterior prolapse in three patients and fourth-degree in four. All patients had concurrent grade III–IV uterine and/or posterior prolapse. Perioperative data

One patient underwent concomitant posterior vaginal wall repair and perineoplasty. Mean operative time was 163.2 min (135–197), with mean estimated blood loss of 41.4 mL (20–50). Mean operative cost was $1,027.9 USD ($987.7–$1,139.5), and mean postoperative stay was 4.6 days (3–6). No perioperative complications exceeded Clavien-Dindo grade 2. Two patients developed lower abdominal subcutaneous emphysema with visual analogue scale pain scores of 2–3 on the operative day, resolving spontaneously within 24 h. One patient experienced urinary retention managed with re-catheterization and traditional Chinese acupuncture; successful catheter removal occurred on postoperative day 5. One patient (the second enrolled) developed a 4 × 2 cm abdominal wall hematoma at the harvest site on postoperative day 1 with localized pain. Following conservative management with compression and heat application, the hematoma stabilized and the patient was discharged 4 days postoperatively with pain relief(Table 2).

Patient information

Postoperative follow-up

At 3-month follow-up, all POP-Q measurements were normal (Table 1). Patient satisfaction was “very satisfied” in six patients and “fairly satisfied” in one; all PGI-I scores indicated “much improved.” Mean PFDI-20 score was 11.9 (8.4–16.6), representing 82.4% improvement from baseline. Two patients reported mild lower abdominal discomfort without significant pain. The patient with the abdominal wall hematoma denied site discomfort, with normal abdominal examination findings.

At 1-year follow-up, no patient reported recurrent prolapse symptoms; mean PFDI-20 score was 12.8 (8.4–20.8). One patient developed intermittent lower abdominal skin numbness at 3 months postoperatively, which gradually improved and resolved by 1 year. One patient developed new-onset mild SUI not impacting daily activities. Patient satisfaction and PGI-I scores remained stable in all patients.

At 2-year follow-up, no patient reported recurrent prolapse symptoms. Mean PFDI-20 score was 12.2 (8.4–20.8), including the patient with new-onset SUI, with no worsening of self-reported symptoms. Patient satisfaction and PGI-I outcomes remained unchanged.

All seven patients completed follow-up at 3 months, 1 year, and 2 years. At manuscript preparation in January 2026, telephone interviews regarding recurrent symptoms were conducted, with mean follow-up duration of 26 months. No patient reported vaginal prolapse symptoms.

Discussion

This article introduces a novel surgical technique for harvesting autologous rectus fascia (ARF) through percutaneous laparoscopy, with subsequent trans-vaginal implantation of the harvested fascial strips for severe anterior vaginal prolapse (SAP) treatment. Outcomes from seven patients followed for a mean of 26 months demonstrate both safety and efficacy.

Symptomatic SAP usually requires surgical treatment. Traditional native tissue repair or sacrospinous ligament fixation may increase the risk of recurrence of anterior vaginal wall prolapse [4]. Trans-vaginal mesh repair can achieve a high rate of subjective and objective success and a low recurrence rate; however, it is associated with complications such as mesh exposure, erosion, and pain, reducing patients’ quality of life [5]. Following U.S. Food and Drug Administration warnings regarding mesh complications in 2008 and 2011, and the 2019 cessation order, trans-vaginal mesh repair has been prohibited in numerous countries and regions [3]. Consequently, alternative implant-based surgical procedures for SAP management are urgently needed. Autologous fascia implantation represents an optimal technique that circumvents mesh-related complications while reducing economic burden. Success rates, durability, and recurrence outcomes appear comparable to trans-vaginal mesh repair and superior to allogeneic fascia implantation [6]. ARF and fascia lata constitute the most commonly utilized graft materials, though conventional harvest techniques have been considered excessively traumatic for routine clinical application [3].

Percutaneous endoscopy, widely employed in gastrostomy, biliary tract puncture, and lumbar discectomy decompression [7, 8], serves as the foundation for this novel technique. The procedure utilizes existing laparoscopic trocar channels for percutaneous endoscopic resection of the anterior rectus sheath without additional incisions. This approach proves less invasive than trans-abdominal horizontal incisions for ARF harvest or lateral thigh incisions for fascia lata procurement described in prior literature [3, 9]. Furthermore, it reduces surgical wound burden compared with semitendinosus tendon harvest using specialized extractors. The technique proves particularly suitable for patients with prior pelvic surgery, who often present technical challenges for vaginal approaches. Percutaneous laparoscopic ARF harvest was successfully completed in all seven patients, with intraoperative blood loss ranging from merely 20 to 50 mL—demonstrating both feasibility and safety. Postoperatively, two patients developed subcutaneous emphysema and one developed harvest-site hematoma; all resolved with conservative management. One patient experienced intermittent lower abdominal skin numbness, though this did not impact satisfaction scores or PGI-I outcomes. Subcutaneous emphysema and rectus sheath hematomas typically remain self-limiting and respond to conservative treatment [10], supporting the safety profile of this harvest method.

Theoretical concerns exist that anterior rectus sheath harvest as an autograft might compromise abdominal wall structural integrity and increase hernia risk. Nevertheless, such complications have rarely manifested clinically. Rajaratnam[11] and Iftekhar[12] reported laparoscopic rectus abdominis muscle flap harvest for pelvic floor defect reconstruction without donor-site hernia or severe complications to date. This favorable safety record may reflect compensatory mechanisms from remaining abdominal wall layers—the posterior rectus sheath and transversus abdominis muscle—or the limited harvest area, which minimally affects overall abdominal wall mechanical balance[13, 14]. Despite these reassuring observations, careful preoperative assessment of individual risk factors including obesity and elevated intra-abdominal pressure remains essential to minimize potential complications.

Unlike the techniques described in most previous literature reports, the pelvic floor reconstruction technique used in this study was HUS + trans-vaginal anterior wall implantation of ARF rather than sacral fixation [3, 6, 9]. This is mainly because we considered the patients to have SAP, and trans-vaginal implantation of ARF provides stronger support for the anterior compartment while HUS simultaneously increase level I support (Fig. 5). Although the surgery was performed through a combined approach, the average operation duration was 163.2 min, which is comparable to the recent literature on autologous sacral fixation (123–199 min) [3, 6]. In the postoperative follow-up, the PFDI-20 score improved by 82.4%, patient satisfaction was high, and no autologous fascia-related complications occurred.

However, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sample size was very small, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, the retrospective design introduces inherent biases, including potential selection bias. Third, the absence of a comparator group precludes any definitive conclusions regarding the relative efficacy or safety of this technique compared to standard approaches. Fourth, the follow-up period was relatively short (no longer than 3 years), and long-term outcomes, including durability of the repair and late complications, remain unknown. Fifth, the technical demands placed on the surgeon for laparoscopic fascial strip harvesting may limit the reproducibility and widespread applicability of this technique. Finally, patient selection criteria were not standardized, and the study population represents a highly selected group of recurrent cases, which may not be representative of the broader population of patients with POP.

Given these substantial limitations, our findings should be interpreted with considerable caution. Additional data from larger, prospective studies with appropriate comparator groups and longer-term follow-ups are essential before any definitive recommendations can be made regarding the routine clinical application of this technique.

Conclusion

Percutaneous laparoscopic harvesting of ARF is a feasible minimally invasive method. The harvested fascial strips, when implanted through the anterior vaginal wall, show good efficacy, high safety, high patient satisfaction, and low cost in the treatment of SAP. This is not a primary surgical procedure but an investigational mesh-free technique chosen after counseling and informed consent which used only in recurrent cases. Definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy and safety of this technique await validation through larger, well-designed randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-ups.
