Autonomy preferences and behaviors towards asthma medication in online asthma education: a secondary analysis of a cluster randomized controlled trial in adults with asthma
Authors: Stefanie Eck, Andrea Baumgärtel, Alexander Hapfelmeier, Klaus Linde, Linda Sanftenberg, Stefanie Stark, Antonius Schneider, for the Bavarian Practice-Based Research Network (BayFoNet)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveEducation programs are essential to involve individuals with asthma in disease management and improve outcomes. We aimed to investigate the effects of a pilot-tested online asthma education program (eAEP) on autonomy preferences and medication behavior in adults with asthma in general practices.MethodsWe performed a pre-planned secondary analysis of a cluster randomized trial comparing the eAEP with usual care (UC) in terms of Asthma Knowledge Test (AKT) scores. The Autonomy Preference Index (API) was used to assess patients’ preferences for information (information preference) and involvement in medical decisions (participation preference). Participants evaluated eight statements regarding medication behavior. Outcomes were analyzed at baseline and six months.ResultsA total of 108 asthma patients (mean age: 47 years, 59% female) were analyzed. Increases in AKT scores were substantially larger in intervention groups (7.9 (95% CI 4.9–10.8), p < 0.001). API subscale scores increased in intervention and control groups, but group-differences were not statistically significant (participation preference: 2.8 (95% CI −4.1–9.6), p = 0.421; information preference: 1.1 (95% CI −1.8–4.1), p = 0.471). There was a positive correlation between changes in AKT and participation preference (r = 0.27, p = 0.031), but not information preference scores (r = 0.08, p = 0.472). Regarding medication behavior, there were some improvements in individual items in intervention groups, but no consistent patterns indicating a clear influence of API scores.ConclusionsThe eAEP did not result in larger increases in autonomy preferences compared to UC, but indicated beneficial effects on some forms of medication behavior. Larger studies are required for further insights.KEYWORDS: Asthma, digital intervention, education, autonomy, decision-making, adherence, primary care

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease that typically requires long-term management to control symptoms and prevent exacerbations [1]. Therefore, active involvement of individuals with asthma in managing their condition is crucial, with patient education playing a key role in providing the necessary knowledge and skills. Optimal asthma self-management education encompasses core information on asthma and its management, symptom monitoring, the use of an action plan and regular medical reviews [2]. According to current evidence, self-management education has reduced hospitalizations, emergency treatments, work absences and improved quality of life [2,3].

Besides these benefits, self-management education can increase patient autonomy and improve adherence to asthma medication [4,5]. In a clinical context, autonomy refers to an individual’s preference for being informed and participating in medical decision-making [6]. Individuals with greater autonomy are more capable of engaging in their treatment plans and making medical decisions that align with their personal values [7]. Promoting patient autonomy directly fosters shared decision-making (SDM), where patients and healthcare providers collaborate on treatment choices [8]. Guidelines advocate for SDM as this approach may improve clinical outcomes, quality of life and medication adherence [1,9]. One key challenge in asthma management is that patients often have their own beliefs about how medications function and when they should be used. However, these beliefs may not always align with established medical knowledge [10]. Poor adherence to asthma medication is associated with substantial consequences for individuals with asthma including poor asthma control, hospitalizations, work absences and increased mortality [11,12].

Despite the proven benefits of asthma self-management education, only about a quarter of patients participate in structured face-to-face asthma education programs (fAEP) as part of usual care (UC) [4,5,13]. Low participation rates may often be related to organizational factors such as limited availability of appointments for fAEPs, as well as patient-related factors including a lack of time or motivation to engage in fAEPs [14]. Due to their convenient and flexible utilization, digital asthma self-management tools are increasingly being recognized to support individuals in managing their condition [15–17].

We previously conducted a cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) that investigated the effectiveness of a pilot-tested online asthma education program (electronic asthma education program, eAEP) [18,19] compared to UC in adults with asthma from general practitioner (GP) practice settings [20]. A unique feature of this intervention is the integration of structured patient education with GP supervision. As part of this approach, GPs conduct an oral test to review patients’ knowledge and skills acquired. Findings from the cluster RCT indicated, that the eAEP was effective in improving asthma knowledge and symptom control [20]. Based on this, the present secondary analysis investigated whether the eAEP led to greater improvements in patients’ autonomy preferences and medication behavior compared to UC. Although SDM is increasingly promoted in asthma care, the interrelations between autonomy preferences, asthma knowledge and medication adherence remain insufficiently explored. Another aim was therefore to examine the relationships between autonomy preferences, medication behavior, asthma knowledge, and symptom control over the longitudinal course.

Methods

Study design

This analysis was based on data from a cluster RCT [20]. Comprehensive details regarding the study methodologies can be found in the published protocol [21] and in the previous article [20]. GP practices, serving as clusters, were randomly allocated to either the intervention group providing access to the eAEP or UC control (allocation 1:1). Between-group analyses as well as longitudinal analyses of the total study population were conducted. The study took place between July 2022 and March 2024. The study had been approved by the Ethical Committee of the Technical University of Munich (reference number 171/22 S-KH, approval date: 24 March 2022), and all patients provided written informed consent. The original study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00028805).

Participants

The GP practices had to be located in Bavaria, Germany, in order to participate in the study. GP practices were primarily recruited via email through the institute’s teaching practices network and the Bavarian practice-based research network (BayFoNet) [22], with additional support from two university departments of BayFoNet: the Institute of General Practice and Family Medicine of the Ludwig-Maximilians-University in Munich (LMU Munich) and the Institute of General Practice of the Friedrich-Alexander-University Erlangen-Nuremberg (FAU).

Asthma patients were consecutively enrolled by the practice personnel if they met the inclusion criteria of age ≥18 years, the capacity to understand German and no previous participation in an asthma education program or participation dating back more than ten years. Inclusion was based on a confirmed diagnosis of asthma. In routine practice, the diagnosis is usually established by a pulmonologist who performs spirometry (FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in 1 s)/FVC (forced vital capacity) ratio < 0.7) and, in some cases, bronchial provocation [1].

Procedures

Following the provision of written informed consent, patients from both groups were invited to attend a baseline assessment at the respective practice. This included a physician documentation on patients’ asthma and medication, current lung function parameters, and a patient questionnaire. The patient questionnaire included the Asthma Knowledge Test (AKT) ranging from 0 to 54 [23], Asthma Control Test (ACT) ranging from 5 to 25 [24], Autonomy Preference Index (API, comprising of two scales: preferences regarding information about the disease (information preference) and involvement in medical decision (participation preference), both ranging from 0 to 100) [25] and questions on patients’ behavior towards asthma medication (eight items) [21]. The items on behavior towards asthma medication addressed patients’ medication behavior based on the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) Guideline [26]. The items were developed by a pneumologist and a GP (AS) who is a member of the national asthma guideline board [27]. In addition, these were discussed with four patients from a pulmonology practice in order to improve comprehensibility.

Patients in the intervention group were given free access to the eAEP along with instructions to complete the program independently within two weeks. The eAEP can be accessed via a webpage (www.asthmaselbstmanagement.de) by entering a password. The online program comprises four main topics: basic knowledge on asthma, asthma medication, asthma self-management and acute exacerbation of asthma. Each chapter concludes with a short quiz, and a final quiz is provided at the end of the program. Completing the online program conscientiously takes approximately two to three hours. The eAEP was developed based on the structured and evaluated fAEP of the Clinic Bad Reichenhall, Germany. Accordingly, a pulmonologist with extensive experience in patient education and a GP (AS) carefully revised the chapters of the fAEP as part of the development process of the online program. Prior to the start of the study, the content of the eAEP was updated to reflect current guidelines [26]. See [18,19,21] for details on the eAEP. Patients in the control group were provided with UC as recommended by current guidelines. This included a referral to a structured fAEP to be attended within twelve weeks. At baseline, patients from both groups were supplied with an asthma diary and a peak expiratory flow (PEF) meter with instructions on using these tools provided during the eAEP and fAEP, respectively.

In order to review asthma knowledge and guarantee patient safety, patients from both groups were invited to attend a follow-up consultation in their GP practice following the completion of the eAEP (three weeks after study enrolment) and fAEP (twelve weeks after study enrolment), respectively. At this appointment, the respective physician conducted an oral test with the patient to discuss the inhalation technique, PEF measurement, and the establishment of an asthma action plan as well as evaluate asthma knowledge and management (see Additional file 3 of the protocol [21]).

A follow-up questionnaire was sent by mail to all patients at two weeks, three and six months.

Outcomes

Outcomes of main interest for this secondary analysis included:

Differences in the total sum score obtained in the API subscales [25] between intervention and control groups at study end (six-month follow-up);

Differences in the total sum score obtained in the API subscales [25] between intervention and control groups at study end (six-month follow-up);

correlations between changes in API subscale scores and changes in AKT [23] as well as ACT [24] scores after six months;

correlations between changes in API subscale scores and changes in AKT [23] as well as ACT [24] scores after six months;

association between group assignment (intervention vs. control) and the odds of agreement on individual items on behavior towards asthma medication [21] at study end; and

association between group assignment (intervention vs. control) and the odds of agreement on individual items on behavior towards asthma medication [21] at study end; and

relationships between medication behavior (reflected by the items on behavior towards asthma medication) and API subscale scores as well as AKT scores at baseline and study end, respectively.

relationships between medication behavior (reflected by the items on behavior towards asthma medication) and API subscale scores as well as AKT scores at baseline and study end, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Missing values in the data were multiply imputed via fully conditional specification using multiple imputation by chained equations. Consequently, all included patients were analyzed according to the intention-to-treat-principle (ITT). Following Rubin’s rule [28], the results from five imputed datasets were aggregated. For consistency in the analyses, imputed baseline data were used in this article. For not imputed baseline data see the main article [20].

API scales were linearly adjusted to range from 0 to 100, with 0 indicating a complete absence of desire and 100 representing the strongest possible desire. Individual items on behavior towards asthma medication were dichotomized for analyses (0: disagreement; 1: agreement). A planned sum score was not calculated, as the items could be interpreted differently depending on the individual disease course (e.g. seasonal asthma) [20,21]. Differences amongst intervention and control groups at baseline were tested using the t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Chi-squared test.

Linear mixed-effects models were applied to investigate baseline-adjusted differences in the API sum scores between intervention and control groups at follow-up. The model included cluster effects of the GP practices as a random intercept and fixed effects of age, sex, smoking status and education status. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) using an exchangeable working correlation matrix and GP practices as clusters were used to model each item on behavior towards asthma medication as outcomes. The GEE models’ complexity was restricted to include a maximum of three covariates, i.e. group allocation, age and the respective baseline value, to enable consistent effect estimation given the number of observed events [29]. The McNemar’s test was applied to compare categorical variables between baseline and follow-up.

Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, partial Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients, adjusted for the respective baseline value, were computed to examine the relationships between changes in API subscale sum scores and changes in AKT as well as ACT scores after six months. Univariable GEE were estimated to examine associations between individual items on behavior towards asthma medication and API subscales as well as AKT scores. Statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

In total, 108 patients participated in the baseline assessment (intervention group: n = 62; control group: n = 46). Follow-up data was available for 91 patients (intervention: n = 50; control: n = 41) at two weeks, 83 patients (intervention: n = 44; control: n = 39) at three months and 78 patients (intervention: n = 44; control: n = 34) at six months. Following the ITT principle, the final analyses included 108 individuals with asthma from 21 GP practices (intervention: n = 10; control: n = 11). In total, 98% of patients in the intervention group (49/50) stated completing the eAEP, whereas participation in the fAEP as part of UC was reported by 10% (4/39) of patients in the control group. The follow-up consultation at the respective GP practice after the eAEP/UC was attended by 84% of patients in the intervention group (n = 52) and 65% of patients in the control group (n = 30).

The mean age of patients was 47.0 ± 14.9 years (± SD), with patients from GP practices in the intervention group being younger than those from GP practices in the control group (43.9 ± 15.3 and 50.1 ± 13.8 years, respectively, Table 1). Participation preference scores correlated negatively with age (r = −0.24, p = 0.011) and positively with level of education (r = 0.34, p < 0.001). Information preference scores were not significantly associated with age (r = 0.16, p = 0.093) and level of education (r = 0.04, p = 0.707). Female participants (59%) demonstrated a higher participation preference score than male participants (41.0 ± 18.2 vs. 33.6 ± 17.7, p = 0.035). Information preference scores did not differ significantly between female and male participants (91.7 ± 9.9 vs. 89.4 ± 10.4, p = 0.252) (data not in table).

Autonomy Preference Index (API) scores and their relation to Asthma Knowledge Test (AKT) and Asthma Control Test (ACT) scores

Mean API subscale scores significantly increased in both intervention and control groups over the study course (Table 2). At study end, the adjusted mean change in the participation preference score from baseline was 7.9 in intervention (95% CI 3.5–12.3, p < 0.001) and 5.5 in control groups (95% CI 1.0–10.0, p = 0.017). Regarding the information preference score, the adjusted mean change from baseline was 3.3 in intervention (95% CI 1.4–5.1, p < 0.001) and 2.8 in control groups (95% CI 0.5–5.1, p = 0.020). No significant between-group differences were observed in both API subscale scores at six months.

The intervention group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in AKT (p < 0.001) and ACT scores (p = 0.023) when compared to the control group at study end (Table 2).

Over six months, greater increases in participation preference scores were significantly associated with greater improvements in AKT scores (r = 0.27, p = 0.031) (Figure 1). This association was slightly more pronounced in intervention than control groups (r = 0.31, p = 0.055 vs. r = 0.14, p = 0.413). Regarding changes in information preference scores, no significant associations were found with changes in AKT scores over the study course. Furthermore, changes in API subscale scores were not associated with changes in ACT scores (participation preference: r = 0.02, p = 0.880; information preference: r = −0.01, p = 0.942).

Patients’ behavior towards asthma medication and their relation to Autonomy Preference Index (API) and Asthma Knowledge Test (AKT) scores

The analysis of items on behavior towards asthma medication showed only few effects within and between groups after six months (Table 3). Agreement with the statement ‘I use a combination inhaler every day’ increased on average from 26% at baseline to 67% at six months in the intervention group, while there was almost no change in the control group (between-group difference at six months: Odds ratio = 4.0 (95% CI 1.2-12.5), p = 0.022). Average agreement with the statement ‘I take as little asthma medication as possible, even if I have asthma symptoms’ decreased from 31% at baseline to 15% at six months in the intervention group and from 33% to 28% in the control group. The odds of agreement with this statement was 70% lower in intervention than control groups (p = 0.060) at study end.

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a cluster RCT, Asthma Knowledge Test (AKT) scores and Autonomy Preference Index (API) scores increased over six months in adults with asthma from both intervention (access to the eAEP) and control groups (UC). However, differences between groups in terms of API scores were not significant. Greater improvements in AKT scores were associated with greater increases in participation preference scores. Some behaviors towards asthma medication appeared to improve slightly in intervention groups.

While participants of this study generally did not demonstrate a strong desire to participate in decision-making, they showed high preferences for knowledge of their condition. This is in line with previous studies on individuals with asthma and highlights the need for structured disease-related information as provided in education programs [6,10]. Consistent with previous research, the present analysis identified younger age, female gender, and higher educational level as factors associated with higher participation preference scores in asthma patients [10,30].

The longitudinal analysis showed that patients either with access to the eAEP or provided with UC significantly increased API scores over the study course, but changes were not substantially different between groups. Notably, not only patients in intervention, but also control groups, substantially increased AKT scores, possibly due to acquiring knowledge from alternative sources, such as the internet [20]. The observed effectiveness of the eAEP in increasing AKT [20] but not API scores might be due to the fact that the intervention was primarily designed to enhance asthma knowledge and self-management skills rather than to directly increase autonomy preferences. Beyond that, given the low attendance rates of control group patients in fAEPs (10%) as part of UC, it is likely that participation in the study itself has affected the observed changes in API scores. Although knowledge is considered a key prerequisite for SDM, it alone may not be sufficient to promote patients’ preferences for participation in decision-making [31]. Evidence suggests a multifaceted approach including strategies aimed at patients (e.g. education), physicians and staff (e.g. training in communication skills) and systems (e.g. design of technical and organizational systems) in order to support involvement in treatment [32]. The observed association between increases in AKT and participation preference scores may indicate that patients who gain more knowledge about asthma also become more willing to participate in treatment decisions. This association was slightly more pronounced in intervention groups, which might be explained by the greater improvements in AKT scores compared to control groups. Besides increased asthma knowledge, greater preferences for involvement in decision-making could contribute to improved asthma self-management as indicated by the positive correlations between both participation preference as well as AKT scores and the item ‘I manage my asthma symptoms well in everyday life’. However, there were no clear hints suggesting a modifying effect of the eAEP on these associations.

Nevertheless, improvements were observed in the responses of patients in intervention groups with regard to the statements ‘I use a combination inhaler every day’ and ‘I take as little asthma medication as possible, even if I have asthma symptoms’. While AKT scores were negatively associated with the latter item at study end, our analysis did not demonstrate an effect of participation preference scores on the aforementioned items. Although there were small to moderate effects of API subscale scores on some items on behavior towards asthma medication in individual groups, no consistent pattern was observed overall. Presumably, increases in autonomy preferences are concomitant effects that cannot be specifically attributed to the intervention. Nevertheless, evidence suggests the potential of digital tools in supporting adherence. A recent Cochrane review found that digital interventions improved adherence to controller medications in both adults and children [16]. In particular, electronic monitoring devices and short messaging services showed the most promising potential for enhancing adherence, possibly due to features such as real-time reminders [16].

From a practical perspective, it seems obvious to strengthen patients’ preferences regarding participation in decision-making and information to enable SDM. This approach has been shown to improve medication adherence and clinical outcomes in adults with asthma [9]. For this purpose, it may be beneficial to incorporate information on SDM into the eAEP and develop appropriate strategies targeted at GPs [30]. In principle, the trends found in this secondary analysis require further investigation using studies specifically designed and powered for these aims.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of the present secondary analysis is its longitudinal design, which allowed for the investigation of associations between autonomy preferences, medication behavior, asthma knowledge and symptom control over the study course.

However, a main limitation is the explorative nature of this analysis, including multiple testing, and the fact that this study was not specifically designed or powered for the present aims. Therefore, further studies with adequate sample sizes are needed to systematically investigate and validate the present findings. Furthermore, patients’ medication behaviors were derived from non-validated self-reports. This might have compromised the reliability and validity of the results due to factors such as misunderstanding individual items or social desirability. Nevertheless, individual items were discussed with patients from a pulmonology practice and a pulmonologist prior to the study conduct in order to enhance comprehensibility. To mitigate the influence of social desirability, patients were informed about the anonymous handling of their data also with respect to the GP. As detailed in the main article [20], a selection bias might have been present in terms of recruitment rates, language proficiency and age of participants. Adjustments for age and other covariates were made in the analyses. Furthermore, a self-selection bias could have been introduced, as more motivated patients with a higher interest in disease management may have participated in the study, as indicated by the high information preference scores. However, previous research on asthma patients has reported similar findings [6,10].

Conclusions

Our results indicated that an online asthma education program in combination with GP supervision and training in guided self-management did not result in greater increases in autonomy preferences compared to usual care. Findings suggest that patients with greater improvements in asthma knowledge might become more inclined to engage in treatment decisions. The online program hinted at beneficial effects on some forms of medication behavior at study end. Further, there may be small to moderate effects of autonomy preferences on medication behavior. However, larger studies specifically designed to address these research questions are required to provide further insights.
