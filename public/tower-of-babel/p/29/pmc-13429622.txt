Photothrombotic and middle cerebral artery occlusion models produce distinct neurovascular injury and sex-dependent outcomes
Authors: Kate Karelina, Tara K. S. Craft, Deborah Corbin, Terri J. Poling, A. Courtney DeVries, James C. Walton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke remains among the top five causes of death and disability in the US. Sex differences in infarct evolution, inflammatory cascades, and functional recovery exist but the mechanisms underlying these differences are not yet well characterized. Preclinical research is needed to bridge these gaps in knowledge and toward this end we set out to clarify some of these differences by providing a systematic comparison of photothrombotic and transient middle cerebral artery occlusion stroke models in both sexes of C57/BL6 mice. We assessed infarct characteristics, vascular protein accumulation, and functional outcomes to generate practical guidance for selecting the most appropriate model for mechanistic or translational studies. Both models yield cortical inflammation and injury, but they interact with sex and diverge in vascular pathology and functional outcomes. MCAO may be the preferred approach where functional outcome is a critical endpoint by generating larger, variable cortical and striatal infarcts with a salvageable penumbra resulting in easy to detect behavioral deficits, particularly in males. PTS produces smaller but denser cortical lesions with sharp borders and marked accumulation of vascular proteins, particularly in female mice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54619-5.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide1,2, yet translation of experimental therapies into clinical success has been remarkably limited3. A key challenge lies in the fact that no single preclinical animal or surgical model fully replicates the complexity of human stroke, making it essential to understand how different models interact with variables such as strain, sex, and age to influence outcomes and therapeutic responses. Among the most widely used experimental stroke models are middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) and photothrombotic stroke (PTS)4. These approaches differ substantially in the brain territory affected, the presence of a salvageable penumbra, and the type of edema and inflammatory response they evoke5–8. Understanding how these distinctions may produce different outcomes in both sexes is essential for selecting the appropriate surgical model for mechanistic studies and for therapeutic screening.

Prior comparative studies have elucidated key differences among preclinical stroke models in terms of lesion characteristics, inflammatory profiles, and pathology9–11. These findings have been instrumental in guiding surgical model selection for specific research questions. Importantly, variables such as rodent strain and age have also been shown to significantly influence stroke outcomes12–14. While these factors are routinely considered, sex as a biological variable has received comparatively less attention in stroke surgical model comparisons. This gap is significant because sex is a major biological variable in stroke. Women and men differ in stroke incidence, symptom presentation, severity, and recovery trajectories15–17. Although preclinical work has revealed sex differences in post-stroke inflammatory responses, infarct evolution, functional recovery, and response to therapy, these outcomes have not been fully characterized and the underlying mechanisms are not well understood18–20. Incorporating biological sex into preclinical design is now recognized as essential for rigor and reproducibility, yet there remains a paucity of preclinical stroke model comparisons across sexes.

Here, we directly compared MCAO and PTS in male and female C57BL/6 mice, a commonly used strain in stroke research, to evaluate how sex influences stroke outcomes between these two widely used surgical models. We assessed infarct characteristics, vascular protein accumulation, and functional outcomes across time. Our primary hypothesis was that stroke outcomes would be shaped by a model by sex interaction, producing distinct patterns of neurodegeneration, vascular pathology, and functional impairment across the two models. In parallel, our secondary aim was to determine how these model- and sex-specific differences can guide experimental model selection for mechanistic and preclinical studies.

Methods and materials

Animals

All procedures were conducted on male (28–33 g) and female (20–24 g) 12-week-old C57BL/6J mice obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were same-sex group-housed in standard mouse cages under a 12:12 h light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and filtered tap water. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at West Virginia University. Animal care and use complied with the ethical standards of the NIH and ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. After acclimation to the vivarium, mice were randomly assigned to experimental conditions, and throughout investigators were blinded to group assignments during data collection.

All procedures were performed during the light phase of the daily light:dark cycle. Mice underwent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), photothrombotic stroke (PTS), or the corresponding sham procedure. Neurological deficits were assessed using the Clark general and focal neuroscore at 1 and 7 days post injury. On day 7, mice were also assessed for locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior in an automated open field test, and for cognitive deficits in a spontaneous alternation task in a Y-maze. Infarct volume was then imaged in vivo via MRI and tissue was collected for histological analysis within 24 h of imaging. The study was comprised of the following experimental groups: male MCAO (n = 9); male (MCAO) sham (n = 7); female MCAO (n = 9); female (MCAO) sham (n = 8); male PTS (n = 11); male (PTS) sham (n = 7); female PTS (n = 9); female (PTS) sham (n = 7). A total 7 MCAO and 6 PTS mice were excluded from the study due to mortality, removal based on predefined humane endpoints, absence of infarct, or being identified as a statistical outlier.

Transient middle cerebral artery occlusion

Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (3.5% induction, 1.5% maintenance) and local anesthetic was administered at the neck incision site (1.5 mg/kg bupivacaine and 0.5 mg/kg lidocaine, subcutaneously). A middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) was induced by inserting a 0.21 mm occluder (Doccol, 602156) into the middle cerebral artery via the internal carotid artery, as previously reported21,22. Reperfusion was initiated by removal of the occluder following a 45-minute occlusion period. Sham-operated mice underwent the same procedure up to the neck incision, without occlusion of the MCA.

Photothrombotic stroke

Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (3.5% induction, 1.5% maintenance) and local anesthetic was administered at the scalp incision site (1.5 mg/kg bupivacaine and 0.5 mg/kg lidocaine, subcutaneously). Rose Bengal (100 µL of 50 mg/kg in sterile saline) was injected retroorbitally, and mice were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. Following a 10-minute circulation period, a cold light (Schott KL 2500 LED) was positioned over the skull through an incision in the scalp at coordinates − 1.5 mm ML and − 0.5 mm AP relative to bregma. The skull orientation was leveled in both the anterior-posterior and medial-lateral planes, and the light guide was held in a micromanipulator to prevent drift. Illumination spot size, intensity, and exposure duration (10 min) were identical across animals, ensuring consistent targeting of the sensorimotor cortex. Sham animals received a retroorbital injection of 100 µL of sterile saline (vehicle for Rose Bengal) and underwent the same procedure.

Clark score

General and focal neurological deficits were assessed by an observer blinded to experimental groups via the Clark Score as reported in23 at 1 and 7 days following stroke or sham surgery.

Open field locomotion

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were assessed 7 days after stroke or sham surgery by an observer blinded to the experimental groups. Mice were placed in a 40.5 × 40.5 × 36 cm open field arena (PAS Open Field, San Diego Instruments). Movement was recorded over 15 min using a 16 × 16 × 2 infrared beam grid. Beam breaks were analyzed to quantify activity in the center, periphery, and during rearing. Chambers were cleaned with Peroxigard between animals.

Y maze

Cognitive ability, specifically spontaneous alternation during exploratory behavior, was assessed 7 days post-surgery by an observer blinded to the experimental groups, following previously described methods24. Mice were placed in one arm of a Y-shaped maze and tracked for 5 min using ANY-maze software (v7.4, Stoelting). Percent alternation was calculated as: the number of triads containing successive entries into all three arms/maximum possible alternations (total number of arms entered-2) X 100. To remove conspecific olfactory cues, the maze was cleaned between animals with Peroxigard.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Infarct volume was measured 8 days post-surgery. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5–2% isoflurane and placed in a 23 mm head coil within an Aspect M7 MRI scanner (Aspect Imaging). Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heated water circulator, and respiration was monitored via a respiratory cushion. T2-weighted images were acquired using the following parameters: field strength = 1.05 T, TR = 3 s, TE = 66 ms, FOV = 30 × 30 mm, matrix size = 192 × 192, slice thickness = 1 mm, no interslice gap, total scan time = 9 min 15 s. DICOM files were analyzed using VivoQuant™ software. Infarct regions were manually delineated based on T2 hyperintensity, and infarct volume was quantified by an observer blinded to the experimental groups.

Histology

Nine days post-surgery, mice were euthanized via sodium pentobarbital overdose and perfused transcardially with 0.1 M PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS. Brains were post-fixed in paraformaldehyde overnight, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PB, frozen, and sectioned in serial sections on a cryostat at 40 μm through the forebrain.

Degenerating neurons were visualized using FluoroJade B staining. Sections were mounted on slides, immersed in 70% ethanol with 1% NaOH, treated with 0.06% potassium permanganate, and incubated in 0.0001% FluoroJade B in 1% acetic acid. Slides were air dried, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with DPX.

For immunofluorescence, free-floating sections were washed in 0.1 M PBS with 0.01% Triton X-100, blocked with 1% normal horse serum, and incubated overnight with rabbit anti-fibrinogen antibody (1:100; Dako). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were rinsed 3x in 0.1 M PBS and then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies in 0.1 M PBS: donkey anti-rabbit AlexaFluor 647 (1:200) to detect fibrinogen and donkey anti-mouse AlexaFluor 488 (1:200) to visualize endogenous IgG. Slides were air dried and coverslipped with Fluoromount-G.

All histology was imaged at a magnification of 20x on a Nikon Ecliplse Ti2 microscope using a 0.75 NA objective. Images were acquired at a sampling resolution of 0.21 μm/pixel in x-y. Staining was quantified using FIJI25 by an observer blinded to the experimental groups. FluoroJade-positive cells were counted in 0.17 mm² regions of interest (ROIs) in the cortex (0.5–1 mm from bregma) and striatum (1.2 to − 0.1 mm from bregma) for MCAO tissue, and cortex alone for PTS tissue. Counts from 2 to 3 ROIs per region were averaged. Fibrinogen and IgG immunofluorescence were analyzed separately in 0.63 mm² ROIs in the infarcted cortex and striatum for MCAO tissue, and cortical infarct alone for PTS tissue. Background was subtracted using a 50-pixel rolling ball radius, and positive staining was thresholded using the FIJI “default” auto-thresholding algorithm with dark background setting. Thresholding was applied uniformly across all images. Percent area stained was quantified within the ROI.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v. 29, graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism 10. Infarct volumes were analyzed only within ischemia groups (excluding shams) using a two-way ANOVA (factors: sex X model). FluoroJade-positive neurodegeneration was analyzed separately in the cortex and striatum and in separate stroke models (excluding shams) using a one-way ANOVA (factor: sex). Behavioral data and vascular histology were evaluated using a two-way ANOVA (factors: sex X ischemia) performed separately within each experimental stroke model. Pearson’s correlations for all markers of pathology and behavioral outcomes were examined separately for MCAO males, MCAO females, PTS males, and PTS females. Results were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Infarct volume and region-specific neurodegeneration vary by sex and stroke model

Based on MRI analysis, infarct volumes were significantly larger following MCAO compared to PTS, (F1,34 = 50.672, p < 0.001, η2p= 0.613) with no significant differences between male and female mice in either surgical model (Fig. 2A, B). FluoroJade B staining revealed sex-specific patterns of neurodegeneration in the PTS model only (Fig. 2C, D). In MCAO, no significant sex differences were observed in either cortical or striatal degeneration. In contrast, female PTS mice showed greater cortical neurodegeneration than males (F1,16 = 16.316, p < 0.001, η2p=0.474), despite having comparable infarct volumes (Fig. 2A, B).

Sex- and model-dependent functional deficits following ischemic stroke

Neurological assessment using the Clark score revealed both general and focal deficits one day post-MCAO (1 DPI) in male (general F1,14=7.747, p < 0.05, η2p=0.356; focal F1,14=14.522, p < 0.01, η2p=0.509) and female (general F1,15=9.412, p < 0.01, η2p=0.386; focal F1,15=26.749, p < 0.001, η2p=0.641) mice. By 7 DPI, only male MCAO mice continued to exhibit deficits (focal F1,14=5.031, p < 0.05, η2p=0.264), while female mice returned to sham levels (Fig. 3A, B). No general or focal deficits were observed at either time point in mice subjected to PTS in either sex (Fig. 3C, D).

Vascular protein accumulation differs by sex and stroke model

Immunofluorescence analyses of fibrinogen and IgG demonstrated model-specific patterns of vascular protein accumulation 9 days following stroke. Following MCAO, fibrinogen immunoreactivity was highly variable and non-significant in both sexes (Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, PTS produced a more consistent pattern of diffuse cortical fibrinogen deposition relative to shams in both males (F1,15=10.265, p < 0.01, η2p=0.406) and females (F1,14=11.454. p < 0.01, η2p=0.450). Overall, PTS produced a substantially higher fibrinogen burden within the injured cortex, indicating more pronounced vascular pathology relative to MCAO (Fig. 6C).

Associations between injury markers and behavioral outcomes differ by sex and stroke model

To determine whether pathology was associated with behavioral outcomes, Pearson’s correlations were examined separately for each sex and stroke model. Among MCAO males, vascular protein accumulation in both the cortex and striatum correlated with reduced spontaneous alternation and open field locomotion, whereas only striatal vascular protein accumulation correlated with higher Clark scores on day 7. There was a strong positive correlation between IgG and fibrinogen expression in both the cortex and striatum of MCAO males (Supplementary Table 1, all p < 0.05). Stroke pathology exerted a weaker effect on behavioral outcomes in MCAO females; however, cortical IgG accumulation correlated with higher Clark scores on day 1 (p < 0.05). (Supplementary Table 1, p < 0.01). No significant correlation was identified between pathology and behavioral outcomes of either sex in the PTS model; however, there was a strong positive correlation between cortical IgG and fibrinogen expression in both sexes (Supplementary Table 2, p < 0.01).

Discussion

The present study compared two commonly used experimental stroke models, MCAO and PTS, to delineate how sex and ischemic model shape neuronal degeneration, vascular pathology, and functional outcomes. These results reveal distinct pathophysiological profiles between models, and two principal conclusions emerge. First, the relationship between structural damage and functional impairment is sex-dependent and differs across surgical stroke models. Second, PTS produced more extensive vascular protein accumulation than MCAO, suggesting greater microvascular compromise despite smaller infarcts. These model-specific differences have direct implications for experimental design and interpretation in preclinical stroke research, underscoring the importance of aligning model choice with the biological question under investigation and incorporating sex as a biological variable. These are critical considerations for preclinical modeling as women and men have differing patterns of stroke lesions leading to divergent functional outcomes26.

In MCAO, transient vessel occlusion produced large infarcts and acute functional deficits in both sexes (Fig. 2). Consistent with what has been reported in rats and aged mice, females exhibited gradual recovery, whereas males remained impaired across the 7-day experimental time frame27,28(Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 1). Locomotor deficits were more pronounced in males in both models (Fig. 4), and cognitive dysfunction in the Y-maze was limited to MCAO males (Fig. 5), despite lack of sex differences in infarct volume or histological markers of pathology (Figs. 6 and 7). This dissociation may reflect increased vulnerability to secondary injury mechanisms in males, or enhanced neuroprotection in females (see mechanistic discussion below). Previous stroke studies that have incorporated both male and female mice have similarly revealed sex differences in post-stroke cerebrovascular reactivity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammatory response29–32, all of which contribute to variability and sex differences in functional recovery.

PTS produced smaller infarcts with sharply demarcated borders and no measurable deficits on the Clark score or y-maze in either sex, which is consistent with a previous report in BALB/c mice33. Although Fluorojade B staining revealed greater cortical neurodegeneration at 7 days in PTS females compared to males, this did not exceed the level of degeneration observed in MCAO-treated animals (Fig. 2). It has been reported that neurons differ by sex in their apoptotic pathways, with XX neurons following a caspse-3 mediated pathway and XY neurons following an ROS-induced apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) pathway, likely independent of gonadal steroids34–37. Because of this, males and females also have different time courses of post-ischemic cell death, with females peaking later than males35. Additionally, it has been reported that females have elevated glycogen in the penumbra 24 h post-PTS33, which may be driven by estrogen38. Whether either or both of these factors affected our observed sex difference in active neurodegeneration 7 days post-PTS remains to be investigated. The absence of functional deficits despite histological evidence of cell death suggests that PTS cortical lesions may spare critical motor and cognitive circuits, or that compensatory mechanisms are more effective in this model. Importantly, the behavioral profile we observed is constrained to the acute and sub-acute post-stroke period as well as by the assays used, which are less sensitive to the subtle sensorimotor impairments typical of small cortical lesions. PTS has been demonstrated to produce persistent sensorimotor deficits when assessed with more sensitive tasks such as adhesive removal, foot fault, or strength and coordination assays39–41. Behavioral outcomes are also influenced by lesion location, thus anatomical differences in the infarcts (i.e. striatum infarcts only in the MCAO model) further limit direct comparisons of functional outcomes across models. Nevertheless, comparing performance on commonly used functional assays across these two widely adopted models remains informative, as it highlights how model-specific lesion location and pathology shape the detectable post-stroke behavioral phenotype.

In both stroke models, infarct volume did not correlate with any functional outcome (Supplementary Tables 1,2). This dissociation has been implicated as a limitation in the translational validity of preclinical stroke models42,43. However, the dissociation between infarct size and functional outcomes is consistent with both preclinical and clinical studies of smaller infarcts reporting weak or absent correlations between infarct volume and outcome44,45. Indeed in recent large multicenter preclinical trials (SPAN) in both mice and rats, infarct volumes were not strongly associated with behavioral outcomes46,47. Our correlational analyses are consistent with the observation that sex and stroke model are critical considerations in choosing a preclinical model to test a specific hypothesis.

As mentioned above, there is a bias toward males in the foundational science literature and a paucity of clinical research on sex differences in stroke outcomes but several studies have begun to address this48,49. One factor not addressed in the current study is age at stroke, as age interacts with sex to affect stroke outcomes. Women < 35 years old have greater risk for stroke than men50. After age 45, men are more likely to suffer first stroke than women, however in later years women are more likely than men to have a stroke and display worse outcomes51. Likewise, preclinically young adult female mice tend to have smaller infarcts and deficits than young adult male mice after MCAO, but by middle age this relationship flips; middle aged females have larger infarcts and deficits than similar aged males52, likely through altered inflammatory processes modulated by gonadal steroids. Indeed, estrogen is neuroprotective in preclinical stroke studies in young males and females52–54. However, both the timing of estrogen exposure55,56 and the estrogenic history can strongly influence ischemic outcomes, likely by altering both central and peripheral proinflammatory cytokines57–59. Furthermore, estrogen therapy can worsen outcomes in some rodent strains60 or if not timed correctly61. The current study utilized young adult mice, and we did not stage the estrous cycle of the female mice, future studies of similar design to the current study could incorporate age and gonadal hormone status as additional variables to address this limitation and provide additional guidance on model selection.

A salient finding of this study is the differential pattern of vascular protein accumulation between models. PTS was associated with elevated accumulation of IgG and fibrinogen within the injured cortex. (Figures 6 and 7). Accumulation of these proteins may reflect acute tissue repair and/or a potential source of exacerbation of neuroinflammation62. Fibrinogen plays a rapid response disease-modifying role in coagulation and immune activation, which are critical for containing tissue damage63,64. However, fibrinogen-mediated activation of microglia and astrocytes also exacerbates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress and promotes neuronal damage65–69. The prominence of both of these signals in PTS indicates that this model is characterized by more severe neurovascular injury and aligns with prior reports of stronger cytokine induction and immune infiltration in PTS6,7. These results identify photothrombosis as a valuable platform for studies focusing on neurovascular structure, endothelial repair, and secondary inflammatory sequelae.

In contrast, MCAO engages a different pathophysiological cascade, with more variable vascular protein deposition. This variability may reflect the dynamic nature of reperfusion injury, which complicates the interpretation of vascular pathology. Transient MCAO includes a defined reperfusion phase, which initiates a cascade of secondary injury processes that include oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and leukocyte adhesion70,71. These processes likely contribute to the variable vascular protein accumulation. PTS produces a permanent microvascular occlusion without reperfusion72, resulting in the dense, sharply demarcated cortical lesion observed in this study. The absence of reperfusion in PTS eliminates the fluctuations in blood flow which may explain the more homogenous vascular profile observed in this model. However, the PTS model is increasingly adaptable, and recent work demonstrates that alternative applications of this model can expand both the targets for ischemia and allow for reperfusion73. For example, MCA-targeted photothrombosis involves illumination directly to the distal MCA trunk, producing a large-vessel occlusion that more closely resembles arterial stroke74. Further, application of UV LED light following PTS-induced clot development induces recanalization of the MCA branch75. Pharmacological recanalization can also be achieved using tissue plasminogen activator in a model of PTS using a co-infusion of Rose Bengal with thrombin76. The PTS paradigm used in the present study targets the cortical microvasculature and produces a permanent, non-reperfused occlusion, which differs considerably from these large-vessel or reperfusion-capable PTS variants, and from the transient MCAO model.

Conclusions

Together, these observations underscore that MCAO and PTS experimental stroke models have both similarities and notable differences. Both models yield cortical injury and inflammatory activation, but they diverge in vascular pathology, functional outcome, and sex interactions. In the present experimental configuration, MCAO generated larger, more variable infarcts involving the cortex and striatum, included a reperfusion phase, and resulted in easily detectable behavioral deficits, particularly in males. PTS produced smaller but denser cortical lesions with sharp borders marked vascular protein accumulation, and no detectable deficits on the Clark test or y-maze. It is important to note that the distinctions observed between MCAO and PTS in this study may not be generalizable to all variants of these models. Moreover, both MCAO and PTS exhibit substantial intra-model variability. MCAO outcomes depend on filament diameter, insertion depth, collateral circulation, and reperfusion; whereas PTS outcomes vary with illumination intensity and duration as well as targeted coordinates. These factors should be considered when extrapolating the present findings to other experimental contexts. The present findings emphasize that model choice should be dictated by the biological question under study, and that sex must be incorporated as a design factor in order to capture the full spectrum of stroke pathophysiology.
