Curriculum design in disaster and mass casualty education: a scoping review of healthcare education
Authors: Naglaa Mohamed Abdelhamied, Abu Omayer, Salma Abdalla Elmisbah, Mohamed Alali, Hossam Hassan Yussef, Azza Yousif, Ives Hubloue, Nabil Zary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective disaster and mass casualty incident (MCI) response depends on healthcare professionals who can apply clinical, operational, and team-based competencies under pressure. However, existing reviews have not comprehensively mapped how disaster and MCI curricula are designed, delivered, and evaluated across healthcare education settings.ObjectiveTo map the structure, educational strategies, competency focus, and outcome evaluation approaches of disaster and MCI education curricula for healthcare learners and professionals.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following Arksey and O’Malley’s framework, JBI guidance, and PRISMA-ScR reporting. PubMed, Embase, Scopus, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar were searched for English-language studies published from 2015 to 2025. The search was updated on 6 January 2026. Two reviewers independently screened studies and charted data. Outcomes were mapped using Moore’s Expanded Outcomes Framework.ResultsForty-one studies were included. Curricula were classified as stand-alone (n = 14, 34.1%), embedded (n = 16, 39.0%), longitudinal (n = 4, 9.8%), or model proposals (n = 7, 17.1%). Outcome evaluation was concentrated at lower Moore levels: 22 studies (53.7%) assessed satisfaction, 16 (39.0%) assessed declarative knowledge, 18 (43.9%) assessed procedural knowledge, 15 (36.6%) assessed competence in simulated or controlled settings, and 5 (12.2%) assessed real-world performance. No study assessed patient-, community-, or system-level outcomes. Only six studies (14.6%) explicitly targeted interprofessional learner groups.ConclusionDisaster and MCI education curricula are increasingly reported but remain heterogeneous in structure and evaluation. Future curricula should strengthen alignment between competencies, teaching methods, and assessments; include interprofessional learning where relevant; and use feasible approaches to assess competence and real-world performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09818-z.Keywords: Mass casualty incident, Disaster medicine, Curriculum design, Healthcare education, Scoping review, Moore's outcomes framework, Interprofessional education, Medical education

Background

When a sudden-impact disaster occurs, emergency medical services may be overwhelmed when the number or severity of casualties exceeds available resources, creating a mass casualty incident (MCI). MCIs may arise from natural or human-made events, including terrorism, transportation accidents, fires, extreme weather, hazardous exposures, and public health emergencies [1, 2]. Effective MCI response requires early scene recognition, incident coordination, rapid triage, and resource allocation to minimize preventable morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. These operational demands make disaster and MCI education a curriculum design challenge: training must prepare healthcare learners not only to recall protocols, but also to apply them under time pressure, resource scarcity, and team-based operational conditions.

Disasters, armed conflicts, and pandemics are happening more frequently, increasing the importance of MCI (Mass Casualty Incident) preparedness across healthcare systems [4]. These crises also disrupt healthcare education, particularly in conflict-affected settings. Farajalla et al. showed that nursing students in Palestine experienced checkpoint-related violence, restricted access to university and clinical training, and poorer psychological and environmental quality of life during the 7th of October War. This highlights the need for disaster and MCI curricula that address learner safety, psychosocial support, and educational continuity alongside clinical competencies [5].

The success of humanitarian and emergency responses depends heavily on strong collaboration within and across different professional and disciplinary groups. Context-specific evidence from emergency settings further illustrates how system-level barriers can affect emergency care quality. Alqaissi et al. identified key barriers to high-quality CPR in Palestinian emergency departments, including unfamiliarity with emergency trolleys, equipment failure, lack of team harmony, and personal stress, while the presence of a designated leader was reported as an important facilitator. These findings are directly relevant to curriculum design because they emphasize the importance of simulation-based training, leadership development, team coordination, equipment familiarity, and stress management in emergency and disaster preparedness education [6].

Global crises have shaped the development of humanitarian and emergency preparedness training, from early ICRC and MSF courses to university-based programs and post-9/11 investments in public health and hospital readiness [7–9]. Contemporary preparedness frameworks emphasize mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery [10]; however, many emergency healthcare workers remain insufficiently prepared for disasters [11]. This gap underscores the need for curricula that are locally relevant, interdisciplinary, and aligned with real response demands, as training across professions can improve shared understanding, role clarity, and coordination during actual incidents [12].

Recent research has examined the effectiveness of disaster and mass casualty incident (MCI) training for specific professional groups; however, important gaps remain in how the curriculum design literature has been synthesized. Baetzner et al. examined disaster training programs for medical first responders, with an emphasis on measurable outcomes and experimental or quasi-experimental evaluation designs, such as randomized controlled trials and pre-post studies. While valuable, this focus excluded much of the literature describing curriculum content, pedagogical strategies, competency alignment, and context-specific implementation, particularly in settings where formal experimental evaluation may not be feasible [13]. As a result, innovative, locally adapted, or descriptively reported curricula may be underrepresented in the current evidence base.

Similarly, Bahattab et al. conducted a scoping review of humanitarian health education and training in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Although their review provided important insights into humanitarian health education, it focused primarily on humanitarian aid frameworks and LMIC settings, and did not comprehensively address disaster medicine and MCI training across both high- and low-resource healthcare education contexts. In addition, it did not specifically map how curricula for clinical healthcare learners and professionals are structured, delivered, scaffolded, and evaluated across prehospital and in-hospital settings [7].

Therefore, a knowledge gap remains regarding the curriculum architecture, pedagogical strategies, competency focus, interprofessional design, cognitive scaffolding, and outcome evaluation approaches used in disaster medicine and MCI education for healthcare learners and professionals. Addressing this gap is important because effective MCI response requires not only individual knowledge, but also applied competence, coordinated team performance, and alignment between educational design and operational response demands.

Using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, this scoping review addresses the following research question: Among healthcare learners and professionals, including students, nurses, paramedics, emergency medical technicians, residents, fellows, and physicians (Population), how are disaster medicine and MCI education and training curricula designed, delivered, scaffolded, and evaluated (Concept) across prehospital and in-hospital healthcare education settings internationally (Context)?

Accordingly, this review maps disaster medicine and MCI training curricula used internationally across healthcare education settings. It examines curriculum architecture, teaching methods, target populations, competency domains, cognitive support strategies, interprofessional training approaches, and outcome evaluation approaches to identify evidence gaps and inform future curriculum design.

Methods

Study design

We conducted this scoping review as part of the MCIPHER (Mass-Casualty Incident - Prehospital Emergency Response) project, a collaborative effort focused on strengthening disaster medicine education, developing innovative training methods, and improving prehospital preparedness. We followed the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley [14] and incorporated later recommendations by Levac and colleagues [15], and followed relevant guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis to ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and comprehensive reporting [16]. Our reporting adhered to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist [17]. We preemptively developed and registered our protocol on Protocols.io [18], with the full text published on preprints.org [19], to guarantee transparency and reproducibility.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

We addressed the following primary and secondary research questions:

Primary: To map how disaster preparedness and mass-casualty incident (MCI) education and training interventions for healthcare learners and professionals are designed, delivered, and evaluated worldwide, and to characterize the structural, pedagogical, and evaluative patterns that influence the current evidence base.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

Search strategy

We searched seven academic databases on June 12, 2025: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, PsycINFO (via APA PsycNet), CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Google Scholar was also used to identify relevant grey literature. We used controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords related to “disaster medicine,” “mass casualty incident,” “training,” “curriculum,” and “simulation,” with database-specific automatic term mapping to improve retrieval accuracy.

An academic librarian contributed to developing and refining the strategy through iterative testing to enhance sensitivity and specificity. The Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines were used to evaluate the initial search strategy [20]. The search was updated before analysis on January 6, 2026, to include recently published studies. An example reproducible search strategy is provided below, and the full database-specific search strategies are provided in Appendix 1. Additionally, we manually reviewed the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles to identify further eligible publications.

Stage 3: study selection

Screening and eligibility criteria

We imported all retrieved records into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), a web-based platform for managing systematic and scoping reviews [21]. Covidence automatically detects and removes duplicates, and handles title/abstract screening and full-text assessment.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Population: Healthcare providers (physicians, nurses, paramedics, EMTs, residents, interns) or university/college students enrolled in health-related fields (medicine, nursing, public health, EMS). (2) Concept: Describe or evaluate curricular design strategies in disaster medicine or MCI training, including curriculum structure, delivery methods, learning outcomes, or training modalities. (3) Context: Disaster medicine or mass casualty incident education and training, in prehospital or in-hospital settings. (4) Study Types: Primary studies (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods), curriculum development reports, and grey literature providing relevant curriculum detail. English language only; publications from the last 10 years.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Do not focus on disaster or MCI training (e.g., general emergency care, trauma surgery without a disaster context). (2) Involve non-healthcare populations (e.g., military, engineers, general public) without a separate analysis for healthcare participants. (3) Editorials, commentaries, or opinion pieces without empirical data or descriptive curriculum details. (4) Systematic reviews, literature reviews, or other scoping reviews. (5) Not available in full text.

Following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews, two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Articles considered potentially relevant by either reviewer were advanced to full-text assessment. The same two reviewers then independently reviewed the full texts to determine final eligibility. Disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion and consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated the decision.

Stage 4: data charting

Two reviewers independently pilot-tested a standardized data-charting template on five studies to verify clarity, reliability, and completeness. The template was revised as needed and remained adaptable, allowing iterative adjustments throughout the review.

The extraction form collected data on study ID, DOI, and country of origin; educational setting and target population; outcome levels mapped to Moore’s Expanded Outcomes Framework with corresponding descriptions and targeted competencies; instruments/tools, timing of training and evaluation activities, and referenced training design frameworks; assessment approaches and operational frameworks; and whether cognitive support strategies were provided. Two reviewers independently extracted data and then compared their findings to ensure consistency, resolving disagreements through discussion or, if necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer. We organized the extracted data in Microsoft Excel.

Moore’s framework and outcome classification

We utilized Moore’s Expanded Outcomes Framework to categorize outcomes in the included studies [22]. Although the protocol initially specified the Kirkpatrick model, Moore’s framework was incorporated during the analysis phase because the extracted outcomes required more granular classification than Kirkpatrick’s broader levels allowed. In particular, the included studies reported heterogeneous outcomes, including factual knowledge tests, self-reported preparedness, simulation-based triage performance, and real-world clinical or field performance. Moore’s framework enabled clearer differentiation between declarative and procedural knowledge, simulated competence and actual performance, and individual learning outcomes and patient-, community-, or system-level outcomes. These distinctions are particularly important in disaster and MCI education, where effective preparedness depends not only on satisfaction or knowledge acquisition, but also on applied decision-making, team-based competence, and transfer of skills to high-stress operational contexts. A crosswalk between Kirkpatrick and Moore’s models is provided in Appendix 2 to support interpretability and comparison with related reviews.

Moore’s seven levels (operational definitions): Level 1 (Participation): Attendance, enrollment, or completion of the training activity. Level 2 (Satisfaction): Learner satisfaction, perceived relevance, engagement, and confidence. Level 3a (Declarative Knowledge): Factual recall and recognition, usually through multiple-choice or true/false tests. Level 3b (Procedural Knowledge): Applied understanding, reasoning, and self-reported skill or attitude gains, typically via validated preparedness or competency scales. Level 4 (Competence): Demonstrated ability in controlled or simulated settings (triage scoring, simulation checklists, OSCE-style evaluations, global rating scales). Level 5 (Performance): Behavior change in real clinical practice or field deployment, assessed through supervisor evaluations or post-deployment feedback. Levels 6–7 (Patient and Community Health): Measurable patient-level and population, or system-level health outcomes.

Studies that develop curricula through expert consensus or curriculum mapping without assessing learner outcomes were categorized as not classifiable. When an outcome could apply to multiple levels, classification was guided by the measure’s primary purpose, the assessment environment (simulated vs. real-world), and pre-established decision rules developed through iterative team discussion. Each study could be assigned multiple Moore levels reflecting different outcome types measured within the same intervention.

Stage 5: collating, summarizing, and reporting results

We analyzed data using descriptive statistics (counts and frequencies) for quantitative results and narrative analysis for qualitative and contextual information. We organized the extracted data into key areas: general study characteristics; study design and populations; educational setting and targeted competencies; outcomes classified by Moore’s framework; instructional, assessment, and operational frameworks; and data collection tools, assessment timing, and cognitive support provided.

Development of analytical constructs

Through an iterative process of comparison and clustering, we identified recurring patterns and derived three original analytical constructs:

Curriculum Architecture Typology: We classified studies into four structural types based on curricular integration, duration, and progression logic: - Stand-Alone: Single delivery of up to 4 weeks, not integrated into an existing program. - Embedded: Component within an existing program. - Longitudinal: Spans multiple terms with sequential scaffolding. - Model Proposal: Expert consensus without learner outcome data. This typology was developed inductively during data charting and synthesis to organize the substantial heterogeneity in how disaster and MCI curricula were structured and delivered. It was not applied as a validated classification system; rather, it served as an exploratory analytical tool to compare curriculum structures and examine their relationship with outcome depth (Appendix 3).

Curricular Coherence Analysis: We cross-tabulated each study’s reported frameworks across three axes: (i) pedagogical or training design framework (e.g., ADDIE, curriculum mapping), (ii) assessment or evaluation framework (formal model or validated instrument), and (iii) operational response framework (ICS, NIMS, triage algorithm). “Alignment” was defined as explicit reference to or integration of all three axes. The term “Curricular Coherence Gap” was developed inductively during synthesis to describe the recurring absence of clear alignment across these axes and should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory synthesis finding rather than a prospectively validated scoring tool.

Cognitive Scaffolding Taxonomy: We inductively categorized cognitive support approaches into five categories based on their mechanism of action: Mnemonic Aids, Algorithmic Supports, Job Aids/Checklists, Scenario Scaffolding, and Mentoring/Team Support.

This thorough mapping method allowed us to analyze how disaster and mass-casualty curricula are created, executed, and assessed across educational settings and learner groups, while also identifying structural patterns, evidence gaps, methodological issues, and priorities for future research and program development.

Ethics

Since this scoping review only integrated previously published, publicly accessible literature and did not involve direct interactions with human participants, formal ethics approval was not required. We adhered to established ethical standards, including transparent reporting of methodology, proper attribution and citation of sources, and avoidance of plagiarism or duplicate publication. Any potential conflicts of interest among the research team were disclosed and managed in accordance with institutional policies.

Results

Literature search and study selection

Our search of the included databases and registers yielded 466 records, with five additional records identified through other sources. After removing 36 duplicates, we screened 435 records by title and abstract, excluding 326. Full-text assessment of 109 articles resulted in 68 exclusions. In total, 41 studies met the inclusion criteria. The selection process appears in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 41 included studies showed a growing but geographically uneven evidence base. Most studies originated from high-income settings, particularly the United States [23–40], with smaller clusters from Taiwan [41–43] and Turkey [44, 45], and one multi-country study from Belgium and Italy [46]. This distribution suggests that published disaster and MCI curriculum research remains concentrated in a limited number of settings, with comparatively fewer studies from low-resource or conflict-affected contexts. The full study-level list, including country and DOI, is provided in Appendix 4.

Publication output increased over the review period, with relatively few studies published between 2015 and 2017 [24, 28, 31, 33, 36, 38, 47, 48] and a clear rise in recent years, especially in 2021 and 2025 [25, 26, 32, 37, 44, 45, 49–54]. This pattern indicates increasing scholarly attention to disaster and MCI education over time (Fig. 2).

Study characteristics

Characteristics of included populations

Study populations were diverse, including undergraduate students and practicing clinicians. Nursing students [23, 28–30, 45, 50–52, 56, 59, 63], medical students [40, 41, 44, 47, 55, 60], and EMS-related learners [24, 31, 32, 53, 54] made up the largest single-profession groups. Only 6 of 41 studies (14.6%) explicitly targeted interprofessional learner groups [26, 36–38, 46, 48], and only one focused on an undergraduate interprofessional group [37]. Cross-tabulation showed that interprofessional curricula had higher representation at advanced Moore levels (L4-L5) than uniprofessional curricula (Appendix 5).

While undergraduate participants dominated, postgraduate trainees appeared in several studies: emergency medicine residents and fellows [25, 49], pediatric residents [27], and EM/EMS fellows through model proposals [34, 35]. Two studies involved physicians in expert roles via program director contributions to model development [33, 39] (Table 1).

Training contexts and competency domains

Disaster curricula covered a range of educational contexts. They outlined competencies across four key domains: (1) disaster systems and incident management, which includes ICS/NIMS principles, hospital readiness, and surge planning [23, 30, 34, 35, 39–41, 57]; (2) clinical and triage skills for MCIs and CBRN/HazMat scenarios, such as decontamination and life-saving procedures [25, 27, 29, 31, 32, 43, 47, 48, 52, 54, 57, 60, 61, 63]; (3) public health, community preparedness, and population response [28, 41, 45, 46, 58]; and (4) psychosocial, ethical-legal, leadership, and interprofessional teamwork skills [26, 34–37, 51]. Several studies detailed education models, including frameworks for geriatric disaster care, disaster nursing, and emergency medicine residency programs [38, 39, 62]. Appendices 6 and 7 provide detailed information on educational settings and competency mapping.

Curriculum architecture typology

The 41 studies included were classified into four curriculum architecture types (empirical studies n = 34; model proposals n = 7) (see Table 2): Type A (Stand-Alone, n = 14) consisted of discrete courses or training modules delivered independently. Type B (Embedded, n = 16) involved curricula integrated into existing degree programs or professional development. Type C (Longitudinal, n = 4) encompassed curricula delivered across extended or sequential training structures. Type D (Model Proposal, n = 7) included studies proposing conceptual frameworks without formal implementation data.

Data collection methods

Across the 41 studies, data collection most commonly relied on structured tools measuring knowledge, attitudes, confidence, preparedness, or satisfaction, typically using Likert scales and multiple-choice questions [23, 26, 27, 40–42, 44–46, 50, 51, 55, 56, 61, 63]. Most evaluation studies used pre-post designs to assess short-term change, while some included additional data points to examine retention or persistence over time [25, 27, 36, 41, 53, 54, 63] and others incorporated follow-up through retention assessments or multi-source ratings [31, 36, 58]. Simulation-based curricula often combined written assessments with performance measures, including checklists, triage accuracy metrics, and global rating scales [31, 32, 36, 43, 51, 53, 54, 57–59]. Qualitative studies used debriefing interviews, focus groups, and open-ended reflections [24, 37, 47, 52], while expert-focused studies used modified Delphi processes, curriculum mapping, or surveys of program directors [33–35, 39, 48].

Training outcomes using Moore’s expanded outcomes framework

Out of 41 studies, outcome reporting aligned with Moore’s framework as follows: Level 1 (Participation), 1 study (2.4%); Level 2 (Satisfaction), 22 studies (53.7%); Level 3a (Learning—declarative knowledge), 16 studies (39.0%); Level 3b (Learning—procedural knowledge), 18 studies (43.9%); Level 4 (Competence), 15 studies (36.6%); Level 5 (Performance), 5 studies (12.2%); Not classified, 7 studies (17.1%) (Fig. 4).

Frameworks guiding training design, evaluation, and operational practice

Training design frameworks

Instructional design frameworks and curriculum models varied among the included studies, although several did not report a guiding framework [24, 33–35, 39, 54, 61, 63]. When described, approaches included competency-based and standards-driven designs such as outcome- and competency-based education models [46, 47, 58] and national or professional curricular standards [37, 44]. Structured program frameworks like HSEEP and PennDemic [23, 26] complemented experiential and adult-learner approaches, including andragogy [28, 47], experiential learning, and clinical competence models [36, 56], as well as cognitive or mastery-based methods [57, 58]. Simulation-focused models, notably the Jeffries Simulation Model [51, 56], and systematic design methods such as ADDIE and instructional systems design were also commonly used [31, 48, 57]. Study-specific approaches included quality improvement frameworks [25], flipped classroom delivery [55], game-based learning [42], backward curriculum design [53], disaster cycle structures [38, 40], and disaster literacy models [45].

Assessment frameworks

Assessment approaches varied widely across studies, with many not specifying a formal evaluation framework [25, 27–31, 34, 35, 38, 40, 41, 43, 44, 46–49, 55, 59, 60, 62, 63]. When mentioned, evaluation methods included institutional processes like EFEE course evaluations [61] and HSEEP “hot wash” [23], as well as theory-based models such as Keller’s ARCS Motivation Model [53] and frameworks focused on preparedness or competency [33, 50, 51, 56]. Formal models encompassed Transfer of Learning, AACN Essentials [30], and Kirkpatrick-based evaluations combined with modified Delphi techniques [57, 58]. Qualitative research references included established analytic standards [24, 37, 52]. Other methods covered KAB-style outcome measures, RACE-aligned behavior scales [42], TIPS and MASCAL/STX grading schemes [36], disaster literacy assessments based on the Health Belief Model [45], and outcome prioritization through Delphi approaches [39]. Two studies used guideline-informed content mapping to evaluate curricula [34, 35].

Operational frameworks

Incident management frameworks were commonly referenced, including ICS, NIMS, HICS, and IMS [23, 30, 41, 47, 48, 57, 58, 63]. Mass-casualty triage methods such as START and JumpSTART were commonly integrated [24, 27, 31, 37, 50, 51, 53, 54, 58–60, 63], whereas variants such as SMART, SIEVE, SORT, and SALT were less frequently included [24, 25, 31, 53]. The curricula also featured broader operational models, including the disaster management cycle and crisis standards of care [25, 45, 47, 63], military trauma protocols like TCCC/DoD CPGs and ATLS [36, 41, 60], and specific procedures such as hazmat zoning [43], METHANE reporting [58], RACE fire response [42], and reverse triage techniques [30].

Cognitive support tools and scaffolding approaches

Multiple studies have detailed cognitive support tools or scaffolding strategies integrated into simulations, drills, or practical learning environments.

Algorithm-based cognitive support has been examined in a limited number of studies. Koziel et al. integrated formal triage algorithms (SMART, START/JumpSTART) into simulations, supported by physical aids such as triage kits, reference cards, and length-based tapes, along with structured debriefings [24]. Shinchi et al. employed the SINCHI mnemonic framework, which included structured information-gathering prompts and checklist-style tasks within simulation exercises [62]. Wright et al. placed large SALT triage reference cards in clinical areas to enable point-of-care access [25]. Kim et al. utilized Emergo Train System materials, such as patient cards with clinical details and treatment cues, to assist decision-making [59].

Many studies reported the use of facilitator-mediated cognitive support. This included real-time cueing, guiding questions, or immediate feedback during or right after scenarios to help learners make decisions and reflect [36, 43, 51, 57, 58, 60]. One study highlighted mentorship-based scaffolding, where novice participants worked one-on-one with experienced EMTs for real-time guidance [32]. Other methods involved START-based triage support [50], the RACE procedure [42], and access to triage algorithms during simulations [31]. Genç et al. described disaster information cards, mobile apps, and serious games with embedded cues and feedback [45], while Altman et al. reported using structured prompts from educators [38].

Discussion

What this review contributes

This scoping review produces three main analytical outputs. We develop a taxonomy for disaster health curricula, categorizing 41 peer-reviewed studies based on architecture type (Stand-Alone, Embedded, Longitudinal, Model Proposal), professional discipline, and learner population. Additionally, we utilize Moore’s expanded outcomes framework as a systematic analytical tool, highlighting significant gaps in outcome depth and showing that traditional Kirkpatrick classification masks important differences in competence assessment. Lastly, we identify two systemic challenges. The Declarative Knowledge Plateau and the Competence-Performance Gap that go beyond individual curricula and reflect broader limitations in how disaster health education is currently understood and evaluated.

Compared with Baetzner et al. [13], our review moves beyond experimentally evaluated medical first-responder training to a broader curriculum-design lens across healthcare learners and professionals. Like Baetzner, we found evaluation dominated by knowledge tests and self-report, but our synthesis also shows how curriculum architecture, scaffolding, and coherence shape what can be measured. Compared with Bahattab et al. [7], we similarly identified missing standardized curriculum and evaluation frameworks, while extending the analysis from humanitarian health training to disaster and MCI curricula across prehospital and hospital education contexts.

Moore outcome analysis: the Moore ceiling

Applying Moore’s framework showed that outcome evaluation was concentrated at lower learner-response and knowledge levels, with relatively few studies assessing real-world performance and none measuring patient-, community-, or system-level outcomes. This pattern exemplifies what we call the Moore Ceiling: a systematic upper limit where disaster health education outcomes remain largely unexplored and unmeasured. Such measurement limits are widely acknowledged in broader medical education [22].

Two key phenomena explain this ceiling. Firstly, the Declarative Knowledge Plateau, where 16 studies assessed declarative knowledge (L3a) but only eight moved on to measure procedural knowledge (L3b). Several factors may explain this process. Applied and procedural outcomes are more difficult to measure because they require simulation, direct observation, workplace follow-up, or performance-based assessment, whereas factual knowledge can be assessed more easily using written tests. This is consistent with Pek et al., who found that full-scale disaster and MCI simulation exercises were mostly evaluated at the level of reaction or learning, while behavior and patient-level results were rarely assessed because real disasters are infrequent, outcomes are difficult to standardize, and full-scale exercises require substantial time, personnel, and resources [64]. Transfer-focused evaluations also face methodological challenges, including reliance on retrospective self-report, social desirability bias, recall bias, low response rates, and difficulty isolating the effect of training from wider organizational factors [65]. Therefore, the Declarative Knowledge Plateau likely reflects both curricular limitations and pragmatic barriers to measuring applied performance.

Secondly, the Competence-Performance Gap reflects the smaller number of studies assessing real-world performance after training compared with simulated or controlled competence. This distinction is important because competence demonstrated in a simulation may not transfer directly into practice, particularly when clinical environments are affected by resource constraints, team dynamics, infrastructure disruption, or conflict-related stressors [5, 66, 67].

Accordingly, disaster curricula should be evaluated not only for learner competence but also for transfer under constraint. Programs should document operational barriers, incorporate protected debriefing and psychosocial support, and assess transfer with realistic indicators such as team coordination, protocol adherence under disruption, and performance in drills or workplace simulations that approximate resource scarcity and access delays. This would align outcome measurement with the conditions that actually determine preparedness in high-stress and conflict-affected systems.

The curricular coherence gap

This review revealed a Curricular Coherence Gap: a lack of systematic alignment among learning objectives, curriculum content, instructional methods, and assessment. About 60% of studies did not clearly connect their competency goals to specific modules or assessments. This disconnect indicates that many curricula, despite covering relevant disaster health skills, lack a coherent logic model that links content delivery, teaching strategies, and evaluations to desired outcomes. Curriculum architecture and outcome depth

Curriculum architecture appeared to vary with outcome depth, although this relationship should be interpreted cautiously. Longitudinal curricula tended to reach deeper Moore levels, while stand-alone formats more often reported lower-level outcomes. These findings imply that features such as duration, integration, and iterative refinement could enhance the potential of the outcome. This typology is conceptually related to Harden’s integration model because both consider the degree of curriculum integration. However, our typology also considers whether the curriculum is delivered as a short stand-alone activity, embedded within an existing program, or structured longitudinally over time [68].

The Interprofessional Training Paradox

The Interprofessional Training Paradox reflects a mismatch between the team-based nature of real-world MCI response and the largely uniprofessional structure of published disaster health curricula. MCI response requires shared operational understanding, role clarity, communication, leadership, and coordinated decision-making across professional groups; however, few studies in this review explicitly targeted interprofessional learners.

This limited representation may reflect the logistical and methodological difficulty of designing interprofessional training, including coordinating learners across programs, aligning discipline-specific requirements, securing simulation resources, and assessing team-based outcomes. Some interprofessional training may also occur in hospital drills, EMS exercises, or institutional preparedness programs that are not routinely published.

The interprofessional studies identified in this review demonstrate several approaches, including interdisciplinary simulation [26], forward surgical team preparation [36], leadership-focused nursing and EMT simulation [37], geriatric disaster care training [38], postgraduate disaster medicine education linked to pandemic response [46], and competency-based CBRN first-receiver training [48]. Although these studies appeared more likely to reach higher Moore levels, the small number of studies limits inference. Future curricula should intentionally integrate interprofessional design and assess observable team outcomes such as communication, leadership, role clarity, shared situational awareness, and coordinated performance.

Core competencies and cognitive support

Across curricula, triage and incident command systems were identified as nearly universal competency targets. These address the key challenges of mass casualty management: fairly distributing scarce resources and coordinating response teams spread out across different locations. However, few curricula explicitly assessed the integration of these skills; most evaluated them separately, which may hinder effective decision-making when clinical and logistical demands occur simultaneously. Cognitive support tools, such as mnemonics, decision trees, job aids, checklists, and scenario-based simulations, are a secondary focus across curricula, helping reduce cognitive load during complex, high-pressure decisions. The five-category taxonomy identified-comprising Mnemonic Aids, Algorithmic Supports, Job Aids and Checklists, Scenario Scaffolding, and Mentoring/Team Support-includes various methods that externalize decision logic to maintain performance under stress. Curricula that incorporate multiple scaffolding strategies within cohesive educational structures tend to produce better outcomes, indicating possible synergistic effects [69].

Future directions and recommendations

Based on the key gaps identified, we propose five prioritized recommendations. First, future curricula should improve curricular coherence by explicitly linking competencies, teaching methods, assessments, and operational response frameworks. Second, evaluations should move beyond satisfaction and short-term knowledge by prioritizing Moore L4 competence and, where feasible, L5 real-world performance. Third, curricula should intentionally include interprofessional training, with assessment of team-based outcomes such as communication, leadership, role clarity, and coordinated decision-making. Fourth, programs should use embedded or longitudinal structures where possible, incorporating repeated exposure, progressive complexity, debriefing, and retention assessment. Fifth, future studies should use standardized reporting of curriculum structure, competencies, frameworks, cognitive supports, and outcomes to support comparison across programs and future evidence synthesis.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Methodologically, classification of studies into Moore’s outcome levels required interpretive judgment, particularly when distinguishing declarative knowledge, procedural knowledge, simulated competence, and real-world performance. In addition, Moore’s Expanded Outcomes Framework was adopted during analysis instead of the initially specified Kirkpatrick model. Although this provided more granular outcome classification, it may affect comparability with reviews using Kirkpatrick alone. To support transparency, we provide a Kirkpatrick–Moore crosswalk in Appendix 2.

Reporting limitations also affected synthesis. Many included studies did not fully describe curriculum components, assessment tools, implementation context, or outcome measurement procedures. This limited our ability to assess curricular coherence consistently across studies. The curriculum architecture typology, Curricular Coherence Gap, and cognitive scaffolding taxonomy were developed inductively and should be interpreted as exploratory classifications rather than validated frameworks.

Generalizability is limited because the review included only peer-reviewed English-language publications published since 2015. Relevant innovations reported in grey literature, non-English publications, local training documents, or unpublished institutional programs may have been missed. The included studies also varied substantially in terms of populations, settings, interventions, and outcome measures, limiting direct comparisons and precluding quantitative synthesis.

Finally, consistent with scoping review methodology, we did not conduct a formal critical appraisal or risk-of-bias assessment of included studies. Therefore, this review maps the scope and characteristics of the evidence but cannot determine the overall strength, quality, or effectiveness of the included curricula.

Conclusions

This scoping review mapped how disaster and MCI education curricula for healthcare learners and professionals are designed, delivered, and evaluated. The findings suggest that the field is growing, but outcome evaluation remains concentrated at lower Moore levels, with fewer studies assessing applied competence, real-world performance, or patient-, community-, and system-level outcomes.

Three main areas require attention: stronger alignment between competencies, teaching methods, and assessments; more intentional interprofessional curriculum design; and more robust outcome evaluation beyond learner satisfaction and short-term knowledge gains. Longitudinal and embedded curricula may offer useful directions for future curriculum development, but the heterogeneity of included studies limits firm conclusions about effectiveness.

Overall, future disaster and MCI curricula should be designed with clearer curricular logic, team-based learning opportunities, and feasible methods for assessing competence and performance in realistic practice contexts.
