Exploring the length and time related concepts for scenario-based multiple-choice questions through perspectives of medical education experts: a mixed-method approach
Authors: Rahul Ramesh Bogam, Harish Chennuri, Girish Manohar Chavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThere is a substantial gap in the scientific literature about specific, evidence-based guidelines for optimal word count (length) in multiple-choice question (MCQ) “stems” and “options” and validated time management strategies for MCQ test-takers. This dearth of research indicates a potential need for more studies to develop and validate these significant aspects of MCQ assessment and strategy. This was the first study which formulated guidelines for the word count of scenario-based MCQ (SB-MCQ) “stems” and “options,” along with justifications, from the perspective of medical education experts. The present research also developed specific, validated time management guidelines to assist test-takers effectively navigate MCQ examination.MethodsA mixed-methods design was implemented where quantitative data from a validated structured questionnaire and qualitative (focus group discussion) data were collected from 76 medical education professionals across multiple Indian provinces, and then analyzed using SPSS and NVivo software.ResultsA recommended best practice for writing SB-MCQs was reported to have question stems between 35–50 words followed by 20–35 words, with three-option formats being preferred over four and five options. Nearly 92.73% of participants reported that, during 1-minute timeframe, maximum time must be spent on “analyzing question stem” followed by on “eliminating options.” The study developed succinct, “eight-step guide” to empower test-takers complete MCQ tests proficiently.ConclusionScenario based MCQs with stems between 35–50 words and options between 1–10 words can improve item quality. Three-option MCQs can be preferred over four- or five-option formats. An emerged “eight-step structured guide” can assist test takers to tackle multiple-choice tests more efficiently and effectively.

Introduction

Multiple-choice questions (MCQs) represent one of the most essential well-established examination tools widely used in assessment at the undergraduate and postgraduate levels of medical examinations [1]. Well-constructed MCQs could test several skills in addition to the recall of factual knowledge, and are reliable, discriminatory, reproducible, and cost-effective [2].

A “standard MCQ” is a MCQ with a “stem” including “lead-in question,” a “key,” and “distractors.” To construct an effective MCQ, it is essential to follow technical guidelines which include avoiding ambiguity, negative phrasing, clues, and absolute terms, ensuring grammatical consistency, a clear stem, plausible distractors, and so forth. Scenario-based multiple-choice questions (SB-MCQs) necessitate more detailed, longer answers to create the situation, ensuring its word length a vital component for precisely presenting the context and gauging complex understanding, rather than just length being inherently essential [3]. Best practices especially for writing scenario based MCQs do suggest making the “stem” concise and the answer “options” relatively similar in length to each other. However, there is no single global standard for word count that applies to these components. There is scarcity of research on these aspects and calls for more studies to bridge this gap.

Shorter stem assist examinees to focus on understanding the core problem without getting bogged down by irrelevant information whereas well-crafted short distractors and key may be more effective at testing knowledge without creating confusion or requiring excessive time for analysis [4]. There is lack of consensus on the ideal number of options for MCQs, but academic research often suggests that three-option formats can provide similar psychometric value to four- and five-option formats with improving test efficiency and decreasing administration time [5]. On the other hand, some experts argue for four-option MCQs as a choice to ensure the balance between eliminating guesswork and not overwhelming test-takers [5]. The debate continues the optimum number of “alternatives” in MCQs, predominantly in medical education, as research provides mixed results and more evidence is required [6].

The 1-minute-per-MCQ guideline is a beneficial starting point for test timing but is not a widespread rule. The appropriate time per question depends on several factors like question complexity, test taker’s familiarity, and so forth and actual practices may vary significantly [7]. During answering of each MCQ, the examinee performs various cognitive processes like reading, understanding the question and options, recalling and correlating relevant knowledge to select the best answer [7,8]. There are no universally agreed-upon time allocation guidelines for the cognitive components of answering MCQ, as recommended time depends on factors like question difficulty and the cognitive skills being assessed. Well-formulated guidelines may assist examinees by imparting a structured approach to examination time, fostering suitable time utilization through strategies like time division per question and pacing, and eventually leading to greater performance by decreasing stress and enhancing efficiency [6,7].

The current study used a mixed-method, exploratory sequential design to formulate guidelines on the optimal length and number of options for MCQs by obtaining expert views, and to generate a step-by-step framework for test-takers to enhance their proficiency under time constraints.

The current study aimed (1) to determine the recommended length (word limit) for the “stem” (including the lead-in question) and “options” of standard single-based response scenario based MCQs; (2) to ascertain the optimal number of “options” in each MCQ; (3) to analyze key actions and time allocation for effectively solving MCQ during permitted time; and (4) to develop “step-wise practical guide” for MCQ test takers to enable them effectually answer questions within limited time.

Methods

1. Ethical consideration

The study was approved by Brij Niramaya Memorial Hospital and Research Center in Maharashtra, India (approval number: BMC/EC/437/2024). The informed consent was obtained from the study participants.

2. Study design

A mixed-method study with an exploratory sequential design was implemented over a period of 3 months from January 2025 to March 2025 at one of the tertiary medical colleges of Maharashtra State of India [9].

3. Study population and study sample

The purposive sampling was employed to select 84 experts from all provinces of India ensuring diverse representation for their collective expertise on addressed topic. The inclusion criteria required participants to have qualification in medical education (e.g., FAIMER [Foundation for Advancement in International Medical Education and Research], MHPE [Master of Health Professions Education], or PhD [Doctor of Philosophy] in HPE [Health Professions Education]) and at least 3 years of teaching experience.

4. Data collection

1) Questionnaire development and validation

In the current research, English language was assumed for MCQs to emphasize its various fundamental aspects. The process began with a comprehensive review of existing literature to ascertain relevant themes and create a conceptual framework for the questionnaire. Preliminary discussions among the research team helped to modify these themes, brainstorm potential questions, and confirm the questionnaire’s focus aligns with the study’s objectives. The draft questionnaire was shared with study participants and they were asked for opinions on the questions, including suggestions for additions, deletions, or revisions to enhance clarity, relevance, and overall effectiveness of questionnaire. Based on their inputs, questions were revised to address any identified issues. The final questionnaire comprised of four sections including demographic characteristics of participants and theme specific close ended questions (Appendix 1). It was disseminated to the entire participants through Qualtrics Software (Qualtrics, Provo, USA), a cloud based online survey platform, to collect the necessary responses. This stage was essential for obtaining high-quality data which could be used for analysis and to achieve the research objectives. The study demonstrated acceptable level of internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.787. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaire within 1 week.

2) Focus group discussions

One week before focus group discussion (FGD) session, two e-books including comprehensive information about MCQs construction guidelines were provided to all participants. Additionally, they were given four PubMed indexed research articles which were specifically related to the required number of options formats (three-option/four-option/five-option format) in MCQ and challenges in formulating ideal MCQs, and so forth. This initiative was taken to activate participants’ prior knowledge, encourage critical thinking, and provoke informed discussions on the topic. Six virtual FGDs were implemented over a month, each with around 12–14 participants, using Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, USA). “A focus group guide” including pre-determined list of open-ended questions was utilized to ensure the discussion remain focused and yields relevant information (Appendix 2). The duration of each FGD session ranged from 45 minutes to 1 hour. A second round of FGD with similar number of sessions for each group was conducted after 2 weeks to deepen the discussion on breaking down the 1-minute time limit for solving MCQs and recognizing the logical steps for effective MCQ test completion. This follow-up session permitted for more in-depth exploration of these two crucial aspects, building on insights from a previous session, to develop and refine effective strategies for answering MCQs under time constraints. During first round of FGD, a comprehensive 21-step checklist for solving MCQs was created through preliminary brainstorming and active interactions among participants facilitated by research team. The checklist included all essential steps to be followed by test taker to solve each MCQ effectively in limited time. The majority of participants expressed their need to convert this 21-step checklist into a more concise and handy checklist which could be more convenient for test takers to counteract the MCQ examination. Participants were encouraged to reflect on the same checklist and the revised actionable eight-step checklist was formulated during second round of FGD. The entire discussion was audio-taped and used for further in-depth analysis.

5. Data analysis

1) Quantitative data analysis

Anonymized data was extracted from the questionnaire tool and entered into Microsoft Excel and formatted for data analysis in the IBM SPSS ver. 30.0 Software (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). Mann-Whitney U test was administered to compare the central tendency of the Likert scale data.

2) Qualitative data analysis

It was collected through open ended survey questions and FGDs and then imported into NVivo software (QSR International Pty Ltd, Denver, USA) using ‘word frequency’ and ‘word search’ options. Thematic analysis was performed on the NVivo software using the method defined by Maguire and Delahunt [10] based on Clarke and Braun [11]. The process included an iterative, inductive analysis to classify initial codes, which were then transformed from NVivo mind maps into written codes, and revised by mapping textual data from transcript reads. Codes were then categorized into themes, with a final review to delineate the definitive themes.

2. Length of stem and number of options in scenario-based MCQ

In the present study, a standard length of SB-MCQ “stem” was recommended to be between 35–50 words followed by 20–35 words (Fig. 1). None of the participants preferred stem length above 80 words. Three-option MCQs (73.68%) were preferred over four-option (23.68%) and five-option formats (2.63%) (Fig. 1).

According to participants’ perspectives, concise MCQ stems between 35–50 words enhance assessment validity and reliability by reducing confusion and improving understanding. This word range promotes effective plausible distractors and efficient time management for examinees, ensuring clarity and similar length among options (Table 2).

3. Rationale for using three-option, four-option, and five-option SB-MCQs

Regarding a specific analysis of option formats of MCQs, three-option MCQs were considered superior in efficiency and potentially in psychometric quality compared to four- and five-option formats due to less time for item construction and administration, wider content coverage, and often similar reliability and validity, making them preferred format for both test-makers and takers (Table 3).

6. Qualitative results

The results analysis yielded four key themes related to MCQs: “length of SB-MCQ,” “options in MCQ,” “time management during MCQ solving process,” and a “step-by-step guide for effective answering of MCQ.”

1) Theme 1: Length of SB-MCQ

Participants agreed that MCQs should be carefully crafted: excessively long questions waste time and obscure core concepts, whereas overly brief questions can be ambiguous or simplistic, impeding precise assessment of knowledge. The consensus emphasized the need for balanced length to effectively assess a test-takers’ comprehension of scenarios without unnecessary cognitive load.

“A length of stem in range of 35–50 words can assist students to explore a topic more thoroughly and avoid shallow responses, ensuring they demonstrate in-depth understanding rather than just memorization.” [Participant 1]

2) Theme 2: Options in MCQ

The optimal number of options for MCQs is a subject of debate; however, participants’ discussion summarized that three-option MCQs are comparable to four- or five-option MCQs in terms of psychometric quality, while being more efficient to develop and administer. However, a criticism of the three-option format was high possibility of guesswork and less potential to differentiate between knowledgeable and non-knowledgeable students.

“Guesswork during MCQ test can definitely hamper the exam’s quality by inflating scores with chance success, distorting results, and affecting the reliability of the assessment as a true measure of a student’s knowledge. Based on participants’ viewpoints, by allotting a certain amount of time per question, students become more efficient, averting them dwelling too long on one question.

Discussion

The word count for a MCQ “stem” is influenced by factors like question’s difficulty and objective, aiming for clarity, conciseness, and the suitable testing of higher-order thinking skills [12]. A word limit for MCQs is essential to ensure fairness, conciseness, and a reliable assessment by making the correct answer less obvious [13]. There is sparse literature on the recommended word limit for MCQ components. The novelty of current research is that, it delivers a structured, expert-driven set of guidelines for determining the optimal word count for SB-MCQ “stem” and “options,” addressing a gap in existing literature by providing evidence-based guidance on a vital aspect of MCQ development, especially considering the time and complexity involved in formulating high-quality test items, particularly when relying on expert input to confirm reliability and validity.

This study on how medical educators perceive the ideal length for MCQ “stem” reported that over half of the participants believed that a stem including lead-in question between 35 and 50 words was the optimal length for clarity, specificity, and validity. This indicates a consensus among these educators that a specific word count in this range leads to higher-quality test items. The argument regarding the optimal number of options in MCQs has spanned decades, with research documenting that three options are often adequate and can be optimal, balancing psychometric quality with test construction efficiency [5,14]. In a manner consistent with findings of previous research, in our study, maximum medical education experts recommended three-option MCQs as a viable alternative to four- and five-option formats to ensure validity, reliability, quality of plausible distractors and cost effectiveness. Several studies suggest that three-option MCQs maintain similar test validity and reliability while potentially refining test efficiency by decreasing preparation time for educators and time spent by test-takers [5,6,13]. However, the common criticism of a three-option format was observed to be the increased possibility of random guessing. This finding was supported by our research as well as Royal and Stockdale [15] where they documented that the chance of random guesswork was 33%, 25%, and 20% with three options, four options, and five option formats, respectively. In contemporary medical education, there is a shift from traditional four- or five-option MCQs towards three-option MCQs mainly due to the difficulty of forming plausible distractors for four- and five-option formats. Though some institutions still adopt four- or five-option formats, many are implementing three-option formats, particularly since a 2019 survey reported that most test-takers were still performing at a similar level as on four-option exams [15,16]. The focused group discussion among medical education experts helped to understand advantages and disadvantages of each option format. The discussion also assisted to explore significant concepts regarding each option formats like psychometric principles, random guesswork, administrative resources which need to be considered while creating MCQs. The consensus among these medical education professionals concluded that three-option MCQs can be preferred over four- or five-option formats. This could be the valuable scientific evidence pertaining to ideal number of options to be used during construction of MCQs.

In medical education, a 1-minute average per MCQ is a common benchmark, but this differs based on question difficulty and format [7,14]. Effective time management is essential for test-takers to complete all questions within the given timeframe, as longer or more difficult questions may take more time, affecting performance and potentially score attainment. Time management is crucial for solving MCQs as it avoids stress, allocates time evenly to each question, reviews challenging ones, and eventually achieves improved exam performance [17]. Key strategies encompass budgeting time for each action, skipping questions to return to later, and emphasizing on easier questions first to build momentum and save time. There is no standard practice or universally agreed-upon method for estimating a time limit on each individual key action within MCQ.

A structured, expert-validated emerged framework for MCQs breaks down the 1-minute timeframe into key actions including understanding the question, applying test-taking strategies, and confirming the answer, which may enhance both speed and accuracy. This approach, supported by qualitative results in this study, addresses a gap in existing research, which tends to emphasis on overall test time rather than granular, action-based time allocation within a single question. The framework permits for proficient time management by allocating precise actions, like carefully assessing the stem, identifying the core concept, utilizing process of elimination, and confidently choosing the answer within the minute.

Test takers unfamiliar with effective MCQ solving strategies create challenges for themselves, leading to misinterpretations, wasted time, wrong answers from guesswork or poor elimination, and overall low performance due to a lack of confidence and time management concerns [13,16]. Mastering systematic steps including meticulously assessing questions, eliminating plausible incorrect choices, managing time, and even guessing strategically may significantly enhance test-taking skills and scores. The existing literature is lacking a single validated checklist or framework for effective solving of MCQ. We attempted to develop consensus based algorithmic guide to improve test performance, timeliness in MCQ examinations. This framework has potential to help test taker to enter the test prepared and equipped with a concrete strategy to tackle each question efficiently and effectively. The refined guide is based on constructive feedback from medical education experts, making it more relevant to needs of test takers and the real-world challenge of solving MCQs.

1. Strengths

The primary strength of this study is its novelty, as it is the first to assess the need for specific length of “stem” and “options” in MCQs. This pioneering approach bridges a gap in the research, offering novel insights into a key aspect of item design and potentially promoting future question creation and assessment practices. Secondly, this research shifts focus from established time management factors in MCQ tests, such as metacognition and question difficulty, to the previously unexplored concept of validating the time allotted for individual key actions within the solving process itself. Such validation of micro-level actions is a new approach which can significantly improve the understanding of how test-takers manage time during MCQ examination.

2. Limitations

The study had some limitations. Our study results are based merely on participants’ perspectives which may be a form of subjectivity bias, which may restrict the study’s generalizability and external validity. Additionally, the present research findings are based only on viewpoints of one specific group, i.e., medical education experts. To overcome the limitation of relying solely on single group’s perceptions, diversifying the data by considering feedback from a wider range of stakeholders like medical students and administrators is highly warranted. The present research is confined to MCQs specifically in English language. Therefore, the applicability of this study may vary when MCQs are used in other languages possibly due to certain factors like cultural context, language proficiency differences as well as challenges of precise translation.

3. Suggestions for future research

The study advocates for implementing larger, multi-center studies with diverse medical educational professionals for generalizability of research findings. It also calls for further research to assess the efficiency of emerged validated checklist, 1-minute time frame, three-option MCQs, and recommended MCQ stem length, to gain comprehensive understanding of topic.

4. Conclusions

A 35–50-word stem and each option of 1–10 words in MCQs can enhance item quality and avert errors, and three-option items are as effective as four-option and five-option formats. Implementing a step-by-step guide with allotted times for individual key actions during the MCQ solving process can facilitate time management and overall test performance of test taker.
