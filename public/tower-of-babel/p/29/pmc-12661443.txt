Co-creation and delivery of a simulation-based learning model for locally employed doctors: Findings from the SIMBA-LED study
Authors: Swetha Palanichamy, Kashish Malhotra, Yun S Sin, Nevil C Philip, Amynta Arshad, Harshin Balakrishnan, Haneesh Johal, Saima Kauser-Malik, Sahrish Khan, Shreyas Moodalbyle, Maiar Elhariry, Lakshmi Narayanan, Punith Kempegowda, DEVI Collaboration
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•LEDs receive less structured teaching than HEE trainees, despite key workforce roles.•A tailored SIMBA model improved LEDs’ confidence in acute care by 32.4%.•LEDs, especially IMGs, preferred simulation over traditional learning approaches.•SIMBA sessions enhanced key ACGME competencies, supporting the integration of LED training.Keywords: Simulation-based learning, SIMBA, Medical education, Collaboration, Continuing professional development

Introduction

Resident doctors (formerly known as junior doctors) play a pivotal role as frontline healthcare professionals tasked with triaging and managing acutely unwell patients.1 However, studies consistently indicate that resident doctors often feel ill-prepared to handle such scenarios, citing concerns about potential patient harm and a lack of readiness in acute care settings. Simulation-based learning has emerged as a promising approach to addressing these challenges, providing a safe environment for healthcare professionals to enhance their confidence and skills across various clinical scenarios.2 Importantly, research has demonstrated that improvements in simulated environments can translate into fewer errors in real-life clinical practice, ultimately bolstering patient safety.3,4

While existing literature extensively explores the benefits of simulation-based learning for medical students and resident doctors-in-training, there remains a notable gap in research and initiatives tailored to locally employed doctors (LEDs).5, 6, 7, 8 LEDs comprise a significant portion of the resident doctor workforce within the National Health Service (NHS), a cohort that continues to expand with the influx of international medical graduates. Gurnett et al underscored the limited exposure to acute medical cases among non-UK-trained LEDs and highlighted the positive reception of incorporating simulation training into their curriculum.9 However, there remains a need for a comprehensive evaluation of such initiatives to understand their impact fully. Unlike doctors in training programmes run by Health Education England (HEE), LEDs do not have structured career progression or access to standard postgraduate educational support, leaving a gap in their continuous professional development. The challenges LEDs face – such as limited access to specialty-specific teaching, difficulties adjusting to the NHS system (especially for international medical graduates) and the pressure to manage acute medical cases – underscore the need for targeted educational interventions.

Advancements in technology have facilitated the emergence of online-based simulations, with virtual reality simulations gaining prominence, particularly amid the COVID-19 pandemic.10, 11, 12 The Simulation via Instant Messaging for Bedside Application (SIMBA) model, utilising platforms like WhatsApp and Zoom, has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing participants’ confidence in managing simulated cases across various medical specialties.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 The SIMBA model addresses these gaps by offering a simulation-based learning experience designed to improve knowledge, confidence and skills, particularly in managing acute medical scenarios. However, the application of this model specifically tailored for LEDs has not been explored.

We adopted the SIMBA model through Kern et al’s six-step curriculum development framework to develop a simulation-based learning (SBL) bespoke model to upskill LEDs in acute medical scenarios. This framework is a systematic approach widely recognised for its efficacy in creating high-quality, accredited healthcare training curricula.19 Through rigorous evaluation and feedback mechanisms, we aim to assess the acceptability and effectiveness of this approach, thereby contributing to the ongoing improvement of healthcare training practices.

Methods

This prospective study was conducted at the University Hospitals Birmingham NHS Foundation Trust from July 2023 to January 2024.

Development of the SIMBA-LED model

The research adapted Kern et al’s six-step curriculum development framework.

Problem identification and general needs assessment

We interviewed consultants and administrators from University Hospital Birmingham engaged in LEDs’ medical education to gain insights into their experiences with LEDs and their perspectives on educational support for LEDs, simulation-based learning, and the SIMBA model.

Targeted needs assessment

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with LEDs to delve into their medical education journey, mainly focusing on acute medical scenarios. LEDs were eligible to participate if they were currently working in acute medical settings and were not enrolled in formal postgraduate training programmes run by HEE. We excluded participants already involved in structured educational schemes, such as specialty trainees. These interviews aimed to understand LEDs’ background, confidence in managing acute medical cases, views on the postgraduate educational support that they received, perceptions of simulation-based learning, and the applicability of the SIMBA model in their education.

Participants for both general needs and targeted needs assessment were recruited through an online invitation process and word-of-mouth recommendations. We aimed to recruit at least 30 participants to ensure meaningful insights.

Goals and objectives

Based on the inputs from the interview above, we set the primary goal of this study to enhance LEDs’ confidence in managing acute medical scenarios through simulation-based learning. Specific objectives included exploring LEDs’ medical education experiences and expectations, assessing changes in self-reported confidence post-SIMBA sessions, and LEDs’ acceptability of the SIMBA model.

Educational strategies

We adopted the SIMBA model as part of our educational strategies. The preparation and delivery of SIMBA sessions, complete with detailed flowcharts and examples, have been thoroughly outlined in our previous publication.13 SIMBA uses WhatsApp and Zoom to create an interactive, accessible and resource-efficient learning environment. This model leverages simulation gaming and Kolb’s experiential learning theory to enhance the educational experience. It involves four phases:

Preparation phase: Our team prepared anonymised real-life case transcripts, validated by acute medical consultants for clinical accuracy. The scenarios were derived from real-life cases, with all patient-identifiable information meticulously removed. Each scenario included comprehensive details such as presenting complaints, medical history, examination findings, clinical observations, investigation results (eg blood tests and imaging), differential diagnoses, management strategies and follow-up plans. The experts chairing the sessions reviewed and approved the case transcripts to ensure scientific accuracy and adherence to current medical guidelines. Medical students and resident doctors were recruited and trained as moderators, allowing them to learn and develop skills.

Registration phase: Participation in the sessions was voluntary and free of charge. Participants registered beforehand by sharing their email addresses and WhatsApp numbers. Emails were used for pre-session communication, whereas WhatsApp enabled real-time interaction during the simulations, ensuring that participants had access to all necessary session details and resources. LEDs were invited to participate via an online registration form, where they provided consent and completed a pre-session survey assessing their confidence in managing acute medical cases.

Simulation sessions: Each session included four real-life medical case scenarios aligned with the Joint Royal Colleges of Physicians Training Board (JRCPTB) curriculum. On the day of the session, participants contacted moderators via WhatsApp. Before the simulation, participants completed a pre-simulation survey that included informed consent, sociodemographic data collection, and self-assessing their confidence in managing various acute medical case scenarios recommended by the JRCPTB for internal medicine training. This survey established a baseline measure of their confidence levels, encompassing both the session-specific scenarios and comparable medical presentations. Participants were not informed of the specific case scenarios to be simulated when they completed the pre-session survey. Participants then solved cases interactively, moderated by medical students. Each case lasted 20 minutes, with brief breaks between sessions. Moderators navigated participants through the scenarios, prompting them to engage as they would in patient interactions. Following case solving, participants joined a Zoom meeting for in-depth discussions with an acute medical consultant. This interactive session offered participants a platform to discuss the cases, ask questions and gain deeper insights. Key resources, including survey links, investigation results and meeting invitations, were shared via WhatsApp.

Post-session feedback: Participants completed a post-session survey to evaluate their confidence levels and provide feedback on the SIMBA experience. They were also requested to self-assess their Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) core competencies.20 Moderators assessed participants’ interactions using an adapted version of the Global Rating Scale (GRS) for each case scenario and sent detailed feedback alongside their attendance certificates. The pre- and post-SIMBA questionnaires for one of the sessions have been shared as Supplementary 1. The 44 cases simulated in our study, along with their scenarios and learning objectives, are shared in Supplementary 2.

Implementation

Under PK’s supervision, SP crafted the study proposal, established a cohesive project team, and obtained clearance from the institute’s clinical governance team to initiate the study. As part of the initial two steps of Kern’s curriculum development framework, SP conducted interviews with various stakeholders. These interviews informed the creation of the project’s objectives, ensuring that they aligned with the identified needs and gaps.

A total of 11 SIMBA sessions were conducted during the study period, ensuring at least two iterations of each acute medical competency recommended by the JRCPTB. All LEDs with jobs equivalent to foundation years 1–3 were invited to participate in the sessions. The team adopted a collaborative and rotating leadership approach, which fostered a dynamic and supportive environment. This allowed each member to leverage their expertise and make meaningful contributions to the project’s success. This collaborative effort and shared responsibility were critical in achieving the project’s objectives within the allocated timeframe.

Evaluation and feedback

We employed the Kirkpatrick model to assess the intervention’s impact through a thematic analysis of interviews, quantitative analysis of confidence changes using pre- and post-session surveys, and performance assessments with the GRS. Moderators provided feedback to participants following the GRS analysis, using Pendleton’s feedback model to highlight participants’ strengths and offer constructive suggestions for improvement. This approach aimed to enhance their skills and confidence in handling similar situations.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Data from the interviews on general needs and targeted needs assessment were analysed thematically using NVivo 12. A similar approach was undertaken to analyse the data from open-ended survey questions. Two research members independently familiarised themselves with the data by reading and re-reading transcripts to gain a deep understanding. This was followed by initial coding, where the data were broken down into segments and labelled with short phrases that captured their essence. These codes were then grouped into broader patterns or themes that emerged directly from the data. The researchers collaboratively refined these themes to ensure that they accurately represented the data. Once finalised, the themes were clearly defined and named to reflect their core meaning. The analysis was then written up, integrating the themes into a narrative that addressed the research questions, supported by illustrative quotes. To ensure the credibility and reliability of the analysis, techniques such as member checking, triangulation and peer debriefing were employed.

Statistical analysis of quantitative data was performed using STATA (STATA/SE 17.0 for Mac). Data were extracted from the survey completed by moderators before and after the session. Data on confidence level were categorised into three groups: confident (strongly agree, agree), unsure (agree somewhat, undecided, disagree somewhat) and not-confident (strongly disagree, disagree). Wilcoxon Signed Rank test was used to investigate differences between pre-simulation and post-simulation variables, and statistical significance was accepted at a 95% confidence level.

Qualitative analysis

We employed reflexive thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clarke, using NVivo 12 for data management.21 Two researchers independently familiarised themselves with the transcripts, generated initial codes, and iteratively developed themes. Themes were refined collaboratively to ensure alignment with the data and research questions. To enhance trustworthiness, we applied member checking (with selected participants) and peer debriefing sessions.22 The interview format has been listed in Supplementary 3.

Reflexivity

All researchers involved in data collection and analysis are healthcare professionals or medical educators/students with direct experience in medical training. We recognise that our professional backgrounds and prior experience with the SIMBA model could have influenced our interpretation of the data. To minimise bias, we sought peer feedback during theme development and discussed alternative interpretations of the data during team meetings.

Patient and public involvement

Although patients did not participate directly in the research process, our study simulated real-life clinical scenarios encompassing the full patient journey through secondary care. The primary focus of this study was to enhance the delivery of medical education for healthcare professionals, with the ultimate goal of improving patient care services. The findings will be shared with the study participants and the global community via social media and/or email to foster further engagement and advance medical education efforts.

Results

Thematic analysis of stakeholders’ input for general needs assessment

Thematic analysis of interviews with 14 consultants and two managers revealed LEDs’ eagerness to learn and develop robust clinical skills and their contributions to workforce stability. However, they acknowledged LEDs’ variable experiences due to their diverse training backgrounds, with some facing challenges adjusting to the NHS system.

Support mechanisms for LEDs included structured teaching programmes, such as a 24-month programme with fortnightly sessions and weekly departmental teachings. Additional supports included CESR guidance, e-portfolios and induction programmes. Despite these measures, a gap in educational support for career progression between LEDs and HEE trainees was highlighted, underscoring the need for further investment in resources tailored to LEDs’ needs.

They felt that simulation-based teaching could enhance LEDs’ confidence and skills. However, barriers such as a lack of protected time for participation were identified. They emphasised the importance of ongoing evaluation and feedback to optimise new teaching methods. Corresponding examples are highlighted in Table 1.

Thematic analysis of targeted needs assessment through LEDs’ perspectives

The 30 LEDs interviewed comprised eight UK graduates and 24 international medical graduates (IMGs) from diverse countries, including India (n = 12), Pakistan (n = 7), Myanmar (n = 2), Bangladesh (n = 1), Brazil (n = 1) and Jordan (n = 1). The median time since graduation was 3 years, with 1 year in the NHS. 24 LEDs worked in medical and six in surgical specialities.

Participants highlighted initial struggles adapting to the NHS’ holistic, multidisciplinary approach. They had varied awareness and confidence in managing acute medical scenarios. Initial confidence levels were generally low, but exposure to clinical practice significantly improved confidence. This emphasised the need for targeted educational interventions to enhance LEDs’ proficiency in acute medical care.

Participants experienced various teaching methods in the NHS, including bedside teaching, ward rounds, case-based discussions and simulation-based learning. Simulation courses were highly valued for their realistic approach, skill training and confidence-building in a safe environment. Key barriers to teaching attendance included staff shortages and excessive clinical workloads. Participants suggested dedicated time for personal learning, intrinsic motivation, and incentives like certificates and CPD points to facilitate increased attendance. Quantitative results of changes in knowledge, skillset and attitude

A total of 46 participants completed both pre- and post-surveys and were included in the analysis. Of these, 38 were practising in the UK, and eight were IMGs due to start working in the NHS shortly after the sessions (five from India, two from Myanmar and one from Indonesia).

All participants rated the overall quality of SIMBA teaching sessions as excellent (n = 23, 50%), good (n = 21, 45.7%), or fair (n = 2, 4.3%). Participants found the content of SIMBA sessions to be impactful at both personal (n = 45/46, 97.8%) and professional (n = 43/46, 93.5%) levels, which translated to improved patient care. They found the sessions engaging (n = 43/46, 93.5%) and accommodating to their learning style (n = 39/46, 84.8%). They reported SIMBA to be preferable to traditional lecture-based learning (n = 40/46, 87%), with the length of the session being reasonable (n = 40/43, 93%).

There was a significant increase in confidence post-SIMBA for all simulated (p < 0.001), non-simulated (p < 0.001), and all cases overall (p < 0.001). All participants either strongly agreed (n = 36/46, 78.3%) or agreed (n = 10/46, 21.7%) that the simulated topics were relevant to their practice. They reported improvements in all six ACGME competencies, with the maximum improvement in knowledge of patient management (n = 39/46, 84.8%) followed by patient care (n = 33/46, 71.7%).

Discussion

The SIMBA-LED model was acceptable to LEDs, accommodating their learning style while ensuring that they met the recommended JRCPTB competencies. It enhanced participants’ confidence, which could translate to improved patient care. Our findings align with previous research highlighting the benefits of simulation-based education in medical training.23, 24, 25 However, our study is unique in its focus on LEDs and the use of instant messaging as a simulation platform, which offers flexibility and accessibility that is not always present in traditional simulation models.

Our findings support a growing body of literature demonstrating that SBL enhances medical trainees’ competence, confidence and preparedness in clinical settings. Numerous systematic reviews have shown that simulation training leads to improved clinical performance, knowledge retention and learner confidence across various healthcare settings and specialties.26 McGaghie et al reported that simulation promotes deliberate practice, reduces anxiety in high-stakes environments, and ultimately improves patient safety.4 In our study, SIMBA-LED participants experienced a significant increase in confidence, which aligns with previous SIMBA studies when participants also reported improved confidence and skill acquisition.13,14

Simulation is not only useful for skills training, but may also improve real-world clinical outcomes. Cook et al demonstrated that simulation-based interventions are associated with improved care processes and, in some cases, better patient outcomes.26 Our participants similarly reported that SIMBA enhanced their confidence in managing real clinical scenarios, particularly inpatient care and management competencies. These results reinforce the notion that simulation, when grounded in real-life clinical pathways and delivered in interactive formats, can improve preparedness for complex, high-pressure clinical environments.27, 28, 29

Our work addresses explicitly a gap in simulation research by focusing on LEDs. LEDs, often IMGs, comprise a rapidly growing segment of the NHS workforce. Yet they historically receive little structured training and are excluded from many deanery-based education programmes.30 Previous studies have highlighted that LEDs often face difficulty adjusting to NHS practices due to differing medical education backgrounds, unfamiliarity with UK guidelines, and reduced access to supervision and feedback.31,32 Our findings reinforce these concerns – participants in this study highlighted barriers such as lack of protected time for learning, limited teaching support, and initial low confidence when managing acute cases. These challenges align with reports from the Royal College of Physicians and HEE, which recommend tailored educational interventions, mentorship and simulation-based upskilling.33

The SIMBA model is uniquely suited to address these needs. Unlike resource-intensive, in-person simulation models, SIMBA leverages widely available digital platforms (WhatsApp and Zoom), allowing flexible, cost-effective delivery of realistic clinical scenarios in a remote setting. This makes SIMBA scalable and accessible, especially for LEDs, allowing for a balance of clinical duties and limited additional protected teaching time. Previous studies of digital simulation approaches have reported high learner satisfaction, improved accessibility and significant knowledge gains, even in low-resource settings.26,34 Our study reaffirms these findings. This echoes studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, where SIMBA was successfully used to deliver high-quality simulation across geographical and time barriers.16,17

Furthermore, the structure of SIMBA maps effectively onto established medical education competency frameworks. Participants self-assessed progress across the ACGME domains, reporting substantial improvements in patient care, medical knowledge and practice-based learning. These domains correspond closely with the GMC’s Good Medical Practice and Foundation Programme curriculum competencies.35 However, we acknowledge that incorporating UK-specific competency frameworks would enhance contextual relevance, and we plan to address this in future research by comparing and aligning our findings with local models. Additionally, the use of Pendleton’s feedback model in SIMBA reinforces communication, professionalism and reflective learning, which are key elements in both UK and international postgraduate medical education standards.36 In this way, SIMBA not only improves clinical preparedness, but also supports holistic professional development aligned with national training goals.

Beyond its immediate impact on LEDs, SIMBA presents opportunities for wider integration into medical education. The model’s simplicity, flexibility and cost-effectiveness make it adaptable for use in other healthcare settings, including interprofessional education. Studies have shown that team-based simulation improves interdisciplinary communication, collaboration and patient outcomes.37,38 With minimal adaptation, SIMBA could be expanded to include nurses, pharmacists and allied health professionals in shared case simulations. Finally, as technology continues to transform medical education, future iterations of SIMBA could incorporate artificial intelligence – driven feedback, adaptive learning pathways, and augmented reality features for enhanced interactivity.39 While our study demonstrates short-term confidence gains, future longitudinal studies should assess sustained knowledge retention, clinical behaviour change and patient-level outcomes.

The small sample size limits comparing the differences in gain between LEDs from UK and IMG backgrounds. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported measures of confidence and competence, which may be subject to response bias. Future studies should include objective assessments of clinical performance to corroborate self-reported data. Future research should aim to replicate this study with a larger and more diverse cohort of LEDs over an extended period to validate these findings. Long-term follow-up studies are necessary to assess the sustained impact of the SIMBA programme on clinical practice and patient outcomes. Additionally, exploring the integration of SIMBA with other educational tools and its application in different medical specialties could provide further insights into its effectiveness and versatility.

Conclusion

SIMBA-LED is an innovative approach to postgraduate medical education for LEDs, garnering high acceptability and strong preference among participants. The model significantly boosts their confidence and core competencies, suggesting that integrating SIMBA into standard teaching curricula could support ongoing professional development and, ultimately, enhance patient outcomes.
