Mesenteric cyst: an unusual cause of recurrent abdominal pain in a child
Authors: Ibukun Adewumi Okunade, Nora Binalaifa Biu, Oluwole Dapo Adeogun, Mary Boluwatife Owolabi, James Olaniyi Adeniran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesenteric cysts are a rare cause of intra-abdominal swellings in the paediatric age group. They are benign lesions with non-specific presentations ranging from incidental findings to features of an acute abdomen. The rarity of the condition and the non-specificity of its symptoms underscore the importance of adequate knowledge for prompt diagnosis and appropriate management. Here we present a 5-year-old preschooler from the Southeastern part of Nigeria with recurring abdominal pain of 3 years’ duration. She was initially managed for symptomatic umbilical hernia and underwent herniorrhaphy. However, due to the persistence of her symptom, she was referred to the Paediatric Surgery Unit of the Teaching Hospital. Physical examination revealed a freely mobile soft mass in the right lumbar region, subsequently confirmed on imaging studies. She eventually had complete excision of the mass with a segmental jejunal resection and jejunojejunal anastomosis. The child is currently being followed up and has no evidence of recurrence.

Introduction

Mesenteric cysts are rare benign swellings that may arise from any part of the gastrointestinal mesentery, most commonly the small bowel, followed by the colonic mesentery, and less commonly the retroperitoneal space [1–3]. They are thought to result from mesenteric lymphatic channel obstruction, leading to abnormal communication between the lymphatic system.1 The incidence of mesenteric cyst is rare, with reported rates of approximately 1 in 20,000 among children and 1 in 100,000 among adults [1–3].

Although mesenteric cysts are often asymptomatic, they may present with non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms and can therefore be mistaken for conditions like small bowel obstruction, appendicitis, volvulus, ischemic bowel necrosis, or bowel perforation [1, 2]. Pre-operative diagnosis is usually made based on clinical and radiological findings; however, the diagnosis may be challenging because mesenteric cysts can mimic gastrointestinal duplications or cystic lymphangioma [1, 2]. A histologic diagnosis is needed to confirm a mesenteric cyst. A complete excision is usually the mainstay of treatment of mesenteric cysts to prevent recurrence [1–3]. The index case was reported because of the diagnostic challenge, as it was initially mistaken for an umbilical hernia and because of the rarity of the clinical condition.

Case report

A 5-year-old preschooler from the Southeastern part of Nigeria presented with recurring centrally located vague abdominal pain of 3 years’ duration. There was no associated vomiting, abdominal distension, constipation, or diarrhea. She had an umbilical herniorraphy on account of a similar complaint at 4 years. The parents were worried about the persistent pain despite having had surgery for the same complaint. Examination findings revealed a cooperative girl who was not in painful distress. She was not pale, anicteric, well hydrated, with no pedal edema. Her weight was 18 kg, which was 100% of the expected weight for her age. The abdominal examination revealed an infra-umbilical scar with no area of tenderness. A freely mobile soft mass measuring 6 cm by 16 cm was palpable in the central abdomen on a repeat examination. The differential diagnoses were mesenteric cysts, omental cysts, and abdominal lymphangioma. The abdominal ultrasound revealed a large multi-septated cystic lesion measuring 6.9 cm by 15.2 cm in the right flank, separated from the right kidney and seen displacing the bowel loops to the contralateral side; however, the exact origin could not be identified. An abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) also confirmed the same, with possible differential diagnoses of mesenteric cyst and omental cyst. Her blood work-up was essentially normal. She had minimal bowel preparation and subsequently had a laparotomy. Intra-operative findings revealed a 6 cm x 16 cm mesenteric cyst arising from the jejunal mesentery about 48 cm from the ligament of Treitz (Fig. 1). A segmental jejunal resection (Fig. 2) with jejuno-jejunal anastomosis (Fig. 3) was performed. The patient had her first bowel movement on postoperative day (POD) 2 and was commenced on oral feed on POD 3 and discharged on POD 4. The histopathologic examination showed multiloculated cysts with a wall composed of fibrofatty tissue (mesenteric wall) and small intestinal tissue w lined by flattened mesothelial cells. The overall picture was consistent with the histologic diagnosis of jejunal mesenteric cyst.

Discussion

Mesenteric cysts are rare benign mass lesions of the mesentery of the gastrointestinal tract with a low risk of malignant transformation [2, 4]. The historical background of mesenteric cysts dates back to 1507, when Benevenni, an Italian anatomist, first described the lesion during a post-mortem examination, and later in 1842, when Rokitansky reported a case of chylous mesenteric cyst, also following a post-mortem examination. However, it was not until 1880 that Tillaux successfully performed the first excision of a mesenteric cystic [5, 6]. The average incidence of mesenteric cysts is approximately 1 in 100,000 hospital admissions globally, and it occurs across all age groups, although they are more commonly found in the paediatric age group [3]. The male to female ratio is about 1:2 [5]. Mesenteric cysts are believed to arise from a proliferating ectopic lymphatic channel which failed to communicate with the rest of the lymphatic system [1]. They may present as solitary or multiple cysts containing serous, haemorrhagic, chylous, or purulent fluid and may be unilocular or multilocular [5]. Mesenteric cysts, particularly those of lymphatic origin, may also be associated with certain genetic anomalies, including Turner’s syndrome and Noonan syndrome, due to underlying lymphatic dysplasia [3]. However, our patient did not show phenotypic characteristics such as webbed neck, short stature, or cardiac defects to suggest these syndromes [7]. Therefore, the targeted screening tests were not pursued in the index case. Other risk factors of mesenteric cyst include a history of abdominal trauma or surgery, which may disrupt the local lymphatic systems of the gastrointestinal tract [3].

Mesenteric cysts often pose serious diagnostic challenges because of their rarity and the non-specific clinical presentation [3, 8]. In many cases, they are discovered incidentally during routine imaging studies or surgical procedures[3, 8]. In other instances, they may present with abdominal pain or an abdominal mass, as observed in the index case, or with other gastrointestinal symptoms, making differentiation from other causes of acute abdomen difficult [1, 2, 9]. Furthermore, difficulty in palpating the abdominal mass may lead to misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment, as seen in this case, who previously underwent umbilical herniorrhaphy. Mesenteric cysts are classified into four clinical types. Type I (pedicled) cysts grow rapidly and may undergo torsion or cause bowel volvulus. Type II (sessile) cysts are attached to the bowel wall and often require bowel resection, as seen in the index patient. Type III cysts extend to retroperitoneal structures, making complete dissection difficult, while type IV cysts involve both intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal structures and are associated with poorer prognosis [2]. The varying presentations and potential complications associated with these types make accurate diagnosis particularly important [3].

The need for accurate diagnosis of mesenteric cyst using radiological techniques becomes essential because of its rarity and the non-specificity of the symptoms [3, 8]. Mesenteric cysts are accurately and easily radiologically diagnosed with ultrasonography, as seen in this patient; however, the exact origin of the mass may not always be clearly defined [1, 3]. For a better characterization of the lesion and its relationship to surrounding structures, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be required [1–3, 10] Endoscopic ultrasound-guided needle aspiration or biopsy may also be useful in cases where imaging findings are inconclusive, or when malignancy cannot be excluded [8].

Management may be achieved either laparoscopically or through laparotomy [1]. The former is minimally invasive, and is associated with a short hospital stay and faster recovery; however, the latter is preferred for large cysts, extensive adhesions, or when bowel resection is anticipated [1]. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice because complications like haemorrhage, infection, torsion, and obstruction can occur if not operated on [2]. Alternative treatment modalities, including percutaneous drainage with sclerotherapy, deroofing procedures, and partial excision with marsupialization, have been described, but are associated with high recurrence rates and a possible risk of malignant transformation [5, 9, 10]. In the index case, complete excision of the cyst without bowel resection was not feasible because of its size and its proximity to the bowel wall [1]. A non-operative management with serial imaging surveillance may be considered in selected asymptomatic patients to monitor changes in size and characteristics of the cyst [3].

Although pre-operative investigations may strongly suggest the diagnosis, histopathological examination remains the gold standard for confirming mesenteric cysts. Histologically, the cysts contain flattened endothelial lining cells and dilated lymphatic channels with lymphocytic infiltration [10]. Mesenteric cysts are classified histologically into six major groups: lymphatic cyst, mesothelial cyst, where the index case belonged, enteric cyst, urogenital cyst, mature cystic teratoma, and pseudocyst [5, 10]. Enteric duplication cysts containing layers of intestine in the cyst wall, and mucosal lining with no lymphatic fluid, and omental cysts are close differentials [2, 10]. The prognosis of mesenteric cyst is generally excellent, with very low recurrence rates following complete excision [3, 4]. Nevertheless, post-operative follow-up every 6 to 12 months is advisable to monitor for recurrence and possible post-operative complications [3].

Limitations

In the index case, preoperative imaging could not accurately determine the cyst’s exact origin, reflecting the diagnostic challenges associated with mesenteric cysts in children. Also, advanced imaging modalities and immunohistochemical studies were not performed, which may have further improved diagnostic characterization.

Conclusion

Mesenteric cysts should be considered among the differential diagnoses of abdominal pain in children. Careful and gentle abdominal examination may aid in detecting an abdominal mass, which can easily be missed, particularly in a crying or uncooperative child. The rarity of the condition and the nonspecific nature of its presentation often make diagnosis difficult without appropriate radiological evaluation. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice.
