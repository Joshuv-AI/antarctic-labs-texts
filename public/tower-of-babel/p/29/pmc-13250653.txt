Mechanisms of postpartum metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in women with a history of gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Weiqin Li, Shuang Zhang, Yubei Huang, Rui Shi, Lingyan Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a rapidly increasing global health challenge that is closely linked to the rising rates of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Similarly, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as a leading cause of chronic liver disease. Emerging evidence indicates that a history of GDM is associated with a significantly elevated risk of postpartum MASLD that may persist for decades. This review synthesizes the shared and distinct pathophysiological mechanisms linking GDM to postpartum MASLD, with a particular focus on the enduring metabolic memory effect induced by GDM. While sharing common features with MASLD, the synergistic effects of amplified insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and oxidative stress during GDM profoundly impact hepatic lipid metabolism. Critically, this programmed vulnerability persists postpartum, causing persistent mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and reprogramming of the adipose–liver and gut–liver axes. These enduring alterations, extending beyond transient hyperglycemia, impair hepatic lipid metabolism and trigger MASLD. Further exploration into emerging mechanisms, including metabolomic profiles, genetic susceptibility, and gut microbiota dysbiosis, reveals a complex association network and highlights potential avenues for early detection and targeted intervention. Understanding these intricate mechanisms is crucial for developing effective intervention strategies. Future research focusing on gut microbiota modulation, precision epigenetic profiling, and multi-omics integration holds promise for dual prevention and management of both GDM and postpartum MASLD.HighlightsThis review synthesizes shared and distinct pathophysiological mechanisms linking GDM to postpartum MASLD. It highlights the critical and unique role of the metabolic memory effect of GDM, where persistent organelle dysfunction, inflammation, and epigenetic reprogramming predispose women with prior GDM to postpartum MASLD. This review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the GDM–MASLD transition, identifying key targets for dual prevention and management, with potential future strategies including gut microbiota modulation, precision epigenetic interventions, and multi-omics integration.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is diagnosed when hyperglycemia is first detected during pregnancy using a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), with threshold values (fasting ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1 h ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, 2 h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L) as recommended by the World Health Organization criteria (1). GDM represents a major public health concern, with a global prevalence of 15.6% (2) and a prevalence of 14.8% in China (3). GDM is not only a transient condition of pregnancy, but also a potent predictor of future metabolic disorders in both mothers and their offspring (4, 5, 6). Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is diagnosed according to the 2023 multi-society consensus led by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and the European Association for the Study of the Liver (7): the presence of hepatic steatosis (by imaging or histology) together with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (e.g. overweight/obesity, prediabetes/type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia), after excluding harmful alcohol intake (defined as <140 g/week for women and <210 g/week for men). MASLD is now recognized as the most common chronic liver disease globally, affecting approximately 30% of the adult population (8), with a prevalence of 29.2% in Chinese adults (9).

Although glucose metabolism typically normalizes postpartum in most women with prior GDM, substantial evidence indicates a significantly increased risk of developing MASLD after delivery. A nationwide cohort study from South Korea demonstrated that GDM confers an 83% increased risk of postpartum MASLD (adjusted odds ratio (OR): 1.83; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.65–2.03) (10). Similarly, the prospective HAPO follow-up study showed that each 1-SD increase in third-trimester glucose summary z-score was associated with a 3.2% absolute increase in estimated 6-year MASLD risk at 10–14 years postpartum (adjusted β: 3.2; 95% CI: 2.7–3.6) (11). Meta-analyses have further quantified this risk, with more recent evidence suggesting a stronger association. A 2024 systematic review reported an approximately 50% increased risk of MASLD after GDM (OR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.21–1.87) (12). A subsequent 2026 meta-analysis including the latest studies confirmed that women with a history of GDM had a 2.46-fold higher odds of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (OR: 2.46; 95% CI: 1.88–3.23). Notably, this study also demonstrated that the risk persists unchanged over decades postpartum, providing epidemiological support for the concept of metabolic memory (13). In addition, this Korean nationwide cohort study also found that hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were an independent risk factor (adjusted OR: 2.20; 95% CI: 1.81–2.67) and noted that pre-pregnancy hypertriglyceridemia may further elevate this risk (10). In the general population, established metabolic risk factors, such as advanced age, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and unhealthy diet, are also well-recognized contributors to MASLD (14, 15, 16).

Given that MASLD is closely associated with cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (17), understanding the mechanisms linking GDM to postpartum MASLD is crucial for improving long-term metabolic health in women with a history of GDM and for halting liver disease progression. Mechanistic studies have linked the high incidence of postpartum MASLD in women with prior GDM to multiple pathological mechanisms. These include persistent insulin resistance (18, 19), chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress (20, 21), and persistent metabolic reprogramming (22, 23, 24). These pathological alterations persist postpartum, chronically impairing hepatic lipid metabolism and ultimately driving the onset and progression of MASLD (25). Crucially, the GDM experience appears to ‘prime’ the liver and systemic metabolic pathways for future disease development, representing a departure from the typical gradual onset of MASLD. In addition, emerging mechanisms involving metabolomic profiles, genetic susceptibility, and gut microbiota dysbiosis reveal a complex association network between GDM and MASLD. This review synthesizes the pathophysiological mechanisms linking GDM and MASLD, emphasizing the unique contributions of GDM and its enduring metabolic legacy, aiming to provide evidence-based support for improving the long-term health outcomes of women with GDM.

To ensure a comprehensive review of the literature, we conducted searches in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases from inception to March 2026. Search terms included combinations of ‘gestational diabetes mellitus’, ‘GDM’, ‘metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease’, ‘MASLD’, ‘NAFLD’, ‘postpartum’, ‘metabolic memory’, ‘epigenetics’, and ‘gut–liver axis’. We prioritized original research articles and prospective cohort studies specifically examining GDM and postpartum MASLD outcomes, while also including high-quality mechanistic reviews where direct evidence was limited. Where direct evidence specific to the pregnancy/GDM/postpartum context was insufficient, we formulated inferences based on established general pathophysiological mechanisms of GDM and/or MASLD. Both human studies and animal model studies were considered to support mechanistic inferences.

Shared pathophysiological mechanisms

Both GDM and MASLD are fundamentally driven by metabolic dysregulation (26). The initial trigger for GDM is the inability of the maternal pancreas to compensate for the physiological insulin resistance of pregnancy. This underlying insulin resistance often persists postpartum, laying the groundwork for future metabolic disorders, including MASLD. However, in women with a GDM history, these common mechanisms are often amplified and persist with greater intensity due to the unique insults of pregnancy and the subsequent metabolic memory. The transition from transient gestational hyperglycemia to chronic hepatic fat accumulation involves a complex interplay of several interconnected mechanisms (Fig. 1). Insulin resistance and lipid metabolism dysregulation

Insulin resistance is a central pathophysiological feature shared by both GDM and MASLD (18, 26). During pregnancy, women naturally experience physiological insulin resistance to ensure adequate nutrient supply to the fetus. In GDM, this resistance is pathologically exacerbated and often persists into the postpartum period (18). This metabolic dysregulation manifests across multiple tissues through interrelated mechanisms, with evidence derived from distinct study populations.

First, in the liver, insulin plays a key role in suppressing very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion in healthy insulin-sensitive states (27, 28). In insulin-resistant states, this acute suppressive effect of insulin is blunted, leading to VLDL hypersecretion (27, 28). Second, in adipose tissue, impaired insulin signaling reduces its antilipolytic effect. This results in enhanced lipolysis and increased release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the circulation (26). Human studies suggest that in women with GDM, this inhibitory effect is significantly attenuated, leading to elevated FFA release and subsequent hepatic fat accumulation (19). Third, hyperinsulinemia is a compensatory response to peripheral insulin resistance. Studies in rat liver and human adipose tissue have shown that this chronic state of elevated insulin contributes to increased VLDL secretion through activation of SREBP-1c-driven de novo lipogenesis, which increases substrate availability for VLDL assembly (28, 29). Whether this mechanism contributes to VLDL hypersecretion specifically in GDM remains to be determined, although it is likely operational and may be intensified in this context.

Beyond insulin resistance, abnormalities in counter-regulatory hormones may further exacerbate metabolic dysregulation. GDM is associated with abnormal glucagon secretion. Evidence from GDM cohort studies shows that impaired glucagon suppression during an early postpartum oral glucose tolerance test predicts glucose intolerance at one year post-delivery (30), and that glucagon levels change significantly from pregnancy to postpartum alongside improvements in insulin sensitivity (31). Mechanistic evidence from animal models indicates that glucagon stimulates hepatic gluconeogenesis via cAMP/PKA signaling and promotes intrahepatic lipolysis (32). In the context of insulin resistance, an elevated glucagon-to-insulin ratio may further disrupt hepatic lipid metabolism and contribute to MASLD risk in the general population (33, 34), though direct evidence in GDM women is lacking and warrants further investigation.

Collectively, these four mechanisms – hepatic insulin resistance (with blunted suppression of VLDL secretion), adipose tissue lipolysis, hyperinsulinemia, and glucagon dysregulation – converge to overload the liver with lipids. Unable to efficiently export or oxidize this cumulative burden, the liver begins accumulating triglycerides within hepatocytes. This leads to hepatic steatosis, the hallmark of MASLD.

Chronic low-grade inflammation

Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation is involved in both GDM and MASLD. In GDM, dysfunctional adipose tissue exhibits increased macrophage infiltration, particularly in the presence of obesity. These macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) (35). One study reported that TNF-α mRNA expression in adipose tissue at delivery was 15–40% higher in women with GDM compared with body mass index (BMI)-matched controls. In addition, a separate study reported higher IL-1β levels in women with GDM on postpartum day 3 compared with controls (20). This inflammatory milieu – characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and immune cell activation – can persist after delivery and thereby influence long-term metabolic health. In a mouse model of GDM, dams exhibited hepatic inflammation and lipid accumulation at postpartum day 30 (23).

Based on evidence from general populations with insulin resistance (35, 36, 37), these pro-inflammatory cytokines interfere with insulin signaling. TNF-α impairs insulin receptor signaling via activation of the c-Jun N-terminal kinase/IκB kinase pathway and insulin receptor substrate-1 phosphorylation. Both TNF-α and IL-6 inhibit insulin signaling pathways, thereby exacerbating insulin resistance (35). In addition to inducing insulin resistance, these inflammatory factors directly participate in hepatic injury. Inflammation is considered a key ‘second hit’ driving the progression of MASLD to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis. The latter is characterized by hepatocellular injury, inflammation, and fibrosis (38). Mechanistically, TNF-α and IL-6 contribute to hepatocyte damage and promote fibrosis. They do so through hepatic inflammation and activation of the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway. TNF-α induces pro-inflammatory gene expression via NF-κB, forming a damaging feedback loop (35, 38). Although the above inflammatory mechanisms are well established in the context of insulin resistance and MASLD, their direct extrapolation to the pathway from GDM to MASLD remains inferential. Direct evidence from human cohorts linking GDM-specific inflammatory priming to subsequent MASLD development remains limited.

Oxidative stress

In GDM and MASLD, key pathogenic factors like hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and inflammation collectively promote excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (39). The precise mechanisms of hepatic oxidative stress in GDM remain less defined than in T2DM. However, given the shared metabolic disturbances of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, the well-established oxidative stress pathways in metabolic diseases are likely similarly operative in GDM. Direct evidence of persistent oxidative stress in the maternal liver following GDM pregnancy is currently lacking. Hepatic oxidative stress arises primarily from mitochondrial dysfunction due to electron leakage from the respiratory chain. Additional sources include microsomal cytochrome P450 enzymes (e.g. cytochrome P450 2E1) and NADPH oxidases. Meanwhile, hyperglycemia drives aberrant glucose metabolism through the protein kinase C, polyol, and hexosamine pathways, disrupting cellular redox balance and causing ROS accumulation (40, 41, 42, 43).

Hepatic FFA overload induces mitochondrial dysfunction, impairs β-oxidation, and establishes a vicious cycle where incomplete fatty acid oxidation generates more ROS (21). Mitochondria overwhelmed by FFA oxidation experience increased electron leakage, producing excessive ROS, and further exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction (39). In addition, sustained hyperglycemia promotes the formation and tissue accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which promote oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular dysfunction (39). Importantly, direct evidence from GDM pregnancies confirms that these oxidative stress pathways are activated during gestation. Specifically, placental and maternal tissues from GDM pregnancies show elevated oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde, 8-isoprostane) alongside compromised antioxidant defenses (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) relative to normoglycemic pregnancies (21). Thus, the shared mechanisms described above are operational in GDM pregnancy, though their persistence into the postpartum period has not been established. Nevertheless, given the central role of oxidative stress in driving hepatocyte injury, fibrosis, and MASLD progression in metabolic diseases (39), it is plausible that persistent oxidative stress – if it occurs – contributes to the elevated risk of MASLD in women with a history of GDM.

Hormonal fluctuations

Pregnancy involves significant elevations in estrogen. These hormonal changes have direct metabolic effects: progressive increases in plasma estrogen levels enhance hepatic VLDL production and reduce lipoprotein lipase (LPL) expression and activity (44, 45). LPL is a key enzyme that hydrolyzes circulating triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (46). Reduced LPL activity impairs triglyceride clearance, causing hypertriglyceridemia, which promotes hepatic ectopic lipid deposition and thus contributes to MASLD (47). In GDM, direct evidence from placental and adipose tissue studies demonstrates altered estrogen receptor signaling: estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) expression is elevated in placental tissues of GDM pregnancies (48), with cell type-specific upregulation in extravillous trophoblasts (49). Conversely, ERα and ERβ expression is reduced in subcutaneous adipose tissue (50). ERα and ERβ differ in function: ERα primarily regulates hepatic lipid oxidation and insulin sensitivity, whereas ERβ modulates inflammation and fibrosis (51). In MASLD, ERα downregulation impairs fatty acid metabolism, while ERβ reduction may worsen hepatic inflammation (51, 52). These alterations in estrogen receptor function, combined with the metabolic stresses of GDM, are associated with disrupted lipid metabolism and exacerbated insulin resistance (49).

Distinct role of persistent metabolic reprogramming induced by GDM

While the mechanisms contributing to MASLD are well documented, the development of MASLD in women with a history of GDM is uniquely and significantly amplified by the concept of metabolic memory effect. This phenomenon was initially described in type 1 diabetes and T2DM. It refers to pathological alterations induced by hyperglycemia or other acute metabolic stressors that persist long after the initial trigger is removed, causing lasting damage to target organs (53). Emerging evidence from a prospective cohort study confirms that women with GDM exhibit a metabolic memory phenomenon, characterized by persistent metabolic alterations that increase susceptibility to subsequent health risks even after postpartum glucose normalization (24). This phenomenon helps explain why women with a history of GDM face a significantly higher long-term risk of MASLD compared to women without GDM (10, 11). Persistent hepatic organelle dysfunction and oxidative stress

While the ‘Oxidative stress’ section focused on oxidative stress during GDM pregnancy, this section addresses how persistent organelle dysfunction sustains such abnormalities postpartum. Evidence from a lean GDM mouse model provides direct support for this mechanism: female mice with GDM exhibited higher hepatic triglyceride content and increased MASLD activity scores at 30 days postpartum, indicating persistent lipid accumulation and inflammation despite transient hyperglycemia during pregnancy (23). These findings suggest that GDM induces lasting damage to key intracellular organelles. Animal studies indicate that GDM exposure leads to persistent mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation, reduced fatty acid β-oxidation, and excessive ROS generation. Direct evidence in maternal liver remains limited. Most studies on GDM-induced persistent mitochondrial dysfunction have focused on offspring rather than the maternal liver (54, 55). Whether similar dysfunction persists in the maternal liver postpartum requires further investigation. Concurrently, evidence suggests that in MASLD, abnormal lipid accumulation and insulin resistance can induce severe ER stress in hepatocytes, interfering with proper protein folding and lipid metabolism (56). Mitochondrial and ER stress are interconnected: excessive ROS can exacerbate ER stress, while chronic ER stress, in turn, can impair mitochondrial function. Thus, sustained organelle dysfunction and oxidative stress can lead to hepatic insulin resistance, promote intrahepatic triglyceride accumulation, and drive chronic low-grade inflammation. This process thereby lays a cellular pathological foundation for MASLD development. Through this programmed vulnerability, these organelles remain compromised, making the liver less resilient to lipid overload even after hyperglycemia resolves.

Remodeling and dysfunction of the systemic metabolic environment

The metabolic impact of GDM extends beyond the liver, potentially inducing maladaptive alterations in other key systemic metabolic organs that persist postpartum and remotely influence hepatic function via endocrine or metabolic pathways. Several peripheral compartments have been implicated as potential mediators of postpartum MASLD risk in women with prior GDM. First, adipose tissue dysfunction, a well-established driver of hepatic steatosis, is characterized by abnormal adipokine secretion (e.g. reduced adiponectin) (57). Whether GDM induces persistent adipose tissue dysfunction postpartum remains unknown, but it is plausible that dysfunctional adipose tissue could continue releasing pro-inflammatory mediators and abnormal concentrations of adipokines, even after weight stabilization or dietary improvement. Second, emerging evidence directly links GDM to persistent gut microbiota alterations: a recent study found that women with GDM exhibited significantly different abundance of 14 microbial taxa at two months postpartum compared to non-GDM controls (58). Based on established gut–liver axis mechanisms in MASLD, gut dysbiosis can compromise intestinal barrier integrity, allowing bacteria-derived metabolites (e.g. lipopolysaccharide) to enter the liver via the portal vein (59). In theory, this could activate hepatic immune cells and induce inflammatory responses, further aggravating insulin resistance and lipid accumulation (59, 60). Whether this mechanism operates specifically in postpartum women with prior GDM remains to be directly investigated.

The brain–liver axis represents a third, less explored pathway that could link GDM to postpartum MASLD. The hypothalamus contains insulin and leptin receptors and is key to metabolic homeostasis (61). During normal pregnancy, the hypothalamus undergoes adaptive suppression of insulin signaling in the arcuate and ventromedial nuclei (62). Animal studies have shown that liver-innervating vagal neurons are essential for hepatic steatosis development (63). Thus, it is plausible that in GDM, metabolic disturbances disrupt this central adaptation, potentially influencing hepatic metabolism via vagal pathways. However, no direct evidence yet supports this mechanism in GDM, making the brain–liver axis an important direction for future investigation.

Epigenetic modifications mediating molecular ‘memory’

Persistent metabolic stressors during GDM can trigger stable and heritable epigenetic modifications within hepatocytes and other relevant cells. This hypothesis is grounded in studies showing that diabetic pregnancy induces CpG hypermethylation of key metabolic genes (e.g. Srebf2) in the fetal liver, associated with decreased gene expression (64). Whether similar epigenetic alterations occur in maternal tissues – such as altered DNA methylation patterns, histone acetylation, or microRNA expression changes – remains largely unknown, as direct evidence in postpartum maternal liver is currently lacking (24). These modifications are hypothesized to ‘encode’ adverse GDM-period signals into the cellular epigenome, forming a long-term ‘memory’ that persists even after glucose levels normalize. Thus, epigenetic modifications could represent a potential mechanism linking GDM to subsequent MASLD, potentially consolidating the liver’s propensity for lipid accumulation, inflammation, and insulin resistance, though this remains to be tested in maternal liver.

Emerging mechanistic and intervention directions

Recent research has expanded our understanding of the GDM–MASLD link by revealing new perspectives on their pathological connection in several areas.

Metabolomic biomarkers

Metabolomic studies have identified several candidate biomarkers that may facilitate early risk stratification for postpartum MASLD in women with prior GDM. In the general population, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index – a surrogate marker of insulin resistance – predicts MASLD risk with superior performance compared to other metabolic indicators (65, 66). A cohort study demonstrated that women with a history of GDM have a significantly higher postpartum TyG index compared to those without GDM (67), suggesting that this simple index could help identify GDM women at a higher metabolic risk. Furthermore, alterations in lipid-related metabolites (e.g. glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids) have been observed in MASLD patients (68), and similar disturbances in lipid metabolism have been reported in GDM (69), pointing to shared metabolic derangements. Complementary evidence from a GDM rodent model shows that postpartum persistence of impaired glucose tolerance is associated with metabolites predictive of early insulin resistance and liver dysfunction (22). These findings inform the development of predictive biomarkers and early intervention strategies, though direct validation in GDM women is needed.

Genetic factors

Genetic factors play significant roles in both GDM and MASLD pathogenesis. Increasing research has focused on potential shared genetic susceptibility between the two conditions. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ), a ligand-activated transcription factor of the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, regulates hepatic lipid metabolism and inflammation in general populations (70, 71). A common polymorphism in this gene, rs1801282, is associated with GDM risk (72). A prospective cohort study demonstrated that T2D risk alleles, including PPARG variants, predict postpartum diabetes in women with prior GDM (73). Based on these findings, it is hypothesized that specific PPAR-γ polymorphisms may contribute to postpartum MASLD development in women with prior GDM, potentially through persistent effects on insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism beyond the postpartum period. Thus, PPAR-γ polymorphisms may serve as a genetic link between GDM and postpartum MASLD, though ethnic-specific effects warrant further validation. However, existing evidence on the rs1801282-GDM association shows ethnic heterogeneity. A meta-analysis revealed that this polymorphism shows no association with GDM risk in Caucasians, yet correlates with reduced GDM risk in Asians – most notably in the Chinese population, though this association does not hold for Koreans (72). These ethnic differences may reflect variations in dietary patterns, lifestyle, or linkage disequilibrium with other functional variants.

PPAR-γ exists in isoforms (PPAR-γ1, PPAR-γ2) with distinct tissue distributions based on basic biological studies (74). Future studies should validate these genetic associations in well-characterized GDM cohorts and elucidate the role of PPAR-γ polymorphisms – along with other variants affecting lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses – in the GDM-to-MASLD transition. Such genetic factors may facilitate early risk stratification for targeted intervention.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis

The role of gut microbiota in metabolic diseases is increasingly recognized. GDM can alter the gut microenvironment, leading to dysbiosis, characterized by reduced beneficial bacteria (e.g. Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) and increased harmful bacteria (e.g. Escherichia coli, Enterococcus) (60, 75). Emerging evidence suggests that GDM-induced gut dysbiosis may persist postpartum. A study comparing women with prior GDM to healthy controls found significant differences in gut microbiota composition after delivery, with 14 microbial taxa showing significantly different abundance between the two groups (58).

Based on evidence from general populations and animal models, dysbiosis compromises gut barrier function, allowing harmful substances such as endotoxins to enter the bloodstream and activate the hepatic immune system and inflammatory responses. Endotoxins bind to receptors on liver cells, triggering inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine release (59, 76). Concurrently, dysbiosis affects bile acid metabolism. Animal studies of GDM have shown that gut dysbiosis alters the bile acid pool composition, reducing signaling through farnesoid X receptor and Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (77). This impairs hepatic lipid oxidation and promotes steatosis. Bile acids are crucial regulators of lipid metabolism; their dysregulation further impairs hepatic lipid handling, potentially promoting MASLD development (78).

Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have investigated probiotic interventions in GDM and MASLD populations (Table 2). In GDM patients, a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs showed that multi-strain probiotic supplementation (e.g. Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) in GDM patients for 4–8 weeks significantly reduced fasting blood glucose, insulin resistance, and total cholesterol levels (79). In asymptomatic postpartum women with prior GDM, a 12-week probiotic cocktail (containing six strains including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) showed significant reductions in fasting glucose, HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein levels (80). In general MASLD populations, several clinical trials have shown that probiotics, especially multi-strain formulations, can reduce liver enzyme levels, improve glycolipid metabolism markers and certain inflammatory factors, and alleviate intestinal dysfunction (81). However, direct evidence for MASLD prevention in post-GDM women remains limited. Based on the mechanisms discussed above – persistent gut dysbiosis, impaired gut barrier function, and altered bile acid signaling – it is hypothesized that targeted modulation of GDM-associated gut dysbiosis through probiotics, prebiotics, or synbiotics may represent a promising strategy to improve liver markers and prevent MASLD. Future prospective studies focusing on strain-specific effects and long-term outcomes are needed for validation.

Lifestyle intervention research

Given the high incidence of postpartum MASLD in women with prior GDM, lifestyle interventions represent crucial preventive and therapeutic strategies. In the general population, appropriate dietary interventions and physical exercise positively impact MASLD prevention (82). Evidence from general MASLD populations shows that weight loss improves metabolic disorders and liver injury in patients with MASLD in a dose-dependent manner (17). Gradual weight loss of 3–5% within one year can reverse steatosis; loss of 7–10% can ameliorate steatohepatitis (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis); loss > 10% can reverse fibrosis; and loss > 15% may even ameliorate T2DM (17). MASLD patients with normal BMI also typically benefit from appropriate weight loss (17). These findings establish the feasibility of lifestyle-based strategies for MASLD management in the general population.

However, there have been no large-scale RCTs investigating whether implementing lifestyle interventions in women with a history of GDM can effectively reduce the risk of developing and progressing to MASLD. In the field of diabetes prevention, landmark studies in the general population have demonstrated that early intensive glycemic control confers health benefits lasting decades, even after intensive therapy is discontinued (83, 84, 85). In women with prior GDM, an RCT confirms that lifestyle intervention initiated postpartum significantly reduces the incidence of T2DM, supporting the importance of timely intervention in this population (86). Based on this evidence, it is reasonable to hypothesize that lifestyle interventions may similarly reduce postpartum MASLD risk in women with prior GDM.

When should lifestyle intervention be initiated? The concept of programmed metabolic vulnerability has important clinical implications for intervention timing. According to developmental programming literature, the critical window for metabolic reprogramming spans the gestational period and the first three years postpartum (87). For women with prior GDM, the peak risk of developing diabetes occurs 3–6 years postpartum (5), coinciding with the tail end of this critical window. Therefore, it is hypothesized that early initiation of lifestyle intervention within this critical window – i.e. a continuous strategy spanning pregnancy through the first three years postpartum – is essential for preventing the consolidation of adverse metabolic programming and achieving sustained metabolic benefits (87, 88). This suggests that, despite the normalization of postpartum glucose levels in most women with GDM, individualized lifestyle interventions should be extended beyond the immediate postpartum period and maintained over time.

Conclusion and perspectives

The development of postpartum MASLD in women with prior GDM is driven by a convergence of mechanisms including amplified insulin resistance, primed inflammation, persistent oxidative stress, and hormonal fluctuations. The metabolic reprogramming initiated during GDM perpetuates and consolidates abnormalities in metabolic pathways and organelle function. Even with normalized blood glucose, hepatic and systemic metabolic capacity for lipid metabolism may remain epigenetically and structurally impaired, ultimately triggering MASLD. However, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding metabolomics, genetic factors, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and lifestyle interventions. Future research should prioritize defining the precise molecular mechanisms by which GDM establishes persistent metabolic alterations in the liver, particularly epigenetic modifications, organelle dysfunction, and systemic metabolic signaling. Leveraging multi-omics findings to develop more precise prediction models and early diagnostic methods is essential for early identifying high-risk individuals. Novel therapeutic agents and optimized lifestyle intervention protocols tailored to the GDM postpartum population are needed. A three-tiered prevention and control system – encompassing early prediction, intensive prevention, and personalized treatment – holds promise for improving the long-term metabolic outcomes in women with GDM and mitigating the burden of postpartum MASLD.
