Modular Inhibitor Approach to Map the Catalytic Trajectory of an Iterative Siderophore Synthetase, DesD
Authors: Collin E. Merrick, Ketan D. Patel, Parismita Kalita, Nicole Gulati, Andrew M. Gulick, Timothy A. Wencewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Under iron limiting conditions, bacteria biosynthesize and secrete small molecule iron chelators, siderophores, to scavenge this essential metal. Siderophores are biosynthesized by nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS) or NRPS independent siderophore (NIS) synthetases, the latter of which are significantly less studied. Streptomyces spp. utilizes an iterative NIS synthetase, DesD, to produce desferrioxamine type siderophores through dimerization, trimerization, and (in some cases) macrocyclization of monomers such as N 1-hydroxy-N 1-succinyl cadaverine (HSC) and N 1-hydroxy-N 1-acetyl cadaverine (HAC). Prior work has utilized an acyl-sulfamoyl adenosine (AMS) inhibitor of monomeric HSC (HSC-AMS) to interrogate the initial adenylation reaction of HSC. However, much is still unknown about how the enzyme active site accommodates substrates of varying sizes during further oligomerization reactions. To answer this question, AMS analogs of the monomer (HSC-AMS), dimer (HSC-HSC-AMS), and trimer (HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS) were chemically synthesized. Biochemical results from in vitro DesD reactions, IC50 assays, and isothermal titration calorimetry along with structural studies conducted via cocrystallization inform an updated mechanistic model for the iterative DesD catalytic cycle. The acyl adenylate motif in the growing substrate chain drives tight binding in the enzyme active site while the N-terminal HSC units dynamically sample conformations en route to terminating macrocyclization of the HSC-HSC-HSC trimer.

Introduction

Siderophores, low molecular weight ferric ion chelators, are crucial metal scavengers for bacteria and fungi during iron limiting conditions. The active transport of iron bound siderophores across the microbial cell membrane has been applied to the targeted delivery of antibiotics, fluorophores, and radioisotopes for therapeutic and diagnostic purposes. − The recent FDA approval of the siderophore-cephalosporin conjugate cefiderocol has shown the clinical utility of this strategy toward treating bacterial infections. − Further insight on the biosynthesis, transport, and utilization of microbial siderophores is needed to inform new applications in siderophore-based therapeutic and diagnostic agents.

Siderophore biosynthesis falls into two major categories: 1) the modular nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) with covalent intermediates shuttled between enzyme active sites via phosphopantetheine transthioesterifications; 2) the NRPS independent siderophore synthetases (NISs) which rely on the free diffusion of substrates and intermediates. − Historically, the NISs are relatively understudied compared to the NRPSs. The NISs from Streptomyces produce the hallmark family of trihydroxamate siderophores known as the desferrioxamines (DFOs) using a conserved block of biosynthetic genes, desABCD. Desferrioxamine B (DFOB) is approved for human use as a treatment for iron overload disease. Starting from l-Lys, the DesABC enzyme cluster produces the key intermediates N 1-hydroxy-N 1-succinyl cadaverine (HSC) and N 1-hydroxy-N 1-acetyl cadaverine (HAC). Tandem decarboxylation (DesA), N-hydroxylation (DesB), and acylation (DesC) with either acetyl-CoA or succinyl-CoA provides HAC or HSC, respectively. , The auxiliary genes, desG and desH, have been proposed to encode for a hydrolase (DesG) and acyltransferase (DesH) that balance the pool of HAC and HSC. These building blocks form the basis of the biosynthesis of the natural products DFOB (HSC-HSC-HAC), DFOG (HSC-HSC-HSC), and DFOE (cy-HSC-HSC-HSC) via iterative adenylation and condensation reactions catalyzed by DesD (Figure ).

The active site of DesD contains a nucleotide binding site with adjacent ligand binding sites for HSC-type substrates used for the sequential adenylation and condensation reactions. , As demonstrated mechanistically for the NISs IucA, NISs display ordered ligand binding where ATP binds first followed by substrates for adenylation and condensation. , The free carboxyl group of HSC is activated by formation of a reactive acyl adenylate with the formation of pyrophosphate (PPi) that remains trapped in the nucleotide binding site in a well-conserved nucleotide binding motif preserved in all characterized structures. The positioning of this intermediate HSC-AMP acyl adenylate in DesD was structurally characterized using a rationally designed acyl sulfamoyl adenosine (AMS) inhibitor, HSC-AMS (Figure ). The exact location of the condensation ligand binding site is unknown. Presumably, the HSC substrate in this site is positioned for nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl of the HSC-AMP to form homodimeric HSC-HSC with release of AMP and PPi. In the next cycle of biosynthesis, the newly formed HSC-HSC serves as a substrate for adenylation followed by condensation with a third molecule of HSC or an alternate nucleophile such as HAC to provide HSC-HSC-HSC or HSC-HSC-HAC, respectively. This sequence of reactions has been interrogated experimentally through the LC-MS/MS-based analysis of in vitro DesD reactions using combinations of synthetic HSC-HSC, HSC-HAC, HSC, and HAC containing stable isotope tracers. The HSC-HSC-HAC product is ‘capped’ with no site for further adenylation. However, HSC-HSC-HSC can be further adenylated and undergo intramolecular macrolactamization to form cy-HSC-HSC-HSC and terminate the catalytic cycle. This step indicates that HSC-HSC-HSC is large enough to occupy both the adenylation and condensation ligand binding sites.

Here, a modular HSC-AMS inhibitor-based approach was employed to trap intermediate steps of the catalytic cycle of DesD from Streptomyces griseoflavus. Additional data for DesD from Streptomyces coelicolor is also presented (see Supporting Information). The AMS mimics (HSC-AMS, HSC-HSC-AMS, and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS) of all three putative acyl adenylate intermediates (HSC-AMP, HSC-HSC-AMP, and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP) were synthesized (Figure ) and characterized with multiple techniques. The biochemical and structural studies described here are used to provide an updated model for the iterative DesD catalytic cycle during conversion of HSC into cy-HSC-HSC-HSC. These findings provide new insight into the structure–function relationships of NISs that will inform future enzyme mechanism studies, NIS inhibitor design, and genome mining for new NIS natural products. Improved mechanistic knowledge of NISs are also of interest for biocatalyst development and design of antivirulence agents targeting siderophore biosynthesis. Additionally, elucidation of the sequence, structure, function relationship of NIS synthetases will facilitate the accurate prediction of uncharacterized biosynthetic gene clusters that harbor NIS synthetases of unknown substrate specificity or the ability to catalyze single, multiple, or iterative reactions.

Results

Design and Synthesis of Modular AMS Inhibitors of DesD

The general synthetic strategy for HSC-based AMS inhibitors was inspired by the previous synthesis of HSC-AMS and danoxamine, the HSC-HSC-HSC analog that replaces the terminal amine with a hydroxyl group. , The sequence proceeded through the Boc-protection of commercially available 5-amino-1-pentanol (1) to provide the N-Boc-5-amino-1-pentanol (2) in 78% yield. Conversion of N-Boc-5-amino-1-pentanol (2) to the corresponding mesylate followed by SN2 substitution with TrocNHOBn and NaH as base provided the orthogonally protected cadaverine intermediate 3 in 75% yield over 2 steps. One-pot reductive Troc deprotection and acid-catalyzed succinylation produced N-Boc-O-Bn-HSC (4) in 92% yield. Treatment of compound 4 with anhydrous TFA followed by deprotonation with Et3N provided O-Bn-HSC that is utilized without purification as the amine nucleophile in subsequent acylation reactions. Treatment of O-Bn-HSC with CbzCl provided N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC (5) in 62% yield over 2 steps (Figure a).

Synthesis of precursors, control compounds, and AMS inhibitors of DesD. (a) Synthesis of protected HSC precursors N-Boc-O-Bn-HSC (4) and N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC (5). (b) Synthesis of bis-TBS-AMS (9) and free AMS (10). (c) Modular synthesis of HSC-AMS (16), HSC-HSC-AMS (17), and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS (18).

Treatment of adenosine (6) with excess TBSCl provided tris-TBS-adenosine 7 in 65% yield (Figure b). , Selective deprotection of the 5′-O-TBS group of 7 was achieved in 87% yield using a 2:1:1 THF:TFA:H2O mixture at 0 °C to provide bis-TBS-adenosine 8. Acylation of the free 5′–OH of bis-TBS-adenosine 8 with sulfamoyl chloride provided bis-TBS-AMS 9 in 43% yield. The free AMS compound 10 was obtained in 88% yield by treatment of compound 9 with TBAF. The protected intermediate bis-TBS-AMS 9 was used as the nucleophile in subsequent acylation reactions to provide protected mono-HSC-, di-HSC-, and tri-HSC-AMS analogs.

The modular synthesis of HSC oligomers was achieved via an acylation reaction sequence using O-Bn-HSC as the nucleophile (Figure c). N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC 5 was converted to the corresponding NHS ester and reacted with O-Bn-HSC (freshly prepared from TFA-treated 4) in Et3N/DCM to provide dimeric N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC-HSC 11 in 65% yield over 2 steps. Similarly, dimeric N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC-HSC 11 was converted to the NHS ester and condensed with O-Bn-HSC to provide trimeric N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC-HSC-HSC 12 in 27% yield over 2 steps. The monomeric- (5), dimeric- (11), and trimeric- (12) N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC analogs were converted to the corresponding bis-TBS-protected acyl AMS analogs 13 (26%), 14 (79%), and 15 (99%), respectively, via a two-step protocol of 1) NHS ester activation with EDC/NHS and 2) condensation with bis-TBS-AMS 9 in the presence of Cs2CO3 as base. Full deprotection of AMS analogs 13, 14, and 15 was achieved via sequential treatment with TBAF and H2/Pd­(OH)2 to provide HSC-AMS 16, HSC-HSC-AMS 17, and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS 18 in 15%, 78%, and 29% yield, respectively. All final AMS analogs (10, 16, 17, and 18) were fully characterized via NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C, COSY, TOCSY, NOESY, HSQC, and HMBC) and the molecular formulas were confirmed by high-resolution MS analysis. Prior to use in biochemical assays, the final purity of all synthetic compounds was validated to be >95% via LC-MS (see Supporting Information).

Inhibition of DesD by Mono-, Di-, and Tri-HSC-AMS Acyl Adenylate Mimics

The ability of each AMS compound to inhibit the biosynthetic activity of DesD was interrogated utilizing a previously established IC50 protocol based on LC-MS analysis of in vitro reaction mixtures. In line with previous reports, the AMS motif alone (10) was unable to inhibit DesD at concentrations as high as 150 μM (Figure S1). The newly synthesized batch of HSC-AMS produced significantly greater inhibition of DesD (apparent IC50 = 1.69 ± 0.05 μM – this work) compared to previously reported batches of HSC-AMS (apparent IC50 = 50 ± 3 μM for DesD) (Figure a), which we attribute to an improvement in the synthesis and diastereomeric purity of HSC-AMS. The use of freshly prepared bis-TBS-AMS (9) provided diastereomerically pure samples of N-Cbz-O-Bn-HSC-bis-TBS-AMS (13) as supported by NMR analysis (see Supporting Information). The improved purity was retained in the final deprotected HSC-AMS product (16). This batch of HSC-AMS had >95% diastereomeric purity compared to the previously reported batch that was ∼ 67% pure. Additionally, the use of A260 to quantify the concentration of HSC-AMS stock solutions improved the accuracy of the IC50 measurements.

The same LC-MS protocol was used to determine the apparent IC50 values of HSC-HSC-AMS (17) and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS (18) (Figure a). Surprisingly, HSC-HSC-AMS (1.38 ± 0.03 μM) and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS (3.22 ± 0.06 μM) produced apparent IC50 values that were roughly equivalent to the potency of HSC-AMS (1.69 ± 0.05 μM). These results were validated using a DesD homologue from S. coelicolor which produced apparent IC50 values of (1.49 ± 0.06 μM, 1.05 ± 0.03 μM, and 1.81 ± 0.09 μM) for HSC-AMS, HSC-HSC-AMS, and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS, respectively (Figure S1). These results stood in stark contrast to the expected trend of increasing potency with increasing ligand size. This led to additional ligand binding studies to test if the apparent binding affinities (Kd) change with ligand size.

Ligand Binding of DesD to Mono-, Di-, and Tri-HSC-AMS Acyl Adenylate Mimics

To test the hypothesis that the extended chains of HSC-HSC-AMS and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS provide additional positive binding interactions with the active site of DesD, the thermodynamics of ligand binding were probed by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). An ITC method was optimized for the titration of inhibitor with DesD to provide stable isotherms (Figures b and S2). The inclusion of pyrophosphate (PPi) was previously shown to stabilize the DesD inhibitor complex; hence, excess PPi was included in the ITC buffer. As expected, mono-, di-, and tri-HSC-AMS inhibitors all demonstrated nanomolar binding affinities for DesD. The apparent trend in binding affinities were: HSC-HSC-AMS (apparent Kd = 6 ± 1 nM) > HSC-AMS (apparent Kd = 11 ± 1 nM) > HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS (apparent Kd = 20 ± 4 nM). This is consistent with the trend in apparent IC50 values (Figure a). There was a slight deviation (N = ∼ 0.47–0.87) from the expected N = 1 value for a one-to-one inhibitor:enzyme binding event which may be rationalized by inhibitor decomposition. The ITC experiments required extensive incubation time of the inhibitors at 25 °C in aqueous solution which allows for some hydrolysis of the inhibitor AMS moiety. The di-HSC-AMS and tri-HSC-AMS inhibitors are prone to intramolecular cyclization to the corresponding cy-HSC-HSC and cy-HSC-HSC-HSC macrolactams as observed by LC-MS (Figure S3). The addition of enzyme throughout the experiment may also promote macrocyclization by positioning the amino group near the acyl sulfonamide in the enzyme active site. DesD is known to be a functional dimer in solution. To rule out any changes in the oligomeric state of DesD upon inhibitor binding, mass photometry experiments were carried out with variable inhibitor concentrations (Figure c). While size exclusion chromatography (SEC) is generally used to study protein oligomeric states, the in-flow nature of this technique is not well suited for catching transient or ligand concentration dependent interactions. Mass photometry, however, involves the direct scanning of a droplet that allows for ligand concentrations to be carefully controlled for the entirety of the experiment. These studies demonstrated that DesD is a stable dimer in both the presence and absence of HSC-based AMS inhibitors.

Structure of DesD Bound to di-HSC-AMS

Initial attempts of cocrystallization with HSC-HSC-AMS ligand resulted in poor electron density for the second HSC moiety of the ligand. Multiple crystallization trials and the inclusion of 5 mM inorganic PPi in the crystallization buffer improved the electron density for the second HSC moiety. DesD crystallized less readily in the presence of HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS and electron density in the crystals that diffracted well did not show evidence of the bound ligand. The structure of DesD bound to HSC-HSC-AMS was solved in space group C2221 with five chains in the asymmetric unit. The five proteins chains are organized into dimers in the asymmetric unit, as four chains form two dimers, while the fifth chain forms a dimer with a symmetry-related chain. The structure closely resembled the DesD:HSC-AMS structure (PDB: 7TGM) (Figure a). Superposition of an individual chain from the two structures yielded a rms displacement of 0.16 Å over 552 Cα atoms, demonstrating minimal conformational variability upon the binding of HSC-HSC-AMS compared to monomeric HSC-AMS. Contiguous density extending from the AMS group to the succinate moiety of the second HSC unit was observed in three of the five protomers of the structure (Figure S5). Notably, the density for the cadaverine moiety of the second HSC unit was weaker but still of sufficient quality to allow tentative placement of the terminal cadaverine moiety toward Glu464 (Figure b). To examine the dimeric structures, we further compared the structure of DesD bound to HSC-HSC-AMS with a dimer of the unliganded structure (PDB: 7TGJ), which resulted in an overall rms displacement of 0.32 Å over 1006 Cα atoms. The comparison of the HSC-HSC-AMS bound DesD dimer with DesD bound to HSC-AMS (PDB: 7TGM) resulted in an overall rms displacement of 0.21 Å over 1112 Cα atoms. The DesD thus remains stable in the presence of the different ligands.

Two dynamic loops, the ATP-binding (AB) and substrate-binding (SB) loops, cover the active site pocket of NIS synthetases. In structurally characterized members of this enzyme family, these loops adopt multiple positions and are often disordered. As observed previously with the HSC-AMS bound structure, the DesD:HSC-HSC-AMS structure revealed that the AB loop adopts a closed conformation; the SB-loop also remained ordered in all five protein chains within the asymmetric unit (Figure a/ Figure S4a). Interestingly, residues Asp571 to Ser576, which constitute a segment of the SB-loop, are displaced toward the HSC-HSC-AMS ligand in all five chains (Figure c/ Figure S4b), suggesting a ligand-induced conformational adjustment of the SB-loop to facilitate the second HSC substrate.

The sulfamoyl group of the AMS moiety from HSC-AMS or HSC-HSC-AMS shows high quality electron density and provides a view of the adenylate intermediate. Previously, three structures of DesD were solved, bound to HSC-AMS and Pi (7TGM), with ATP (7TGK), or with AMP and PPi (7TGL). The HSC-HSC-AMS structure was also crystallized with PPi. All three structures that contain ions in the β- and γ-phosphate positions highlight a second Mg2+ ion, which makes interactions with Glu463 and Glu464. Adjacent to this loop is Lys459 that interacts with the γ-phosphate and Asp460, which together form a conserved Mg2+ ion binding motif. The bond between the HSC and sulfamoyl groups mimics the newly formed bond between the carboxylate substrate and AMP. This bond is sandwiched between the side chains of Arg303 and His440, which are both present in all characterized NIS synthetases. These two residues neutralize the charge build up from the approach of the carboxylate on the anionic phosphate in the initial adenylation reaction. A large cavity remains that provides space for the incoming nucleophilic amine of the attacking HSC molecule in the second partial reaction catalyzed by each DesD cycle (Figure b).

Comparison of the structures of DesD bound to the inhibitors that contain a single or dimeric HSC groups revealed binding modes for mono-HSC and di-HSC adenylated substrates in the large DesD substrate-binding pocket (Figure b). The first HSC of all HSC-HSC-AMS structures aligns very well with the HSC group in the previous monomeric HSC-AMS-bound structure. The second HSC molecule in five chains of the DesD:HSC-HSC-AMS structure exhibits greater conformational flexibility when compared to the first unit. Overall, the electron density for the second HSC unit is weaker. The succinate group of the second HSC molecule from all five chains adopts a consistent position, while the terminal cadaverine moiety adopts different positions in the large substrate-binding pocket. In the HSC-AMS bound structure, Asp497 makes an ionic interaction with the terminal amine group of the first HSC substrate, which likely pulls the end of the HSC group toward the side chain (Figure c). While we are aware of the resolution of the structures and the disorder in some ligands, we measured the interactions to assess the strength of the interactions; in the chains with the best ordered ligands, this interaction is 2.6 – 3.1 Å. In contrast, in the HSC-HSC-AMS structure, this nitrogen, which now forms the amide linkage between the two HSC units, forms a weak hydrogen bond of ∼ 3.3 – 3.5 Å with the side chain of Ser556. While the ionic interaction between the first HSC unit and Asp497 is lost, the terminal amine of the second HSC unit is now positioned for ionic interaction with Glu464 with interactions of 3.0 – 3.4 Å. None of the protein side chains move; instead, this shift in interactions reflects a repositioning of the HSC-HSC portion of the AMS inhibitor. The dimeric structure does not appear likely to influence the binding of the ligand as the terminal amine of the HSC-HSC-AMS ligand is >25 Å from the closest atoms of the alternate chain.

Interestingly, despite the high polar content of the HSC-HSC group, no other hydrogen bonding or ionic interactions between this group and the protein are observed. Instead, the hydroxamate and cadaverine components of the second HSC molecule were positioned within a hydrophobic cradle formed by the side chains of residues Ala436, Val466, Met468, Val485, Pro575, Leu579, Leu492 and Ser493 (Figure S4a). The helix formed from Met489 to Asp497 forms a wall at the base of the DesD active site pocket that forces the second HSC moiety to turn, directing the terminal cadaverine back toward the nucleotide within the pocket. The increased flexibility of this cadaverine suggests that the pocket may be designed to avoid the nucleophilic attack of the second HSC amine group and formation of cy-HSC-HSC macrolactam (also known as bisucaberin). Alternately, the pocket may simply reflect the need to be able to adenylate HSC monomers, dimers, and trimers, which all bind to allow the carboxylate to attack the ATP, leaving room for a nucleophilic HSC to bind and attack the adenylate as a free molecule or as the terminal group in HSC-HSC-HSC necessary for the formation of the cy-HSC-HSC-HSC macrolactam.

Substrate, Intermediate, and Product Pool of DesD is Dynamic

Analysis of the in vitro DesD reactions via LC-MS provides useful insight on the dynamics of the substrate, intermediate, and product pools in the presence of AMS inhibitors. The extracted ion counts (EICs) for the expected [M + H]+ molecular ions of HSC, HSC-HSC, cy-HSC-HSC, HSC-HSC-HSC, and cy-HSC-HSC-HSC were analyzed in the presence of variable inhibitor concentrations ranging from 0 – 12.5 μM (Figure a). The EICs were summed and the percent contribution of each species to the total ion pool was determined for HSC-AMS (Figure b), HSC-HSC-AMS (Figure c), and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS (Figure d). Variation of the inhibitor concentrations at a single time point (35 min) acts as a functional surrogate for probing the time-dependent formation of products by decreasing the fraction of enzyme that is actively turning over. A consistent trend was observed in which decreasing inhibitor concentrations resulted in increased consumption of HSC to form HSC-HSC-HSC. At sufficiently low inhibitor concentration, the accumulation of HSC-HSC-HSC allowed for competitive binding to the enzyme active site and formation of cy-HSC-HSC-HSC. Notably, HSC-HSC and cy-HSC-HSC were not observed under these reaction conditions. These results were recapitulated with the DesD homologue from S. coelicolor (Figure S3). In the absence of inhibitor, HSC-HSC-HSC dominates the product pool with a smaller fraction of cy-HSC-HSC-HSC. These results suggest that the DesD-catalyzed condensation of HSC-HSC and HSC to form linear HSC-HSC-HSC is faster than the DesD-catalyzed macrolactamization of HSC-HSC-HSC to form cy-HSC-HSC-HSC.

Analysis of the DesD product pool dynamics during inhibition. (a) The HSC-derived metabolites were monitored by LC-MS using in vitro reactions of DesD with variable inhibitor concentrations. The graphs show normalized extracted ion counts (EICs) that reflect the relative concentrations of substrates, intermediates, and products in the presence of variable (b) HSC-AMS, (c) HSC-HSC-AMS, and (d) HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS quenched at 35 min. Error bars represent standard deviations for three independent trials from a single ‘master mix’ of protein in aqueous buffer with pyrophosphate.

Discussion

DesD and related NISs catalyze the adenylation and condensation of N-hydroxy-polyamines to form the corresponding N-hydroxy-polyamides, including desferrioxamine siderophores. The stepwise NIS reaction can be performed on a single pair of substrates, such is the case for IucA and IucC in aerobactin synthesis, , or it can be iterative on a collection of substrates as observed for DesD. Comparison of the DesD structures bound to HSC-AMS and HSC-HSC-AMS provides a unique insight into the active site features that allow DesD to iteratively adenylate and condense HSC units (Figure c). A pair of anionic side chains, Asp497 and Glu464, are positioned to stabilize the terminal amine group of bound HSC and HSC-HSC, respectively. Ser493, located on a helix at the base of the HSC-HSC binding pocket, is replaced with a tyrosine at this position in the homologs BibC, PubC, and AvbD that all produce cyclic dimers of HSC, or the shorter analog N 1-hydroxy-N 1-succinyl putrescine. The tyrosine could serve to constrict the active site and force the HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate into a conformation that leads to macrocyclization of the dimeric products (Figure d). , The trajectory adopted by the bound HSC-HSC-AMS structure allows additional room in the active site for a third HSC unit to bind, attack the HSC-HSC adenylate, and form trimeric HSC-HSC-HSC. Hence, the DesD catalytic cycle preferentially terminates with the macrolactamization of the HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate to form cy-HSC-HSC-HSC (Figure ).

Interestingly, when provided the opportunity to accept larger substrates, DesD has been shown to produce larger analogs. For example, when provided HSC-HSC as the sole substrate, DesD can combine two dimeric units to produce the tetrameric product, HSC-HSC-HSC-HSC, illustrating that HSC-HSC can be both adenylated – as observed in the conventional pathway to HSC-HSC-HSC – and also can bind in the nucleophilic binding site to attack the adenylate. Even larger analogs have been observed by reacting the DesD homologue from Salinispora tropica with HSC and HSC-HSC-HAC, which contains a terminal amine but no available carboxylate. In these reactions, HSC is adenylated and attacked by HSC-HSC-HAC to form longer [HSC]n-HAC (where n = 3–6) multimers. As the binding pocket of DesD appears limited to accommodate three HSC units, these reactions to form larger products must involve the longer substrates extending from the active site cavity.

Although subtle, the apparent DesD IC50 (Figure a) and Kd (Figure b) values suggest that the HSC-HSC-AMS inhibitor is more effective than HSC-AMS and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS. Analysis of the DesD product pool by LC-MS is also consistent with favored binding and turnover of HSC-HSC since this intermediate does not accumulate under the tested conditions (Figure ). Due to conformational and steric constraints, the accumulation of HSC-HSC-HSC suggests that the final macrolactamization may be the rate-determining step likely due to suboptimal orientation of the terminal amine nucleophile for intramolecular attack of the HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP acyl adenylate carbonyl group. Indeed, appreciable formation of the cyclic product only occurs when HSC is nearly depleted, suggesting that this final step only occurs when little HSC remains. These results inform a working model that binding of the AMS inhibitors, and analogously the AMP intermediates, is dominated by ligand interactions in the nucleotide binding site. Hence, ligand binding in the DesD active site is proposed to be reversible and driven thermodynamically primarily by the formation of the acyl adenylate.

The observed pattern of dynamics in the substrate and product pool is consistent with a model where DesD relies on substrate plasticity for iterative catalysis. Hence, our findings are consistent with prior reports by the Böttcher and Codd groups demonstrating that DesD is under control of the substrate pool. , Böttcher et al. determined that the substrate promiscuity of the NIS active site varies greatly across the adenylation and condensation ligand binding sites, with the condensation ligand binding site being the more promiscuous of the two. The barrier to product formation, therefore, could reasonably be assumed to be the formation of an activated acyl adenylate intermediate with a given carboxylic acid. Furthermore, the adenylation ligand binding site incorporated non-natural substrates more readily when the alkyl chain was extended in the cadaverine subunit compared to extension in the succinate subunit. The observed substrate preference supports that the ligand-protein interactions driving substrate binding and optimal reactivity are largely concentrated toward the carboxyl terminus of HSC (the site of adenylation). The rough equivalence of ligand binding affinity and inhibitory capacity across [HSC]n-AMS inhibitors shown in this work is consistent with these previous reports, as the structural differences in the inhibitors are present on the amine terminus of the parent HSC-AMS scaffold.

Informed by the modular AMS inhibitors reported in this study, the precursor-directed feeding studies from prior work, and the elucidation of an ordered binding mechanism for IucA, an updated model is proposed for the iterative DesD catalytic cycle (Figure ). (I) ATP binds to the DesD nucleotide binding site followed by HSC binding to both the adenylation and condensation sites. HSC binding to the adenylation site is stabilized by electrostatic interaction of the free amine with Asp497. Fast adenylation forms the HSC-AMP intermediate stabilized by trapped PPi. (II) The HSC-AMP intermediate undergoes fast condensation with HSC bound in the condensation site with release of HSC-HSC, AMP, and PPi. (III) ATP binds to the DesD nucleotide binding site followed by HSC-HSC binding to the adenylation site with stabilization of the terminal amine by Glu464. Fast adenylation forms the HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate stabilized by trapped PPi. (IV) The HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate undergoes fast condensation with HSC attacking from the condensation site with subsequent release of HSC-HSC-HSC, AMP, and PPi. The HSC-HSC-HSC accumulates until the HSC substrate pool is depleted. (V) Under volume control, HSC-HSC-HSC then binds after ATP to occupy both the adenylation and condensation sites. Fast adenylation forms a tightly bound HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate. Prior studies on IucA, the NIS synthetase involved in aerobactin biosynthesis, , demonstrated an ordered catalytic mechanism in which ATP, citrate, and N-hydroxyl-N-acetyllysine bind sequentially before any chemical steps occur. This indicates that adenylation of the carboxylate substrate cannot proceed without the presence of the amine substrate in the condensation site. Assuming the same ordered mechanism occurs with all NIS synthetases, the activity of DesD in this final cyclization step suggests that the third HSC unit is able to fulfill the ordered binding mechanism by occupying the condensation site. This model is consistent with the location of the third HSC group in the docked position of HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS that we had previously proposed, though the lack of such a ligand bound crystal structure leaves this mechanistic step more speculative. It is worth noting that the N-terminus of HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP may sample conformations extending outside of the active site cavity, a phenomena previously proposed for a DesD homologue from S. tropica, thus slowing the rate of cyclization. , Additionally, the recently identified enzyme NcdF from the nocardichelin biosynthesis pathway is proposed to combine HSC with a fatty acyl analog, which may also extend outside of the catalytic pocket. (VI) The HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP intermediate undergoes macrolactamization, which is proposed to be rate-limiting, to provide cy-HSC-HSC-HSC, AMP, and PPi to terminate the iterative catalytic cycle. While many specific aspects of the stepwise kinetic model require further validation, this refined DesD mechanistic model provides an improved logic scheme that encompasses experimental data from all published DesD biochemical and structural studies performed to date. Other cellular factors likely contribute to metabolic flux including efflux of HSC-HSC-HSC, cy-HSC-HSC-HSC, and mixed products such as HSC-HSC-HAC. Efflux of HSC-HSC-HSC could alleviate the macrolactamization step in the DesD catalytic cycle. The coupled activities of the putative diversifying hydrolase (DesG) and putative acyltransferase (DesH) could further contribute to specific product outcomes in cells. Future efforts will be focused on determining presteady state kinetic parameters for each step of the DesD catalytic cycle, structural elucidation of the ligand-occupied condensation site, and study of metabolic flux in cultures of Streptomyces that produce unique desferrioxamine natural products.

Mechanistic model for DesD catalytic trajectory informed by modular (HSC)n-AMS inhibitors. (I) ATP and two molecules of HSC bind to the DesD active site. HSC in the adenylation site is stabilized by Asp497 and undergoes fast adenylation with the formation of PPi. (II) The HSC-AMP intermediate undergoes fast condensation with the second molecule of HSC bound in the condensation site followed by release of the HSC-HSC product and AMP/PPi. (III) ATP, HSC-HSC, and HSC bind to the DesD active site. HSC-HSC is stabilized by Glu464 and Ser556 in the adenylation site and undergoes fast adenylation. (IV) HSC-HSC-AMP undergoes fast condensation with HSC followed by release of the HSC-HSC-HSC product and AMP/PPi. (V) ATP and HSC-HSC-HSC bind to the DesD active site. HSC-HSC-HSC undergoes fast adenylation while being stabilized by Ser556 and occupying both the adenylation and condensation ligand binding sites to prevent the binding of a new molecule of HSC. (VI) HSC-HSC-HSC-AMP undergoes SLOW intramolecular macrolactamization which may serve as the rate-limiting step. Release of cy-HSC-HSC-HCS, AMP, and PPi ends the iterative cycle of catalysis. Conclusions

The synthesis and validation of a suite of AMS inhibitors targeting the iterative siderophore synthetase, DesD, are reported herein. HSC-AMS, HSC-HSC-AMS, and HSC-HSC-HSC-AMS were synthesized in high purity from 5-amino-1-pentanol in ∼ 1%, 13%, and 2% yields, respectively, for the longest linear sequence. DesD inhibition assays proved that ligand binding is driven by the affinity of the AMS component for the nucleotide binding site and that the number of HSC units has little impact on inhibitor binding affinity and potency. An X-ray cocrystal structure of HSC-HSC-AMS and PPi bound to DesD revealed the ordered binding mode of the C-terminal HSC-AMS component and the less well-ordered binding mode of the N-terminal HSC component, particularly the cadaverine group. The stabilization of HSC-HSC-AMS by Glu464 and S556 demonstrates how the DesD active site can accommodate modular growth of the HSC oligomers. DesD catalysis is under the control of the substrate pool and is limited by the active site volume where macrolactamization of the trimeric HSC-HSC-HSC acyl adenylate is predicted to be the rate-determining and terminal step of the catalytic cycle. The revised model of DesD catalysis proposed in this work will improve the sequence and structure-based prediction of NIS function and will enhance genome mining efforts for new NIS-derived natural products.

Experimental Methods

Materials and Methods

General solvents and reagents were used without further purification from commercial suppliers unless noted otherwise. All NMR spectra were obtained on a Varian Unity-Plus 300 MHz, Varian Unity-Inova 500 MHz, or Agilent PremiumCompact+ 600 MHz spectrometer in borosilicate glass NMR tubes (Wilman LabGlass part no. 535-PP-8). NMR data processing was performed using Mestrenova software (v11.0.0) with chemical shifts (δ) reported in parts per million (ppm) and coupling constants (J) reported in hertz (Hz). Unless otherwise noted, NMR spectra are referenced to residual solvent peaks. 13C signals were assigned via direct 13C NMR, HSQC, and HMBC analysis. Protein concentrations and optical density were determined by measuring optical absorbance (A280) of a 2 μL droplet or optical density (OD600 nm) in a polystyrene cuvette on a Thermo Fisher NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer. Low resolution LC-MS was performed using an Agilent 6130 single quadrupole mass spectrometer with G1313 autosampler, G1315 diode array detector, and 1200 series solvent module. LC separations utilized a Phenomenex Gemini C18 column, 50 × 2 mm (5 μm) with the corresponding guard column. A linear gradient was employed spanning 0% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid to 95% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid over 25 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min before MS analysis by electrospray ionization (ESI+). Compounds purified by reverse phase semipreparative HPLC used an HP1050 system and a Luna 10 mm C18(2) 100 Å column (250 mm × 21.2 mm) from Phenomenex fitted with a guard column of the same matrix (15 mm × 21.2 mm). The solvent system for HPLC was 0.1% formic acid in H2O (A) and 0.1% formic acid in ACN (B). Unless noted otherwise, semipreparative HPLC was performed with a linear gradient and flow rate of 9 mL/min with the following sequence: 100% A for 2 min, a linear gradient from 0% B to 95% B over 19 min, and a post run re-equilibration to 100% A for 3 min. LC–MS and HPLC data were processed using ChemStation software version B.04.02 SP1. SDS-PAGE analysis was carried out using Bio-Rad Any kD gels with staining by Coomassie brilliant blue or GelCode Blue Safe Protein Stain and comparison to a Bio-Rad Precision Plus Protein Dual Xtra prestained protein standard ladder. Protein structures were visualized with The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System Version 2.3.2 (Schrodinger LLC).

Overexpression and Purification of DesD

Recombinant DesD from Streptomyces coelicolor and Streptomyces griseoflavus was overexpressed in and purified from E. coli BL21 as previously reported. 2 L of terrific broth was autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min and cooled to room temperature. An overnight culture was prepared by adding a sterile loop of E. coli BL21 with pET28 ScDesD and SgDesD to individual sets of 5 mL of terrific broth with 50 μg/mL of kanamycin sulfate (5 μL of 50 mg/mL kanamycin). They were grown overnight at 37 °C on a rotating drum. One mL of each overnight culture was added to a separate 1 L of sterilized terrific broth followed by 1 mL of 50 mg/mL kanamycin. The cultures were grown at 37 °C with shaking at 225 rpm while monitoring the optical density (OD) at 600 nm. When the OD reached 0.4–0.8 (SgDesD 0.579, ScDesD 0.581), the cell culture was allowed to cool at 4 °C for 20 min. One mL of 0.5 M IPTG solution was added to each culture. The flasks were placed in an 18 °C incubator shaker and continued to rotate at 225 rpm. The culture was allowed to express protein for ∼ 18 h. 100 mL lysis buffer was placed into a bottle with SigmaFast (protease inhibitor, EDTA free – 1 tablet per 100 mL). Cultures were distributed into centrifuge bottles (masses within 0.5 g) and spun at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in 30 mL lysis buffer per culture and placed in 50 mL falcon tubes. The cells were sonicated. Time 0:02:00, Pulse 30 30, Ampl 40%. Cell lysates were transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 48,380g for 30 min at 4 °C (water filled tubes used to balance). The supernatants were collected. Nickel NTA columns were prepared by washing with 300 mL of water followed by 300 mL of lysis buffer. The supernatants were incubated with the nickel columns for 30 min. The initial flow through was collected in 50 mL falcon tubes. Washes with two 40 mL portions of lysis buffer were collected in separate 50 mL falcon tubes. The protein was eluted with 50 mL of elution buffer. The eluted protein was transferred to dialysis tubing and placed into a beaker with 2 L of dialysis buffer (1 L per protein) and soaked overnight in the cold room. The protein solutions were transferred to the top of 30,000 MWCO centrifuge filters and concentrated by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The flow-throughs were saved, and the tops were refilled with additional protein solution. The process was repeated until the entire protein solutions had been concentrated to ∼ 2 mL each. The final concentration was measured using the nanodrop under A280 (SgDesD 44.8 AU, ScDesD 51.2 AU). Protein beads were prepared by dropwise addition to liquid nitrogen. The beads were collected and stored at −80 °C.

Enzyme Inhibition Studies

Apparent IC50 measurements were taken according to previously described protocols. Briefly, DesD orthologs at 2 μM final concentrations were preincubated with NaPPi (150 μM) variable inhibitor (12.5, 6.25, 4, 3.125, 2, 1.5625, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, and 0 μM) or AMS (150, 100, 50, 10, 1, 0 μM) concentration and aqueous buffer (25 mM KH2PO4, 5.9 mM K2HPO4, 10 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol) for 30 min. The reaction was initiated by addition of premixed ATP and HSC (150 μM final working concentration each) for a working volume of 100 μL. After 35 min, the reaction was quenched by addition to 400 μL of 25 μM Fmoc-Ala in MeOH, centrifugation, and filtration. Samples were analyzed by ESI LC-MS extracted [M + H]+ ion counts for product formation HSC-HSC-HSC (619 m/z) and DFOE (601 m/z) compared to uninhibited enzyme. Peaks were smoothed by a Gaussian, and peak areas were normalized to Fmoc-Ala [M + Na]+ (334 m/z). Apparent IC50 values were calculated by plotting percent product formation versus the logarithmic concentration of inhibitor and fitting to a nonlinear dose–response (inhibition) function in GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0. All data is the average of three trials from a single ‘master mix’ of protein in aqueous buffer with pyrophosphate.

Crystallization and Structure Solution

DesD crystallization was performed as described previously. HSC-HSC-AMS ligand and NaPPi were both added to a final concentration of 5 mM to protein at 30 mg/mL and preincubated for 1 h. Cocrystallization was carried out using a cocktail containing 50 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.0, 200 mM ammonium sulfate, and 16% PEG 4000. Crystals were cryoprotected by serial transfer into crystallization cocktail containing 8%, 16%, 24% and 32% glycerol and flash-frozen into liquid nitrogen. Data collection was performed at NSLS-II beamline FMX 17-ID-2 with automated mode. Crystals diffracted to a resolution of 2.47 Å in the space group C2221. Diffraction data were integrated and scaled using the autoPROC 1.0 pipeline. A single chain of the ATP-bound DesD structure (PDB: 7TGK) with ligand and solvent molecules removed was used as search model for molecular replacement, identifying five chains in the asymmetric unit as observed with prior structures. Model building and refinement were done with COOT v0.8.9.3and PHENIX v1.21.2, respectively. , Electron density maps for the ligand were generated with the polder map tool of PHENIX. Diffraction and refinement statistics are presented in Table S1.

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry

Measurements were performed at 25 °C using a MicroCal VP-ITC Calorimeter (Malvern Panalytical). Proteins were dialyzed in ITC buffer (50 mM HEPES at pH 7.5 containing 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM TCEP, 15 mM MgCl2, 1 mM NaPPi, 15% glycerol) overnight at 4 °C. The inhibitors (∼15–50 μM) were loaded into the cell chamber and DesD (∼150–300 μM) into the injection syringe. Concentrations were varied as needed to achieve the desired signal intensity and sufficient data points pre- and postsaturation. Experiments consisted of an initial test injection of 2 μL, followed by 29 injections of 10 μL, spaced 210 s apart. Calorimetric data were integrated and analyzed using Origin software (v7.0552, MicroCal) and fitted to a single-site binding model. Postsaturation peaks (heats of dilution or secondary processes) were excluded from integration to provide ideal fitting of the initial transition. This methodology provides a more accurate apparent Kd value for the initial binding event but occludes quantitative analysis of other thermodynamic properties.

Mass Photometry

Mass photometry experiments were performed at 25 °C on a mass photometer (TwoMP, Refeyn Ltd., Oxford, UK). MassGlass slides (Refeyn, catalog no. MP-CON-71002) and sample well cassettes (Refeyn, Catalog no. RD501078) were used for the experiment. A drop of immersion oil (Immersol 518 F, ZEISS, catalog no 4Y00-R0DY-1007-3VF3) was added to the mass photometer objective lens prior to positioning of the glass slides. 1xPBS buffer was used to adjust and focus the mass photometry objective using the droplet dilution method. Protein molecular weight standards of Conalbumin (75 kDa), Aldolase (158 kDa), and Thyroglobulin (669 kDa) (Cytiva, Gel Filtration HMW Calibration Kit, Catalog no. 28403842) were used to prepare a calibration curve. 2.0 nM of protein sample in the presence of variable inhibitor concentration (ranging from 0 nM to 10 nM) was used in the measurements and data was recorded using Acquire MP software followed by analysis using DiscoverMP software. The contrast measurement of the protein samples was presented as histograms with fitted Gaussian curves, which were subsequently converted to molecular masses for final data presentation.
