Monoallelic BRCA1/2 variants in pediatric, adolescent, and young adult patients with central nervous system tumors
Authors: S Cipri, E Agolini, GD Baldo, A Cacchione, G Megaro, S Rossi, S Barresi, A Carai, GS Colafati, L Boccuto, A Mastronuzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlterations in the BRCA1 DNA repair associated (BRCA1) and BRCA2 DNA repair associated (BRCA2), two key DNA repair genes, are strongly linked to adult-onset malignancies and play a significant role in tumor development. Although BRCA1 and BRCA2 are firmly recognized as hereditary cancer risk genes in adult populations, their role in central nervous system (CNS) tumor susceptibility in younger patients is still debated.Materials and methodsWe analyzed 367 pediatric, adolescent, and young adult (AYA) patients with primary CNS tumors. Tumors were classified according to the WHO Classification of Tumors of the CNS, fifth edition. All patients underwent clinical exome sequencing, including BRCA1 and BRCA2.ResultsRare germline BRCA1/2 variants were identified in 17 of 367 patients (4.6%). Among them, 5 patients (1.4% of the overall cohort) carried pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, whereas 12 patients (3.3%) carried variants of uncertain significance (VUSs).ConclusionOur findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that monoallelic pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants may act as low-penetrance susceptibility factors in pediatric and AYA CNS tumors rather than as primary oncogenic drivers. Interpretation is limited by cohort heterogeneity, lack of a noncancer control group, and absence of systematic somatic analyses to assess biallelic inactivation or homologous recombination deficiency. Nevertheless, these data support the inclusion of BRCA genes in germline testing panels for pediatric and AYA CNS tumors, with relevant implications for genetic counseling and long-term surveillance.Key words: BRCA1, BRCA2, cancer susceptibility, CNS tumor, HBOC, pediatric cancer

Introduction

Primary tumors of the central nervous system (CNS) are the second most common cancer in children, after leukemia, and the leading cause of cancer mortality among children, adolescents, and young adults (AYAs).1,2 Although diagnostic refinement through histopathologic and molecular criteria has improved tumor stratification, substantial heterogeneity persists in their biological behavior and clinical outcomes.3 The implementation of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies has revealed that a notable proportion of children with cancer harbor inherited variants in established cancer susceptibility genes.3 Some cancer predisposition syndromes (CPSs), such as Li–Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) or neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), are well established in the context of CNS tumors.3,4 However, the role of genes typically implicated in adult neoplasms is less clear. The BRCA1 DNA repair associated (BRCA1) and BRCA2 DNA repair associated (BRCA2) genes, known to encode proteins essential for DNA damage repair through homologous recombination, are traditionally associated with hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) syndromes.5 Germline variants in the BRCA genes have also been associated with isolated solid tumors, affecting the prostate and pancreas among other organs.6,7 In addition, less common presentations of Fanconi anemia (FA), a genetic disorder featuring chromosomal instability due to defective function of proteins involved in DNA repair, are associated with pathogenic biallelic variants in the BRCA1/2 genes.8 However, recent studies suggest that monoallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (PV/LPV), particularly in the BRCA2 gene, may be associated with a low-penetrance susceptibility factor for developing brain tumors in childhood.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Genetic analyses of large pediatric cohorts have shown a higher frequency of PVs in BRCA2 in patients with CNS tumors.9 In line with these findings, studies conducted using extensive genetic panels for cancer predisposition have shown that a proportion of pediatric patients, including those with brain tumors, have LPV/PV in genes traditionally associated with adult-onset cancers.9 Such findings frequently arise without suggestive clinical features at baseline and highlight the increasing shift toward genetics-led models of oncologic risk assessment. This model has important implications for family support, cancer surveillance, and long-term patient management. In this context, the identification of germline LPV/PV in genes involved in DNA repair mechanisms is becoming increasingly relevant in the clinical setting. Despite these advances, current knowledge regarding the prevalence, tumor spectrum, and clinical implications of BRCA1/2 germline variants in pediatric and AYA CNS tumors is still incomplete. Much of the available evidence comes from case reports or small case series. The aim of this study was to systematically analyze a cohort of 367 pediatric and AYA patients with primary CNS tumors who underwent clinical exome sequencing (CES), including BRCA1 and BRCA2, to determine the frequency of LPV/PV in BRCA genes.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective observational study included 367 patients with primary CNS tumors diagnosed and treated at Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital in Rome, Italy, between August 2011 and August 2025, and reclassified according to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria.

We enrolled pediatric and AYA patients with primary CNS tumors who underwent CES, including BRCA1/2 analysis. In this cohort, CES was systematically carried out as part of the diagnostic work-up because the early age at tumor onset raised the clinical suspicion of an underlying CPS, according to institutional clinical practice during the study period. The definitive diagnosis was made by integrating radiological and histopathological evaluations with any molecular results, including DNA and/or RNA analysis and DNA methylation profiling, carried out when available or deemed necessary for the correct classification of the neoplasm.

All patients, or their legal guardians, were adequately informed about the value and purpose of the molecular investigations and provided data on their personal and family history.

The genetic protocol included pretest counseling, molecular testing, and posttest counseling aimed at discussing and interpreting the results.

For each clinical case, the following data were collected: sex, age at diagnosis, location and histological type of tumor, presence of germline variants, molecular and immunohistochemical characteristics, as well as any associated clinical manifestations (such as café-au-lait spots, neurocognitive developmental delay, or congenital malformations) and a family history of cancer.

Genetic testing and data analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted from circulating leukocytes by using the DNA blood mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was quantified using a Qubit fluorimeter (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) with the dsDNA HS Assay Kit.

The genetic analysis was carried out using NGS with a custom clinical exome panel (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA), which encompassed >8500 genes, including those implicated in CPSs, on NovaSeq 6000 platforms (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

The BaseSpace pipeline (Illumina)15 and the GeneYX software (LifeMap Sciences, CA) were used for variant calling and annotation, respectively. Sequencing data were aligned to the hg19 human reference genome. The variants were functionally annotated by Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) V.1.3, Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PolyPhen-2), and Mutation Taster for the prediction of deleterious single nucleotide variants. Variants were examined for coverage and Qscore (minimum threshold of 30) and visualized by the Integrative Genome Viewer. All variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.16

Sanger sequencing was carried out on all variants annotated as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), or uncertain significance (VUS), using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Capillary electrophoresis was carried out on the ABI 3500 Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems), and electropherograms were analyzed with Mutation Surveyor software (SoftGenetics, Pennsylvania).

Finally, all variants were periodically reassessed according to updated ACMG/Association for Molecular Pathology criteria through integration of population frequency data from gnomAD,17 disease databases ClinVar,18 and HGMD Professional,19 segregation analyses when available, Varsome, and review of published literature in PubMed,20 evaluation of genotype-phenotype correlations if possible, a family history and in silico prediction tools [PolyPhen-2, SIFT, Mutation Taster, MetaRNN, EIGEN PC, FATHMM-XF, LIST-S2, LRT, MutPred, PROVEAN, SIFT4G, BayesDel_addAF, MetaSVM, FATHMM-MKL, EIGEN, BayesDel (noAF), REVEL, MVP, MutationAssessor, MetaLR, BLOUM, DANN, DEOGEN2, FATHMM, FATHMM-XF, M-CAP, MVP, PROVEAN, PrimateAI, and SpliceAI].

NGS and somatic cancer susceptibility gene analysis

The presence of BRCA1/2 somatic variants was investigated by NGS in six cases. DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue using the NucleoSpin Tissue Kit (Machery-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) and the dsDNA high-sensitivity assay. DNA libraries were prepared according to the TSO500 Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA), pooled in equimolar amounts of 4 nM (eight libraries/pool), denatured, appropriately diluted, loaded in a paired-end mode (2 × 101-bp reads) using NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina), and sequenced to a mean coverage depth of >500× for up to 500 cancer-related genes.

Results

Our study included 367 pediatric and AYA patients diagnosed with CNS cancer—213 males and 154 females—followed at the Bambino Gesù Children's Hospital between August 2011 and November 2025, and undergone by the year 2017 to CES including the analysis of the BRCA1/2 genes.

The tumors were classified according to the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the CNS. The cohort included 140 patients (38.1%) diagnosed with low-grade gliomas (LGGs), 113 patients (30.8%) diagnosed with high-grade gliomas (HGGs), 108 patients (29.5%) diagnosed with medulloblastomas (MBs), and 6 patients (1.6%) diagnosed with CNS germ cell tumors.

An oncological family history was investigated up to the third generation.

Where feasible, trio-based NGS analysis was carried out to identify inherited or de novo genetic variants, which were subsequently classified according to ACMG guidelines.

Case series: BRCA1/2 variants

We identified rare germline BRCA1/2 variants in 17 of 367 (4.6%) pediatric and AYA patients with CNS tumors. Among these patients, 5 of 17 (29.4% of variant-positive cases; 1.4% of the overall cohort) carried PV/LPV, while 12 of 17 (70.6%; 3.3% of the overall cohort) carried VUSs.

PV/LPVs were found in patients diagnosed with HGG (n = 2), LGG (n = 1), and MB (n = 2), whereas VUSs were distributed across multiple CNS tumor subtypes.

Of the group of patients in whom LPV/PV was found, monoallelic P variants in BRCA2 were found in P1 [c.8247_8248delGA (p.Lys2750AspfsTer13)], P2 [c.5782G>T (p.Glu1928Ter)], P3 [c.5796_5797del (p.His1932GlnfsTer12)], and P4 [c.657_658delTG (p.Val220IlefsTer4)]; while a monoallelic LP variant in BRCA1 was found in P5 [c.65T>C (p.Leu22Ser)].

Moreover, two patients carried monoallelic BRCA1 VUS variants: the c.4418T>C (p.Ile1473Thr) in P6 and the c.2407A>G (p.Ser803Gly) in P7; while eight patients carried monoallelic BRCA2 VUSs: the c.66A>C (p.Ala22Ala) in P8, c.6172T>A (p.Phe2058Ile) in P10, the c.3499A>G (p.Ile1167Val) in P12, the c.442T>A (p.Cys148Ser) in P13, the c.5546G>A (p.Gly1849Asp) in P14, the c.9325C>G (p.Leu3109Val) in P15, the c.3827A>G (p.Glu1276Gly) in P16, and the c.5707A>G (p.Ile1903Val) in P17.

In addition, we detected in P11 a BRCA2 VUS—c.9104A>C (p.Tyr3035Ser)—inherited from the mother and the two BRCA1 VUSs—c.2555T>G (p.Leu852Arg) and c.2456C>G (p.Ser819Cys)—inherited from the father; while in P9, we found two BRCA2 VUSs—c.3310A>C (p.Thr1104Pro) and c.3503T>A (p.Met1168Lys)—inherited from the mother.

Finally, P16 (5.9%) had an additional germline pathogenic alteration in the ELP1 gene.

Trio-based NGS analysis was carried out in 11 of 17 patients (64.7%), with a BRCA1/2 variant detected (P2, P3, P4, P6, P8, P10, P11, P12, P13, P14, and P16); 1 parent was studied in 4 of 17 patients (23.5%) with a BRCA1/2 variant found (P1, P5, P9, and P15); and in 2 of 17 (11.8%), no parents were tested (P7 and P17).

An oncological family history was positive in 8 of 17 patients (47.1%) with a BRCA1/2 variant detected, while it was negative in 4 of 17 (23.5%), and unknown in 5 of 17 (29.4%).

Discussion

In this study, we present a case series of 367 pediatric, adolescent, and young adult patients with primary CNS tumors, including 113 HGGs, 140 LGGs, 108 MBs, and 6 germinomas, who underwent clinical exome analysis, including the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes. Overall, 17 of 367 patients (4.6%) carried a rare germline BRCA1/2 variant; among them, 12 patients carried VUSs, and 5 patients (1.4% of the total cohort) harbored PV or LPVs in BRCA2 (HGG n = 1, LGG n = 1, MB n = 2) or BRCA1 (HGG n = 1). The heterogeneity of the cohort, including multiple CNS tumor subtypes with distinct biological backgrounds, may have diluted potential tumor-specific associations and limited the possibility of drawing conclusions regarding individual tumor entities.

The BRCA1 and BRCA2 are firmly established as adult cancer susceptibility genes; their relevance in pediatric CNS tumor risk has yet to be clearly defined. A recent meta-analysis showed a statistically significant enrichment of heterozygous PVs in BRCA1/2 among children and adolescents with solid tumors, including brain tumors, suggesting a possible low-penetrance susceptibility effect in this group, although not associated with classic adult tumor syndromes. However, subsequent scientific correspondence highlighted the challenges in distinguishing true biological predisposition from incidental findings emerging from large-scale sequencing studies. In this context, our results support a cautious interpretation of monoallelic BRCA1/2 variants in pediatric CNS tumors, favoring a susceptibility-modifying role rather than a direct oncogenic driver effect. The evidence is still limited and insufficient, highlighting the importance of further studies with large cohorts and adequate controls.11

Recent studies investigating inherited rare variants in DNA repair and homologous recombination pathways have further expanded the understanding of genetic susceptibility in pediatric tumors. Li et al.,26 analyzing >47 000 individuals from BRCA1/2-mutated families, found no evidence of a significantly increased risk of nonbreast/nonovarian cancers before the age of 30 years, suggesting that the contribution of monoallelic BRCA1/2 variants to pediatric and AYA tumor predisposition, if present, is likely characterized by low penetrance. In contrast, several genomic studies support a broader role of homologous recombination defects in pediatric oncogenesis. Murphy et al.27 demonstrated that ∼5% of patients with synchronous bilateral Wilms tumors harbored germline alterations involving BRCA-related genes, supporting the hypothesis that defects in DNA repair pathways may contribute to genomic instability in pediatric tumors. Similarly, Yang et al.,28 described a familial case of peripheral neuroblastic tumors associated with compound heterozygous BRCA2 variants, showing disruption of DNA damage response pathways and reduced chemosensitivity in neuroblastoma models. Moreover, Bonfiglio et al.,29 through whole-exome sequencing analysis of a large neuroblastoma cohort, identified a significant enrichment of germline LPV/PV in cancer predisposition genes involved in homologous recombination pathways, including BRCA1, further supporting the relevance of inherited DNA repair defects in pediatric tumor susceptibility. Daugs et al.9 showed that the analysis of gene panels related to HBOC syndromes in pediatric cohorts allows the identification of PVs in adult-onset genes even in the absence of pretest clinical suspicion, highlighting the shift toward genetics-first strategies in risk assessment and follow-up planning. Although BRCA1/2 do not emerge as major drivers in their results, the work reinforces the validity of including these genes in pediatric diagnostic pathways. Taken together, these observations are consistent with our findings and support the interpretation that BRCA1/2 germline variants in pediatric CNS tumors may act as susceptibility-modifying factors rather than direct oncogenic drivers.

In our study, the predominance of PVs in BRCA2 and their cross-sectional distribution among HGG, LGG, and MB suggest that BRCA2 germline alterations could contribute to a broader background of genomic instability across multiple tumor subtypes, rather than being associated with a single tumor entity. In support of this, Wang et al.30 reported BRCA2 among the recurrent genes identified in cohorts of CNS tumors associated with genetic predisposition syndromes, along with TP53, MSH2, and NF1.

The correct interpretation of germline variants in neuro-oncology must be read in light of the evolution of WHO classifications, which have progressively integrated genetic tumor syndromes as an integral part of patient characterization. As pointed out by Agarwal et al.,31 the expansion of genetic knowledge has led to the recognition of new syndromes involving the CNS, placing germline genetic assessment at the core of modern neuro-oncologic diagnostics.

From a translational perspective, the presence of LPVs/PVs in BRCA1/2 raises the hypothesis of a vulnerability associated with homologous recombination defects and therefore a theoretical rationale for targeted therapeutic strategies, such as poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors. However, significant challenges remain in primary CNS tumors, particularly related to the blood–brain barrier and drug penetration. Even in BRCA-related oncologic contexts with intracranial involvement, the effectiveness of such approaches remains limited by these biological constraints.

In our study, the absence of systematic tumor data [loss of heterozygosity (LOH), biallelic inactivation, and homologous recombination deficiency signatures] calls for caution: the mere identification of a germline variant in one of the BRCA genes does not allow us to predict a targeted therapeutic benefit.

Despite the absence of direct molecular tumor evidence, family histories provide relevant information. In all patients carrying LPVs/PVs (P1-P5), the variants were inherited from the mother, consistent with the autosomal dominant pattern of BRCA-related syndromes. In particular, P1, a patient diagnosed with HGG, has a highly suggestive family history, with BRCA2-mutated breast cancer in the mother and pancreatic cancer in the maternal grandmother. In addition, P1 has a twin sister who appears to be in good health and who inherited the BRCA2 variant. P2, a patient diagnosed with LGG, has a family history suggestive of several cancers on both the maternal and paternal sides, consistent with variable penetrance. Gupta et al.32 presented two cases diagnosed with gangliogliomas, LGGs, with a mutation in BRCA2, one of which was confirmed as germline. Although somatic variants of BRCA2 have been described in both HGG and LGG, their pathogenic role in these tumors remains undefined to date.32

In patients diagnosed with MB (P3 and P4), the family history is poor; however, P4, a patient with an MB WNT subgroup who was described by our group as the onset case.33 Subsequently, the patient deceased for a secondary primary neoplasm, an HGG. The appearance of a second primary tumor is of particular interest, consistent with what has been described in the context of germline predisposition, even in the absence of a strongly indicative family history. The causal role of BRCA2 in MB is well documented exclusively in the context of biallelic germline variants, typically associated with FA syndrome, characterized by very early onset, SHH subtype, and extremely poor prognosis.23,34 In contrast, in our cases of MB associated with monoallelic germline variants of BRCA2, in the absence of a syndromic phenotype, the data suggest a possible role of low-penetrance susceptibility rather than a direct causal mechanism.

Finally, P5, a patient diagnosed with HGG, carrier of an LPV in BRCA1, reports a relevant family history on the paternal side, as his grandfather was diagnosed with brain cancer, but at the age of 80 years. This information does not suggest a direct role in the development of the neoplasm but neither does it suggest an additional role, in line with the literature, which presents indirect and poor data on the role of BRCA1 and CNS tumors.

Instead, it is known that the CPS associated with BRCA2 involves an increased risk of specific brain tumors, particularly MB, although this risk remains significantly lower than that of breast and ovarian neoplasms in adulthood.32

In addition, a recent study in young patients with breast cancer suggests that the functional consequence of BRCA LPV/PV, rather than the type of variant itself, may contribute to modulating prognosis, with a possible unfavorable impact of truncating variants compared with nontruncating ones; however, the relevance of these observations in pediatric CNS tumors remains to be clarified and requires specific studies.35

Several methodological constraints should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. Firstly, the heterogeneity of the cohort, which includes different histologies of primary CNS tumors and a wide age range, may have diluted any specific associations between germline variants in BRCA1/2 and individual tumor entities. Secondly, the lack of a control cohort composed of noncancer patients does not allow for formal statistical enrichment analyses or estimates of the relative risk associated with the observed variants. Furthermore, the study does not include a systematic analysis of the somatic tumor profile aimed at searching for BRCA1/2 variants in neoplastic tissue. In cases where the tumor was analyzed, these data did not allow for the demonstration of LOH events or a double hit mechanism, nor did they contribute significantly to the reclassification of VUS. In the absence of evidence of biallelic inactivation, LOH, or HRD signatures, the direct pathogenic contribution of the identified germline BRCA1/2 variants to tumorigenesis remains uncertain. In addition, interpretation of family history data is limited by the absence of a comparative analysis with patients without BRCA1/2 variants, preventing assessment of whether familial cancer burden differed significantly between groups. Although our findings may be consistent with a possible low-penetrance susceptibility role of monoallelic BRCA1/2 variants in pediatric CNS tumors, the absence of a control cohort and formal enrichment analyses prevents definitive conclusions regarding causality or relative risk. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in the context of monoallelic variants; it is not possible to determine whether and to what extent the identified germline variants played a direct functional role in tumorigenesis. Future studies integrating control cohorts, comprehensive somatic analyses, and functional approaches will be necessary to clarify the biological and clinical significance of BRCA1/2 germline variants in CNS tumors. Finally, VUSs were descriptively reported due to their potential future clinical relevance; these variants cannot currently be interpreted as evidence of CPS in the absence of sufficient functional or clinical evidence supporting pathogenicity.

Conclusion

Our findings support the potential utility of including BRCA genes in extended germline testing panels for selected pediatric and AYA patients with CNS tumors; however, these observations should be considered a hypothesis and require validation in larger controlled studies integrating somatic and functional analyses. The identification of a germline variant has immediate implications for family counseling, cascade testing, and long-term cancer surveillance planning.
