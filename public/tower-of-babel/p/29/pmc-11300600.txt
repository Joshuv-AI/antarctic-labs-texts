Drug–target interaction prediction through fine-grained selection and bidirectional random walk methodology
Authors: YaPing Wang, ZhiXiang Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study of drug–target interaction plays an important role in the process of drug development. The subject of DTI forecasting has advanced significantly in the last several years, yielding numerous significant research findings and methodologies. Heterogeneous data sources provide richer information and comprehensive perspectives for drug–target interaction prediction, so many existing methods rely on heterogeneous networks, and graph embedding technology becomes an important technology to extract information from heterogeneous networks. These approaches, however, are less concerned with potential noisy information in heterogeneous networks and more focused on the extent of information extraction in those networks. Based on this, a potential DTI predictive network model called FBRWPC is proposed in this paper. It uses a fine-grained similarity selection program to first integrate similarity on similar networks and then a bidirectional random walk graph embedding learning method with restart to obtain an updated drug target interaction matrix. Through the use of similarity selection and fine-grained selection similarity integration, the framework can effectively filter out the noise present in heterogeneous networks and enhance the model's prediction performance. The experimental findings demonstrate that, even after being split up into four distinct types of data sets, FBRWPC can still retain great prediction performance, a sign of the model's resilience and good generalization.Keywords: Drug–target interaction prediction, Heterogeneous network, Random walk, Similarity integrationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Drug–Target Interaction (DTI) prediction refers to applying bioinformatics techniques and computer algorithms to predict the interactions between drugs and biomolecules, which is one of the core tasks in drug development and translational medicine1. Anticipating whether drugs will interact with their targets can speed up the drug development process, cut down on expenses and time spent on experiments2, aid in understanding how drugs work, help scientists assess the safety and effectiveness of drugs, and be extremely important for virtual drug screening, drug redirection, and studies on the toxicity and side effects of drugs3. For example, drug repositioning refers to the discovery of a new indication for an approved drug or an experimental drug. As one of the downstream applications of DTI prediction, computational drug repositioning will serve as a direct beneficiary to accelerate the development of new drugs and shorten the process of drug discovery, which is crucial to accelerate drug discovery. Computational drug repositioning is one of the downstream uses of DTI prediction that will directly benefit from accelerating the creation of novel medications and expediting the drug discovery process—a critical step in accelerating drug discovery4. By learning from previous drug and target data, the calculating technique makes use of computer models and algorithms to anticipate the interactions between novel medications and targets5. The following are the categories of computational techniques: structural gene prediction, machine learning6, network analysis7, and hybrid methods. The structural gene prediction method relies on the structural information of drugs and targets. When there is no access to the target's 3D structure, this method will not predict well.

The subject of DTI prediction has advanced significantly in the last several years, yielding numerous noteworthy research findings and methodologies. As new research findings and technological advancements arise, the field of DTI prediction is likewise continuously advancing and evolving8. Recently, a lot of widely used approaches integrate network topology theory with machine learning, using a lot of comparable networks as well as different graph embedding and graph representation learning algorithms for DTI prediction9. For instance, the TriModel model10 builds a knowledge graph (KG) from multi-source data, converts the DTI prediction task into a link prediction task in the biomedical knowledge graph, and forecasts potential drug -target pairs based on the interaction scores calculated by the training tensor decomposition on the knowledge graph embedding. The DTI2Vec model11 is an ensemble learning and graph embedding technology that applies biased random walk to the node2vec framework to produce feature vectors for every node. A drug–target network is entered into the GRMF by Ezzat12 who then utilize the graph regularization matrix decomposition to produce the DTI prediction score after converting the drug–target interaction value into the interaction probability value. However, the prediction performance of medicines and targets that are far away in the network is low, and the drug–target network input obtained by this technique only comprises homogenous information about drugs or targets. IGRLDTI13 an improved graph representation learning technique, is based on classical graph neural networks and is designed to mitigate the issue of excessive smoothing by adaptively determining the propagation depth k of each biomolecule in heterogeneous graphs using the node-dependent local smoothing (NDLS) strategy. However, DTI's predictive power is diminished because of inadequate biological understanding. Zhao14 created a geometric deep learning (GDL) framework for medication retargeting using intricate biological data.The feature representation of nodes in each biological network is learned by projecting the geometric prior knowledge of the network structure of drugs and diseases in the non-Euclidean domain into the potential feature space. The best projection vector of drugs and diseases is then screened using multiple neural networks. Zhao15 developed a motif sensing MDA prediction model that incorporates many high and low order pieces of information in mirNA-disease heterogeneous networks, taking into account the possible value of network information of MDA (miRNA-disease associations) at the motif level. In Hu's16 novel two-channel hypergraph convolutional network, higher-order patterns characterizing the multi-component characteristics of herbs are captured by hypergraph convolution in the first channel, which depicts the interaction between herbs and their components as hypergraphs. A network of higher-order interactions between illnesses and their target proteins is shown by the second channel in a manner akin to a hypergraph. After then, the illnesses and herb embeddings are combined to get the anticipated outcomes of their interactions. The fuzzy Depth Attribute graph (AG) clustering model, which was presented by Yang17 uses graph convolution to encode node attributes and network topology. It then uses reconstruction error estimation and self-monitoring training procedures to improve node embedding and enhance cluster cohesiveness.In addition to optimizing the clustering results, this dependent link between graph representation learning and the natural coupling of AG clustering is another step toward exploring the underlying network structure (Supplementary file 1).

Many earlier approaches have also integrated data on drug-side-effects, drug-disease correlations, and target-disease linkages, in addition to chemical data of pharmaceuticals and genetic data of targets18. Studies have demonstrated that these heterogeneous data sources improve DTI networks' prediction ability and, to some extent, increase their semantic richness. For instance, Luo5 created a heterogeneous network in DTInet that contained information on drug-disease associations, target-target similarity networks, drug-drug similarity networks, and drug-side-effect associations. To forecast prospective DTI, the matrix completion approach is introduced by restarting the characteristics of random walk learning nodes. However, because there are a lot of unidentified positive samples within the current negative data, this method's random selection of negative samples results in a high false positive rate and insufficient utilization of important information. In order to estimate probable drug–target interactions, Li19 combined heterogeneous networks and employed DeepWalk to gather node topological information. Heterogeneous networks were also merged in NeoDTI by Wang20. In heterogeneous networks, neighbor information is integrated through the transmission and aggregation of several information kinds. Low-dimensional feature representations of drugs and targets are extracted by means of a nonlinear learning model that maintains the network topology. Next, the potential DTI is predicted using an inductive matrix completion approach, too. In order to combine many similarity matrices into a single similarity matrix, DDR21 employed the nonlinear similarity integration technique SNF. The random forest model was then utilized to determine the graph-based properties of various nodes from the heterogeneous network. It is challenging to distinguish the features of many nodes in the network because this method uses a coarse-grained approach to feature extraction. Specifically, the similarity integration method SNF filters and fuses each similarity view from a global perspective, deletes and integrates the entire similarity matrix, and ignores the utility of each similarity view to each different entity (drug or target).

Node topology information gets more precise when heterogeneous networks have more information overall22. Based on this, this research suggests a viable DTI prediction model called FBRWPC based on the random walk approach and similarity integration at a finer resolution, which is used to predict potential DTI from heterogeneous networks. Specifically, we map the importance of each similarity view for each medication or target during the similarity integration process using an entity weighting technique to decrease noise and redundant information. Sparsify the weight matrix based on local interaction consistency using a fine-grained selection method to eliminate redundant similarity23. In the weight matrix, the similarity with zero weight values is discarded, whilst the final similarity matrix is integrated using the similarity with non-zero weight values. Next, in order to potentially obtain DTI prediction results, we build a drug–target heterogeneity network based on the integrated drug similarity matrix, integrated target similarity matrix, and known drug–target interaction binary matrix. On this network, we implemented the first bidirectional random walk algorithm (Luo)24. Next, similiarity selecting phase was implemented, which involves keeping similarity links with greater similarity weights in order to maintain more dependable similarity relationships and further decrease duplicate interference information. Lastly, chemical data from medicinal compounds and protein genetic data are combined to calibrate the model. New drug–target heterogeneous network is constructed again using the similarity matrix of chemical fingerprints, the similarity matrix of amino acid sequences in proteins, and the predicted drug–target interaction matrix from the previous step. The selected bidirectional random walk algorithm is then applied again to the new network in order to correct the predicted results. The model's ability to find novel DTIs is demonstrated by the experimental data, and the prediction results are solid. Furthermore, the model has proven to perform better when it comes to handling the problem of drug repositioning.The entire workflow of FBRWPC is shown in Fig. 1 below.

This flowchart displays the FBRWPC process in its entirety. First, using the similarity view of drugs and targets, a fine-grained selection similarity integration is carried out. Next, the integrated drug similarity matrix, integrated target similarity matrix, and known drug–target interaction binary matrix are used to build a drug–target heterogeneous network. On this network, a bidirectional random walk process is used to determine the anticipated feasible DTI outcomes. The anticipated drug–target interaction matrix from the previous stage, the protein amino acid sequence similarity matrix, and the chemical fingerprint similarity matrix are then used to build a drug–target heterogeneity network once more. To modify the projected outcomes, the selected bidirectional random walk method is applied once again on the rebuilt network.

Experiment

Luo data set similarity from5, including four drug and three target similarity used to describe the relationship between drugs and targets in various aspects. Three of the four drug similarity constructs are based on drug-drug interaction (DDI), drug-disease interaction (DIA), drug-side effect interaction (SE), and one is based on chemical structure (CS). Of the three targets, two were based on target-target interaction (PPI), target-disease (PDA) interactions, and the other was based on amino acid sequence (AAS). Six drug similarities were constructed based on the chemical structure (CS) of different indicators, and the other three were constructed based on drug side effects (SE). Seven target similarities are based on protein amino acid sequences (AAS), and the other two target similarities are derived from gene ontology terms and protein–protein interactions (PPI), respectively (Table 1).

Baseline method

FBRWPC was compared with the following six baseline methods:

DTINet5 combines a network diffusion algorithm (random walk with restart) with a dimensionality reduction scheme to extract low dimensional feature representations of drugs and targets from the similarity network of drugs and targets. Then, inductive matrix completion (IMC) is used to predict new drug–target interactions.

DRLSM28 builds a dual Laplace regularized least squares model (DLapRLS) of multi-kernel learning based on the Hilbert–Schmidt independence condition, to compute DTI.

NRMLF29 suggested a matrix decomposition approach, by using logistic matrix decomposition to first represent the likelihood of DTI and then domain regularization to obtain prediction accuracy.

MSCMF30 is a factor model that projects drugs and targets onto a common low-rank feature space and collaboratively predicts drug–target interactions through two low-rank matrices.

NetMF31 Turlaplacian theory and jump graph-based network embedding techniques are effectively connected by NETMF, which offers a new theoretical foundation for network representation learning.

NEDTP32 extracts drug and target features through the Word2Vec algorithm. Then, it constructs a Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) to predict new drug target interactions based on the learned features.

Evaluating indicator

The assessment criteria for the model in this study are primarily the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC)33 and the area under the precise recall curve (AUPR)34. In binary classification, this article can obtain the counts of true negative (TN), false negative (FN), true positive (TP), and false positive (FP). Precision is the ratio of TP/(TP + FP). It reflects the classifier's ability to not label negative samples as positive. The recall rate is the ratio of TP/(TP + FN). It represents the classifier's ability to find all positive samples.

Experiment setting

In this paper, sample data of drugs and targets are divided into four different types of training set and test set to better simulate real-world situations, and then cross-validation (CV) is used to report results under four prediction Settings. In the S1 setting, the samples were divided into positive samples and negative samples according to whether there was a definite interaction. For example, 1923 known drug–target interaction pairs in Luo dataset5 were taken as positive samples, and the remaining drug–target pairs with no observed interaction or no interaction were taken as negative samples9. The predictive performance of FBRWPC was evaluated using a tenfold cross-validation procedure, in which 10% of the positive and negative samples were randomly selected as the test set, and the remaining 90% of the positive and negative samples as the training set were used to train the model. Under the setting of S2, a tenfold cross-validation procedure of drugs was applied, that is, 10% of drugs and all their corresponding targets were randomly selected as the test set, and the remaining 90% of drugs and all their corresponding targets were used as the training set to train the model. Cross-validation of drugs by S2 can better assess the generalization ability of drugs. Under the setting of S3, the tenfold cross-validation procedure of the target is applied, that is, 10% of the target and all the drugs corresponding to it are randomly selected as the test set, and the remaining 90% of the target and all the drugs corresponding to it are used as the training set to train the model. S3 cross-validates against targets to better evaluate the generalization ability of targets. In the S4 setup, a double-10 × cross-validation procedure of drugs and targets is applied, with 10% of the drug and 10% of the target combination randomly selected to become a test set, and the remaining 90% of the drug and 90% of the target combination to become a training set to train the model. In addition to guaranteeing that the training and test sets' data do not overlap, S4 covers various segments of the entire data set, thoroughly takes into account the cross-validation of medications and targets—which is the scenario that is closest to reality—and is capable of comprehensively evaluating the model's capacity for generalization.

In FBRWPC, the similarity integration part is determined by grid search method, and K = 5 is set in KNN similarity selection. In the section of bidirectional random walk prediction of potential DTI, the restart parameter μ is selected from 0.6,0.8,1, the number of similarity branches K retained after similarity selection is selected from 5,10,20, and the corresponding weight coefficients assigned to different drug (target) neighbors in the similarity matrix are determined by the decay factor, and the decay factor c is selected from 0.5,0.6,0.7. For l1andl2, in the course of the experiment, it is found that when they reach the fifth iteration, the model reaches convergence, and the predicted interaction matrix A almost no longer changes. In order to balance AUC and AUPR, we set in this paper the similarity integration part KNN similarity selection K = 5, the bidirectional random walk part, the restart parameter of random walk μ=0.6, the number of retained similarity branches K = 20, and the decay factor τ=0.7, l1=l2=5. The parameters of all baseline methods are set according to the recommendations in their respective articles.

Discussion

In order to forecast possible DTI, this article suggests a network model called FBRWPC that is built on similarity integration and the random walk approach. By conducting bidirectional random walks on two networks, the technique successfully removes interference information from heterogeneous networks and enhances the prediction capabilities of the model. Sparsify the weight matrix based on local interaction consistency using a fine-grained selection method to eliminate redundant similarities and accomplish the first round of noise reduction. Reliable similarity associations are extracted from the network again using the similarity selection strategy used when implementing a random walk algorithm in heterogeneous networks, which increases prediction accuracy. Ultimately, the model was modified to forecast potential DTIs using dependable similarity features of drug and targets that were taken from the network and integrated with chemical information of drug molecules and genetic information of proteins. According to the testing results, FBRWPC performed the best and showed exceptional promise for solving drug redefinition problems. Case studies have shown that Several of the DTIs predicted by FBRWPC have been validated. Therefore, FBRWPC has the potential to narrow down the experimental scope for biologists in wet experiments to determine drug–target interactions.

We intend to build an efficient and low-noise heterogeneous network from a multimodal perspective, extract more precise drug target characteristics, and contribute positively to the prediction of downstream drug targets in the future by building on the foundation provided by this paper.

Material and method

Problem formulation

Let D=d1,d2⋯,dnd, and T=t1,t2,⋯,tnt , respectively, represent the set of drugs and the set of targets, where nd is the number of drugs, and nt is the number of targets. Let Hd=Sd,1,Sd,2,⋯,Sd,md and Ht=St,1,St,2,⋯,St,mt respectively represent a set containing md drug similarity matrices and a set containing mt target similarity matrices, where Sd,iϵRnd×ndi=1,2,⋯,md, St,jϵRnt×ntj=1,2,⋯,mt. The matrix A^ represents the interaction between drug and target,A^ϵRnd×nt, where if there is an interaction between drug i and target j, A^ij= 1, and if it is unknown whether there is an interaction between drug i and target j, A^ij= 0. A drug (or target) is referred to as a new drug (or target) if there isn't a known interaction for it. That is, if A^.i=0, the drug is termed a new drug, where A^.i is the ith row of A^; and if A^.j=0, the target is termed the new target, where A^.j is the jth column of A^. A medication (or target) is considered known if it has at least one pair of known interactions. Using drug, target, and interaction data from established drug target pairs, the DTI prediction model seeks to estimate and identify novel drug target pairings with possible interactions.

Similarity integration

The goal of similarity integration (also known as similarity integration) is to fuse the similarity matrices of the upper and middle drugs (or the similarity matrices of the middle drugs) into one matrix, capture the contribution values of each similarity matrix from different perspectives, and provide a more concise and informative input for downstream DTI prediction tasks, reducing the redundancy and interference of noise information to a certain extent. Formally, similarity integration can be defined as a mapping f:Rn×n,⋯,Rn×n→Rn×n, where the fused drug and target similarity matrices are obtained by f (Sd,1,⋯,Sd,md) =Sd and f (St,1,⋯,St,mt) = St, respectively, where SdϵRnd×nd, StϵRnt×nt.Many similarity integration methods are also used for DTI prediction tasks, such as average weighting. There are both linear and nonlinear methods. In this paper, we fuse the similarity matrix by fine-grained selection of similarity integration, which differs from previous global fusion methods. This method starts from the similarity utility of each drug (target) and linearly summarizes the similarity of selected information from different perspectives at a finer granularity. Fine-grained similarity selection

If the local interaction consistency is low, it indicates that similar drugs usually interact with different targets, which is inconsistent with the principle of “guilt-by-assocoatio”n, and this further indicates that there is noise information in the corresponding similarity view. This research ,to reduce noise interference, employs a fine-grained selection program that filters the redundant similarity of each drug (target) by thinning the weight matrix. To be more precise, substitute zero for the the lowest weight in each row of weight matrix .Because the lower local interaction consistency of a drug (or target) correlates with the lowest weights in each row, and this lower local interaction consistency corresponds to a noisy similarity perspective. Therefore, it is desirable to set the weight to zero and discard the similarity information of the drug (target) containing noise in this similarity perspective, so as to achieve the effect of reducing redundant information and reducing noise.

The similarity with zero weight values in the weight matrix is discarded. In contrast, the similarity with non-zero weight values is ultimately used to integrate the final similarity matrix. This is different from the existing coarse-grained similarity selection method that deletes and integrates the entire similarity matrix. In this paper, similarity is evaluated at a finer granularity, that is, noise similarity information is retained or eliminated from the level of each drug (target) under each similarity view without affecting the useful similarity information of other drugs (target) under this view.

Similarity selection strategy

Based on the principle of “guilt-by-association”, we reselect the similarities in the drug (target) similarity network to extract more reliable similarity relationships. Specifically, only the top K neighbors of each drug (target) are retained, with the similarity values for all other positions set to zero.
