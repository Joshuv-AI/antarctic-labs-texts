Coronary Artery Disease in Female Athletes
Authors: Shirit Kazum-Beisr, Nili Schamroth-Pravada, Avital Porter, Roi Moshe, Ashraf Hamdan, Ran Kornowski, Naama Constantini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case series highlights the unique presentation and diagnostic challenges of coronary artery disease (CAD) in female athletes. Despite generally lower prevalence, women may experience atypical symptoms and delays in diagnosis, often due to the under-recognition of risk and subtle clinical signs. We describe 3 cases of CAD among women during different stages of life: a postmenopausal long-distance runner with complex coronary anatomy, a postmenopausal triathlete with severe left anterior descending artery stenosis, and a young climber in her reproductive years with acute coronary syndrome. These cases underscore the importance of thorough evaluation, including advanced imaging such as coronary computed tomography angiography, and reveal how gender-specific factors, menopause, and training patterns influence CAD detection and outcomes. There is a need for tailored, athlete-specific diagnostic algorithms to ensure early identification and management of CAD in women, ultimately reducing gender disparities in cardiovascular care and improving prognosis in female athletes.

Case 1

A 51-year-old female recreational long-distance runner presented with new-onset palpitations and an irregular pulse during exertion. Her medical history was notable solely for untreated mild hyperlipidemia (total cholesterol: 220 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein: 138 mg/dL). She had 2 pregnancies and 2 live births, with no adverse pregnancy outcomes. She had experienced menopause within the past 2 years and was not receiving hormone replacement therapy. Physical examination and baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) were unremarkable. A 24-hour Holter monitor documented sinus rhythm with sinus arrhythmia and isolated episodes of atrial tachycardia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic and diastolic function without significant valvular pathology. Two months later, the patient reported worsening symptoms and decreased exercise capacity, leading to a referral to the cardiology department. She underwent an exercise stress test, which was terminated at minute 6 (stage 3, Bruce protocol) given significant exertional dyspnea at a submaximal heart rate of 135 beats/min. ECG monitoring during exercise revealed horizontal ST-segment depressions, raising suspicion for myocardial ischemia, and she was subsequently referred for coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). Given the patient's complex anatomy and high procedural risk, a multidisciplinary heart team recommended surgical revascularization. She subsequently underwent successful coronary artery bypass grafting with a left internal mammary artery graft to the LAD, chosen to ensure long-term myocardial perfusion and avoid the risks associated with left main bifurcation stenting. Her postoperative course was uneventful, and by 6 months after completion of a structured cardiac rehabilitation program, she had made significant progress. The program commenced with light-intensity aerobic exercise specifically tailored to her needs, with heart rate zones adjusted based on the results of cardiopulmonary exercise testing. One year after surgery, she resumed her 10-km running routine at a pace near her baseline.

Case 2

A 53-year-old competitive master female triathlete with protein S deficiency presented with exertional left arm pain. Her risk factors included a past history of smoking (quit 20 years ago) and a family history of sudden cardiac death, as her grandfather had died of cardiac arrest from unknown cause at the age of 50 years. She had 3 pregnancies resulting in 3 live births, experienced no adverse pregnancy outcomes, was currently postmenopausal, and was not taking any medications regularly. In the weeks leading up to a triathlon competition, she experienced recurrent sharp pain in her left arm during peak exertion while running. Initially attributing the discomfort to a cycling-related musculoskeletal injury, she continued training. However, one evening the pain recurred at rest during dinner, accompanied by heartburn. When symptoms reappeared again during a run the following morning, she discontinued exercise and presented to the emergency department for evaluation.

Upon presentation to the emergency department, the patient reported ongoing chest pain. ECG showed no ischemic changes (Figure 2A), and high-sensitivity cardiac troponin levels were within normal limits. Her symptoms were thought to be due to heartburn, and she was prescribed proton pump inhibitors and discharged home. However, persistent chest discomfort at rest and during exertion prompted her to seek evaluation by a sports cardiologist. Resting echocardiography revealed a small pericardial effusion, prompting further evaluation with CCTA 2 weeks after symptom onset. Imaging revealed a mixed plaque in the proximal LAD causing 70% to 99% stenosis, as well as a 50% stenosis in the proximal right coronary artery with noncalcified components. Her postrevascularization course was uneventful, and she successfully resumed training through a gradual cardiac rehabilitation program. After the initial 6 weeks of light-intensity exercise and following a stress echocardiogram that ruled out ischemia, she progressively resumed moderate and, eventually, high-intensity aerobic activity.

Case 3

A 38-year-old female recreational wall climber with no cardiovascular risk factors aside from a family history of ischemic heart disease (maternal grandfather at age 60) presented with epigastric pain radiating to the left shoulder. The symptoms began during intensive training 1 week earlier and were initially self-attributed to a dietary cause. However, given their recurrent and persistent nature, she sought evaluation by her general practitioner. Three days later, owing to symptom progression, she presented to the emergency department. ECG revealed poor R-wave progression in the precordial leads and T-wave inversions in leads V1 to V5 (Figure 3B), and her high-sensitivity cardiac troponin was elevated at 203 μg/L. Bedside echocardiography demonstrated apical and anteroapical akinesis. Urgent coronary angiography revealed critical mid-LAD bifurcation stenosis and 70% stenosis of the ostial first diagonal artery. The angiographic appearance was not typical of spontaneous coronary artery dissection.

The patient underwent successful percutaneous coronary intervention to the mid-LAD. This was followed 1 month later by elective balloon dilation of the diagonal branch stenosis owing to persistent symptoms and a positive modified stress test. Both procedures were uneventful. Follow-up TTE demonstrated normalized left ventricular function (ejection fraction: 60%) without residual wall motion abnormalities. Stress echocardiography showed no evidence of inducible ischemia. Despite this, the patient continued to experience symptoms and needed an extended rehabilitation period before gradually returning to near-baseline activity levels. She incrementally increased her exercise to moderate-intensity aerobic activities but has not yet returned to indoor climbing.

Discussion

Coronary disease in the female athletes: Is a new approach needed?

CAD in female athletes differs significantly from that in their male counterparts. Although historically under-represented in research studies, few existing reports highlight notable distinctions in incidence, symptoms, severity, or underlying mechanisms. Unlike male athletes, who may develop accelerated atherosclerosis with high-intensity training, female athletes appear less susceptible to such changes, as they tend to have lower rates of coronary artery calcification and plaque burden, including both calcified and noncalcified plaques.1 When atherosclerosis is present in the female athlete, it is more often associated with age and traditional cardiovascular risk factors, rather than with exercise exposure.1 Similarly, the incidence of sudden cardiac death is consistently lower in female athletes across all ages, with arrhythmic causes more common and coronary disease less frequent compared to males.2

Several mechanisms have been suggested to explain this “female advantage,” including higher estradiol and lower androgen levels, differences in body composition and cardiovascular anatomy, and lower athletic training intensity compared to male3; however, all remain largely speculative.

While the benefits of physical activity in relieving menopausal symptoms and supporting metabolic health are well established,4 the menopausal transition itself is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Reduced estradiol levels are associated with increases in insulin resistance, low-density lipoprotein, blood pressure, body weight, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular stiffness.5 In master female athletes, age, blood pressure, and lipid profile were reported to be the most important determinants of coronary atherosclerosis rather than the volume, duration, and intensity of exercise.6 Thus, menopausal and postmenopausal female athletes remain at increased risk for coronary artery disease and should be carefully assessed.

We presented 3 female athletes—2 postmenopausal and one of reproductive age—all of whom reported effort-related symptoms that should have prompted early evaluation for CAD. However, their diagnoses were delayed because of initial misinterpretation by physicians who viewed them as relatively young, physically active, and healthy, thereby underestimating their true cardiovascular risk.

The delayed diagnosis of CAD in women reflects a persistent and significant gender gap in cardiovascular care, often resulting in worse outcomes compared to men. It is influenced by the atypical nature of symptoms—which often differ from the classic chest pain commonly seen in men—as well as a lack of awareness as to the importance of cardiovascular disease in women and an underestimation of cardiovascular risk. These challenges are further amplified when evaluating female athletes, as clinical assessment requires a more nuanced and detailed history, particularly regarding subtle signs such as decreased exercise tolerance, reduced athletic performance, or new-onset effort intolerance.

In case 1, an athlete accustomed to running 40 km a week for years noticed subtle changes in her performance. She reported palpitations and a gradual decline in her ability to complete her usual runs. Despite these symptoms, her primary care physician assumed that her ability to run 10 km indicated good cardiovascular health. However, in an athlete, even mild reductions in exercise capacity compared to their personal baseline should prompt thorough evaluation.

In case 2, the athlete experienced classic effort angina symptoms of sharp chest pain during exertion radiating to her shoulder, yet delayed her visit to the emergency department by several days. This delay reflects a common trend among women with CAD, in which denial or lack of awareness about symptom significance leads to postponed care. When she eventually presented to the emergency department, the initial evaluation—including ECG and cardiac biomarkers—was unremarkable, and she was discharged with a diagnosis of atypical chest pain. This case highlights both patient-related and physician-related factors contributing to delayed diagnosis, especially the under-recognition of cardiovascular risk in female athletes.

In case 3, a young athlete in her reproductive years, with no apparent cardiovascular risk factors, presented late with typical exertional chest pain and T-wave inversions on ECG. Although anterior T-wave inversions are common in female athletes and can sometimes represent a benign athlete's heart variant,7 their presence particularly when associated with symptoms or extended to other leads, especially inferolateral leads, necessitates further investigation to rule out ischemia or cardiomyopathy. Despite this, her primary care physician underestimated her cardiovascular risk and discharged her with a diagnosis of musculoskeletal pain. This case highlights the tendency to overlook cardiac symptoms in low-risk, physically active young women, which can lead to missed diagnoses. It emphasizes the importance of recognizing nonatherosclerotic causes of cardiac ischemia, such as spontaneous coronary artery dissection, especially in young female patients with CAD or during pregnancy, even in the absence of typical angiographic features. The nature of the climbing exercise, involving repeated Valsalva-like maneuvers and significant pressor responses, was particularly relevant in this regard.8

Another critical aspect underscored by these cases is the appropriate selection and timing of diagnostic modalities in evaluating CAD in female athletes. In case 1, after an initial evaluation with Holter monitoring and resting TTE, the athlete was referred for a standard exercise stress test. The test was both electrocardiographically and clinically positive for ischemia, prompting a subsequent referral for CCTA. In case 2, the patient was discharged from the emergency department for further outpatient evaluation. Only after a follow-up cardiology visit 1 week later was she referred for a CCTA, an application that was initially denied given her lack of traditional risk factors. In case 3, the athlete was misdiagnosed with musculoskeletal pain and returned days later with a non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

These cases illustrate a missed opportunity to use CCTA earlier, a noninvasive tool with a high negative predictive value for excluding obstructive CAD. Although its role in rapidly triaging suspected coronary syndromes in emergency settings is increasing, cardiac computed tomography was not included in the initial assessment for any of these athletes. Why was this valuable diagnostic tool overlooked? Would the approach have differed if these athletes had been male? Or if they had been nonathletes?

These examples highlight a consistent diagnostic gap and underscore the importance of developing a tailored, athlete-specific strategy for CAD assessment in women. In response, we propose a dedicated diagnostic algorithm for evaluating suspected CAD in female athletes, aimed at addressing the unique challenges faced by this population and facilitating timely, accurate diagnosis.
