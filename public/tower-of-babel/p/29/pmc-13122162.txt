Effectiveness of Naltrexone and Baclofen Combination in Reducing Craving among Patients with Alcohol Dependence: An Open-label Trial
Authors: Aman Naqvi, Anil Nischal, Amit Singh, Anuradha Nischal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlcohol use disorder is among the most prevalent mental disorders worldwide. Craving, a key feature of alcohol dependence, is also a major reason for relapse. We aimed to determine the efficacy of naltrexone, baclofen, and naltrexone-baclofen combination in reducing craving among patients with alcohol dependence.MethodsIn this open-label, randomised trial of eight weeks duration, 97 participants were randomly allocated to three groups, namely naltrexone alone, baclofen alone, and naltrexone-baclofen combination, using a computer-generated random number table. The dosage was 40 to 60 mg/day for baclofen and 50 mg a day for naltrexone. Tools used for assessment included the Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS), the visual analogue scale (VAS) for craving, World Health Organization Quality of Life BREF (WHOQoL BREF), and World Health Organization Well-being Scale (WHO-5). Mixed design ANOVA was used for analysis.ResultsThe naltrexone-baclofen combination group had a significantly greater decline in OCDS score as compared to the naltrexone group, but not as compared to the baclofen alone group. Also, there was a greater reduction in VAS, WHOQoL BREF, and WHO-5 score in the naltrexone-baclofen group compared to the other two groups; however, the difference was not significant. No significant differences were found in relapse rates and heavy episodic drinking between groups.ConclusionThe naltrexone-baclofen combination may be better in comparison to naltrexone used alone in reducing craving in alcohol dependence and is well tolerated.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol use disorders (AUD), with a 7% prevalence in people aged 15 years and above, are among the most prevalent mental disorders globally [1,2]. Alcohol use is a key risk factor associated with morbidity and mortality, especially among users aged 15 to 49 years, where it is a leading risk factor for premature death and disability [3]. In 2019, the prevalence of heavy episodic drinking (HED), defined as the consumption of at least 60 grams of pure alcohol at least once in the past month, was 17% in people aged 15 years and above. In India, 160 million individuals, or 14.6% of the population (aged 10−75 years), consume alcohol regularly, making it one of the most widely used psychoactive substances in the country [4]. An estimated 2.7% or 29 million Indian population consume alcohol in a dependent pattern [4]. Also, those seeking treatment for AUD frequently return to alcohol use soon after treatment [5].

Craving, which is a key characteristic of the dependence syndrome, is also one of the most important factors responsible for relapse [5]. Different pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions have been used to manage the craving for alcohol. The evidence-based pharmacological agents include acamprosate, naltrexone, baclofen, topiramate, nalmefene, and gabapentin [6,7]. Naltrexone, an antagonist mainly at the mu-opioid (μ) receptor, with additional antagonistic activity at the kappa (k) and delta (δ) opioid receptors, is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of alcohol dependence [8]. The opioid receptor blockade prevents increased dopaminergic activity after the consumption of alcohol, thus reducing its rewarding effects [9]. It has been shown that small doses of opioid agonists increase motivation to consume more alcohol and the intake of larger amounts of alcohol to reinstate or increase opioid activity [10,11]. Since alcohol intake enhances endogenous opioid activity in the central nervous system, it has been proposed that opioid antagonists such as naltrexone may reduce the risk of relapse [9]. While naltrexone targets opioid receptors, many other neurotransmitter systems are targeted by ethanol and contribute to the modulation of the reinforcing effects of ethanol [11]. The γ-aminobutyric acid receptor agonist, baclofen, has been shown to reduce alcohol intake and craving for alcohol in trials [12]. Baclofen suppresses alcohol-stimulated dopamine release and, in turn, dopamine-mediated, alcohol-reinforced, and motivated behaviours. However, the relapse rates continue to be high even with the use of either naltrexone or baclofen in people with alcohol dependence [13,14].

An approach consisting of using a combination of medications to improve treatment outcomes for alcohol dependence has also been tried [15]. A study comparing naltrexone, acamprosate, and their combination found the combination of naltrexone and acamprosate to be more effective in lowering relapse rates than placebo and acamprosate [15]. However, the results with the combination were not significantly different from naltrexone alone [15]. Meanwhile, studies using any other combination of pharmacologic agents aimed at the treatment of alcohol dependence in humans are lacking. No human research has studied the effect of the naltrexone and baclofen combination on craving for alcohol. An animal study on the acquisition of alcohol-drinking behavior in alcohol-preferring rats found that neither naltrexone nor baclofen, when given alone, produced a significant effect on alcohol consumption. However, the combination of both drugs resulted in a significant reduction in daily alcohol intake and retardation in the acquisition of alcohol-drinking behavior [16].

The current research aimed to study the effectiveness of the combination of naltrexone and baclofen, medications with different mechanisms of action, in improving the treatment outcomes in patients with alcohol dependence. Our primary aim was to compare the effectiveness of naltrexone alone, baclofen alone, and naltrexone-baclofen combination in reducing craving among patients with alcohol dependence. Also, we compared the three treatment groups for episodes of heavy alcohol use, relapse rate, and quality of life. We hypothesised that the patients with alcohol dependence treated with a naltrexone-baclofen combination would have less craving compared to naltrexone alone or baclofen alone treatment.

METHODS

Study Design

The study was an open-label, randomised trial of eight weeks duration.

Settings and Participants

The participants were patients attending the psychiatry and addiction treatment outpatient clinics of a tertiary care centre in North India. Participants were patients aged 18 to 60 years, diagnosed with alcohol dependence syndrome as per the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) [17]. The other inclusion criteria were (a) complete alcohol abstinence for at least 10 days, (b) a Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol revised version (CIWA-Ar) score of less than 8, with no more than one score on any of the scale items, and (c) willingness to give informed consent.

Any individual who had a current diagnosis of any other psychiatric disorder except nicotine dependence and personality disorder, current use of any psychotropic medication, evidence of severe neurologic or physical disorders, history of cirrhosis or laboratory evidence of significant hepatocellular injury, pregnancy, nursing, or refusal to use a reliable method of birth control in women were excluded from the study. Participants were included in the study soon after acute withdrawal management, when they became eligible for participation. Trazodone up to 50 mg was prescribed as rescue medication for sleep disturbance.

Occurrence of any clinically significant side effects after the initiation of therapy warranting stopping the medications; relapse during the study (defined as five HED days in a week) or consent withdrawal during therapy; failed attempts to contact participants either during the last follow-up visit (± 3 days) or ≥ 2 visits out of the 1st, 2nd and 3rd follow-up visit (± 3 days); missing > 20% of doses as per self-report/pill count was considered as a dropout.

Sample Size

The estimated total sample size for this study was 30, computed using G*Power version 3.1.9.7, based on an effect size of 0.25, a power of 0.80, an α-error probability of 0.05, four measurement points, and a correlation between the repeated measures of 0.5. It was assumed that the sphericity assumption was met, and consequently, the non-sphericity correction epsilon was maintained at one. However, we attempted to reach 30 participants in each group. Eventually, a total of 97 participants were randomized into the three study groups using a random number table, and their data were analysed.

The data collection was done between April 2023 and March 2024. The institutional ethics committee approved the study (letter no 2110/ Ethics/2023 with reference code XIV- PGTSC- IIA /-P38). The prospective registration of the trial was done in the Clinical Trials Registry, India (Registration number CTRI/2023/03/050775).

Procedure

All the participants aged 18 to 60 years attending adult psychiatry outpatient department (OPD) and addiction treatment clinic OPD were screened. Those fulfilling the selection criteria and giving written informed consent were included in the study. The diagnosis was ascertained, and psychiatric co-morbidities were ruled out clinically using ICD-11 [17]. Patients with at least 10 days of abstinence were included, so that the withdrawal scores remain low in those enrolled and thus would not influence the craving per se. They were randomized into three groups, namely naltrexone alone, baclofen alone, and naltrexone plus baclofen. The sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope technique was used for allocation concealment (done by AS). The randomization group was written on a piece of paper and kept in an opaque, sealed envelope. Then the envelope was labeled with a serial number & investigator (the first author) opened the sealed envelope once the patient had consented to participate and then assigned the treatment accordingly. The dosage was 40 to 60 mg a day (20−30 mg given twice daily) for baclofen and 50 mg a day for naltrexone. In the combination treatment group, baclofen (20−30 mg) was given twice daily, and naltrexone (50 mg) once daily. Those experiencing sedation with baclofen were given a lower dosage (40 mg per day) of medication. At baseline, a semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect information about socio-demographic data concerning family, social, psychological, medical, and legal problems and history of substance use patterns. Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS) and the visual analogue scale (VAS) for craving were used to assess craving for alcohol. The VAS score ranged from 0 to 100, with zero representing no craving and 100 representing the most intense alcohol related craving ever experienced. World Health Organization Quality of Life BREF (WHOQoL BREF) was used to assess the quality of life, and the World Health Organization 5 Well-being Scale (WHO-5) was used for well-being. For each treatment group, the medication was constant for 8 weeks. Patients were asked to complete a daily structured drinking diary regarding the type and the amount of alcohol consumed. At the follow-up assessment, they were asked to report if any alcohol was consumed, and the same was corroborated with the drinking diary and attendants (if present). Thereafter, an investigator transformed this information into standard drinks. Medication-related adverse effects were recorded on an adverse effect checklist, rating each adverse effect for its presumed association with the study medication. All the participants were sent a reminder text message regarding the follow-up date. The patient was reassessed by the first author at week 2 (+/− 3 days), week 4 (+/− 3 days), and week 8 (+/− 3 days). At each scheduled visit, the strip of used medication was collected and counted to evaluate medication adherence. Patients who relapsed were treated as dropouts.

Statistical Analysis

The results were obtained using SPSS software version 25 (IBM Co.) [18]. The quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. The chi-square test and ANOVA test were used to compare the socio-demographic and clinical variables. All analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle. Missing data were handled using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) imputation method. The LOCF method was also used for the participants who relapsed and were thus dropped from the study. Analysis was conducted for the clinical data using mixed design repeated measures ANOVA to analyse the change in scores over 8 weeks. The Greenhouse-Geisser method was used wherever the assumption of sphericity was violated. Simple effects analyses were performed to examine the differences between the groups at different time points. Tukey test was used for post-hoc analysis. The effect sizes were represented by partial eta squared (ηp2). The significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 145 patients were screened to enrol 97 patients in the study. Forty-eight patients were excluded. Eighty patients completed the final follow-up at 8 weeks. The total number of patients was 30, 32, and 35 in naltrexone alone, baclofen alone, and naltrexone-baclofen combination groups, respectively. The number of patients who followed up is depicted in Figure 1.

All the participants were male. No significant difference was present between the groups with respect to age, marital status, education, domicile, and level of education. The groups were comparable at baseline on all the clinical variables, including comorbid tobacco use disorder, family history of alcohol dependence, duration of alcohol dependence & CIWA-Ar score. However, there is a significant difference between the groups in the age of onset of alcohol use (Table 1).

The mean daily dose of trazodone used in the group naltrexone, baclofen, and naltrexone-baclofen combination was 47.7 ± 7.3, 45.28 ± 9.85, and 48.33 ± 7.12, respectively, and the difference was not significant (p = 0.31). The mean daily dose of baclofen used in the baclofen group and naltrexone- baclofen combination group was not significantly different (p = 0.25).

Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale Score

There was a significant main effect for time, F(2.62, 246.31) = 516.20, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.84 and time-by-group interaction, F(5.24, 246.31) = 5.27, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.10, indicating a decrease in OCDS scores with time. Additionally, there was a significant main effect of the treatment group, F(2, 94) = 4.15, p = 0.019, and ηp2 = 0.08, indicating that the reduction in OCDS scores was significantly different between the groups. Post hoc analysis revealed a significantly higher reduction in the OCDS score in the naltrexone-baclofen combination group as compared to the naltrexone group (p = 0.025) but not the baclofen group (p = 0.072). Also, the difference between the naltrexone group and the baclofen group was not significant (p = 0.889). Simple effects analysis revealed significant differences between naltrexone-baclofen combination group and the naltrexone and the baclofen group, at all the follow-ups (Table 2). Differences between the baclofen and the naltrexone group were not significant at baseline or follow-ups (Fig. 2).

Visual Analogue Scale Score

There was a significant main effect for time, F(2.46, 231.61) = 411.34, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.81 and time-by-group interaction, F(4.92, 231.61) = 2.36, p = 0.04, and ηp2 = 0.048, indicating a decrease in VAS scores with time. Additionally, there was a significant main effect of the treatment group, F(2, 94) = 3.12, p = 0.019, and ηp2 = 0.049, indicating that the reduction in VAS scores was significantly different between the groups. However, post hoc analysis reveals no significant difference between the three groups. Simple effects analysis revealed that naltrexone and baclofen group scores differed significantly from the naltrexone-baclofen combination group at week 2 and week 4, but not at week 8 (Table 2). Differences between the baclofen and the naltrexone group were not significant at baseline or follow-ups (Fig. 3).

World Health Organization Quality of Life BREF Score

There was a significant main effect for time, F(2.64, 248.98) = 467.57, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.83 and time-by-group interaction, F(5.29, 248.98) = 3.99, p ≤ 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.078, indicating an improvement in WHOQoL BREF scores with time. There was no significant main effect of the treatment group, F(2, 94) = 2.22, p = 0.113, indicating that the reduction in WHOQoL BREF score was not significantly different between the groups.

WHO Well-being Scale Score

There was a significant main effect for time, F(2.44, 229.63) = 523.64, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.84 and time-by-group interaction, F(4.88, 229.63) = 3.09, p < 0.01, and ηp2 = 0.062, indicating an improvement in WHO-5 scores with time. However, there was no significant main effect of the treatment group, F(2, 94) = 2.06, p = 0.132, indicating that the reduction in WHOQoL BREF score was not significantly different between the groups.

Heavy Episodic Drinking

There were four patients from the naltrexone alone group, six patients from the baclofen alone group, and six patients from the naltrexone-baclofen combination group who had episodes of heavy drinking during the study. However, the difference was not significant (χ2 = 0.34, p = 0.84).

Relapse Rates

There were four patients from the naltrexone alone group, three patients from the baclofen alone group, and three patients from the naltrexone-baclofen combination group who had relapsed during the study. However, the difference was not significant (χ2 = 0.44, p = 0.80).

Safety of the Treatment

The number of participants reporting side effects in the groups naltrexone alone, baclofen alone, and naltrexone-baclofen combination was 14 (46.6%), 13 (40.6%), and 21 (60.0%), respectively (Table 3). The commonly reported side effects in the naltrexone group included fatigue (n = 10), nausea (n = 8) and dizziness (n = 4). The common side effects in the baclofen group were nausea (n = 5) and dizziness (n = 3). The common side effects in the naltrexone-baclofen combination group included fatigue (n = 12), headache (n = 9), and nausea (n = 7). The side effects observed were minor and self-limiting. There was one dropout from the study in the naltrexone alone group due to gastrointestinal side effects.

DISCUSSION

Patients with alcohol dependence often experience multiple relapses during the course of their illness. Research suggests that only a small proportion of treated people with alcohol dependence remain abstinent over six months after treatment [19]. In this study, we attempted to determine the effectiveness of the combination of naltrexone and baclofen in managing craving and reducing relapse. The three groups were similar with respect to age, marital status, education, domicile, and level of education. All the study participants were males. This reflects the profile of alcohol use in general population in the study geography, where alcohol use is present in only about 0.3% of the women aged 15 years and above [20]. We ensured that the participants with minimal withdrawal scores get enrolled, reducing the need for additional medications [21].

The naltrexone-baclofen combination fared significantly better compared to naltrexone given alone in reducing craving. Similar findings of a greater benefit in terms of reduced alcohol consumption have been reported in animal studies [16,22]. However, despite a greater reduction in the craving scores in the combination group compared to the baclofen group, the difference was not significant. Also, there was a trend towards a decline in the difference over the study duration. Craving for alcohol is known to decline with increasing duration of abstinence [23]. Thus, the duration of abstinence itself may be a factor strongly influencing the decline in craving. These study findings related to change in OCDS and VAS scores indicate that the naltrexone-baclofen combination may be better in reducing craving than either medication given alone, particularly in the initial phases of abstinence.

Alcohol dependence is a chronic illness that requires long-term treatment and has a major impact on the quality of life of the affected individuals [24,25]. Deficits were found in all the quality of life domains in previous studies [25,26]. In the current study, the quality of life improved with time in all groups. Earlier studies also report improvement in the quality of life as a result of abstinence, controlled or minimal drinking [27]. However, the difference between groups in the current study was not significant. Quality of life is a composite index that encompasses various perceptions related to physical health, mental health, social relationships, and the environment. Thus, only a reduction in craving due to the combination treatment may not be sufficient to substantially influence the subjective quality of life among people with alcohol dependence.

Mental well-being, which denotes positive mental health and emotional well-being, is of particular importance in behavior change efforts aimed at improving lifestyles in patients with alcohol dependence. In this study, the well-being of participants in all three groups was low at baseline, indicated by the WHO-5 scores of less than 50 [28]. The scores improved over eight weeks in all three groups. However, the difference between groups was not statistically significant. Thus, the combination treatment failed to significantly improve the well-being of the study participants. This highlights the fact that while craving management is an important aspect of the treatment of alcohol dependence associated with improved outcomes, there is a crucial role of interventions aimed at addressing othermental well-being domains, including emotional, physical, social, spiritual, and environmental health, in improving mental well-being [29].

Although we observed a significant difference in craving between the three groups, the relapse and lapse rates are not very different in the treatment groups because lapse and relapse depend on many other factors, and craving is only one of them [30,31]. We observed a smaller number of relapses and lapses compared to the previous studies [19]. It may be due to the shorter study duration and the text reminders sent to participants for follow-up. The follow-up reminders could have contributed to better retention in treatment.

The frequently reported side effects in this study included nausea, fatigue, and headache. Similar findings of mild and tolerable side effects have been reported in other studies [32]. No new side effect not previously mentioned in the literature was observed or reported. One patient in the naltrexone group discontinued the medication for two days due to diarrhoea, after which he restarted the medication. One participant dropped out of the study in the naltrexone group owing to side effects, including weakness, dizziness, reduced appetite, headache, and anxiety, after which he discontinued the medication. None of the side effects required any emergency medical intervention.

In this open-label study, the lack of blinding was an important limitation of the study. Also, though the participants in all three groups were sent reminders, these reminders could have affected the study outcomes by influencing the retention of participants in the study. Additionally, the follow-up period for participants in this study was 8 weeks. A study with a longer follow-up would provide deeper understanding of how the intervention impacts the parameters examined. Moreover, the findings could not be generalised to female patients with alcohol dependence due to the lack of female participants in this study.

In conclusion, the availability of limited alcohol craving management options underscores the need to explore new strategies and regimens. This randomized, open-label trial supports the use of combination therapy for the reduction of craving in AUD. The naltrexone-baclofen combination was better in reducing the craving scores compared to naltrexone and baclofen given alone. Also, the combination treatment is well-tolerated and can be used for the early reduction of craving for alcohol. Based on the findings of this study, further exploration of the role of combination therapy in the management of alcohol dependence is recommended.
