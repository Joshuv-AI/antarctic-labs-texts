Omental Cystic Lymphangioma as a Rare Abdominal Tumor: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ali Mirsadeghi, Sina Esmailpour, Arefeh Moballegh, Farnood Forouhar, Narges Mesbah

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the case of a 30‐year‐old man who experienced intermittent abdominal pain for 1.5 years. He presented with worsening pain during defecation and bladder filling. A CT scan revealed a large abdominal cystic lesion, along with smaller cysts in the pelvis. Surgical resection of the cystic lesions was performed via laparotomy, which also included an appendectomy and omentectomy. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of cystic lymphangiomas.Keywords: abdominal pain, case report, cystic lymphangioma, omental tumor

Introduction

Cystic lymphangiomas are rare benign tumors that result from a developmental failure of the lymphatic system or inflammation of lymphatic vessels causing obstruction and subsequent development of lymphangioma [1, 2]. Roughly 90% of cystic lymphangiomas are diagnosed in individuals younger than 2 years old, and about 65% of them are present at birth. The neck is the most commonly affected area, accounting for 75% of cases, followed by the axilla at 20%. Abdominal involvement is considerably rarer, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Among those with abdominal cystic lymphangiomas, roughly 1% are located in the mesentery; within this subset, the small intestine is the most frequent site, comprising 85% of cases, followed by the mesocolon at 10% and the retroperitoneum at 1% [3].

The clinical features of Abdominal Cystic lymphangiomas (ACL) in adults remain unclear; the prevalence in adults may be vastly underestimated because of the lack of recognition of the disease or atypical clinical presentation. It may be misdiagnosed as a pancreatic pseudocyst, ovarian cyst, renal cyst, or other diseases based on location [4, 5]. Imaging using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), or ultrasound (US) is the primary method used to diagnose CL. It should thereafter be confirmed by histology [6, 7].

In this case, we have presented a 33‐year‐old man with atypical abdominal pain. Imaging has revealed cystic masses in his abdomen and pelvis.

Case History

Case Presentation

A 30‐year‐old man came to our hospital with intermittent abdominal pain that had started 1.5 years earlier. He described the pain as a dull, aching sensation that varied in intensity and worsened with certain movements. An initial ultrasound showed a bulky lesion in the right lower quadrant; however, the patient did not seek further evaluation as the pain subsided. Three months before admission, the patient experienced increasing pain during defecation and bladder filling, which significantly affected his quality of life. A colonoscopy yielded normal results; however, an ultrasound revealed several masses in the pelvic vesicorectal region. The pain subsided three weeks before admission, and the patient remained active without any systemic symptoms.

At admission, the patient experienced pain during bladder filling but did not report any other notable symptoms. The patient did not report any family history, past medical history, or drug history. He has no known allergies, no surgical history, and does not smoke or consume alcohol. The patient's vital signs were as follows: blood pressure was 105/70 mmHg, respiratory rate was 19 breaths per minute, temperature was 36.7°C, and heart rate was 85 beats per minute. During the abdominal examination, fullness was noted in the right abdomen and mild tenderness in both the right abdomen and hypogastrium. Laboratory results indicated a slightly elevated fasting blood sugar level of 104 mg/dL, with no other abnormalities. The CT scan revealed a relatively large cystic lesion (approximately 10 × 16 × 20 cm) on the right side of the abdomen, between the anterior abdominal wall and the intestinal loops, extending to the superior pelvis. Additionally, two cystic lesions, measuring 5 and 3 cm in diameter, were found in the pelvis between the bladder and rectum (Figure 1).

Axial and coronal CT scan of abdomen and pelvis before surgery. The lesion is marked with a black arrow and star.

Management and Surgical Findings

During the laparotomy, a large multinodular cystic lesion was discovered in the right abdomen. This lesion originated from the greater omentum and was located between the abdominal wall and the loops of the small intestine. It was connected to both the ascending colon and the liver. Additionally, a pelvic tumor was identified between the bladder and the rectum. All of these lesions were successfully resected. An appendectomy and an omentectomy were also performed (see Figure 2).

Findings during laparotomy. Pelvic tumor was identified between the bladder and the rectum (white arrow and star). Lesion was discovered in the right abdomen (black arrow and star).

Postoperative Course

During his three‐day stay in the hospital, the patient did not experience a fever. He began a liquid diet on the second day, and by the third day, his bowel movements had returned. He was discharged with a prescription and returned ten days later for suture removal. Upon evaluation, it was noted that he was eating adequately and his overall condition was stable.

Pathology Report

A creamy‐colored multi‐cystic mass measuring 12 × 10 × 6 cm with many variable‐sized cysts containing watery fluid showed a cystic mass lined by cuboidal or flat epithelium containing acidophilic fluid without RBCs (Abdominal tumor). Furthermore, a large black cyst measuring 4 × 3 × 3 cm, brown with turbid fluid and a thin wall, showed a cystic lesion with a single attenuated layer, inflammatory reaction, focal necrosis, congestion, and aggregation of hemosiderophages (pelvic tumor). The final diagnosis for both was cystic lymphangioma.

Discussion and Conclusion

This is the case of a 30‐year‐old man with a multifocal abdominopelvic cystic lymphangioma, who had an atypical presentation of the disease, therefore raising the diagnostic difficulty of this condition. The patient was managed conservatively initially, then underwent surgery one year later, and the pathology report established the diagnosis of cystic lymphangioma.

Lymphangiomas are benign tumors that occur due to the failure of early lymphatic spaces to communicate with the lymphatic system [8]. Congenital conditions commonly occur in children, particularly affecting the limbs, head, neck, and axilla. Though adult cases are infrequent, these lesions have been observed in the omentum, gastrosplenic ligament, and pouch of Douglas, and as intramural lesions in the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, liver, kidneys, adrenals, and pancreas [9]. The presentation of the patient with lymphangiomas in the abdominal wall, omentum, and pelvis is rather atypical.

The following are the common signs and symptoms of lymphangioma, which include: distention of the abdomen, tenderness of the abdomen, ascites, nausea, and constipation, as reported in this case. However, this patient had atypical presentation, including bowel motion difficulty, passing urine at night, abdominal pain, right flank pain, and bladder distension. This goes to show that the presentation of lymphangiomas in the adult abdomen is extremely versatile, ranging from no symptoms at all to severe abdominal pain and compression symptoms. Some of the less common presentations are weight loss, infection, and intra‐cystic hemorrhage [4, 10, 11].

Diagnosing abdominal cystic lymphangioma before surgery is difficult, often leading to incorrect initial diagnoses. Ultrasound is usually the first imaging test. It helps distinguish lymphangioma from other fluid‐filled sacs in the abdomen by examining the cyst wall's thickness, internal structures, and any echoes within the fluid. CT scans show these cysts as areas of low density. MRI scans provide more detailed information. They show a low signal on T2‐weighted images and a high signal on T1‐weighted images, without any enhancement after contrast injection. MRI is also helpful in determining the cyst's relationship with surrounding organs. While these imaging techniques can provide clues, a definitive diagnosis is only possible through a microscopic examination of the tissue removed during surgery [12].

In this specific case, before the correct diagnosis, other possibilities considered included mesenteric cysts and cystic mesothelioma. Despite diagnostic uncertainty, laparotomy was performed. Management of abdominal cystic lymphangiomas depends on the presentation: asymptomatic, incidentally discovered lymphangiomas may warrant observation due to a reported spontaneous regression rate of up to 16%. However, asymptomatic cases may require intervention if complications like bleeding or volvulus arise. Percutaneous methods are typically reserved for patients with contraindications to surgery [13, 14, 15]. Both laparoscopy and open surgery are choices for treatment, but in cases with huge masses (mass > 2 cm), open surgery is recommended by well‐experienced specialists [16, 17]. Our patient had a large and multifocal cystic abdominal lymphangioma and we didn't know about its origin ACL.

Histopathological examination reveals three main types of lymphangiomas: capillary, cavernous, and cystic. Capillary lymphangiomas are characterized by a network of small, uniform lymph spaces; cavernous lymphangiomas consist of dilated lymphatic spaces and lymphoid stroma connected to adjacent normal lymphatic systems; and cystic lymphangiomas are composed of dilated lymphatic spaces embedded within a stroma of smooth muscle and collagen, lacking connection to the normal lymphatic drainage system [18]. This histopathological classification is essential for accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate treatment strategies [19].

This case presented a young man with atypical symptoms. Cystic lymphangiomas are rare tumors occurring commonly in childhood, and they are rare in adults. The clinical symptoms differ from patient to patient. Despite all of the paraclinical data, surgery and resection of the mass is the gold‐standard method for diagnosing and treatment of cystic lymphangiomas.
