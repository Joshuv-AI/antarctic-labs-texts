When Does Hip Dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement Peak After Primary Total Hip Arthroplasty?
Authors: Nicholas R. Olson, Alexander V. Strait, Nancy L. Parks, Tobenna N. Nwankwo, William G. Hamilton, Robert A. Sershon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Hip dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS, JR) metric has been increasingly utilized to evaluate pain and function following total hip arthroplasty (THA); however, less is known about when these scores reach a maximum. The purpose of this study was to determine when HOOS, JR scores peak and surpass established clinical thresholds.MethodsA cloud-based patient engagement platform was queried for all HOOS, JR responses before and after elective primary THA at our institution. This resulted in a total of 8615 responses from 1866 THAs collected between December 2019 and March 2024. Procedures were performed between January 2011 and March 2024. Surveys were collected preoperatively and at 4 weeks, 4 months, and 1, 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively. Primary outcomes included time points when HOOS, JR peaked, and when clinically important improvements were achieved. Survey responses were analyzed to compare recovery times for specific pain and function parameters.ResultsStatistically significant (P < .05) increases in HOOS, JR scores occurred through 1-year follow-up, although mean scores continued to increase incrementally through 10 years (P > .05). Substantial clinical benefits (+22) from baseline (53.4 ± 14.1) and the patient acceptable symptom state (76.7) were both achieved between 4 weeks (73.6) and 4 months (84.5) postoperatively. After 4 months, no additional clinically meaningful improvements occurred.ConclusionsThe statistical peak in HOOS, JR scores after primary THA occurred around 1-year follow-up, and most clinically meaningful improvements occurred by 4 months. Patients can expect early meaningful improvements walking on an uneven surface and can expect prolonged difficulty with bending to the floor. Clinicians may incorporate this information to better set patient expectations and encourage their postoperative rehabilitation.

Introduction

The utilization of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) continues to increase for patients undergoing elective primary total hip arthroplasty (THA) [1,2]. Efficient short-form scoring systems are now widely available, including the validated Hip dysfunction and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS, JR) [3], which quantifies the subjective patient experience of pain and functional limitations during activities of daily living. The HOOS, JR questionnaire is a validated, short-form survey developed in 2016 that measures hip-related pain and function for patients undergoing THA (Fig. 1) [3]. The HOOS, JR questionnaire was deemed to have high internal consistency, excellent external validity when compared to other hip surveys, high responsiveness and favorable floor and ceiling effects, demonstrating the usefulness and value of administering HOOS, JR surveys to all patients receiving a THA [3].Clinical thresholds have been proposed to interpret patient improvements from baseline through follow-up, including the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [[4], [5], [6], [7]], minimal detectable change (MDC) [4,7,8], substantial clinical benefit (SCB) [7,8], and patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) [4,6,9,10]. Preoperatively collecting hip-specific PROMs has been useful in predicting which patients will (and will not) receive a meaningful clinical benefit from surgery [11,12], and routine assessment of postoperative scores has proven beneficial in identifying patients at increased risk of early revision [13]. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services in the United States has also begun mandating the collection and reporting of PROMs following THA, and hospitals that do not track their patient outcomes will face penalties [14,15]. Incentivized by United States government mandates as well as the betterment of their patients, the utility of PROMs has been pushed to the forefront.Figure 1The HOOS, JR outcome survey, developed at the Hospital for Special Surgery.

In the literature on hip-related PROMs, previous studies have focused on identifying patient-specific factors associated with not achieving clinically meaningful improvements in pain or function after THA, such as female sex, morbid obesity, poor mental health, and higher preoperative hip scores [6,[16], [17], [18]]. There has also been evidence to suggest that patients with a history of lumbar spinal fusion, smokers, and patients taking preoperative opioids achieve lower mean HOOS, JR scores after surgery [[18], [19], [20]]. This information is particularly valuable during the preoperative shared decision-making process between surgeons and patients regarding appropriate timing of THA and for whom surgery may need to be postponed or avoided. However, limited data are available to aid postoperative counseling for patients who are unsatisfied or frustrated by their recovery progress, and many surgeons rely on anecdotal evidence and clinical experience to support these discussions.

While the largest relative improvements in pain and function occur in the first few months after surgery for the majority of patients [21,22], less is known about the time required for HOOS, JR scores to reach a maximum and the specific recovery times of individual functions after THA. The purpose of this study was to determine the clinically relevant time points when HOOS, JR scores reached a statistical peak and surpassed established clinical thresholds after elective, primary THA at our high-volume institution. With the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services in the United States mandating the collection and reporting of PROMs, this tool and its projected pathway is important to understand and document, including which questions improve early and late, and when significant changes are expected and predicted to level off. This information would inform clinicians on the progression of HOOS, JR scores throughout patient recovery to better counsel patients and set recovery expectations. Secondarily, we aimed to assess individual survey responses linked to suboptimal scores and identify pain and function parameters that were slowest to recover after surgery. We hypothesized that improvements in HOOS, JR would be seen at all time points; however, they would no longer be statistically significant beyond 1-year follow-up.

Material and Methods

An institutional review board determined that this study met the criteria for exemption prior to the start of the investigation. Study data were prospectively collected and analyzed using an integrated, cloud-based patient engagement platform (PatientIQ, Chicago IL). Queries were performed for all HOOS, JR responses before and after elective primary THA at our high-volume institution. This validated, short-form survey is collected for all patients receiving a THA who choose to participate. All HOOS, JR surveys that were collected between December 2019 and March 2024 were included in this study. A small percentage of the cohort (5%) had a prior contralateral THA. Given the focus of the study, responses from these cases were not separated or analyzed distinctively from unilateral THA cases. The only surveys that were excluded were those that were incomplete at the time of query, as the survey needs to be complete in order for a score to be calculated, and those corresponding to revision THA, as only primary surgeries were included in this study. With a response rate of 76% over the study period, we collected a total of 8615 HOOS, JR responses. These questionnaires were regarding 1866 elective primary THAs performed between January 2011 and March 2024 by 7 fellowship-trained surgeons. The HOOS, JR surveys were collected preoperatively at an average of 14 days before surgery, and at 4 weeks, 4 months, 1 year, 2 years, 5 years, and 10 years postoperatively. All patients at our institution receive the same standard of care postoperatively, which includes home therapy aimed to increase function and independence upon returning home after surgery. The study cohort comprised 57% women, and the mean age at the time of surgery was 70.5 ± 10.8 years. The mean body mass index (BMI) of subjects was 29.85 ± 0.212 kg/m2. No matching was performed between the cohorts at each follow-up interval, however, the mean age at the time of surgery was similar among respondents at every interval except the respondents at the 10-year interval had a significantly lower age at the time of surgery and the largest mean age of any interval at the time of survey collection. The 10-year interval cohort had a significantly lower BMI than respondents at every other interval, the 5-year interval cohort had a significantly lower BMI than the preoperative, 4-week, and 4-month intervals, and the 2-year interval group had a significantly lower BMI when compared to the preoperative interval respondents.

The primary outcome measures included the time points when HOOS, JR scores peaked statistically and clinically important improvements were achieved (MCID, MDC, PASS and SCB). Secondarily, individual survey responses at all time points were analyzed (with possible answers of none, mild, moderate, severe, and extreme) for questions related to both hip pain and function during the preceding week. This breakdown allowed for identification of the pain and function parameters that were the slowest to recover after surgery in the study population.

Improvements in mean HOOS, JR scores at follow-up were compared to the preoperative baseline and evaluated using previously established MCID, MDC, PASS, and SCB values from the literature (Table 1). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies. Continuous variables were reported using means and standard deviations and were compared using independent sample t-tests. The threshold for statistical significance was defined as 0.05. Results

Statistically significant increases in HOOS, JR scores, relative to the previous collection interval, were observed at all time points through 1-year follow-up (P < .05; Table 2), indicating the statistical peak (Fig. 2). Mean scores continued to increase incrementally beyond the peak through 10 years postoperatively (P > .05; Table 2). Regarding the established clinical thresholds, the SCB of 22 points from the mean baseline score (53.4 ± 14.1) and the PASS of 76.7 for HOOS, JR were both achieved between 4 weeks (73.6 ± 12.3) and 4 months (84.5 ± 13.0) postoperatively. Between 4 weeks and 4 months follow-up, the increase in mean HOOS, JR scores constituted an additional improvement that met the MDC (using an 80% confidence interval) of 8 points, and the combined median MCID of 10 points. This reflects that walking on an uneven surface is the earliest activity to see improvement in pain when performing. The activity that patients report the least difficulty with postoperatively is sitting. Patients report having difficulty sitting the most infrequently out of any subcategory at every follow-up interval except at 2 years due a small increase at this interval. The activity that patients have the most difficulty with later in recovery is bending to the floor and picking up objects. At 10 years postoperatively, 1.9% of patients report severe or extreme difficulty and 9.1% of patients report moderate, severe, or extreme difficulty bending to the floor and picking up objects. After showing marked improvement with pain while going up or down stairs, walking on an uneven surface, and rising from sitting, there was a second peak in patients reporting pain at 5 years postoperatively. Discussion

Our institutional experience with HOOS, JR collection before and after elective primary THA demonstrated continued improvement in scores over time, and statistically significant score increases between intervals up to 1 year postoperatively. Interestingly, all clinically important thresholds examined in this study were surpassed by 4-month follow-up, including MCID, MDC, PASS, and SCB. Analysis of individual pain and function parameters suggested that for most patients, pain while accessing stairs and difficulty bending to the floor were the greatest limiting factors in their recovery. However, as with the other functions in the questionnaire, patients achieved dramatic improvements in these areas by 4 months, and the percentage of favorable responses (none or mild pain/difficulty) continued to grow at both 1 and 2 years after surgery.

While improvements were observed in mean HOOS, JR scores between 1 and 10 years postoperatively, the total increase during this interval was 2.2 points and did not reach statistical significance. Although a clear upward trend was present, no clinically important improvements can be attributed based on the previously established thresholds for this specific PROM. Still, analysis of individual survey responses suggested that small, subjective improvements may occur for patients beyond 1-year follow-up for functions that require greater balance and strength, such as climbing stairs and bending over to pick up objects. Surgeons may apply this finding to clinical practice when counseling patients at postoperative visits. Specifically, if patients are unsatisfied with their pain or function levels at 4 months, these issues may improve or completely resolve by the 1- or 2-year follow-up visit. Likewise, if a patient reports new or unresolved pain and functional limitations at 1 year after surgery, further diagnostic workup may be warranted to identify an underlying cause.

In a large multicenter series of 1898 patients, Sato et al. [21] described the recovery curve of PROMs and physical function within the first postoperative year following THA. In agreement with our results, they reported statistically significant improvements in HOOS, JR at all intervals up to 12 months of follow-up, with the greatest rate of recovery occurring during the first month. From data collected using wearable technology, they found that the number of flights of stairs climbed by patients took 1 year to surpass the MCID, which agrees with the qualitative survey response data regarding pain with stairs in the current study. Furthermore, gait speed and walking asymmetry, which are not accounted for in the HOOS, JR questionnaire, did not reach one MCID by the maximum follow-up time of 1 year, indicating additional room for improvement in these functions at longer follow-up times.

There are several limitations of this investigation. It is a retrospective review of all prospectively collected HOOS, JR responses, which is a PROM that was developed in 2016 and implemented at our institution in December 2019. As a result, mean scores at each time point before and after surgery are based on total responses, and not necessarily the same individual patients. For example, for the 10-year follow-up data, these patients underwent THA prior to the time when HOOS, JR was adopted, and thus baseline scores are unavailable. However, as the focus of this investigation was on the entire study population, and the mean scores and response type frequencies at different time points, we believe this limitation to be less important. As far as introducing variation due to differences between patient groups, no matching was performed but the cohorts were compared to evaluate for similarities. The groups are similar concerning BMI and mean age at the time of surgery. Only the 10-year follow-up interval group displayed a statistically significant difference in age at the time of surgery, which was significantly lower than all other groups. We believe this is likely due to the fact that older surgical patients may be deceased or less likely to return for follow-up appointments 10 years postoperatively and beyond. The 10-year interval group was also the only group to demonstrate a statistically significant difference in BMI when compared to every other interval group and was lower than respondents at every other interval. The variation seen in age and BMI reflect the institutional trends over the previous 10 years, with a decrease in the age at the time of primary arthroplasty and increase in BMI. In regard to the HOOS, JR metric, a potential limitation of this scoring system is that a ceiling effect may be reached, as evidenced by the large proportion of patients achieving high numerical scores within the first year after surgery. Study data were also collected at regular follow-up intervals previously established at the institution and was collected preoperatively and at 4 weeks, 4 months, 1 year, 2 years, 5 years, and 10 years postoperatively. Although graphically the curve in Figure 2 would be smoother and more refined with more frequent sampling than was performed here, the trend observed and the conclusions drawn would be unchanged. Additionally, HOOS, JR was designed as an efficient and reliable short-form PROM and may lack the ability to detect more subtle differences in a patient’s experience. As we strive toward improving outcomes, patient-reported metrics with greater sensitivity and higher success thresholds are warranted.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the follow-up time required for HOOS, JR scores to peak following THA. Additionally, this is one of few studies to provide long-term HOOS, JR response data from a high-volume institution. Our findings provide an evidence-based understanding of subjective patient recovery in pain and function parameters after surgery and add to the growing body of literature on hip-related PROMs. Surgeons may utilize this information, the collection of which is now mandatory, to define realistic expectations prior to surgery, counsel patients at follow-up visits, and identify patients early that are deviating from the expected recovery course.

Conclusions

HOOS, JR score collection before and after elective primary THA demonstrated continued improvement in scores over time, and statistically significant score increases between intervals up to 1 year postoperatively. Improvements in HOOS, JR scores were observed up to our longest follow-up of 10 years postoperatively, but the increase in mean score between 1 and 10 years did not reach statistical significance, and no additional clinically important changes were seen in this window. All clinically important thresholds were surpassed by 4-month follow-up. Pain while climbing stairs and difficulty bending to the floor were the greatest limitations to recovery, and patients achieved dramatic improvements in most areas by 4 months.
