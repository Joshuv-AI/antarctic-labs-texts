Does pre-operative left ventricular function predict mitral valve function after anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery repair?
Authors: Raji Venkitachalam, Katherine Cashen, Christopher W Mastropietro, Liyun Zhang, Yan Ke, Peter C Frommelt, Ronald K Woods, Collaborative Research from the Pediatric Cardiac Intensive Care Society (CoRe-PCICS) Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the progression of mitral valve (MV) function before and after anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) repair in those with preserved versus impaired left ventricular (LV) function.MethodsThis is a post-hoc analysis of patients who underwent repair for ALCAPA at 22 Collaborative Research from the Pediatric Cardiac Intensive Care Society centers. Mitral regurgitation (MR) severity was compared on the basis of LV function at diagnosis and through 3 years of follow-up.ResultsAmong 228 patients, 76 had normal/mild LV dysfunction and 152 had moderate/severe LV dysfunction (median ejection fraction 60% vs 23%, P < .0001). Preoperatively, moderate or greater MR was more common with moderate/severe LV dysfunction (51% vs 26%, P < .001). At 3-year follow-up, those with moderate or greater MR decreased significantly in this group (51% to 13%; odds ratio, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.05-0.25; P < .0001), with no significant change in the normal/mild LV dysfunction group (26% to 16%; OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.06-1.39; P = .12).ConclusionsIn patients with ALCAPA and MV dysfunction with preserved LV function, MV recovery is less common, highlighting the consideration for concomitant MV repair.Key Words: ALCAPA surgery, concomitant mitral valve repair, intermediate outcomes, mitral regurgitation

Progression of moderate/severe MR based on preoperative LV function in ALCAPA patients.

Central Message

For patients with ALCAPA who present with normal/mild LV dysfunction, improvement in MR after ALCAPA surgery is less likely compared with patients presenting with moderate/severe LV dysfunction.

Perspective

Spontaneous improvement in MR after ALCAPA repair appears to vary based on LV function at presentation. In the subset of patients with moderate or severe MR and normal LV function at diagnosis, clinicians should assess the MV anatomy and morphology preoperatively for consideration of concomitant MV repair.

For the infant presenting with an anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), severe left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, and mitral regurgitation (MR), most advocate for isolated ALCAPA repair via coronary reimplantation.1,2 In the majority of patients, LV function and MR normalize after a period of months.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 However, as the result of compensatory collateralization and other factors, older patients and even some infants may present with preserved function and clinically relevant MR. One possible explanation for the latter finding at presentation is that a previous ischemic event involving the subvalve apparatus results in patchy infarction (more vulnerable terminal vasculature), which leads to mitral valve (MV) dysfunction. Compensatory flow, likely from collateral circulation, then restores function of an LV that did not experience significant infarction but does not restore function of the variably infarcted subvalvular apparatus.

The traditional approach for most patients with ALCAPA is to manage the MR medically and avoid surgical intervention of the MV at the time of repair.8, 9, 10 Some authors recommend consideration of concomitant MV repair in the presence of severe MR.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 To our knowledge, data comparing the intermediate-term outcomes of MR in patients presenting with preserved LV function and those presenting more classically with moderate-to-severe LV dysfunction have not been published. We aimed to compare the trajectory of MV function in patients with ALCAPA who present with preserved LV function with those with more severe LV dysfunction. We hypothesized that those presenting with preserved LV function at presentation would have persistent MR after isolated ALCAPA repair.

Methods

Study Population

This study is a post-hoc subanalysis of retrospectively collected data contained within previously published studies for Collaborative Research from the Pediatric Cardiac Intensive Care Society,7,16,17 a multicenter research collaborative that included 22 tertiary care congenital cardiac centers. Patients who underwent surgery for isolated ALCAPA from January 2009 to March 2018 at participating institutions were included. Patients with additional complex congenital heart lesions and patients who underwent MV surgery at the time of ALCAPA repair were excluded from analysis. The scope of the original Collaborative Research from the Pediatric Cardiac Intensive Care Society study was to describe characteristics and operative outcomes of patients and to identify factors related to major cardiovascular adverse events.17 A subsequent multicenter analysis further examined the characteristics and outcomes of patients with late presentation of ALCAPA.18 Our study includes data from both the original cohort and the late-presentation cohort in addition to newly accrued patients from Children's Wisconsin and aims to further analyze the evolution of MR on the basis of preoperative LV function rather than age at presentation.

Data Acquisition and Definitions

The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the coordinating center (Indiana University IRB Study #1804018472, approved April 27, 2018) and IRBs of each participating site. Because of the retrospective nature of the data collection, the need for informed consent was waived. Deidentified data were collected by investigators at individual institutions using a centralized Indiana University REDCap database. Echocardiogram findings were entered into the database as qualitative and quantitative variables, as available from institutional reports.

We sought to compare the progression of MV function before and after surgical repair for patients with ALCAPA and preserved LV function versus those with ALCAPA and LV dysfunction. We compared patient outcomes on the basis of LV function at initial presentation and classified them into 2 groups on the basis of qualitative LV function: normal or mildly diminished LV systolic function and moderate-to-severely diminished LV systolic function. The groups were formed on the basis of the preoperative LV function. There was no crossover between the groups with any change in LV function over time. Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes of the 2 cohorts were compared, including LV functional status and the degree of MR after surgery. MR data were obtained from the institutional echocardiogram reports. We compared the proportion of subjects who had moderate or greater MR between the 2 LV function groups at 4 different time points: (1) preoperatively at the time of presentation/diagnosis; (2) at the time of postoperative discharge; (3) at 1 year follow-up after surgery, and (4) at 3 years follow-up after surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR] and categorical variables are presented as frequency (%). Variables were compared using χ2, Fisher exact, and Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate for individual variables. A generalized linear mixed model with maximum likelihood estimation was used to examine the change of MR over time between comparison groups. The model used the binary distribution, with the event of having moderate or greater MR, and logit link function. Subjects were treated as clusters, and the covariance matrix for within-subject correlation used was compound symmetry. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc).

Median LV ejection fraction at presentation was 60% (IQR, 53%-68%) in patients with normal/mild LV dysfunction and 23% (IQR, 17%-31%) in patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction (P < .0001). Patients with normal/mild LV dysfunction were older at presentation (P < .0001; median 5 years IQR, 0.4-15.0 years) than patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction; median 0.25 years (IQR, 0.2-0.5 years). Papillary muscle ischemia was present in 70% of patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction group versus 51% of the normal/mild LV dysfunction group. Median LV ejection fraction for patients who presented with moderate/severe LV dysfunction normalized at 1 year and remained normal at 3 years follow-up. Mortality occurred in 1 subject after ALCAPA repair but prior to hospital discharge, which was attributed to multiple pre-existing comorbidities.

In total, 97 of 228 patients at diagnosis had moderate or greater MR. This decreased to 25 of 152 patients at 1-year follow-up and to 15 of 108 patients at 3 years follow-up. All the patients who had severe MR at 3-year follow-up also had severe MR at diagnosis. There was missing data in both the LV function groups. In the normal/mild LV dysfunction group, 34 patients did not have data regarding their MR severity at 1-year follow-up and 51 at 3 years follow-up and in the moderate/severe LV dysfunction group, 42 did not have data about MR at 1-year follow-up and 69 at 3 years follow-up (Table 1 and Figure 2 show the attrition).

Comparison of MR Over Time

At the time of diagnosis, moderate or greater MR was present in 26% of patients with normal/mild LV dysfunction and in 51% of patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction (P < .001). By postoperative discharge, the proportion of patients with moderate or greater MR decreased to 18% among those with normal/mild LV dysfunction and to 32% among those with moderate/severe LV dysfunction. After hospital discharge, 2 patients with normal/mild LV dysfunction at diagnosis and 4 patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction underwent MV surgery. These patients were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Odds ratios (ORs) for having moderate or greater MR over time in both LV function groups are provided in Table 2. Among patients with normal/mild LV dysfunction, the odds of moderate or greater MR were significantly lower at 1 year compared with preoperative values (OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.08-0.96; P = .043). However, at 3 years, this was no longer statistically significant (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.06,1.39; P = .12), indicating that the initial improvement was not sustained beyond 1 year follow-up. In addition, comparisons between postoperative discharge and subsequent follow-up demonstrated no significant changes in the odds of moderate or greater MR at 1 year (OR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.17-2.40; P = .50) or at 3 years (OR, 0.66, 95% CI, 0.13-3.47; P = .62).

In contrast, patients with moderate/severe LV dysfunction at diagnosis demonstrated significant and sustained reductions in moderate or greater MR at both 1 year (OR, 0.14; 95% CI, 0.07-0.28; P < .0001) and 3 years (OR, 0.11; 95% CI, 0.05-0.25; P < .0001) compared with preoperative values. These improvements remained significant when follow-up time points were compared with postoperative discharge, with a reduction from 32% at discharge to 16% at 1 year (OR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.18-0.69; P = .002) and to 13% at 3 years (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.13-0.62; P = .002).

Patients Who Underwent Concomitant MV Repair

In total, 24 patients underwent concomitant MV repair at the time of ALCAPA repair and were excluded from further analyses. At diagnosis, 3 of them had trivial or mild MR, 7 had moderate MR, and 14 had severe MR. At 1-year follow-up, 6 patients were lost to follow-up, and by 3 years, 8 were lost to follow-up. Among patients with available follow-up data, only 1 patient with severe MR at diagnosis continued to have severe MR at 3-year follow-up, and 2 patients with moderate MR at diagnosis had persistent moderate MR at follow-up. All remaining patients demonstrated improvement in MR severity over time.

Discussion

In this study of a large, multi-institutional cohort of patients with ALCAPA, we focused on the intermediate-term status of MV function in patients with preserved LV function at presentation and isolated ALCAPA repair. Our primary finding was that, in contrast to the group with LV dysfunction and MR at presentation, the group with preserved LV function and MR demonstrated notably less improvement in MV function out to 3 years of follow-up. Our findings suggest that concomitant MV repair at the time of ALCAPA repair should be considered for this subset of patients.

Other reports comment on the debate of concomitant MV repair, with some emphasizing the influence of evidence of irreversible damage to the papillary muscles or structural abnormalities of the MV as important factors in making this decision.4,15,18 However, these are typically stated as institutional practices or approaches, without the provision of clearly supportive data. A meta-analysis by Brancaccio and colleagues19 highlights the intercenter heterogeneity in indications for MV surgery, with some authors advocating a more proactive approach to concomitant MV, particularly in patients with moderate or greater MR.12,14,20 Hu and colleagues12, 14 associated concomitant MV intervention during coronary revascularization with greater MR improvement during the immediate postoperative period, whereas Isomatsu and colleagues12, 14 reported consistently favorable early outcomes with routine MV intervention when MR was severe. Yu and colleagues20 identified concomitant MV surgery as an important factor for postoperative MR improvement in their cohort of patients. The alternative practice is not to intervene on the MV irrespective of the severity of MR or LV function at the time of ALCAPA repair.2,3,8, 9, 10,21 This is based on the observations that functional MR improves when coronary circulation is restored, and LV geometry normalizes. MV surgery is therefore reserved for patients with preoperatively identified structural valve disease, rather than for those with MR solely on the basis of severity, or for patients who have persistent MR on surveillance.

The typical argument against concomitant mitral repair invokes the potential downside of prolonged ischemic and operative times in a child who is already quite ill with severe LV dysfunction.4,21 We view this argument as much less of a concern in a patient with preserved LV function. Therefore, our current approach at Children's Wisconsin for patients with preserved LV function and moderate or worse MR is to perform concomitant MV repair tailored to specific echocardiographic findings. In the absence of real or relative leaflet prolapse or other specific valve/subvalve abnormalities, we would still consider at least an annular-reduction procedure—for a growing child, extensive commissuroplasties and reduction of the mid portion of the posterior annulus, typically tying suture over a dilator sized to no smaller than an annular z score of −2. For an adult-sized patient, we would use a formal annuloplasty ring. In older children and adults, we would also consider a reduction of the interpapillary muscle distance.

Our study is unique in that it distinguishes patients on the basis of relevant MR and preserved versus impaired LV function. We think this approach is more physiologically relevant and reflects the underlying mechanisms by which MV function might or might not improve with isolated ALCAPA repair. In patients with severe LV dysfunction and MR, ischemic or hibernating myocardium results in a reversibly dilated LV and mitral annulus with recoverable LV myocardium and papillary muscles after revascularization, which most likely explains why the isolated ALCAPA repair is usually successful. However, preserved LV function and MR strongly imply a nonischemic etiology of MR; therefore, isolated ALCAPA repair would be less likely to result in notable improvement in MR. We suspect that for most of these patients (assuming no chordal rupture), there are varying locations of completed infarction of the sub-valve apparatus or perhaps some with varying locations of hibernating papillary muscle without infarction. If the latter is the explanation, then isolated ALCAPA repair would theoretically result in improved MV function. Perhaps this accounts for our finding that a small percentage of patients with preserved LV function demonstrated improvement in MR with isolated ALCAPA repair. Unfortunately, we were unable to extract data about the morphology of the MV such as leaflet prolapse that would corroborate our findings and allow further speculation.

Our study demonstrates the need to consider that the subset of patients with moderate or greater MR may have lower odds of spontaneous recovery of MV function, emphasizing the need for exploration of which patients within this subset may benefit from concomitant MV repair at the time of ALCAPA repair. Further research incorporating granular LV and MR characteristics, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging–based assessments of ischemia and papillary muscle injury, may help distinguish patients likely to benefit from revascularization alone from those who would derive a meaningful benefit from concomitant MV repair at the time of revascularization.

Limitations of the study include its retrospective design and the limited data available at follow-up (Table 2). We speculate that the missing data are random and reflective of the nature of tertiary referrals for a complex and rare congenital heart lesion and not likely related to the presence or absence of MR. Data regarding MV were obtained from institutional echocardiogram reports, and therefore, data regarding the dimensions and abnormalities of MV apparatus differed between institutions and were not uniformly available for analysis. Similarly, the data on LV dimension, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter were missing during follow-up to truly assess for longitudinal analysis. Data on annular dilation, direction of regurgitation (central or eccentric), and LV volumes would be beneficial to obtain in future studies to add to our understanding of the MV characteristics, function, and impact on LV.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that spontaneous improvement in MR after ALCAPA repair appears to vary on the basis of LV function at presentation. For patients with ALCAPA and MV dysfunction in the setting of preserved left ventricular function, MV recovery is less common. In this subset of patients, clinicians should closely assess the MV anatomy and morphology preoperatively and consider if concomitant MV repair is indicated.
