Dual-Level Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction in an Adult
Authors: Elliot Roufeh, Ziad Hindosh, Joshua Lee, Jesse Wolfsohn, Anthony S. Koppula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiscrete subaortic membranes are an uncommon cause of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction in adults and may coexist with aortic valve disease.Case SummaryA 50-year-old man presented with acute decompensated heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography showed reduced ejection fraction (30%-35%) and demonstrated severe calcific aortic stenosis with regurgitation. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a discrete subaortic membrane causing additional subvalvular obstruction, consistent with dual-level LVOT obstruction. Invasive hemodynamics confirmed advanced afterload-mediated heart failure. The patient underwent surgical membrane resection and mechanical valve replacement with subsequent clinical and hemodynamic improvement.DiscussionCoexisting subvalvular and valvular obstruction is uncommon in adults and may present diagnostic challenges. Multimodality imaging is essential to differentiate obstruction levels and guide surgical management.Take-Home MessageComprehensive multimodality imaging is essential to identify dual-level LVOT obstruction and ensure correction of both valvular and subvalvular disease.

History of Presentation

A 50-year-old man with a history of repaired congenital heart disease presented with progressive dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, nonproductive cough, and worsening bilateral lower-extremity edema. On arrival, he was hypertensive and mildly hypoxemic (peripheral oxygen saturation 93% on room air). Examination revealed diminished breath sounds at the bases, a harsh systolic murmur best heard at the right upper sternal border without significant change with provocative maneuvers.Take-Home Messages•Turbulent LVOT flow in the setting of aortic stenosis should prompt careful evaluation for subvalvular obstruction.•Multimodality imaging, particularly transesophageal echocardiography with advanced imaging planes, is essential for accurate diagnosis and operative planning in dual-level LVOT obstruction.

Past Medical History

The patient underwent congenital heart surgery at age 4 in Guatemala; specific details of the lesion and repair were unavailable. He had no other known chronic medical conditions.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for acute decompensated heart failure with reduced ejection fraction included severe valvular aortic stenosis, recurrent or residual congenital left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, and ischemic cardiomyopathy.

Investigations

Laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated B-type natriuretic peptide level of 805 pg/mL with a mild troponin I level elevation of 0.09 ng/mL. Chest radiography showed pulmonary vascular congestion with bilateral pleural effusions. Computed tomography angiography of the chest ruled out pulmonary embolism but revealed bilateral pleural effusions without evidence of prior median sternotomy.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 30% to 35% with moderate diffuse hypokinesis. The aortic valve was trileaflet with marked leaflet thickening and severe calcification. Continuous-wave Doppler interrogation revealed a peak systolic velocity of 4.0 m/s and a mean transvalvular gradient of 39 mm Hg. Severity of valvular aortic stenosis was adjudicated using planimetry, yielding an aortic valve area of 0.8 cm2 (indexed 0.31 cm2/m2), a dimensionless velocity index of 0.22, and a stroke volume index of 39 mL/m2, consistent with severe low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis. Planimetric assessment was prioritized, given the potential for Doppler overestimation in the presence of subvalvular obstruction, at least moderate aortic regurgitation, and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. On initial transthoracic imaging, discrete separation of the subvalvular and valvular gradient contributions was not performed; however, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography subsequently confirmed distinct gradients above and below the membrane using pulsed-wave Doppler interrogation, localizing the relative contributions of each level of obstruction (Figure 1). Given the level of obstruction was not clearly defined on transthoracic imaging, transesophageal echocardiography was performed. Two-dimensional midesophageal long-axis imaging revealed a discrete echogenic membrane within the LVOT, immediately proximal to the calcified aortic valve (Video 1). Color Doppler interrogation demonstrated high-velocity turbulent flow originating at the level of the subaortic membrane, consistent with fixed subvalvular obstruction (Video 2). These findings confirmed dual-level obstruction involving both the subvalvular and valvular regions.

X-plane imaging further delineated the spatial orientation and circumferential extent of the membrane within the LVOT (Video 3). The midesophageal short-axis view demonstrated a severely calcified trileaflet aortic valve with restricted leaflet excursion (Video 4).

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging further quantified severe aortic regurgitation with a regurgitant fraction of 67% and visualized a subaortic projection consistent with a membrane (Figure 3, Video 5). The indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume was 117.7 mL/m2, end-systolic volume 99.7 mL/m2, stroke volume 18 mL/m2, and left ventricular mass 112.5 g. Late gadolinium enhancement demonstrated near-circumferential diffuse subendocardial enhancement, consistent with chronic pressure overload cardiomyopathy. Coronary angiography showed no obstructive coronary artery disease. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 70 mm Hg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 31 mm Hg, a transpulmonary gradient of 39 mm Hg, a pulmonary vascular resistance of 8.4 WU, and a cardiac index of 2.1 to 2.2 L/min/m2, consistent with combined pre- and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension. Although pulmonary vascular resistance was elevated, suggesting a reactive pulmonary vascular component, the degree of pulmonary hypertension was attributed predominantly to longstanding left-sided afterload excess from dual-level obstruction. This interpretation was supported by the substantial postoperative reduction in pulmonary artery systolic pressure (from 62 to 40 mm Hg), consistent with reversibility of the pulmonary vascular changes after relief of outflow tract obstruction. Management

The patient was admitted for acute decompensated heart failure and underwent aggressive intravenous diuresis with hemodynamic monitoring for volume optimization. Given symptomatic severe subaortic stenosis (SAS) with concomitant subvalvular obstruction and reduced left ventricular systolic function, cardiothoracic surgery was consulted. After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent surgical resection of the discrete subaortic membrane and mechanical aortic valve replacement (21-mm St Jude Medical prothesis). Intraoperative findings confirmed a fibromuscular subaortic membrane and a severely calcified trileaflet aortic valve. Pathology demonstrated marked fibrosis and calcification of the excised aortic valve, consistent with advanced degenerative aortic stenosis (Figure 4). Outcome and Follow-Up

The early postoperative course was initially favorable; the patient was extubated on postoperative day 2 with weaning of vasopressors. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed no residual subaortic membrane and a normally functioning mechanical prosthesis with a peak velocity of 2.8 m/s and a mean gradient of 18 mm Hg. However, on postoperative day 3, the patient developed acute respiratory failure and hemodynamic collapse, requiring reintubation. He subsequently developed mixed cardiogenic and distributive shock with biventricular failure (new severely reduced right ventricular function on echocardiography), acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, and refractory hypoxemia. On postoperative day 4, he was cannulated for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and transferred to a higher level of care. Cardiogenic shock was attributed to myocardial stunning in the setting of severely reduced preoperative ventricular function (cross-clamp time 131 minutes; cardiopulmonary bypass time 158 minutes), compounded by a superimposed septic process. The patient was supported on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for 11 days and was successfully decannulated on postoperative day 15. He subsequently stabilized with recovery of end-organ function, was weaned from mechanical ventilation via tracheostomy, and was ultimately discharged for continued rehabilitation.

At 6-month follow-up, the patient reported marked improvement in exertional tolerance without recurrent dyspnea. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a well-seated mechanical prosthesis with a mean gradient of 14 mm Hg and no paravalvular leak. There was no residual subaortic obstruction. Left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 45% to 50%, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure decreased to 40 mm Hg.

He was maintained on a regimen of guideline-directed anticoagulation for his mechanical prosthesis and enrolled in routine surveillance imaging, consistent with contemporary valvular heart disease recommendations.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a rare presentation of dual-level LVOT obstruction due to a discrete SAS coexisting with severe calcific trileaflet aortic stenosis and regurgitation. Discrete SAS is an uncommon but recognized cause of fixed LVOT obstruction, accounting for approximately 6.5% of adult congenital heart disease cases and up to 8% to 20% of LVOT obstruction cases.1,2 Although subaortic membranes are typically diagnosed in childhood, delayed recognition into adulthood has been associated with progressive aortic valve disease, though a direct causal mechanism remains unproven.3,4 Contemporary adult cohort data demonstrate that SAS is frequently associated with aortic valve disease and carries meaningful long-term morbidity.5

Persistent subvalvular obstruction generates turbulent LVOT flow and abnormal shear stress across the aortic valve, which has been hypothesized to contribute to endothelial injury, fibrosis, and calcification over time. Prior literature has described an association between SAS and progressive aortic regurgitation, likely related to abnormal flow dynamics and leaflet stress.2,6 However, the long-term impact of subvalvular obstruction on the development of calcific trileaflet aortic stenosis remains incompletely defined, and a direct causal relationship has not been established. Calcific degeneration of a trileaflet valve remains the most common etiology of aortic stenosis in adults and occurs independently of subvalvular pathology.7 In this patient, severe valve calcification at age 50 raises the possibility of hemodynamic interactions between the 2 lesions; however, the subaortic membrane and degenerative valvular disease may equally represent independent coexisting processes. The temporal relationship and pathophysiologic interaction between these lesions cannot be determined from a single case.

Dual-level obstruction presents important diagnostic challenges. Fixed subvalvular obstruction may augment Doppler velocities and complicate accurate localization of stenosis severity. Echocardiographic guidelines emphasize careful anatomic visualization and Doppler interrogation to differentiate subvalvular from valvular obstruction.8 Invasive left ventricular-to-aortic pullback assessment was not performed in this case, which represents a limitation in quantifying the relative hemodynamic contribution of each level of obstruction. However, intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography provided direct visualization of separate gradients above and below the membrane, confirming dual-level obstruction. On initial transthoracic imaging, the combined gradient was recognized but discrete separation of subvalvular and valvular contributions was not achievable; planimetric assessment of the aortic valve independently confirmed severe valvular stenosis.

Current guidelines recommend surgical intervention for symptomatic SAS with significant gradients or left ventricular dysfunction attributable to obstruction.6 Comprehensive correction of both obstruction levels is critical, as failure to recognize subvalvular pathology may result in persistent postoperative gradients. The patient's substantial postoperative ventricular recovery supports a predominantly afterload-mediated cardiomyopathy and underscores the importance of recognizing dual-level LVOT obstruction in adults presenting with severe aortic stenosis.

Conclusions

This case illustrates dual-level LVOT obstruction due to a coexisting discrete subaortic membrane and severe calcific trileaflet aortic stenosis presenting as acute decompensated heart failure in adulthood. Comprehensive multimodality imaging was essential to accurately localize the level of obstruction and guide definitive surgical management. Although altered flow dynamics from subvalvular obstruction may influence aortic valve stress, a direct causal relationship cannot be established in this case. Recognition of potential dual-level obstruction is critical to ensure complete surgical correction and optimize clinical outcomes.
