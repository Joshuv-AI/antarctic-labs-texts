Enzyme-Free In-Situ Electrochemical Measurement Using a Porous Membrane Electrode for Glucose Transport into Cell Spheroids
Authors: Yoshinobu Utagawa, Kosuke Ino, Yasuhiko Shinoda, Masateru Yamazaki, Hiroya Abe, Hitoshi Shiku
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Microphysiological systems have attracted attention because of their use in drug screening. However, it is challenging to measure cell functions in real time using a device. In this study, we developed a cell culture device using a porous membrane electrode for in situ electrochemical glucose measurements for cell analysis. First, a porous membrane electrode was fabricated and electrochemically evaluated for enzyme-free glucose measurement. Subsequently, the glucose uptake of MCF-7 spheroids was evaluated using living spheroids, fixed spheroids, supernatants, and glucose transporter inhibitor-treated spheroids. Conventionally, the direct optical measurement of glucose uptake requires fluorescence-labeled glucose derivatives. In addition, the glucose uptake can be evaluated by measuring the glucose concentration in the medium by optical or electrochemical measurements. However, glucose needs to be consumed in the entire cell culture medium, which needs a long culture time. In contrast, our system can measure glucose in approximately 5 min without any labels because of in situ electrochemical measurements. This system can be used for in situ measurements in in vitro cell culture systems, including organ-on-a-chip for drug screening.

Microphysiological systems (MPS) have attracted attention for drug screening because reliable preclinical testing can reduce costs and resolve ethical issues related to animal testing in drug discovery.1,2 Spheroids have been widely studied as promising three-dimensional (3D) tissue models3 that can mimic a biological microenvironment of the gradient of oxygen and nutrients in native tissues better than conventional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture models.4 Recently, spheroids have been cultured on porous membranes, which allowed for the coculture of spheroids and other 2D cultured cells.5 In addition, these techniques have the potential to evaluate the influence of biomolecules and cell migration through porous membranes.5 Moreover, spheroids have been cultured on porous membranes to achieve integrated MPSs6 and separation of the cell culture chambers. Thus, porous membranes are useful for the analysis of spheroids in MPSs, and porous membranes are widely used for the vasculature,7 blood-brain barrier,8 lung,9 and gut10 models where cells are cultured on a porous membrane in a device. Traditionally, cells cultured in MPS have been evaluated using gene analysis techniques such as qPCR and histological examinations. However, these methods often damage the cells, making them unsuitable for real-time and time-course analyses.

Electrochemical (bio)sensors have been considered to address this issue. Electrochemical techniques have been used to evaluate spheroids because of their excellent properties, including high sensitivity and real-time and in situ measurements. Spheroid functions such as oxygen consumption,11,12 enzyme expression,13 and secretions14,15 have been evaluated electrochemically. Cellular activity, proliferation, and differentiation are metrics for evaluating cellular metabolism. In our group, oxygen consumption has been electrochemically measured using scanning electrochemical microscopy11 and electrochemiluminescence imaging.12,16 Glucose consumption is also an indicator of cellular activity as well as oxygen consumption. Cancer cells primarily generate energy through glycolysis, even under aerobic conditions, thus consuming more glucose than normal cells. Cellular glucose uptake and consumption are important for understanding cellular activity and providing insights into drug discovery. Glucose uptake and consumption have been determined using analytical methods such as electrochemistry,17 fluorescence,18 and nuclear magnetic resonance.19 In the optical method, fluorescence-labeled glucose derivatives are used, and the changes in the intensity of the fluorescence were observed for monitoring glucose uptake in the cells. In electrochemical methods, enzyme-modified electrodes are used for the electrochemical measurement of glucose, which does not require labeling. For example, continuous glucose metabolism monitoring during cancer cell proliferation20 and in situ glucose metabolism monitoring have been reported.21 However, few systems have been reported to assess the glucose uptake of spheroids.17 Moreover, glucose uptake measurements for spheroids within the MPS have not been achieved.

The enzymes allow for selective measurements. However, enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, which limits the environment where the sensor can be used. In addition, the formation of enzyme-immobilized membranes on an electrode with a characteristic structure may cause the electrode to lose its features. Enzyme-free glucose sensors have been used to address this issue.22 Glucose is oxidized directly on metallic electrodes, including Au, under alkaline conditions without enzymes.23 However, it is challenging to measure biosamples in real time because pH adjustment is required. Applying negative potential can produce hydroxide ions through hydrolysis, which forms the alkaline condition on the electrode surface only. A wearable glucose sensor has been reported to provide temporary alkaline conditions on the electrode surface, allowing for continuous monitoring even on the skin.24,25

In this study, we developed an enzyme-free in situ electrochemical glucose sensor using a porous membrane electrode to evaluate cell spheroids. First, we demonstrated a proof-of-concept for enzyme-free glucose sensing using a porous membrane electrode. Subsequently, we measured the glucose uptake by spheroids. Conventional glucose consumption measurements required a long culture time due to the time taken to change the glucose concentration in the medium. For example, glucose consumption by 3T3 cells was measured electrochemically by collecting the medium.26 In contrast, our system has several advantages such as in situ measurements, which can be performed in minutes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on enzyme-free in situ glucose uptake measurement of spheroids in MPS. This system measures glucose levels without labels or electrode modifications. This system can be used for real-time sensing in in vitro cell culture systems such as organ-on-a-chip.

Experimental Section

Fabrication and Characterization of a Device

The porous Au membrane electrode was fabricated using a porous polyurethane membrane (Toyoda Gosei Co., Ltd., Japan). The porous membranes were sputtered with Ti, Pt, or Au at 200 W for 1, 0.5, or 2 min, respectively. The cell culture chambers (Φ 4 and 8 mm) were fabricated using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SILPOT W/C, DuPont Toray Specialty Materials K.K., Japan). The chambers and porous membrane electrodes were bonded overnight using precured PDMS at room temperature. The porous membranes and porous membrane electrodes were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (S-4800, Hitachi High-Tech Co., Japan).

The electrochemical behavior of the device was evaluated using ferrocenemethanol (FMA, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Japan). A Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl) as a reference electrode and a Pt wire as a counter electrode were used. These electrodes were connected to a potentiostat (HA1010 mM4, Hokuto Denko, Japan), and cyclic voltammetry was performed.

Electrochemical Measurements of Glucose

Glucose (d-Glucose, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Japan) was dissolved in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, pH 7.4; Nacalai Tesque, Japan). Sodium l-lactate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in PBS. A Au disk electrode (Φ 1.6 mm) was used as a preliminary experiment.

The device with a porous membrane electrode was treated with air plasma at a flow rate of 40 mL/min for 30 s. Before glucose measurement, the devices were measured with −1.6 V for 1 s and +0.3 V for 5 s as a preparation.

Cell Culture and Spheroid Formation

MCF-7 cells (Institute of Development, Aging and Cancer, Tohoku University, Japan) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Gibco). These cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cell suspension (50,000 cells/mL) was prepared, and 200 μL of the cell suspension was added to a U-shaped 96-well plate (Sumitomo Bakelite, Japan). Spheroids were used after a 3-day culture period.

Glucose Uptake Measurements of Spheroids

Spheroids were placed on the device for 5 min, and the glucose levels were measured. The supernatant was used as a control. Afterward, five spheroids were placed in 200 μL of 5 mM glucose solution for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and measured. The spheroids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Alfa Aesar, USA) in PBS for 120 min. The fixed spheroids were immersed in PBS for more than 120 min to remove PFA. The spheroids were also treated with 30 μM WZB117 (Sigma-Aldrich) in RPMI medium for 90 min.

Normalized current values were calculated by dividing the current value measured for the samples (spheroids in the 5 mM glucose solutions or supernatants) by the current value measured for the 5 mM glucose solutions. These results were evaluated statically using Dunnett’s test.

Glucose Measurements for Supernatants Based on Absorbance Measurement

Five spheroids were placed in 200 μL of 5 mM glucose solution for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatants were analyzed using a Glucose Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo, Japan). For time-dependent sampling, 10 μL of each supernatant was collected at 5, 10, 30, and 60 min.

Calculation of Glucose Uptake

Before the measurements of samples, the oxidation currents of 0 and 5 mM glucose solutions were measured to create the calibration curves for each device. The normalized currents were calculated by dividing the current values of samples by the current values of 5 mM glucose solutions. The concentrations were calculated using the calibration curves for each device.

For the calculation of the uptake rate, the uptake concentration was calculated by subtracting the immobilized cell measurement results from the live cell measurement results to account for diffusion inhibition by spheroids.

Simulation for Glucose Uptake

The simulation model was constructed using the COMSOL Multiphysics software (ver. 5.4, COMSOL Ink., USA). A time-dependent model was developed, and the model is shown in Figure S6. The diameter of the spheroid was 600 μm, and the diameter of the electrode was 4 mm. The physics was the transport of diluted species of the chemical species transport. The mesh size was finer. In this study, the glucose uptake rates (mol/(m2 s)) were represented as a product of the reaction rate constant (k m/s) and glucose concentration (c mol/m3). Then, the glucose uptake rate per spheroid (F mol/s) was determined by calculating the average glucose concentration on the electrode after 5 min. The model was filled with 5 mol/m3 glucose. The diffusion coefficient was set to 6.7 × 10–10 m2/s.

Live/Dead Staining

The spheroids were treated with a Double-Staining Kit (Dojindo) for 30 min at room temperature. Calcein AM and PI in the spheroids were observed under a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2, Nikon, Japan). The exposure times were 10 and 30 ms for living and dead cells, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Device Fabrication and Glucose Measurement Scheme

Figure 1A shows a scheme of the device fabrication. A porous membrane electrode was fabricated by sputtering a porous polyurethane membrane. In the previous study, track-etched membranes with 16-μm thickness were used. In contrast, the present study used asymmetric porous membranes with 5–10-μm thickness for the use of thinner membranes to shorten the distance between the spheroids and electrode when the spheroids were set opposite to the sensor. Previously, asymmetric porous membranes were used for cell culture.27 Afterward, a polydimethylsiloxane cell culture chamber was glued onto the porous membrane electrode. Figure 1B shows electrochemical reactions for glucose measurement in this study. First, a negative potential was applied to create alkaline conditions on the electrode surface. Subsequently, +0.3 V was applied to oxidize glucose. This strategy enables the measurement of glucose without the use of enzymes in a neutral pH solution. In this study, cancer spheroids were placed on a porous membrane electrode for in situ glucose uptake measurements (Figure 1C).

Characterization of a Porous Membrane Electrode

Figure 2A shows the cross-section of the porous membrane before and after Au sputtering. The pore sizes differed between the front and back (Figure 2B), which is a unique feature of this membrane. The thickness of the porous membrane measured from the SEM image was 6.4 μm. Track-etched polyester membranes are typically used as porous membranes with straight pores. Many pores were present on the membrane and some pores randomly penetrated the membrane (Figure 2C). These unique pore structures will contribute to cell adhesion on the porous membranes of MPS devices in the future. Figure 2D shows the back of the porous membrane electrode. The membrane was sputtered and exhibited no cracks. In addition, the pores were maintained even after sputtering. The metals did not spatter onto the backside because the porous membrane adhered to the substrate during sputtering. Figure 3A shows a device fabricated with a porous membrane electrode. The penetration was confirmed by the addition of red ink (Figure S1A). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed with 1 mM FMA at various scan rates. The peak current increased with increasing scan rate, and the square root of the scan rate corresponded to the peak current (Figure S1B,C). Figure S1D shows an SEM image of the front side of the porous membrane electrode. This supports that the devices could be fabricated without cracks on the porous membrane electrodes. Therefore, porous membrane electrodes can be used for electrochemical measurements.

Enzyme-Free Electrochemical Measurements of Glucose

Before using the porous membrane electrode, a disk electrode (Φ 1.6 mm) was used for nonenzymatic electrochemical glucose measurements. First, amperometry at −2.0 V for 20 s was performed as a pretreatment followed by CV. When 5 mM glucose was used, glucose oxidation peaks were observed at +0.3 V (Figure S2A), and +0.3 V was selected as the measurement potential to provide sufficient energy for glucose oxidation. The pretreatment potential was then optimized (Figure S2B). The glucose concentration was measured sensitively using a pretreatment potential more negative than −1.4 V. In contrast, a larger negative potential causes numerous bubbles and damages the electrode.

Cyclic voltammetry was performed using a porous membrane electrode. A −1.6 V was used as a pretreatment potential to reduce bubble generation. However, this was sufficient to measure glucose levels (Figure S2B). Figure 3B shows cyclic voltammograms obtained using porous membrane electrodes. In Situ Electrochemical Measurements of Glucose Uptake by Cancer Spheroids

MCF-7 spheroids were cultured for 3 days and used for glucose uptake measurements. First, five spheroids were placed on the device for 5 min and glucose measurements were performed. As shown in Figure 4A, glucose levels decreased near the electrode owing to glucose uptake by spheroids. When living spheroids were measured, the normalized current was approximately 0.58 (Figure 4B). The glucose oxidation current decreased as the number of measurements increased, with a maximum rate of change of 23% (Figure S3); these results indicated glucose uptake by the spheroids. The fixed spheroids and supernatants were used as controls. Supernatant measurements were performed for comparison with in situ measurements. When measuring fixed spheroids and supernatants, the normalized currents were approximately 0.82 and 0.90, respectively (Figure 4B). Thus, living spheroids decreased the glucose oxidation current compared to fixed spheroids and supernatants. The decrease in normalized current at the controls could be due to various reasons. When measuring fixed spheroids, glucose diffused into the spheroids with low glucose concentrations because the spheroids were treated with a glucose-free buffer. In addition, glucose diffusion was prevented by the presence of the spheroids. Glucose concentrations in the supernatants decreased because of uptake by the spheroids. Based on the absorbance measurements, the glucose concentrations changed only 2.8% for 5 min (Figure S4). Therefore, this system can measure glucose uptake sensitively compared to analyzing supernatant because of in situ measurement.

Subsequently, spheroids treated with glucose transporter inhibitors were used to investigate the applicability of drug screening. WZB117 is an inhibitor of glucose transporter 1 and affects glucose transporters within minutes.29 In this study, spheroids were treated with 30 μM WZB117 for 90 min. As a result of glucose measurement, the normalized current was approximately 0.70 (Figure 4B). There was no statistically significant difference. This was because the 90 min treatment might not have been sufficient to inhibit glucose transporter 1 owing to their 3D structures. However, there was an average difference of 12% in the normalized current value, suggesting that this system would measure the differences in glucose uptake of spheroids after drug treatment. Finally, the glucose concentration was calculated (Figure S5). Some samples showed negative concentrations because the current values were larger for 0 mM solutions than for 5 mM solutions with spheroids. This result may be due to the influence of alkaline conditions from previous measurements. Therefore, these plots (two plots for living spheroids and one plot for WZB117-treated spheroids) were removed from Figure S5. The results showed that the glucose concentration in living spheroids was 2.1 mM. In contrast, the concentrations in fixed spheroids, supernatants, and WZB117-treated spheroids were 3.8, 4.3, and 2.9 mM, respectively. The glucose uptake rate per single spheroid calculated from these results using COMSOL (Figure S7A–C) was 8.2 pmol/s, which is higher than that estimated in a previous report.19 This system has the potential to measure conditions in the early stages of a fast uptake rate (Figure S7D).

Glucose measurements were performed after the removal of spheroids. The current was recovered from the spheroid measurements. In contrast, the current decreased compared to that before spheroid measurement (Figure S6), which might have been caused by electrode contamination and/or device degradation. The secrets released from spheroids adhered to the electrodes,30 which prevented reactions on the electrodes. The glucose uptake of spheroids was measured at the back of the porous membrane electrode to remove the effect of electrode contamination. In addition, this porous membrane electrode has advantages for measurement on the back side because the electrodes are placed near the spheroids compared to those of previous track-etched membranes. As a result of measuring the back, the normalized current was larger than that of the front (Figure 4C), indicating low sensitivity. However, this result supported the idea that in situ measurement of the electrode is important for sensitive cell analysis. After removing the spheroid, the current recovered to that before the spheroid measurement, indicating that the effect of electrode contamination was small. Thus, the sensitivity of measurement on the back side can be improved by optimizing the pore size and density. Finally, the spheroids were stained to investigate cell damage during glucose measurements. Most of the cells were alive after measurements on the front and back sides of the porous membrane electrodes (Figure S8). However, the effects of applying a −1.6 V on the physiological state and glucose uptake of spheroids are unknown. The effects of applying potentials when performing multiple measurements need to be considered in future research.

Conventional glucose consumption measurements based on the decrease in glucose in the medium require long cell cultures. The consumption rate was calculated as the average value over the entire culture period. However, the consumption rate changes in the culture because of changes in the glucose concentration in the medium. This system may be able to measure glucose uptake by spheroids and calculate the uptake rate in the initial phase. The total supernatant volumes were collected in these experiments, which is not realistic for monitoring change over time. However, it is difficult to determine glucose uptake using part of the supernatants up to 1 h of incubation (Figure S9). In contrast, in situ measurements enable us to measure glucose uptake for 5 min sensitively compared to that of supernatant measurements, which is a great advantage. MPSs using porous membranes have been developed for cell culture platforms to simulate the organ microenvironment, including microfluidic devices.31,32 Microfluidic devices with porous membranes have also been used for spheroid cultures.33 In addition to spheroids, organoids have been cultured on a chip with porous membranes.34 The porous membranes allowed perfusion culture in these experiments. The vertical-flow perfusion through the pores improved cell functions because the flow provided oxygen and nutrients to the spheroids.35 The porous membrane electrodes can evaluate cell functions while maintaining the cell culture environment. In addition, porous membrane electrodes have the potential for evaluating not only spheroids but also organoids in MPSs through glucose uptake measurements for drug screening.

Conclusions

In this study, we present an enzyme-free in situ glucose measurement method that uses a porous membrane electrode for cell analysis. The Au-sputtered porous membrane was used as the electrode and quantitatively detected electrochemical analytes of FMA. In addition, enzyme-free glucose measurement was demonstrated after pretreatment of the electrode through electrolysis on the porous membrane electrode. Glucose uptake by spheroids is shown as a proof-of-concept. This system can rapidly measure glucose uptake through in situ measurements. This system can be used for the real-time sensing of in vitro cell culture systems, including organ-on-a-chip systems.
