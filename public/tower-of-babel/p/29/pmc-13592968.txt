Identification of therapeutic targets for rheumatic heart valve disease based on systematic druggable genome wide Mendelian randomization
Authors: Wei-Hua Shi, Jing-Chang Zhang, Zhi-Tao Xie, Yi-Sheng Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rheumatic heart valve disease (RHD) is a chronic immune valvular heart disease caused by rheumatic fever, primarily affecting the mitral and aortic valves. It often leads to atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and even premature death. Currently, there are no effective therapeutic drugs available, partially due to the lack of appropriate therapeutic targets. To identify therapeutic targets for RHD, we employed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization approach integrating identified druggable genomics to assess the causal effect of expression quantitative trait loci of druggable genes in the blood on RHD. Subsequently, we performed Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses on the druggable genes. We used colocalization analysis to test whether the risk of RHD and gene expression are driven by common single nucleotide polymorphisms. Additionally, the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database was utilized to evaluate the impact of environmental exposures on druggable genes, and molecular docking was conducted to identify potential small molecule interactions. A total of 6888 druggable genes were collected. After conducting various Mendelian randomization analyses and applying false discovery rate correction, we identified 13 drug targets for RHD: TFRC, FMO4, CA2, HLA-DPB2, OXTR, GRAMD1B, PNP, HLA-DPB1, leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor B1 (LILRB1), TUBB, LGR6, F13A1, and LPL. These targets were found to be closely related to immune regulation and inflammatory response in Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses. Bayesian colocalization analysis established an interaction between LILRB1 and RHD, with a PP.H4 >0.5, LILRB1 demonstrated a protective effect in RHD (OR = 0.833, 95% confidence interval, 0.699–0.993). Comparative Toxicogenomics Database analysis identified several small molecules influencing LILRB1 mRNA expression, with lipopolysaccharide showing excellent binding affinity in molecular docking with LILRB1 against available structural data for drugs and proteins. Based on a cohort of European ancestry, this study reveals 13 potential therapeutic targets for RHD, with LILRB1 showing particularly promising prospects as a future therapeutic target for RHD.

1. Introduction

Rheumatic heart valve disease (RHD) is a chronic heart disease caused by rheumatic fever, mainly manifested as inflammation and damage to the heart valves. This condition usually stems from streptococcal infection, which leads to an abnormal immune response and then affects structures such as the mitral and aortic valves of the heart.[1] RHD often results in the occurrence of heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke and systemic embolism, and even lead to premature death.[2] As of 2020, RHD accounted for 1.6% of all deaths from cardiovascular diseases globally, causing approximately 306,000 deaths each year,[3] and this number is still on the rise, especially being more common in developing countries. Moreover, due to many factors such as the lack of antibiotics, overcrowding of the population and poor hygiene conditions related to poverty, the incidence of RHD in some poor countries is still gradually increasing, which has a significant impact on the quality of life of patients and increases the burden on the medical system.[2]

Heart surgery remains the most common treatment method for RHD, including mitral valve plasty, mitral valve repair, and valve replacement surgery, etc. However, due to the scarcity or nonexistence of human resources for surgery and interventional procedures as well as the burden of high costs, patients who want to access to heart surgery is still severely limited. Currently, there is no specific drug treatment for RHD. The treatment mainly focuses on relieving symptoms and improving cardiac function. Patients usually need to receive long-term drug treatment, including antibiotics, diuretics, and drugs for heart failure.[4] In recent years, with the continuous deepening of understanding of rheumatic heart disease, new treatment strategies have been gradually proposed. For example, regulating the inflammatory pathways or the fibrotic process is expected to improve the function and structure of the heart valves.[5] Nevertheless, there are still some patients who do not respond well to the existing treatments, which poses a challenge for clinical treatment.

In the research of RHD, the application of genetics has gradually gained attention. Through genome-wide association studies (GWAS), researchers can identify genetic variations associated with rheumatic heart disease, providing a foundation for the development of new therapeutic targets.[6] Mendelian randomization (MR) can be used to assess causal relationships between exposures or risk factors and disease outcomes,[7] and it is now widely applied in repurpose licensed drugs and discover novel therapeutic targets by integrating summary data from disease GWAS and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) studies, potentially offering new insights for the treatment of RHD.[8]

This study aims to explore new therapeutic targets and strategies for RHD. Firstly, by utilizing the MR method and large-scale GWAS data, we identified new targets related to the risk of RHD, thus providing a new direction for drug development. Then, gene enrichment analysis was performed to reveal the biological relevance of the potential targets, and to deeply understand their roles in the development and treatment mechanisms of RHD. In addition, we also conducted gene colocalization analysis to confirm the causal relationship between the therapeutic targets and the risk of RHD. Finally, through drug prediction and molecular docking studies, we verified the pharmacological activities of multiple potential targets and evaluated their correlations with cardiac function and feasibility.

In conclusion, our study provides a new perspective and targets for the treatment of RHD. By integrating MR, gene enrichment analysis, gene colocalization analysis, drug prediction, molecular docking, hoping to provide an important guidance for the development of more effective and targeted treatment strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Acquisition of druggable genes

Druggable genes were sourced from the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb V.4.2.0, https://www.dgidb.org/) as well as from recent reviews addressing the “druggability” of various genes. The DGIdb: this database integrates a large amount of drug-gene interaction information from published literatures, databases, and online resources, and provides an annotated list of druggable genes, we obtained the latest drug-gene interaction by DGIdb;[9] The study by Finan et al: this study systematically compiles genes with “druggable potential,” from which we extracted supplementary druggable genes.[10] Finally, by integrating the above 2 sources and removing duplicate genes, unique druggable genes were obtained.

2.2. Acquisition of exposure data

The eQTL data were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/). The eQTLGen dataset encompasses a total of 16,987 genes and 31,684 cis-eQTLs derived from blood samples predominantly collected from healthy European individuals. A comprehensive description of the dataset is available in the original publication.[11] Given that eQTLs are in closer proximity to target genes in drug development research and exert a more direct regulatory effect on gene expression, we selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within ± 1000 kb of the transcription start sites of druggable genes for this study.

2.3. RHD FinnGen dataset

The data of RHD were obtained from the FinnGen R11 study (https://www.finngen.fi/en/), which includes 800 cases and 452,616 controls.

2.4. eQTL MR analysis

MR analysis was performed utilizing the R package TwoSampleMR version 0.5.6 (developed by MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit, University of Bristol, Bristol, United Kingdom). eQTLs derived from the drug genome were selected as exposure data. To generate instrumental variables (IVs) that meet the assumptions of MR and ensure their reliability, we selected SNPs with a false discovery rate of <0.05, located within ±1000 kb of the transcription start sites of each gene. A series of stringent criteria were implemented for the eQTLs of druggable genes: SNPs were selected based on genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and acceptable mutation probabilities (minor allele frequency > 1%); clumping (r² < 0.001, kb = 10,000) was performed to eliminate linkage disequilibrium among genetic instruments; F-statistics were employed to estimate the strength of each genetic instrument, selecting all strong IVs (F > 10).[12] RHD was subsequently utilized as the outcome data for the 2-sample MR analysis. The Wald ratio method was applied to compute the MR estimates for each SNP. When 2 or more genetic instrument SNPs were available, inverse variance weighting (IVW) served as the primary analytical method. This approach integrates the causal effects of individual SNPs, accommodates heterogeneity among SNPs, and yields an unbiased estimate of causality when all IVs are valid and pleiotropy levels are balanced. An MR-Egger intercept test was performed to evaluate the presence of horizontal pleiotropy (with a significance threshold of P < .05), and Cochran Q was computed using both IVW and MR-Egger techniques to assess heterogeneity.

2.5. Enrichment analysis

To explore the functional characteristics and biological relevance of the identified prospective therapeutic target genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the R package clusterProfiler. GO comprises 3 categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. KEGG pathways offer insights into metabolic pathways.

2.6. Colocalization analysis

Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed utilizing the “coloc” R package (developed by MRC Biostatistics Unit, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, United Kingdom) to evaluate the probability that 2 traits share the same causal variants. This analysis facilitates the examination of potential genetic confounding by evaluating the posterior probabilities of various causal variants, shared causal variants, and colocalization probabilities.[13] We performed colocalization analysis on genes significantly associated with RHD risk in both cohorts of the study, utilizing the coloc R package. The COLOC package was employed to test 5 distinct hypotheses. Posterior probabilities were employed to quantify support for all hypotheses, defined as PPH0 to PPH4:PPH0 indicates no association with any trait; PPH1 indicates association with gene expression but not with RHD risk; PPH2 indicates association with RHD risk but not with gene expression; PPH3 indicates association with both RHD risk and gene expression with different causal variants; PPH4 indicates association with both RHD risk and gene expression with shared causal variants. Given the limited power of colocalization analysis, we restricted our analysis to genes with PPH4 ≥ 0.5.

2.7. Exploring chemical–protein interactions using Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD)

The CTD (https://ctdbase.org/) is a comprehensive public database aimed at enhancing our understanding of the impact of environmental exposures on human health. We employed this database to gather information on chemical–protein interactions.

2.8. Molecular docking

To gain a deeper understanding of the effects of candidate drugs on drug target genes and the druggability of these targets, molecular docking was conducted at the atomic level to assess the binding energy and interaction patterns between candidate drugs and their targets. Molecular docking simulations enabled us to analyze the binding affinity and interaction modes between ligands and their corresponding drug targets. By identifying ligands exhibiting high binding affinity and favorable interaction patterns, we prioritized drug targets for subsequent experimental validation and optimization of potential drug candidates. Drug structure data were sourced from the PubChem compound database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Protein structure data were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/), and molecular docking of proteins and drugs was conducted using the online molecular docking platform at https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php.[14]

3. Results

3.1. Identification of exposure data

Through the DGIdb, we identified a total of 5012 potential druggable genes. Additionally, we extracted 4463 druggable genes from the study conducted by Finan et al. After integrating both sources and eliminating duplicate entries, we ultimately obtained a total of 6888 unique druggable genes. Furthermore, under a series of stringent criteria: P < 5 × 10−8 for SNPs; r² < 0.001 with a window of 10,000 kb; and F > 10, we conducted an analysis of the eQTLs for the druggable genes, ultimately yielding data for 8600 SNPs.

3.2. MR results

We employed TwoSampleMR to identify eQTLs from the druggable genome as exposure data, utilizing rheumatic valvular disease from the FinnGen R11 study as the outcome data for MR analysis. By applying the Wald ratio or IVW method and adjusting for the false discovery rate (<0.05), we identified 26 candidate druggable genes associated with RHD (see Fig. 1). Among these genes, NAMPT, MAP3K8, SLC23A2, AKT3, ADAM8, TCL1A, S1PR4, TDP2, DHFR, TOP1MT, RGS17, RPS18, and IL1R1 exhibited fewer than 3 SNPs. Given the potential for false positive and false negative results, these genes were subsequently excluded from further analyses. Ultimately, through the MR analysis, we identified 13 potential drug targets for RHD: TFRC, FMO4, CA2, HLA-DPB2, OXTR, GRAMD1B, PNP, HLADPB1, leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor B1 (LILRB1), TUBB, LGR6, F13A1, and LPL. The Cochran Q test and MR-Egger intercept test were used to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity in our study. The results are shown in Table S1 (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q112). Additionally, we presented the results of the MR analysis pertaining to LILRB1 (Fig. 2A and B). The results indicated a negative correlation between LILRB1 and RHD, suggesting a potential protective role for LILRB1 in the context of RHD (odds ratio [OR] = 0.833, 95% confidence interval: 0.699–0.993).

3.3. Enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis is typically used to illustrate the interactions between genes and terms, while KEGG enrichment analysis can elucidate the relationships between genes and functional pathways. The GO enrichment analysis result was showed in Figure 3A. The most significant pathways in the biological process category are related to the regulation and proliferation of immune cells, including regulation of T cell proliferation, T cell proliferation, regulation of lymphocyte proliferation, regulation of mononuclear cell proliferation, positive regulation of T cell activation, regulation of leukocyte proliferation, positive regulation of leukocyte cell–cell adhesion, and lymphocyte proliferation. In the cellular component category, drug target genes are enriched in related terms such as secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, vesicle lumen, clathrin-coated endocytic vesicle membrane, and clathrin-coated endocytic vesicle. Additionally, in terms of molecular function, these genes are associated with functions closely related to immunity, including clathrin-coated pit, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I protein binding, MHC protein complex binding, MHC protein binding, heparin binding, and inhibitory MHC class I receptor activity. KEGG enrichment analysis was showed in Figure 3B, the top 3 pathways identified by KEGG enrichment analysis are phagosome, hematopoietic cell lineage, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism. Among these, phagosome is a type of vesicle within the cell that is typically formed when the cell engulfs external substances such as bacteria or cellular debris. Phagosomes play a crucial role in the immune response and waste clearance of the cell.

3.4. Colocalization analysis

Recent studies have showed that significant MR outcomes may emerge from SNPs situated at gene loci exhibiting tight linkage disequilibrium. Furthermore, the SNPs linked to exposure and outcomes may derive from 2 distinct causal SNPs, potentially resulting in inferred false positive findings.[15] When SNPs are unequivocally associated with both exposure and outcome, colocalization analysis can be utilized to investigate whether the exposure and outcome share a common causal SNP.[13] There is evidence suggests that proteins subjected to both MR and colocalization analyses are more likely to serve as drug targets and are more likely to attain regulatory approval.[16] Consequently, we performed a colocalization analysis to further ascertain the probability of shared causal genetic variation among SNPs associated with RHD and eQTLs, employing SNPs located within a ±100 kb range of 13 potential druggable genes. The results are shown in Figure 4, the eQTL of LILRB1 and the GWAS signal of RHD share causal SNPs (PP.H4 > 0.5), suggesting that the expression regulation of LILRB1 and RHD risk may be driven by the same genetic variant, and it may be a candidate drug target.

Purple diamonds indicate the SNPs. GWAS = genome-wide association study, SNP = single nucleotide polymorphism.

3.5. Molecular docking

Through CTD, we identified the effects of some environmental exposure molecules on LILRB1 expression. We selected molecules that have an impact on cardiovascular diseases, including arsenic, arsenic trioxide, benzo(a)pyrene, catechin, grape seed proanthocyanidins, hydrogen peroxide, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), ozone, valproic acid, vincristine, zinc, levonorgestrel, methotrexate, nickel, and tetrachlorodibenzodioxin. Given that LILRB1 is negatively correlated with RHD and plays a protective role in RHD, we selected several molecules that can upregulate LILRB1 expression (those with both upregulatory and downregulatory effects were not considered) and have been proven to have cardiovascular effects for further molecular docking experiments. These molecules include: LPS, nickel, ozone, valproic acid, and S-(1,2-dichloroethenyl)cysteine. Although S-(1,2-dichlorovinyl) cysteine does not have direct cardiovascular effects, it interacts with LPS to enhance LILRB1 expression, and therefore is included in our molecular scope. These molecules were found to stably bind to LILRB1 (Fig. 5), with LPS showing a stable binding to LILRB1 and a free energy of −7.9 kcal/mol.

4. Discussion

In this study, we conducted MR analysis on the eQTLs of druggable genome related to the outcome data of RHD from FinnGen. Based on various MR analysis methods including Wald ratio/IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median method, horizontal pleiotropy test, and Cochran Q heterogeneity test, we identified 13 drug targets associated with RHD: TFRC, FMO4, CA2, HLA-DPB2, OXTR, GRAMD1B, PNP, HLA-DPB1, LILRB1, TUBB, LGR6, F13A1, and LPL. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, as well as PPI pathway analysis, indicated that these targets are related to immune regulation and inflammatory response. Subsequently, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis, which revealed an interaction between LILRB1 and RHD, suggesting that LILRB1 may be a potential drug target for RHD. Finally, we predicted small molecules corresponding to LILRB1 and conducted molecular docking, further demonstrating the druggable potential of LILRB1 as a target gene.

As we all know, the activation of immune cells and their regulation of immune and inflammatory responses are central to the occurrence and development of RHD, especially the regulation of T cell activation. The inflammatory infiltration described in the rheumatic mitral valves of patients at the end stage of the disease is primarily composed of monocytes, mainly helper T cells (CD4 and cytotoxic T cells) CD8 lymphocytes, macrophages, and B cells.[17,18] The autoimmune response of CD4+ helper T cells is considered to play a core role in coordinating immune responses.[19] Another study also indicated that MHC-II molecules are primarily expressed in professional antigen-presenting cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and B lymphocytes. These cells uptake exogenous antigens, such as protein components produced by bacteria and viruses, and form MHC II–peptide complexes, which are then transported to the cell surface. When CD4+ helper T cells carrying specific T cell receptors recognize this complex, it initiates the adaptive immune response. Once activated, CD4+ T cells differentiate into various functional subsets, such as Th17 cells, which may lead to inflammation and subsequent damage to heart valves.[20–22] Interestingly, our GO enrichment analysis indicates that most druggable genes are related to the regulation, activation, and immune response of immune cells. Additionally, KEGG analysis shows a relationship with phagosomes, which are closely related to adaptive immunity. Some digested antigen peptides can bind to MHC molecules and, after processing by antigen-presenting cells, are delivered to the cell surface to activate CD4+ helper T cells, thereby initiating the adaptive immune response.[23] and the hematopoietic cell lineage is involved in immune cell activation, which is consistent with the results of T cell regulation in GO. All the above evidence suggests that most druggable genes may participate in the occurrence and development of rheumatic heart disease by regulating immune cell responses and inflammatory reactions.

To explore whether the druggable exposures and RHD outcomes share the same causal SNPs, we conducted colocalization analysis to further determine the likelihood of shared causal genetic variations associated with RHD and eQTLs in the 13 potential druggable genes. We found that LILRB1 exhibited an interaction relationship with RHD in terms of colocalization, suggesting that it may be a candidate drug target gene.

LILRB1 is an important immune regulatory factor that belongs to the leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor family. It is highly expressed in human immune cells such as B cells, monocytes, macrophages, DCs, as well as NK cells and various T cell subsets.[24] Its extracellular region consists of immunoglobulin-like domains that bind to MHC class I molecules, interfering with T cell receptor signaling and inhibiting T cell activation, thereby suppressing immune responses.[25] The immunoreceptor tyrosine inhibitory motif (ITIM) in its intracellular region acts as a “switch” for its immunosuppressive function. Upon ligand binding, ITIM undergoes tyrosine phosphorylation, which subsequently recruits phosphatases such as SHP-1 and SHP-2 to initiate downstream inhibitory signaling, regulating the activation process of immune cells.[26,27] Furthermore, studies have shown that, in addition to impairing T cell responses through ITIM signaling, LILRB1 may also interfere with T cell activation by blocking the binding of CD8, thereby suppressing immune responses.[28] As mentioned above, the activation of T cells and the regulation of immune responses are central to RHD, and since LILRB1 can interfere with T cell activation to suppress immune responses, we hypothesize that LILRB1 may play a protective role in the occurrence and development of RHD by modulating T cell activation. Therefore, LILRB1 may be a potential core druggable target for RHD.

In CTD database, we identified several small molecules that affect the mRNA expression of LILRB1. Since LILRB1 is negatively correlated with RHD, we screened out small molecules that can upregulate LILRB1 and conducted molecular docking. These small molecules include LPS, nickel, ozone, valproic acid, and S-(1,2-dichlorovinyl) cysteine. They bind stably to LILRB1, among which LPS has the lowest binding energy. This may be related to the activation of DCs. LPS promotes their secretion of specific cytokines (such as IL-10 and TGF-β), which contribute to the development and expansion of regulatory T cells.[29] It is worth mentioning that patients with valve damage show a greater deficiency in the number of regulatory T cells.[22] Additionally, S-(1,2-dichlorovinyl) cysteine and ozone have been proven to be related to immunity and inflammation.[30,31] Nickel-doped maghemite nanoparticles may hold a promising prospect,[32] but they have not yet been studied in the heart at present. In conclusion, our current findings lay the foundation for further research on the development of drug therapies targeting LILRB1 for RHD. However, the exact mechanisms still require further exploring.

Our study has several notable strengths. This is the first study utilizing MR to identify drug targets for RHD, and the data is derived from publicly available RHD GWAS data. Furthermore, we employed multiple MR analysis methods to validate our results, enhancing the reliability of our findings. Additionally, we performed colocalization analysis to identify druggable genes, followed by drug prediction and molecular docking, with high binding activity from molecular docking indicating the significant potential of these genes as drug targets.

However, our study also has several limitations: first, while we utilized MR to provide valuable insights into causal relationships, the nonlinear relationship between exposure and outcome may not accurately reflect the effect sizes observed in real clinical settings and may not fully predict drug effects. Second, another limitation arises from the diversity of the study cohort. Although the eQTL analysis included individuals of non-European ancestry, the RHD population consisted solely of Europeans. Differences in genetic background and linkage disequilibrium patterns may introduce potential biases in MR effect estimates. Third, relying on blood eQTLs for MR detection poses challenges in determining the most effective therapeutic tissue. Different tissues may have distinct genetic regulatory mechanisms, and focusing solely on blood eQTLs may not provide a comprehensive understanding of the disease and its potential therapies. Fourth, although we performed colocalization analysis, the evidence for colocalization between PPH4 and the studied phenotype was limited, clinical trials and experimental validation are needed to confirm our conclusions in the future. Fifth, while we analyzed potential drug targets and conducted molecular docking, we cannot guarantee their efficacy in clinical settings, necessitating further experimental exploration.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study utilized MR analysis to identify potential drug targets for RHD, revealing 13 significant candidate genes that are related to immune function and closely associated with the development of RHD. Subsequently, we confirmed LILRB1 as a druggable target for RHD through colocalization analysis. Additionally, drug prediction and molecular docking were employed to validate the therapeutic value of LILRB1. Our research provides promising insights for more effective treatments for RHD; however, further clinical trials and experimental validations are needed to ensure the safety and efficacy of targeted therapies.
