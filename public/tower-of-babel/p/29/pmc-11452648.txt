The impact of fatigue on dynamic balance in coopers athletes with chronic ankle instability
Authors: Małgorzata Kowalczyk, Aleksandra Truszczyńska-Baszak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Athletes who have successfully regained high sports performance despite prior inversion injuries, and who do not have persistent symptoms, are referred to as “copers” in the literature. The aim of the study was to assess dynamic stability under fatigue in patients with chronic ankle instability in comparison to healthy controls. We conducted a case - controlled study on a group of 60 young, physically active individuals aged 29.8 ± 4.6. They were divided into 3 groups: I - unilateral ankle instability n = 14, II - bilateral ankle instability n = 15, III - no ankle instability, n = 31. All participants filled out questionnaires on the health, the FADI and FADI-S. The study protocol consisted of a dynamic stability measurements by Biodex Balance System, followed by a fatigue test, followed by the series of the same measurements. General stability index value before and value after fatigue test did not differ significantly. The differences between groups in measurements taken before fatigue test (F (2.49) = 1.59; p = 0.214; ηp2 = 0.06) and after fatigue test also proved insignificant (F (2.49) = 1.28; p = 0.286; ηp2 = 0.05). The incidence of structural ankle instability did not affect functional stability. “Copers” had efficient neural-muscular control in dynamic stabilography tests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73908-5.

Introduction

Inversion injuries of the ankle are the most common type of injuries among individuals practising sports1–3. According to Kobayashi and Gamada1 among all ankle injuries, 85% are inversion injuries, which most commonly, in 77% of cases, result in injuries of the anterior talo-fibular ligament (ATFL)4. The anterior talo-fibular ligament acts as the main stabilizer of the lateral compartment of the ankle, as it limits the anterior roll and internal rotation of the talus. Injuries to the ATFL happen during plantarflexion, supination and adduction of the foot5.

The usual consequences of inversion injury are decreased sensorimotor control, including proprioception, decreased muscle strength, decreased balance and sport performance5. These changes were observed both in individuals with a recent inversion injury and in those who had developed chronic ankle instability6–8. According to Anandacoomarasamyi & Barnsley9 and Verhagen et al.10, apart from symptoms such as: swelling, pain or instability, deficits in sensorimotor control may lead to the development of chronic ankle instability – CAI11,22.

Athletes who have successfully regained high sports performance despite prior inversion injury or injuries, and who do not have persistent symptoms, are referred to as “copers” in the literature12,13. Studies suggest that even though “copers” with a history of prior ankle inversion injuries and healthy individuals may have the same level of subjective function assessment, sensorimotor control in individuals after inversion injury may still be distorted13,14. This is why we decided to conduct a biomechanical study to determine how much dynamic balance changes under fatigue in this group of individuals. This study compliments an already published study on static balance in the same group of individuals15.The aim of the study was to assess dynamic stability under fatigue in participants with chronic ankle instability in comparison to healthy clinical controls. We made a hypotheses that dynamic balance changes under fatigue in coopers will be significantly worse comparing to healthy controls.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a case – controlled study. All methods were carried out in accordance with STROBE guidelines. All protocols were approved Józef Piłsudski University of Physical Education in Warsaw Senate Research Ethics Committee no. 01–19/2017.

Setting

The tests were performed at the Centre for Functional Diagnostics Carolina Medical Center in Warsaw, Poland between 2018 and 2020.

Participants

Participant recruitment

The recruitment of participants, both for the study populations and for the clinical control group, was announced on posters and in social media. Individuals who were interested in participation in the study completed a questionnaire on health as well as the FADI and FADI-S forms16. Consequently, 74 participants were initially qualified for the study. Eight participants did not report for the test, and six participants resigned during the test (diagram flow- Fig. 1).

Criteria for subject inclusion in the study population

Age between 18 and 35 years, a minimum of two episodes of ankle inversion injury to the same lower extremity within the past 3 years (a minimum of one serious inversion injury, preventing from sports activity ≥ for at least 21 days)17, damaged anterior talo-fibular ligament and/or talocalcaneal ligament III°, confirmed with medical imaging test of ultrasound and/or MRI, a minimum score of 60 points on the FADI and on its sports module (FADI -S) (FADI- Foot and Ankle Disability Index, S- sport module)6, full recovery in sports activity (a minimum of 6 months before the participation in the study), individuals engaged in physical activity a minimum of 2 h per week.

Criteria for subject exclusion from the study population or the clinical control group

recent or chronic pain of the locomotor system, prior surgical treatment within lower limbs, pain or swelling in the joints of the lower limbs, perceived joint instability, anxiety related to physical activity, ongoing physiotherapeutic process, movement range limitations in lower limb joints, GP’s contraindications to stress test, being a professional athlete.

Criteria for subject inclusion in the clinical control group

Age between 18 and 35 years, healthy and physically active individuals (a minimum of 2 h per week), without any injuries to the lower limbs.

Study size. The G*Power 3.1.9.4. Programme was used to establish if the test power of the obtained results was sufficient. The effect size was calculated basing on the general stability index for the three groups. Analysis of variance scheme was used to conduct the calculations in the mixed pattern 3 groups x 2 measurements with α = 0.05. The estimated test power was 0.94. Before entering the measurements, the studied individuals were informed about main assumptions, aim, course, duration and procedure of the experiment, and about the voluntary character of their participation. Before the measurements, the studied individuals filled out questionnaires on the health, the FADI and FADI-S (the sports module).

The study protocol consisted of a series of biomechanical measurements, followed by a fatigue test, followed by the series of the same measurements to assess the changes in kinetic parameters under fatigue. The general fatigue protocol was presented in the article15. The proposed fatigue trial was based on the modified Short-Term Fatigue Protocol (FAST-FP). The trial consisted of tasks involving lower extremities. It is a commonly applied fatigue protocol used in scientific studies. The protocol is based on four exercises performed in a single series: 1st exercise: three countermovement jumps on the height of a minimum of 90% Hmax. 2nd exercise: step up and down a stepper of a height of 30 cm in 30 s; the speed of the task was 200 beats per minute, timed with a metronome. 3rd exercise: five knee-bends to the position of 90° bend at the knees. 4th exercise: shuttle run between cones 5–10 m-5 m. The number of series depended on participants’ individual endurance; this allowed to achieve optimal fatigue level for each subject.

Variables

Dynamic stabilography with the use of Biodex Balance System. The platform was set at the 2nd level. The subject performed 3 trials of 30s each for each leg with eyes open. The aim of this test was to assess the ability to maintain balance on unstable surface.

We have evaluated three dynamic stability parameters: general stability index, stability in AP- anterior- posterior plane, and ML- medial lateral plane.

To analyse the differences between groups in respect of general stability index, we conducted analysis of variance in the mixed scheme 3 × 2 (study population I vs. study population II vs. clinical control group; measurement: before vs. after fatigue test).

To analyse the differences between groups in respect of stability index in the A/P and M/L plane, we conducted analysis of variance in the mixed scheme 3 × 2 (study population I vs. study population II vs. clinical control group; measurement: before vs. after fatigue test).

Results

Subsequently, we conducted a case-controlled on a group of 60 young, physically active individuals. The individuals were divided into 3 groups: I - individuals with unilateral ankle instability n = 14 (5 females, 9 males), II - individuals with bilateral ankle instability, n = 15 (5 females, 10 males) III - healthy individuals, with no ankle instability, n = 31 (10 females, 21 males).

The FADI and FADI-S are reliable, international questionnaires designed to assess functional limitations of participants with CAI; to differentiate between healthy individuals and individuals with CAI and to monitor changes resulting from physiotherapy.

To assess whether the test power for the obtained results was sufficient, an analysis was performed using the G*Power 3.1.9.4 program. Effect size was calculated based on the overall stability index in three groups (group I n = 14, group II n = 15, group III n = 31) (f = 0.47). The 3 × 2 mixed analysis of variance scheme (3 groups x 2 measurements) for α = 0.05 was adopted for calculations. The estimated test power was 0.94, which proves that the tested sample was sufficient for the analyzes performed.

Comparison of the studied populations and the clinical control group regarding the general stability index

The conducted analysis did not reveal main effect for the value of the general stability index, F (1.49) = 1.42; p = 0.239; ηp2 = 0.03. This means that general stability index value before (M = 4.24; SE = 0.30) and general stability index value after fatigue test (M = 4.45; SE = 0.35) did not differ significantly.

The main effect for the group also proved not to be significant, F (2.49) = 1.49; p = 0.235; ηp2 = 0.06. Participants from study population I (M = 4.30; SE = 0.63), study population II.

(M = 4.96; SE = 0.56) and clinical control group (M = 3.77; SE = 0.40) did not differ significantly in respect of the general stability index.

The interaction of both variables proved insignificant, F (2.49) = 0.36; p = 0.698; ηp2 = 0.02. We did not find differences between measurements before and after fatigue test within the study population I (F (1.49) = 0.01; p = 0.958; ηp2 = 0.01), study population II (F (1.49) = 1.38; p = 0.246; ηp2 = 0.03), and clinical control group (F (1.49) = 1.48; p = 0,29; ηp2 = 0,03). The differences between groups in measurements taken before fatigue test (F (2.49) = 1.59; p = 0.214; ηp2 = 0.06) and after fatigue test also proved insignificant (F (2.49) = 1.28; p = 0.286; ηp2 = 0.05). Discussion

The aim of the study was to assess changes in dynamic balance under fatigue in the studied populations. Fatigue results in generating decreased maximum voluntary contraction. It has been proved that in healthy adults with stable ankles fatigue decreases both passive and active sense of joint position18. Fatigue is a factor predisposing to ankle inversion injuries even in healthy individuals13,19.

Our analysis of dynamic stabilography did not reveal main effect for the general stability index values. This means that general stability index value before and general stability index value after fatigue test did not differ significantly. The main effect for the group also proved not significant. Participants from study population I, study population II and clinical control group did not differ significantly in respect of the general stability index. We did not find differences between the measurement taken before and after fatigue test within study population I, study population II and clinical control group. Comparisons of the studied populations and the clinical control group regarding dynamic stability in the antero-posterior plane (A/P) and medial-lateral plane did not reveal any significant changes either. Steib et al. 2013 conducted a study which was similar to our study in many respects, though their fatigue test was based on treadmill run. They found that fatigue-induced alterations of dynamic stability were greater in athletes with a previous ankle sprain16. This was confirmed by studies by Yanan et al. 202320. These authors found that after fatigue protocols of turn back runs and vertical jumps, individuals with CAI had poorer results in dynamic balance and ankle proprioception tests. Muhammad et al. investigated the effects of lower limb muscle fatigue on jumping performance and balance in netball players with CAI. Fatigue protocol involved maximal bilateral counter movement jumps. Results of their study suggested that fatigue might a risk factor for sports injuries including ankle sprains during jumping but does not contribute to dynamic stability21.

Studies on impact of fatigue use a variety of fatigue protocols with different criteria for reaching fatigue. It was crucial for our study to choose a fatigue protocol so that it reflected as much as possible type of sports activity done in volleyball, football, or basketball, i.e. sports which have the highest incidence of ankle injuries. Such fatigue protocol had to be based on interval training that engaged the muscles of lower limbs. It was important that the protocol include jumps or sudden changes of movement direction, i.e. activities typical for these sports disciplines. Of the protocols available in the literature, we decided to choose FAST-FP (Functional Agility Short-Term Fatigue Protocol) used by Cortes et al.22. This is a set of 4 different exercise types forming a single series. The number of series depended on participants’ individual endurance. In the study by Cortes et al. participants completed a mean of 9.4 exercise series (±2.7), while in our study the participants completed a mean of 12 series (±3.1). The mean duration of fatigue test in our study was approximately 15 min. In most of the cases (n = 46) the test was discontinued because the subject refused to go on with the exercise due to excessive fatigue; in 13 cases the participants did not reach the height criterion of the jump, and in one case the test was discontinued because the subject felt pain in the chest.

The duration of the test also was important for the choice of the fatigue protocol. On the one hand, it was important to choose an experiment that involved the muscles of lower limbs, based on jumping ability, while on the other hand, uncertainty arose as to whether too quick fatigue protocols would be long enough to induce kinematic and kinetic changes after exercise. The study by Quammen et al.23 provided an answer - its results showed that similar biomechanical changes to the lower limbs developed after a five-minute FAST-FP protocol and 45-minute SLOW-FP protocol. The Quammen et al.23 experiments analysed the effects of two different fatigue protocols and the risk of injury in patients after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Using both the long oxygen protocol and the fast functional fatigue protocol showed that the lower limb was under similar risk of injury under the conditions of fatigue and of unexpected movement. Fatigue causes the decrease of flexion angle at the hips and knee and results in more upright body position, therefore resulting in increased anterior subluxation at the knee and extension of anterior cruciate ligament.

Practical implications. It seems that “copers” develop efficient compensatory mechanisms that enable them to participate in the challenging sports activities without injury or without the feeling of joint instability. Determining the functional and biomechanical differences between individuals who regain high sports activity levels despite experiencing serious prior inversion injury or injuries and those individuals who develop chronic instability is essential in the context of introducing adequate treatment strategies and physiotherapeutic programmes. Undoubtedly, there is a need for further studies involving measurement methods that would to a greater extent reflect the realistic conditions of sports activities and therefore will allow for identifying differences between these groups of individuals.

Limitations of the study. In order to reflect the conditions during real sports activity, the test should involve an unexpected motor task. This would, to a certain extent, eliminate a possibility of pre-planning of a movement by a studied individual and therefore reflect more changes in the neural-muscular control. Even though it is difficult to reflect the specific conditions from the sports pitch in a laboratory, there are studies in the literature which analyse the impact of unexpected movement on kinematics and kinetics, these are lacking in our study.

Our study used fatigue protocol in which the number of series depended on participants’ individual endurance. Despite receiving considerable external motivation when doing the stress test, some of the participants may not have put their maximum effort into the test, which may have affected the result of the study.

The value of the study. The study was conducted on a homogeneous study population with full medical documentation and verification of joint instability through medical imaging tests (USG, MR).

Conclusions

The incidence of structural ankle instability did not affect functional stability. Participants with ankle instability, or “copers”, had good functional levels, enabling them to perform sports activities. “Copers” had efficient neural-muscular control in dynamic stabilography tests.
