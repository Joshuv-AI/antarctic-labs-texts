Optimization of weld bead geometry in single-pass WAAM-CMT deposits of Inconel 625 and DSS 2507 on mild steel using RSM and ANN models
Authors: G. Gogulraj, G. Rajamurugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of wire feed rate and travel speed on the geometry of weld beads and the dilution of WAAM components of DSS-2507 and IN-625 alloys employing the CMT approach. The geometry of the weld beads, including width, height, and dilution percentage, has been examined via the CCD approach alongside RSM and ANN. The ANN has yielded a lower prediction error than the RSM, hence affirming superior prediction accuracy. All trials were conducted with a constant shield gas flow rate of 15 L/min. The optimum wire feed rate of 6800 mm/min, travel speed of 120 mm/min, and resultant dilution of 4.18% for the DSS-2507 produced weld beads with a height of 4.710 mm and a width of 6.686 mm. The optimum parameters for IN-625 include a wire feed rate of 6200 mm/min, a travel speed of 120 mm/min, and a consequent dilution of 4.95%, yielding a weld bead height of 4.98 mm and a width of 6.312 mm. Optical microscopy of the components has disclosed a ferrite–austenite structure exhibiting columnar features and Laves phases. The microhardness of the components, measured in VHN, yielded values of 297–316 for DSS-2507 and 211–241 for IN-625, respectively. X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the presence of a dominant γ-austenitic phase in the IN-625 deposit, while the DSS-2507 deposit exhibited a typical duplex structure consisting of both ferrite (α) and austenite (γ) phases. The residual stresses in the parts have been measured along the weld beads.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing, also referred to as 3D printing, constructs build incrementally by depositing and solidifying material layer by layer. This bottom-up methodology delivers considerable benefits compared to conventional subtractive manufacturing, particularly for complex geometries. WAAM distinguishes itself among metal additive manufacturing techniques with its superior control, flexibility, and automation abilities1–3. WAAM employs welding techniques, a prevalent way of joining metals through thermoplastics conditions. Welding involves the melting and amalgamation of materials, which solidify upon cooling to establish a relationship. In contrast to lower-temperature joining methods such as soldering and brazing, welding amalgamates the base material itself, facilitating the union of comparable or different metals, with or without the use of filler material, heat, and pressure4–6.

Filler wire liquefies and combines with the molten base metal when employed, forming a hardened junction. Despite the protective function of shielding gases, contamination of the molten metal may nevertheless transpire. Welding utilises multiple energy sources, such as electric arcs, electron beams, gas flames, friction, ultrasonics, and lasers, and can be showed in numerous conditions, including open air, underwater, and outer space7. In this research observes focusing on parameter optimisation in WAAM utilising the CMT8,9, a sophisticated variation of gas metal arc welding10. CMT reduces heat input, diminishes spatter, and develops arc stability. It employs a short-circuiting metal transfer mode in conjunction with accurate wire movement11. The shape of each deposited bead significantly influences the quality of components produced by WAAM prepared a parameter changes essential.

DSS-2507 and IN-625 have excellent mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature competences due to their composition of chromium and nickel presence. The main inspiration for using nickel-based superalloys is their outstanding material properties, including mechanical strength, surface degradation resistance, and creep resistance at high temperatures12,13. Over the years, researchers have created a variety of nickel-based superalloys14,15. IN-625 is a frequently used nickel-based superalloy. Niobium, molybdenum, and nickel–chromium combine to form this superalloy, which maintains its mechanical properties and corrosion resistance at temperatures as high as 650 °C. These exceptional properties are maintainable even in the absence of precipitation heat treatment. The aerospace sector widely using in IN-625 components in the “hot section” of gas turbine engines, nuclear reactors, the chemical industry, and other high-temperature and corrosion-resistant applications16. DSS-2507 are recognized for their superior corrosion resistance and mechanical properties across many environmental conditions, making them extensively used in petrochemical, marine, nuclear, and chemical applications17. The microstructure of DSS-2507 has two phases, like ferrite (α) and austenite (γ). Moreover, it is vital to sustain an approximately equal quantity of ferrite and austenite phases (i.e., a 1:1 ratio) to guarantee superior strength and improved corrosion resistance. Recently, super DSS-2507 has garnered significant attention owing to its superior tensile and fatigue strength, admirable toughness, high pitting corrosion resistance, high weldability and formability, and remarkable resistance to stress corrosion cracking in chloride and sulphur environments18. This explicates its extensive applications of using in steam pipelines, heat exchangers, desalination plants, pressure vessels, and boiler.

The RSM includes a collection of statistical and mathematical methodologies employed to detect the most efficient procedures. RSM is generally utilised in scenarios where several input elements influence a process’s performance or a quality characteristic19. The studies utilise a RSM design integrated with Particle Swarm Optimisation to determine optimal TIG welding parameters, although they focus on different alloys and implement varied modelling approaches. In SS-310, a hybrid BP-PSO model integrated with an RSM-CCD design is employed to concurrently minimise hot cracking and improve microhardness via a composite performance index that amalgamates crack sensitivity and hardness. Conversely, an RSM-PSO framework is employed for AA7075 to optimise microhardness and manage weld flaws during both conventional and ultrasonic-assisted TIG welding processes20,21. In this recent study discusses and compares the RSM and ANN predictions, highlighting the higher prediction accuracy of RSM compared to ANN when validated against the experimental values22.

To investigate the ultra-cold WAAM technique for producing High Strength Low Alloy steel, emphasising its superior arc stability and less spatter generation attributable to accelerated solidification rates. The ultra-cold WAAM technology reduces thermal accumulation throughout part of fabrication while maintaining the deposition rate23. Furthermore, investigations into the microstructural characteristics of DSS built via the TIG arc technique indicated that extreme austenite development disrupts the equilibrium between the austenite and ferrite stages within the materials structure. The presence of CrN and Cr2N additions decreases the materials ductility and toughness24. The influence of respectable heat input on the microstructure of austenitic steel, observing that during the WAAM process, this heat input encourages the accelerated growth of essential dendrites, which can fully mature owing to reduced cooling rates25.

The studies focus lack on research focused on developing the IN-625-claddined EN8 steel substrates through the WAAM method for optimizing their properties for harsh utilisations in engineering fields. The analysis clearly illustrates the production of error-free deposits with excellent metallurgical bonding for ensuring overall superiority in the integrity of the deposited layers26. The addition of hardened particles like VC and TiC in weight percentages (5–30%) yields remarkable improvements in wear and erosion resistance properties, especially with the addition of 5% VC for optimizing agricultural tillage wear resistance in samples oriented vertically, which are more wear resistant than samples fabricated in the horizontal direction27. Similar results are produced by coatings reinforced with various weight percentages (5%, 10%, and 30%) of TiC, for which wear resistance is increased in transverse deposits with smaller microstructural details and more aligned grains; nevertheless, increased samples of weight percentage (30%) resulted in a higher value of the friction coefficient but yet displayed increased wear resistance properties. Conclusively, this research work experimentally verifies IN-625-claddined WAAM deposits to be remarkable wear- and erosion-resistant surface-modified engineered structures for harsh utilisations in both hydraulic and agricultural machines operating in extreme wear and erosion regimes28,29.

Although many studies concentrate on WAAM methods for Inconel alloys, particularly Inconel 71830–32, there is a scarcity of research on IN-625. Components fabricated from Inconel 718 generally necessitate post-processing heat treatment, but IN-625 does not require such treatments, rendering it more suitable for hybrid WAAM processes within a single equipment32. Assessing material attributes is essential in developing invention strategy, facilitating a deeper comprehension of the essential ethics of the manufacturing process. To establish dependable manufacturing techniques, it is crucial to examine the material properties of produced by diverse complimentary processes. The CMT discontinuous WAAM technique results in a finer, more varied microstructure assessed to the CMT incessant WAAM approach, which yields fewer and smaller internal pores. Samples produced using the CMT discontinuous WAAM technique exhibit enhanced wear resistance at 650 °C in comparison to those invented with the continuous approach33. The main objective of the study to examine the effects of welding current and travel speed on bead geometry and microstructure in WAAM produced SS-308L. It seeks to identify optimal parameters for achieving uniform, high-quality layer deposition. This study aims to provision the fabrication of customized biomedical components, particularly orthopaedics knee implants34.

The existing literature specifies that the influence of process parameters on DSS-2507 and IN-625 weld beads produced via CMT-based WAAM has not been adequately examined. Previous research has predominantly focused on similar filler metals and base metals, with minimal exploration of dissimilar weld beads. This study investigates wire feed rate and travel speed as critical factors in dissimilar metal WAAM. The primary aim is to assess the mechanical characteristics and microstructure of weld beads produced using WAAM-CMT, emphasising the need for further optimisation of input parameters for weld bead geometry when utilising DSS-2507 and IN-625. The research employs RSM with a CCD matrix to systematically explore these relationships and compare the ANN model prediction. Subsequently, the microstructure, microhardness, and XRD phase analysis for the IN-625 and DSS-2507 weld beads are evaluated within the WAAM-CMT process, as demonstrated by the experimental configuration shown in Fig. 1. This study uses RSM-based multi-condition decision-making to identify optimal input parameters that yield favourable outcomes across various responses while complying with ANN model prediction. A systematic methodology is employed to assess the impact of these parameters on weld bead shape and dilution in WAAM-CMT processes, an area currently lacking extensive research. The experimental design for the present study was developed using the RSM framework to investigate the influence of travel speed (A) and wire feed rate (B) on the weld bead geometry during WAAM-CMT deposition of DSS 2507 and IN-625. Since only two process parameters were considered, each parameter was evaluated at three levels (− 1, 0, + 1). Therefore, a three-level full factorial design (32) consisting of nine experimental runs was adopted for each material, as presented in Tables 3 and 4.

This design enables the estimation of a second-order quadratic regression model including linear terms, interaction effects, and quadratic terms. For a two-factor quadratic model, six regression coefficients (intercept, two linear terms, one interaction term, and two quadratic terms) must be estimated. With nine experimental runs, sufficient degrees of freedom are available for estimating the model parameters and evaluating statistical significance through ANOVA.

The central level (0,0) corresponds to the intermediate combination of process parameters and represents the centre point of the design. Due to the high material consumption and experimental cost associated with WAAM-CMT deposition trials, replication of the centre point was not performed. However, the adequacy of the developed models was validated using ANOVA, coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, and comparison between predicted and experimental responses, confirming the reliability of the developed regression models.

The Table 5 shows that welding with DSS-2507 and IN-625 has regression models that show how travel speed (A) and wire feed rate (B) affect BW, BH, and D%. These models include linear, interaction (AB), and quadratic (A2, B2) effects. For both materials, bead width diminishes as travel speed and wire feed rate increase, indicating a negative correlation. Travel speed adversely affects bead height in DSS-2507, while wire feed has a negligible positive effect. Conversely, in IN-625, wire feed has a more pronounced positive impact on bead height. The dilution % models indicate that transit speed employs a significant positive influence on both materials, supplemented by contributions from wire feed rate, interaction effects, and quadratic terms that introduce nonlinear behaviour. The inclusion of interaction (AB) and quadratic (A2, B2) terms in all equations indicates the intricacy of these welding responses, requiring meticulous optimisation of parameters to attain the required weld bead quality. These equations function as effective predictive instruments for process optimisation, guaranteeing accurate parameter of welding parameters to improve quality and performance in DSS-2507 and IN-625 for industrial AM applications.

Plot diagrams

Combining WAAM-CMT is a state-of-the-art method for producing intricate metallic structures with less heat input and more accuracy. The motivation of this study is on the link between input and output parameters as it examines the process behaviour of WAAM-CMT for DSS-2507 and IN-625. Because of their substantial impact on weld bead properties, heat input and travel feed rate are the primary input parameters considered. Deposition efficiency and material integrity are assessed by evaluating the output characteristics, which include bead width, dilution percentage, and bead height. In order to find the optimal procedure for improving the mechanical characteristics and structural stability of both materials, plot diagrams are used to show how these variables are dependent on one another.

Plot diagrams in DSS-2507

The Fig. 13a the heat input and travel speed during WAAM-CMT welding of DSS-2507 are affected by important elements such as BH, BW, and D%. Typically, the input heat varies between 0.4 and 1.3 kJ/mm, the heat input calculated in Eq. (3). The heat input is directly proportional to the BH this is because melting and fusing material at a greater bead height demands more energy, leading to a larger total heat input. Because it takes more time and energy to weld bigger amounts of material, the heat input is highest at bead heights between 4.5- and 5.5-mm. Bead width is another factor that affects heat input; a higher heat input is necessary to melt material with a wider bead width. Nevertheless, there is a tipping point beyond which wider beads may improve heat distribution, resulting in less heat intake even at extremely wide beads. Higher dilution levels in weld bead outcome in better mixing of the base metal with the filler material, making a more diluted molten pool that requires less heat to preserve fusion. As the filler material concentration drops under 8–12%, the weld pool becomes easier to withstand reduced energy contribution, demonstrating an inverse relationship between heat input and dilution percentage. This ensues since the diluted pool, now predominantly composed of the base metal, has different thermal properties that allow for effective fusion with lower heat, making the process more energy-efficient at higher dilution levels.Fig. 13Plot diagrams in DSS-2507 (a) Heat input vs BH, BW, and D% (b) Travel speed vs BH, BW, and D%3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{H}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t} \mathrm{I}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{t} \left(I\right)=\frac{Voltage\times Amperage\times 60}{Travel speed\times 1000}$$\end{document}

The Fig. 13b characteristic varies from 120 to 240 mm/min in terms of travel speed. Because the welding process needs less time to deposit material at greater rates when the bead heights are within an ideal range, the travel speed remains higher at bead heights of about 240 mm/min. Nevertheless, the microstructure of the weld can be compromised if the bead height is inadequate, as the travel speed will be reduced to meet the required temperature cycle for effective fusion. The travel speed is most affected by the bead width, with widths between 3.5 and 4.8 mm showing sharp drops. Since wider BW increase the amount of molten metal, travel speeds need to be slow down to ensure suitable fusion and cooling. This, in turn, deviations the microstructure and rate of solidification of the weld bead. The travel speed is significantly affected by the D%. A lower viscosity and surface tension of the molten pool are achieved with a larger D%, which allows for faster travel speeds without surrendering weld quality. Initially, the travel speed stays high as the bead height grows, but it declines when the height reaches about 4.5–5 mm. Micro-hardness test

Conducting micro-hardness measurements according to the ASTM E34 standard, this section to study determines the hardness distribution across the weld beads in the WAAM-CMT process for DSS-2507 and IN-625. By analysing the outcome of process parameters, such as heat input and travel feed rate, on hardness differences, we gain insight into the material response to thermal cycles and deposition characteristics. The hardness values obtained shed light on the metallurgical properties of the alloys, which can be used to optimise the process for AM applications by improving durability and wear resistance.

Micro-hardness test on DSS-2507

Figure 15 demonstrates the examination of Vickers microhardness numbers (VHN) for DSS-2507 weld beads, assessed with a Vickers microhardness tester engaging a 500-gf load and a 10-s dwell duration. The typical hardness for each bead was determined using microhardness measurements obtained at five distinct locations across the top region to bottom region. Figures L1 to L10 demonstrate the microhardness indentations along with their respective locations depicted in Fig. 15. The graph demonstrations VHN data for nine weld beads L1–L9, each generated with different travel speeds and wire feed rates, resulting in modifications in weld characteristics. L1, L4, and L7 were welded at a travel speed of 120 mm/min L2, L5, and L8 at 180 mm/min and L3, L6, and L9 at 240 mm/min. L1 (120 mm/min) attained the maximum hardness of 315 VHN, representative that a reduced travel speed, associated to elevated heat input and slower cooling, may facilitate transformations or secondary phase precipitation that locally enhance hardness by altering the ferrite/austenite ratio. Conversely, L4 at 120 mm/min and L7 120 mm/min demonstrated hardness values of 312 VHN and 316 VHN, respectively, suggesting that, despite equal travel speeds, additional parameters such as wire feed rate and subsequent heat input might affect the microstructural response and hardness results.Fig. 15Micro-hardness values of DSS-2507.

Micro-hardness values of DSS-2507.

Upon increasing the travel speed to 180 mm/min L2, L5, and L8, the recorded hardness values were 308 VHN, 309 VHN, and 308 VHN, respectively. The increase in travel speed leads to a reduction in heat input and enhances cooling, thereby obstructing significant grain growth and secondary phase development, ultimately resulting in marginally reduced microhardness. The maximum travel speed of 240 mm/min L3, L6, and L9 correlates with a further reduction in hardness, measuring 297 VHN for L3, 305 VHN for L6, and 299 VHN for L9. This phenomenon is expected attributable to accelerated cooling and limited solid-state transformations, resulting in a finer grain structure but diminished capacity for hardening phase precipitations. Comparisons of weld beads produced at the same travel speed, such as L1, L4, and L7, indicate that at a constant travel speed, fluctuations in wire feed rate and consequently in actual heat input can affect hardness, which typically increases with increasing deposition and heat input. The microhardness variations in DSS-2507 weld beads underscore a complex relationship between the welding parameters and the resultant microstructural features like phase distribution, grain size, and secondary precipitates, which collectively influence the mechanical performance of the beads. This study strongly correlates with and expands upon existing research by systematically connecting variations in microstructural grain size, caused by cyclic thermal histories during the WAAM-CMT process, to variances in microhardness across weld bead. The observed trends in hardness variation and the relationships between microstructure and properties validate and quantify discoveries from previous studies37,38.

Microstructure investigations

To comprehend phase formation, grain morphology, and metallurgical transformations, it is essential to examine the microstructural behaviour of DSS-2507 and IN-625 weld beads in WAAM-CMT procedures. This study assesses the influence of heat input and deposition locations on structural integrity by microstructurally analysing the maximum heat input and elevated microhardness of weld bead L7 from each material, conducted at the centre of the weld beads. The ferrite–austenite phase equilibrium of DSS-2507 and the dendritic growth and carbide precipitation of IN-625 are examined, as these elements affect the mechanical properties of these alloys, respectively. The results illuminate the microstructural evolution of the two metals, which is crucial for optimising methods to enhance material qualities in additive manufacturing.

Microstructure of DSS-2507

The Fig. 17 analysed under Olympus BX53M microscopy, the microstructure of WAAM-CMT produced DSS-2507 demonstrations a distinct dual-phase structure with austenitic and ferritic phases. The presence of discernible grain boundaries indicates regulated solidification, an essential component in preserving phase balance. A modest cooling rate, with the highest heat input of 1.233 kJ/mm weldment bead, ensures the creation of a fine microstructure while minimising excessive grain growth. It is important to control the cooling rate so that the austenitic and ferritic phases stay balanced39. If the cooling rate is too high, the ferrite content will rise, and if it is too low, phase changes like the formation of the sigma phase will happen, which will lower the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance. Intermetallic precipitates form during welding because the moderate heat input controls precipitation without making too many brittle phases, which could make the material less flexible and tough.Fig. 17Microstructure images of DSS-2507.

Microstructure images of DSS-2507.

The microstructure probably exhibits a Widmanstätten austenite morphology, characterised by austenite platelets or needles precipitating within the ferrite matrix or a more globular, less distinct austenite distribution. The precise phase balance and shape are determined by the chemical composition, cooling rate, and heat cycles encountered throughout the WAAM process. Because WAAM-CMT solidifies quickly, it may lead to non-equilibrium conditions that change the ratio of ferrite to austenite compared to 2507 that has been treated in the usual way40–43. Moreover, the heat input and interpass temperature during WAAM might affect the diffusion and precipitation of secondary phases, such as sigma phase or carbides, which may not be discernible at this magnification but can influence the material’s characteristics. The observed microstructure is essential in ascertaining the mechanical characteristics and corrosion resistance of the duplex stainless-steel weldment.

Microstructure of IN-625

According to Fig. 18 Olympus BX53M microscopy, the microstructure of IN-625 produced with WAAM-CMT displays a dendritic morphology. This means that there are clearly defined grain boundaries, slip bands, carbide precipitates, and deformation characteristics. Crucial to the regulation of the alloy’s cooling rate, solidification behaviour, and phase evolution is the heat input of 1.136 kJ/mm weldment bead. The regulated temperature gradient, made possible by this moderate heat input, prevents the grain from coarsening too much and promotes the solidification of fine dendrites, which in turn improves mechanical qualities such as toughness, creep resistance, and strength. Grain boundaries show that the solidification was done in a specific direction, and the microstructure was refined by cooling at a pace that minimised columnar grain formation and promoted equiaxed dendritic structures, which improved mechanical stability and fatigue resistance. The alloy’s resistance to cyclic loading is enhanced by strain hardening caused by thermal stresses during layer deposition, as suggested by slip bands, which are indicators of plastic deformation. By avoiding dislocation movement, the carbide precipitates that develop because of solidification segregation help to reinforce the alloy44,45. The alloy’s corrosion resistance and ductility can be preserved by avoiding the invention of harmful secondary phases, like the γ-phase or Laves phase, recognizes to the uniform carbide distribution achieved by the modest heat input. The observed deformation patterns recommend that the heat input promotes a slow release of heat, which in turn reduces the probability of hot cracking by preventing the accumulation of residual stress. This heat input maintains the ideal mechanical performance and metallurgical integrity by efficiently balancing fusion and penetration.Fig. 18Microstructure images of IN-625.

The more cooling rates in WAAM intensify this micro segregation, which is essential to the solidification process. Precipitates, such as Laves phase, may be present in the interdendritic zones. The dendritic structure grows because of the obvious temperature gradients and fast solidification characteristic of the WAAM process46–48. As the weld pool cools, solidification occurs directionally, resulting in the formation of elongated, dendritic formations. The elected WAAM parameters, like heat input, travel speed, and wire feed rate, change the shape and size of the dendrites as well as the amount of micro segregation and precipitation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The XRD patterns of the WAAM-CMT deposited IN-625 and DSS-2507 samples are presented in Fig. 19. For the IN-625 deposit, the diffraction pattern shows prominent peaks corresponding to the FCC γ-phase of nickel-based austenite. The major diffraction peaks are observed at approximately 2θ ≈ 43°, 50°, and 74°, corresponding to the crystallographic planes (111), (200), and (220), respectively. These peaks confirm that the deposited IN-625 predominantly consists of a γ -Ni solid solution phase, which is consistent with the typical microstructure of nickel-based superalloys. The obtained diffraction peaks match well with the standard reference pattern of γ -Ni (FCC structure) corresponding to JCPDS card No. 04–085049, confirming the presence of a single-phase austenitic structure in the deposited IN-625 sample. For the DSS-2507 deposited sample, the XRD pattern indicates the presence of both ferrite (α) and austenite (γ) phases, which is characteristic of duplex stainless steels. The diffraction peaks corresponding to the ferritic BCC phase are observed at approximately 2θ ≈ 44° and 82°, associated with the (110) and (211) planes, respectively. These peaks match well with the standard reference pattern of α-ferrite with JCPDS card No. 06–0696. This phase balance is typical for duplex stainless steels and plays an important role in providing the combination of strength and corrosion resistance associated with DSS-2507.

Residual stress measurements

The residual stress in an alloy means the stresses that are already in the material when no more outside force is applied. These stresses must meet the requirements of static equilibrium, which means that no net force can act on any internal plane. The objective of residual stress analysis is to measure these internal stresses and figure-out what kind they are, whether they are tensile or compressive. There are several ways to do this, such as stress relief methods are hole drilling, non-destructive ultrasonic testing, and X-ray diffraction. X-ray diffraction is the maximum popular of these because it is very accurate and can see things that are less than 1 mm3 in size. It is important to note, though, that this study only expressions at surface-level strain measurements and does not include a full valuation of bulk residual stress.

In the present study, the residual stresses developed in the WAAM-CMT deposited samples were measured using the X-ray diffraction technique after the welding deposition process. The measurements were carried out on the top surface of the deposited weld bead to evaluate the residual stress generated during the WAAM process. Initially, the measurement location was carefully identified on the top of the weld bead to ensure accurate stress evaluation in the deposited region. The X-ray detector was then positioned at an appropriate angle during the measurement process. For the iron-based alloy, which has an austenitic crystal structure, the detector angle (2θ) was set at approximately 30°. Similarly, for the nickel-based alloy, the detector angle was also maintained at around 30° to obtain reliable diffraction peaks for residual stress calculation. The X-ray diffraction method determines residual stress by measuring the change in lattice spacing caused by internal stresses within the material. The residual stress analysis of the WAAM-CMT fabricated weld beads, depicted in Fig. 20a for DSS-2507 L7 bead and (b) for IN-625 L7 bead, yields noteworthy insights into the thermal and mechanical properties of these materials in WAAM techniques. The measured rivetted residual stress for DSS-2507, with a heat input of 1.233 kJ/mm, is − 386 MPa. The Debye ring images and extracted profiles for DSS-2507 show a fairly even strain distribution. This is exposed by the well-defined, continuous Debye rings and the sharp intensity peak in the profile plot. These traits point to a consistent pattern of residual stress along the bead, which is what you would expect from DSS-2507 under the best WAAM-CMT conditions. The IN-625 weld bead, on the other hand, has a much higher amount of absorbed compressive residual stress at − 392 MPa, even though it was made with a slightly lower heat input of 1.136 kJ/mm. The Debye ring and distortion profiles that go with them show a superior deviation and a wider intensity spread, especially in the profile plot. This means that there are sharper residual stress gradients and possible microstructural heterogeneity. The higher residual stress in IN-625 is because it has a higher thermal expansion coefficient and a lower thermal conductivity than DSS-2507. This means that it cools down faster and builds up more compressive stresses in the bead. These outcomes show how the configuration of the material and the process parameters work collected to change the residual stress states in WAAM-CMT produced weld beads. IN-625 shows a much higher ability to build up compressive stresses than DSS-2507 when made in the same technique.Fig. 20(a) DSS-2507 L7 weld bead and, (b) IN-625 L7 weld bead for residual stress measurement.

Future work

Future endeavours will focus on optimising parameters to achieve efficient combination of dissimilar metals, notably DSS-2507 and IN-625, via the WAAM-CMT technology. This optimisation seeks to augment interfacial adhesion, reduce flaws, and enhancement mechanical performance, thereby maintaining the structural integrity of multi-material components in innovative AM applications.
