Cost of reusable gastrointestinal endoscopes to the NHS: findings from a micro-costing study
Authors: Mandana Zanganeh, Yufei Jiang, Norman Waugh, Bu Hayee, Shaji Sebastian, Trudi Gillespie, Ramesh P Arasaradnam, Lazaros Andronis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to quantify the per-procedure costs of acquiring, maintaining/repairing and reprocessing reusable gastrointestinal endoscopes by observing practices in a large National Health Service (NHS) hospital.MethodsWe conducted a bottom-up micro-costing analysis to capture the costs of reusable gastrointestinal endoscopes using a detailed resource-use data sheet and observations at the University Hospital Coventry and Warwickshire (UHCW). The data sheet drew on the published literature and NHS decontamination guidance. Cost categories included (1) measuring personnel time for reprocessing endoscopes, (2) reprocessing materials and (3) acquisition and maintenance/repair of endoscopy and reprocessing equipment. Data were obtained through observation and interviews with staff. Costs were calculated using the data collected at UHCW and cross-checked with data from two other NHS Trusts, manufacturers and the literature.ResultsStaff time for reprocessing averaged 35 min per procedure (£23.57: 22% of the total cost). The reprocessing materials’ cost per procedure was £16.41 (15% of the total cost). Total capital acquisition cost per procedure was £46.9 (44% of the total cost), including endoscopy capital (£37.4) and reprocessing capital (£9.5). Total maintenance/repair cost per procedure was £20.46 (19% of the total cost). These led to a total cost of £107.34 per endoscopy procedure.ConclusionsSome observed values were slightly lower than but generally comparable to similar studies. We identified the key drivers of costs, led by capital costs. The results of our study could be used in economic evaluations involving reusable gastrointestinal endoscopes. The methodology can inform the cost evaluation of medical devices that require intensive reprocessing.Keywords: GASTROINTESINAL ENDOSCOPY, DECISION ANALYSIS, STATISTICS

Introduction

There is debate on the comparative merits of single-use and reusable medical devices, and one example is endoscopes in gastroenterology. Every year, around 1.5 million endoscopies are undertaken in the UK.1 In the UK National Health Service (NHS), most endoscopes are reusable and are used numerous times after cleaning and disinfection. Decontamination processes for reusable devices are essential to prevent cross-infection but require considerable resources, including personnel time and disinfectants. Disposable single-use endoscopes are being marketed on the grounds that they reduce or eliminate the risk of infection. Their technical performances are comparable to reusable endoscopes2 3; however, switching to disposable endoscopes may result in much higher costs to the healthcare system.4

Accurate information about the cost of gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopes enables more informed decision-making about the costs of alternative options. The per-procedure cost of GI endoscopy has a sizeable impact on evaluations comparing single-use versus reusable devices. Our recent review of economic evidence of GI endoscopes found that the per-procedure cost of endoscopy with reusable GI endoscopes varied considerably among different countries, settings and reprocessing strategies.5 A study in the USA6 assessed the impact of the new reprocessing guidelines (2015–2016) and estimated that the overall cost of reprocessing one endoscope ranged from US$114 to US$281 using the standard high-level disinfection (HLD) approach. Their costing methodology was later adapted in the UK by Mouritsen et al7 to assess the per-procedure cost of £249 for bronchoscopy with a reusable scope. No study has rigorously assessed the per-procedure cost of reusable GI endoscopes in the UK.

We therefore aimed to provide reliable per-procedure cost estimates of endoscopy with reusable GI endoscopes by observing practices in a large NHS hospital, which are essential inputs for relevant economic evaluations and decision-making. This study provides data for an economic evaluation comparing reusable versus single-use duodenoscopes. In addition to providing a UK-based cost of reprocessing, we aimed to identify the key drivers of costs, which will be useful for assessing the impact of new technologies such as drying equipment.

Methods

We conducted a bottom-up micro-costing analysis to capture the costs of reusable GI endoscopes based on a detailed resource-use data sheet and observations at the University Hospital Coventry and Warwickshire (UHCW), which serves a catchment of 1.3 million. The data sheet was developed based on the frameworks used in Mouritsen et al,7 Ofstead et al6 and NHS decontamination guidance,8 with adjustments made to reflect the practices at this NHS site.

Cost categories included (1) staff time for reprocessing reusable endoscopes, (2) reprocessing materials, including consumables and personal protective equipment (PPE) and (3) acquisition and maintenance/repair of endoscopes and reprocessing equipment.

Costs of the endoscopy procedure itself (largely personnel-related costs for carrying out the endoscopy) are not included, as they are the same across single-use versus reusable endoscopes.

One author (MZ) visited the UHCW endoscopy unit and was assisted by an experienced technician (TG). Data were collected by directly observing the reprocessing steps, materials and capital equipment used in a setting where only reusable endoscopes are employed. The unit comprises one room for reprocessing and five rooms for endoscopy procedures. The endoscope reprocessing team includes 11 technicians, each working 4 days per week to provide coverage across a 7-day week. Reprocessing steps were undertaken as per the manufacturer’s guidance.

Costs were calculated using the data collected at this site, combined with additional data from two hospitals in London and Hull, Pentax Medical, Steelco Group (IM Med), the NHS Supply Chain online catalogue, the Personal Social Services Research Unit (PSSRU)9 and published literature. All costs were converted to 2024 Great British Pound (GBP) using the Bank of England Inflation Calculator.10 Salary on-costs and overheads were included in the PSSRU unit cost of labour. 10% overheads were allocated to reprocessing materials (expert opinion) and 20% to capital acquisition and maintenance/repair costs.11 A 3.5% discounting rate was applied to the capital acquisition and maintenance/repair cost, where applicable.

We consulted senior members from two other hospitals (Hull and Kings) to review our methods and check whether the reported values aligned with those in their units. They engaged with their local reprocessing staff to review the data and provide feedback.

To calculate the mean capital and maintenance/repair cost per procedure, we used the annual number of GI endoscopies (approximately 14 000 all using reusable GI endoscopes: Olympus). For the base-case analysis, acquisition costs for endoscopes and reprocessing capital were annualised across a 7-year period, in line with both our observations and manufacturers’ recommendations.

Sensitivity analyses

We used a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results. First, we calculated the cost per endoscopy procedure by applying different capital lifespans (5 and 10 years). Second, we varied the annual volume of endoscopy procedures from 100% to 50% of the base case to make our results generalisable to hospitals with lower procedure volumes. We assumed that the number of pieces of equipment does not decrease with the annual volume of endoscopy procedures.

Economic evaluations are typically focused on duodenoscopes due to their complex design and cleaning challenges, which increase the risk of infection; therefore, we conducted a scenario analysis specifically on duodenoscopes. For endoscopy capital costs, we used the base case of the annual endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) volume of 1040 procedures at UHCW, while also testing alternative volumes ranging from 100% to 50% of the base case. Other cost calculations remained unchanged.

Results

Reprocessing staff time and labour cost

The reprocessing staff time per procedure, based on observations at the UHCW, was estimated at 30 min, with an associated labour cost of £20.20 (table 1). Feedback from the other two hospitals confirmed that the practices were in line with the established reprocessing guidelines and that the time allocation appeared reasonable. One notable difference was reported by the NHS Trust in Hull, where longer reprocessing staff time was recorded, mainly due to the longer distance between the endoscopy unit and the decontamination facility. A member of the decontamination staff at Hull indicated that while a 30-min duration is feasible in practice, a 40-min estimate would be more appropriate for their costing purposes due to logistical considerations. Therefore, we used a time cost of 35 min to account for variation among NHS Trusts. This adjustment increased the estimated labour cost associated with reprocessing to £23.57 per procedure (table 1).

Reprocessing materials cost

The cost of reprocessing materials per procedure at the UHCW was estimated at £16.41, including 10% overheads (see table 2). To validate our estimates, we consulted with the other two NHS Trusts, both of which confirmed the categories and values presented in table 2. For example, the ward manager of the GI endoscopy unit at a London NHS Trust noted that the consumables costs at the UHCW (table 2) were similar to what is used in their trust.

Endoscopy and reprocessing capital acquisition and maintenance/repair cost

Based on cost data from different sources, including UHCW, King’s College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, manufacturers and the literature. The endoscopy capital cost per use at UHCW was calculated as £37.40 (see table 3). The capital cost of reprocessing equipment per procedure at UHCW was calculated as £9.50 (see table 3). The total capital cost per procedure was estimated to be £46.90, with 20% overheads. The maintenance/repair cost per endoscope use was £9.17 for endoscopy capital and £11.29 for reprocessing capital, resulting in a total of £20.46. The categories and values presented in table 3 were generally validated by the consulted NHS Trusts. The NHS Trust in London identified some minor discrepancies, and the relevant revisions were incorporated into the analysis.

Discussion

Reasons for differences in each cost category with the literature

Some values for reprocessing of reusable endoscopes seemed lower but generally in line with published literature (Mouritsen et al,7 Ofstead et al6 and Bang et al14) and evidence from our systematic review.5

We observed some differences in staff time spent in reprocessing steps (see table 1) compared with studies by Mouritsen et al7 and Ofstead et al.6 Reprocessing staff time and its cost per procedure in our study averaged 35 min (£23.57 including salary on-costs and overheads). In Mouritsen et al’s study7 of bronchoscopes (simpler in structure than duodenoscopes), it was 50 min (£14.76 in 2017 and £19.11 in 2024), which included 15% of training time, and in the study by Ofstead and colleagues6 of GI endoscopes, it was 76 min, including an assumed proportion (20%) of recleaning (between $21.27 and $43.22 depending on staff level); price year not stated. Reasons for these differences are as follows. (1) Less time for each reprocessing step at UHCW ((a) less time in transportation because of shorter distance and (b) in the ideal case, experienced staff can reprocess more efficiently, but in practice, the reprocessing may take longer). (2) Fewer reprocessing steps at the UHCW ((a) endoscopes are not put in bags or vacuumed before storage and (b) cleaning verification tests are not performed after reprocessing, therefore no recleaning is performed).

The reprocessing material cost per procedure in our study was £16.41 in 2024, including 10% overheads (see table 2), lower than the reported value for bronchoscopes by Mouritsen et al7 (£25.1 in 2017 and £32.51 in 2024). Potential reasons for this difference include: (1) there were a few materials not used at UHCW; in Mouritsen et al’s study,7 there were a washroom and a clean room, which both require PPE; at UHCW, there is only one reprocessing room, no cleaning verification test is performed, and there is no disinfection of transport boxes; and (2) Some costs were recorded slightly higher in Mouritsen et al’s study7; for example, the power cost for automated endoscope reprocessors.

Endoscopy capital cost per procedure in our study was £37.40 in 2024, including 20% overheads (see table 3), which was considerably lower than the estimate in Mouritsen et al’s study7 (£113 in 2017 and £146.34 in 2024). Reprocessing capital cost per procedure in our study was estimated at £9.50 in 2024, including 20% overheads (see table 3), higher than the reported value in Mouritsen et al’s study7 (£3.67 in 2017 and £4.75 in 2024).

One potential reason for this discrepancy is the annual number of GI endoscopy procedures and, therefore, the number of times of reprocessing GI endoscopes. The annual procedure volume at UHCW was 14 000, and a high volume results in a lower reprocessing cost per endoscope. Other contributing factors to the differences in endoscopy capital cost included the shorter capital lifespan (5 years used by Mouritsen et al7) compared with our 7-year base-case analysis. However, we conducted two sensitivity analyses using 5- and 10-year capital lifespans. Furthermore, the scopes at the UHCW were intensively used. For instance, four duodenoscopes were used for around 20 ERCPs weekly (around 1040 ERCPs annually). On average, each scope would be used 1820 times during its 7-year lifespan. At a high-volume hospital in the USA (Bang et al14), nine duodenoscopes with a 3-year lifespan were used for 1752 ERCPs annually. On average, each scope was used 583 times during its lifespan. According to Mouritsen et al,7 19 bronchoscopes (5-year lifespan) were used for 1174 procedures annually at Guy’s Hospital. On average, each scope was used 309 times during its lifespan.

According to Mouritsen et al,7 the bronchoscopy maintenance/repair cost per use and reprocessing capital’s maintenance/repair cost per use were £87 in 2017 (£112.67 in 2024) and £5.71 in 2017 (£7.39 in 2024), respectively. Endoscopy maintenance/repair cost per use in our study was much lower, which could be explained by the endoscopy type, volume and lifetime usage.

Mouritsen et al7 included additional capital elements such as the reprocessing room itself and also internal and external auditing. Documentation was recommended by Ofsted et al,6 including using biohazard labels on dirty transport containers, using a system (tag or label) to verify reprocessing occurred and maintaining records linking patients to endoscopes. However, we did not include these items in our analysis due to a lack of information. Instead, we applied an overhead rate of 10%–20% to account for these hard-to-estimate costs.

Methodological issues of bottom-up costing

The costs reported in this study were based on detailed observation in one hospital, but with checks from two others and data from the literature and from manufacturers. The largest cost component was the acquisition cost of equipment, and this will vary among units according to the make and age of the equipment. As reported by the Wessex Innovation Unit,12 a new drying system reduced costs. In our study, the key cost components were identified, which can be adapted for costing purposes in other sites. In addition to reprocessing materials and acquisition and maintenance/repair costs of equipment, we added 10%–20% to cover hospital-level costs such as heating, lighting, building maintenance and administration. As with direct costs, these will vary among hospitals. Similarly, the cost of consumables may vary depending on whether items such as gowns are reused. Gowns can be reused without increasing the risk of infection.13

Strengths and limitations

We used comprehensive methods for our micro-costing analysis. Our information was cross-checked with and validated by two other NHS hospitals, ensuring the robustness and representativeness of our results within the UK context. Additionally, we consulted endoscope manufacturers and the Steelco group to obtain information on endoscope-related costs and emerging technologies, which were incorporated into our analysis. Several limitations should be noted. Some necessary cost data were unavailable, requiring us to use information from the literature. This included the cost of the leak tester, water filtration system, tracking system, and sinks and counters. Moreover, we used ‘list’ prices provided by manufacturers, while actual costs can vary due to negotiated discounts depending on purchase volumes. The absence of a centralised procurement system in NHS England contributes to this variation, leading to inconsistencies in pricing and procurement practices across trusts. Finally, reprocessing costs in our model only represented the standard HLD approach, because it is the strategy reflected by the three NHS Trusts consulted and thus likely similar for most other UK hospitals. Costing on enhanced sterilisation strategies (eg, ‘cultural and quarantine’ strategy and the ethylene oxide sterilisation strategy5 15) was not feasible in our context, limiting the generalisability of our results to settings where these strategies are used.

Conclusion

The total cost of reusing was £107.34 per endoscopy procedure, of which 44% was due to acquisition costs of endoscopes and reprocessing equipment, 22% to labour, 19% to maintenance/repair and 15% to reprocessing materials. The results of sensitivity analyses were generally similar to our base-case analysis, but reducing the annual volume of GI endoscopies to 50% led to an increase in per-procedure cost to £174.7. Our results can be used in economic evaluations involving reusable GI endoscopes, particularly from a UK NHS perspective. In addition, our methods can inform the cost evaluation of medical devices that require intensive reprocessing.
