Anxiety and depression may amplify self-reported physical health problems: evidence from EQ-5D-5L data
Authors: Yanming Hong, Zhihao Yang, Nan Luo, Jan Busschbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOur previous study has shown that individuals with poor positive mental well-being (measured by the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale, SWEMWBS) tend to report more problems on the EQ-5D-5L. Another study has shown that the anxiety/depression item of the EQ-5D-5L and all items of SWEMWBS loaded onto the same factor in an exploratory factor analysis, suggesting they measure the same construct. This study aims to analyze the potential impact of anxiety/depression on responses to the four physical health dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L.MethodsAn online survey and face-to-face interviews were conducted to collect data from participants with and without different health conditions. Generalized ordered logistic regression was employed to examine the influence of anxiety/depression on responses to the four physical health dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the effects of anxiety/depression on EQ-VAS and utility values. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to identify the minimum set of variables required to adjust for confounding.Results500 participants completed online surveys, 509 participated in face-to-face interviews; 237 healthy individuals, 430 individuals with physical conditions, 234 with mental conditions, and 108 with both physical and mental conditions. According to graphical rules applied to the DAG, the final minimally sufficient adjustment set comprised age, carers, education level, sex, and health conditions. Generalized ordered logistic regression indicated that individuals with higher levels of anxiety/depression were more likely to report problems in the four physical health dimensions. Multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that anxiety/depression was significantly associated with EQ-5D-5L utility values and EQ-VAS scores, with individuals experiencing high levels of anxiety/depression reporting lower values for both measures.ConclusionMental health conditions, particularly anxiety and depression, may lead to heightened perceptions of problems in physical health dimensions, potentially biasing self-reported health data. Addressing anxiety and depression in clinical and therapeutic settings may not only improve mental health outcomes but also positively influence perceived physical health. Future research is needed to establish causal relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02497-w.

Introduction

The EQ-5D-5L is a widely used generic preference-based instrument for assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL). It comprises five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression [1, 2]. Its brevity and capacity to generate utility scores have made it indispensable for economic evaluations and health policy decision-making [3]. Traditionally, the EQ-5D-5L has been treated as a set of independent dimensions; however, recent evidence suggests that psychological states may be associated with variation in how individuals report problems across physical domains [4].

Emerging studies indicate that mental health may shape the reporting of physical symptoms. In our earlier research, individuals with lower levels of positive mental well-being—as measured by the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (SWEMWBS)—tended to report more problems across EQ-5D-5L dimensions [4]. SWEMWBS captures positive aspects of mental well-being, including psychological functioning, optimism, emotional resilience, and life satisfaction, and is conceptually distinct from negative mental health constructs that focus on symptoms such as anxiety, depression, and psychological distress [5]. This observation is consistent with the broader literature suggesting that psychological distress may influence physical symptom perception through mechanisms such as somatization, attentional bias, or altered pain thresholds [6–8]. Furthermore, exploratory factor analyses have shown that the anxiety/depression item of EQ-5D-5L shares latent structure with broader well-being constructs measured by SWEMWBS [9]. However, these earlier studies were based on cross-instrument comparisons and therefore susceptible to measurement inconsistencies stemming from differing scale designs and framing. Evidence shows that only the EQ-5D-5L anxiety/depression item moderately overlaps with SWEMWBS, while the other physical dimensions load on distinct factors [9]. Psychometric research further demonstrates that well-being scales (e.g., WEMWBS, GHQ-12) form a coherent positive-well-being factor, whereas EQ-5D items—designed to measure functional limitations—exhibit different measurement properties [5]. In addition, SWEMWBS uses positively framed items, whereas EQ-5D-5L uses negatively framed, problem-oriented items, which can systematically affect response patterns [10, 11]. These differences may introduce measurement artefacts in cross-instrument comparisons, making it difficult to interpret associations observed between mental well-being and physical health across different scales. To address this limitation, the present study examines these relationships entirely within the EQ-5D-5L by focusing on its internal dimensions, allowing a more direct and methodologically robust assessment of cross-dimensional associations while avoiding potential artefacts inherent in cross-instrument comparisons.

Despite EQ-5D-5L’s widespread application, no study has explored whether self-reported anxiety or depression is associated with the way individuals respond to the physical dimensions of the same instrument. Existing studies have largely examined associations with overall utility scores or selected dimensions [12–14], leaving the internal cross-dimensional relationships of the EQ-5D-5L insufficiently explored. Most analyses assume independence across dimensions or focus on aggregate indices. Yet if psychological distress does coincide with higher reporting of physical problems, it may be informative to consider such patterns in interpretation—particularly in populations with complex health profiles. At the same time, such observations do not undermine the instrument’s established validity; rather, they may enrich our understanding of the factors that contribute to response variation. Importantly, this kind of analysis has not been done before. Conducting it may help to better interpret EQ-5D-5L data, for example, by showing that individuals with mental health problems are more likely to report problems with physically related dimensions. This also provides insight into the connection between mental and physical health, adding a novel perspective to EQ-5D-5L research. To investigate this, we employ a within-instrument framework to examine whether responses to the anxiety/depression dimension of EQ-5D-5L are associated with physical domain reporting. This approach avoids cross-instrument inconsistencies and allows for a more focused examination of internal response patterns. We hypothesize that individuals endorsing problems with anxiety or depression may have a greater likelihood of reporting physical problems, independent of demographic and clinical characteristics.

This study aims to explore these potential associations and contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how reporting of mental health problems may coincide with the responses of the other four physical dimensions in EQ-5D-5L. While EQ-5D-5L remains a robust and validated tool for HRQoL assessment [15–17], insights into its internal response patterns may inform future refinements in interpretation and application, especially in contexts involving coexisting physical and mental health concerns.

Methods

Study design and participants

This research uses data collected from the online and face-to-face surveys conducted as part of the E-QALY project in China [18]. The E-QALY project aims to develop a new quality-of-life measure relevant to various sectors, including health, social care, and public health [19, 20]. While prior publications have utilized this dataset for distinct analytical objectives [4, 9, 15, 19], the current study applies it to address novel research questions, underscoring the dataset’s adaptability for diverse scientific inquiries.

The online survey was conducted between April and July 2019, with a sample size of 500 participants. These individuals were recruited through Accent, a UK-based online survey company. Participants included individuals with various health conditions (such as asthma, arthritis, heart conditions, stroke, emphysema, thyroid disorder, bronchitis, liver disease, cancer, diabetes, epilepsy, hypertension, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Chronic Hepatitis B (CHB), HIV/AIDS, disabilities, depression, and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)), as well as healthy individuals, all aged 18 or older. They were first given a summary of the study’s purpose and then asked to disclose their current health status. Individuals who met the eligibility criteria were also asked to supply demographic information, including their age, sex, education level, and caregiving status. Carers were defined as individuals providing assistance to family members or friends due to illness, disability, or old age. After completing this initial section, participants proceeded to the main part of the survey, which included a set of E-QALY candidate items, the EQ-5D descriptive systems (both EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L), the EQ-VAS (administered once), and the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (SWEMWBS). The sequence of the EQ-5D-5L and SWEMWBS instruments was randomized, with half of the participants completing the SWEMWBS first and the other half starting with the EQ-5D-5L. All 500 online respondents were included in the analysis: 231 reported one or more physical conditions (such as asthma, arthritis, heart disease, stroke, liver issues, cancer, diabetes, and others), 21 reported having depression or generalized anxiety disorder, 108 had both physical and mental health issues, and 140 identified as healthy. The survey required complete responses, and no data were missing from the final dataset used for analysis.

The face-to-face survey followed the same setting as the online survey, with the key difference being that participants were recruited in person. This version used a fixed sequence of instruments, including the E-QALY candidate items, the SWEMWBS, and the EQ-5D-5L (along with the EQ-VAS). A total of 550 individuals diagnosed with lung cancer, diabetes, depression, or schizophrenia were recruited from several medical institutions in Guizhou Province: the Provincial Hospital of Guizhou, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Yuqing Caring Psychiatric Hospital, and Fenggang Caring Psychiatric Hospital. In addition, healthy individuals were recruited from various counties within the province. The survey was administered on paper by a team of ten trained undergraduate students from Guizhou Medical University between December 2021 and September 2022. Participants were encouraged to complete the questionnaires on their own, and interviewers were instructed not to assist with or explain the questions. Participants were informed that they could skip any questions they found unclear or preferred not to answer. Respondents who reported being unhealthy but did not specify a medical condition (n = 15), as well as those with missing data for EQ-5D-5L (n = 4), SWEMWBS (n = 18), or caregiving status (n = 4), were excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, 509 face-to-face interview participants were included in this study, comprising 101 individuals with depression, 118 with diabetes, 81 with lung cancer, 112 with schizophrenia, and 97 who were considered healthy.

Ethical and procedural compliance

Both cohorts adhered to rigorous ethical standards. The online survey received approvals from the Ethics Committee of the University of Sheffield, United Kingdom (Approval letter number 025524), and the IRB of Jinan University, China (Approval letter number JNUKY-2020-001), while the face-to-face study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval letter number 2021 − 101). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to engagement.

Data integration and categorization

This study utilized the background information, EQ-5D-5L, and EQ-VAS data collected in the psychometric survey in China. Because both surveys relied on self-reported data, they were combined for the purposes of analysis. For analytical clarity, the conditions were grouped into three categories: physical health conditions (such as asthma, arthritis, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, emphysema, thyroid disorders, bronchitis, liver diseases, cancer, diabetes, epilepsy, hypertension, IBS, CHB, HIV/AIDS, physical disabilities, and other related conditions), mental health conditions (including generalized anxiety disorder, depression, and schizophrenia), and a combination of both physical and mental health conditions.

Instruments

The EQ-5D-5L is a preference-based health-related quality of life (HRQoL) instrument developed by the EuroQol Group. The instrument consists of a five-item descriptive system and a visual analog scale (EQ-VAS) [2, 21]. The descriptive system covers five health dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, with each dimension having five response levels (1 = no problems, 2 = slight problems, 3 = moderate problems, 4 = severe problems, and 5 = unable/extreme problems). This instrument allows for the calculation of health utility values, which are used to assess the desirability of a particular health state. The EQ-5D-5L has been validated in various health conditions in China and is widely used in health economics and clinical research [16, 22–26]. For this study, the health utility values derived from the EQ-5D-5L were calculated using the value set specific to China [21]. The EQ-VAS is a visual analog scale that captures participants’ self-assessed health, with scores ranging from 0 (the worst possible health) to 100 (the best possible health) [27].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were first used to characterize the study sample, including demographics, caregiving status, health conditions, and anxiety/depression levels. Due to the low number of respondents reporting levels 4 and 5 on the anxiety/depression dimension, these categories were combined into a single group for statistical analysis in this study. To explore how the distributions of the four physical health dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L (mobility, self-care, usual activities, and pain/discomfort) varied across different anxiety/depression levels, cross-tabulations were conducted. The mean outcomes between the groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H Test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were then calculated to assess monotonic associations between anxiety/depression and each of the four physical dimensions. The results of the correlations were interpreted using the following thresholds: Weak: 0.10–0.29, Moderate: 0.30–0.49, and Strong: ≥0.50 [28, 29].

To account for potential confounders, a DAG was developed using DAGitty (www.dagitty.net), grounded in prior evidence mapping causal pathways among variables relevant to anxiety/depression and physical health outcomes. DAGs provide a visual representation of hypothesized causal relationships [30], with directed arrows indicating influence between nodes (e.g., age → anxiety/depression → mobility limitations). This approach identifies a minimally sufficient adjustment set—variables necessary to block confounding paths without introducing overadjustment or collider bias [20]. According to graphical rules applied to the DAG, the final adjustment set included age, sex, education level, carers, and health conditions.

To further evaluate the association between anxiety/depression and each physical dimension, generalized ordered logistic regression models were applied. These models accommodate the ordinal structure of the dependent variables and allow partial proportional odds when assumptions are violated. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were estimated. Covariates in the adjusted models included age, sex, education level, caregiving status, and type of health condition—selected based on a DAG. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the impact of anxiety/depression on overall health utility and perceived health, as measured by EQ-5D-5L utility scores and EQ-VAS, respectively. Anxiety/depression was treated as an ordinal predictor, and the same DAG-derived covariates were included.

To verify the robustness of the findings, we conducted stratified regression analyses, running the generalized ordered logistic and linear models separately for the online (n = 500) and the face-to-face (n = 509) survey cohorts. The results were presented in the Appendix. All analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 22.0. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study sample included 1,009 participants, with a slightly higher number of females (529, 52.4%) than males (480, 47.6%). The participants were evenly distributed across four age categories, with an average age of 41.5 ± 14.82 years. A significant portion, 544 (53.9%), had at least a college degree or higher education. Regarding health conditions, 430 (42.6%) reported having physical health conditions, 234 (23.2%) had mental health conditions, and 108 (10.7%) experienced both physical and mental health issues. Of the total sample, 317 (31.4%) were carers. The data collection methods were almost equally split between online surveys (500, 49.6%) and face-to-face interviews (509, 50.4%). Table 3 presents the results of generalized ordered logistic regression analyses examining the relationship between anxiety/depression and four physical health dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L, both in crude and adjusted models. In the crude analysis, higher levels of anxiety/depression were significantly associated with increased odds of reporting problems in all four dimensions. For instance, individuals with Level 4/5 anxiety/depression had markedly higher odds of reporting issues in mobility (OR = 9.32), self-care (OR = 6.12), usual activities (OR = 17.95), and pain/discomfort (OR = 40.18), all p < 0.01. After adjusting for age, sex, education level, caregiving status, and health condition, the associations remained statistically significant. Participants with Level 4/5 anxiety/depression were significantly more likely to experience limitations in mobility (OR = 16.82), self-care (OR = 10.92), usual activities (OR = 23.00), and pain/discomfort (OR = 40.38). Several covariates, including age, sex, education, health status, and caregiving experience, also showed significant associations with one or more physical dimensions. Older age was significantly associated with increased odds of mobility (p = 0.01) and self-care problems (p < 0.01). Male was linked to lower odds of problems in mobility, usual activities, and pain/discomfort (p < 0.05). Lower education levels (middle/high school) were significantly associated with reduced odds of problems in mobility, self-care, and usual activities (p < 0.01). Physical and combined physical/mental health conditions were strongly associated with greater odds of problems across all four dimensions (p < 0.001). Mental health conditions alone were significantly related to limitations in usual activities (p = 0.04) and pain/discomfort (p = 0.02). Finally, non-caregivers had significantly higher odds of reporting problems in mobility, self-care, and usual activities (p < 0.05). When analyzing the data from online surveys and face-to-face interviews separately, the results remain consistent with those presented in Table 3, as shown in Appendix Table 1.

Table 4 presents the findings of multiple linear regression analyses evaluating the association between anxiety/depression levels and two outcomes: EQ-5D-5L utility scores and EQ-VAS scores. In both the crude and adjusted models, higher levels of anxiety/depression were significantly associated with lower health utility and self-rated health. In the crude analysis, compared to those with no or minimal anxiety/depression (Level 1), participants at Level 2, Level 3, and Levels 4/5 had progressively lower EQ-5D-5L utility scores (β = -0.121, -0.265, and − 0.458, respectively; all p < 0.001) and EQ-VAS scores (β = -6.243, -18.825, and − 38.470; all p < 0.001), indicating a strong negative gradient. In the adjusted analysis, which accounted for age, sex, education, caregiving status, and health conditions, these associations remained statistically significant and followed the same dose-response trend. Specifically, compared to Level 1, the coefficients for EQ-5D-5L utility scores were β = -0.102 (Level 2), β = -0.253 (Level 3), and β = -0.460 (Level 4/5), all p < 0.001. For EQ-VAS scores, the corresponding coefficients were β = -6.810, -18.060, and − 36.474 (all p < 0.001), suggesting that increasing anxiety/depression severity is associated with substantial declines in perceived health and utility values. Additionally, several covariates were significantly associated with outcomes. For instance, individuals with physical conditions (utility: β = -0.064, p < 0.001; EQ-VAS: β = -4.302, p = 0.003), mental conditions (EQ-VAS: β = -7.930, p < 0.001), and both conditions (utility: β = -0.066, EQ-VAS: β = -6.035; both p < 0.01) reported significantly worse outcomes. Lower educational attainment was also linked with lower EQ-VAS ratings. Caregiver status was significantly associated with EQ-VAS scores, with non-caregivers reporting higher scores (β = 2.977, p = 0.014). Overall, these results highlight a strong negative impact of anxiety/depression on both perceived health status and health-related quality of life. The results of the multiple linear regression analyses in Appendix Table 2, which stratify by survey mode, are largely consistent with those in Table 4.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that anxiety/depression, as captured by the EQ-5D-5L’s internal item, is strongly associated with individuals’ reporting of physical health dimensions. Through a quantitative analysis examining the impact of anxiety/depression on the four physical health dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L, utility values, and EQ-VAS scores, we found that higher levels of anxiety/depression were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of reporting physical health problems. The use of a DAG to pre-specify confounders based on theoretical and empirical evidence further strengthens the internal validity of our findings. DAGs offer a transparent and reproducible approach to covariate selection that avoids overadjustment and collider bias, which are common pitfalls in observational studies [31]. The robustness of our results across both the online and face-to-face samples, despite different modes of administration, further underscores the stability of the anxiety/depression effect. This finding suggests that psychological distress may independently shape how individuals perceive and report on their physical health. Importantly, by focusing on cross-dimensional associations entirely within the EQ-5D-5L, this study offers a novel perspective on how mental health may shape the reporting of physical problems. To our knowledge, a within-instrument analysis of this kind has not been conducted previously. Such an approach can improve the interpretation of EQ-5D-5L data by uncovering systematic patterns in which individuals with anxiety or depression are more likely to report difficulties across physical dimensions. These findings also highlight the interconnection between mental and physical health, addressing an underexplored aspect of HRQoL assessment. Furthermore, the primary contribution of this study lies not in establishing causal relationships or estimating prevalence, but in elucidating cross-dimensional response patterns within the EQ-5D-5L itself. By minimizing measurement artefacts inherent in cross-instrument comparisons, this within-instrument approach provides a clearer framework for interpreting how anxiety/depression responses relate to physical health reporting.

These findings extend prior research that has demonstrated associations between mental well-being and EQ-5D-5L responses using external instruments such as the SWEMWBS [4, 9]. However, unlike previous studies that compared across instruments—often differing in response formats and framing—this study adopted a within-instrument analytical framework, thereby minimizing measurement bias and enhancing interpretive clarity. By focusing on the internal relationships among EQ-5D-5L items, we reveal that the instrument’s anxiety/depression dimension may systematically influence responses to ostensibly physical dimensions, challenging the assumption of dimensional independence commonly applied in HRQoL modeling and utility estimation.

The strongest association was observed between anxiety/depression and pain/discomfort, a finding consistent with previous evidence that psychological distress can amplify pain perception through neurobiological and cognitive mechanisms such as central sensitization, somatic hypervigilance, and negative affective bias [7, 8, 32, 33]. Furthermore, individuals experiencing psychological distress often exhibit negative appraisal biases, which may lead them to interpret minor physical discomforts as more severe, contributing to exaggerated problem reporting even in the absence of clinical pathology [34]. Moderate associations were also found with mobility and usual activities, which may reflect how emotional distress contributes to fatigue, reduced motivation, or fear-avoidance behaviors, thereby constraining physical functioning. Although the association with self-care was relatively weaker, it remained statistically significant, suggesting that even basic functional tasks can be affected by mental health status. In addition, anxiety/depression also has a substantial and graded influence on both utility scores and EQ-VAS ratings. Higher levels of anxiety/depression were also significantly associated with lower EQ-5D utility values and self-rated health. This effect followed a clear dose-response pattern, wherein individuals with severe anxiety or depression (Levels 4/5) reported notably reduced utility scores and VAS scores compared to those with no or minimal symptoms. Importantly, the magnitude of the observed reduction in utility exceeded what would be expected from the anxiety/depression dimension alone based on the EQ-5D-5L value set. This suggests that anxiety/depression may be associated with broader response patterns across multiple dimensions, leading to additional reductions in overall utility beyond its direct contribution. These results suggest that psychological distress not only skews responses to specific health dimensions but also systematically depresses global health evaluations—both in preference-based utility estimation and subjective health perception.

These findings carry important methodological and clinical implications for health assessment and policy. Methodologically, the results challenge the assumption of independence among EQ-5D-5L dimensions, suggesting that reporting problems in anxiety/depression may systematically influence responses to other physical domains and depress overall utility values. The results highlight response heterogeneity between individuals with and without mental health problems. Specifically, individuals experiencing anxiety or depression tend to report more problems in the physical dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L, even after adjusting for objective health conditions. This suggests that mental health status can systematically influence how respondents interpret and rate physical health domains, raising concerns about measurement validity and comparability across subgroups. Such heterogeneity may affect the interpretation of health status data, particularly in research and policy settings that rely on self-reported outcomes. Clinically, the pervasive influence of psychological distress on physical symptom reporting highlights the importance of integrated care. Treating anxiety and depression may not only improve emotional well-being but also reduce perceived physical limitations and enhance overall quality of life. At the same time, clinicians should interpret physical complaints with caution in the presence of psychological distress, as self-reports may reflect emotional amplification rather than objective impairment. Collectively, these findings reinforce the view that mental health is a fundamental determinant of both perceived and preference-based health, with far-reaching implications for accurate diagnosis, prioritization of interventions, and equitable health policy [35].

The current study also confirmed several previously documented sociodemographic patterns in HRQoL reporting. Individuals with physical health conditions alone consistently reported more problems across all four physical dimensions of the EQ-5D-5L, lower utility scores, and diminished EQ-VAS ratings compared to healthy individuals. This is consistent with the known burden of chronic diseases—such as cardiovascular conditions, diabetes, or respiratory illnesses—on physical functioning and overall quality of life [36, 37]. By contrast, mental health conditions alone showed weaker and, in some cases, non-significant associations with the physical dimensions (Table 3). This pattern is expected, as the EQ-5D-5L physical domains primarily reflect functional and somatic limitations that are more directly affected by physical disease, while mental health conditions may influence physical functioning in a more indirect and heterogeneous manner. Notably, individuals with combined physical and mental conditions showed stronger associations across physical domains, suggesting a greater overall functional burden in this subgroup. Older individuals were more likely to report problems with mobility and self-care, consistent with age-related declines in physical functioning [38, 39]. Male was associated with fewer reported physical limitations, possibly reflecting sex differences in health perception. Interestingly, carers were less likely to report problems in several dimensions, a finding that may reflect resilience, role adaptation, or selective participation bias in this subgroup [40, 41]. These findings provide additional context for interpreting the main associations and highlight the multifactorial nature of self-reported health outcomes.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference. Although this study was theoretically motivated by the hypothesis that psychological distress may influence the reporting of physical health problems, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits our ability to formally establish causal directionality. While our analyses adjusted for multiple confounders using a DAG-based approach, it remains possible that reverse causality—whereby physical health limitations exacerbate anxiety and depression—or that bidirectional mechanisms operate simultaneously. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted primarily at the level of cross-dimensional response patterns within a self-reported health instrument, rather than as conclusive evidence of direct etiological effects. This clarification of interpretive scope helps avoid overextension of causal claims in the absence of longitudinal or experimental data. Second, all data were self-reported, which may introduce shared method variance and social desirability bias. Third, while we attempted to adjust for all known confounders using DAG-based criteria, unmeasured variables such as personality traits (e.g., neuroticism), chronic stress exposure, or coping strategies may still play a role in shaping both mental and physical self-assessments [42, 43]. Fourth, while EQ-5D-5L is widely validated, its single-item per domain structure may not capture the full complexity of health experiences compared to multidimensional tools. Fifth, the relatively small proportion of participants reporting severe levels of anxiety/depression (Levels 4/5) may limit the statistical precision for this subgroup and affect the generalizability of the estimates at the highest severity levels, although the consistent and graded associations observed across severity levels suggest that the overall pattern is robust. Finally, there may be a cultural component influencing the observed associations. As the present findings are based on a specific population, it remains unclear whether similar effects would be observed in other cultural or demographic contexts. Future research should therefore aim to replicate these analyses in diverse populations to evaluate generalizability. Future research should therefore aim to replicate these analyses in more diverse populations to assess the generalizability of the observed cross-dimensional response patterns. Studies with larger sample sizes would be particularly valuable for improving precision among subgroups with severe anxiety/depression and for enhancing statistical power to detect more nuanced associations across EQ-5D-5L dimensions. In addition, longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs may help to further examine the temporal ordering and potential bidirectionality between psychological distress and physical health reporting, thereby providing a more rigorous test of the causal hypotheses that motivate the present analysis. Beyond causal inference, future work using advanced analytical approaches—such as structural equation modeling or item response theory—could offer deeper insight into how mental and physical health dimensions interact within the EQ-5D-5L measurement framework itself, and help clarify whether the observed associations reflect underlying latent constructs, reporting tendencies, or both.

Conclusion

This study shows that anxiety and depression, as measured within the EQ-5D-5L instrument, are systematically associated with the reporting of the other four health dimensions, with clear dose-response relationships across all four physical dimensions. Importantly, these results do not call into question the validity of the EQ-5D-5L. Rather, they highlight how mental health status may shape self-perceptions of physical health. The primary contribution of this work lies in enhancing the understanding of internal cross-dimensional relationships within the EQ-5D-5L, providing a clearer interpretive framework for how mental health may shape self-reported physical health. Understanding such interactions can enrich the interpretation of EQ-5D-5L data and may support more nuanced application of the instrument in different settings. Overall, this study contributes to ongoing efforts to understand how psychological and physical health intersect in patient-reported outcomes. Further research is warranted to explore these dynamics longitudinally and assess their implications for integrated care strategies in populations with coexisting mental and physical health concerns.
