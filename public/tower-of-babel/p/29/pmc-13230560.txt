Influence of gradient wave velocity structure of 3–D Gaussian–shaped rocky hills on seismic wave propagation
Authors: Qingquan Fan, Xiaojun Li, Suyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe seismic response of terrain sites has long been a hot topic in the field of geophysics and earthquake engineering over decades, which plays a critical role in evaluating the seismic safety of major engineering in mountain areas. In current practice, rocky mountain structures are often treated as a homogeneous body for the simulation of seismic wave propagation, with only the topographic effects taken into account. However, the in–situ stress field can introduce spatial heterogeneity in wave velocity in the hilly body, which may have a significant influence on the seismic wave propagation. In this study, a three–dimensional (3–D) finite element model of a homogeneous Gaussian–shaped hill was first constructed and validated against existing simulations using the boundary element method. Then, an empirical model for the hilly body with shear wave velocity varying with depth in gradients is established based on the results of laboratory tests from the literature. Finally, with the validated model and the empirical relationship, the seismic wave propagation of the 3–D Gaussian–shaped rocky hilly body with gradient shear wave velocity was investigated numerically via the finite element method. The results show that: (1) The gradient shear wave velocity structure of hilly body can significantly influence the seismic wave propagation and alter the spatial distribution of seismic motion near the hilly surface; (2) As the gradient of shear wave velocity structure intensifies, the amplification of surface ground motion on hill generally increases, accompanied by a shift of the transfer function toward lower frequencies; (3) For the complex site effects of rocky hilly body, there exists a complicated interaction effect between the topography and the gradient shear wave velocity structure.Keywords: Complex seismic effect, Finite element method, Artificial boundary condition, Gradient wave velocity, Terrain site effectSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

It is well known that terrain sites can significantly amplify ground motions due to the interaction of seismic waves with the topography, which can trap and focus energy, leading to increased shaking intensity at the crests of hills and ridges1. For instance, during the 2008 Mw7.9 Wenchuan earthquake, the horizontal peak ground acceleration (PGA) recorded at the hilltop of Xishan Park in Zigong City was up to 1.6 times that measured at the hill foot2–4. Meanwhile, on a hill near Wen County in Gansu Province, the average amplification ratio of horizontal PGA between the crest and the hill foot reached about 1.77 for seven aftershocks5. Additionally, during the 2013 Ms7.0 Lushan earthquake, the PGA value recorded by the 51BXD station located on a hill exceeded 1 g, which is the highest PGA value among all recordings of this earthquake6. Therefore, investigating the seismic response of hills is of great significance to the evaluation of seismic safety for major engineering in mountain areas, such as mountain tunnels, canyon bridges, and reservoir dams7,8.

Since the 1970 s, numerous researchers have investigated the topographic effects of small–scale hills using various numerical methods. In early studies, Boore9, Bard10, and Geli et al.11 employed the finite difference method to investigate the seismic response of two–dimensional (2–D) homogeneous hills under different types of seismic inputs. Subsequently, Sánchez–Sesma and Campillo12,13, as well as Pedersen et al.14, introduced the frequency–domain boundary element method (BEM) to study the 2–D topographic effects, which improved both numerical accuracy and computational efficiency. Meanwhile, Sánchez–Sesma15 and Luzón et al.16 extended the application of the BEM to analyze the seismic response of 3–D homogeneous hills, and this method has been widely adopted and further developed17–20. At present, the BEM has become the most common approach for analyzing topographic effects of hilly bodies. It does not require meshing within the computational region, and inherently satisfies the radiation condition in a semi–infinite space, thereby offering advantages in both modeling simplicity and numerical accuracy. However, it is generally limited to homogeneous or horizontally layered half–space models. When dealing with complex heterogeneous sites, it requires the introduction of extensive volume integrals or additional boundaries, which can substantially compromise computational accuracy, efficiency, and stability21,22.

Extensive borehole data have revealed a pronounced depth dependence of shear wave velocity of the lithosphere within a depth range of 1–5 km23–28. Specifically, with increasing depth, the shear wave velocity of rock generally follows an exponential relationship characterized by a rapid initial increase followed by a gradual leveling–off27. Furthermore, existing literature has demonstrated that rock sites may have a non–negligible amplification on seismic waves due to gradient wave velocity structure. However, rocky hills are often treated as homogeneous bodies in current practice, with only topographic effects induced by surface irregularities considered29. This simplification neglects the wave scattering and refraction within the rocky hilly body caused by gradient subsurface wave velocity structure, which may alter the seismic wave propagation and lead to an underestimation of the seismic response in some parts of the hilly body. Therefore, it is essential to account for both effects of the topography and gradient shear wave velocity structure of hilly body in seismic wave propagation and ground motion analysis of terrain sites.

Compared with the BEM, the finite element method (FEM) offers higher computational efficiency and stability when dealing with heterogeneous media, and can accurately capture the seismic response characteristics of actual sites30,31. However, when employing the FEM to simulate local seismic site response, artificial boundaries must be introduced to convert the problem of wave propagation in a semi–infinite domain into a dynamic analysis problem in a finite domain. Currently, the earliest used artificial boundary condition is the far–field boundary condition, which places the truncated boundaries sufficiently far from the region of interest to minimize the effects of wave reflections. However, in 3–D simulations, the use of the far–field boundary substantially enlarges the model size, greatly raising the number of elements and demands for computational resources, and leading to low computational efficiency. Therefore, it is essential to apply artificial boundary conditions at the truncated boundaries near the region of interest to ensure that outgoing waves are not reflected by the artificial boundaries.

Generally, artificial boundary conditions can be classified into displacement–type boundaries (e.g., transmitting boundaries32 and stress–type boundaries (e.g., viscous or viscoelastic boundaries33. In recent years, stress–type artificial boundaries have been widely adopted due to their high accuracy and ease of implementation in general–purpose finite element analysis software. For the problems of wave propagation driven by external sources, such as seismic site response analysis, when using stress–type boundaries, equivalent loads need to be applied on the truncated boundaries to properly account for the influence of the exterior region on the local site. It should be noted that when truncated boundaries are located near the region of interest, the effectiveness of the artificial boundaries and the precision of the equivalent loads are critical to the reliability of numerical simulation results.

In this study, a 3–D finite element model of a homogeneous Gaussian–shaped hill was first constructed, using viscoelastic artificial boundaries and the seismic wave input method proposed by Liu and Lv34. The validity of this model was then confirmed through comparisons with one–dimensional (1–D) analytical solutions and simulations using the BEM. Subsequently, an empirical model of rock sites for the shear wave velocity varying with depth in gradients was established based on the results of laboratory tests from the literature, and was employed to construct the shear wave velocity structure of 3–D Gaussian–shaped rocky hills. Finally, a vertically propagating Ricker wavelet was selected as the incident wave to numerically investigate the seismic wave propagation and spatial distribution of ground motion of the hilly body with gradient shear wave velocity using the FEM. Our goal is to model the seismic wave propagation in the rocky hilly body considering the gradient wave velocity structure, and to investigate the influence of shear wave velocity gradient on the seismic wave propagation and spatial distribution of ground motions on and within the hilly body.

Methodology and validation

The seismic wave propagation and seismic response analysis of large–scale 3–D heterogeneous sites has gained widespread attention in the field of earthquake engineering. Although the FEM can handle geometric irregularities and medium heterogeneity well in the modelling of 3–D seismic site response, the artificial boundary conditions and seismic wave input methods remain to be the key technologies for the wave propagation simulation. That is to say, the suppression of outgoing wave reflections and the accurate input of seismic waves at the truncated boundaries are essential prerequisites for obtaining reliable seismic responses of 3–D complex site models. Therefore, before performing the simulations of the 3–D seismic responses of rocky hills with the gradient shear wave velocity structure, the reliability of the employed artificial boundary and the seismic wave input method should be validated first.

Modelling of 3–D Gaussian–shaped hills with the gradient shear wave velocity structure

Existing studies have demonstrated that the shear wave velocity structure of the lithosphere exhibits significant depth dependence, influenced by factors such as temperature, pressure, rock joints, and mineral composition39. Among these factors, pressure and temperature play a pivotal role in affecting the shear wave velocity of rocks. When the pressure increases, the closure of pores in the rock leads to tighter contact between the rock particles, thereby increasing the shear wave velocity. In contrast, rising temperatures cause thermal expansion to reduce the effective contact area between particles, resulting in a decrease in shear wave velocity; however, temperature has only a minor effect when it does not exceed 100 °C39. In this study, we only considered the influence of the geostress on the shear wave velocity of rock, and established an empirical model between shear wave velocity and depth of rock according to the relationship between shear wave velocity and pressure.

Notably, the spatial distributions of both high–amplification and significant–reduction zones are closely controlled by wave scattering and refraction effects induced by topographic features and shear wave velocity gradients, especially the topographic effects, as well recognized and discussed in recent studies54–56. Specifically, wave scattering induced by both the topography and the gradient velocity structure jointly contributes to wave focusing. It has been recognized that wave scattering caused by topographic effects leads to the focusing of seismic waves toward the summit. Moreover, the significant–reduction phenomena observed in Fig. 6a indicate that such wave scattering and the wave refraction induced by the gradient velocity structures contributes to wave defocusing.

Additionally, the vibration direction of the input seismic wave plays an important role in shaping the distributions to PGA of surface ground motions, further modulating the locations and extents of ground-motion amplification and attenuation. As shown in Fig. 6c, as α increases, the z–direction PGA increases significantly in the area near the y axis in the upper part of the hill, indicating that the vibration direction of seismic waves changes significantly after reflection at the ground surface, and the affected area gradually expands. This also indicates that the gradient of shear wave velocity has a significant influence on the propagation process of seismic waves within the hilly body.

Furthermore, as can also be observed from Fig. 6a, when α = 0.15, the ground motion within the annular region outside the hill foot is still affected by the hill. Within this region, the maximum value of PGA/PGAoutcrop in the x–direction reaches approximately 1.4. In other words, the surface ground motion exhibits significant differences from the 1–D GRA results in this case, making it inappropriate to set truncated boundaries in this region. Thus, we recommend to set truncated boundaries at a distance of at least 0.5 times the hill radius outside the hill foot.

Complex site effects on the surface of the hills

Existing studies have demonstrated that the differences between surface ground motion and incident seismic waves mainly originate from free surface effects, topographic site effects, and the stratigraphic effects57,58. Among these, the free surface effects refer to the superposition of incident waves with reflected waves after the former are reflected at the free surface, thereby causing the amplification of surface ground motion. The topographic effects refer to the amplification or reduction of ground motions caused by wave scattering induced by surface topographic irregularities59,60. The stratigraphic effects refer to the amplification or reduction of ground motions caused by anelastic attenuation of the medium and changes in wave impedance61–64. It should be noted that there may be a complicated interaction between the topographic effects and the stratigraphic effects1,65–67, which is the origin of the term “complex” in “complex site effect”.

In this study, the stratigraphic effects are originated from the gradient shear wave velocity of the rocky hill. From Fig. 10, it is evident that as the gradient of shear wave velocity increases, the transfer function exhibits a gradual shift toward lower frequencies. Meanwhile, as the surface observation points move from the toeslope to the summit, the magnitude of the shift of the transfer function becomes more pronounced with increasing elevation. It is inferred that this may be related to the reduction in the near–surface shear wave velocity. To be specific, although the average shear wave velocity from the ground surface to the model bottom remains constant across different values of α, the near–surface shear wave velocity diminishes as α increases. Consequently, given that the site period decreases with increasing shear wave velocity (e.g., for a 1–D homogeneous site), the transfer function shifts toward lower frequencies as the near–surface shear wave velocity reduction.

Figure 12 reveals that the shape and amplitude of the coupling effects closely align with those of the SR curves at different observation points, sharing nearly identical spatial distribution characteristics and frequency variation trends. This agreement occurs because the impedance contrast (IC) keeps approaching 1 along the depth in this study, resulting from the shear wave velocity gradient. Thus, the amplification induced by the shear wave velocity gradient in rock sites is relatively weak, with values ranging between 1.0 and 1.3 across the frequency range of interest for case of α = 0.05, 0.10, and 0.15. Among these, the relative higher amplification is attributed to the IC of the near surface not being as close to 1 as that of the deeper media in the hills, owing to the rapid reduction of the near–surface shear wave velocity. These findings further confirm that the coupling effects play a dominant role in the influence of the gradient shear wave velocity on the surface ground motion of the hills.

In addition, the coupling effects are most pronounced at the midslope regions of the hills, followed by the hilltop, and weakest at the toeslope. Specifically, the peak value of the coupling effects at observation points 4–10 ranges from 4.9 to 15.0; at points 1–3, it ranges from 2.3 to 5.7; and at points 11 and 15–17, it varies between 2.1 and 3.1. These findings imply that neglecting these coupling effects in frequency–domain analyses may introduce significant bias, especially at the hilltop and midslope regions. Therefore, it is essential to account for the complicated interaction between the effects of the gradient wave velocity and topography in the seismic wave propagation and seismic response analysis of rock hills.

Conclusions

In this study, the seismic wave propagation in 3–D Gaussian–shaped rocky hills with the gradient shear wave velocity is modeled using the FEM. Then, the influence of the gradient shear wave velocity on the seismic wave propagation and seismic response of the rocky hills is investigated, and the complex site effects of the rocky hills, the coupling effects between topography and gradient shear wave velocity, are revealed and discussed. The main conclusions are listed as follows.

The gradient of wave velocity in terrain sites can significantly alter the spatial distribution of seismic response near the hill surface. In this study, both the high–amplification and the significant–reduction zones on the hill surface gradually converge toward the summit as this gradient increases. Meanwhile, the amplification of ground motion at the hilltop becomes more pronounced, which means the gradient wave velocity intensify the focusing effect of seismic waves at the hilltop.

The amplification effect of rocky hills on surface ground motion generally increases with the enhancement of the shear wave velocity gradient. When α ranges from 0.05 to 0.15, the PGA of hills with the gradient is 1.2 to 2.1 times that of hills without the gradient at the hilltop and midslope regions. Meanwhile, comparing with those for rocky hill without the gradient wave velocity, the transfer functions of surface ground motion for the hills with the gradient shear wave velocity generally shift toward lower frequencies.

The seismic responses of hills are closely related to the vibration direction of incident shear waves. In the direction perpendicular to the vibration direction, the ground motion exhibits high amplification on both sides of the hilltop, whereas in the direction parallel to this vibration direction, significant–reduction zones occur on both sides of the hilltop.

For the wave propagation simulation in rocky hills, there exists a complicated interaction between the topographic site effects and the effects of the gradient shear wave velocity. That is to say, the complex site effects of the rocky hills with obvious gradient shear wave velocity cannot be simply expressed as the product of the free surface effects, the topographic effects, and the effects of gradient shear wave velocity in the frequency domain.

This study reveals that the influence of the gradient shear wave velocity on the seismic response of the hills is very complex. Specifically, the amplification and reduction effects are different in different parts of the hilly body. However, the ground motions in the foot and upper part of the hilly body are significantly amplified, which means that the influence of hill on ground motions might be significantly underestimated by the homogeneous wave velocity model of hilly body widely used, and it may lead to additional risks in seismic fortification of engineering projects in mountain areas.

It should be noted that our study simulates the seismic response of 3–D Gaussian–shaped rocky hills featuring the gradient wave velocity structure, specifically subjected to the vertically incident S–wave with dimensionless frequency ranging from 0 to 6. Subsequent studies could expand upon this work by investigating the seismic response to incident P–waves, incorporating broader dimensionless frequency ranges that correspond to actual earthquake motions, and evaluating the influence of oblique incidence. Additionally, the rocky hill models in the study are generalized, whereas actual rocky hill sites exhibit complex site conditions. These differences need further investigation in earthquake engineering.
