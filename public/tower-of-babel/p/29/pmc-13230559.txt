Development and assessment of a reactive oxygen species generator independent of external stimuli utilizing metal–organic frameworks
Authors: Omid Feghhe Miri, Ali Ramazani, Ali Morsali, Somayeh Tarasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our research showed that the nanoparticles incorporated within the Metal–Organic Framework (MOF) substrate can facilitate self-driven electron transfer without the need for external stimulation to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). The potential difference between bismuth nanoparticles and the central metal in the MOF is recognized as a key factor in the process of autonomous electron transfer. This study investigates the impact of central metal on electrochemical performance of bismuth nanoparticles and the production of active radical species. In this work, the MOF-303 with aluminum central metal was chosen to reduce the potential difference compared to previous our research focused on zirconium metal. The results indicate that the reduction in potential difference leads to a decrease in impedance and an increase in semiconductor capability. These changes improve the performance of bismuth nanoparticles in electron transfer, resulting in increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under physiological conditions. Concurrently, this system depletes intracellular glutathione (GSH), converting it to oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and thereby disrupts redox homeostasis in tumor microenvironments. The acidic pH further enhances GSH oxidation, demonstrating the potential of Bi@MOF-303 as a pH-responsive, self-driven ROS amplifier. Live/dead cell staining assay validated the findings, revealing that Bi@MOF-303 had the highest percentage of cell death. This was due to significant oxidative stress caused by its self-driven electron transfer and depletion of GSH. Our innovative protocol, which emphasizes the accumulation of ROS in cancer cells through self-driven electron transfer, highlights the importance of central metal selection and its impact on electrical behavior and active species production for the first time.

Introduction

The ability of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-based cancer therapies to precisely target tumor cells has attracted a lot of attention recently1. Superoxide anion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{O}}_{2}^{ - }$$\end{document}), singlet oxygen (1O2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are examples of ROS, which are tiny molecules produced when oxygen is partially reduced2. Cellular malfunction and damage are closely linked to the buildup of ROS3,4. Because tumor cells are especially susceptible to harm from excessive ROS, these findings have prompted the creation of cancer therapy techniques that seek to increase intracellular ROS levels or disrupt the cellular redox equilibrium5,6. Several nanoplatforms have been developed in recent years to improve intracellular ROS generation using techniques including chemodynamic therapy (CDT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and sonodynamic therapy (SDT)7. CDT primarily utilizes the fenton reaction, involving endogenous H2O2 and metallic ions like iron or copper, to generate ROS without the need for external energy input8. PDT and SDT require external stimuli, such as light and ultrasound waves, to activate photosensitizers or sonosensitizers, leading to ROS production9. However, these approaches still face several significant limitations. One major challenge is their dependence on external energy sources to activate the therapeutic process. In addition, tumor cells possess strong antioxidant defense systems particularly the elevated levels of GSH which can neutralize therapeutic effects and reduce treatment efficiency. Furthermore, these strategies encounter practical constraints, including limited tissue penetration of the applied energy (especially the poor penetration depth of light in photodynamic therapy, PDT), which restricts their effectiveness against deep-seated tumors. Also, their implementation often requires sophisticated, complex, and costly equipment such as lasers or radiation sources, which may hinder their widespread clinical application10. GSH plays a critical role as an antioxidant, scavenging excessive ROS and thus limiting the therapeutic efficacy of ROS-based strategies11. To overcome these challenges, researchers are focused on developing therapeutic platforms that can autonomously generate ROS and simultaneously deplete intracellular GSH to enhance anticancer efficacy. One promising approach involves using MOFs, which have garnered attention for their structural tunability, high surface area, and biodegradability in biomedical applications12,13. Among these, aluminum-based MOFs, such as MOF-303, are particularly noteworthy due to their excellent stability and biocompatibility, making them suitable for therapeutic use14,15. Bismuth (Bi) has emerged as a potential candidate for ROS generation due to its relatively low toxicity compared to other heavy metals, making it attractive for medical applications16. However, bismuth nanoparticles (Bi NPs) are prone to aggregation and oxidation under physiological conditions, which significantly limits their practical application17. Embedding Bi NPs within MOF structures offers a promising strategy to overcome these obstacles by improving nanoparticle stability and boosting their therapeutic efficacy18. According to earlier research, nanoparticles incorporated into MOF substrates can promote self-driven electron transport without the need for outside stimulation, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS)19. The mechanism of autonomous electron transfer is known to be significantly influenced by the potential difference between the core metal in the MOF and bismuth nanoparticles. This study aims to explore how central metal influences the electrochemical performance of bismuth nanoparticles. For this purpose, we selected MOF-303, which features aluminum as the central metal, to achieve a lower potential difference compared to earlier studies that concentrated on zirconium metal. The findings demonstrate that a reduced potential difference correlates with decreased impedance and enhanced semiconductor properties. These changes help bismuth nanoparticles move electrons more efficiently, which eventually increases the amount of ROS produced in physiological settings. This approach will provide a more effective and targeted treatment for cancer cells, overcoming the limitations of external stimuli and antioxidant defenses19.

Materials and methods

All starting materials, including and aluminum chloride hexahydrate, pyrazole-3,5-dicarboxylic, Bismuth Nitrate were purchased from Aldrich and Merck Company and used as received. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) and 3-(4,5-dimethylimidazole-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, C18H16BrN5S, 97.5%) were purchased Sigma-Aldrich. 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, Glutathione and DTNB were purchased from Company Sigma-Aldrich, Company Merck and Company Bio Basic, respectively. All solvents (reagent grade) were commercially available from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd and used as received without further purification. FT-IR spectra were recorded using Thermo Nicolet IR 100 FT-IR. Xray powder diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Philips X” pert diffractometer with mono chromated Cu-Kα radiation. The samples were also characterized by field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) SIGMA ZEISS and TESCAN MIRA with gold coating. The UV–vis absorbance spectra were detected using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Lambda Bio40, Perkin–Elmer). The hydrodynamic size was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a PSS Z3000 instrument. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area measurement was conducted using a Belsorp mini II device from Microtrac Bel Corp. Additionally, atomic absorption spectroscopy was performed with the B32-GH 400 model from Mermet, Germany. For structural analysis at the nanoscale, a transmission electron microscope (TEM) em208s operating at 100 kV from Philips, Holland, was employed. Furthermore, the pH measurements were conducted using a Model 780 pH meter from Metrohm, Australia. Human embryonic kidney (HEK-293) cells and MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells were obtained from a certified cell bank [were obtained from the National Cell Bank of Iran]. As both are commercially available established cell lines, ethical approval was not required for this study.

Synthesis MOF-303

MOF-303 was synthesized following a hydrothermal method. Initially, 43 mmol of aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl3·6H2O) and 43 mmol of pyrazole-3,5-dicarboxylic acid (H2PZDC) were dissolved in 500 mL of deionized water under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature. Then, 65 mmol of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), dissolved in a small volume of water, was added dropwise to the reaction mixture with constant stirring to facilitate deprotonation of the ligand. The resulting homogeneous solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 100 °C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, a white precipitate was collected by vacuum filtration, washed three times with deionized water and three times with methanol to remove unreacted species and residual salts. The obtained solid was air-dried at room temperature and subsequently activated by heating at 150 °C for 48 h to remove guest molecules and ensure pore accessibility. The final product was obtained as a dry, white powder and stored in a desiccator for further characterization.

Synthesis Bi@MOF-303

A 20 mg portion of MOF-303 was weighed in a centrifuge tube, dissolved with 5 mL of deionized water, and crushed with an ultrasonic cell disruptor for 5 min, then the solution was transferred to a 50 mL beaker, bismuth nitrate of 4 mg was added, dissolved in 5 mL of glycol and then added to the above solution, and then an additional 12.5 mL of deionized water and 2.5 mL of glycol were added with stirring. The mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature. Then, 1 or 2 mL of the NaBH4 solution (4 mg mL−1) prepared with ice water was added separately under the condition of agitation, respectively. Five minutes later, the solution was centrifuged at 1200 rpm and 5 min, and then it was subjected to several washes with deionized water and ethanol to remove impurities and unreacted materials. The obtained materials were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h.

Ion release

To investigate the release pattern of Al and Bi ions from the material, it was placed in dialysis bags and fully submerged in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) with pH values of 7.4, 6.2 and 5 at 37°C. The material was immersed in a total volume of 40 mL of PBS. At intervals of 4, 8, 10, 24, 48, and 96 h, 4 mL of the ion-containing solution was withdrawn and replaced with fresh PBS. The concentrations of released Al3+ and Bi3+ ions were then measured using AAS.

Assessment of glutathione (GSH) depletion

To evaluate the glutathione depletion ability, 50 mg of MOFs were added to 20 mL of a 3 mM glutathione (GSH) solution prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 5). The mixture was stirred continuously for 24 h at room temperature to allow sufficient interaction between GSH and the MOF materials. After incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged to remove the solid precipitates, and the remaining supernatant solution was collected for subsequent analysis.

GSH sensing by DTNB

5,5′ Dithiobis (2 nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) was used as a colorimetric reagent to determine the consumption of GSH by MOF 303 and Bi@MOF 303. Briefly, 100 mg of MOF 303 or Bi@MOF 303 was added to the GSH solution and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged and the clear supernatant was collected. Then, 2 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of DTNB solution and allowed to react for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured using UV–Vis spectroscopy.

In vitro cytotoxicity

Cell toxicity of MDA-MB-231 cells was identified using the MTT method (n = 3). Cells that adhered and grew for 1 day in a 96-well plate had the DMEM medium discarded. At that time, an aqueous solution of substances with concentrations of 100 and 50 ppm was prepared with DMEM medium, and 200 µL of the mixture was added to each well containing the adhered cells. After simultaneous incubation of the substances and cells for 1 day in a cell incubator at 37 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and an equal volume of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added. After incubation for 4 h, the MTT solution was discarded, and an equal volume of DMSO was added and mixed thoroughly. After standing for 2 h, 100 µL of the purple supernatant were taken and added to another well plate, and the OD values were measured at wavelengths of 490 or 570 nm.

GSH depletion in vitro

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in six-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Then cells were incubated with different samples (PBS, Samples) for 4 h. Subsequently, the medium was removed and cells were washed with PBS for 3 times. 80 μL of Triton-X-100 lysis buffer (0.4%) was added for lysing cells obtained. Afterwards, the lysates were centrifuged with 6000 r/min for 5 min, and 50 μL supernatant was mixed with DTNB (200 μL, 0.5 mM). In the end, the absorbance (405 nm) of the solutions was measured by a micro plate reader.

Intracellular ROS Detection

The intracellular ROS was measured via a fluorescence inverted microscope and DCFH-DA was served as the ROS indicator. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in small dishes and cultured for 24 h. Then, cells were incubated with different samples for 4 h. After washed with PBS, DCFH-DA solution (1 × 10−6 M) was added and incubated with cells for 15 min. then cells were observed analyzed by fluorescence inverted microscope.

Live/dead cell staining assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were plated in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing MOFs for 4 h of co-culture. After 4 h, cells were stained with 5 μL of AO (4 × 10−6 M) and 10 μL of PI (4 × 10−6 M) for 30 min and then observed using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

Cell apoptosis and necrosis assay

The assessment of apoptosis and necrosis in MDA-MB-231 cells was conducted using flow cytometry. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. They were then incubated with MOFs for 4 h. Finally, the cells were harvested and stained with PI and Annexin V-FITC for 15 min, followed by analysis with flow cytometry.

Findings and analysis

Characterization and preparation

MOF-303 [Al(OH)(PZDC)] was synthesized by dissolving pyrazole-3,5-dicarboxylic acid (H2PZDC) and aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl3·6H2O) in H2O, followed by the dropwise insertion of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. To create a white precipitate, the resultant mixture was further heated to 100 °C for 24 h20. Next, in situ stirring and reduction were used to create Bi NPs on the MOF-303 (Fig. 1A). We used a variety of characterization techniques to examine the materials’ composition and morphology. The images captured by field emission scanning electron microscopy confirmed that we successfully synthesized MOF-303. Furthermore, the successful integration of Bi NPs into the MOF-303 framework was shown by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and elemental mapping21 (Fig. 1B, C). To have a better understanding of the phase composition, an X-ray diffraction (XRD) examination was also carried out. The obtained XRD pattern showed that our sample’s diffraction peaks closely matched those for MOF-303 in previous studies. Remarkably, the effective inclusion of bismuth nanoparticles while maintaining the structural integrity of MOF-303 was demonstrated by the appearance of additional peaks at 101, 003, 012, 104, 110, 015, 006, 202, and 02422,23 (Fig. 2A). The material compositions were confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), which revealed that MOF-303 and Bi@MOF-303 both included the elements C, O, Bi, and Al (Fig. 2B). Bi@MOF-303’s Bi 4f. range expanded to show two distinct peaks at 164.2 and 158.2 eV, which corresponded to Bi0 4f5/2 and Bi0 4f7/2, respectively24 (Fig. 2C). This confirmed the presence of Bi NPs. The addition of Bi NPs caused a negative shift in the binding energies of Al, according to an analysis of the Al 2p spectrum. This finding suggested a charge transfer from the Bi NPs to the MOF as it showed that adding Bi NPs to MOF-303 increased the electron density of the Al clusters (Fig. 2D). FT-IR was used to investigate the structural characterization of MOF-303 in the 400–4000 cm−1 range. Peaks at 1001 cm−1, 1478 cm−1, and 1529 cm−1 were identified as the vibrations of the N–NH, C–C, and C–N bonds, respectively. The successful attachment of H2PDC ligands to Al3+ ions was validated by the carboxyl group signals detected at 1386 cm−1 and 1604 cm−1. The specificity of the functional groups further demonstrates the realistic synthesis of Al-based MOF-303, which is consistent with the molecular structure of the previous research. (Fig. 2E). Bismuth-doped nanoparticle properties on MOF-303

Additionally, Mott–Schottky (M–S) spectra were analyzed to assess Bi@ MOF-303 heterojunction type. To clarify this point, the mechanism illustrated in Fig. 3A has been revised and further explained based on the Mott–Schottky (M–S) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results26. The Mott–Schottky analysis (Fig. 3B) shows a positive slope, confirming the n type behavior of the Bi@MOF-303 composite. When metallic bismuth nanoparticles are anchored onto the MOF framework, the difference in work function and Fermi level between metallic Bi and the MOF leads to spontaneous charge redistribution at the interface. Specifically, electrons migrate from Bi nanoparticles to the MOF framework until the Fermi levels reach equilibrium. This process results in electron depletion in the Bi domain and electron accumulation within the MOF structure, leading to the formation of a Schottky junction at the Bi@MOF-303 interface. Consequently, an internal electric field is generated, which facilitates directional electron migration and effectively suppresses charge recombination. This phenomenon explains the “self-driven electron transfer” indicated in the schematic mechanism. Following this interfacial charge redistribution, the accumulated electrons on the MOF surface participate in oxygen activation. The generated hydroxyl radicals are among the most powerful ROS and are responsible for inducing strong oxidative processes. As shown in the proposed mechanism, these radicals oxidize reduced glutathione (GSH), converting it into its oxidized form (GSSG), which disrupts the cellular redox balance and amplifies oxidative stress. Also, the EIS measurements (Fig. 3C) further support the proposed mechanism. The Bi@MOF-303 exhibits a significantly smaller semicircle radius compared to pristine MOF, indicating reduced charge transfer resistance and enhanced electron mobility. In addition, it was found that the reduction in impedance in Al MOF (MOF-303) after loading with bismuth nanoparticles is significantly greater than the reduction in impedance in zirconium MOF (MOF-801), which were examined in our previous research. This observation confirms that the incorporation of Bi nanoparticles significantly improves interfacial electron transport through the formation of the Schottky heterojunction Importantly, the Al based metal nodes provide a more polar Al-O coordination environment compared with Zr based frameworks, which facilitates stronger electronic coupling between Bi nanoparticles and the MOF lattice. This stronger interaction allows more efficient electron delocalization and transfer at the interface. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy further supports this mechanism, as Bi@ MOF-303 exhibits a significantly smaller semicircle in the Nyquist plot, corresponding to a reduced charge transfer resistance and faster interfacial electron transport. Consequently, electrons can be transferred more efficiently to surface adsorbed oxygen species, promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals. Therefore, the enhanced ROS generation in Bi@MOF 303 can be attributed to the synergistic effects of increased electron density, reduced charge transfer resistance, and stronger electronic coupling between Bi nanoparticles and the Al based MOF framework.

ROS detection and extracellular GSH consumption

To assess the material’s ability to deplete GSH, 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) was used as a probe (Figs. 5A). DTNB reacts with GSH to yield a yellow-colored product, 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB), which leads to a notable redshift. As shown in Fig. 5B, C, the addition of GSH caused a significant decline in the DTNB absorption peak at 323 nm, accompanied by the emergence of a new peak at 408 nm, confirming the reduction of DTNB. However, when GSH was reacted with Bi@MOF-303 and mixing the with DTNB, only a faint 408 nm peak was detected, indicating that a large portion of GSH was consumed through redox interaction with structure. However, a strong 408 nm peak was observed in the sample treated with MOF-303 after GSH exposure, suggesting that unreacted GSH remained available to reduce DTNB. As shown in Fig. 5D, a noticeable color change to yellow occurs when GSH solution is exposed to DTNB. However, this color change is not observed when GSH is mixed with DTNB and Bi@MOF-303, indicating the complete conversion of GSH to GSSG. The same results were observed at both pH 5 and 7.4 (Figs. 5B, C). Also, according to Fig. 5E, GSH and DTNB interact to generate TNB and GS-TNB molecules as part of the mechanism of GSH and GSSG sensing by DTNB. GSH then produces GSSG and TNB by reducing GS-TNB. Additionally, GR and NADPH convert TNB to GSH and TNB. Only compounds with yellow circles indicate absorption at 412 nm. Furthermore, in the presence of Bi@MOF-303, GSH is converted to GSSG. The relationship between ROS generation and glutathione (GSH) depletion is intrinsically interconnected and plays a synergistic role in inducing cytotoxicity in cancer cells. The elevated ROS produced by Bi@MOF 303 rapidly oxidize intracellular GSH to its disulfide form (GSSG), which is the primary antioxidant defense system responsible for neutralizing oxidative stress. As GSH is consumed, the cellular redox buffering capacity is progressively weakened, limiting the cell’s ability to scavenge newly generated ROS. Consequently, ROS are no longer efficiently detoxified and begin to accumulate intracellularly. In vitro experiments

Biocompatibility is a critical factor when considering the biomedical application of nanomaterials. Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK-293) were used in an MTT assay to evaluate the cytocompatibility of MOF-303. MOF-303 demonstrated an excellent biocompatibility profile by maintaining cell viability above 80%, even at a dose of 150 μg/mL−1, as shown in Fig. 6A. MOF-303 and Bi@MOF-303 were administered to MDA-MB-231 cancer cells at three distinct doses to examine their therapeutic effects in vitro (Fig. 6B). Among all studied concentrations, Bi@MOF-303 showed the maximum cytotoxicity, killing nearly 75% of cancer cells at 100 μg mL−1. This notable therapeutic improvement is attributed to self-driven electron transfer, which enhances ROS production without the need for external stimulation. Elevated levels of glutathione (GSH), a crucial intracellular antioxidant, can neutralize ROS and reduce treatment efficacy in malignant environments. However, Bi@MOF-303 was found to effectively deplete GSH, thereby amplifying oxidative stress. Quantification using DTNB indicated that Bi@MOF-303 reduced intracellular GSH levels by up to 70% (Fig. 6C). To further investigate intracellular hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation, MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with DCFH-DA, a fluorescent ROS probe28. As depicted in Fig. 6E, the untreated control group displayed no fluorescence, while only weak green fluorescence was observed in the MOF-303-treated group, indicating limited ROS activity. In contrast, cells treated with Bi@MOF-303 exhibited strong green fluorescence, confirming substantial •OH generation and elevated intracellular ROS levels. In addition to the MTT assay, the mechanism of cell death was further evaluated using AO/PI fluorescent staining and Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry analysis to distinguish between apoptosis and necrosis. AO/PI staining showed that most control cells exhibited green fluorescence, indicating cell viability, while cells treated with MOF-303 largely maintained green fluorescence with minimal red staining, confirming the good biocompatibility of MOF-303. In contrast, cells treated with Bi@MOF-303 showed a markedly increased number of red/orange fluorescent cells, indicating enhanced cell death (Fig. 6D). To further clarify the cell death mechanism, Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry analysis was performed. The results indicated that the majority of cells in the control group remained viable (~ 73%). Similarly, the MOF-303 group maintained a high proportion of viable cells (~ 74.5%), confirming its low cytotoxicity. In contrast, treatment with Bi@MOF-303 significantly increased the apoptotic population, with a total apoptosis rate of 37.1%, markedly higher than that observed for MOF-303 (12.1%) (Fig. 6F). Notably, both early and late apoptotic populations increased substantially, while the necrotic population remained minimal. These findings demonstrate that although MOF-303 itself exhibits good biocompatibility, the Bi-loaded MOF system induces cell death predominantly through apoptosis rather than necrosis. Collectively, these results establish Bi@MOF-303 as a potent ROS-generating platform that disrupts intracellular GSH homeostasis, leading to enhanced oxidative stress and improved therapeutic efficacy. Conclusion

Our findings show that bismuth nanoparticles embedded in MOFs may efficiently promote self-driven electron transfer without requiring outside stimulation, which raises ROS generation. Compared to our earlier study with zirconium, the MOF-303 with aluminum as the center metal is an ideal option since it reduces the potential difference, which in turn lowers the impedance and increases the semiconducting capabilities. These changes significantly enhance the performance of bismuth nanoparticles in ROS production under physiological conditions. The results indicate that this system can selectively induce oxidative stress in cancer cells and reduce the intracellular glutathione levels, disrupting the redox balance in the tumor microenvironment. Acidic pH also aids in the oxidation of glutathione, thereby increasing ROS production. This improvement in electrical conductivity and electron flow enhances the generation of active radical species. Our innovative protocol, which emphasizes the accumulation of ROS in cancer cells through self-driven electron transfer, highlights the importance of central metal selection and its impact on electrical behavior and active species production. These findings could provide a solid foundation for future research aimed at optimizing materials and electrochemical processes, contributing to the development of novel technologies in cancer treatment.
