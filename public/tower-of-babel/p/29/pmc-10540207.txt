Characterization of the Biosynthetic Gene Cluster for the Ribosomally Synthesized Cyclic Peptide Epichloëcyclins in Epichloë festucae
Authors: Wei Zhang, Natasha T. Forester, Pranav Chettri, Maurice Heilijgers, Wade J. Mace, Evelyne Maes, Yulia Morozova, Emma R. Applegate, Richard D. Johnson, Linda J. Johnson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The various grass-induced epichloëcyclins of the Epichloë spp. are ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs), produced as small, secreted cyclopeptides from a single gene, gigA. Here, four clustered and coregulated genes (gigA, gigB, gigC, and kexB) with predicted roles in epichloëcyclin production in Epichloë festucae were evaluated through gene disruption. Subsequent chemical analysis indicates that GigB is a DUF3328 domain-containing protein associated with cyclization of epichloëcyclins; GigC is a methyltransferase enzyme responsible for N-methylation of desmethylepichloëcyclins; and KexB is a subtilisin-like enzyme, partly responsible for the propeptide cleavage of epichloëcyclin intermediates. Symbiotic effects on the host phenotype were not observed for gigA, gigC, or kexB mutants, although ΔgigB infection correlated with increased host tiller height and biomass, while only ΔkexB exhibited an effect on endophyte morphology. Disrupting epichloëcyclin biosynthesis showed negligible influence on the biosynthesis of E. festucae-associated alkaloids. Epichloëcyclins may perform other secondary metabolism functions in Epichloë and other fungi.

Introduction

Fungi produce a variety of secondary metabolites among which polyketides, terpenes, alkaloids, and peptides are the most abundant, with many of them having bioactive and medicinal properties (e.g., penicillin, cyclosporine, and lovastatin).1,2 Small peptides are the second most extremely diverse class of natural products found in fungi.1 They can be synthesized either non-ribosomally via large enzymes called non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS) or via a ribosomal-derived route. Of the latter, ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs) from fungi are an underexplored class of natural products that constitute a biochemically/structurally diverse range of natural products with numerous possibilities for bioactivities, including antimicrobials (antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, and virulence activity), and therapeutics.3Epichloë spp. are endophytic fungi that form symbiotic relationships with cool season grasses of the Poaceae family.4Epichloë spp. confer a range of benefits to the host, especially protection against mammalian and insect herbivores through the production of alkaloids.5

RiPPs are usually synthesized from precursor peptides and then processed by peptidases.6 Precursor propeptides contain a signal peptide at the N-terminal followed by perfect or imperfect repeats of core peptide sequences.7 Individual enzymes are usually responsible for the various modifications of a core peptide, which are encoded by genes clustered around the coding gene for the precursor peptide.8 In eukaryotes, site-specific proteolytic cleavage is a common feature in protein maturation and plays an important role in the activation of a large number of enzymes.9 Kexin, a member of the subtilisin-like serine proteinase subfamily, is a critical enzyme in the endoproteolytic process in the Golgi compartment, with most fungi containing only a single gene coding for kexin,10 such as KexA from Kluyveromyces lactis(11) and KexB fromCandida albicans andCandida glabrata.12 Peptides derived from Kex2-processed repeat proteins are widely distributed with highly diverse sequences and roles in the Fungi kingdom from fungal mating factors to toxins.13

By comparison to bacteria, few RiPPs have been functionally characterized in filamentous fungi, including the ustiloxins discovered in Ustilaginoidea virens and Aspergillus flavus,14,15 victorin found in Cochliobolus victoriae,16 and hexapeptide mycotoxins produced by Phomopsis leptostromiformis.17 In Aspergillus flavus, a biosynthetic gene cluster that involves 17 genes has been identified for synthesizing ustiloxin B. The precursor propeptides are cleaved by Golgi protease KexB that is outside the ustiloxin B gene cluster.18 Two genes, ustYa and ustYb, located near candidate genes for ribosomal peptide precursor proteins containing signal peptides and repeated peptides, are involved in the cyclization of ustiloxin B.14

Until recently, four families of RiPPs have been found in fungi, including amatoxins/phallotoxins, borosins, dikaritins, and epichloëcyclins as described in Vogt and Künzler.19 The RiPPs produced by ascomycetes are classified as dikaritins which include the ustiloxins, asperipins, and phomopsins,20 although there is a question as to whether the epichloëcyclins should be grouped, by virtue of belonging to the Dikarya subkingdom and some inferred similar characters to other members, into dikaritins or should remain a separate RiPP family until chemical structural analyses to determine the chemical nature of the cyclizing bridge are completed.19,21,22 Johnson et al.23 hypothesized that the cyclization is through amination of tyrosine in the epichloëcyclins, while Kessler and Chooi24 proposed that the cyclization was formed through an ether bond incorporating the hydroxyl group of the tyrosine side chain. However, other types of cyclization modes cannot be ruled out as further investigation is still required. The five epichloëcyclins (A, B, C, D, and E) of the Fl1 strain differ slightly in the amino acid composition of the seven-amino-acid cyclic ring structure and also in the presence of the amino acid(s) that are outside of the cyclic ring binding to tyrosine (C-terminus threads).23

Previously, Johnson et al.23 discovered that epichloëcyclins are produced from Epichloë species. They identified GigA (grass-induced gene) as a small, secreted protein that is cleaved by Kexin to liberate epichloëcyclins. These macrocyclic heptameric RiPPs are all produced from a single precursor peptide via gigA, which is exceptionally high in the planta-expressed gene. Strain-specific variant combinations and quantities of the 8–9-mer epichloëcyclins (epichloëcyclins A–N) can result due to a variable number of imperfect repeats of a 27 amino acid sequence encoded in the gigA gene. For example, Epichloë festucae strain Fl1 synthesizes five epichloëcyclins (A–E) from four repeats, with epichloëcyclins B and D derived from (identical) repeats 1 and 2, epichloëcyclins C and D from repeat 3, and epichloëcyclin A from repeat 4. Methylated epichloëcyclins were discovered to contain both double- and monomethylated lysine residues,23 thus it was postulated that the activity of a lysine-methyltransferase enzyme was involved. The post-translational modifications to produce the final product were predicted to involve kexin processing, cyclization, methylation, and truncation. However, these mechanisms have not been confirmed for epichloëcyclins. Deletion of gigA, while abolishing the normally widely dispersed in planta production of the epichloëcyclins, was not essential for ryegrass-Epichloë symbiosis suggesting a bioactive role for these compounds. In the Epichloë genome, the gigA gene is clustered with three other genes that are coregulated,25 including a gene-encoding kexB, one of two kexin subfamily proteins found in Epichloë.26 Multiple roles have been suggested for the epichloëcyclins, including as pheromones or signaling molecules in host-fungal interaction (effectors), yet the function of epichloëcyclins remains unconfirmed.

Continuing the work of Johnson et al.,23 this study has characterized the three coexpressed genes adjacent to gigA to determine their functions in epichloëcyclin biosynthesis in E. festucae strain Fl1, which produces five epichloëcyclin variants A–E. We also provide further insights into the potential roles for RiPPs synthesized in the symbiosis between perennial ryegrass and Epichloë endophytes.

Methods

Culture Conditions and Growth Assays

E. festucae strain Fl1 (ex-cultivar SR3000) was initially isolated from perennial ryegrass and is the wild-type (WT) strain for this study.27,28E. festucae WT and derivatives (ΔgigA, ΔgigA/gigA, ΔgigB, ΔgigB/gigB, ΔgigC, ΔgigC/gigC, ΔkexB, ΔkexB/kexB) generated from this study were maintained on 3.9% potato dextrose agar (PDA; Oxoid Ltd., UK) at a temperature of 22 °C in an 8 h light/16 h dark cycle. Immunoblotting of tissue prints was utilized to confirm endophyte infection.29 Ryegrass plants infected with endophytes were cultivated in a glasshouse in 1 L pots filled with potting mix and irrigated as needed.

Gene Disruption, Transformation, and Endophyte Inoculation

All E. festucae strain Fl1 gene disruptions in this study (i.e., ΔgigA, ΔgigB, ΔgigC, and ΔkexB) were generated over time using different variations of the split-marker (SM) homologous recombination-based gene replacement with the gene for hygromycin resistance, hph.30 Briefly, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products amplified from Fl1 genomic regions flanking the gene region for replacement and from essential plasmid vector elements (for replication and selection) were assembled in E. coli Top 10 cells following transformation according to the manufacturer’s method (Invitrogen, USA), with the appropriate selection agent (ampicillin 100 μg·mL–1 or kanamycin 50 μg·mL–1). The resulting constructed plasmids were then used directly or as a template for a secondary PCR to generate SM fragments for E. festucae transformation. All PCR amplicons were generated using standard PrimeSTAR HS DNA polymerase reactions (Takara Bio, Japan) or Platinum SuperFi PCR master mix (Invitrogen) with or without touchdown PCR strategies as required. The PCR products used for construct generation, SM fragment generation, and mutant screenings are shown in Figure S1. The generation of plasmid pLJΔgigA was previously described by Johnson et al.23 The gigB gene replacement plasmid (pNFΔgigB) was constructed using a four-fragment Gibson assembly reaction31 in a standard NEBuilder reaction (New England Biolabs, USA). PCR amplicons derived from gigB flanking DNA (FB1 and FB2) were assembled with a 4.7 kb PCR product (primer set LJNTF3:07/LJNTF3:11) encoding for a hygromycin resistance gene (hph) cassette from pAN7–1-derived vector pDONR221-HYG as described by Rahnama et al.30 and a 2.1 kb (LJNTF2:47/LJNTF2:48) vector sequence containing the origin of replication and ampicillin resistance of PII99.32 A list of primers used in this study can be found in Table S1. The gigC gene replacement plasmid (pMHΔgigC) was constructed using a Golden gate strategy.33 Two amplified regions of DNA flanking gigC FC1 (5′ of gigC) and FC2 (3′ of gigC) and one hygromycin resistance gene (hph) cassette that was amplified from the plasmid pPN1688 (LJNTF4:28/LJNTF4:29) were each independently cloned into a TOPO TA vector (Invitrogen). The three resulting vectors Topo-5′gigC, Topo-3′gigC, and Topo-Hyg were digested with AarI restriction enzyme and then ligated to vector, pType IIs, using the GeneArt Type IIs Assembly Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For generation of ΔkexB, SM vectors, pSMkexB_L and pSMkexB_R, and purified PCR products FX1 and FX2 were cloned into pDONR-SML and pDONR_SMR using a Gateway BP Clonase II Enzyme mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plasmid DNAs were extracted using the ZymoPURE Plasmid Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, USA) and verified by PCR, restriction analysis, and/or DNA sequencing (Massey Genome Service, New Zealand). Secondary PCRs generated SM fragments for E. festucae transformation from: pLJΔgigA (i.e., SMA1 and SMA2); pNFΔgigB (SMB1 and SMB2); and pMHΔgigC (SMC1 and SMC2). Each of the SM fragments comprised one gene flanking region and approximately half of the hygromycin cassette with a 384 bp overlapping region between the two fragments to facilitate homologous recombination within E. festucae protoplasts. However, for ΔkexB generation, pSMkexB_L and pSMkexB_R were used directly to transform E. festucae as described previously.30 Complementation of ΔgigA (i.e., ΔgigA/gigA), ΔgigB (ΔgigB/gigB), and ΔkexB (ΔkexB/kexB) mutants was achieved by following the cotransformation method of Johnson et al.23 and using PCR products CA1, CB1, and CX1, respectively. For ΔgigC complementation, two amplicons, CC1 and CC2, with a 370 bp overlap were used in a SM-type recombination transformation of the ΔgigC mutant creating ΔgigC/gigC. Genomic integration of the full-length gigA-encoding gene for complementation of the knockout was confirmed using PCR for ΔgigA/gigA (SA1b).

Transformants were nuclear purified by subculturing three times prior to genomic DNA extraction and screening by PCR for each of the gene deletion events depicted in Figure S1. Transformants showing PCR products of the predicted size for a gene deletion event were inoculated into perennial ryegrass seedlings for further analysis. Eight-week-old seedlings grown from perennial ryegrass cultivar GA66 (endophyte-free) were used for endophyte inoculation using the previously described method.34 All mutants were confirmed for single-locus insertions using a qPCR method.35 Inoculated seedlings were grown on 4% water agar for 5 days in the dark and then on an 8 h light/16 h dark cycle at a temperature of 22 °C for an additional 5 days. They were then transplanted into root trainers (W: 50 mm, L: 50 mm, H: 150 mm) and allowed to grow for 4 weeks before being transferred to larger pots (W: 90 mm, L: 90 mm, H: 150 mm) and grown for an additional 8 weeks. The presence of endophytes was detected using the tissue-print immunoblotting technique.29,36

Chemistry Analysis

Herbage samples of 12-week-old ryegrass plants were harvested above the roots, frozen at −20 °C, and then freeze-dried. Freeze-dried herbage was pulverized to fine powder using a 2010 Geno/Grinder (SPEX SamplePrep LLC, USA) at 1000 rpm for 2 min and then analyzed for demethylated and methylated epichloëcyclins and other Epichloë-associated alkaloids. Subsamples (50 mg) of freeze-dried and ground herbage were extracted with 80% methanol (1 mL, containing 1.7 μg·mL–1 homoperamine, 0.20 μg·mL–1 festuclavine, and 0.51 μg·mL–1 ergovaline as internal standards) for 1 h in the dark by end-over-end rotation. The supernatant after centrifugation (5000 g, 5 min) was transferred to HPLC vials via a 0.45 μm syringe filter.

The epichloëcyclins were measured by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) using a Thermo Accela 1250 UHPLC coupled to a Thermo LTQ XL linear ion-trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected using electrospray ionization in positive ion mode. Epichloëcyclins and homoperamine (internal standard) were separated using a Gemini-NX column (150 × 2.0 mm, 3 μm) eluted at 200 μL·min–1 with aqueous 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and 97% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent B) using the following gradient: T0 10% B, T2 10% B, T22 95% B, T24 95% B, T25 10% B, T30 10% B. Mass spectral (MS1) data was collected over the range 300–600 m/z enabling semi-quantitation of the epichloëcyclins A, B, C, D, and E ([M + 2H]2+ ions, 554.3, 539.8, 538.8, 511.3, and 510.3 m/z, respectively), and the respective desmethylepichloëcyclins ([M + 2H]2+ ions, 540.3, 525.8, 524.8, 497.3, and 496.3), relative to homoperamine ([M + H]+ ion, 262.2 m/z). The identification of the epichloëcyclins and desmethylepichloëcyclins was confirmed by collecting MS2 data (normalized collision energy 35%) from selected peaks (Figure S2). As no standards are available for the epichloëcyclins or desmethylepichloëcyclins, results are reported as μg·g–1 equivalents of the homoperamine internal standard.

The method for measuring the Epichloë-associated alkaloids followed Berry et al.37 Previous (unpublished) work has shown that the calibration curves for the endophyte alkaloids are linear over the range of concentrations commonly observed for Fl1. Therefore, a single point calibration at the upper limit was used for quantifying the endophyte alkaloids peramine (homoperamine as the internal standard), chanoclavine and agroclavine (festuclavine as the internal standard), and ergovaline and ergine (ergotamine as the internal standard). Due to the low availability of the endophyte indole diterpenes and the lack of an internal standard, paxilline is used as an external standard for paxilline, paspaline, paspaline B, 13-desoxypaxilline, and terpendoles E, I, J, C, K, M, and N. Similarly, lolitrem B is used as an external standard for lolilline, lolicine A, lolitriol, and lolitrems M, K, E, A, and F. Table S2 details the MS parameters used to detect and quantify the endophyte alkaloids.

Microscopic Analysis of Hyphal Growth in Axenic Culture

We examined the morphology of hyphal tips and hyphal fusion using cultures grown on PDA for 1 week. The fresh cultures were dissected along their edges, mounting on a glass slide, and stained with calcofluor white (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 min. The images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV10i-w; Olympus, Japan) with an UPLSAPO-equivalent phase contrast 60× objective using FV10i-ASW 3.1 Viewer software (Olympus, USA). The laser was used as the excitation source at a wavelength of 405 nm, and fluorescence was measured between 425 and 475 nm.

Plant Growth, Endophyte Phenotyping, and Endophyte Biomass Analysis

Plant height and tiller number were determined after 12 weeks of growth in soil under glasshouse conditions at a temperature of ∼22 °C with natural light. Plant height was determined as the distance from the soil surface to the top of the plant. At week 8, four basal 1 cm pseudostem tissues from randomly selected endophyte-infected tillers were pooled, freeze-dried, and analyzed for endophyte biomass by ELISA.38 The leaf sheath at a height of 5 cm above roots was used to phenotype endophytes using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with the same microscopy settings and staining method as described earlier.39 At the end of week 12, all aboveground herbage was harvested, and the fresh weight was determined, followed by freeze-drying and determining the dry weight.

Statistical Analyses

The analysis of the MS data was carried out as described by Johnson et al. (2015). All standard statistical analyses were performed using RStudio v1.1.44740 and R v4.1.2.41 For data with equal variances, ANOVA was performed using the aov function in the “stats” package following testing using shapiro.test and leveneTest R functions in the “car” package42 for normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity, respectively. Subsequently, the function TukeyHSD was used to perform the two-sided Tukey’s honest significant difference post hoc test to determine significant groups. For data that was not normally distributed and/or had unequal variances, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test (kruskal.test function) was applied, and the two-sided Dunn post hoc test was performed with function dunn.test from R package “dunn.test”.43

Results

Orthologous Analysis on gigA Cluster Genes

The coexpression of three adjacent genes with gigA, including kexB, supports the hypothesis for an epichloëcyclin biosynthetic gene cluster.25 These four genes, designated gigA, gigB, gigC, and kexB, colocate on a ∼7.7 kb region of E. festucae strain Fl1 chromosome 4 (accession CP031388.1: bases 800,376–808,466), as shown in Figure 1. The gene boundaries were determined using available transcriptome sequences, and the predicted proteins were inferred from protein prediction information, with manual correction for translation of the optimal protein sequences. BlastP analyses using amino acid sequences of the putative open reading frames of E. festucae strain Fl1 GigA, GigB, GigC, and KexB proteins as queries revealed the possible presence of similar proteins in other fungi. A summary of results for the top non-Epichloë hits is given in Table 1. All matches returned were annotated as uncharacterized or hypothetical proteins. Searches for conserved domains using BlastP/CDD and/or Hmmscan (HMMER) did not return any results for GigA, as previously reported.23 However, conserved domains were detected for GigB, a domain of unknown function DUF3328; for GigC, a SET domain; and for KexB, a peptidase S8 domain. The fungal species with most similar proteins included the following: Ophiocordyceps sinensis CO1844 with significant identities to all four genes, Aspergillus versicolor CBS 583.65 to GigB,45 GigC, and KexB, and Aspergillus bombycis to GigB and KexB.46 Further BlastP of the O. sinensis homologous sequence to GigA additionally found more GigA-like sequences in other fungi; in Aspergillus species including A. felis, A. leporis, A. viridinutans, A. puulaauensis and A. sydowii. Additionally, a tBlastN search of related non-Epichloë Clavicipitaceae genomes revealed GigA-like peptides encoded by three Clavicipitaceae family members with 30–100% coverage and 53–63% overall identities, these being Atkinsonella hypoxylon B4728 (contig JFHB01000178.1), Periglandula ipomoeae IasaF13 (contig AFRD01000062.1), and Aciculosporium take MAFF-241224 (contig AFQZ01000093.1),47 with the last of these having two single repeat gigA-like genes. Investigation of the microsynteny between these fungi, through comparison of the respective genomic sequence for each species (Figure 2), revealed that the gigA and kexB equivalents were conserved in all 12 epichloëcyclin-like gene clusters, and gigB was found in all genomes, including a C-terminally truncated GigB encoded for in A. take. However, for A. leporis and A. viridinutans, the gigB equivalent gene was found on separate genomic scaffolds. The gigC gene was not necessarily found intact or at all in certain species, which suggests that methylation is dispensable for some RiPPs. Furthermore, for three Aspergillus species (A. bombycis, A. versicolor, and A. viridinutans), an extra gene was found in the middle of the gene cluster and has been annotated as encoding for a putative alpha/beta-hydrolase (ABH)-fold subfamily enzyme. Alignment of the propeptide repeat sequences for the nine species showed conservation of the 9-mer core sequence indicating a high degree of conservation between these species (Figure 3). Of note, A. versicolor had examples of highly similar and divergent core RiPP sequences, and for all but one putative GigA-like propeptide, the tyrosine is absolutely conserved, suggesting an important role for this amino acid. The one sequence without tyrosine appears to have arisen from a mutation producing a unique variant. Similarly, the presence and placement of the lysine residue that is dimethylated in epichloëcyclins seem conserved in all biological gene clusters but not for A. sydowii which putatively produces the most diverse RiPPs. The tail sequences appeared conserved in the different species (not shown).

Functional Analysis of gigA, gigB, gigC, and kexB Genes in Axenic Cultures

Culture studies were undertaken to examine whether mutation affected the growth habit of the mutants when grown under axenic conditions for 1 week on PDA. All mutants displayed growth indistinguishable from WT cultures (Figure 4A). Characterization of the Epichloëcyclin Biosynthetic Gene Cluster

Symbiosis is required to activate the expression of the grass-induced genes (gig). Therefore, to investigate secondary metabolite production, as well as to determine phenotypic alterations to either symbiotic partner due to gene mutation, perennial ryegrass seedlings were infected with at least two independent transformants of each of the gigA cluster mutants. LC–MS measurements of epichloëcyclins were performed for at least five ryegrass genotypes infected with each transformant. WT-infected perennial ryegrass produced epichloëcyclins at varying concentrations (μg·g–1), with highest to lowest being epichloëcyclin B > C > D = A > E (Table 2). Epichloëcyclin B is the common variant RiPP octamer derived from two copies of the identical sequence INFKIPYT explaining the much higher abundance of this RiPP. However, the amount of epichloëcyclin A octamer, which was translated from Repeat 4, was the least among all epichloëcyclins. In contrast, herbage infected with ΔgigA, ΔgigB, or ΔgigC abolished production of all epichloëcyclins (epichloëcyclins A–E). Plants infected with ΔkexB yielded comparable amounts of epichloëcyclins B–E relative to WT-infected tissue, but no epichloëcyclin A was detected. Further, given that gigC putatively encodes for a N-lysine-methyltransferase enzyme and that epichloëcyclins are dimethylated, we also targeted and measured demethylated versions of all epichloëcyclins, desmethylepichloëcyclins, in all the symbioses. Of all these strains, only plants infected with ΔgigC produced higher concentrations of desmethylepichloëcyclins (Figure S3), while only low concentrations of desmethylepichloëcyclins were detected in ryegrass plants infected with WT (desmethylepichloëcyclins A, B, C, and D) and ΔkexB (desmethylepichloëcyclins B, C, and D) (Table 2). The results suggest complete complementation of ΔkexB and ΔgigB with epichloëcyclin concentrations of these strains being similar to those of WT. However, the concentrations of the various epichloëcyclins in herbage infected with ΔgigA/gigA and ΔgigC/gigC were mostly significantly lower for certain epichloëcyclins compared with WT-infected ryegrass plants, indicating overall restoration of epichloëcyclin production, but to lower levels than WT. In the case of ΔgigC/gigC, the total concentrations of epichloëcyclins and desmethylepichloëcyclins approximate those of WT.

To strengthen the evidence for GigB as being responsible for cyclization to epichloëcyclins, multiple peptidomics approaches (sample, reference databases, and MS types) were used to detect linearized peptides within ryegrass plants infected with ΔgigB as compared with ryegrass plants infected with WT and ΔgigA. Despite this, none of the peptides of interest containing the core epichloëcyclin amino acid sequence could be detected in any samples. Optimization of acquisition methods for the high-resolution Q-TOF or Q-Exactive mass spectrometer instruments was achieved using a synthetic linear oligopeptide mimic, LOPD/AGR3 (INFK*IPYT) which was also dimethylated at the lysine residue, and the results indicated that the peptide itself could be detected. Many features were detected in peptide extracts from the ground pseudostem tissue, and peptides were identified using in-house Epichloë and ryegrass databases. Yet, none of the peptides of interest were detected, and almost all the peptides found were plant peptides. Similarly, applying the method using less “complex” samples, i.e., apoplastic fluid did not yield identifiable peptide. Furthermore, spiking the control peptide into a complex matrix (endophyte-free plant peptide extract) did not allow its identification within the sample. Even though gene inactivation studies have shown that GigB is required for the cyclization of epichloëcyclins, perennial ryegrass infected with ΔgigB did not produce any detectable linear intermediates or they were rapidly degraded.

Discussion

In Epichloë species, epichloëcyclins are proposed to arise via the expression of the conserved and coexpressed gigA gene cluster. In this study, we have confirmed, through functional characterization, the predicted roles for the gigA cluster genes (gigA, gigB, gigC, and kexB) in epichloëcyclin biosynthesis based on the foundation work.23,48 Gene disruptions followed by chemical analyses of plants infected with the various mutants proved the hypotheses as follows. Consistent with Johnson et al.,23 disrupting gigA eliminated epichloëcyclin production and the gigA gene complementation partially restored their production. Loss of gigB was expected to prevent maturation of epichloëcyclins due to homology to DUF3328-type proteins that are typically involved in RiPP cyclization. Accordingly, no cyclic peptides were detected in ryegrass plants infected with ΔgigB but were present when infected with the ΔgigB/gigB strain. The absence of methylated epichloëcyclins in ryegrass plants infected with ΔgigC and their reoccurrence in those infected with the ΔgigC/gigC strain supports the hypothesis that gigC encodes the lysine-methyltransferase responsible for mono- and dimethylation of epichloëcyclins. Finally, the observation that epichloëcyclins B to E (but not epichloëcyclin A) were still detected in ryegrass plants infected with ΔkexB suggested the involvement of another protease in the cleavage of epichloëcyclins (e.g., kexA). However, the processing to epichloëcyclin A does appear to specifically require KexB as this RiPP reappears in the ΔkexB/kexB strain, thus verifying a specialized role for KexB in epichloëcyclin biosynthesis. Likewise, the recurrence of all epichloëcyclins in ryegrass plants infected with complemented gigA cluster mutants confirmed the proposed gene functions. However, the much lower concentrations of epichloëcyclins observed in ryegrass plants infected with ΔgigA/gigA and ΔgigC/gigC strains indicate only partial complementation of gigA and gigC gene functions through using ectopic integration of the full-length gene, suggesting that the original genomic context may be required for full expression and activity.

We have found that epichloëcyclins appear to be synthesized in a pathway similar to that of other fungal RiPPs, in which a multicore preprotein featuring a leader, several cores, and follower peptides is post-translationally modified, in this case by cleavage, tyrosine cyclization, methylation, and unknown truncation. Despite different resulting RiPP products, epichloëcyclin biosynthesis resembles the early steps in the pathway leading to ustiloxin B, a mycotoxin which was initially found in rice false smut caused by the pathogenic fungus Ustilaginoidea virens.49 An equivalent biosynthetic gene cluster has been elucidated for Aspergillus flavus.18,50 In this proposed pathway, UstA, a multicore RiPP precursor protein, is converted to ustiloxin F, first via digestion into 16 trideca-/tetradecapeptides by Kex2 proteinases.13 A combination of three oxygenases, including a tyrosinase (UstQ) and two domains of unknown function DUF3328 cyclic oxidases ustYa and ustYb, forms via meta-oxidation of the tyrosine aromatic ring, a stable macrocyclic intermediate desmethylustiloxin F, which is further methylated by UstM to produce ustiloxin F.50 Similarly (Figure 7A), we speculate that in Epichloë, GigA is first digested by KexB (and other proteases), followed closely by cyclization by the DUF3328 domain-containing GigB through amination of the conserved tyrosine linking to the first position amino acid of the RiPP, as confirmed for a first position proline residue [W. Mace, unpublished nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) results]. The preliminary NMR analysis also showed that this cyclization mode differed from the ether bond cyclization as previously proposed24 and was via the meta position of the tyrosine aromatic ring, which was potentially unique among fungal RiPPs (Figure 8). However, more data are needed to accurately uncover the chemical structure of epichloëcyclins. Finally, N6, N6 dimethylation of the conserved RiPP lysine residue is performed by GigC since the presence of desmethylepichloëcyclins indicates that methylation can occur last in the order of biosynthesis of epichloëcyclins. The conservation of the core lysine among fungi producing epichloëcyclin-like RiPPs, regardless of whether a methyltransferase-encoding gene was present in the biosynthetic cluster, is interesting and suggests that methylated mature peptides are dispensable for some fungi. It could also be that other methyltransferase enzyme(s) encoded elsewhere in the genome may provide this function.

Whereas kexin B likely cleaves the leader peptide sequence, the protease responsible for digesting the follower peptide remains unknown. In contrast to ustiloxin B production in A. flavus, tyrosinase involvement in epichloëcyclin cyclization was not expected to be required as amination of tyrosine is predicted, and this enzyme is not encoded by any of the genes neighboring the gig gene cluster. However, two candidate tyrosinase-encoding genes (EfM3.076380 and EfM3.019440) that are present elsewhere in the E. festucae Fl1 genome and have very low homology to UstQ (<22% identity to USTQ_ASPFN) may be involved in epichloëcyclin biosynthesis. Ideally, the detection of linear epichloëcyclin-like peptide products would have strengthened the evidence for cyclization by GigB; however, none were found using multiple high-resolution peptidomic approaches. The most likely explanation is that the intermediary peptides are unstable and susceptible to proteolysis and have limited ability to penetrate cells when compared to the cyclopeptide form.51 The lack of stable linear epichloëcyclin intermediates is not uncommon as linear intermediates were not discovered in the biosynthesis of ustiloxin B.50 It may be that cleavage and cyclization are closely coupled to reduce epichloëcyclin loss. Removal of the follower peptide may involve GigB as the coincidence of the A. take predicted GigB protein with C-terminal truncation, together with the two gigA genes, each encoding for a single epichloëcyclin might suggest that the GigB C-terminal region could be required for processing from a multiple repeat preprotein. This could implicate the GigB N-terminal region in the epichloëcyclin cyclization reaction. However, genes encoding for a full-length GigB and one single copy epichloëcyclin-like peptide was observed in P. ipomoeae but does not exclude the possible involvement of GigB either alone or in combination with other enzymes for peptide processing (for future investigations).

The partial redundancy of function observed for the ΔkexB strains may be explained by the fact that E. festucae unusually contains two copies (kexA and kexB) rather than the one copy of kexin-like genes found in most microorganisms.9,26 Why epichloëcyclin A is not present in ΔkexB mutant-derived samples (while the other epichloëcyclins are) could not be addressed in this study but may be dependent on processing location or specificity of the kexin enzyme. For example, the production of epichloëcyclins B to E which is predicted to occur in the Golgi apparatus52,53 could be performed by KexA or other proteases with overlapping but differing specificities for the kexin cleavage site. An example of the latter is the preference for basic residues surrounding cleavage sites of Kex2 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and54Aspergillus niger55 and for furin, a protease enzyme found in humans and animals, which has specific requirements at P1’ relative to the cleavage site.56 Basic residues surrounding the Kexin cleavage sites may specify epichloëcyclin A as a KexB target. Therefore, we hypothesize that the Kexin enzymes in Epichloë have unique cleavage sites and/or a requirement for appropriate cellular localization for Kexin B function, which awaits further validation, including a comparison of function with that of Kexin A on the epichloëcyclins of multiple Epichloë strains. GigB should not be excluded as a candidate for leader peptide removal as no unprocessed or processed derivatives were detected in peptide analyses (Figure 7B). Proof for this function would require mutagenesis studies of GigB and search for intermediate cyclized products in the absence of KexB.

Previously, gigA was reported as a novel endophyte gene,23 but our homology searches on gigA cluster proteins discovered orthologous proteins in Ophiocordyceps sinensis and seven Aspergillus species (Figure 2). The top hit organism, O. sinensis, is a high-altitude entomophagous ascomycetous fungus, infecting and feeding on the larvae of Hepialidae family moths including ghost moths (Thitarodes spp.). It has been classified as a medicinal mushroom57 with over 30 bioactivities, including immunomodulatory, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects.58 Three types of peptides have been found in O. sinensis culture extracts, including cyclodipeptides, cordymin, and myriocin,58 but none of these share sequence similarity with E. festucae GigA. Given epichloëcyclin-like peptides have not been reported in these culture extracts, we conclude that the peptides are likely produced only under specific conditions, e.g., symbiotic conditions.59 Interestingly, of the next two top matching organisms, A. versicolor and A. bombycis, the former strain was isolated from silkworm frass,60 while the latter was isolated in “rotted algae” but has also been isolated from the gut of Aspongopus chinensis (stink bug).61A. leporis (NRRL 3126) is known to produce leporins, a family of 2-pyridone metabolites with anti-insectan/antifeedant properties and have been shown to influence conidial and sclerotial development.62 Interestingly, an interdependent relationship between Epichloë and Botanophila fly life cycles exists, whereby the flies are vectors for Epichloë sexual spore dissemination to the stroma of new hosts leading to new infections, while laying eggs in and grazing fungal tissues supports the proliferation of the fly progeny. Moreover, the grass symbionts of the Clavicipitaceae family are thought to have arisen from an interkingdom host jump from animals to plants, with the closest related subclades including Hypocrella and Metarhizium genera of arthropod pathogens,63 although we did not find epichloëcyclin-like peptides in the genomic resources for these genera. The curious coincidence of insect-gut associated fungi with the presence of a respective gig-like gene clusters suggests a possible function related to insects, maybe as attractants or deterrents.64 The requirement for in planta evaluation of epichloëcyclin production provided an opportunity to determine whether the genes within the gigA cluster had any roles in symbiotic maintenance. Our findings that the disruption of any of the genes caused no gross phenotypical changes to the ryegrass plants implies that the epichloëcyclins have no or little effect on host development (Figures 5 and 6) and is consistent with previous observations for ΔgigA.23 Endophyte phenotypic changes were only detected in leaf sheaths when infected with ΔkexB. Considering no effects were observed for the other gig mutants, the atypical growth may arise from pleitrophic effects owing to the loss of kexin B activity in the cleavage-based maturation of other compound(s), which may be required for the normal growth of E. festucae. Consistent with the findings of this research, which revealed highly branching epiphyllous hyphae in the leaf sheath tissues of ryegrass plants when infected with E. festucae ΔkexB strain, the Aspergillus niger ΔkexB strain exhibited increased branching and also thicker cell walls,65 and a potential function for KexB in surface attachment processes such as biofilm formation has been suggested. Therefore, the negligible effects of epichloëcyclins on the symbiosis under standard glasshouse conditions suggest that these secondary metabolites are not essential for the growth, development, and reproduction of Epichloë but likely act as bioactives against other organisms such as bacteria, fungi, amoebae, plants, insects, and viruses or as attractants or deterrents to insects.66,67

Furthermore, having established that the function of GigB is cyclization of epichloëcyclins, our additional findings of only mutant (ΔgigB)-infected plants with significantly more tillers and plant biomass (than WT-infected plants) was not expected. However, putting this together with the disappearance of expected linear peptide intermediates in ΔgigB-infected hosts, and observations that gig cluster mutants produce either cyclic peptides or no epichloëcyclins at all, brings us to the hypotheses that the comparatively unstable linear peptides may act as some type of plant growth promoters or simply may be absorbed as nutrients by the host plant.51

In summary, this study discovered the biosynthetic functions for genes in the gig gene cluster which were shown to be essential to produce epichloëcyclins. The full structural determination of the epichloëcyclins is still to be completed, although the evidence produced here, with the several similar characters to dikaritins, suggests that the epichloëcyclins should indeed belong to the dikaritin family of RiPPs. The study also confirmed that epichloëcyclins are not involved in maintaining the symbiotic relationship between Epichloë and its host ryegrass plants. As a result, any ecological roles of epichloëcyclins remain unknown. Our research is currently constrained by epichloëcyclin biosynthesis only occurring in planta, making their isolation difficult. A future opportunity might be investigating how to activate the gigA cluster genes under culture conditions for epichloëcyclin purification, which could then be used for subsequent antagonistic tests and insect feeding assays. Moreover, with a number of antifungal and antiviral cyclic peptides discovered in other fungi,68,69 further research might investigate the effects of epichloëcyclins on the microbial diversity of ryegrass, e.g., as antibiotics, toxins, pheromones, and/or enzyme inhibitors.
