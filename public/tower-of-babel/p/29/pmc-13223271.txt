Hybrid vision transformer framework for congenital heart disease diagnosis
Authors: Khalil Khan, Din Irfanud, Rehan Ullah Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital heart disease is one of the most common birth defects and has been a source of significant morbidity and mortality among infants. Early diagnosis allows for early intervention and better patient outcomes. Traditional methods are effective yet have limitations such as high costs, dependency on expert knowledge, and noise. Recent developments in deep learning showcase the potential to address these limitations and automate diagnosis with high accuracies. This work proposes the use of a hybrid vision transformer (H_ViTs) model for the diagnosis of congenital heart disease based on heart sounds. The proposed vision transformer-based architecture leverages both local feature extraction with convolutional neural networks and global pattern modeling by vision transformer. Thus, the model is able to capture local dependencies with convolutional neural networks and global dependencies with vision transformers in heart sounds’ spectrogram representations. This study utilized a recently released open-source ZCHSound dataset of children’s pediatric heart sound recordings. To evaluate this model, we use accuracy, precision, recall, F-1 measure, and the confusion matrix as evaluation measures. Our proposed model obtained better results as compared to previously reported results on the same dataset. However, we notice comparatively poor results for multi-class classification, particularly for classes with limited data. In summary, the proposed hybrid model effectively detects congenital heart disease, though additional efforts are needed to improve robustness against class imbalance and noise.

Introduction

Reports show that congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most prevalent congenital defect worldwide. It remains a significant contributor to neonatal mortality. CHD is a broad spectrum of anatomical abnormalities affecting the heart or major vessels1. According to the recent reports of World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 60,000 newborns are diagnosed with CHD annually. In 2017, the global burden of CHD was estimated to exceed 12 million individuals, corresponding to an age-standardized prevalence of 170.6 cases per 100,000 population1. The prevalence is particularly elevated in low- and lower-middle-income countries in Africa and Asia, where incidence rates surpass 30 per 1,000 live births1. Figure 1 shows survival rates to adolescence for infants born with CHD in high-income and low-income countries. The Figure 1 also shows that survival to adolescence is sufficiently higher in high-income countries, primarily due to improved access to pediatric cardiac surgery and healthcare facilities. These disparities are often attributed to limitations in initial prenatal screening, and the availability of some advanced diagnostic tools, underscoring the urgent need for scalable diagnostic problems and its solutions.Fig. 1A comparison for babies born with critical CHD in high-income and low-income countries. CHD is a leading cause of mortality that is associated with congenital disorders. According to recent reports and research, approximately 4.5% of affected babies before birth die in utero, and 21% do not survive beyond the perinatal period2. Similarly, among survivors, the risk of long-term complications is substantial; children with CHD have a nine-fold higher likelihood of neuro developmental or functional impairments1. Moreover, it is also noticed that CHD remain asymptomatic for extended periods, which impedes timely detection and intervention2. These complex and serious problems highlight the necessity for improved early screening protocols, as prompt diagnosis significantly enhances treatment planning. This also limits disease progression, and prevents life-threatening outcomes3.

Some modalities that are utilized in clinical practice for CHD screening, including echocardiography (Echo), electrocardiography (ECG), pulse oximetry (POX), chest X-ray imaging, and phonocardiography (PCG)4–6. Research shows that Echo is the gold standard for diagnosis; its accuracy depends heavily on operator expertise and equipment quality, and its availability is limited in resource-constrained settings4. Another modality that is used frequently in diagonistics is ECG. It is affordable and widely accessible but it mostly lacks sufficient sensitivity to detect complicated structural abnormalities7. Similarly a cost effective approach is provided by POX, yet it is typically used in conjunction with other methods to reduce false negatives8,9. Chest X-ray imaging remains a primary tool for general cardiac assessment, but it offers limited structural information and is subject to interpretive variability10. PCG is similarly economical and portable; however, its diagnostic accuracy depends heavily on clinician expertise, and recordings are particularly susceptible to noise11.

Multiple diagnostic modalities are being employed in clinical practice for CHD screening, including echocardiography (Echo), electrocardiography (ECG), pulse oximetry (POX), chest X-ray imaging, and phonocardiography (PCG)4–6. Echo, while regarded as the gold standard, exhibits accuracy that is significantly influenced by the operator’s proficiency and the quality of the equipment, with restricted accessibility in resource-constrained areas4. It is also noticed that ECG is most cost-effective and readily available modality, although it frequently lacks adequate sensitivity for intricate structural anomalies7. POX is an economical method for identifying significant CHD but this modality is employed alongside other modalities to mitigate false negatives8,9. X-ray continues to serve as a primary instrument for general cardiac evaluation. However, it offers limited structural detail and is susceptible to interpretive variability10. Similalry, PCG is comparably economical and portable; nevertheless, its diagnostic precision is significantly reliant on clinician expertise, and recordings are particularly vulnerable to noise11.

Despite the benefits of existing diagnostic approaches, no single modality provides a fully reliable, accessible, and operator-independent solution. This serious limitation is especially pronounced in resource-limited regions, where shortages of trained pediatric cardiologists and advanced imaging equipment frequently lead to delayed or missed diagnoses. We believe that Deep learning (DL) models can emerge as valuable tools for improving clinical decision-making by using automating feature extraction and reducing operator dependence. Among modern DL methods, vision transformers (ViTs) demonstrate outstanding performance in image, signal, and time-frequency analysis due to their ability to capture global context, and efficiently process structured data. Recent studies indicate that ViT-based models perform better than convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in medical imaging applications and offer greater resilience to noise, variability, and class imbalance as well.

A major gap exists in the use of AI for the diagnosis and treatment of patients with CHDs across their lifespan. The utilization of AI in CHD has been restricted by the lack of CHD-specific datasets with labels for model training, the complicated modelling demands caused by diverse clinical characteristics and age-related pathophysiological changes, and the fragmented nature of data within center-specific repositories. Furthermore, at baseline, information regarding particular rare forms of CHDs is limited, requiring multidisciplinary collaboration to compile sufficient datasets. Substantial deficiencies in clinical training, knowledge, experience, and familiarity with AI exist. CHD continues to be an inadequately explored area for both cardiovascular and ML experts. The region remains unexplored due to various intricate factors. Hospitals generate significant quantities of data, encompassing clinical information and data from electronic health records. The continuous progression of big data is crucial in healthcare management, as it enables the analysis of large datasets to improve illness treatment, determine appropriate therapeutic dosages, and generate predictions. Healthcare produces significant data; nevertheless, a considerable portion remains underused due to challenges in storing, organizing, and understanding complex datasets that often involve multidimensional and nonlinear relationships among variables.

This study introduces a ViT–based DL framework for diagnosis and classification of CHD. PCG data are well-suited for such applicationa because it contain clinically relevant physiological cues that are associated with structural heart abnormalities. These can be acquired through non-invasive, portable, and cost-effective methods. These attributes render PCGs particularly valuable in resource-limited settings where access to conventional diagnostic modalities is limited. Building on these strengths, an H-ViTs framework for CHD classification is developed and its performance is systematically evaluated against existing methods.

Related works

CHD can be diagnosed using different methods, including POX, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ECG, Echo. Each modality provides unique advantages, from cost-effectiveness in POX to detailed anatomical structures in CT scans. On the other hand, some diagnosis types possess some challenges and disadvantages, such as the need for expert knowledge, cost, or ineffectiveness in CHD type classification. In this part of the paper, various modalities that address CHD recognition using machine learning (ML) are addressed.

CT scan

Ecabert et al.12 introduced one of the earliest model-based methods for heart segmentation in CT scans. It comprised a 3D Generalized Hough Transform for initial localization, piecewise affine transformation, and deformable adaptation. This method achieved a mean surface-to-surface error of 0.82 mm, which showed high accuracy for segmenting CT scans of a heart into its major components, namely four chambers, myocardium, and major vessels. Later, Khan et al.2 presented the Cardiac Deep Learning Model (CDLM) that employed a 3D U-Net architecture with graph matching for vessel classification. Overall, CDLM showed effectiveness in heart segmentation into chambers, blood pool, and vessels. CDLM demonstrated notable accuracy in aortic segmentation with high mean Dice scores across heart structures, surpassing earlier methods. While CT scans may seem promising in CHD diagnosis, collecting data for training and affordability of equipment may suggest that it may not be the most effective way for heart defect classification.

Echocardiograms

Jingjing et al.13 explored AI-assisted auscultation (AI-AA) for CHD diagnosis using heart sound recordings. The model used a CNN to classify time-frequency representations of heart sounds and obtained a sensitivity of 97%, a specificity of 89%, and an overall accuracy of 96%. This method aligns closely with traditional auscultation, offering accessible and effective remote CHD screening in certain resource-limited areas. Qiao et al.4 introduced a novel model for fetal screening. The model integrated a Multi-Scale Gated Axial-Transformer Network (MSGATNet) along with residual learning to segment and parse the four chambers of the fetal heart in ultrasound images. The proposed method addressed such challenges of fetal screening as movements, receiving precision of 96%, recall of 94%, and overall accuracy of 95%, surpassing 12 state-of-the-art models in fetal CHD diagnosis.

Electrocardiograms

Chen et al.7 developed CHDdECG, a DL model that integrates raw ECG waveforms with wavelet-transformed and human-concept features. The model achieved ROC-AUC scores of 0.915 (for internal tests) and 0.917/0.907 (external test sets). This model got a specificity of 0.881 on the internal test set, which might outperform traditional diagnostic approaches. Similarly, Du et al.14 proposed a Residual of Residual (RoR) Network for CHD diagnosis from ECG signals. The RoR model reached an accuracy of 92.45%, a sensitivity of 74.73%, and a specificity of 94.07% using 10-second ECG segments, with potential for increased sensitivity through longer recordings.

Phonocardiograms

Hassanuzzaman et al.15 proposed a DL model that integrates a one-dimensional CNN with an attention transformer that accepts raw PCG signals for the binary classification of CHD and non-CHD. They utilized a dataset of 484 patient recordings collected in clinical settings. Their model achieved promising results with an accuracy of 92.3%, a sensitivity of 98.3%, a specificity of 83.3%, an F1 score of 93.9 % and an AUC of 0.964.

Chen et al.16 introduced two lightweight CNN-based models for CHD diagnosis from pediatric PCG data. The first model was dense-block architecture, and the other one was clique-based architecture. Both models were utilizing raw PCG signals. This study used a pediatric heart sound dataset consisting of 528 recordings. The dense-block model showcased superior performance with an accuracy of 96.21%, a specificity of 98.08%, and a precision of 91.67% and 98.77% for CHD and non-CHD cases, respectively. The clique-based model showed worse but still promising performance with accuracy of 91.68%, specificity of 89.17%, sensitivity of 93.40%, and precision of 88.96% respectively. Authors also revealed that both models do not require high computational demands and thus can be used for real-time CHD diagnosis via IoT-enabled healthcare systems.

Yadav et al.17 proposed an approach for CHD classification from PCG signals by leveraging Fourier Transform and Cepctrum analysis. The method employed down-sampling of recorded signals from 2000 Hz to 1000 Hz for faster processing and bandpass filtering to reduce noise. Then, a Fourier transform was performed for frequency domain analysis. The ratio of the mean and maximum values of the Fourier transform signal was used as the discrimination between CHD and non-CHD cases. Additionally, they used the mean values of cepstrum coefficients extracted from heart sounds. Authors utilized the 2016 PhysioNet/CinC Challenge database with a total of 100 heart sound recordings. A support vector machine (SVM) was then trained and tested on these statistical features. The classifier achieved an accuracy of 95%, a sensitivity of 100 %, and a specificity of 90% respectively.

Diagnostic procedures such as X-rays, cardiac MRI, dual-source tomography scans, and CT examinations are also used; nonetheless, they are time-consuming, intricate, costly, and necessitate skilled cardiologists.

Authors in11 proposed a dataset of PCG data for CHD diagnosis. They present a comprehensive effort to create a large-scale, high-quality, open-source dataset of PCG recordings for research purposes. The dataset used in this study is publicly available at http://zchsound.ncrcch.org.cn/data. Since this is the latest dataset, we also use the same dataset to evaluate our model. The total number of participants who participated for data is 1259. The data was collected from 3 children’s hospitals. Participants’ ages range from newborns up to 14 years. To ensure correct diagnosis labeling, 2 cardiac experts reviewed the heart sounds based on their ECG results, which provide more reliable diagnostic information for decision-making. The study extracted 84 time-domain and frequency-domain features with the usual 60-20-20 dataset split.

All heart sound recordings were taken using a standard ChildCare G-100 smart stethoscope at an 8000 Hz sampling rate, and each recording was 11–30 seconds in duration and stored in standard.wav format. To provide reliability and consistency, all recordings were conducted on subjects in a supine position and underwent a stringent quality control procedure; trained clinicians manually screened the data to form two distinct subsets–a high-quality set of 941 clean recordings (both normal and various cases of CHD such as ASD, VSD, PDA, and PFO and a low-quality set of 318 noisy recordings from neonates. Furthermore, confirmatory cardiac US examinations and professional opinions were employed to strictly allocate diagnostic labels, thus providing an accurate foundation upon which to construct and test wise auscultation algorithms. The gender distribution is adequately balanced for both high-quality and low-quality sets.

Pre-processing

We preprocessed the heart sounds into Mel spectrograms, which represent spectral features. A Mel spectrogram is a visual representation of sound where the x-axis represents time and the y-axis represents frequency in terms of the Mel scale for frequencies and decibels for amplitude, similar to how humans hear. Mel spectrograms are different from traditional spectrograms and are superior to audio because they preferentially emphasize areas of frequency and intensity in more human-hearing-relevant terms and thus extract more features for DL models. Audio recordings were resampled at 16 kHz, normalized, and divided into fixed-length 5-second segments. Mel spectrograms were calculated with a window size of 1024 samples, a hop length of 512 samples, and 64 Mel frequency bins. The spectrograms were log-scaled and normalized to improve feature representation.

CNN-based feature extraction

The core idea of the model proposed in this paper is a depthwise separable CNN that serves as the backbone for the extraction of spatial features from heart sound recordings. CNN-based feature extraction architecture.

Vision transformer

The CNN architecture produces a structured feature map that captures spatially localized spectral and temporal variations within the spectrogram. This representations retain both fine-scale temporal fluctuations and broader frequency-based patterns characteristic. The resulting feature tensor is then reshaped into a sequence of tokens that actually ensure compatibility with transformer-based processing. The vision transformer module, designed to learn long-range contextual structures, operates on these tokens to model complicated dependencies spanning multiple cardiac cycles and spectral regions. In contrast to CNNs, which use fixed-size kernels and thus maintain a local perspective, the proposed architecture leverages a global receptive field through the self-attention mechanism.

Each attention head computes an independent attention distribution, allowing the model to capture distinct semantic, temporal, or spectral relationships. This provides a richer feature representation than a single-head formulation, as multiple subspaces of the embedding dimension can be processed concurrently. Weighted random sampling

The imbalanced data is not only for representation of subtypes but also for abnormal-normal samples. The imbalance creates enormous difficulty in ML tasks, affecting performance and generalization ability. To resolve the problem, the weighted random sampling technique was employed. It provides a good approach to assigning sampling probability to minority classes. Particularly, the weighted random sampling assigns a weight to each sample according to the inverse proportion of class frequencies in each class in the training data set.11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_i = \frac{1}{N_{y_i}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In the above formula, the weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i$$\end{document} of every sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i$$\end{document} is set to be inversely proportional to the number of samples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{y_i}$$\end{document} in its own class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i$$\end{document}. Because of this, less frequent class samples will have larger weights, thus an increased probability of selection when training. Through these normalized weights, the weighted random sampler perfectly balances the data set such that all the classes are proportionately represented when being trained.

10-fold cross validation experiments

To better test how robust and general our proposed hybrid framework is, we ran extra 10-fold cross-validation experiments. We split the dataset into ten equal, non-overlapping parts. For each round, nine parts were used for training and validation, and the last part was used for testing. This way, every sample was tested once. We averaged the results from all ten rounds to get the final results. This method gives a more reliable and fair estimate of how the model performs because it does not rely on just one train-test split and helps reduce random variation. The cross-validation results show that our model performs consistently across different splits, which supports the stability of what it has learned and the reliability of our findings, even when data is limited.

Conclusion

CHD is a global health challenge, especially among pediatric populations and in resource-limited settings. In resource limited areas access to expert clinicians and advanced diagnostic equipment is extremely limited. Research reveals that early diagnosis is essential for improving clinical outcomes. However, it is also a fact that conventional screening methods such as auscultation and imaging are often subjective, very expensive, and more dependent on human expertise. Our work proposes a hybrid ViT–based DL framework for CHD diagnosis using PCG recordings.

The proposed model combines a lightweight depthwise separable CNN for efficient local temporal–spectral feature extraction with a Transformer module that models long-range dependencies. Conversion of raw PCG signals into Mel spectrogram representations enables the model to capture clinically relevant acoustic patterns associated with both normal and pathological heart sounds. The CNN backbone extracts discriminative local features, while the transformer encoder utilizes self-attention to learn global contextual relationships across multiple cardiac cycles, resulting in a complementary and robust feature representation.

We perform extensive experiments on the large-scale, publicly available ZCHSound pediatric heart sound dataset using both a standard 60–20–20 split and 10-fold cross-validation. The model demonstrated strong performance in binary CHD classification, achieving high accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, and surpassing conventional ML baselines previously reported on the same dataset. These findings confirm the effectiveness of integrating convolutional inductive biases with transformer-based global modeling for heart sound. However, the model’s performance for multi-class classification was comparatively lower, primarily due to significant class imbalance and limited sample availability.

In summary, this study demonstrates that a hybrid CNN–ViTs architecture is a promising direction for automated CHD screening from PCG recordings. The proposed hybrid ViTs framework offers an effective balance between accuracy, computational efficiency, and clinical practicality, making it well suited for deployment in real-world pediatric screening. Future work will focus on improving multi-class classification performance through advanced data augmentation, imbalance-aware learning strategies, and noise-robust modeling, as well as validating the framework on larger and more diverse multi-center datasets.
