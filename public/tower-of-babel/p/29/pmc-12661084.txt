How I Do It: Interprofessional Education and Radiation Therapy—Establishing Opportunities for Student Learning in the Workplace
Authors: Melissa Higson, Sonya Morgan, Eileen McKinlay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClinical workplaces such as hospitals are often keen to arrange interprofessional learning activities for students on clinical placements, but radiation therapy (RT) students are often overlooked because of a lack of awareness and understanding of their role in patient care. Furthermore, there are challenges in setting up hospital‐based interprofessional education (IPE) because of the logistics of liaising with a large number of staff, as students are from different disciplines. Hospitals that offer RT are ideal sites to offer IPE, as invariably staff in these settings work interprofessionally to support patients with cancer and can immediately see its value. This How I do it guide lays out the practical steps needed to initiate and implement workplace‐based IPE involving RT students modelled on a long‐standing Cancer Care IPE learning activity in New Zealand.

Introduction

Curriculum accreditation processes increasingly require health disciplines such as clinical psychology, dental, dietetics, medicine, nursing, oral health, paramedicine, pharmacy, physiotherapy, radiation therapy (RT) and social work to have interprofessional competencies [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Students progressively attain these competences through undertaking interprofessional education (IPE) [6, 7]. IPE is defined as learning activities where two or more professions learn with, from, and about each other [8]. The benefits of undertaking IPE in preregistration years result in students being collaborative practice ready and knowing how to work effectively with other disciplines when they graduate [9, 10]. IPE builds interprofessional competencies so students are collaborative practice ready as graduates, and this ultimately leads to improved safety and better outcomes for patients as well as increased staff satisfaction [11, 12, 13, 14].

Disciplines that educate large numbers of students, such as nursing, medicine, pharmacy and physiotherapy, are frequently mentioned in published examples of IPE [15]. Yet, it is important for RT students to be included in IPE to ensure they are equipped with the interprofessional collaboration skills required of qualified staff who work closely with a team, including radiation oncologists, medical physicists, nurses, dieticians, social workers and dentists [16] to support patient‐centred care [17]. Furthermore, it is equally important for students of other disciplines to meet and work with RT students, as opportunities to come across them are limited, and students of other disciplines report knowing little about RT and radiation therapists [18, 19].

There are, however, challenges in setting up hospital‐based IPE with few published examples of learning activities that include RT students [20, 21]. Those published describe the following impacts on participating students: enhanced attitudes towards and perceptions of interprofessional teams [22, 23] and interprofessional learning [24], improved knowledge of each other's professions [25, 26] and better teamworking and communication skills [27].

In this How I do it paper, we describe the practical steps needed to organise and deliver hospital workplace‐based IPE involving RT students using an example of a New Zealand (NZ) hospital workplace‐based Cancer Care IPE learning activity involving radiation therapy students and including patients‐as‐teachers.

Context: The NZ Cancer Care IPE Programme

The development of the IPE learning activity originated in 2015/2016 after Palmerston North Hospital cancer care clinical staff sought guidance from the Otago University IPE Centre about developing a local IPE Programme for preregistration students on clinical placement. After a year of planning, in 2017, a pilot IPE programme involving 21 RT, dietetics, medicine, pharmacy and physiotherapy students and including patients‐as‐teachers commenced. It was independently evaluated at the time [28] looking at reactions to the IPE [28], and since then ongoing research has been undertaken on experiences of students and staff, and experiences of the patients involved [19, 28, 29, 30].

Since 2022, the IPE programme has been delivered twice a year when there are natural groupings of healthcare students of an increasing range of disciplines and institutions, mainly on clinical placement in the hospital [29]. Currently, there are five key IPE facilitators in the interprofessional teaching team (depending on discipline attendance, sometimes additional facilitators are used); a programme leader who receives a small stipend from Otago University (a radiation therapist who also has a teaching role), and three co‐facilitators (palliative medicine specialist, physiotherapy clinical tutor and nurse academic). A university‐based administrator provides remote support for the IPE learning activity.

In our experience, the following steps are needed to set up and implement hospital workplace‐based IPE involving RT students using the Cancer Care IPE as an example (see Figure 1).

How I Do It: Step One—Gaining Interest in IPE and Getting Started

Setting up and delivering IPE is not a solo activity and needs a team of interested parties who are prepared to be partners (clinical teacher or educator, training institution, hospital clinical placement provider). It takes longer than you think to set up IPE and obtain funding, so be patient and be prepared for challenges that take time to overcome. Resist rushing the process of gathering partner disciplines, as each is needed and trust with each must be built. Allow a year's lead‐in time to develop these relationships and to start having preparatory meetings.

It is important to train the IPE facilitators [31, 32, 33, 34]. Arrange for those who want to take part to first watch another IPE learning activity (this can be undertaken via Zoom or similar). It is facilitation skills they need to develop (the ability to ask questions that stimulate inclusive discussion) [35], not learning about topic content as this will be familiar to them. Once trained together, IPE facilitators become committed to IPE and will return year on year.

Try to keep costs of the IPE learning activity to a minimum as this may act as a deterrent. The hospital or a partner university may have a small education fund or a grant from a philanthropic organisation such as a cancer society to pay a stipend to the programme leader, cover the administration required (the university partner may do this) and the modest cost of food for the first session and thank you cards. IPE facilitators will often contribute their time as goodwill.

How I Do It: Step Two—Set Achievable Learning Outcomes for the IPE Learning Activity

Once you have identified partners to facilitate the IPE learning activity, arrange to meet. Keep minutes of key decisions and the reasons for making them. Develop a small number of learning outcomes aligned to recognised interprofessional competencies and then develop a learning activity (see Table 1) [36, 37].

How I Do It: Step Three—Develop the Learning Activity and Identify the Students

Once you have decided the learning outcomes, devise the learning activity for the IPE. The aim of a learning activity is to enable students to test and develop ideas about how to interprofessionally collaborate for optimal patient care. The Cancer Care IPE learning activity is 8–10 h of interprofessional learning spread over 8 days and fits around existing clinical placement requirements. It follows a four‐part design where students: (1) attend the first workshop to form connections, meet their small group, share food, engage in topic content; (2) interview a patient‐as‐teacher in their small group; (3) compile a presentation about the patient in their small group; (4) attend the second workshop where small group presentations are delivered (see Figure 2). We have found this model to be effective in other IPE activities [26, 38], as it utilises both social constructivism and experiential learning theory [39]. The timing of the components can be adjusted according to the time available.

The components of the Cancer Care IPE learning activity.

Once the IPE learning activity has been developed, identify which students and disciplines could take part. They are likely on clinical placements in the hospital, or some may be studying in training providers nearby. Different approaches will be needed to recruit students, such as through their training institution or hospital clinical placement tutor; sometimes ‘cold calling’ is needed.

Carefully consider the stage of learning of each discipline selected to participate in the IPE. Generally speaking, they should be relatively equivalent in their stage of learning (beginning, middle or senior), even though the number of years in each programme may be different. The Cancer Care IPE programme includes mainly final‐year students from RT, dietetics, medicine, nursing, pharmacy, physiotherapy, psychology, speech language therapy and social work. Between 20 and 30 students participate, and the range, mix and number of students varies according to the time of year and hospital clinical placement timetable. In our experience, the Cancer Care IPE works best for senior students (final or almost final year) as they can more confidently express their professional identity, cope with meeting people with cancer, see the need for interprofessional collaboration and more easily envisage how to work with other disciplines. Irrespective of the stage of learning, students may or may not have taken part in previous IPE learning activities, and for some it is their first experience in interprofessional learning.

How I Do It: Step Four—Prepare the Learning Environment

Prepare the learning environment for students so they are set up to succeed. There are several elements to consider including before, during and after the learning activity. Some of these are pragmatic time‐sensitive steps (booking rooms) whereas others need careful thought (developing learning outcomes, authentic meaningful assessment, recruiting patients). Research shows that patients are increasingly being asked to give feedback on services and cocreate changes as well as engage in education with staff and students [40, 41]. In this IPE programme very few patients decline to be interviewed. They are aware that students need to learn and see it as an opportunity to enhance their skills. Mainly final‐year students undertake this learning activity in small groups without direct supervision. Students are reminded about the skills of communicating with patients with cancer at the first workshop and encouraged to develop their questions according to the learning outcomes and remembering they are part of an interprofessional team with different skill‐sets (see Table 2).

How I Do It: Step Five—Establish a Manageable Annual Cycle of the Learning Activity

Ensuring that the IPE learning activity is sustainable and does not tax the IPE facilitator team is important as most will be taking part on goodwill. After the first year of running the IPE, the team will be able to gauge the tasks that need to be done and what time in the year they are best undertaken. An administrator can support this cycle once established.

Towards the end of each year, establish the dates when the IPE could be run; this will depend on each training provider's semester dates, which can differ between providers, as well as the availability of facilitators. Often, there are very few dates when there is a natural group of students from different disciplines available in the hospital. Depending on the training institutions' requirements, students can be required to attend as part of their programme of study or be asked to volunteer, or there may be a ‘mix’ in the same class [48, 49]. There are pros and cons in having volunteers (a larger and more disciplinary‐diverse class but unable to determine the size in advance) or a ‘mix’ (the assessment will not ‘count’ for volunteers). Irrespective of how the students are enlisted, it has not affected the overall enthusiasm for the Cancer Care IPE learning activity because of the novel topic, and for students from other institutions, this may be their first opportunity for IPE.

In our experience distributing the work between the academic programme leader and an IPE administrator spreads the load. The academic programme leader liaises with hospital clinical placement staff and training institutions, books the teaching space, oversees the content and delivery of the IPE, calls together facilitators to meet and undertake planning, allocates contributions to the student learning and recruits the patients. The administrator manages, updates and enrols the students onto the e‐learning platform, updates the material on the platform, communicates with and deals with student enquiries, sets up and collates the e‐survey evaluations.

How I Do It: Step Six—Use Evaluation Data to Quality Improve and/or Research the IPE

Quality assurance evaluation data to assess students' satisfaction and learning outcomes can be routinely gathered by e‐surveys which usually do not need ethical consent. Research evaluation data can be gathered from patients, students and facilitators through electronic surveys and/or focus groups or interviews after gaining ethical approval and using an informed consent process. Consider embedding research into the IPE learning activity to enable publication of the initiative so that others can learn from your experience and thus build the evidence base [19, 28, 29, 30]. This is important as IPE is a developing pedagogy and, as stated above, examples of hospital workplace‐based IPE including RT students are limited.

How the Cancer Care IPE Learning Activity Has Been Received and Modified

Feedback data shows that the IPE is highly valued by all involved. An e‐survey (Qualtrics) is undertaken each time the IPE is undertaken. As a result of this feedback and research (described in the subsequent section), the learning activity has undergone continuous quality improvement since 2017 (see Table 3).

Conclusion

Offering hospital workplace‐based IPE about Cancer Care to a range of students (including RT students) is advantageous to all participants, particularly when it includes patients receiving RT. It results in students learning about the role of RT and meeting RT students, gaining an understanding about interprofessional collaboration and having the privilege of hearing patients' stories of having cancer and receiving treatment. In this How I do it guide, we have not only outlined practical steps to plan and deliver workplace‐based IPE involving RT, but also shown that logistical challenges can be overcome by taking time when planning the IPE and building strong and trusted relationships with clinical and educator colleagues. As well as being useful for those wishing to implement a Cancer Care IPE involving RT students, the How I do it guide has relevance for educators in other settings looking to establish workplace IPE.
