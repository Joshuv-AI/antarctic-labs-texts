Peptidylarginine deiminase 4 is a differential regulator of salt-sensitive and salt-resistant hypertension
Authors: Xue Mei, Piu Saha, Blair Mell, Sareh Zeydabadinejad, Beng San Yeoh, Amira Gohara, Matam Vijay-Kumar, Bina Joe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeutrophils contribute to immune surveillance by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) through NETosis. While essential, dysregulated NETosis is linked to pathology. We examined its role in salt-sensitive versus salt-resistant hypertension using Dahl rat models. Hypertensive Dahl S rats, but not normotensive Dahl R rats, showed increased NETosis, higher neutrophil counts, and chronic low-grade inflammation. Inhibiting NETosis with DNase I or by the genetic deletion of peptidyl arginine deiminase (Pad4) exacerbated blood pressure (BP) in S rats, suggesting NETosis protects against salt-sensitive hypertension. Pad4 deletion also caused neutropenia, reduced reactive oxygen species, and exaggerated BP elevation under a high-salt diet. In contrast, Pad4 deletion in R rats lowered systolic BP regardless of diet, indicating NETosis supports BP homeostasis in normotension. These findings highlight the context-dependent role of NETosis in BP regulation, with NETs acting as a protective mechanism in salt-sensitive hypertension.

Introduction

Essential hypertension is the single largest modifiable risk factor for serious cardiovascular and renal disorders, and it remains a major health problem worldwide. According to the 2017 Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure (BP) in Adults by the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology, 130/80 mmHg rather than 140/90 mmHg is considered elevated arterial BP.1 Following this redefinition, over half of the US adult population now meets this criterion for hypertension.2 These staggering statistics call for new methods for abatement or better clinical management of hypertension.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is one of the most consistent biologic features of age-related diseases,3 including cardiovascular diseases.4 Chronic low-grade inflammation persists due to dysregulated immune responses contributed by both innate and adaptive arms of the host immune system. Neutrophils are components of innate immunity and are the most abundant white blood cells in circulation. They are the first responders to injury and inflammation.5 Timely neutrophil recruitment and function are required for the initiation and perpetuation of inflammation. As part of host innate immunity, neutrophils are specialized to combat microbial transgression through the release of antimicrobial factors, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) via NADPH oxidase and myeloperoxidase. In addition, neutrophils release their DNA as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) to ensnare and kill microbes.6 This process, called “NETosis,” is a form of programmed cell death which depends on the enzyme, peptidyl arginine deiminase-4 (Pad4). Pad4 removes the positive charge of histones by removing the -NH2 group from arginine residues and generates citrulline. This process facilitates the uncoiling of chromatin for the expulsion of DNA as NETs.7 NETs are tightly regulated by their eventual degradation by the constitutively expressed enzyme deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) in circulation. Dysregulated, and thereby excessive NETs, have gained significant infamy due to their pathologic roles associated with delayed wound healing,6 obstructive pulmonary disease,8,9 age-related organ fibrosis,10 atherosclerosis,11 gallstone formation,12 intracranial aneurysm rupture,13 various cancers,14,15,16,17 and hypertension.18 In these contexts, NETs are typically associated with promoting inflammation. However, emerging evidence also suggests that NETosis helps to resolve inflammation19 by various mechanisms. For example, NETs-derived citrullinated histones may act as signals to shift immune responses toward resolution rather than persistence. Secondly, NETs can bind and neutralize inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby reducing their bioavailability and limiting their inflammatory effects.20 NETs are also demonstrated to mitigate inflammation and prevent overactivation of the immune system in experimental models of Klebsiella pneumoniae.21 Taken together, these data suggest that NETs have a paradoxical role depending on the context and balance between their generation, function, and clearance.

Here, we question the function of NETs in the context of BP regulation. Under normal physiological conditions with no underlying low-grade inflammation, activation of neutrophils and NETosis is unnecessary and required to remain quiescent. However, as BP elevates to result in hypertension, persistent low-grade inflammation sets in, thereby requiring neutrophilia and NETosis to be continually active. If this is indeed the case, we hypothesized that the ablation of NETs in a normotensive rat would lower BP, whereas the ablation of NETs in a hypertensive rat would further increase hypertension. To test this hypothesis, here we present a first comparative study of the extent of low-grade inflammation and NETs in two genetic models of hypertension, the hypertensive salt-sensitive Dahl S rat and the normotensive salt-resistant Dahl R rat. Next, we examined their BP by modulating NETs using both pharmacologic and genetic approaches to suppress NETs. The findings presented support our hypothesis that, depending on the context, NETs play a paradoxical role. While NETs are crucial for preventing salt-sensitive hypertension, they preserve salt-resistant BP homeostasis.

Results

Ablation of NETosis by exogenous DNase I treatment exacerbates BP in hypertensive S, but not normotensive R rats

To evaluate whether elevated NETs in the hypertensive S rats were a pathological or protective response, we first utilized a pharmacological approach of treating rats with DNase I as shown in Figure 2A, a constitutively expressed enzyme required for the degradation of NETs.26 Administration of DNase I to normotensive R rats had no impact on systolic, diastolic BP and mean arterial pressure (MAP) as noted by a lack of change in BP compared to control R rats (Figures 2B–2D). In contrast, administration of DNase I increased the systolic BP and MAP in Dahl S rats, with no significant differences observed in diastolic BP (Figures 2E–2G). Analysis of circadian BP data is presented as daytime BP recordings shown in yellow and nighttime recordings shown in gray (Figures 2B–2G) for both R and S rats in the vehicle control and DNase I-treated groups. A cosinor analysis was performed to appropriately assess circadian BP variation. These data suggested that in hypertensive S rats, NETs may serve as a compensatory or adaptive function to thwart the progression of hypertension. To confirm the proinflammatory response after DNase I treatment, we measured serum Lcn2 as well as kidney Lcn2, IL-6, and IL-17 levels in vehicle and DNase I-treated R and S rats. In S rats, both vehicle and DNase I treatments resulted in increased serum Lcn2 levels. CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing of the Pad4 locus using CRISPR/Cas9 in the salt-sensitive Dahl S rat

Next, to further examine the potential adaptive/compensatory response of NETosis in the hypertensive S rat, we used a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genetic approach by targeting Pad4. Pad4 is essential for the formation of NETs via PAD4-mediated histone citrullination. Genetic disruption of PAD4 abrogates the formation of these NETs.27 Guide RNAs were designed to target and disrupt exon 2 of the Pad4 locus located on rat chromosome 5 (RNO5) and injected into single-cell embryos. Genomic sequence data of this homozygous founder showed a deletion of 85 bp (Figure 3A). Genotyping revealed a deletion PCR product of 293 bp in the founder SPad4−/− rats versus the wild-type PCR product of 378 bp (Figure 3B). The predicted amino acid sequence of Pad4 in the SPad4−/− rats contained a 578 amino acid deletion (Figure 3C), which drastically changed the predicted protein structure of Pad4 in the SPad4−/− rats (Figure 3D). When compared to S, SPad4−/− rats displayed a significantly reduced NETs formation measured by SYTOX Green (Figure 4B) in low salt-fed conditions. Neutrophils from SPad4−/− rats displayed minimal H3Cit positivity (Figure 4C), indicating less NETosis in SPad4−/− neutrophils. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio has recently emerged as a well-validated biomarker in predicting individuals at risk of developing hypertension.30,31,32 We noticed a deficiency of NETs induced low-grade inflammation in S rats as observed by their renomegaly in SPad4−/− rats, though no difference was observed in spleen weight (Figures S4A–S4C). We interpret the selective increase in kidney weight as a potential indicator of low-grade, tissue-specific renal inflammation or remodeling in the absence of NET formation. The lack of splenic enlargement suggests that PAD4 deficiency does not broadly affect systemic lymphoid organ morphology but may instead preferentially influence inflammatory processes within the kidney. To assess kidney inflammation, we measured IL-6 and IL-17 levels in kidney lysates; however, no significant differences were observed in renal IL-6 or IL-17 levels between S and SPad4−/− rats maintained on a low-salt diet (Figures S4D and S4E). Next, we asked whether the deficiency of NETs in SPad4−/− rats has any impact on their circulating immune cell population, primarily in neutrophil numbers. Interestingly, SPad4−/− rats displayed reduced neutrophil numbers when compared to S rats (Figures 4F and 4G). The neutrophil-to-lymphocytes ratio was lowered in SPad4−/− rats (Figure 4H). Additionally, SPad4−/− rats displayed a reduced intracellular ROS generation measured by flow cytometry (Figure 4I), indicating that ROS generation and NET formation were both impaired in SPad4−/− rats. Serum DNase I activity reflects the capacity to degrade extracellular DNA, including cell-free and NET-derived DNA, in the circulation. Therefore, we measured serum DNase I activity in both S and SPad4−/− rats. We observed a trend toward increased serum DNase I activity in SPad4−/− rats (Figures 4J and 4K) compared with S rats.

NETosis is crucial to protect S rats from salt-sensitive hypertension

Next, to determine whether the reduction in NETs formation in the SPad4−/− rats impacted BP, we measured BP in male rats using radiotelemetry. As seen in Figures 5A–5C, systolic, diastolic, and MAPs of the SPad4−/− were significantly higher compared to S control rats. To determine if this BP-increasing effect was independent of sex, we monitored the BP of female SPad4−/− rats. The above studies were conducted with S rats maintained on a low-salt (0.3% NaCl) diet. However, the S rats are genetically selected to further escalate their BP in response to a dietary salt challenge.33 Chronic exposure to a high-salt diet has been linked to heightened low-grade inflammation. Therefore, we asked if NETosis was essential to prevent or promote dietary high salt-induced hypertension in the S rat. To address this, both SPad4−/− and S rats were maintained on a high salt diet (2% NaCl diet) for 24 days prior to examining their BP. Compared to S and SPad4−/− fed with a low salt diet, the systolic, diastolic, and MAPs of the high salt-fed S and SPad4−/− rats were elevated (Figures 5A–5C). Importantly, the magnitude of BP elevation in SPad4−/− rats was significantly higher than that of the S rats maintained on a high salt diet, indicating that NETosis is critical for preventing salt-sensitive hypertension of the S rat.

High-salt fed SPad4−/− rats exhibit neutrophilia and reduced NETs and ROS

SPad4−/− rats on a high-salt diet exhibited splenomegaly and increased kidney weight, indicating that the SPad4−/− rats were perhaps suffering from increased low-grade chronic inflammation and renal inflammation (Figures S6A–S6C). The inflammatory response was evaluated by quantifying IL-6 and IL-17 levels in kidney lysates. No significant differences in renal IL-6 or IL-17 expressions were detected between S and SPad4−/− rats maintained on a high-salt diet (Figures S6D and S6E). Next, we asked whether such enhanced chronic inflammation in SPad4−/− rats was associated with any altered immune responses related to neutrophil function. Indeed, the high-salt fed SPad4−/− rats demonstrated nearly ∼1.4-fold higher neutrophil than high-salt fed control S rats (Figure 5D, represented as absolute numbers). In addition, we observed a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in high-salt fed SPad4−/− rats compared to high-salt fed control S rats (Figure 5E). However, despite the increased neutrophil count, SPad4−/− rats displayed a significant reduction in the formation of NETs and a substantial decrease in cfDNA, indicative of reduced NETosis, whereby lesser DNA leaked from netotic cells in SPad4−/− (Figures 5F and 5G). In addition, we observed reduced intracellular ROS generation by the neutrophils of SPad4−/− rats, which aligned with their impaired NETosis and enhanced susceptibility to chronic low-grade inflammation (Figure 5H). Collectively, these data suggest that it is not just neutrophil abundance but their ability to form NETs that play a regulatory role in salt-sensitive hypertension. This implies that the production of NETs is an active physiological process contributing to BP homeostasis rather than simply being a byproduct of neutrophil activation via NETosis.

To investigate the impact of elevated BP on systemic inflammation and end-organ injury in high-salt-fed SPad4−/− rats, we assessed serum levels of Lcn2, 24-h urinary protein excretion, and renal malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Our results demonstrated that high-salt fed SPad4−/− rats exhibited increased serum Lcn2, elevated 24-h urinary protein levels (Figures 5I and 5J), and no difference in renal MDA concentrations compared to high-salt fed S rats (Figure 5K). These findings indicate that NETs’ deficiency under high-salt conditions exacerbates systemic inflammation and perhaps kidney function associated with BP, as often seen with S rats. Moreover, serum DNase I activity was significantly increased in high-salt fed SPad4−/− rats, suggesting a potential compensatory mechanism to counteract inflammation in the setting of NET deficiency (Figures 5L and 5M).

To evaluate kidney injury and inflammation, histological analyses were performed on kidney sections from S and SPad4−/− rats using H&E staining. Histological scoring assessed the presence of protein casts, inflammatory cell infiltration, focal tubular dilation, and intratubular hemorrhage. However, no significant differences were observed between S and SPad4−/− rats under either low-salt or high-salt conditions (Figures S7A–S7C).

CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing of the Pad4 locus using CRISPR/Cas9 in the normotensive salt-resistant R rat

Having demonstrated that NETs are important for regulating salt-sensitive hypertension, we asked if NETs have any role in regulating BP in normotensive R rats. To address this, we chose to delete the Pad4 locus in the R rat, which is a normotensive strain and a genetic counterpart of the S rat. Using the CRISPR/Cas9 technology with the same guide RNA as used for the S rat, we developed RPad4−/− rats. The resultant RPad4−/− rats had a shorter genomic fragment around exon 2, which was confirmed by sequencing as a 164 base pair deletion in its genomic DNA (Figures 6A and 6B). This genome editing resulted in a dramatic alteration in the predicted protein structure of Pad4 from 666 amino acids to only 50 amino acids in the RPad4−/− rats (Figures 6C and 6D). Disruption of Pad4 in normotensive R rats decreases BP

In contrast to hypertensive S rats, wherein genetic disruption of the Pad4 locus increased BP, genetic disruption of the Pad4 locus decreased BP of the normotensive R rat. Specifically, systolic and MAPs of the RPad4−/− rats were significantly lower compared to R rats (Figures 7A and 7C), with no change observed in diastolic BP (Figure 7B). Similar to male rats, the systolic BP of female rats was significantly lower compared to R rats (Figure S8A) with no effect on diastolic BP (Figure S8B). Since the R rat is a salt-resistant normotensive strain, we reasoned that ameliorating NETosis would have the same effect as it did in low salt-fed RPad4−/− rats. To ascertain this, we measured BP in RPad4−/− rats maintained on a high (2% NaCl) salt diet for 24 days. Like the data obtained from low salt-fed rats, the systolic and MAPs (Figures 7A and 7C), but not the diastolic BP of the RPad4−/− on a high-salt diet, were significantly lower compared to R rats (Figure 7B). These results demonstrate that, irrespective of dietary salt consumption, NETosis has a role in maintaining BP homeostasis of R rats.

High-salt fed RPad4−/− rats do exhibit higher ROS, but not NETs

To address why the ablation of NETs lowers BP in both low and high-salt-fed R rats, we investigated whether low or high-salt-fed R rats differ in their ability to generate NETs. We found that high-salt fed R and RPad4−/− rats displayed comparable neutrophil counts. However, an increase in ROS production was observed in high-salt RPad4−/− rats’ neutrophils in the presence of PMA compared to R rats’ neutrophils. However, in response to a dietary salt challenge, there was no significant difference in NET formation between R and RPad4−/− rats’ neutrophils (Figures 7D–7F). Moreover, serum DNase I activity was slightly decreased in high-salt fed RPad4−/− rats (Figures 7G and 7H), suggesting that high salt intake may impair systemic DNase I-mediated DNA degradation in RPad4−/− rats. Kidney inflammation was evaluated by quantifying IL-6 and IL-17 levels in kidney lysates; however, no significant differences were observed in renal IL-6 or IL-17 levels between R and RPad4−/− rats maintained on a high-salt diet (Figures S9A and S9B).

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a significant role for NETosis in regulating BP homeostasis, particularly in the setting of salt-sensitive hypertension. The elevated levels of the inflammatory marker, Lcn2, indicate the presence of chronic low-grade inflammation in S rats, which is accompanied by neutrophilia and enhanced NETs formation. The suppression of NETs by DNase I treatment led to an increase in BP in hypertensive S rats, suggesting that NETosis may contribute to mitigating hypertension in this strain. However, DNase I may have exerted effects beyond NETs degradation, so these results should be viewed as supportive rather than conclusive evidence of a NETs-specific role in BP regulation. In contrast, DNase I treatment had no impact on BP in the normotensive R rat, suggesting distinct regulatory mechanism(s) for NETs in normotensive versus hypertensive conditions.

The use of CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to disrupt Pad4, a key enzyme required to generate NETs, provided further insights into the functional relevance of NETs.7 The genetic ablation of Pad4 in S rats resulted in a deficiency of NETosis as evidenced by reduced NET formation and decreased ROS production. Notably, ablation of NETs in SPad4−/− rats resulted in increased BP, indicating that NETosis could contribute to the mitigation of salt-sensitive hypertension. The effect was independent of sex differences, as both male and female SPad4−/− rats exhibited elevated BP compared to their S counterparts.

An exciting finding of this study is that a high-salt diet further exacerbated the BP increase in SPad4−/− rats. Despite developing neutrophilia, SPad4−/− rats displayed reduced NETs formation and ROS production, highlighting the distinction between neutrophil abundance and their functional capacity to generate NETs. The heightened BP and inflammation observed in these rats suggest that NETosis may act as a compensatory mechanism to counteract the detrimental effects of chronic low-grade inflammation in hypertension.

In contrast to the hypertensive S rats, disruption of Pad4 in normotensive R rats led to a decrease in BP, reinforcing the notion that NETs play distinct roles in different genetic backgrounds. Unlike the hypertensive S rats, in which the loss of NETs led to an increase in BP, NETs may contribute to maintaining normal systolic BP homeostasis in R rats. The BP-lowering effect observed in RPad4−/− rats was independent of sex and persisted under both low and high dietary salt conditions, indicating that NETosis is a fundamental regulator of BP stability in normotensive strains. Interestingly, while RPad4−/− rats exhibited increased ROS production, their capacity to generate NETs remained unchanged, even under high salt conditions. This observation suggests that, unlike in hypertensive S rats, NETs may not be a critical response mechanism to salt intake in normotensive strains.

Recent studies have highlighted the complex role of NETs. For instance, NETs contribute to endothelial cell dysfunction and vascular inflammation, leading to elevated BP.18 Inhibition of NETs in an AngII-infused mouse model of hypertension is shown to attenuate the increase in BP and improve vascular function.18 NETs are also associated with vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, contributing to vascular remodeling in hypertension.34 The release of ROS during NETosis can induce oxidative stress, further exacerbating vascular damage and BP elevation.35 Conversely, other studies suggest that NETs may have protective roles in BP regulation.36 Discrepancies among these studies likely reflect differences in animal models (mouse vs. rat), baseline inflammatory state, and experimental variables such as the mode of NET inhibition (pharmacologic inhibition vs. genetic Pad4 deletion), which may variably affect NET formation, duration, and immune responses. Using both salt-sensitive and salt-resistant models of hypertension, our study clearly demonstrates this dual role of NETs and provides evidence for the function of NETs as being context-dependent, influenced by factors such as genetic predisposition and inflammatory status. We chose to use three complementary methods to measure NETs because no single assay is fully specific or sufficient on its own. SYTOX Green fluorescence, H3Cit immunofluorescence, and circulating cfDNA each capture different aspects of NET formation but have distinct limitations regarding specificity and sensitivity. By combining these approaches, we aimed to cross-validate NET measurements, reduce method-specific bias, and provide a more robust and reliable assessment of NETosis than any single technique alone.

We acknowledge that our current study does not fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms. While sex-specific immune and hormonal responses may contribute to these differences, additional targeted experiments are needed to clarify these observations. A limitation of this study is that not all parameters were measured in both male and female rats. Therefore, our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding sex-specific differences is limited. Future studies in both sexes will be necessary to fully evaluate potential sex-dependent results.

In summary, while NETosis plays a significant role in hypertension, its effects are multifaceted. NETs can both promote and mitigate hypertension, highlighting the complexity of their involvement. As such, therapeutic strategies targeting NETosis must consider individual genetic predisposition and inflammatory status. Our findings suggest a protective role for NETosis in salt-sensitive hypertension and its contribution to maintaining the normotensive condition. However, the results are indeed primarily descriptive. Although genetic and pharmacological manipulation of NETosis pathways provides important functional insights, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Specifically, the molecular mediators, signaling, and cellular interactions through which NETosis influences vascular function and immune responses in hypertensive versus normotensive conditions are yet to be fully elucidated. Future studies aimed at uncovering these mechanistic pathways will be essential to advance our understanding of NETosis in BP homeostasis and pathogenesis.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study is that, while the findings indicate a definite dichotomy of the relationship between BP and NETosis in the hypertensive and normotensive setting, mechanistic insight is incomplete. Also, we acknowledge that while SYTOX Green fluorescence, H3Cit immunofluorescence, and circulating cfDNA measurements were combined to strengthen the assessment of NET formation, each method has inherent limitations in specificity and sensitivity, and none provides a definitive quantification of NETosis in vivo. Furthermore, although genetic and pharmacologic modulation of NET pathways offered functional support, direct protein-level validation was technically constrained. Specifically, reliable detection of PAD4 protein by western blot was not feasible due to inadequate antibody performance in rat tissue. As a result, we present the most robust and reproducible data (including mRNA expression analyses) while acknowledging the absence of definitive protein-level confirmation. In addition, not all parameters were evaluated in both male and female rats, restricting conclusions regarding sex-specific effects. Finally, the molecular mediators and signaling pathways linking NETosis to vascular dysfunction, immune modulation, and BP regulation remain to be elucidated. Future studies incorporating improved molecular tools and comprehensive mechanistic interrogation will be necessary to define the causal pathways through which NETs influence context-dependent discrepancies in BP regulation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Targeted disruption of Pad4 using CRISPR/Cas9 and screening for homozygous Pad4mutant rats

The targeted disruption model was generated at the University of Michigan Transgenic Animal Model Core (Ann Arbor, MI) as previously described.37 Briefly, guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed to target the locus within exon 2 on Pad4. Oocyte microinjections were performed in one-cell stage S or R rat embryos. Microinjected embryos were implanted into pseudo-pregnant Sprague-Dawley female rats. The forward (F) and reverse (R) sequences for 2 primer sets (1 and 2): 1-F: CAACGTTACCAGATTCACTCTCTG; 1-R: CAGCAGTAGGATAGATGACCAGAA; 2-F: ATCTCATCATGGGTGCCTCAG; 2-R CTGCATCCCCTTGAACATTC were used for genotyping potential founders. Once the potential founders were bred to homozygosity, the PCR products of the genotyping from the homozygotes were sent for sequencing validation (Eurofins Genomics, https://eurofinsgenomics.com/en/home/) and sequencing data was analyzed using Sequencher 5.4.6. The homozygotes of targeted disruption were used for subsequent phenotypic studies.

All animal procedures described were approved by the University of Toledo’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The inbred Dahl salt-sensitive/Jr (S) and Dahl salt-resistant/Jr (R) rat strains were from stocks maintained in our animal facility at our institution (RRID numbers: RRID:RGD 724573 for the Dahl S rat and RRID:RGD 70453 for the Dahl R rat). Each set of wild-type S or R rats and the respective Pad4mutant rats were bred, housed, and studied concomitantly to minimize environmental effects. All rats were fed with 0.3% NaCl (low-salt) diet (Harlan Teklad 7034) and weaned at 28–30 days of age. For experiments involving high-salt, 2% NaCl diet (Harlan Teklad, TD 94217) started at 9 weeks of age and continued throughout the experiment.

Method details

Isolation of neutrophils from peripheral blood

Rat blood was collected via cardiac puncture into EDTA-coated tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Catalog No. 454428). 3 mL of histopaque 1119 (sigma) was layered in a 15 mL tube. 3 mL of histopaque 1077 (sigma) was layered slowly on top of histopaque 1119. Next, 6 mL of whole blood was carefully layered on top of the histopaque gradient (ratio must be 1: 1: 2). Next the gradient was centrifuged 700×g for 30 min at room temperature without break (setting both acceleration and deceleration to ∼1). The interface between two histopaque layers was collected in a new 15 mL tube. Then the cell suspension was washed twice with 1× sterile PBS (room temperature). The PMNs purity was determined by flow cytometry using leukocytes surface marker: BB700 Mouse Anti-Rat CD45 (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 742159), and granulocytes markers: APC Mouse Anti-Rat CD11b (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 562102) and PE Mouse Anti-Rat RP-1 Antigen (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 550002). Cells were gated based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC). Neutrophils were defined as CD45+ CD11b+ RP-1 Antigen+ and their purity was determined to be 90–92%. The neutrophil gating strategy was clarified in Figure S1.

Quantitative PCR analysis of Pad4 expression

Total RNA was extracted from neutrophils using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop. A total of 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Superscript III First-Strand reverse transcription kit with oligo(dT). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR master mix on a QuantStudio 3 using forward and reverse primers sequences. All reactions were run in technical triplicates. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene L36a ribosomal protein, and relative expression was calculated using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCq method. For L36a ribosomal protein: Primers used: F:5′-ATTGTGCTCAGGCTTGAGTGT-3′ and R: 5′-TGGATCACTTGACCCTTTCTC-3′, for Pad4 gene: primers used F: 5′-CGCTGGATATCCGTGGCTCT-3′ and R: 5′-TGCTGGCTGCTTTCATCTGT-3′.

Serum collection

Blood samples were collected into BD microtainers (BD Biosciences). Serum samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and hemolysis-free sera was collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Detection of lipocalin 2 as chronic low-grade inflammation

Serum Lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) was used as a marker for chronic low-grade inflammation. Lcn2 in fecal, serum and kidney samples were quantitated as described previously23,38 using the DuoSet enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from R&D Systems (cat# DY3508). Transcript levels of lcn2 were quantified in S and R rats kidney samples by real-time PCR.38,39

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in kidney samples

Kidney samples were processed quantitated as described previously23,38 using the DuoSet enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from R&D Systems for IL-6 and IL-17 (cat# DY506 and cat# DY8410 respectively). The cytokines level was normalized to total protein concentration.

Immunophenotyping in peripheral blood

Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia into EDTA-coated tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Catalog No. 454428). 3.0 mL of 1× RBC lysing solution (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 555899) was added to 300 μL of whole blood and gently vortexed immediately. The samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min and then immediately centrifuged at 200×g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated carefully without disturbing the pellet. The cell pellet was washed twice with 2.0 mL 1× PBS containing 1% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (PBS-FBS) at 200×g for 5 min. Then the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS-FBS (1 × 106 cells/100 μL). Single-cell suspension was stained with surface antibodies, BB700 Mouse Anti-Rat CD45 (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 742159), APC Mouse Anti-Rat CD11b (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 562102) and PE Mouse Anti-Rat RP-1 Antigen (BD Bioscience, Catalog No. 550002) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Following washing with 1× PBS, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Accuri c6; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Cells were gated for the percentages of CD11b+ RP-1+ cells (i.e., neutrophils) among the total CD45+ cells (i.e., all WBC populations). The final values, which are normalized to CD45 cells, are now reported in terms of absolute number of neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+RP-1+) per mL of blood.

Intracellular ROS assay

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified in ex vivo neutrophils isolated from peripheral blood using flow cytometry. Freshly isolated polymorphonuclear (PMN) cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were incubated for 3 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator in presence or absence of phorbol myristate acetate [(PMA, 50 nM, reactive oxygen species inducer, primarily through the activation of protein kinase C (PKC)]. After 3 h incubation, cells were washed with PBS and stained with 5 μM CellROX Deep Red dye (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark, followed by two additional PBS washes. Fluorescence was measured using an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (Becton Dickinson). Intracellular ROS levels are presented as fold change in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) relative to untreated controls.

Quantification of NETs

SYTOX Green (5.0 μM, a cell-impermeable DNA dye that binds to extracellular DNA and is commonly used to quantify NET formation)40 was added to the PMNs at the initial stage of Ca2+ ionophore [A23187 (Sigma-Aldrich), a well-established compound known to induce NET formation by increasing intracellular calcium levels] stimulation described above.41 The plates were read in a Spectra MAX Gemini fluorescence microplate reader (Molecular Devices; Sunnyvale, CA) with a filter setting of 485 (excitation)/527 (emission). The data were presented as fold change of NETs formation normalized to the controls.

NETs staining

To visualize NETs, the PMNs (5×105/wells) were seeded in 8-wells glass-chambered slides (Lab-TekII) in incomplete RPMI media. Cells were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde for 10 min and stained with anti-histone H3 (citrulline R2 + R8 + R17) antibody (Abcam) overnight at 4°C and using H3-Cit (a NET marker) and Fluoroshield with DAPI (Sigma) for 5 min H3Cit+/DAPI+ (co-stained) cells were imaged. Fluorescent microscopic images were acquired at both 4× and 10× magnifications to demonstrate clear colocalization on Olympus FV10i – LIV confocal microscope. NET quantification using ImageJ was performed by analyzing 10–15 randomly selected, non-overlapping fields per sample to capture representative variability. Quantitative analysis was conducted across these fields, and the average number of H3Cit+/DAPI+ cells per field was calculated for each sample.

Circulating cell-free DNA

Serum cfDNA was quantified by the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol28 as mentioned before. The within-batch CV was 9.2.

DNase I activity

Serum DNase I activity was quantified using the Single Radial Enzyme Diffusion (SRED) assay as previously described.28 Briefly, 5 μL of rat serum was applied to 1.0% agarose gels containing calf thymus DNA (0.2 mg/mL) intercalated with ethidium bromide (0.01 mg/mL), supplemented with magnesium chloride (20 mM) and calcium chloride (2 mM), and cast in Petri dishes. DNase I mediated degradation of DNA produced a zone of clearance (halo) within the gel. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 16 h, after which halo radii were measured using ImageJ software. Halo radius was directly proportional to DNase I activity. DNase I concentrations were calculated using a standard curve generated with known concentrations of bovine DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

DNase I administration

Twelve weeks old S and R male rats were fed a 2% NaCl diet for 3 weeks. DNase I (dornase alfa, Genentech) was administered at 100 μg/200 g body weight by intraperitoneal injection to S (n = 7) and R (n = 6–7) rats and control rats were given vehicle control (8.77 mg/mL NaCl and 0.15 mg/mL CaCl2).6 DNase I or vehicle control was administered twice a week, and BP was recorded for 24–72 h immediately following the injections by telemetry.

Blood pressure measurements by radiotelemetry

Blood pressure (BP) was recorded and analyzed using software from Data Sciences International, (St. Paul, MN) as described previously.42 Briefly, Pad4mutant and the respective wild-type control (S or R) were surgically implanted with HD-S10 radio-telemetry transmitters for males and HD-X10 for females. The body of the transmitter was placed into the left flank, and the catheter was inserted into their femoral artery all the way into the lower abdominal aorta. Rats were allowed to recover from surgery for 3 days before the transmitters were turned on for recording BP.

Urinary protein measurement

Rats were placed in metabolic cages individually for 24-h urine collection. Rats were fast during the 24-h period but did have access to drinking water. Pyrogallol based QuanTtest Red Total Protein Assay from Quantimetrix (Redondo Beach, CA) was used to quantify total urinary protein and presented as 24-h urinary protein (mg).

Lipid peroxidation assay (MDA determination)

Approximately 80–100 mg of kidney tissue was weighed and homogenized in 1 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) using a tissue homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. For the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay, 1 mL of 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) prepared in 20% TCA was added. Subsequently, 400 μL of the sample supernatant was added. For the blank, 400 μL of 0.1% TCA was used instead of the sample. All tubes were incubated in a water bath at 80°C for 30 min, then immediately transferred to ice and kept for approximately 5 min. Samples were then centrifuged again at 13,000×g for 5 min at 4°C, and 200 μL of the resulting supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microplate. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm using a microplate reader. The concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) was calculated using the following formula:nmolMDA/gFW=ΔAcorrected×3.5×X×1000ε×b×YWhere: ΔA corrected = A532 − A600, corrected by the blank; ε = 155 mM−1 cm−1 (molar extinction coefficient of MDA-TBA complex); b = 0.56 cm (path length for 200 μL in 96-well plate); X = 1 mL (volume of TCA used for extraction); Y = fresh weight (FW) of the tissue in grams; 3.5 = dilution factor (from 400 μL of extract +1 mL of TBA/TCA solution); 1000 = unit conversion factor (μmol to nmol).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses for significance between two groups were determined using Student’s t test (unpaired, two-tailed) with ∗p < 0.05 considered as significant. Statistical analysis for significance between the means of three or more groups was performed using One-way ANOVA with pairwise multiple comparisons by Tukey post hoc test. For blood pressure analysis, repeated measures two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey multiple comparison test was the primary analysis used to compare data between the groups at each time point. The “overall BP average” was analyzed using unpaired t test with Welch’s correction to account for variances in sample size and SEM and does not replace the primary repeated-measures analysis. A cosinor analysis was performed to assess circadian BP variation. All statistical analyses were performed with the GraphPad Prism 9.0 program (GraphPad Software, Inc, La Jolla, CA).
