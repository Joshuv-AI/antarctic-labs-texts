Allocentric spatial representations dominate when switching between real and virtual worlds
Authors: Meaghan McManus, Franziska Seifert, Immo Schütz, Katja Fiehler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
After removing a virtual reality headset, people can be surprised to find that they are facing a different direction than expected. Here, we investigated if people can maintain spatial representations of one environment while immersed in another. In the first three experiments, stationary participants were asked to point to previously seen targets in one environment, either the real world or a virtual environment, while in the other environment. We varied the amount of misalignment between the two environments (detectable or undetectable), the virtual environment itself (lab or kitchen), and the instructions (general or egocentric priming). Pointing endpoints were based primarily on the locations of objects in the currently seen environment, suggesting a strong reliance on allocentric cues. In the fourth experiment, participants moved in virtual reality while keeping track of an unseen real-world target. We confirmed that the pointing errors were due to a reliance on the currently seen environment. It appears that people hardly ever keep track of object positions in a previously seen environment and instead primarily rely on currently available spatial information to plan their actions.

Introduction

In order to interact with objects around us, we need to have an understanding of their location in relation to us and to the other objects in the environment. To this end, two types of spatial reference frames can be used (Figure 1): an allocentric frame of reference where the spatial properties of an object are determined in relation to other objects (e.g., landmarks) (Fiehler, Wolf, Klinghammer, & Blohm, 2014) or larger environmental structures such as room corners and wall textures (Cheng, 1986; Fiset & Malenfant, 2013), and an egocentric frame of reference where the location of objects are determined in relation to the observer's position (Andersen, Snyder, Li, & Stricanne, 1993; Colby, 1998; Forster, Fiehler, & Karimpur, 2023). Humans usually use both types of reference frames (Forster et al., 2023; Thompson & Henriques, 2010), which might be combined based on task relevance (Thompson & Henriques, 2010) or the reliability of sensory information (Neely, Tessmer, Binsted, & Heath, 2008).

Spatial reference frames. (A) A person wearing a VR headset wondering where their coffee cup is. (B) The location of the coffee cup is provided both egocentrically (left of the person) and allocentrically (next to the plate).

In the real world, we are surrounded by multiple objects that can function as potential landmarks for a given target object. Which of the objects are used depends on a series of low-level and high-level cognitive factors (for a review on the topic, see Fiehler & Karimpur, 2022). To probe allocentric coding Fiehler and colleagues (2014) established an object-shift paradigm in which participants first encoded the locations of multiple objects in a naturalistic scene. After a delay, one of the objects was hidden from view and the remaining objects were shifted imperceptibly. Participants then had to reach toward the remembered location of the hidden object. The results showed that the participants’ reaching endpoints deviated in the direction of the imperceptible shift, indicating the use of allocentric information for guiding actions. To quantify the influence of the imperceptible shift on the reaching endpoints, allocentric weights (how much the allocentric cues influenced reach endpoints) were calculated and were found to be around 0.5. This suggests that other factors might also have contributed to the reach errors, such as other stable objects in the environment or egocentric (e.g., gaze-centered) information. Similar allocentric weights were found in monitor-based as well as virtual reality (VR) experiments for lateral shifts and shifts in depth (Klinghammer, Blohm, & Fiehler, 2015; Klinghammer, Schütz, Blohm, & Fiehler, 2016). These results show that representations of remembered objects can be influenced by our currently visible environment.

When performing an action such as making a sandwich, people are able to make movements to locations outside their field of view (Land, 2009; Land, Mennie, & Rusted, 1999). These movements are guided by the remembered representation of the larger unseen space. Objects in this unseen space might be remembered egocentrically (“the fridge is behind me”) or allocentrically (“the jam is in the fridge”). As we move around the room we need to update the egocentric locations of the unseen objects. These unseen locations, however, are all within the same environment the sandwich maker currently occupies. What if we need to keep track of object locations in an unseen environment while occupying another environment (e.g., while navigating through a mall, one might need to keep track of the location of the bus stop outside). In order to orient successfully to an unseen location, we need to have a mental representation of the location (the bus stop) with respect to ourselves (egocentrically) as well as other landmarks (allocentrically). It has been found that, when pointing to the locations of previously seen objects in our environment while blindfolded, we are fairly accurate (Wan, Wang, & Crowell, 2009); however, when asked to point to external locations such as familiar nearby buildings, accuracy decreases to the point of being random (Wang & Brockmole, 2003). If we then exit the room we are in and walk around the other external space, we can no longer orient an image of the initial room with respect to our new position, suggesting that when we orient to one environment we lose track of our position relative to the other environment (Wang & Brockmole, 2003).

Similarly, when removing a VR headset, it is not unusual for the wearer to realize that they are facing a completely different direction in the real world (RW) than they expected. They may have started the VR game facing the couch but might now be standing directly in front of a wall. This surprising phenomenon leads to the main question of this article: Can people maintain and update an accurate spatial representation of one environment (e.g., the real world) while being immersed in another environment (e.g., a virtual environment)? With the increasing use of VR in gaming, conferencing, and research, it is important to understand how well people can maintain a spatial representation of one environment, such as the real world, while they are immersed in VR, where risks include walking into RW walls and knocking over objects.

Previous work shows that people can maintain multiple spatial representations of one environment, including differing egocentric and allocentric representations (Carrozzo, Stratta, McIntyre, & Lacquaniti, 2002). These separate representations might then be optimally integrated for action (Byrne & Crawford, 2010; for a review on factors that affect spatial representations, see Fiehler & Karimpur, 2022). Similarly, when we encode one environment from different viewpoints, we form separate viewpoint-dependent spatial representations that are integrated when we perform an action (Meilinger, Berthoz, & Wiener, 2011; Shelton & Mcnamara, 1997). The same might be true when we form spatial representations of different environments with similar spatial layouts. Here, reaching toward an object in an unseen environment might be based on a combined representation of the unseen and currently seen environments, leading to systematic reaching errors. For example, imagine you are wearing a VR headset that is displaying a virtual living room while you are physically standing in your own living room. The virtual living room does not look exactly like your RW living room and possibly also has a different orientation than the one in the real world. When attempting to reach for your coffee cup in the real world, then, based on previous findings (e.g., Fiehler et al., 2014; Klinghammer et al., 2016), the location of the misaligned virtual coffee cup might be integrated together with your representation of the RW coffee cup, leading you to reach between the real and virtual cup. Thus, people might not be able to maintain an accurate spatial representation of one environment while immersed in another; instead, they might integrate the two representations. Understanding how people can maintain and update the locations of objects in an unseen environment while immersed in another will provide important insight into our understanding of human spatial representations and how they are used to interact with the environment.

In a series of four studies, we investigated how people can keep track of remembered locations of objects in an unseen environment while immersed in another environment. This research, therefore, offers a first insight into people's spatial abilities when moving between virtual and real worlds. Similar to the object shift paradigm used by Fiehler et al. (2014), we applied a shift in one environment and measured how this influenced people's pointing behavior in the other environment. However, instead of shifting the locations of local objects, as in previous work, here we manipulated the orientation of the entire room as a global allocentric cue.

Participants repeatedly switched between a VR and a RW environment by donning and doffing a VR headset. In the first environment, participants encoded the locations of target objects before changing into the second environment, in which the target objects were not visible. The participants’ task was to point to the remembered locations of the objects in the first environment while being immersed in the second environment.

Importantly, the VR room could be aligned or misaligned relative to the RW room. Both sequences, VR world to RW and RW to VR world, were tested. The VR environment was either an identical copy of the RW lab room or a virtual kitchen room, with the same size and layout but containing different objects, allowing us to test if the similarity of the environments influenced the participants’ spatial memory performance. If the pointing locations in the unseen environment were affected by the misalignment of the currently visible environment, then this would indicate that participants used the currently visible environmental cues to localize the pointing targets. The integration of spatial representations might be facilitated by feature overlap, as suggested by studies where a participant's ability to orient themselves in a RW environment was facilitated by previous exposure to similar VR environments (Chabanne, Peruch, & Thinus-Blanc, 2003). We therefore expected that the influence of the room misalignment would be larger when the two environments were identical (RW and VR labs) compared to when they were different (RW lab and VR kitchen).

Two pilot studies were completed and are described in the Supplementary Materials. The first pilot study was used to determine the perceptual threshold of misalignment between the two environments (VR and RW) that was still imperceptible to the participant (Spatial Memory Task). This resulted in a pitch misalignment (VR environment tilted up and down) of ±2.6°. We also tested other misalignment axes (roll and yaw), but pitch showed the largest misalignment before detection. In the second pilot study, we determined the participants’ baseline accuracy for pointing to ensure that a misalignment effect could be detected in the main experiment based on the results of the first pilot. Overall, it was found that the participants’ baseline pointing accuracy was high enough (between 0.09° angular pointing error [SE = 0.05°] and 0.55° angular pointing error [SE = 0.10°], depending on the condition) that an effect could be detected using such a small misalignment (±2.6°).

Using the results of the two pilot studies, we then ran four separate experiments to investigate how people maintain and update spatial representations when switching between real and virtual worlds. In Experiment 1, we misaligned the two environments below the detection threshold of the participants using the value determined in the first pilot. In Experiment 2 we applied an obvious misalignment of ±20°, combined with explicit instructions about the misalignment and concrete examples about how the pointing should be done. In Experiment 3, to foster egocentric encoding of the pointing targets, the concrete examples were changed to use more egocentrically focused language. All three of these experiments used similar methods and analyses, which are described below. In Experiment 4, we used a different method (described after the first three experiments) to verify the results of the first three experiments.

General methods

Transparency and openness

All data and analysis code are available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9B38K. Data were cleaned and the pointing angles were calculated in Excel 360, version 2404 (Build 16.0.17531.2015; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). The data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 28.0.1.1 (IBM, Chicago, IL), and the pointing data for Experiment 4 were plotted with R 2021.09.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The study design and analysis were not preregistered.

Sample size estimation

Given the similarity in both the hypotheses and methods for the first three experiments, we kept the sample size the same for all three. This allowed us to best compare the results of the three experiments. We ran an a priori sample estimation in order to determine the number of participants needed using the data from the second pilot study.

First, we calculated the partial eta squared (ηp2). Using the data in the second pilot study, we took the angles of each participant (n = 10) for each target (n = 4) and condition (RW–RW, VR–VR, RW–VR, and VR–RW) and calculated the average pointing error for each participant and each condition. As explained in the Supplementary Materials, due to technical difficulties, this resulted in nine means for the RW–RW and VR–VR conditions, 10 means for the VR–RW condition, and eight means for the RW–VR conditions. We then ran a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) investigating the effect of condition on the mean pointing error. There was no effect of condition, F(1.50, 8.97) = 3.30, p = 0.093, ηp2 = 0.355. The values were corrected for a violation of sphericity using Greenhouse–Geisser correction.

Using the ηp2 value from the pilot data, we estimated the sample size required for the main experiment. We then ran an a priori sample estimation using G*Power 3.1.9.7, “F tests: ANOVA: Repeated measures, within factors.” We calculated the effect size using the ηp2 (indicating in G*Power that the ηp2 was calculated in SPSS Statistics), an alpha error probability of 0.05, a desired power of 0.95, one group, two measurements, and a non-sphericity correction of 1. Two measurements were used to determine the number of participants necessary to potentially find a difference between the two virtual environments. This resulted in a sample size of 27 participants. To account for potential outliers, we collected data from two additional participants.

Apparatus

Computer and headset

For presentation of the VR environments, an HTC VIVE Pro Eye Virtual Reality head-mounted display (HMD; HTC Corporation, Taoyuan, Taiwan) was used. The experiment was run on a Dell Alienware computer (Dell Technologies, Round Rock, TX) with an Intel Core i9 7980XE processor (Intel Corporation, Santa Clara, CA), 32 GB RAM, and two NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080Ti graphics processing units (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA). The experiment was built in Unity 2020.3.26f1 (Unity Technologies, San Francisco, CA), and the VR headset was powered by SteamVR 1.17.16. The participants held a VIVE VR controller to make their pointing movements and enter their final pointing location by pressing a button (“trigger button”).

Laser pointer

To indicate the pointing direction in the RW, the participants used a RW laser pointer that was mounted to the VR controller held in the participant's right hand. The laser pointer was operated by the participant pressing and holding a button located on the top side of the laser pointer using their left hand. In the VR environment, a raycast was projected from the VR controller and displayed a pink dot where the ray hit the virtual wall. The participant held the VR controller in their right hand and their left hand was in the same place as when pointing in the RW, just without pressing the button of the RW laser pointer. The raycast was offset to match the location of the RW laser pointer such that if both were turned on they would point to the same location. When participants were pointing in the RW, the raycast still operated in the VR environment so that the pointing location could be determined. Because of this, the coordinates of where the raycast hit the wall could be used as the pointing location when pointing in both in both VR and the RW.

Stimuli

Virtual environments

Two VR environments were used in the experiment. The environments were created using Blender 2.93 and Unity. One of the VR environments, the VR lab, was a replication of the RW lab (see Figures 2A, 2C); the other was a VR kitchen (see Figure 2D). The spatial layout of the two VR environments were similar to one another, and the objects themselves were kept as similar as possible while being semantically different (e.g., the VR lab had a blue computer screen and a black desktop; in the VR kitchen, we added a blue microwave and a black coffee machine, both with similar dimensions) (see Figure 2B). Both VR rooms had the same dimensions as the RW lab (length, 666 cm; width, 492 cm; height, 330 cm).

(A–C) images of the three environments used in the spatial memory task: the RW lab (A); the virtual lab environment (B); and the kitchen environment (C). (D) The virtual kitchen environment superimposed on the virtual lab environment to highlight the similar spatial layouts.

Targets

The target objects were four different colored posters. The RW targets were printed on a square piece of paper (21 cm × 21 cm) and displayed a colored circle (diameter, 17 cm) with a smaller black circle in its center (diameter, 4 cm) centered on a white background. The colors of the circles were orange, yellow, blue, and green (see Figure 3). The targets were placed on the wall above the desk which the participant faced while sitting in a chair in the center of the room (approximately 335 cm away from the wall). Two targets were located on the right side and two on the left side of the participant’s body midline. The targets were 175 cm from the ground, measured from the center of the target, and each target was 92 cm away from the center of its neighboring target. The locations of the targets were identical in all three environments (the two VR environments and the RW). In the VR environments, the targets were identical to the RW targets except that they had a depth of 1 cm.

The target objects used in the spatial memory task superimposed on the RW wall.

Task

General

A participant’s task was to point to the location of the targets in the previously seen environment. First, the participant saw the targets in one environment and then switched to the other environment. When in that second environment, they had to point to the location where the targets were in the first environment. Both sequences, RW to VR (RW–VR) and VR to RW (VR–RW), were completed. Both sequences were completed using one VR environment (the lab or kitchen) and then performed again using the other environment, all counterbalanced across participants. One block consisted of one VR environment, such as the kitchen environment, and one sequence, such as RW–VR. Each block consisted of 15 trials, where, in five trials the VR environment was pitched up by 2.6° relative to the RW, and in five trials the VR environment was pitched down by –2.6° relative to the RW. In the remaining five trials, the VR environment was aligned to the RW (0°). The order of the trials was randomized for each block.

Encoding the target location in VR and pointing while in the RW

The VR–RW conditions started with the participant wearing the headset. Inside the headset, the participant saw one of the two VR environments plus the four different colored virtual targets on the wall in front of them. The participant had 5 seconds to encode the location of the targets. Afterward, the screen inside the VR headset turned blue and the participant removed the headset. The participant was then facing the RW wall without the targets present. The participant was then instructed to point to the location of a previously seen colored target in the other environment, where the experimenter announced the name of the color of the target at which the participant should point. The participant aimed the VR controller at the remembered location of this target and pressed the trigger button on the VR controller to confirm their pointing location. While pointing toward the remembered location of the target the participants also pressed and held the button on the laser pointer so that they could see where they were aiming. The VR headset made a sound when the pointing location was registered. The experimenter then announced the next color, and the participant pointed to that one. After all four targets had been pointed at, the next trial started and participants put the VR headset back on to look at the targets again. The order of the colored targets on the wall remained the same for the entire block. There were five different possible combinations of the target colors that could be announced to the participants (e.g., blue, orange, yellow, and green would be one combination). These five combinations were repeated three times, one for each pitch condition (–2.6°, 2.6°, and 0°) for a total of 15 trials. The order of these 15 trials was randomized four times prior to the start of the whole experiment to create four “randomization sheets.” For a given block, the pointing order for a trial was announced based on one of the four randomization sheets. Each sheet was used once per participant, and which randomization sheet was used per block was counterbalanced across the participants. In total, the experiment took about 60 minutes.

Encoding the target location in the RW and pointing while in VR

The RW–VR condition started without the participant wearing the headset. Each participant had 5 seconds to look at the targets in the RW environment. After a beep, they put the headset on and saw one of the VR environments without the targets. Similar to the VR–RW condition, the four colors were announced one after the other, and the participant pointed to the remembered location of this target in the other environment using the VR controller and then pressed the trigger button to confirm their pointing location. After they pointed to all four targets, the VR environment was rendered off and the next trial started with the participant taking off the VR headset and looking at the targets in the RW again.

Procedure

Virtual room setup

Prior to the start of each block, the VR environment had to be carefully set up and aligned to the RW where the origin of the VR environment was manually set by the experimenter. To do this, the experimenter placed the tip of the VR controller on the center of the RW lab environment room, which was marked with tape. The experimenter then pressed the trigger button to set the center of the VR environment to the same location. Then, the location of the virtual targets was set by having the experimenter stand at the center of both the real and virtual environments; the experimenter then used both hands to point the VR controller toward the center of each target in the RW and pressed the trigger button to confirm its location. The experimenter pressed and held the RW laser pointer so that they could see where they were aiming. This set the location of the VR target at the same location as the in the RW target.

Experimental procedure

Participants signed informed consent forms. Afterward, they completed the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI) (Oldfield, 1971) to verify that they were right handed, as well as a demographic information form. Participants were then given detailed written instructions about the task followed by verbal instructions (see Appendix A) and were shown how to use the VR controller (participants were told to hold the VR controller with both hands and to use one of their hands to operate the RW laser pointer while the other hand used the trigger button). Finally, each target was shown to the participants, and they were told the name of the corresponding color.

Each block started with the participant facing the back wall (180° from the wall where the targets were placed). After the experimenter performed the room setup, the participants were free to turn around and sit in the chair located in the center of the room while the task for that specific block was explained to them. After the instructions, the participants were given the VR controller and headset, and the experiment started. See Figure 4 for a schematic of the task. After the experiment, the participants were asked if they had noticed anything about the rooms during the experiment to determine if they were aware of the change in pitch; if this was the case, they were excluded from the analyses.

Experiment 1

Methods

Sample size estimation

The number of participants was estimated using the data from the second pilot study and resulted in a sample size of 27. To account for potential outliers, we collected data from two additional participants for a total of 29 (see Sample size estimation under General methods).

Participants

Twenty-nine participants took part in the experiment (mean age, 24.10 years; SD = 3.76; 20 females and eight males, with one person not stating their gender). The experiment was conducted in agreement with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (except preregistration of the study) and was approved by the local ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology and Sports Science at the Justus Liebig University Giessen, Germany. The participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The EHI (Oldfield, 1971) was used to verify that they were right-hand dominant. Participants reported no vestibular or auditory impairments, no impairments in their upper extremities, and no colorblindness. All participants gave written informed consent and received course credit or money as compensation for their participation. All participants were naïve to the purpose of the study.

Data analysis

Preprocessing of the data and outlier analysis

No participant reported noticing misalignment of the VR room to the RW room. For one participant, the kitchen VR–RW condition was missing due to technical difficulties; therefore, only the data of 28 participants could be analyzed in the kitchen VR–RW condition. For each participant and block, we removed any button presses that occurred twice for a particular color. As we cannot be certain which of the clicks the participant intended, particularly in the case of the double click, both trial clicks were removed. This resulted in the removal of 474/20,700 data points (2.23%). Then, for each participant, the average pointing location per target was calculated for each combination of conditions: two environments, two orders per environment, four targets, three coordinates (x, y, z), and three pitch angles. This resulted in 144 means per person over all experimental conditions.

Pointing errors

The angle between the pointing location and the floor for each averaged pointing location of a given target for each person and condition combination was calculated. For the angular pointing errors, only the y- and z-coordinates (up and down, backward and forward) were used, as our task only manipulated pitch. The angular pointing errors were calculated using the formula: ATAN2Targetdepth,targetheight×180/PIAdditionally, the angles between the actual position of a target at the start of the experiment (as set by the experimenter) and the floor for each person, environment, and order were determined using the same formula. By using the actual angle of the target object, we could then subtract the angles determined by the pointing locations in order to calculate the directional pointing error. For example, if the red target was placed 28° above the floor in the RW at the start of the experiment, and the participant pointed at 29° above the floor in the 0° misalignment pitch condition, the participant would have a directional pointing error of +1° when the two environments were aligned. Positive directional errors indicate pointing errors above the target and negative pointing errors below the target.

For each participant, environment, and order, the average directional pointing error per pitch was then calculated from the pointing errors by averaging over the four targets. This resulted in 12 means per person across all experimental conditions. Across all participants and experimental conditions, 345 means (29 participants in three conditions and 28 participants in one condition, with three means per condition) could be used in the following analyses. All of the following statistical analyses were run in SPSS Statistics 28.0.1.1.

A repeated-measures ANOVA (rANOVA) was run to determine if pitch (–2.6°, 0°, and 2.6°), virtual environment (lab and kitchen), or order (VR–RW and RW–VR) had an effect on the angular pointing error. If sphericity was not assumed, then Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used. Alpha was set to 0.05. For multiple comparisons, Šidák correction was applied.

Effect of room misalignment on allocentric weights

Similar to previous studies (e.g., Fiehler et al., 2014), we calculated the influence of the pitch of the environment on pointing behavior. First, for each participant, their average pointing error in the 0° condition was subtracted from their average pointing error in the –2.6° and 2.6° pitch conditions to remove the baseline variance of the participant's pointing behavior. Ratios were then calculated by dividing the normalized pointing errors by the degree of pitch, and the average ratio was determined for each sequence and VR environment in the 2.6° and –2.6° pitch conditions. These numbers were then multiplied by 100 to form a percentage. Doing so gave us the allocentric weight for the RW–VR condition. However, for the VR–RW conditions, a small reliance on the pitch of the previously seen VR environment would correspond to a strong reliance on the allocentric information in the currently seen RW environment, so the allocentric weights correspond to the inverse of the ratios. In other words, in the VR–RW environment, if a participant saw a target in a pitched-up VR environment and then pointed straight ahead in the RW, where no misalignment is possible, this would provide a low ratio. However, this would indicate a strong reliance on the currently seen RW environment and thus a high allocentric weight. Therefore, in the VR–RW condition the allocentric weights were subtracted from 100% to provide the inverse weight.

Results

Pointing errors

Violations of sphericity were corrected via Greenhouse–Geisser correction for the effect of pitch, and the interaction between sequence and pitch. There was a significant main effect of pitch, F(1.26, 33.98) = 857.92, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.97. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that participants pointed higher when the VR room was pitched upward by 2.6° (M = 1.03°; SE = 0.11; confidence interval [CI], 0.81–1.26) compared to when it was aligned with the RW room, or 0° (M = –0.26°; SE = 0.12; CI, –0.50 to –0.02; p < 0.001), and that participants pointed lower when the VR room was pitched downward by –2.6° (M = –1.48°; SE = 0.14; CI, –1.76 to –1.19) compared to the aligned RW room (p < 0.001). When the room was pitched upward, it also differed from when the room was pitched downward (p < 0.001). Overall, the orientation of the VR environment affected participants’ pointing behavior. There was also a significant main effect of sequence, F(1, 27) = 89.61, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.77. The pairwise comparisons indicated that participants pointed higher in the VR–RW sequence (M = 0.75°; SE = 0.19; CI, 0.36–1.14) than in the RW–VR sequence (M = –1.22°; SE = 0.12; CI, –1.46 to –0.98; p < 0.001).

The results additionally revealed a significant interaction of VR environment and sequence, F(1, 27) = 6.05, p = 0.021, ηp2 = 0.18. The average pointing errors indicate that the effect of the sequence was larger when the VR environment was the kitchen (RW: M = 1.07°; SE = 0.25; CI, 0.56–1.57 vs. VR: M = –1.34°; SE = 0.18; CI, –1.71 to –0.98) than when it was the lab (RW: M = 0.44°; SE = 0.17; CI, 0.09–0.79 vs. VR: M = –1.10°; SE = 0.16; CI, –1.44 to –0.77). When participants pointed in the RW, they tended to point higher than when pointing in VR. This difference was more pronounced if participants had to point while being in the VR kitchen than the VR lab environment.

Finally, there was an interaction of sequence and pitch, F(1.46, 39.41) = 405.07, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.94, where the average pointing errors indicate that the effect of pitch was more pronounced when pointing in the VR environment at –2.6° (M = –3.50°; SE = 0.15; CI, –3.82 to –3.19), 0° (M = –1.22°; SE = 0.13; CI, –1.48 to –0.96), or 2.6° (M = 1.06°; SE = 0.12; CI, 0.81–1.31) compared to the RW at –2.6° (M = 0.55°; SE = 0.19; CI, 0.15–0.95), 0° (M = 0.70°; SE = 0.19; CI, 0.30–1.10), or 2.6° (M = 1.00°; SE = 0.19; CI, 0.61–1.40). Overall, when participants had to point while in VR it appears that they were influenced by the pitch of the environment. When pointing in the RW, the orientation of the previously seen VR environment did influence their pointing behavior but did not appear to have any major effect. The significance values and CIs for each pairwise comparison are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Experiments 1–3: Pointing Error Statistical Breakdown, Supplementary Table S1). Figure 5A shows the interaction between sequence and pitch for the normalized pointing errors (after the pointing errors in the 0° pitch trials were subtracted from the pitch up and down conditions). No other significant main effects or interactions were found.

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we investigated how well participants could maintain the representations of objects in an unseen environment while immersed in a second environment that could be aligned or misaligned relative to the first environment. In order to test this, participants were tasked with pointing to previously seen objects in the first environment while they were in the second environment. Importantly, the participants were not aware of the spatial misalignment.

Differences in visual environments

In this experiment, two VR environments were used, a lab environment that visually matched the RW lab, and a kitchen environment that was semantically different. We hypothesized that the integration of spatial representations of the two environments (VR and RW) would be weaker if the two rooms semantically differed compared with if they were the same room. Participants’ pointing responses were therefore expected to be less influenced by the misalignment of the room when the VR environment was the kitchen compared with the VR lab environment. Instead, there was no difference in pointing errors between the two environments. This might be due to the similar spatial layouts of the two environments (Figure 5A), which might have facilitated an integration of both VR environments with the RW environment. Overall, our results suggest that participants relied on similar allocentric information in the VR and RW conditions to direct their pointing.

When environments are aligned

Overall, when in VR, the participants tended to point lower than the target placement, but in the RW the participants tended to point higher, as supported by the significant main effect of sequence. It is possible that at least some of the differences in the pointing heights between the real and VR environments might have resulted from any minor differences between the virtual laser pointer and the real laser pointer attached to the VR controller. Although the endpoints of the two laser pointers were aligned as accurately as possible, small differences could still have resulted in the observed pointing pattern. Alternatively, differences in pointing behavior in VR compared to the RW could be due to differences in perceptual processes when wearing an HMD; for example, perceived distances are compressed in VR (Armbrüster, Wolter, Kuhlen, Spijkers, & Fimm, 2008; Messing & Durgin, 2005; Witmer & Sadowski, 1998), which could have affected the estimated sizes of the objects or the perceived locations of the targets in relation to the other objects.

When environments are misaligned

When participants pointed in a misaligned environment, we expected, based on previous studies (Klinghammer et al., 2016), that they would integrate the spatial representations of both environments, resulting in the targets being placed in between the locations of the two. Because the pointing errors were calculated relative to the target placement in the 0° pitch condition, a pointing error of 2.60° or –2.60° in the misaligned VR environment would indicate a complete reliance on the second environment. If participants had a pointing error of 0° when they pointed in the RW after seeing a misaligned VR environment, this would also indicate a complete reliance on the second environment. Given that, for the VR–RW sequence, the angle of the previously seen VR environment affected the pointing errors (Table 1), as well as the fact that the allocentric weights were below 100%, does suggest that participants were potentially using some information from the first environment. However, referring to Figure 5, it is evident that participants’ pointing behavior was strongly influenced by the second environment (mean allocentric weight of 89.66%). This suggests that participants mostly relied on the allocentric spatial representation of the second, currently seen environment, and the egocentric representation built up in the first environment was barely integrated, as supported by the large allocentric weights (Figure 5B).

Large reliance on allocentric cues

In the experiment, the participants were seated while encoding and pointing. As the position of their torso relative to the targets did not change, they could have used a torso-centered representation of the target locations. Previous studies have found that people used a torso-centered representation in a perspective taking task (Alsmith, Ferrè, & Longo, 2017). This might explain why the allocentric weights were below 90%. During task performance, participants could freely move their eyes and head. Previous research showed stronger allocentric coding in free gaze compared to gaze fixed conditions (Lu, Klinghammer, & Fiehler, 2018), which could have further facilitated the use of allocentric cues.

Additionally, it is possible that, because the misalignment was below detection and the VR environment was immersive, participants had no reason to not rely primarily on allocentric cues, as they would have assumed them to be the same as the egocentric cues. In this case, the roughly 10% not taken by the currently seen visual environment is surprising. Even when participants could not detect a misalignment and were in an immersive visual environment, they could still have had some residual effect of the unseen environment. However, this remaining 10% could also include factors such as participant error and might not solely reflect egocentric encoding. With a larger, noticeable degree of misalignment, the changes in the target locations relative to the participant should become larger. If participants noticed the misalignment between the two environments, they should have tried to neglect the current environment in order to do the task. Doing so would have led to smaller pointing errors and a lower allocentric weight.

Experiment 2

The results from Experiment 1 suggest that, when pointing to objects in an unseen environment, participants encountered difficulties in maintaining and updating the spatial representations of the objects in the first environment. Instead, they appeared to rely almost exclusively on the second, currently seen environment to direct their pointing. This strong reliance on the second environment might be due to the subthreshold misalignment of the environments. In Experiment 2, we increased the extent of misalignment such that it was clearly noticeable to participants. This should have resulted in a lower weighting of allocentric cues and thus a decrease of the allocentric weights.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, we hypothesized that a large, noticeable spatial misalignment of the real and virtual worlds would result in decreased allocentric weights. In contrast to the hypothesis, we observed allocentric weights that were similarly high as in Experiment 1 (Figure 6), suggesting that participants predominantly relied on the currently seen environment despite the obvious misalignment and the more direct instructions. It is also possible, however, that the nature of the task or the instructions had primed the participants to act more allocentrically. To test this possibility, we ran Experiment 3.

Experiment 3

In Experiment 3 we used egocentrically focused, written instructions to foster an egocentric strategy for target encoding. We hypothesized that this change in instruction would reduce the reliance on allocentric cues, resulting in lower allocentric weights.

Discussion

In Experiment 3, we hypothesized that with an obvious misalignment between the real and virtual worlds, as well as with instructions prompting the use of egocentric cues, the weight given to allocentric cues would be reduced compared with Experiment 1. Similar to Experiments 1 and 2, we found that the allocentric weights still dominated in directing pointing behavior (Figure 7B); additionally, the two t-tests showed that the allocentric weights did not differ by experiment. Six participants did have lower allocentric weights (below 80%); however, the other 23 remained strongly allocentric (above 80%). Despite both an obvious misalignment and egocentrically focused instructions, participants still primarily relied on allocentric cues to direct their pointing toward the remembered target locations.

Although some of the results in Experiments 2 and 3 suggest there might be differences in performance between the two different virtual environments—namely, the three-way interaction among environment, sequence, and angle in Experiment 2 and the main effect of environment in Experiment 3—we do not believe that the effect is genuine. The effect of environment was inconsistent across the experiments. In Experiment 2, participants pointed higher in the kitchen environment compared with the lab environment, and in the VR condition in Experiment 3 they pointed higher while in the kitchen, but there was no effect in Experiment 1. Moreover, pointing errors were overall very small compared with the misalignment effect.

Previous studies in VR have found that allocentric cues contribute ∼50% when reaching to a remembered object location (Karimpur, Eftekharifar, Troje, & Fiehler, 2020; Klinghammer et al., 2016). However, in the current three experiments, we continuously found substantially higher allocentric weights of above 80%. It is likely that egocentric information was not completely ignored, as the pointing errors did deviate slightly in the direction of the targets in the first environment (Figure 6A and Figure 7A). Regardless, this information contributed far less to their movements than was found in previous work (Fiehler et al., 2014; Klinghammer et al., 2015). Based on the findings in the previous three experiments, either participants had great difficulty in updating egocentric information between the RW and VR environments or they viewed it as a suboptimal strategy, even when the task primed egocentric encoding.

In the current study, participants remained seated while performing the task. Thus, the induced changes in the room orientation in the first three experiments were externally introduced and not caused by the participant's own movement. This might have hampered egocentric spatial updating of the participant's position within the new room (Klatzky, Loomis, Beall, Chance, & Golledge, 1998). Additionally, memory for objects in height might be worse than on the horizontal axis (Smith, 2022), although this is not always found (Hinterecker et al., 2018). It is possible that the misalignment of the objects in height could lead to the adoption of different strategies than if the misalignments were along the horizontal axis.

It is also possible in the first three experiments that participants might have used head-centric egocentric encoding such that they encoded everything relative to the position of their head or eyes. Then, when they entered the second environment, they might have changed the orientation of their head accordingly and therefore failed to update their egocentric reference frame, as they would essentially be taking the unseen first environment with them. In this case, the pointing errors as observed in the first three experiments would actually be egocentric in a head-centered reference frame. We believe that this is unlikely as it would suggest that, despite the participants being explicitly told that they were looking up or down in the misaligned conditions and despite access to vestibular information indicating a head tilt, the participants did not update their egocentric representation of the RW in line with the pitch of their head. However, previous work has shown that neurons in the intraparietal cortex, an area important for processing spatial information, encode the position of visual targets in a hybrid manner, incorporating both head-centric and eye-centric coordinates (Mullette-Gillman, Cohen, & Groh, 2009)

Finally, it is possible that people are not able to maintain separate representations of the same location. Wang and Brockmole (2003) proposed that, when people approach or, in the case of our experiments, enter a new environment, they stop processing locations in the environment they have exited and reorient to the new environment, leading to fragmented spatial locations. Our experiments support this finding and suggest that this occurs in seconds (within the time to put on or take off an HMD) and that it might be specifically the egocentric information that is forgotten. This would suggest that, upon entry to a new immersive visual environment, participants should lose track of the egocentric locations of objects in the unseen world and reorient to the new environment. If that environment has allocentric information similar to that of the previous environment, the participants should rely on that information to guide their movements. If the environment contains no relevant allocentric information, the participants should struggle to point to the previously seen objects, as they will have stopped maintaining the old egocentric information. If no visual information is presented, participants should still be accurate in pointing to the locations of the objects, at least within a short time frame.

In Experiment 4, we investigated how different virtual environments (aligned or misaligned in yaw and an empty visual environment) affect spatial updating of objects when pointing to their remembered locations.

Experiment 4

The results of the first three experiments suggest that the participants’ pointing behavior was based primarily on the allocentric cues in the currently visible environment. In a study by Wan et al., (2009), participants memorized the locations of objects in a VR environment and in the RW. They were then blindfolded and had to point to objects in either environment. They found that participants could accurately point to both sets of objects from memory. The results of our experiments suggest that the introduction of an immersive environment (either real or virtual) leads to participants quickly forgetting about the egocentric locations of objects in the unseen environment. Thus, if participants are asked to point to the locations of objects in a previously seen environment and a visual environment is not presented (e.g., when blindfolded), participants should be accurate, at least within a short time frame. If the participants are placed in an immersive environment, they should quickly forget about the locations of objects in the previously seen environment. If this new visual environment contains objects, then the participants should point primarily based on the visual information in the new environment. If the new environment does not contain relevant allocentric information, they will be forced to rely on the egocentric information that has not been maintained and so they should show high variability in their pointing.

To test these hypotheses, the participants performed a movement task in VR. In this task, they had to keep track of the RW door while actively moving through the VR environment to hit briefly presented spheres. Prior to the start of the experiment, the participants stood facing the front of the room, they put on the HMD (which showed a black screen), and they then turned from the front of the room and pointed to the remembered location of the RW door (+138° from the front of the room, clockwise). This provided a measure of a participant’s ability to point to the unseen RW door in the absence of any visual cues. After this, the experiment started with the participant being placed in one of three VR environments: a VR lab that was misaligned with respect to the RW lab by +45° in the yaw axis (clockwise), a VR lab that was aligned (0°) to the RW lab, and a featureless VR environment that consisted of just a floor with a seamless texture. This immersive visual environment contained no objects and so had no relevant allocentric information in contrast to the aligned and misaligned environments.

We calculated the participants’ mean pointing errors. In the aligned VR lab condition, the participants were expected to point accurately at the RW door, as its position was identical to the seen virtual door. In the misaligned VR lab condition, a participant’s pointing behavior should have been influenced by the currently seen environment such that they pointed toward the misaligned virtual door (i.e., 45° clockwise). If participants did not maintain and update a representation of the unseen environment (that is, the egocentric representation of the location of the RW door), then from the very first trial their pointing errors would be close to 45° and not change across the trials. If instead the noise associated in the egocentric representation increased over time or distance moved, then a participant’s reliance on the allocentric information in the currently seen environment would be expected to increase across the trials.

If entry to a new visual environment led to participants forgetting about the locations of objects in the unseen environment, such that they pointed primarily based on the currently seen environment, then removing the objects in the currently seen environment should have hindered performance. The featureless floor-only condition contained no relevant allocentric information; instead, participants had to rely on optic flow and physical motion information due to walking to try to keep track of the unseen RW door. Although previous studies have shown that optic flow cues alone are sufficient to allow for spatial updating of visual information, this appears to only be the case when the visual environment is structured and contains objects (Riecke, Cunningham, & Bülthoff, 2007; for an alternative finding, see Klatzky et al., 1998). However, people can perform spatial updating while walking in the dark (Martin & Thomson, 1998; Rieser, Guth, & Hill, 1986). From these results, we expected that participants would be able to update the unseen location of the RW door as they walked, but that their performance would be worse than in the aligned condition.

Methods

Participants

We opted for a similar sample size as for our other three experiments. Twenty-nine participants took part in the experiment (mean age, 22.03 years; SD = 2.90; 17 females and 11 males). Otherwise, all information regarding the participants, such as the exclusion criteria, was the same as in the three previous experiments.

Apparatus

Computer and headset

For the presentation of VR environments, a Meta Quest 2 was used (Meta, Menlo Park, CA). The experiment was run on a Lenovo Legion laptop (Lenovo, Beijing, China) with an Intel Core i7 12700H processor, 16.0 GB RAM, and a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 graphics processing unit, with wireless projection to the headset via a hotspot connection. The experiment was built in Unity 2019.4.24fi, and the VR headset was powered by SteamVR 2.3.5. The participants held a Meta VR controller in each hand.

Stimuli

Virtual environments

Two VR environments were used in the experiment: a VR lab and a floor-only (optic flow) environment. The VR lab environment was the same as in Experiments 1 to 3. This VR environment could be aligned to the RW room or misaligned (yawed) by 45° such that the objects in the room were yawed clockwise relative to the RW room (see Figure 8). The optic flow environment consisted of only a floor with infinite, seamless, repeating floor material. The floor texture was a simulated gray marble, and the rest of the environment was a blue featureless space (see Figure 8). This environment provided no relevant allocentric cues, only optic flow during movement. In the same location as the VR lab walls, when the VR lab was aligned to the RW, there were invisible walls and an invisible ceiling. This was used as a collider for measuring the pointing location. Each of the three VR environments (optic flow, aligned, and misaligned) constituted a different condition.

Tasks

VR room setup

The center of the RW room was marked with an “X” made of tape on the floor. When the participants were standing such that their head was positioned over the center of the “X” and they were facing the front of the RW room, the VR room could be set up. The participant clicked the menu button on the VR controller held in their left hand. They then clicked the “recenter position” button and stared forward for 3 seconds, the time of which was projected in the VR headset. This positioned the center of the VR environment where the center of the participants head was located and indicated the direction of forward in the direction the participant was facing. The experimenter ensured that during the 3 seconds the participant was facing forward with their head straight and shoulders square and that the participant did not move. The alignment was run again if the participant did not maintain the correct position and posture. After the alignment was complete, the participant was asked to confirm that their virtual feet were aligned with a dot on the virtual floor. If the participant said “no,” the room alignment was re-run. If the participant said “yes,” they were then instructed to look at the white virtual line drawn on the floor. The participant was asked if this line was pointing straight forward with respect to their view. If they said “no,” the room alignment was rerun. If they said “yes,” then the experiment could proceed. The room alignment task and the menu button were explained to each participant prior to the start of the experiment.

Red sphere task

On each trial, a red sphere (0.2-meter diameter) was randomly positioned in the environment and could appear anywhere left/right and forward/backward within 2 meters from the center of the room. The red sphere was always 1.6 meters above the floor. The sphere stayed at this location for 3 seconds and was then teleported to a new, randomly determined location within the predefined area. This new location also had to be at least 2 meters from the center of the previous location of the red sphere. Each trial consisted of the occurrence of six red spheres. The participant’s task was to move to the red sphere as quickly as possible without running and to place their right hand (and right VR controller) into the center of the red sphere. If this was done within 3 seconds, the red sphere turned invisible for the remainder of the 3 seconds; thus, each trial had the same length of 18 seconds. At the end of the 3 seconds, the participant heard a sound that indicated if they were successful on the trial (a coin-acquiring sound from a popular video game) or a swish sound. Both this sound and the next red sphere placement occurred at the same time.

Pointing

At the end of each trial (after hitting six red spheres), the participant performed the pointing task. The pointing task was indicated by a gray screen that popped up for 3 seconds asking the participant to point to the RW door. After the gray screen disappeared, a virtual magenta laser was projected from the front of the VR controller. The participants aimed the VR laser to where they believed the RW door was positioned and pulled the trigger button. The location of where the laser hit the wall surface was recorded as the position the participant thought the RW door was located. While the participant pointed, the current VR environment was visible. There were eight trials per condition and a total of 24 trials.

Procedure

The HMD was connected to the laptop via a hotspot so that the Unity experiment could be wirelessly streamed to the headset. This was done because a wire connecting the headset and laptop could be used as an orientation cue when the participant moved around the room.

When the participant arrived, they were given instructions and then gave their written consent to participate in the study. The participant was placed in the center of the RW room facing the front of the room (the desk, as in Experiments 1 to 3), and detailed instructions were given, including showing the participant the front of the RW lab and the RW door, which the experimenter walked over to and knocked on. The participant was also shown the VR controllers along with the menu button and the trigger button. The room setup was then explained to the participant. The participant then donned the headset and put in ear plugs to mask the sound of the laptop, which could be used as a directional cue. The room setup was then completed, and the experiment was started.

Participants first viewed a completely blue environment with no visual cues. A magenta laser line was projected from the right VR controller. Participants were asked to point to the RW door and pull the trigger button on the right VR controller. The RW door was located 138.88° from the front of the RW room, where the participant was facing, so they had to turn in order to successfully point to the door. This location was used as their baseline measure for where they remembered the location of the unseen door to be in the absence of any visual cues. After this, a gray screen obscured the participant’s view, which informed them about the start of the experiment, and the participant turned back to face the front of the RW room. The start of the red sphere task was indicated by hearing the word “go” through the headphones. The first red sphere then appeared somewhere in the VR environment. After the presentation of six red spheres, a gray screen then obscured the participant’s view, again telling them the pointing task was to begin. After 3 seconds, the gray screen disappeared, and the participant performed the pointing task. Then, the participants heard the word “go” again and restarted the red sphere task. This was done for a total of eight trials followed by a gray screen indicating that the participant would be guided back to the center of the RW lab by the experimenter while still wearing the HMD. The participant was then asked to take off the headset and was shown again where the RW door was before starting the next condition (see Figure 9 for a schematic of the task). The order of the conditions was randomly determined for each participant. The experiment took roughly half an hour.

Data analysis

Preprocessing of the data

In order to ensure that the participants were actively participating in the task, any participant who did not hit at least 60% of the red spheres was excluded from the data analysis. This resulted in the removal of two of the 29 participants (6.90%).

Pointing errors

Similar to Experiments 1 to 3, the pointing errors were calculated with the following equation: ATAN2Pointingpositionindepth,Pointingpositingonthehorizonalaxis×180/PIThe angle between the pointing location and the front of the room was calculated for each trial for each person and condition where 0° corresponded to the front of the room. When calculating the angular pointing locations, only the x- and z-coordinates (left/right, and backward/forward) were used, as we were not interested in variations along the y-axis (up/down).

The angle of the RW door from the front of the room was then subtracted from all of the angular pointing locations to give the pointing error. The pointing errors reflected the angle from the RW door such that an angle of 0° indicates that the participant successfully pointed to the RW door while in the VR room. Because the angles reported are in Unity global space coordinates, in the misaligned condition an error of +45° indicates that the participant pointed toward the virtual door, because rotating the VR room did not affect the global coordinate system. For all pointing errors reported in the following sections, a positive pointing error indicates that the participants pointed clockwise from the RW door (in the direction of the virtual door), and a negative pointing error means that the participants pointed counterclockwise from the RW door.

Mean pointing errors and slopes

In order to investigate how the VR environments influenced the pointing behavior, we calculated the mean pointing error on each trial of each condition for each participant. If the participants were relying mainly on allocentric information, then their pointing behavior would be strongly influenced by the currently seen environment. To test this, we calculated the overall mean pointing error for each participant in each condition by averaging across the trials. To determine if the noise in the egocentric representation of the location of the RW door increased the more the participant engaged with the currently seen environment, we calculated the slope of the pointing error for each participant across the eight trials in each condition.

Outlier analysis

A between subjects outlier analysis was performed on each of the different data types (overall mean pointing errors and slopes) where, for all of the data in a particular condition, a data point was removed if it was ±2.5 SD above or below the mean of that data type. In the aligned condition, two mean pointing errors (7.41%) and one slope (3.70%) were removed. In the misaligned condition, one slope (3.70%) was removed. In the optic flow condition, one mean pointing error was removed (3.70%).

Allocentric weight

Similar to Experiments 1 to 3, the allocentric weights were calculated in the misaligned condition. First, for each participant, their mean pointing error in the aligned condition was subtracted from their misaligned condition. Then, ratios were calculated by dividing the normalized pointing errors by the degree of yaw (45°) and then multiplied by 100 to form a percentage.

Statistics

In order to test the hypotheses, three separate analyses were performed. In order to investigate if participants could reliably point to the remembered location of the RW door without any visual information (i.e., when the participants pointed to the remembered location of the RW door prior to the start of the experiment when the HMD screen displayed only black), we performed a two-tailed, one-sample t-test to determine if the participant’s baseline pointing accuracy differed from the actual position of the RW door. In the case of a null result, we calculated the inverse Bayes factor (BF01). BF01 is evidence for the null hypothesis (H0) over the test hypothesis (H1), P(D|H0), where a Bayes factor above 1 indicates support for H0 and numbers below 1 indicate support for H1. Values between 3 and 10 or 0.3 and 0.1 indicate moderate evidence; values greater than 10 or less than 0.10 indicate strong support; and values between 0.3 – 1 or 1 – 3 indicate no evidence (anecdotal) for or against the null hypothesis respectively (see Wasserman, 2000).

In order to investigate if the pointing errors found were influenced by the currently seen environment, an rANOVA was run comparing the different conditions (optic flow, aligned, and misaligned). The dependent variable was the mean pointing error. If sphericity was not assumed, then Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used. Alpha was set to 0.05. For multiple comparisons, Šidák correction was applied. The allocentric weight for the misaligned condition was also calculated.

To determine if the participants were either not maintaining a representation of the unseen environment upon entry into the currently seen environment or if instead they were failing to properly update the egocentric representation such that the amount of noise in the representation of the unseen environment increased over time, an rANOVA was run comparing the different conditions (optic flow, aligned, and misaligned). The dependent variable was the slope of the pointing errors. If sphericity was not assumed, then Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used. Alpha was set to 0.05. For multiple comparisons, Šidák correction was applied. Three two-tailed, one-sample t-tests were run, one for each condition, to test if the slopes for that condition differed from 0°.

Results

Pointing with no visual information

If it was the introduction of an immersive visual environment that led to participants forgetting about the locations of objects in the unseen environment, then the participants should still have been able to accurately point to the remembered location of the door when there was no visual information (Wan et al., 2009) (see Figure 8). The mean baseline pointing error was 2.31° (SE = 1.85). There was no difference between where the participants pointed to the unseen RW door when they had no visual information and the location of the RW door, t(29) = 1.251, p = 0.221. The inverse Bayes factor indicates moderate evidence for the null hypothesis, BF01 = 3.368.

Influence of the currently seen environment on pointing errors

When an immersive visual environment was provided, it appears that the participants’ mean pointing errors were influenced by the currently seen environment (Figure 8). In the lower panel in Figure 8, it can be seen that, in the aligned condition, the participants’ mean pointing errors appear to overlap with the location of the RW door. In the misaligned condition, the mean pointing errors appear to be consistently misaligned clockwise relative to the RW door in the direction of where the virtual door was located. In the optic flow condition, the mean pointing errors appear to be more dispersed but still centered around the location of the RW door.

There was a significant main effect of condition on the mean pointing errors, F(2, 46) = 53.80, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.70. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that participants’ pointing accuracy differed in the misaligned condition (M = 43.67°; SE = 4.46; CI, 34.45–52.90) compared with the aligned condition (M = –0.69°; SE = 0.73; CI, –2.21 to 0.82; p < 0.001) and the misaligned condition compared to the optic flow condition (M = –4.39°; SE = 5.21; CI, –15.16 to 6.84; p < 0.001). No other effects were found (see Figure 10). We also calculated the standard deviations of the pointing errors per person across the eight trials per condition (see Figure 11). The average allocentric weight for the misaligned condition was found to be 97.20% (SE = 9.88%).

Discussion

In Experiment 4, we investigated how the presence of an immersive visual environment might influence the participants’ pointing behavior. We also investigated whether (a) the (egocentric) spatial representation of the objects in the unseen environment was not maintained (or at least not used) as soon as the participant entered the currently seen environment, or (b) the participants were failing to properly update the spatial representation such that the noise in the egocentric representation increased the more the participant engaged with the currently seen environment.

Our results support the hypothesis that pointing behavior relies heavily on the visual cues present in the currently seen VR environment (Figure 8 and Figure 10A). When no visual environment was provided (the baseline), the participants could accurately point to the remembered location of the door. When immersed in a VR environment with no relevant visual information related to the location of the RW door (the optic flow condition), participants’ pointing accuracy was generally low and variable. In the aligned condition, where the virtual and real objects were in the same location, participants were very accurate at pointing to the unseen RW door; however, in the misaligned condition, participants were heavily biased by the currently seen environment (Figure 10B) and pointed toward the virtual door (i.e., 43.67% yawed relative to the real door) as early as the first trial (Figure 11). Overall, it appears that participants primarily relied on the currently seen environment to guide their pointing behavior and that this happened almost immediately upon their entering the environment. This suggests that the participants either did not fully maintain a representation of the unseen environment or only minimally relied on it. It is possible that, if a participant removed the headset after each pointing, we might have seen an increased influence of the egocentric representation of the location of the unseen door. However, this is not supported by the first three experiments, where despite re-encoding the location of the targets after each trial, allocentric weights remained high (+80%).

General discussion

Moving between real and virtual environments

Previous studies have found that people are able to make movements to the locations of previously seen objects that are currently outside their field of view when they are in the same environment (Land et al., 1999), suggesting that they are able to make use of the information provided by the currently seen environment to direct their movement to the unseen part. Further studies have shown that blindfolded participants are equally as accurate when pointing to the locations of objects previously seen in real and virtual environments (Wan et al., 2009).

In our four experiments, we have shown for the first time, to our knowledge, that when people are placed in an immersive environment they hardly rely on (∼18% or less) spatial information about the position of objects in the unseen environment, but instead primarily use the spatial information from the currently seen environment to guide their actions. When participants moved between the two environments, pointing locations were based primarily on the relative allocentric spatial relationships learned in the first environment that were then transferred to the second environment even when (a) the misalignments were obvious, (b) the instructions encouraged egocentric encoding, and (c) participants moved through the VR environment. These results suggest a prominent role of an allocentric reference frame for spatial perception and action when changing between real and virtual environments. This phenomenon is likely explained by something similar to a visual reorientation illusion (VRI) which commonly occurs in microgravity but can also be induced in VR or unusual environments (Howard & Hu, 2001; McManus & Harris, 2021; Oman et al., 2003). A VRI is the spontaneous reinterpretation of a person's perceived orientation or the subjective interpretation of the surfaces around them (such as floor or wall) based on the visual cues in the environment or the orientation of the body. In the current experiment, when placed in an immersive VR environment, participants may have reinterpreted their perceived orientation in line with the currently seen VR environment.

It has been proposed that the world might act as an external memory to compensate for the high costs of working memory. For example, Ballard, Hayhoe, and Pelz (1995) found that, when people are asked to manually copy a configuration of blocks, instead of memorizing the configuration and building the model from memory, participants made frequent eye movements between the model and their work space; that is, they nearly always looked at the model prior to placing each block. This was done even when the number of blocks to be placed was within working memory capacity. The authors therefore argued that people prefer to acquire task-relevant information just before it is required rather than relying on memory. This aligns with the findings in our current study where participants might have simply relied on what they could currently see, such as the virtual door, to direct their pointing instead of relying on their memory of the location of the unseen RW door. From the results of our four experiments, we conclude that the participants primarily rely on allocentric information to guide their pointing behavior and that it is likely that the egocentric representation of the unseen environment is not maintained (or at least mostly unused). However, it remains possible that, in an immersive environment, the egocentric representation is automatically re-mapped to the currently seen visual environment. If true, this would suggest that people cannot maintain both a remembered and a current egocentric spatial representation at the same time; instead, the remembered egocentric representation is overwritten by the new egocentric representation of the currently seen environment.

Facilitating the use of allocentric reference frames

An important distinction between the current experiments and previous studies is that here the shifted allocentric information (the room), was continuously visible while pointing, whereas in previous studies it was only shown for 1 second and participants performed their reaching movements towards a gray screen (e.g., Fiehler et al., 2014; Klinghammer et al., 2015) or an empty table lacking the critical allocentric information (Karimpur, Morgenstern, & Fiehler, 2019; Klinghammer et al., 2016). It is possible that the continuously displayed visual information in the current experiments could have increased the influence of allocentric cues, particularly because the allocentric information was similar in both the real and virtual environments. Additionally, in previous studies, the participants fixated the center of the scene when performing their action (e.g., Fiehler et al., 2014; Klinghammer et al., 2015), which would have led to a more stable egocentric representation, whereas in the current experiments the participants could freely move their gaze, an effect that has been shown to increase allocentric coding (cf. Lu et al., 2018). Apart from these possible adverse effects on the egocentric representation, perhaps the largest change from previous studies is the introduced spatial change between the two environments. Here, we applied a global shift to the entire room, whereas in previous studies they shifted individual local objects (Fiehler et al., 2014; Klinghammer et al., 2015) and found an increase of the allocentric influence from 10% to 47% as more objects were shifted. Thus, the larger change in the environments could also have facilitated the use of allocentric reference frames.

Conclusions

When switching between two environments people are strongly influenced by the allocentric information in the environment they are currently in when they need to remember object locations from another, previously seen environment. This finding can help to explain the surprising phenomena experienced by people using VR, where, when they take off the VR headset, they find they are facing in a different direction than expected. Related to this, the use of a “play space guardian,” an indicator of the edges of the play environment, is likely very beneficial for people, as it is likely that they will not be able to keep track of locations such as their desk, chairs, or even walls when immersed in virtual environments. Finally, outside of the applications to VR, these results also have important implications for our understanding of how people maintain and update spatial representations of different environments.
