Impact of obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome severity on quality of life, anxiety disorders, and productivity in an active population
Authors: Amene Fki, Imène Kacem, Amira Ben Lazreg, Rania Bouchech, Sonia Ksibi, Ghazi Sakly, Mohamed Kahloul, Ridha Ben Cheikh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is a growing public health concern because of its increasing prevalence and harmful effects on physical and mental health. This disorder leads to disruptive symptoms such as daytime sleepiness and fatigue, which negatively affect vigilance, productivity, and quality of life. To investigate the impact of OSAHS severity on quality of life, productivity, and anxiety disorders in an active population. This cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2018 to June 2023 at Sahloul University Hospital among patients diagnosed with OSAHS. Excessive daytime sleepiness was assessed via the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, quality of life via the SF-12 Health Survey, mental health via the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HAD), and work productivity via the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) questionnaire. During the study period, 93 active patients diagnosed with OSAHS were included. Severe OSAHS was identified in 35 patients (37.6%). Male sex and tobacco smoking were associated with increased severity of OSAHS (p = 0.002 and 0.005, respectively). Patients with severe OSAHS had a higher body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.043). A significant association was observed between depression and anxiety scores and the severity of OSAHS (p = 0.018 and 0.025, respectively). An alteration in quality of life was more pronounced in patients with severe OSAHS (p < 0.001). In terms of productivity, the severity of OSAHS was correlated with alterations in the four dimensions of the WPAI. After binary logistic regression, the severity of OSAHS was associated with BMI (p = 0.005; aOR = 1.16; 95% CI [1.04–1.29]) and presenteeism (p < 0.001, aOR = 1.08; 95% CI [1.04–1.11]). OSAHS has substantial professional repercussions and negative impacts on patients' quality of life. As this is a cross-sectional study, only statistical associations can be inferred. Nonetheless, promoting screening and access to care is crucial at both the individual and organizational levels.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome, severity, quality of life, anxiety, productivity

Introduction

Sleep is a periodic physiological condition characterized by decreased conscious activity and responsiveness to external stimuli. It is a complex process involving brain activity, consisting of several distinct stages categorized into paradoxical and nonparadoxical sleep [1]. It is essential for several physiological functions, including physical and hormonal recovery, strengthening the immune system and improving mental health [2–4]. Thus, sleep disorders can compromise the physical and mental functioning of the body, as has been demonstrated with several sleep conditions, such as obstructive sleep apnea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS).

This is a sleep disorder, corresponding to a collapse of the pharyngeal airways during sleep, causing repeated interruptions (apneas) or marked decreases (hypopneas) in ventilation [5]. It is the most common sleep disturbance [6], affecting nearly one billion people worldwide, approximately one-third of adults, with an even higher prevalence among the elderly [7–9].

OSAHS leads to intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation [10], often causing daytime sleepiness and cognitive impairment. Long-term consequences include increased risks of psychiatric [11], cardiovascular [12], and metabolic complications [13], which can lead to increased mortality rates and healthcare costs [14].

In addition to its somatic consequences, OSAHS has a major impact on patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [15], affecting not only physical well-being but also psychological and social functioning [16]. Patients frequently report diminished emotional balance, social interactions, and physical vitality [11–13,17]. Several studies have also demonstrated strong associations between OSAHS and anxiety-depressive disorders [15,16,18]. The physio-pathological mechanisms underlying this association are complex. Recurrent interruptions of breathing during sleep can lead to disturbances in the sleep cycle, alterations in stress regulation, and fluctuations in the neurotransmitters involved in mood regulation [16,19].

Consequently, patients with OSAHS may experience impairments in various cognitive and executive functions, such as language, memory, attention, problem-solving, vigilance, and psychomotor skills [20]. These deficits negatively affect daily functioning and occupational performance, leading to reduced productivity, increased risk of errors or accidents, and lower work safety [21,22].

OSAHS affects a substantial proportion of the working-age population [23], with recent meta-analyzes reporting prevalence rates of up to 26.9% among adults between 2021 and 2024 [24].

Despite this high prevalence, it remains significantly underdiagnosed in active individuals, who may minimize or normalize symptoms such as fatigue or lack focus [25]. This is particularly concerning given that untreated OSAHS has been associated with impaired workplace performance and elevated occupational risk, especially in shift workers or individuals in safety-critical jobs [26,27].

Furthermore, recent studies have confirmed that excessive daytime sleepiness in OSAHS patients is strongly associated with decreased HRQoL and considerable impairment in professional productivity [28,29]. Global economic modeling has also highlighted the growing burden of OSAHS, with productivity losses estimated at more than 100 million workdays annually due to absenteeism and presenteeism and financial costs exceeding 30 billion USD in 2023 alone [30].

Considering its significant medical, psychological, and socioeconomic impact, OSAHS represents a growing public health concern, especially in modern societies where productivity and cognitive performance are increasingly emphasized [31,32]. However, few studies have focused specifically on the prevalence and functional impact of OSAHS within professionally active populations.

In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the impact of OSAHS severity on quality of life, work productivity, and anxiety disorders in an active population.

Methods

Study design

This is an analytical cross-sectional epidemiological study carried out in the Sleep Exploration Unit, Department of Functional Explorations of the Nervous System, Sahloul University Hospital, Tunisia, from 1 January 2018, to 30 June 2023.

Study population

All active patients with OSAHS confirmed by polysomnography who were treated with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) during the period from 1 January 2018, to 30 June 2023, were included. Patients were recruited at the Sleep Exploration Unit, Sahloul University Hospital, Tunisia.

The inclusion criteria were confirmed OSAHS according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines [6], current professional activity, and patient consent to participate in the study. Patients without active occupational activities at the time of the study were not included.

Data collection

The data were collected via a preestablished medical questionnaire and included sociodemographic data (age, sex, marital status), lifestyle habits (tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, and the practice of physical activities), occupational data (workstations, professional seniority, shift work, workplace accidents, working hours per day), anthropometric data (height, weight), and clinical data (polysomnography parameters, including the Apnea‒Hypopnea Index (AHI), desaturation index (DI), average oxygen saturation (%) and minimum oxygen saturation (%)). Information regarding CPAP use was also recorded. In Tunisia, CPAP treatment for OSAHS is fully reimbursed by the national health insurance system (CNAM), ensuring equal access for patients.

Measuring instruments

Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS)

This self-administered questionnaire assesses the risk of daytime sleepiness in eight mainly passive situations of daily life and is scored from 0 to 3. The total score ranges from 0 to 24. The threshold of normality, established in healthy volunteers, is generally set at 10. A total score above 10 is indicative of excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) [33]. The validated Arabic version was used in the study [34].

The hospital anxiety and depression (HADS) scale

To assess anxiety and depressive disorders in our patients, we used the Arabic version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [35]. It consists of 14 questions divided into two subscales: seven items for anxiety and seven items for depression. Each item offers four response options, which are coded from 0 to 3. A diagnosis of depression or anxiety is made when the HADS score is ≥11.

The SF−12 health-related quality of life scale

Health-related quality of life was assessed via the Arabic version of the SF-12 questionnaire, which evaluates eight dimensions of health [36]. Responses were scored from 0 to 100, and average scores were calculated for each dimension. These scores were summarized into two components: the physical health score (PHS) and the mental health score (MHS). Higher scores indicate better quality of life, with an average reference value of 50. Scores below 50 were considered low.

Work productivity and activity impairment (WPAI)

The WPAI is a validated standardized questionnaire comprising six questions and is available in Arabic [37]. It includes four domains: absenteeism (the percentage of time missed from work due to illness or health problems), presenteeism (the percentage of the reduction in productivity at work due to illness or health problems, even when the person is present at work), total productivity impairment (an overall assessment of the combined impact of absenteeism and presenteeism on overall productivity at work) and impact on daily activities (measures the overall impact of illness on daily activities outside work). The results are expressed as a percentage of time lost and are calculated by multiplying the scores by 100 as follows:

- Absenteeism: Q2/(Q2 + Q4)

- Absenteeism: Q2/(Q2 + Q4)

- Presenteeism: Q5/10

- Presenteeism: Q5/10

- Total Productivity Impairment: Q2/(Q2 + Q4) + [(1-Q2/(Q2 + Q4)) × (Q5/10)]

- Total Productivity Impairment: Q2/(Q2 + Q4) + [(1-Q2/(Q2 + Q4)) × (Q5/10)]

- Daily activity impairment: Q6/10.

- Daily activity impairment: Q6/10.

Operational definitions

The occupational survey specified the work category. Blue-collar workers, who perform manual labor and earn hourly wages, contrast with white-collar workers, typically office workers, particularly executives.

The classification of obesity is based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [38]. Overweight corresponds to a BMI ranging between 25 and 29.9 kg/m2. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. OSAHS is defined according to the criteria of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) [39].

The OSAHS severity is classified into three stages considering the AHI: mild stage (AHI between 5 and 15 events/h), moderate stage (AHI between 15 and 30 events/h), and severe stage (AHI ≥ 30 events/h).

Sample size

Sample size calculation: n = Zα/2 × p × (1−p)/d2 = 92, estimating that the prevalence of OSAHS in the general population is 4% [36].

n: Sample size, Zα/2 (for a risk of error α = 5%, Zα/2 = 1.96) p: expected prevalence, d: error margin = 0.05.

The initial feasibility-based sample size estimation was guided by population prevalence; however, formal power analysis was conducted for our primary HADS-Anxiety score to detect a clinically meaningful between-group difference of 3.5 points. A standard deviation of 4.2, which is consistent with the typical range (3.8–4.5) reported in clinical populations [40], corresponds to a large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.83).

On the basis of a two-tailed independent t test with α = 0.05 and 80% power [41], a balanced design would require 46 participants per group. In anticipation of potential attrition and group imbalance, we aimed to recruit at least 60 patients.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS for statistical analysis, version 25.

We calculated frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables. For quantitative variables, we calculated means and standard deviations for normally distributed data and medians, interquartile ranges (IQRs), and the extent of extreme values for nonnormally distributed data.

For the analytic study, we divided patients with OSAHS into two groups: nonsevere OSAHS (including mild and moderate OSAHS) and severe OSAHS.

The percentages were compared via Pearson's chi-square test, whereas the means were compared via Student's t test. The Pearson correlation coefficient ‘r’ was used to measure the associations between two quantitative variables.

The multivariate analysis was carried out via binary logistic regressions following the step-down strategy. All explanatory variables related to the dependent variable ‘OSAHS severity’ in univariate analyzes with degrees of significance <0.2 were included in the initial multivariate analysis models. In the final models, the strength of the associations between independent variables and dependent variables was estimated via adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For all the statistical tests used, the threshold of significance was 0.05.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board. This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with full respect for the rights, dignity, and integrity of all participants.

Results

During the study period, 93 active patients who were diagnosed with OSAHS through polysomnography and who consented to participate were included (Figure 1).

The mean age was 48.56 ± 8.04 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.32. Among the included patients, 35 (37.6%) presented with severe OSAHS.

When sociodemographic characteristics according to OSAHS severity were compared, we found that severe forms were significantly more common among male patients (p = 0.002), smokers (p = 0.005), and those with a higher BMI (p = 0.043). Conversely, no significant correlation was detected between occupational characteristics and OSAHS severity (Table 1).

Discussion

OSAHS is a chronic condition that can significantly affect the mental and emotional health of individuals. Frequent breathing interruptions during sleep disrupt the normal sleep cycle, leading to excessive daytime sleepiness, persistent fatigue, and reduced energy levels. These consequences may impair concentration and productivity at work and in daily activities and may even increase the risk of accidents [42–44].

In our study, 33.3% of patients had a low quality of life, as assessed by the SF−12. An overall decline in quality of life, including physical and mental components, was significantly correlated with the severity of OSAS (p < 10−3). Indeed, OSAHS can negatively affect many domains of health-related quality of life.

These effects have been well documented in the literature [29,45,46]. Lopez et al. [47] reported a marked reduction across all quality-of-life domains in patients with an AHI greater than 30. Similarly, Moore et al. [48] demonstrated that sleep fragmentation-related disorders exert a significant negative effect on multiple dimensions of quality of life, suggesting that fatigue and excessive daytime sleepiness are strongly linked to a deterioration in quality of life. Briones et al. [49] also conducted a study enrolling 129 adults with OSAHS and reported that excessive sleepiness substantially influenced patients’ perceptions of quality of life.

In Tunisia, a study conducted on 104 patients with OSAHS revealed that the majority reported a deterioration in their quality of life. A positive linear correlation was observed between the physical and mental components of the SF-36 and factors such as sex, age, BMI, and the presence of anxiety and depressive disorders [17].

In addition to the impact of OSAHS on quality of life, our study revealed that 32.3% of patients presented with elevated levels of depression as assessed by the HADS. According to the literature, OSAHS is frequently associated with psychiatric disorders, with depressive disorder identified as the most prevalent condition [50–53]. The prevalence of depression in patients with OSAHS varies considerably between studies, ranging from 20% to 65% [8,54,55].

Guilleminault et al. [56] reported that 28% of patients diagnosed with OSAHS presented depressive symptoms. According to Reynolds et al. [57], the prevalence of depressive symptoms in adult men is directly correlated with the severity of OSAHS.

In our study, we observed a statistically significant association between severe OSAHS and depression (p < 0.05). This finding is consistent with the study of Millam et al. [58], who reported that 50% of patients with OSAHS presented depressive symptoms and that the self-rating depression scale (SDS) score was significantly correlated with OSAHS severity. Sirvesten et al. [59] examined 7028 working individuals diagnosed with OSAHS and demonstrated that the prevalence of depression was markedly greater in patients with severe OSAHS than in those with nonsevere OSAHS.

The underlying mechanisms linking OSAHS and depression include sleep fragmentation and recurrent hypoxia, leading to reduced sleep quality and excessive daytime sleepiness; neurochemical alterations affecting mood-regulating neurotransmitters (e.g. serotonin, norepinephrine); and oxidative stress and inflammation, all of which contribute to depressive symptoms and reduced quality of life [60–62].

In terms of anxiety, the mean HADS-A score in our study was 7.87 ± 7.62, with 51.6% of anxious patients (HADS-A score ≥ 11) having severe OSAHS (AHI score ≥ 30) (p = 0.025). The literature reports that the prevalence of anxiety in patients with OSAHS ranges from 11% to 70% [63,64], with some studies suggesting that anxiety severity is more strongly correlated with daytime sleepiness than with hypoxemia [7,8]. The relationship is likely bidirectional: OSAHS may exacerbate anxiety through symptoms such as sleep disruption and breathing difficulties, whereas anxiety can increase airway muscle tension and impair sleep, further worsening OSAHS symptoms [65].

OSAHS can also have implications for professional performance. Excessive daytime sleepiness, fatigue, and other symptoms associated with OSAHS can negatively affect work performance and career development. In our study, the average level of absenteeism was 6.67 ± 12.2 d per year. The decrease in productivity was 46.24 ± 40.42%, and the reduction in daily activity was 39.13 ± 30.91%. These impairments were significantly associated with OSAHS severity (p < 0.001). After binary logistic regression, presenteeism remained significantly correlated with OSAHS severity (adjusted OR = 1.08; 95% CI [1.04–1.11]).

Studies have shown a correlation between OSAHS and decreased work productivity, as well as increased absenteeism. For example, a Tunisian study involving 51 soldiers with severe OSAHS reported a 65% reduction in productivity and a 19% increase in absenteeism [66].

Another Tunisian team [67] conducted a cross-sectional study to assess the impact of OSAHS on work performance among 139 subjects. Among the four domains of the WPAI scale, presenteeism was found to be associated with the AHI (p = 0.012). Additionally, a significant association was found between daytime sleepiness and three of the WPAI domains. The authors concluded that patients with excessive daytime sleepiness (ESS ≥ 11) had lower work performance in terms of presenteeism, absenteeism, total work productivity loss, and daily activity reduction than nonsleepy participants did. These results are consistent with previous research showing that higher ESS scores are associated with greater work limitations and reduced productivity in OSAHS patients [28,68,69]. Furthermore, a systematic review by Guglielmi et al. [70] confirmed that OSAHS negatively affects occupational health, leading to impaired work performance, reduced productivity, and increased absenteeism across diverse populations.

In addition to its direct impact on performance, OSAHS can increase occupational risk, including workplace accidents. A meta-analysis conducted by Garbarino et al. [71] highlighted the connection between OSAHS and workplace accidents, underscoring the need for preventive strategies. Individuals with OSAHS may experience difficulties with concentration, memory impairment, and reduced alertness due to daytime sleepiness. This can affect work performance, particularly in tasks requiring sustained attention. Additionally, persistent fatigue associated with OSAHS can lead to decreased productivity, as affected individuals may struggle to remain alert and engage throughout the workday. OSAHS can also impact sleep quality and, consequently, overall professional well-being. Finally, the repercussions of OSAHS, such as depression and anxiety, can also influence emotional well-being in the workplace.

The analysis of sociodemographic factors associated with the severity of OSAHS revealed that severe OSAHS was more common in men (p = 0.002). O'Connor et al. [72] reported a male-to-female ratio of 3.2:1 among all patients with OSAHS, but this ratio varied from 2.2:1 for patients with mild OSAHS to 7.9:1 for those with severe OSAHS. The Cleveland Family Study also indicated that men tended to develop more severe OSAHS than women did over the five-year study period [73]. However, Araujo et al. [74] reported in a meta-analysis that female sex was a risk factor for severe OSAHS.

In addition to male sex, smoking was significantly associated with severe OSAHS in our study, in line with previous findings. Bakkal et al. [75] reported higher average AHI values in smokers than in nonsmokers. Similarly, Zaibi et al. [76] observed more severe OSAHS among smokers, with a higher average AHI (30.2 vs. 20.7; p = 0.044). Chronic smoking induces airway inflammation, which can increase the risk of obstruction during sleep [77].

In our study, the majority of patients did not engage in regular physical activity. A statistically significant association was found between low levels of physical activity and the severity of OSAHS (p = 0.05). Regular physical activity can play a crucial role in managing OSAHS. Research has shown that consistent exercise can improve sleep quality and potentially reduce OSAHS symptoms [78]. Additionally, moderate, regular physical activity may contribute to weight reduction, which in turn can decrease the severity of OSAHS symptoms [79,80].

Consistent with this observation, our results also highlighted obesity as an independent risk factor for severe OSAHS (p = 0.005; OR = 1.16; 95% CI [1.04–1.29]). This finding is in agreement with several studies reporting obesity as a major determinant of OSAHS development and progression [80,81]. Excess adipose tissue, particularly in the cervical region, increases upper airway collapsibility and predisposes patients to obstruction during sleep. Furthermore, central obesity contributes to reduced lung volume and impaired respiratory mechanics, whereas metabolic and hormonal changes linked to obesity may further exacerbate disease severity.

In terms of polysomnographic data, the average AHI was 31.4/h, with a mean SpO2 of 96.7 ± 1.2 and a minimum SpO2 of 78.5 ± 9.26. The minimum SpO2 was significantly lower in the group with an AHI ≥ 30 (p < 0.001), whereas the mean oxygen saturation was comparable between the two groups. The oxygen desaturation index was greater in patients with severe OSAHS (p = 0.018). This finding is consistent with the study of Fessi R et al. [82], which reported a strong correlation between the AHI and the desaturation index (r = 0.66).

To our knowledge, our study is the first in Tunisia to evaluate quality of life, anxiety-depressive disorders, and productivity in individuals with OSAHS via three different validated scales. This study presents a low risk of selection bias since the diagnosis was confirmed by experts. Additionally, we used standardized and validated Arabic questionnaires to assess quality of life, anxiety-depressive disorders, and productivity, minimizing measurement bias. Furthermore, taking into account certain nonprofessional factors, such as personal history and lifestyle habits, adds a multidimensional aspect to the study and helps reduce confounding bias.

This study has certain limitations. The relatively small sample, restricted to employed patients, may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to women and older or unemployed individuals. The absence of a nonactive comparison group further restricts conclusions beyond working populations. Recall bias related to self-report questionnaires, as well as the use of Arabic versions of the ESS, SF-12, and HADS validated outside Tunisia, may also have influenced the results. Although the study was adequately powered, future research could include post hoc power analyzes and more diverse populations. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal interpretation, underscoring the need for larger longitudinal studies.

Nevertheless, despite these limitations, our results provide important practical insights. These findings emphasize the need for early screening of OSAHS among active workers, particularly those in high-risk occupations, to identify individuals with excessive daytime sleepiness or severe disease. The implementation of workplace policies, such as flexible scheduling, health promotion programs, and awareness campaigns, could help mitigate the impact of OSAHS on productivity, safety, and overall well-being.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that OSAHS is a serious condition with significant personal and professional consequences. It is associated with mood disorders such as depression and anxiety, as well as impaired quality of life.

These results highlight the importance of raising awareness and implementing early screening strategies to identify individuals at risk. Healthcare professionals play a key role in promoting such programs and facilitating access to appropriate medical care, which may ultimately improve both well-being and workplace productivity.
