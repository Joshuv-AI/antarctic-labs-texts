Artificial Insemination in Cattle: Efficiency, Opportunities and Challenges in Selected Districts of West Wallaga Zone, Oromia Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Tolasa Edosa Tola, Abriham Kebede Deresa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial insemination (AI) is a technique in which semen is artificially collected from a selected male animal, processed, stored and artificially introduced into the selected female reproductive tract for genetic improvement. The study was conducted from December 2022 to November 2023 to determine the efficiency, opportunities and challenges of AI in the West Wallaga Zone in Oromia, Ethiopia. The zone is located 441 km away from Addis Ababa. It is located at 9°10′N–9°17′N latitude and 35°44′E–36°09′E longitude. The three selected districts were Gimbi, Nadjo and Ayira and six representative kebeles from each district (Lalisa Yesus, Chuta Gochi), (Eba waqayo, Humna waqayo) and (Wayu kolli, Jarso Abayani), respectively. Purposive sampling methods were used to select the study area districts, kebeles and households based on the number of livestock population, the potential of AI implementation, accessibility and households who have one or more artificially inseminated dairy cows. Methods of data collection are questionnaire surveys, field monitoring, focal group discussions and retrospective data from the record book of each kebele and district office of agriculture from 2019 to 2023, which were used for the study. Both primary and secondary sources of information were used to obtain the data. In the three representative districts, the total target population (N = 900) of households’ owners of artificially inseminated cows was determined using the Yamane formula (n = 277). The sample size of the households of owners of artificially inseminated cows for each of the three selected districts was 92, and the households of owners of artificially inseminated cows for each kebele were surveyed 46. The collected data were statistically analysed using the Statistics Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 25). The efficiency/conception and calving rate in 2019–2023 from the total 2565 inseminated cows were 1285 (50%) and 1254 (97%), respectively. The overall average level of education was 53.8% (elementary school). The top opportunities and challenges were farmer interest and heat detection, respectively. Therefore, there should be an extension on AI, the AI technicians should be trained to improve their skills and increase the efficiency of AI, and the owners of dairy cows should be trained on heat detection.Keywords: artificial insemination, cattle, challenges, efficiency, opportunities, West Wallaga

Introduction

Ethiopia is the most productive country in Africa and ranks first in terms of animal husbandry, it is estimated that there are about 70 million cattle in total in the country, with about 12 million dairy cows that produce an average of approximately 1482 L of milk/cow/day with a lactation period of 7 months (CSA 2021). According to recent estimates, 97.8%, 1.91% and 0.32% of cattle are indigenous, hybrid and exotic breeds, respectively, with neither hybrid nor exotic breeds accounting for more than 2% of the total cattle population and low production and productivity (FAO and IGAD 2019).

Oromia ranks first in Ethiopia with a cattle population of 24.43 million. Although there is a large number of indigenous dairy cattle in Ethiopia, it is necessary to work hard; efforts must be made to enhance the production and reproductive capabilities of cattle by carefully selecting the most desirable breeds; crossbreeding these native breeds with highly productive exotic cattle has been suggested as a workable solution, as well as artificial insemination (AI) activities (CSA 2021).

The first recorded cases of AI are dated back to AD 1322. It is interesting to note that old Arab documents suggest an Arab chieftain desired to mate his prize mare with an excellent stallion belonging to a rival. The Russian Ivan Off pioneered research into the AI of birds, horses, cattle and sheep. In 1938, Asmara, which was then part of Ethiopia, was, however, interrupted due to the Second World War. AI technology to the cattle with the goal of improving the genetics of dairy cows (Barros et al. 2006). AI was introduced in the country more than 35 years ago, and it is still used on a small scale for breeding purposes and to increase the production performance of dairy cows (Getu and Campus 2015).

The primary factors that hinder the effectiveness of AI technologies are infrastructural, administrative and financial limitations along with technical problems, such as early embryonic death leading to loss of performance in dairy cows, delivery systems, lack of transportation, wrong timing of insemination, management problems, lack of awareness among people, insemination techniques, record books, communication between AI technicians (AITs), dairy owners and AI centres, insufficient inputs, poor logistics and problems in heat detection from farmers; these were the major challenges in AI service delivery (GebreMedhin et al. 2009; Mohammed 2018; Birhanemeskel et al. 2014).

Statement of Problem

Some problems that influence AI efficiency include infrastructure, managerial and financial constraints and also technical problems (GebreMedhin et al. 2009; Mohammed 2018; Temesegen et al. 2021). In Western Wallaga, particularly in Gimbi, Nadjo and Ayira, AI was practised several years ago. However, the success rate of this technology is still low. The main causes of the low success of AI in the study area were not precisely known, and no attention was paid to this problem. Despite the significant role that AI plays for cattle, the status of AI efficiency and the opportunities and constraints encountered by cattle have not been systematically studied and documented in the study area. Therefore, this research was conducted with the objectives of:

Assessing the efficiency of AI in the study area;

Determining the opportunities for AI in the study area;

Materials and Methods

Description of the Study Area

West Wallaga Zone is one of the zones of the Oromia Regional State. It is bordered by Kelem Wallaga Zone on the west, Benishangul Gumuz Region on the north, East Wallaga on the east and Illubabor on the southeast (Figure 1). The three selected districts in the West Wallaga Zone have 396,262 heads of cattle. Six kebele (Lalisa Yesus, Chuta Gochi), (Eba waqayo, Humna waqayo), (Wayu kolli, Jarso Abayani), Gimbi, Nadjo and Ayira were selected as representatives of each district, respectively (West Wallaga Zone Agricultural Office 2023).

Map of the study area.

The most common species of livestock is cattle. Small‐scale farmers use a mixed crop–livestock production system, and almost all of the livestock are local cattle. The future of the study area is bright because it is gifted with a variable climate range, and a large number of cattle have a high potential for dairy farming (Galmessa et al. 2013). Dairy production has so many advantages; in addition to providing food and nutrition, it also raises social status, creates income, creates jobs and recycles organic waste; the government should put more emphasis on it. Building alternative animal feed processing facilities, raising consumer awareness of dairy and growing consumer demand for dairy products could all be beneficial factors for the growth of the area dairy industry (West Wallaga Zone Agriculture Office 2023).

Gimbi District

Gimbi district is in the West Wallaga Zone, in the Oromia Region of Ethiopia. It is bordered to the south by Haru District, to the south‐west by Yubudo District, to the west by Lalo Asabi District and to the north by the Benishangul Gumuz Region. It is located at 9°10′N–9°17′N latitude and 35°44′E–36°09′E longitude. It is approximately 441 km from Addis Ababa, which is the capital city of the country, and it occupies an area of 112,969 ha (1129.69 km2). Its highest peak is Mount Welel (Gimbi District Agriculture Office 2023).

The district has a total of 31 kebeles; two were urban and 29 of them rural. Gimbi town receives 1400–1800 mm of precipitation on average per year, with the majority of that precipitation falling between mid‐June and August. The town's annual temperature fluctuates between 10°C and 30°C, and its elevation varies from 1200 to 2222 m.a.s.l. The study area has a total population of 181,274, with 90,177 males and 91,097 females. The principal livestock species in the district study area are as follows: 130,710 cattle, 53,670 sheep, 11,392 goats, 13,140 donkeys, 128 mules and 118,250 poultry (Gimbi District Agriculture Office 2023).

In addition to raising a sizable number of cattle, small‐scale farmers in the district cultivate maize, sorghum, teff, millet, wheat, barley and beans as their primary crops. The study area and the region economies both heavily rely on livestock. The main purpose of keeping livestock is to use it as a source of capital that can be turned into cash when needed. Additionally, it is utilized as a source of compost to increase soil fertility, provide food for humans and power livestock (Gimbi District Agriculture Office 2023). The study area terrain that is appropriate topography for agriculture activities is categorized as Dega, Winadega and Kola at 10%, 70% and 20%, respectively (Chemeda et al. 2023). Coffee is the main cash crop grown in this district. More than 5000 ha are planted with this crop (Birhanemeskel et al. 2014). Iron and phosphate deposits have been found in the vicinity of Bikilal, a village located 18–20 km north of Gimbi, although they have not yet been commercially developed (Tadesse et al. 2003).

Nadjo District

Nadjo is a district in the West Wallaga Zone of the Oromia Region, Ethiopia. Bodji Dirmaji Nadjo on the east, Jarso and Lata Sibu border it on the west, Bodji Chokorsa borders it on the south and Benishangul Gumuz Region border it on the north. Situated along the main road that connects Finfinnee to Asossa and roughly 541 km from the capital city of the country. It is situated at an elevation of 1600–2200 m.a.s.l.; the location is 9°30′N latitude and 35°30′E longitude on the map (Nadjo District Agriculture Office 2023).

The district receives an average of 1350–2300 mm of precipitation, which falls on average each year. 18°C–28°C is the average annual temperature. 72,601.777 ha, or 726.01777 km2, make up the land area. 102,307 people living in the study area overall, with 50,163 males and 52,472 females. The total livestock population is estimated at 379,128 head: 210,317 cattle, 34,477 sheep, 22,733 goats, 504 mules, 25 horses, 9500 donkeys, 101,572 poultry and 68,779 colonies of honeybees. In the study area, the topography that is suitable for agriculture is 1%, 99% and 0% in Dega, Wainadega and Kola, respectively.

The district is made up of 35 kebeles in total; except for a few low zones, the majority of the district kebeles are categorized as medium agro‐ecological zones. Four kebeles are urban, three are peri‐urban, and 28 are rural throughout the province. Less than 12% of the population is employed in trade and other nonagricultural activities, whereas more than 88% of the population supports itself through agriculture and the raising of livestock. The district primarily cultivates maize, sorghum, teff, millet, wheat, barley, beans and other crops. The area also produces fruits, vegetables and root crops in backyards, as well as bananas, oranges, mangoes, papayas, avocados, potatoes, sweet potatoes, carrots, cabbages and red roots. Smallholder farmers also raise a decent number of animals. The area used the mixed crop–livestock farming system (Nadjo District Agriculture Office 2023).

Ayira District

The Ayira area consists of the 23 kebeles that together make the West Wallaga Zone, Oromia region. It is 504 km distant from the capital city of the country and 78 km away from Gimbi, the capital town of the zone. Its borders are as follows: Kelem Wallaga Zone to the south and west, Yubdo to the east and Gulliso to the north. The administrative hub is Ayira. Agro‐ecology categorizes the district as 75% midland and 25% lowland, with average temperatures, highs of 28°C and lows of 10°C (Ayira District Agriculture Office 2023).

The district has an average annual rainfall of 1000 and 2000 mm, and its elevation ranges from 1500 to 1750 m.a.s.l. A total of 70,643 people living in the study district overall, with 33,326 males and 37,317 females. The agricultural system in the study area is characterized by a combination of mixed farming practices and extensive animal production. The animals raised in the study area include 55,234 cattle, 14,596 sheep, 4087 goats, 68,988 chickens, 6505 donkey, 90 mules and 266 horses (Ayira District Agriculture Office 2023).

In the district, 90% of the labour force is employed in agriculture, which is the primary economic activity (CSA 2021). Pulses: Horse beans, field peas and lentils make up about 11% of the total cultivated areas. Maize accounts for 26% of the area under cultivation, followed by millet (24%), sorghum (10%), teff (6%) and barley (4%). Rainfall is essential to crop productivity. The district also grows vegetables and root crops in backyards, such as red root, sweet potatoes, carrots and cabbage. The majority of animals raised by smallholder farmers are also raised by them. The economy of the study area and the surrounding area both depend extensively on livestock. The primary purposes of keeping livestock are as a source of capital, which can be converted into cash when needed, as well as for draught power, human food and manure for enhancing soil fertility (Ashebir et al. 2016).

Study Population

The study population consisted of animal owners using AI for their cows and AI technicians in the districts. Moreover, secondary data from AI recorded books from 2019 to 2023 in the three selected districts.

Study Design

The cross‐sectional type of study was supported by a questionnaire survey and retrospective data from the record to assess and analyse the pertinent information from the three districts of the West Wallaga Zone on the operation of AI service, the process of AI service, heat detection knowledge and the opportunities and challenges of AI service in the study areas.

Moreover, a retrospective study of AI data recorded from 2019 to 2023 about cow insemination owners and inseminated cows in three districts of choice. Throughout the study period, special focus was given to the AI service in the study areas.

Sampling Technique and Sample Size

The zone was selected by purposive sampling techniques, which were employed to select the study area by considering the number of livestock and the potential of AI. From the zone, the three districts (Gimbi, Nadjo and Ayira) and six representative kebeles of each district (Lalisa Yesus, Chuta Gochi, Eba waqayo, Humna waqayo, Wayu kolli and Jarso Abayani). The households were also purposively selected from the kebeles that have one or more artificially inseminated dairy cows. Relevant information was gathered for a retrospective study from the record books maintained by the AI inseminators in each kebele and each district of the agricultural office. For the study, documents from 2019 to 2023 were used.

Methods of Data Collection

Both primary and secondary sources of information were used to obtain the data. To collect primary data from farmers and AITs, semi‐structured questionnaires, interviews and direct observations were employed. Secondary data, including the number of cattle and human population, were taken from published and unpublished available sources; AI service efficiency and challenges data were taken from zonal and district office record books.

Questionnaire Survey

Semi‐structured questionnaires and interviews with chosen farmers from the six peasant associations (PAs) of the three districts in the zones were used to gather primary data. Equal households were chosen randomly and interviewed from each PA. For total uniformity, the questionnaires were first written in English, then translated into the vernacular language of the study area (Afan Oromo) and finally rewritten in English for complete uniformity. An appropriate adjustment and correction were made to the questionnaire, as it was suitable in terms of design, unity, information, meaning and importance of the questions, as well as the duration of time needed for an interview. Acceptable modifications were made after the questionnaire format was pretested (pilot testing) on 5% of the randomly selected sample sizes, and it was validated by comparing it with the findings of other researchers in the field, as it contains important information needed (Appendix I).

The variables include the following: type, knowledge of heat detection; demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, family size, educational background, primary occupation); cattle herd structure; dairy breeds; knowledge and aim of dairy AI cows; feed sources; water sources; management; and disease problems are some of the variables. The researchers discovered respondents to carry out the responses to the survey while conducting interviews or conducting the questionnaire with the district development workers. Participants received specialized training on how to administer and gather the questionnaire. Additionally, the researchers closely supervised the data collection process (Appendix II).

Retrospective Study

Important information gathered from the AI inseminator's record book for each kebele and district of the agricultural office's written records from 2019 to 2023 was collected for the retrospective study. The efficiency rate of each type of cow, including inseminated cows, pregnant cows and calves born, was studied on the basis of the recorded data (Appendix III). All the data having full information were considered and used in the study.

Field Monitoring

In all phases of the current study, AITs and participants in focus groups were monitored through the use of checklists, follow‐up/technical skills of AITs and challenges during AI, any problems that emerged during the study period, and assessments between the data collected and the recorded artificially inseminated cows from the yearly retrospective study were conducted.

Focal Group Discussions

Discussions with key participants and the group were planned to support the information that would be collected through a questionnaire survey to investigate completely the subject area of the research. A focus group is a small group discussion guided by a researcher leader. It is used to discuss complex ideas in‐depth. The group format stimulates discussion, generates new ideas and promotes the exploration of unknowns. Key informant interviews are qualitative in‐depth interviews with people who know what is going on in the community. The purpose of key informant interviews is to collect information from a wide range of people, including community leaders, professionals or residents who have firsthand knowledge about the community. It could have been conducted by staff in person, by meetings and by telephone with staff and focus group discussion (FGD). A single group was established for each district. Five individuals from each group were district natives. Fifteen individuals in total, representing three districts. Each group's members were invited to join in the discussions and assigned an orientation on the purpose of the research before starting. FGD and key informants were selected (i.e., three elders of agriculture with knowledge of AI, three experts in animal science, three experts in animal health, three leaders of the extension program for animal science and three AITs) based on their depth of knowledge and experience of AI and discussed animal production.

Data Analysis

The study data were checked, coded and entered into the Microsoft Excel (2007) data spreadsheet and completed to make analysis possible. The Statistics Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 25) was used to statistically analyse the data that were collected. The descriptive statistics, like frequency, percentages, mean and tables, were used to express/present the analysed data, whereas Pearson's chi‐square (χ 2) test was used to assess the strength of the association between AI services and different factors. The qualitative data were analysed using thematic coding, and triangulation was achieved by comparing qualitative themes related to AI challenges with the quantitative findings on efficiency and conception rates (CRs).

Way of Communication Between Farmers and Delivery Systems in the Study Area

AI services within the study areas were established a few 25 years back. Since then, the AI service has been delivered in two methods: service centre/stationed and movable service systems. The former is a way in which inseminators are stationed at the AI centre, and person beneficiaries bring their animals from different kebeles of districts to receive service. This service delivery system is better at keeping records, semen keeping and handling procedures and overall service delivery. In movable service delivery systems, AITs move out of the AI centre by using a different approach to transportation (motorcycle, vehicle, bicycle and sometimes public transport) to give service to beneficiaries. Most often this is done on a telephone call basis and sometimes upon a visit to Animal Crush constructed in nearly all peri‐urban kebeles and even to some villages/sites where more dairy animals are available. AITs and dairy beneficiaries in a group discussion showed that these kinds of service delivery systems were facing problems like on‐time service, lack of proper heat detection, poor recording systems and semen handling problems resulting in decreased AI services efficiency in the study area.

The overall frequency study of the primary means of communication between farmers and AITs, as well as the farmers’ responses within the study area, showed the most common methods of communication between them were: We take our cows to the AI service centre; we call AITs when we need them (mobile); AITs come to the home when she/he wants times; and AITs visit daily/always (147 [42%], 64 [24%], 54 [19%] and 42 [15%]), respectively (Table 8, Figure 4).

Discussions

In this study area, the majority of the 93.1% of the surveyed dairy cow owners/households are male, whereas 6.9% are female. This indicated that male households owned the majority of the dairy cattle in three districts that made up the study area production system. This result was lower than the overall percentage of about 75.6% and 24.4%, respectively, for male and female households reported by Menta (2019). This showed that males have more attitudes and involvement in AI operations than women.

In the current study area, the total land area per family is 1.98 ± 0.05 ha. This result is nearly the same as the national average household size of 4.6 people and land holding per household of 1.18 ha per household reported in West Shoa Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia, which is close to the national average (CSA 2005; Menta 2019).

The average of herd composition and size are small differences per family in the research area: 0.80 ± 0.63. Gimbi has less hybrid per family presence (0.67 ± 0.10) compared to Nadjo (0.90 ± 0.123) and Ayira (0.82 ± 0.101). According to this study, on average, local breed cows have lower productivity than crossbreed cows. This is similar to findings from the West Gojam district, the West Shoa district of the Oromia region and Debretabour village in Amahara (Baheriw et al. 2013; Fekata et al. 2020; Woretaw et al. 2015). The mean daily milk yield per cow for indigenous and crossbred cows was 1.18 ± 0.52 and 5.83 ± 0.28 L, respectively.

The beneficiaries of AI in the study area vary from district to district. The higher number of hybrids in the production process in the Nadjo study area may be due to the uniqueness of the insemination process, professional insemination skill, detection of oestrus, better milk production work, availability of technical services and production processes. To manage more improved crossbreed animals and to improve local breeds, it needs properly providing all AI inputs regularly and supporting them with other interventions such as better feeding, housing, health care, and extension services in order to exploit the genetic potential of the animals (Tegegne et al. 2013).

The study area on average predicted age for AFS for local breeds was 3.9 ± 0.76 and for crossbreeds 2.8 ± 0.84; AFC for local breeds was 4.6 ± 0.83 and for crossbreeds 3.6 ± 0.73; and CI for local breeds was 1.9 ± 0.49 and for crossbreeds 1.5 ± 0.38 years. No significant distinction (p > 0.05) was observed between local and crossbred dairy cows managed in selected district locations with regard to AFC and CI. However, AFS those were believed (Bekuma et al. 2020) to have mean CIs of 15.03 and 14.48 months, respectively, in Gimbi West Wallaga. Disagrees with the findings stated by using Legesse (2016) for local and crossbred dairy cows managed in rural and urban areas in the Bona Zuria district of the Sidama zone of Southern Ethiopia, mentioning a CI of 16.04 months (1.37 years) for crossbred cows, which is in agreement with the 1.5 years reported in the current study.

According to the current study, the river water (73.6%), stream water (17%) and pond water (9.4%) were used. This showed that primarily river water is provided to the livestock, followed by streams and ponds. The results of the current study are similar to those reported by Lakew et al. (2017) and Kenfo et al. (2018), which reported that water run‐off was responsible for livestock grazing in the Woliyta and Bensa districts in Southern Ethiopia. For this reason, the use of water run‐off causes health problems in cattle, directly affects the production and reproduction of cattle and increases challenges for AI.

Out of the total 5 years/retrospective data, 2565 inseminated cows, the overall AI efficiency rate and calving rate were 1285 (50%) and 1254 (49%), respectively, which was in line with the study by Temesegen et al. (2021); a total of 350–795 animals were inseminated annually in the Wolemera area of Ethiopia's Oromia regional state between 2012 and 2017, with CRs varying from 50% to 79.9% and calving rates from 48% to 90%. The current result of the study is lower than the overall CRs of 74.67% in dairy cows in and around Kombolcha town (Befikadu et al. 2019) and 64.8% in dairy cows in and around Adama town (Kebebew and Bekele 2018).

The current study is higher than the overall CRs with the finding of Ashebir et al. (2016) in the Tigray Regional State of Ethiopia; from 83,885 semen doses produced (from 2000 to 2010), 51,032 animals were inseminated, and approximately 28,972 (56%) animals were pregnant, with 18,852 (37%) calves delivered and 38.35% in the Adami Tullu East Shoa Zone of the Oromia region (Woldu et al. 2011). From 2009/10 to 2013/14, the national AI centre (now the Livestock Development Institute [LDI]) produced approximately 2.85 million straws of semen, with approximately 2.76 million straws disseminated and 988,073 (35.79%) confirmed pregnancies recorded from regional livestock offices (Tazeze 2019), and this study disagrees with my research.

The AIT's service availability in the current end result over all frequency: It was found that 78.3% of respondents used natural service, whereas only 21.7% of households passed the date without insemination when the AITs were not available, avoided/switched NM and waited for the next 21 days of oestrus cycle. This research supports the finding of Woretaw et al. (2015) report on dairy cattle owners in Debretabour town, Ethiopia, as 29% of the small‐scale farmers chose to bypass the date without insemination, and 44% of respondents in the West Gojjam Zone chose to complete the cycle without breeding (Baheriw et al. 2013).

The current research disagrees with the report of Gizaw et al. (2016); both NM and AI breeding are performed in Ethiopia, but a national‐level research found that approximately 36.0%, 39.3% and 27.5% of the farmers used NM primarily with crossbred bulls, a combination of bull and AI and AI service, respectively. Moreover, disagrees with the report of Juneyid et al. (2017), which reported that 33% of the small‐scale farmers in West Haraghe, Ethiopia, interrupted/passed their AI in order to use natural service. When the cows do not conceive insemination, overall frequency 43.3% of respondents use AI again and again and 56.7% NM.

The present study also correlates with the findings of Bekana et al. (2005), who observed the factors leading to failure of conception were associated with problems like management problems, heat detection problems, time of insemination, unskilled technicians, poor sperm quality, poor semen handling practices and genital tract diseases.

From Monday to Friday, Gimbi (84.8%) and Ayira (82.6%) had a higher percentage of AI service interruption than Nadjo (78.5%). A reported AI service interruption was nearly identical. The differences in AI service disruptions across districts could be caused by a lack of AI inputs and local political unrest. The study district's current AI service interruption does not agree with the finding of the Ibrahim et al. (2014) report, which states that 85% of small‐scale farmers in Jimma Zone, Ethiopia, receive regular AI service. These significant service deviations could be the result of disparities in small‐scale farmers’ awareness and AITs’ commitment.

However, reports that 76% of respondents in the Tiyo district and 60% in the Sagure district do not receive regular AI services are consistent with the disruption/sequence of AI services in the study districts (Mekonnen and Berhe 2020; Menta 2019), and 60% of respondents in Eastern and South‐eastern Tigray regions of Ethiopia stated that they did not receive AI services on weekends or public holidays (Ashebir et al. 2016).

In the Arsi Zone, 68% of small farmers in specific districts reported not receiving AI services on a regular basis, and 55% of respondents said they never received AI (Mekonnen and Berhe 2020; Juneyid et al. 2017). In the Tullo district, West Hararghe, Ethiopia, 51% of respondents reported that AI service was interrupted on weekends and public holidays, and 67% of AITs in the West Gojjam Zone reported that 66% of dairy cattle owners in Debretabour town, Ethiopia, reported that they did not receive regular AI services. Similarly, in the town of Adama, 59% of small‐scale farmers in and around Adama town reported not receiving regular AI services due to various constraints (Baheriw et al. 2013; Woretaw et al. 2015).

The overall frequency of studying found 85.6% of AITs reported that they get AI service regularly; a small number of respondents do not get it (14.4%). This does not agree with the study of Juneyid et al. (2017), who reported 44% of respondents in West Hararghe did not use AI service, whereas 56% of the dairy cattle owners obtained AI service without interruption, and agrees with Ashebir et al. (2016), who reported that 25% of the small‐scale farmers in Eastern and South‐eastern Tigray, Ethiopia.

Of the 277 respondents who provided feedback on the overall frequency in the study areas, 139 (50.2%) expressed satisfaction, whereas approximately 138 (49.8%) expressed dissatisfaction with AI service delivery. The current study has a look at becoming comparable with the report of Duguma et al. (2012), who observed around Addis Ababa dairy producers that about 50% of respondents/half of them were satisfied with the overall AITs’ service delivery system and 50% of AI beneficiaries were not satisfied with the AITs’ service delivery system.

The country and West Wallaga Zone were in political unrest for a long period of time, and it had an impact on livestock production and productivity both in the past and present. These situations have resulted in the losses of resources, no transportation, a shortage of AI service inputs and the avoidance of movement from place to place by both AITs and farmers. The political unrest was expressed in frequent damage to both private and public resources such as farmer teaching centres/FTCs, office equipment, shelters, AI service centres and other farm equipment; this is a real crisis of visions that could bring a total failure of understanding and an unwillingness to work devotionally following this unrest and harassment, especially discouraging private investors in the agricultural sector (FAO 2019).

The current study indicated that the majority of the households’ (58.8%) AIT performance was very good, 19.5% excellent and 21.7% good. This variation may be due to differences in educational heritage and level of activity training of the AITs. The technical understanding of most AITs within the study area was above the category of very good (58.8%). This agrees with the report by GebreMedhin et al. (2009) on the assessment of the technical recognition of AITs, which classified them as excellent (20%), very good (57%) and good (23%). It is not consistent with the record by Baheriw et al. (2013) on AITs in the West Gojjam Zone, which classified them as excellent (15%), very good (40%), good (23%) and poor (22%).

The effect of wrong timing of the AI on the efficiency of AI in the current study was higher than the previous finding of Ibrahim and Seid (2017); assessment of the problems associated with AI service on dairy cattle in Debretabour town, Ethiopia, who reported 40.9% of households inseminate their cows at the right time of insemination, but the rest (59.1%) of households inseminate their cows at the wrong time, and Tegegne et al. (2013), gender and urban dairy production system: The case of Debre Zeit Town, Ethiopia, who showed that 39.8% of respondents have inseminated their cows at the right time of insemination, but the rest (60.2%) of respondents inseminate their cows at the wrong time.

According to the current results, which agree with Kebebew and Bekele (2018) and Sisay et al. (2017), the vast majority of the smallholders reported that their animals showed heat in the afternoon and morning and were inseminated as the sign was visible. Based on various indicators of heat, visual observation is used to detect heat for 1 h in the morning and afternoon. Poor heat detection results in extended CIs, protracted lactation, low milk yield, few calf crops and exposure to time, money and loss from repeated breeding, energy and expense to repeat AI at the centre repeatedly (Tadesse 2010). Most of cow fertility has been achieved when inseminating between the mid‐oestrus and the end of oestrus (Kumar 2005, Woretaw et al. 2015).

The existence of artificial hormones for oestrus synchronization (ES), veterinary service and professionals, the availability of a huge dairy livestock populace, the interest of smallholders and high demands for milk have been most of the opportunities that have been mentioned by many smallholders of dairy cows in Ethiopia (Fekata et al. 2020). The respondents claimed that due to the fact there have been insufficiently progressed cattle inside the study vicinity, to improve dairy cows, one of the number one possibilities applying AI has come to be one of the most essential technologies that plays a crucial role in developing milk production and genetic enhancements of livestock (Mekonnen et al. 2010). Selecting a superior breed for crossing local breed has significant importance for AI utilization and breed improvement. AI increases the selection intensity, as less bull is needed, and it is the basis for selection progress (GebreMedhin et al. 2009).

In this study period, the overall most outstanding constraints of the AI delivery system in the study site were heat detection 71 (25.6%), lack of AI inputs and semen handling 57 (20.6%), management and nutrition 37 (13.4%), timing of insemination 36 (13%), conception failure 26 (9.4%), AI process 15 (5.4%), competence of AITs 12 (4.3%), people awareness 12 (4.3%) and political unrest and conflicts 11 (4%). The results of this investigation are similar to those reported in the study in and around Adama; the major constraints of AI service were conception failure (31.5%), heat detection problem (68.8%), insufficiency of support of the concerned body (19.3%), artificial technician problem (10.5%) and semen and liquid nitrogen not arriving on time (29.8%) (Kebebew and Bekele 2018).

Menta (2019) listed the following as some of the constraints to AI service in the Essera districts of the Dawaro Zone: inability to conceive, lack of semen and liquid nitrogen, problems detecting heat, lack of AI inputs and semen to deal with, management and nutrition, AI method, timing of insemination, competence of AITs, lack of AITs, disruption of weekends and holidays and long distance to get the services. The end results also agree with Sisay et al. (2017), who stated that the Ejere district AI service is limited by a lack of AITs, farmer's long‐distance insurance and discontinuation of service on weekends and holidays.

The major concepts needing attention to improve AI services for the future were AIT training 82 (30%), AIT motivation 80 (29%), adequate provision of all AI inputs 65 (23%) and infrastructure improvement 50 (18%), which were first, second, third and fourth for AI services improving for the future, respectively. The results also agree with ideas (GebreMedhin et al. 2009) of working closely with structural linkage, infrastructure improvement, AIT training and herd recording systems and providing adequate resources in terms of inputs and facilities and rewards to motivate AITs.

Conclusions and Recommendations

The study is characterized by cattle AI efficiency, challenges and opportunities in the area. The AI technologies have been utilized in West Wallaga for genetic improvement of cows for several years in the past. However, its efficiency is influenced via factors which include infrastructure, managerial and financial constraints and also technical issues, which include AI system, AIT competence, lack of useful AI resource inputs, heat detection, timing of insemination, management and nutrition, people awareness, cow‐associated factors, semen quality, political unrest and conflicts; these are some of the present‐day issues facing the zones. The future of study area is bright because of the truth that the study area is gifted with a variable climate and a large number of domestic cows; that's opportunities for AI, interest of farmers, large cattle resources and the best breeds for crossbreeding, access to services and land inputs/feed, and income generation/market and employment were first, second, third and fourth most attractive opportunities, respectively. According to the current end result, the primary constraint of AI efficiency in the study place was heat detection, with lack of AI inputs and semen handling and management coming in as the second and third constraints, respectively. Overcoming these challenges is not an easy task. Based on the above conclusions, the following recommendations were forwarded:

AI technology requires committed and greater efforts of government and its experts/people. Moreover, other stakeholders like non‐governmental and funding agencies are required to contribute towards solving the critical problems facing them.

The government should prepare sustainable training dissemination services to address AI service issues; to improve the skills of AITs and animal owners on heat detection and proper selection of cows/heifers with good physical condition, cyclic animals and good management and health is required to improve the efficacy of AI.
