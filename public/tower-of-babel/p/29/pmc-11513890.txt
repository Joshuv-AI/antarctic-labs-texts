Discovery of KT-474—a Potent, Selective, and Orally Bioavailable IRAK4 Degrader for the Treatment of Autoimmune Diseases
Authors: Xiaozhang Zheng, Nan Ji, Veronica Campbell, Anthony Slavin, Xiao Zhu, Dapeng Chen, Haojing Rong, Brad Enerson, Michele Mayo, Kirti Sharma, Chris M Browne, Christine R Klaus, Haoran Li, Ginny Massa, Alice A McDonald, Yatao Shi, Mike Sintchak, Stephanie Skouras, Dirk M Walther, Karen Yuan, Yi Zhang, Joe Kelleher, Guang Liu, Xinbo Luo, Nello Mainolfi, Matthew M Weiss
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin-1 receptor associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) is an essential mediator of the IL-1R and TLR signaling pathways, both of which have been implicated in multiple autoimmune conditions. Hence, blocking the activity of IRAK4 represents an attractive approach for the treatment of autoimmune diseases. The activity of this serine/threonine kinase is dependent on its kinase and scaffolding activities; thus, degradation represents a potentially superior approach to inhibition. Herein, we detail the exploration of structure–activity relationships that ultimately led to the identification of KT-474, a potent, selective, and orally bioavailable heterobifunctional IRAK4 degrader. This represents the first heterobifunctional degrader evaluated in a nononcology indication and dosed to healthy human volunteers. This molecule successfully completed phase I studies in healthy adult volunteers and patients with atopic dermatitis or hidradenitis suppurativa. Phase II clinical trials in both of these indications have been initiated.

Introduction

The interleukin-1 receptor associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) is a key mediator of the interleukin 1 receptor (IL-1R) and toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway. TLR and IL-1R activation via their cognate ligands leads to a hierarchical signaling cascade beginning with recruitment of the adapter protein MyD88 and subsequent recruitment and phosphorylation of IRAK4 and other family members such as IRAK2 to form a large multiprotein complex known as the myddosome.1,2 IRAK4 serves a key role in signal transduction of the myddosome, as both its kinase and scaffold functions are required for proper myddosome assembly and signaling.3−7 The myddosome complex triggers activation of downstream pathways such as NFkB and interferon response factors 5/7 (IRF 5/7), culminating in the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as TNF-α, IL-6 IL-1β, IL-33, and IL-36. These cytokines have been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple autoimmune conditions such as hidradenitis suppurativa, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis.8,9 IRAK4 is therefore a promising therapeutic target to attenuate TLR/IL-1R-mediated inflammation. However, IRAK4 kinase inhibitors developed as therapeutics for chronic inflammatory and autoimmune diseases have had limited clinical success, potentially due to an inability to target the key scaffolding function of IRAK4 (Figure 1).10,11 In light of this, IRAK4 is ideally suited for targeted protein degradation, an approach that would enable the complete removal of the protein.

One approach for targeted protein degradation leverages heterobifunctional molecules to bring together an E3 ligase with a protein of interest to mediate ubiquitination of the target protein and subsequent degradation by the proteosome.12 Thus, heterobifunctional degraders afford an opportunity to address multifunctional therapeutic targets like IRAK4 that have both kinase and scaffolding functions, where ablation should serve to eliminate all activity of the therapeutic target and potentially be superior to small molecule inhibitors that are unable to address the scaffolding effects of the protein of interest.13

The design of potent and selective IRAK4 degraders described herein led to the identification of KT-474, an orally bioavailable heterobifunctional degrader currently in phase II clinical trials for hidradenitis suppurativa and atopic dermatitis patients (NCT06028230 and NCT06058156).

Results and Discussion

Upon initiation of this work, limited descriptions of IRAK4 degraders were reported in the literature. These degraders typically utilized either the von Hippel–Lindau protein (VHL) or cereblon protein (CRBN) as the E3 ligase and achieved only moderately potent degradation of IRAK4.14,15 The work discussed herein focuses on the utilization of CRBN.

Many of the CRBN-based first generation heterobifunctional degraders employed CRBN ligands such as thalidomide, lenalidomide, and pomalidomide (POM, 1), which are also known as immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs). It is well-known that IMiDs have a propensity to degrade a host of transcription factors, including but not limited to Ikaros, Aiolos, and SALL4. This class of compounds, most notably lenalidomide, has demonstrated remarkable success in a number of oncology indications. However, the degradation of this class of transcription factors (e.g., Ikaros and Aiolos) manifests in a number of adverse effects such as neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, effects that would significantly limit the utility of IMiDs in nononcology indications. Furthermore, the degradation of SALL4 has been associated with teratogenic toxicities.16−20 IMiDs induce the degradation of these transcription factors by serving as molecular glues. These are monovalent molecules that, in the case of IMiDs, engage CRBN and facilitate a neo-interface between this E3 ligase, the target protein (e.g., Ikaros), and the molecular glue.21 This ternary complex subsequently leads to proteosome-mediated degradation of the target protein. The mechanism by which IMiDs recruit these neosubstrates has been very well characterized and results from the recognition of a common structural degron containing a key glycine residue also known as the G-loop.22 To eliminate the risk of IMiD-associated adverse effects, we undertook a structure-based drug design approach to identify novel CRBN binders devoid of IMiD substrate degradation. Evaluation of the ternary complex structure of CRBN:POM:Aiolos suggests that the phenyl ring of the phthalimide group in POM may play a critical role in the engagement and stabilization of the complex by simultaneously interacting with the Gly151 of the degron motif and Pro352 of CRBN through CH/π interactions (Figure 2A).23 Based on these observations, our designs focused on mitigating the engagement of Gly151 while maintaining interactions with Pro352. To achieve this goal, we designed molecules wherein the aromatic ring of the ligand was in closer proximity to CRBN. This modification in turn required conversion of the succinimide motif to a cyclic urea to preserve key interactions with CRBN. This work ultimately led to the identification of compound 2 (Figure 2B), a compound that demonstrated improved CRBN binding affinity relative to POM (IC50: 0.25 μM vs 1.51 μM, respectively) and, more importantly, did not induce degradation of Ikaros or Aiolos at concentrations up to 10 μM. In addition to maintaining affinity to CRBN and dialing out the IMiD activity, it was important to retain the favorable drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics (DMPK) properties of the parent IMiDs. Toward this end, it was found that compound 2 demonstrated significantly reduced passive permeability (Papp) relative to POM when evaluated in a Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell line (Papp: 4.0 vs 25.4 × 10–6 cm/s). This reduced permeability led to a significant drop in exposure, relative to POM, when dosed orally to rats. We hypothesized that the reduced passive permeability was a result of the addition of free NH present on the cyclic urea. This additional hydrogen bond donor (HBD) was removed by the addition of a methyl group on the urea to provide 3. This compound maintained high affinity to CRBN (IC50 = 0.20 μM) and showed significantly improved passive permeability relative to 2. This improved passive permeability was accompanied by a significant boost in exposure upon dosing orally with rats (Table 1). Our design strategy to eliminate the risk of IMiD-associated adverse effects was further confirmed in a subsequent X-ray cocrystal structure of 3 with CRBN. Interestingly, the methyl group which was originally installed to remove the free NH of the cyclic urea was shown to sterically clash with the degron (Figure 2C) potentially contributing to further elimination of the G-loop degron recruitment.

The degradative selectivity of 3 was evaluated by using tandem mass tag-based global discovery proteomics. Treatment of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with 1 μM of 3 for 24 h did not impact protein levels of any IMiD substrates (Figure 3). To determine if 3 induced the degradation of the transcription factor SALL4, we evaluated 3 in a SALL4 HiBiT assay in the SKNDZ cell line and found that degradation of this transcription factor was not observed (DC50, >10 μM; Dmax, 0%; details in Supplementary Figure 1). In light of the lack of IMiD activity, improved binding affinity to CRBN and favorable DMPK properties of 3 we leveraged this ligand in the preparation of subsequent heterobifunctional degraders of IRAK4 (Table 1). It should be noted that this ligand contains a chiral center at position 3 of the glutarimide ring. It is well-known that this moiety is prone to racemization, hence all compounds discussed below, with the exception of compounds 20 and 21, are racemic at this stereocenter.24−26 This phenomenon will be discussed in more detail below.

With the discovery of this new CRBN ligand, our attention shifted to identification of an IRAK4 ligand as a starting point for degrader design. We prepared degraders utilizing a large number of different IRAK4 ligands, including binders reported in the literature as well as novel IRAK4 ligands designed at Kymera.10,27,28 The work culminating with the identification of KT-474 utilized 4 as a starting point for the IRAK4 ligand. This compound demonstrated potent binding to IRAK4 and a favorable kinome selectivity profile (% of kinases > 100X, >95% over 101 kinases).29

As illustrated in Figure 4B and C, modeling of compound 4 in the ATP binding pocket of IRAK4 suggested that the N-methyl group on the pyrazole ring provided a potential exit vector to which a linker could be appended to enable the preparation of heterobifunctional degraders. In this model, compound 4 makes several favorable contacts with IRAK4, including a hydrogen bond with hinge residue Met265, a salt-bridge, and a hydrogen bond with Asp329 and backbone carbonyl of Ala315, respectively. Compound 4 also engages with the gatekeeper Tyr264 residue through π–π interactions (Figure 4B and D). An initial set of degraders was prepared to determine whether this exit vector could induce IRAK4 degradation (Table 2). A series of degraders containing flexible linkers of varying lengths was prepared, of which representative examples are shown in Table 2. It was determined that linkers of 11–12 Å were optimal for degradation (compounds 5 and 6) as compounds with shorter linkers (7) did not induce degradation. The disparity in the degradative activity of these compounds is also manifested in the propensity of these compounds to induce a ternary complex with CRBN and IRAK4. Both 5 and 6 demonstrated robust ternary complex formation while 7 did not induce a ternary complex with CRBN and IRAK4. We have previously demonstrated that, within the context of IRAK4 heterobifunctional degraders, the incorporation of a cyclohexyl moiety immediately off the protein of interest ligand and the appropriate placement of a basic amine in the linker could significantly boost affinity for IRAK4.28 This improved affinity was driven by the ability of these motifs to engage Ile185 and Asp278, respectively (Figure 4D). The enhanced affinity of 6 to IRAK4, relative to 5, led to a significant boost (10-fold) in the degradation efficiency compared to 5.

Upon achieving single-digit nanomolar degradation potency, our efforts shifted to improving the drug-like properties of our degraders with the goal to achieve oral bioavailability. This work initially focused on modifying the IRAK4 ligand to enable oral absorption. To establish a benchmark against which to gauge progress, compound 6 was profiled in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, this compound was found to demonstrate modest passive permeability (Papp = 4.8 × 10–6 cm/s), and when evaluated in an MDCK cell line expressing MDR1 was found to exhibit a high efflux ratio (ER = 35). When dosed to rats, this compound demonstrated a very high in vivo clearance and no oral absorption (Table 3).

A key motif of the IRAK4 ligand contained in the compounds in Table 2 is the amino group appended to the piperidine. This moiety interacts with Asp329 and Ala315 residues of IRAK4 and is critical for maintaining potent affinity (Figure 4B). We have previously demonstrated that the oral absorption of heterobifunctional degraders can be significantly impacted by the number of HBDs.28 Hence, a focus of our work was aimed at removing one or both of the HBDs contained within the aminopiperidine with the understanding that this could potentially negatively impact binding to IRAK4. First, we tried to remove this amino piperidine motif completely or replace this motif with a much smaller dimethylamino group, leading to compounds 8 and 9, respectively. Indeed, removal of both of the HBDs contained within the aminopiperidine led to a significant decrease in affinity for IRAK4, and in turn neither compound demonstrated degradation of IRAK4 up to concentrations of 10 μM (Table 4). However, we were encouraged to see that these changes led to significantly improved passive permeability and reduced efflux. Following extensive exploration of this portion of the IRAK4 ligand, we identified piperazines such as that contained within 10 as a unique motif that contained one less HBD, relative to 6, yet maintained good affinity and demonstrated robust degradation. Although this compound contained one less HBD donor, this unfortunately did not significantly impact passive permeability or efflux relative to 6, both remaining suboptimal. Methylation of the piperazine NH led to 11, which did demonstrate improved permeability and reduced efflux; however, binding affinity to IRAK4 was reduced over 120-fold compared to 6. Encouraged by the permeability and efflux of 11 as well as the potency of 10, we modified piperazine to the corresponding bridged morpholines 12 and 13. Although this change led to a drop in affinity for IRAK4, both of these compounds demonstrated potent degradation as well as permeability and efflux that compared favorably to 6. Interestingly, while 11 demonstrated affinity for IRAK4 and CRBN comparable to 12 and 13, this compound did not induce significant degradation of IRAK4 when tested up to 10 μM. The rationale for the lack of degradation is unclear, but this highlights some of the challenges that can be associated with degrader optimization. Compound 13 was slightly more potent than its diastereomeric counterpart 12 and demonstrated slightly better permeability and lower efflux and, hence, was advanced into rat pharmacokinetic experiments. We were pleased to find that, while this compound exhibited elevated iv clearance, it demonstrated a measurable level of oral absorption (3.2% F; Table 3).

Having successfully designed an IRAK4 ligand that enabled the identification of heterobifunctional degraders with encouraging potency and permeability, efforts shifted to improving the overall DMPK profile of this class of degraders (Table 5). Of particular focus was improving the metabolic stability, wherein one of the approaches taken involved rigidification of the linker. Conversion of the 1,3 aminoalkoxy group of 13 into an alkoxy piperidine generated 14, a compound which retained degradation potency and had slightly improved metabolic stability in both human and rat liver microsomes. Interestingly, this change led to a reduced passive permeability and a lower potential for efflux. When evaluated in vivo, 14 demonstrated slightly improved iv clearance but oral absorption remained comparable to 13.

Removal of the ether in 14 led to 15, which demonstrated further improved metabolic stability in both human and rat liver microsomes; however, this improvement was accompanied by a 5× loss in potency.

Ternary complex modeling suggested that the oxygen atom in the linker may engage in a hydrogen bond with His353 in CRBN (Figure 5) and that the loss of this interaction was responsible for the reduced degradation potency of 15. This SAR was seen across a number of compounds and in turn helped prioritize linkers that retained this oxygen atom in the linker. Subsequent work focused on further rigidification of the linker, as exemplified by 16. The profile of this compound is illustrative of congeners identified through these efforts. Namely, it retained potent degradation of IRAK4 but demonstrated high turnover in human liver microsomes and suffered from slightly elevated efflux. With a focus on attenuating the polarity of the oxygen atom in the linker, an acetylene moiety was incorporated into the linker, generating propargyl ether 17. Quantum mechanical calculations using a model system confirmed that the electron density of this oxygen atom was significantly reduced on going from 16 to 17 (Supplementary Figure S2), and this in turn led to improved passive permeability and efflux. This modification was noteworthy in its ability to merge potency and favorable stability in human liver microsomes while also mitigating the potential for multidrug resistance [MDR1]-mediated efflux, leading to good oral bioavailability when evaluated in rats (F = 12%; Table 3). In an attempt to further shield the polarity of the oxygen atom in the linker, a methyl group was incorporated adjacent to the ether oxygen, leading to 19. While this modification did lead to a further reduction in the potential for MDR1-mitigated efflux, it also led to a 10× loss in potency. The potency of this series of heterobifunctional degraders was very sensitive to the attachment point on the CRBN ligand, as illustrated by the loss of potency when the linker was appended to the 5 position of the imidazolidinone ring (compound 18).

In light of the overall profile of compound 17, this compound was selected for additional profiling. The cross-species DMPK profile for this compound is illustrated in Table 6. Compound 17 showed moderate to high plasma clearances, high volumes of distribution at steady state (Vss), and moderate terminal half-lives (t1/2) across species. As noted previously, all the compounds discussed thus far are a mixture of (R) and (S) isomers at the 3 position of the glutarimide ring. Separation of the individual isomers provides diastereomers 20 and 21. It is well documented that this center can be prone to racemization,28,32 hence, efforts were undertaken to understand the propensity of racemization both in vitro and in vivo. The absolute stereochemistries of 20 and 21 were assigned based on a significant amount of literature, which demonstrates that the (S)-enantiomer of glutarimide-based CRBN binders more potently binds CRBN.33 Compound 20 had significantly greater affinity for CRBN relative to 21, and hence, the absolute stereochemistry of this compound was assigned as (S). Interestingly, while there was a large disparity in binding affinity of 20 and 21 to CRBN, the difference in the cellular degradation potency between the two diastereomers was significantly smaller (3-fold; Table 7). To explore whether the discrepancy between binding affinity and cellular activity was driven by in vitro racemization, the epimerization rate was evaluated in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The half-lives of conversion from 20 to 21 was 5.3 h, while the half-lives of the conversion from 21 to 20 was 5.2 h. The lack of a significant shift in the degradation potencies is likely driven by the longer time frame of this assay (4 h) relative to that of the CRBN binding assay (15 min).

Based on the characteristics discussed above and a favorable preclinical safety package, 17 was selected as a development candidate (KT-474) and entered into a phase I clinical trial (NCT04772885)—a single ascending dose and multiple ascending dose trial of KT-474 in healthy adult volunteers and patients with atopic dermatitis or hidradenitis suppurativa. Here, dose dependent exposure increases, and dose-dependent IRAK4 degradation in PBMCs was observed in this phase 1 trial. The robust IRAK4 degradation in PBMCs gave rise to a significant reduction of disease relevant cytokines in ex vivo stimulated PBMCs from these patients where evaluation of both hidradenitis suppurativa and atopic dermatitis patients was associated with preliminary clinical activity.35

Conclusion

Herein, we have reported the drug discovery efforts resulting in the identification of KT-474, a molecule that induces the potent degradation of IRAK4. Optimization efforts focused on achieving robust and selective degradation of IRAK4 that was done within the context of achieving favorable pharmacokinetics, including oral absorption. This effort ultimately led to KT-474, which demonstrated robust efficacy in preclinical in vivo models and subsequently preliminary activity in a phase I clinical trial. This heterobifunctional molecule is currently being evaluated in a phase II clinical trials (NCT06028230 and NCT06058156).
