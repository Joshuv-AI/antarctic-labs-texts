Background

Periorbital abscesses, including both preseptal and orbital forms, most commonly arise as complications of sinusitis or odontogenic infections [1, 2]. These infections may progress rapidly and require prompt diagnosis and aggressive management in order to prevent severe complications such as orbital cellulitis, intracranial spread, and permanent vision loss. Standard management generally consists of systemic antibiotic therapy combined with surgical drainage when an abscess is present.

The Streptococcus anginosus group (SAG), which includes S. anginosus, S. intermedius, and S. constellatus, comprises commensal bacteria that colonize mucosal surfaces of the oral cavity, pharynx, gastrointestinal tract, and genitourinary tract. Although typically part of the normal flora, these organisms are well known for their marked tendency to cause abscess formation [3]. Among them, Streptococcus constellatus has been particularly associated with aggressive pyogenic infections characterized by tissue necrosis and recurrent abscess formation.

Despite the known abscess-forming propensity of SAG organisms, the literature provides limited guidance regarding the optimal management of orbital or periorbital abscesses specifically caused by S. constellatus. Conventional management strategies generally rely on surgical drainage combined with systemic antibiotics. However, in clinical practice, infections caused by S. constellatus may demonstrate a more aggressive course, with rapid re-accumulation of purulent material and extensive necrotic debris, potentially rendering standard single-drainage approaches insufficient.

In this report, we describe two complex cases of periorbital abscess caused by S. constellatus in which conventional management proved inadequate. Based on our experience, we propose a more aggressive management strategy involving repeated surgical drainage, local antibiotic irrigation, and adjunctive therapies to control infection and prevent recurrence. These cases highlight the unique clinical characteristics of S. constellatus infections and underscore the need for additional treatment approaches.

Case presentation

Case 1

A previously healthy 25-year-old male presented with a three-day history of a left maxillary dental abscess, followed by one day of ipsilateral periorbital pain, erythema, and swelling. On examination, best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye (OD) and 20/100 in the left eye (OS) (LogMAR 0.70). Pupillary responses were normal, with no relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). The left lower eyelid exhibited marked erythema and edema, with limitations of -3 in upgaze and − 2 in downgaze. Orbital MRI revealed left orbital cellulitis associated with a subperiosteal abscess adjacent to the maxilla (Fig. 1A and B).

Orbital MRI T1-weighted image (A) coronal, (B) axial image, abscess and maxillary sinusitis image starting from periorbital and extending subperiosteally. (C) The dirty gray colored membranous structure extending from the abscess during the drainage of the patient’s recurrent periorbital abscess, (D) the placement of an ointment-soaked tampon in the abscess cavity, (E) the appearance of cicatricial ectropion due to fibrosis in the healing process, (F) the patient’s image 1 month after subcutaneous 5-Fluorouracil application

The patient was admitted, and empirical intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g q12h) and clindamycin (600 mg q8h) were initiated. Functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS) along with abscess drainage was performed under general anesthesia; purulent material was collected for culture. Despite initial intervention, the collection persisted over the following three days (Fig. 1C). During subsequent drainage, the abscess material was found to be necrotic (grayish in color), with membranous extensions and an extremely foul odor. Following consultation with the infectious diseases department, antibiotics were escalated to ceftazidime (2 g q8h) and vancomycin (1 g q12h). Furthermore, the abscess cavity was irrigated with ceftazidime, and a local antibiotic-impregnated pack was placed to prevent re-accumulation (Fig. 1D). Cultures identified S. constellatus, sensitive to penicillin, clindamycin, and cephalosporins. The clinical signs gradually resolved. One month later, subcutaneous 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) was administered twice at 3-week intervals to mitigate potential ectropion due to residual fibrosis (Fig. 1E). At the two-month follow-up, complete functional and aesthetic recovery was achieved (Fig. 1F).

Case 2

An 18-year-old male with autism spectrum disorder, epilepsy, and cognitive impairment presented with a two-day history of left periorbital swelling. Physical examination revealed significant periorbital edema, erythema, and warmth; pupillary testing could not be performed due to severe eyelid edema. Anterior segment examination suggested opaque membranous structures in the inferior cornea and conjunctival edema; however, visualization was limited. The patient had a history of poor oral hygiene and recurrent toothaches. Due to the patient’s agitation, clinical assessment remained suboptimal. Orbital computed tomography (CT) scans revealed widespread soft tissue edema and a preseptal abscess involving the upper and lower eyelids (Fig. 2A and B).

(A) Axial and (B) Sagittal CT images show maxillary sinusitis with periorbital edema and air densities, (C) Severe periorbital edema and hyperemia at the patient’s first presentation, (D, E) Skin lesion and foul, gray discharge after abscess drainage, (F) The patient’s periorbital edema regressed on the 10th day

The patient was hospitalized and commenced on intravenous vancomycin (1 g q12h) and sulbactam/ampicillin (3 g q6h), complemented by topical vancomycin and ceftazidime. When the abscess recurred on the second day, repeated drainage and antibiotic irrigation were employed, mirroring the management of the previous case (Fig. 2C). Surgical drainage was performed daily for five days, accompanied by ceftazidime irrigation (Fig. 2D and E). The drainage revealed the same characteristics observed in Case 1: a dirty-gray appearance, membranous extensions, and a putrid odor. Cultures again yielded S. constellatus. Systemic antibiotics combined with local management led to rapid resolution of the inflammation. The patient was discharged with significant functional and cosmetic recovery on day 10 (Fig. 2F).

Discussion and conclusions

Periorbital abscesses typically arise from the contiguous spread of sinusitis or dental infections. If left untreated, these infections can precipitate vision-threatening and life-threatening complications, such as cavernous sinus thrombosis [1, 2]. Although the SAG has been implicated in brain and liver abscesses, periorbital involvement remains rare and frequently refractory to standard therapy [4, 5]. SAG organisms, particularly S. constellatus, have an established but underrecognized role in deep-seated head and neck abscesses due to their invasive virulence factors, including hyaluronidase and streptolysins [6]. Clinically, S. constellatus presents diagnostic challenges owing to prolonged culture times and phenotypic overlap with other SAG members [7].

Several cases of orbital or periorbital infections associated with S. constellatus have been reported in the literature, although they remain relatively uncommon [8–14]. These reports consistently highlight the aggressive nature of this organism and its strong association with odontogenic infections. As summarized in Table 1, previously reported cases frequently presented with severe orbital inflammation and abscess formation, occasionally accompanied by life-threatening complications such as cavernous sinus thrombosis or septic embolism. Visual outcomes varied considerably, ranging from complete recovery to permanent visual impairment [8, 11]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potentially destructive clinical course of S. constellatus infections and the frequent need for prompt surgical intervention in addition to systemic antibiotic therapy. In contrast to earlier reports, our cases provide a detailed description of the intraoperative morphology of the abscess material. Specifically, we observed necrotic grayish purulent material with distinct membranous extensions and a pronounced putrid odor (Fig. 1C). These membranous structures may act as a physical barrier that limits antibiotic penetration and facilitates persistent bacterial sequestration within the abscess cavity. Such characteristics may partly explain the refractory clinical course observed in some patients and the tendency for recurrent purulent accumulation despite appropriate systemic therapy.

Consistent with our observations, previously reported cases often required secondary drainage despite aggressive initial management [8, 9]. In the present cases, the extension of the abscess into the preseptal space facilitated the direct clinical observation of these distinctive, aggressive features. Recurrent collections, despite initial intervention, were effectively managed by integrating a daily local antibiotic irrigation protocol into the surgical drainage regimen. We think this intensive approach, combining serial surgical drainage with targeted ceftazidim irrigation, delivers a significant therapeutic improvement compared to traditional treatment, which often involves a single surgical intervention. To the best of our knowledge, this report represents the first description of such an intensive local irrigation strategy specifically tailored to counteract the uniquely recalcitrant and necrotic nature of S. constellatus infections in the periorbital region.

Consequently, while most subperiosteal orbital abscesses secondary to sinusitis resolve with effective functional FESS and a single drainage, this approach appears insufficient for S. constellatus infections due to their high tendency for re-collection. Furthermore, the management of long-term sequelae is a distinctive aspect of our report. The tissue destruction caused by this pathogen can lead to significant fibrosis. Our use of subcutaneous 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) to alleviate scarring and prevent cicatricial ectropion (Fig. 1F) offers a comprehensive management perspective that extends beyond the acute phase of infection.

Repeated surgical drainage combined with local antibiotic irrigation may shorten the disease course and reduce the need for extensive revision surgery.

Study Limitations: Despite the successful outcomes achieved with our intensive management protocol, this study has several limitations. First, the fact that our study was limited to 2 cases limits the generalizability of our findings to a broader clinical context. Second, while we emphasize the importance of identifying specific abscess morphology, the rarity of such detailed descriptions in the literature makes it challenging to establish these features as definitive diagnostic criteria for S. constellatus. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the efficacy of repeated local antibiotic irrigation.
