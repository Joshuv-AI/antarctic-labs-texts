Mapping heterogeneous brain structural subtypes in alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment using normative models
Authors: Xiaotong Wei, Tingting Zhang, Ronglong Xiong, Qiuzhu Zhang, Junjun Zhang, Zhenlan Jin, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a heterogeneous disorder with individual variability in clinical and biological features. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents transition phase and provides a critical window for early intervention. Clinically applicable metrics for quantifying individual brain structural variations remain limited. This study aimed to delineate neuroanatomical heterogeneity and identify brain structural subtypes using individual deviation analysis. We constructed normative models of regional gray matter volume across lifespan using T1-weighted MRI data from 1185 healthy controls in the Cam-CAN and OASIS-3 cohorts. Individual deviations from normative trajectories were evaluated for AD and MCI patients in the ADNI dataset. Based on deviation patterns, unsupervised clustering analysis was used to identify distinct AD and MCI subtypes. Clinical features, longitudinal progression, and gene expression profiles were evaluated for each subtype. We found that substantial heterogeneity in the spatial distribution and severity of structural abnormalities across individuals. Two robust subtypes were identified in each group. Subtype 2 of AD and MCI showed more pronounced negative deviation patterns than subtype 1, particularly in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and amygdala. These subtypes demonstrated significant differences in cognitive performance, biomarker profiles, disease progression, and gene expression patterns. AD and MCI subtypes exhibited similar deviation patterns, and MCI subtype with more severe negative deviations had a higher risk of converting to AD. This study reveals the neuroanatomical heterogeneity of AD and MCI and demonstrates that individualized deviation mapping can detect clinically valuable subtypes. These findings provide a foundation for personalized diagnosis and monitoring strategies in neurodegenerative diseases.Subject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the deterioration of multiple cognitive domains, particularly affecting memory functions [1]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is regarded as a transitional phase between normal aging and AD, offering a critical window for early intervention [2]. While some patients with MCI may remain stable or even return to normal cognitive levels, others progress to AD at an annual rate of 10–15% [3–5]. This heterogeneity reflects individual differences in disease progression, which poses challenges for clinical diagnosis and management. Given that AD and MCI represent different stages of the disease continuum, understanding their heterogeneity is crucial for predicting disease progression and developing personalized treatment strategies.

Both AD and MCI exhibit significant heterogeneity in clinical manifestations, neuroimaging features, and progression rates [6, 7]. The heterogeneous subtypes of AD and MCI have been investigated based on cognitive and biological features [8]. Subtyping research using neuropsychological assessments have revealed cognitive variants within each condition [9], but similar clinical phenotypes may exhibit distinct underlying neuropathological processes, which could be attributed to temporal variability and symptom subjectivity [10, 11]. Hence, relying solely on clinical profiles may obscure underlying biological diversity, indicating the need for more sensitive and objective markers to better identify potential disease subtypes.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have detected distinct atrophy patterns in AD, such as medial-temporal or parieto-occipital atrophy, and mild or diffuse cortical atrophy, with corresponding MCI subtypes being similarly recognized [12–15]. These subtype-specific atrophy patterns are linked to distinct cognitive profiles and biomarker signatures, suggesting that structural features had the potential to identify clinically and biologically meaningful subgroups [7]. Although previous studies have investigated the heterogeneity of AD and MCI separately, a unified exploration of their neurobiological subtypes is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiological continuum. However, traditional subtyping approaches define subtypes at the group level and subsequently assign individuals to these categories, which may fail to adequately capture subject-specific deviation patterns [16].

Normative model is a statistical framework for quantifying individual deviations in cognitive, behavioral, or neuroimaging measures relative to a healthy reference population [17]. Analogous to pediatric growth charts that statistically define normal ranges of height and weight by age and sex, normative model can establish typical trajectories of brain development and aging across the lifespan [18]. In recent years, this method has been increasingly applied to identify individual deviations from healthy norms in heterogeneous neuropsychiatric disorders [19–22]. By establishing expected distributions for brain features in a large reference database, an individual’s brain image can then be compared against these norms to determine whether they fall below or above the expected values for their age and sex. Unlike conventional case-control comparisons that focus on group-level abnormalities, normative model can capture individual deviations from typical trajectories without relying on predefined categories [23, 24]. The patterns of individual deviations derived from normative models can be further used to discover potential disease subtypes, offering a typing foundation for personalized precision diagnosis [21, 22].

In this study, we employed a novel normative model approach to investigate the neurobiological heterogeneity of AD and MCI using brain structural features. This approach enabled the quantitative assessment of individual neuroanatomical deviations from population norms. Subsequently, we identified neurobiological subtypes of AD and MCI based on these deviation patterns. The differences between subtypes were evaluated in cognitive performance, pathological markers, and longitudinal progression. Furthermore, we explored the association between brain deviation patterns of each subtype and transcriptomic gene expression data to examine whether structural variability is grounded on key genetic foundations. Materials and methods

Participants

In this study, we collected structural MRI (sMRI) scans and clinical data from three publicly available datasets. The reference cohort comprised 1185 cognitively normal individuals (CN) aged 18 to 92 years, sourced from two datasets: the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN; n = 645; http://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/datasets/camcan) [25, 26] and the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies-3 (OASIS-3; n = 540; https://www.oasis-brains.org) [27]. The clinical discovery dataset included 334 CN controls, 601 MCI, and 293 AD patients from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 1/GO/2/3 database (http://adni.loni.usc.edu). The demographic characteristics of the samples are summarized in Table S1. Additionally, we obtained longitudinal sMRI scans and clinical assessments from ADNI participants (Table S2). The detailed inclusion criteria for each diagnostic category are available on the ADNI website (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data-samples/adni-data/study-cohort-information). For independent clinical validation, we also collected 185 MCI and 55 AD patients from the OASIS-3 dataset (Table S3). All participants or legal representatives gave written informed consent. For more detailed information, see Figure S1 and Supplementary Method S1.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

T1-weighted (T1W) sMRI acquisition details are available at https://camcan-archive.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/dataaccess/pdfs/CAMCAN700_MR_params.pdf, https://sites.wustl.edu/oasisbrains/home/oasis-3, and https://adni.loni.usc.edu/data-samples/adni-data/neuroimaging/mri. All T1W images were processed using a standard voxel-based morphometry (VBM) pipeline, including anterior commissure alignment, tissue segmentation, spatial normalization, modulation, and smooth (Supplementary Method S2). The Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas was used to parcellate the cerebrum into 90 cortical and subcortical regions (Table S4) [28]. For each subject scan, regional gray matter volume (GMV) and intracranial volume (ICV) were extracted. Next, ComBat harmonization was applied to eliminate site effects on the GMV values while preserving biological covariates of interest, including age, sex, and disease status (Supplementary Method S3) [29].

Normative models for GMV

For each cerebral region, we established a normative model of GMV using Gaussian process regression (GPR) in the reference cohort from 1185 CN subjects (Fig. 1B) [17]. Briefly, as a non-parametric Bayesian interpolation approach, GPR can robustly fit nonlinear relationships between variables while providing consistent measures of predictive confidence and point estimates [30]. In this study, we used GPR to assess expected variations of brain features based on age and sex and estimate the uncertainty in these variations. To evaluate the generalizability of normative models, we adopted 10-fold cross-validation in the healthy population and then applied the trained normative models to other individuals for subsequent deviation analyses. A total of 90 regional normative models were developed to characterize age-related GMV trajectories across distinct sex groups (Supplementary Method S4).

The z score estimates how much an individual’s features deviate from the normative pattern in each brain region. Thus, each individual’s deviation map was presented by regional deviation z scores.

We defined extreme deviations with a threshold at |z | > 2.6 (corresponding to a P < 0.005), which demarcated both extreme negative (z < -2.6) and positive (z > 2.6) deviations [19, 21, 24]. For each participant, we calculated the whole-brain mean deviation and the number of regions showing extreme deviations and compared group differences using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc t-tests (P < 0.05). To assess intersubject heterogeneity, we generated spatial overlap maps by calculating the percentage of individuals with extreme deviations in each region. Group-level mean deviation patterns were visualized by averaging z scores across participants, with between-group differences assessed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/90). In addition, a linear support vector machine (SVM) classifier was used to evaluate whether individual deviation patterns could differentiate diagnostic groups or predict MCI conversion (Supplementary Method S5).

Subtypes identification based on individual deviation patterns

We performed a data-driven K-means clustering analysis based on individual whole-brain deviation patterns to explore AD and MCI subtypes. Specifically, the z score vectors of all 90 regions per patient were used as clustering features. Prior to determining the optimal number of clusters, the Gap statistic (including the one-cluster solution, k = 1) was calculated to evaluate whether meaningful clustering structure existed in the data [31]. Subsequently, an automatic grid search strategy was used to optimize clustering hyperparameters [32, 33], and the product of Calinski-Harabasz Index (CHI) and Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) was used to determine the best solution (k = 2 to 10; Supplementary Method S6). After subtype identification, the overall deviation indices and mean deviation map for each subtype were calculated, and differences among subtypes were compared using one-way ANOVA (P < 0.05, Bonferroni correction).

Statistical analysis

Differences in clinical measures between subtypes

We compared age, educational years, cognitive scores, fludeoxyglucose (FDG), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid-β (Aβ), Tau, and P-tau measures among groups using one-way ANOVA, while sex and APOE ε4 genotype were compared using chi-square test. Following previous studies in which Aβ+ was defined as Aβ < 976.6 pg/ml and Tau+ as P-tau > 21.8 pg/ml, participants were divided into four subgroups: Aβ-&Tau-, Aβ-&Tau+ , Aβ+ &Tau-, and Aβ+ &Tau+ [34, 35]. The ratio of four CSF biomarker levels was calculated for each group.

To further investigate the cognitive progression, a linear mixed-effects (LME) model was used to delineate trajectories of cognitive change across subtypes. This approach can flexibly model both fixed and random effects, accounting for inter-individual variability and handling unbalanced longitudinal data. The model incorporated subject-specific and site-level random effects, and included follow-up time, subtype, and their interaction as fixed effects while controlling for age, sex, and education (Supplementary Method S7).

Longitudinal analysis of individual deviation patterns

Similarly, to examine longitudinal trajectories of deviations, we employed LME models to characterize longitudinal changes in regional z scores across follow-up time and assess differences in these trajectories between diagnostic groups, adjusting for age, sex, education, and ICV as covariates. We further constructed subtype-related LME models to characterize longitudinal z score trajectories for each subtype and compare differences in progression patterns between subtypes (Supplementary Method S8).

For each MCI subtype, a Kaplan-Meier survival curve was calculated to estimate the probability of conversion to AD, with the end event defined as the first diagnosis of AD during follow-up. Additionally, 226 of the MCI patients who progressed to AD had available sMRI scans after conversion. Using their first post-conversion scans, we derived individual deviation pattern maps to identify AD stage subtypes, and then examined their correspondence with previously identified MCI stage subtypes.

Association analysis with other disease markers

We used linear regression to relate mean and regional z scores to cognitive composite scores while adjusting for covariates and testing whether these associations differed across diagnostic groups or subtypes. As APOE ε4 is the major genetic risk factor for AD, we used two-way ANOVA and LME model to evaluate the influence of APOE genotype on the mean and regional z scores and their longitudinal trajectories in diagnostic groups or subtypes (Supplementary Method S9).

Transcriptomic analysis

We separately investigated the transcriptomic relationships with mean deviation patterns in each AD and MCI subtype. Gene expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA, http://human.brain-map.org), including 3702 samples from six postmortem neurotypical adult brains, were processed using the abagen pipeline [36] (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen), yielding a 90 regions × 15,633 genes expression matrix (Supplementary Method S10). Partial least squares (PLS) regression was then applied to relate regional gene expression to mean deviation map, with the first component (PLS1) capturing the linear combination of genes most strongly associated with the deviation map [37]. Statistical significance was assessed using a spatial autocorrelation-preserving spin test, and gene weights were estimated via a bootstrapping method [38–40] (Supplementary Method S11). Finally, the selected genes were input into Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html) for enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) terms of biological process, molecular function, cellular component, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, and cell type signatures [41]. These analyses were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR), with significance set at P < 0.05.

Reproducibility analysis

To further validate the reproducibility of the main clustering results, we conducted the following strategies: (1) hierarchical clustering algorithm (Supplementary Method S6); (2) other brain atlases, including AAL2 (94 cerebral regions) [42], AAL3 (140 cerebral regions) [43], Brainnetome atlas (BN246, 246 cerebral regions) [44]; (3) without ComBat harmonization; (4) validation using regional GMV extracted from sMRI data processed with the FreeSurfer pipeline (Supplementary Method S2); (5) a pooled clustering analysis combining all AD and MCI participants (Supplementary Method S12); (6) validation on an independent OASIS-3 dataset using the same methods as the main experiment. The ARI value was calculated to assess the consistency of subtyping results [45].

Results

Normative models of GMV

The normative models showed robust fitting performance through 10-fold cross-validation, achieving an overall standardized mean squared error of 0.63 ± 0.09 and a mean squared log-loss of -0.23 ± 0.07 (Figure S2). Normative models demonstrated age-related nonlinear GMV trajectories across 90 brain regions for both sexes. Most cortical regions exhibited slight volume change in early adulthood, while several regions had transient volume increases during this period, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and calcarine cortex. Many brain structures showed a gradual decrease in GMV during middle age, eventually progressing to widespread volumetric decline in late life (Fig. 2A and Figure S3).

Individual deviations from normative models

In the ADNI dataset, compared to the CN controls, AD patients exhibited greater GMV negative deviations, while MCI patients showed intermediate deviations. At the overall level, these differences were evident in both the mean deviation z score and the sum of brain regions with extreme negative deviations (Fig. 2B). Regionally, AD patients had obvious negative deviation patterns with significantly lower z scores in most areas, particularly in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, and temporal cortex. While less severe than in AD, MCI patients still showed significantly greater negative deviations in these regions than CN controls (Fig. 2C and Figure S4). The percentage of subjects with extreme deviations differed in regional patterns across groups. As expected, patients with AD showed a higher proportion of extreme negative deviations, with the highest percentage in the right hippocampus (46.08%). In the MCI group, the right amygdala had the highest negative deviation percentage (16.47%), while the CN group exhibited a lower extreme deviation percentage in any region (<4.19%) (Figure S5). The linear SVM based on individual deviation patterns showed good classification performance, effectively distinguishing AD from MCI and providing informative predictions for MCI conversion (Supplementary Results S1 and Figure S6).

Subtypes identification in AD and MCI

The Gap statistic value for k = 1 was markedly lower than that for k ≥ 2 in both the AD and MCI groups, indicating the presence of non-random clustering structure (Figure S7). Two subtypes of both AD and MCI were identified using K-means clustering based on individual GMV deviations (Fig. 3A). The CHI × ARI index consistently determined the optimal number of clusters is 2 for the AD (highest = 87.64) and MCI (highest = 180.42) groups (Figure S8). Among AD patients, 50.17% were classified as AD-1 (n = 147) and 49.83% as AD-2 (n = 146). Among MCI patients, 47.25% were classified as MCI-1 (n = 284) and 52.75% as MCI-2 (n = 317). The mean brain deviation maps revealed more pronounced negative deviations in AD-2 and MCI-2 compared to AD-1 and MCI-1. Globally, the AD-2 and MCI-2 consistently exhibited significantly lower mean z score and greater sum of regions with extreme negative deviations than AD-1 and MCI-1, respectively (Fig. 3B). Regionally, both AD-2 and MCI-2 consistently showed significantly lower deviation z scores in almost all brain regions compared to AD-1 and MCI-1, respectively. Meanwhile, AD-1 had lower z scores than MCI-1, and AD-2 lower than MCI-2 in several regions. However, AD-1 showed higher z scores than MCI-2 in multiple brain regions except the left hippocampus. MCI-1 displayed mild positive deviations and did not differ significantly from CN controls in most regions, except the hippocampus and amygdala (Fig. 3C and Figure S9, P < 0.05, Bonferroni correction). From the spatial overlap maps of extreme deviations, AD-2 and MCI-2 had a significantly higher proportion of subjects with extreme negative deviations across brain regions compared to AD-1 and MCI-1 (Figure S10).

Demographic and clinical differences between subtypes

Statistical comparisons indicated significant differences in demographic and clinical measures among subgroups. Pairwise analysis revealed that MCI-2 had significantly worse performance across ten cognitive domains than MCI-1, and AD-2 was worse than AD-1 in seven domains (Fig. 4A and Table S5-S6). Additionally, significant differences were observed in FDG values between AD-1 and AD-2 or between MCI-1 and MCI-2 subtypes. The MCI-2 had significantly lower CSF Aβ levels than MCI-1, but AD subtypes showed comparable levels. There were no differences in CSF Tau or P-tau levels between subtypes. A higher proportion of Aβ+ &Tau+ individuals was found in AD-2 and MCI-2 compared to AD-1 and MCI-1, respectively (Fig. 4B and Table S7). After controlling for age, gender, and education, the LME models revealed that AD-2 showed a significantly faster decline in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores during follow-up than AD-1. Additionally, MCI-2 exhibited more rapid cognitive decline than MCI-1, except for the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) learning scores and visuospatial ability (Fig. 4C and Table S8).

Longitudinal progression of subtypes

Longitudinal z score changes differed significantly between diagnostic groups in almost all brain regions, especially in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, fusiform, and inferior temporal lobe (Fig. 5A). Post hoc comparisons indicated that z scores decreased most rapidly over time in AD group, followed by MCI group, and remained stable in CN group (Fig. 5B, C and Figure S11). Further subtype comparisons revealed no significant differences in the longitudinal z score decline trajectories between AD-1 and AD-2 or between MCI-1 and MCI-2 in these brain regions (P > 0.05/90, Bonferroni correction). Notably, although AD-1 exhibited higher baseline z scores (i.e., milder atrophy) than MCI-2, it showed a faster rate of atrophy during subsequent disease progression. Survival analysis indicated that MCI-2 had a higher conversion rate and faster progression to AD than MCI-1 (log-rank test: χ2 = 50.24, P < 0.001, Fig. 5D). Among 226 MCI converters, 70 of 78 patients with MCI-1 (89.74%) converted to AD-1, while only 8 (10.26%) progressed to AD-2. 46 of 148 patients with MCI-2 (31.08%) converted to AD-1, while 102 (68.92%) progressed to AD-2 (Fig. 5E), showing a significant difference in conversion patterns between MCI subtypes (χ2 = 70.36, P < 0.001).

Association analysis with cognitive scores and APOE ε4 status

AD patients showed a strong positive correlation between executive function and global deviation score (i.e., mean z score), while MCI patients exhibited a predominant memory-related association. Subtype analyses further identified that AD-2 and MCI-2 had significant correlations between cognitive functions and global deviation score (Supplementary Results S2, Figure S12, and Table S9-S10). Statistical analyses revealed significant effects of APOE ε4 genotype on brain deviation patterns, with carriers exhibiting lower z scores and faster rates of decline than noncarriers, particularly in the MCI group and its subtypes (Supplementary Results S3 and Figure S13).

Genes associated with mean deviation patterns

We performed PLS regression analysis for each of the four subtypes and found that the spatial map of PLS1 is positively correlated with the mean deviation maps in all subtypes (AD-1: R = 0.71; AD-2: R = 0.73; MCI-1: R = 0.57; MCI-2: R = 0.67, Pspin < 0.001). After ranking the PLS1 genes by their weights, we separately identified 2008 significant genes in AD-1, 2353 in AD-2, 750 in MCI-1, and 2084 in MCI-2 (P < 0.05, FDR correction) for subsequent enrichment analysis (Fig. 6A-D). Gene enrichment in GO and KEGG pathways revealed significant associations with neuronal projection development and synaptic processes, particularly in AD-2 subtype (Fig. 6E). In cell type signatures, these genes were enriched in brain endothelial cells across all four subtypes. Additionally, embryonic cortex oligodendrocytes were the most significantly enriched cell types in AD-1, AD-2, and MCI-2 subtypes (Fig. 6F).

Reproducibility analysis on different strategies

Both AD and MCI patients were consistently classified into two subtypes using different schemes, including hierarchical clustering algorithm, distinct brain atlases, and without ComBat harmonization (Figure S8). The ARI values were computed to assess clustering consistency, with higher values indicating better consistency (AD: 0.62-0.97; MCI: 0.60-0.97; Figure S14). Subtype patterns derived from regional GMV extracted using the FreeSurfer pipeline showed good agreement with those obtained from the VBM analysis (Figure S15). The pooled clustering analysis identified two subtypes with distinct clinical profiles and progression (Figure S16 and Table S11), and the subtype assignments highly aligned with those obtained within diagnostic groups (Supplementary Results S4 and Figure S17). In the independent OASIS-3 dataset, similar subtype patterns were identified based on individual deviation maps. MCI-2 showed more severe GMV abnormalities, poorer cognitive performance, and faster clinical progression compared to MCI-1. In contrast, differences between AD subtypes were less pronounced (Figure S18).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the individual variability in brain structural alterations across AD and MCI patients by quantifying deviations from normative GMV models. Our findings revealed substantial heterogeneity in both the severity and spatial distribution of brain atrophy among individuals. Based on these individual deviation patterns, we employed a data-driven clustering approach to identify subtypes of AD and MCI with distinct structural phenotypes. These subtypes differed in clinical manifestations, pathological markers, rates of disease progression, and regional gene expression.

Normative models of GMV

Unlike traditional case-control analyses that focus on group-level differences, normative model enables the detection of individual-level abnormalities relative to a healthy reference population [17]. It flexibly captures nonlinear age-related changes without imposing predefined trajectories and provides regional-level estimates by modeling each brain region separately. Using large healthy samples, we built regional GMV normative models and found an overall age-related GMV decline, though trajectories varied across regions. Subcortical areas such as the hippocampus and amygdala showed early-adult increases, whereas many cortical regions remained stable, and pronounced atrophy appeared in older age [46–48]. These findings underscore the importance of normative model in capturing complex and nonlinear patterns of brain maturation and aging.

Heterogeneous individual deviations from normative models in AD and MCI

Using the normative models, we characterized regional GMV deviations in AD and MCI patients and observed substantial interindividual heterogeneity in both the extent and spatial distribution of abnormalities. Specifically, significant negative deviations (i.e., abnormal atrophy) were observed in AD patients, particularly in the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and amygdala, consistent with evidence of medial temporal atrophy caused by AD neuropathological changes [49–51]. We found that 46.08% of AD patients had extreme negative deviation values in the right hippocampus, but not all patients had significant outliers, suggesting individual variability that may be obscured by group-level statistics. Moreover, we observed that greater normative deviations in the AD group were significantly associated with worse memory and executive function, which is consistent with prior studies [52–54].

Individuals with MCI also showed moderate negative deviations in medial temporal structures, suggesting that structural abnormalities have emerged during the MCI stage and may serve as potential biomarkers [55]. Notably, the MCI group had a lower proportion of patients with extreme negative outliers than AD, consistent with relatively milder atrophy but persistent structural heterogeneity in MCI [10, 56]. Moreover, a small proportion of CN controls exhibited outlier deviations, which may reflect undetected preclinical pathology or inherent biological variability [57]. These findings emphasize the need to consider individual differences, even among control populations, and caution against assumptions of group homogeneity in case-control study designs.

Mapping deviation patterns over time with normative models enables dynamic monitoring of individual disease progression [16, 54]. Both AD and MCI exhibited accelerated progression of negative deviations, especially in the hippocampus and temporal cortex, aligning with accumulating pathology [58, 59]. The stable trajectories in CN controls further indicate that the accelerated brain atrophy in the AD or MCI groups reflects disease-related pathological processes, not just normal aging. We also assessed the effect of APOE ε4 allele on brain deviation patterns, with carriers exhibiting greater GMV negative deviations and faster longitudinal atrophy than noncarriers within disease groups, which reinforces its role as a risk factor and the need to consider genotype in individualized analyses [48, 60].

Neuroanatomical subtypes of AD and MCI based on deviation patterns

Clustering analysis identified two robust subtypes in both AD and MCI patients: severe-deviation subtypes (AD-2/MCI-2) with extensive brain atrophy and mild-deviation subtypes (AD-1/MCI-1) with relatively mild atrophy. These subtypes also exhibited divergent clinical and biomarker profiles. Specifically, the severe-deviation subtype was associated with more pronounced cognitive impairment, accelerated cognitive decline, reduced FDG metabolism, and a higher proportion of abnormal amyloid (Aβ+) and tau (Tau+) status. In contrast, the mild-deviation subtype presented with less cognitive deterioration and more favorable biomarker profiles, suggesting the possible involvement of compensatory mechanisms such as neuroplasticity or alternative pathways [61, 62]. Notably, the reproducibility of subtypes without ComBat harmonization indicates that these patterns are largely driven by biological signals rather than site-specific effects, although ComBat correction remains important for reducing inter-site variability.

Previous neuroimaging studies have identified distinct structural subtypes within AD and MCI, such as medial temporal-dominant, parietal-predominant, and diffuse atrophy patterns in AD [15], as well as MCI subgroups exhibiting either AD-like or CN-like atrophy patterns [35, 63]. Recent multimodal studies integrating MRI, PET, and cognitive measures have confirmed that different disease subtypes exhibit distinct patterns of brain disruption and clinical trajectories [64–66]. However, most of these studies relied on separate clustering within diagnostic categories, limiting the ability to characterize cross-diagnostic heterogeneity or to test whether similar neuroanatomical patterns exist across disease stages. In contrast, our study employed a unified normative modeling framework to jointly analyze structural deviations in both AD and MCI, enabling direct comparisons across subtypes.

Our findings revealed that MCI-1 and MCI-2 exhibited structural deviation patterns closely resembling those of AD-1 and AD-2, respectively, indicating that subtype-specific neuroanatomical heterogeneity is already present in the prodromal phase. A recent study using normative models of dynamic functional connectivity reported a highly similar finding, where AD-1 and MCI-1 shared positive deviation patterns and AD-2 and MCI-2 showed negative deviation patterns [22]. These similar deviation patterns between structural and functional subtypes support the notion that AD and MCI share common underlying pathological mechanisms along a continuous disease spectrum [67]. Moreover, longitudinal analysis further showed that most MCI-1 cases progressed to the AD-1 subtype, while MCI-2 primarily converted to the AD-2 subtype. These consistency of subtype patterns across the MCI and AD stages suggests that these subtypes are not merely driven by differences in disease duration or stage but may reflect underlying biological heterogeneity. Future longitudinal studies incorporating illness-duration information are needed to further validate this finding.

Interestingly, we found that AD-1 patients exhibited less severe atrophy than MCI-2, despite more pronounced hippocampal involvement. However, longitudinal analyses revealed that AD-1 patients exhibited faster brain atrophy than MCI-2, suggesting that AD-1 may represent a focal atrophy subtype with relatively preserved cortical structure at early stages but accelerated structural deterioration. In contrast, MCI-2 showed more diffuse atrophy and a higher risk of conversion to AD. This pattern is consistent with previous imaging studies reporting MCI subgroups with AD-like or even more pronounced structural damage, indicating that some individuals with MCI already exhibit substantial structural pathology that is not always reflected by their clinical diagnostic stage [68]. Our deviation-based subtyping approach could uncover neurobiological heterogeneity often obscured by conventional diagnostic labels, offering a more nuanced understanding of disease progression across clinical stages.

To explore the potential neurobiological underpinnings of structural heterogeneity in subtypes, we conducted the transcriptomic correlates of regional deviation patterns. The spatial alignment between PLS-derived gene maps and subtype-specific deviation maps suggests that structural alterations may reflect regionally organized molecular signatures. Enrichment results highlighted biological processes related to neuronal projection development and synaptic function, particularly in the AD-2 subtype. These pathways are critical for establishing and maintaining neural connectivity, which is often disrupted in AD [69]. Cell-type enrichment further revealed significant associations with endothelial cells and embryonic cortex oligodendrocytes. These cell types are known to contribute to blood-brain barrier integrity and myelination, both of which may be altered in neurodegenerative conditions [70, 71]. Together, these findings offer preliminary insight into distinct molecular mechanisms underlying subtype-specific brain alterations.

Precision medicine aims to tailor personalized therapeutic strategies by considering disease heterogeneity. In this study, individual GM deviation maps have the potential to serve as an imaging biomarker for predicting disease status and progression. On this basis, we further defined AD and MCI subtypes using structural deviation patterns and demonstrated their robustness across different datasets, brain atlases, and morphometric processing pipelines, providing a reproducible way to capture the neurobiological heterogeneity underlying disease progression. These imaging-derived subtypes can facilitate patient stratification in clinical trials and inform the design of targeted therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the classification framework has the potential to be developed into quantitative decision-support tools for early detection, risk assessment, and personalized management of disease.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, although 10-fold cross-validation and an external cohort testing were conducted, additional validation across more diverse populations is needed to enhance generalizability. Second, the current lack of subtype-specific treatment data limits direct assessment of the clinical utility, and future multicenter studies and targeted trials are required to facilitate clinical translation. Third, as different modalities (e.g., functional, diffusion MRI, and PET) may identify distinct subtypes [72], their consistency and variability remain unclear [73, 74]. Future studies integrating multimodal biomarkers would be essential to systematically explore disease heterogeneity. Fourth, the AHBA gene expression data were derived from six postmortem donors, which may limit evaluation of transcriptome-neuroimaging associations across groups. Finally, since MCI may progress to non-AD dementias, expanded subtyping in larger cohorts is necessary to clarify distinct progression pathways.

Conclusion

Using neuroanatomical normative models derived from a large healthy cohort, this study captured individual heterogeneity in AD and MCI. Based on personalized brain deviation patterns, we identified two distinct structural subtypes, which differed significantly in clinical manifestations, disease progression and gene expression profiles. These findings highlight the potential of individualized deviation maps as biomarkers for early detection and disease stratification. Our work advances the understanding of AD and MCI heterogeneity and lays solid foundations for developing individualized diagnostics systems in precision medicine.
