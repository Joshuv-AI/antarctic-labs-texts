Clinical characteristics of snake envenomation-related acute kidney injury in South Korea
Authors: JeongMi Moon, ByeongJo Chun, YoungSoo Cho, KwangHyn Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication of bites by Viperidae snakes. The main venomous snakes in South Korea are members of the Viperidae family, but there is limited information on snake-bite induced AKI in South Korea. Therefore, we investigated snake envenomation-related AKI and the performance of surrogate baseline creatinine to diagnose true AKI. This retrospective, case-control study divided 71 patients with baseline serum creatinine into two groups according to the development of envenomation-related AKI determined based on Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcome Classification. The incidence of AKI was 14.1% with 10% having stage-3 AKI. During hospitalization, the AKI group did not develop venom-induced consumption coagulopathy more frequently than did the non-AKI group. Only one patient needed renal replacement therapy, and no patients died. The serum creatinine concentration at presentation underestimated the incidence of AKI, while the lowest creatinine concentration during hospitalization overestimated the incidence of AKI. After discharge, the incidence of chronic kidney disease did not increase in the AKI group. Snake envenomation-related AKI is characterized by a relatively lower incidence and severity, has no correlation with coagulation disturbance, and has favorable short- and long-term outcomes in South Korea. Based on this study, it is recommended that future envenomation-related AKI studies would utilize the baseline creatinine for diagnosing AKI. If not, envenomation-related AKI studies should include the definition of baseline creatinine used to diagnose AKI and report bias if a baseline creatinine surrogate is used.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74142-9.Subject terms: Acute kidney injury, Toxin-induced nephropathy, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) indicated that Viperidae bites were the causative snakebites for community-acquired acute kidney injury (AKI) associated with envenomation1. AKI develops in 8–75% of patients after snake envenomation in other Asian countries, including India, Sri Lanka, and Myanmar, where the majority of bites were of Viperidae, with 15–92% of patients requiring renal replacement therapy and mortality occurring in 21–46% of patients2–7. In particular, the mortality of patients with envenomation-related AKI was greater than that of patients without AKI8. Despite surviving the illness, up to 50% of post-envenomation AKI patients subsequently develop chronic kidney disease (CKD)9–12.

Understanding the signs and symptoms of regional snake envenomation is essential for determining the target for treatment and ultimately establishing regional treatment guidelines for snakebites. Because the venom composition of snakes may vary within genera and even within species due to differences in ontogeny and geographical distribution, renal involvement and the risk stratification of AKI to CKD can differ depending on the type of snake responsible13.

Terrestrial venomous snakes in South Korea include three Gloydius species (Viperidae family), G. brevicaudus, G. saxatilis, and G. ussuriensis, as well as Rhabdophis tigrinus (Colubridae family)14. Gloydius species were responsible for 96.6% of the snakebite cases in South Korea in which the snake species was identified14. Local tissue damage, venom-induced consumption coagulopathy and rhabdomyolysis have been commonly reported as clinical features of envenomation in South Korea. People in their 50s were bitten most frequently, with males (57%) being bitten more frequently than females (35%); 8% of reports were made by people who did not report their sex14.

Although AKI is a recognized complication of bites from the Viperidae family, to which the Gloydius species belongs, snake envenomation-related AKI in South Korea remains understudied. In previous retrospective studies, the incidence of envenomation-related AKI in South Korea was relatively low (2.6–7.0%); however, these studies focused on other envenomation symptoms and did not describe AKI outcomes15–17.

AKI diagnosis relies on the magnitude of changes in serum creatinine from a baseline premorbid creatinine concentration18. However, baseline premorbid creatinine (baseline creatinine through manuscript) is often unavailable in clinical practice and studies. In particular, patients bitten by snakes are healthy enough to engage in outdoor activities, so most of them have no medical records prior to the snakebite, making it more difficult to obtain the baseline creatinine. Although several creatinine concentrations, such as creatinine at presentation or the lowest creatinine during hospitalization, have been used as surrogates for baseline creatinine for AKI diagnosis, these surrogates can under- or overestimate the incidence of AKI and affect outcomes associated with presumed AKI19–21. Therefore, the rationale behind the choice of surrogate baseline creatinine used for diagnosis, such as the time trend of serum creatinine in the corresponding population, should be explained to improve comparability between studies of envenomation-related AKI. However, most envenomation-related AKI studies have used a surrogate creatinine despite the limited evaluation regarding how accurately this surrogate diagnoses AKI or omitted how baseline creatinine was determined7,8,12,15–17,22,23.

The aim of this study was to investigate the clinical characteristics and risk factors for snake envenomation-related AKI diagnosed using baseline creatinine in South Korea. Additionally, we described the changes in serum creatinine over time and assessed the performance of commonly used surrogate creatinine to diagnose envenomation-related AKI to help standardize envenomation-related AKI definitions.

Results

Among the 84 patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria, 1 who was pregnant, 3 with a history of renal dysfunction, and 9 whose treatment did not adhere to our hospital guidelines were excluded. Ultimately, 71 patients who were given antivenom a median of 1 h after a snake bite at a primary hospital or in our emergency department were included (Fig. 1). Patients presented to our emergency department for a median of 3 h, with an interquartile range of 1–10 h after snake envenomation. The median age of the patients was 63 years.

Prevalence and risk factors of envenomation-related AKI

The incidence of AKI was 14.1% (10/71); 7 patents were classified as KDIGO AKI stage 1, 2 as stage 2, and 1 as stage 3. AKI was diagnosed at presentation in 77.8% of AKI patients.

The clinical course of envenomation-related AKI during hospitalization

During hospitalization, neither complete venom-induced consumption coagulopathy nor partial venom-induced consumption coagulopathy was related to AKI (Table 3). The AKI group was given a larger amount of fluid during the first 24 h (p = 0.039) and a larger total amount of antivenom during hospitalization (p = 0.007). Additionally, the AKI group more frequently required the administration of vasopressors (p < 0.001), diuretics (p = 0.021), and mechanical ventilation support (p < 0.001) than the non-AKI group. The AKI group was admitted longer (p = 0.014).

Only one patient with stage 3 AKI required renal replacement therapy. A 68-year-old patient had underlying hypertension and was given antivenom 1 h after a snake bite. This patient suffered hypotension requiring dopamine and epinephrine and showed elevated white blood cell count (13.5 × 103/mm3), creatine kinase (8,830 U/L) and troponin I (0.83 ng/ml) with ischemic change on electrocardiogram at presentation. Renal replacement therapy commenced 27 h after the snake bite due to oliguria with metabolic acidosis, and the patient was weaned off after 49 days. His eGFR was 68 ml/min/1.73 m2 at presentation and dropped to 12.8 ml/min/1.73 m2 9 days after admission. It was 22 ml/min/1.73 m2 at discharge 53 days after envenomation. Three years later, his eGFR had increased to 89.8 ml/min/1.73 m2.

One additional patient with AKI did not completely recovered from AKI at discharge: 69.4 ml/min/1.73 m2 at 14 days after admission.

None of the patients died during hospitalization.

Progression to CKD after discharge

After discharge, thirty-five (57.4%) patients in the non-AKI group and 6 (60%) in the AKI group had an available follow-up eGFR beyond at least one year after envenomation; the eGFR was 88.2 (73.4-100.5) ml/min/1.73 m2 at 109 (23.5–147) months and 73.1 (30.3–82) ml/min/1.73 m2 at 164 (66–192) months after discharge. Six patients in the AKI group included 1 of 2 patients who did not completely recover from AKI at discharge, and AKI did not progress to CKD in this patient. The incidence of CKD did not differ between the AKI group and the non-AKI group (22.9% (8/35) in the non-AKI group vs. 33.3% (2/6) in the AKI group, p = 0.581).

Discussion

Our study was the first to focus on envenomation-related AKI in South Korea and to reveal the differences in the short- and long-term prognoses of envenomation-related AKI and the lack of association with envenomation-related AKI with coagulopathy compared to other viper envenomation-related AKI patients. Additionally, we assessed the performances of surrogate baseline creatinine for the diagnosis of AKI-related with envenomation and presented guidelines for envenomation-related AKI studies.

The incidence of AKI (14.1%) in our study was lower than that of AKI, which developed in more than 50% of patients after snake envenomation in other Asian countries where the majority of bites were of Viperidae4. In addition, the proportion of AKI stage 3 (10%) was lower than the 24-78.6% of AKI induced by other Viperidae snake envenomation4,7,22. However, the lower incidence and severity of AKI in this study is unlikely due to the effect of antivenom because there was no difference in the delay of antivenom administration between the AKI group and non-AKI group (p value = 0.135). Four patients (40%) in the AKI group were given antivenom within 2 h after envenomation before presentation to our hospital. However, the incidence of AKI in this study is higher than the 2.6-7% of envenomation-related AKI reported in previous studies in South Korea15–17. This different incidence might be explained by the inconsistent use of baseline creatinine definition; these previous retrospective studies used creatinine at presentation with a tendency to underestimate the incidence of AKI or did not define baseline creatinine.

We showed that the clinical course of AKI after venomous snakebites in South Korea seems to be relatively favorable, compared with 39% morality and an incidence of 25–100% renal replacement therapy of other Viperidae envenomations4,7,8,10,24. The zero fatality rate and 1.4% requirement of renal replacement therapy for snake envenomation-related AKI in this study were similar to the results of other studies conducted in South Korea reporting zero fatalities and no need for renal replacement therapy after snake envenomation during hospitalization17,23. However, the increased requirement for intensive therapy, including vasopressor and mechanical ventilation support, in the AKI group compared with the non-AKI group was similar to the AKI related to other viper snake envenomations or medical diseases such as infection4,25. Additionally, the AKI group needed a higher total amount of antivenom and was admitted longer than the non-AKI group, similar to those experiencing other types of viper snake envenomation-related AKI4. It is possible that the clinical importance of envenomation-related AKI is its association with the need for critical care rather than the complications of AKI itself in South Korea. The larger amount of fluid administered during the first 24 h in the AKI group is explained by the higher incidences of severe rhabdomyolysis and hypotension. Early and aggressive volume administration is the primary treatment for hypotension and rhabdomyolysis26,27.

The time interval from snake bite to arrival at the hospital or antivenom administration, clinical or laboratory coagulation disturbance, leukocytosis, and increased creatine kinase at presentation have been reported as predictors of AKI in other Viperidae envenomations4,7,24, while age, underlying hypertension and leukocytosis were predictors of AKI in this study. The older age of patients (median age of 63 years) in this study compared to the median age of 31–48 years in populations of other Viperidae AKI studies, might partially explain why age and underlying hypertension were selected as predictors for AKI in this study4,7,22. Twenty-two (30%) patients had underlying hypertension in this study.

The pathogenesis of envenomation-related AKI is unclear and probably multifactorial, including direct nephrotoxicity and secondary injury due to hypotension, venom-induced consumption coagulopathy, rhabdomyolysis and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia7,24.

Gloydius spp. venom protein family consists of phospholipase A2, snake venom metalloproteinase and cysteine-rich secretory protein families9,28. Metalloproteinase and phospholipase A2 can induce direct nephrotoxicity through proteolytic degradation of the glomerular basement membrane and tubular necrosis29. In particular, the incidence of bleeding, overt disseminated intravascular coagulation and alteration in laboratory parameters of coagulation increased up to 100% in direct proportion to the severity of AKI in other Viperidae envenomation4,7,8. This positive correlation between renal damage and the degree of coagulation disturbance suggests that hematoxic venom is a common cause of renal injury in Viperidae envenomation. However, we noted that venom-induced consumption coagulopathy was not related to AKI in this study. The pathogenesis of AKI is likely snake species-specific due to the variation in venom components25. The lower proportion of severe AKI cases, the relatively favorable outcome of AKI and the lack of an association between AKI and venom-induced consumption coagulopathy in this study might be evidence of different pathogeneses of envenomation-related AKI from different snake species in South Korea4,7,8. The association between AKI and leukocytosis and the lack of an influence of delay time to antivenom administration might point to the contribution of inflammation rather than the toxic venom to envenomation-related AKI in South Korea. Antivenom does not reverse the action of endogenous inflammatory mediators, in contrast to neutralizing venom. Proinflammatory cytokines generated by the injection of metalloproteinase, which is the major component of Gloydius venom, results in vasoconstriction and increased renal vascular resistance30,31.

AKI and CKD are well recognized as interconnected syndromes32. Up to 30% of postoperative AKI or other Viperidae snake envenomation AKI progressed to CKD2,33. Contrastingly, a recent study from Sri Lanka showed no association between snake bite-associated AKI and CKD34. Along with the relatively favorable short-term outcome of AKI, we observed that envenomation-related AKI in South Korea appears not to increase the risk for long-term CKD. The latest time after discharge did not differ between the patients with CKD and patients without CKD (p value = 0.544). The incidence of CKD (22.9%) in the non-AKI group might be explained by the increased incidence of CKD with increasing age. The patients in the non-AKI group had a mean age of 65 (56–74) years at the time of their last follow-up eGFR to diagnosis CKD. The incidence rates of CKD in subjects aged 55–64 years and 65 years or older in South Korea are 15% and 31%, respectively35. Additionally, age, not history of snake-envenomation-related AKI, was associated with the development of CKD according to multivariate regression analysis (supplemental Table 2).

In the absence of baseline creatinine, the lowest inpatient creatinine and creatinine concentration at presentation are commonly used surrogates of baseline creatinine in envenomation-related AKI studies. Because they presented 3 (1–7) (median and interquartile) hours following a snake bite, we expected that measured creatinine at presentation might reflect the baseline creatinine well. However, in contrast to our expectation, the serum creatinine at presentation underestimated AKI, likely due to the peak creatinine at presentation. A potential contributor to the overestimation by the lowest creatinine during hospitalization might include the effect of fluid administration and lower creatinine production during acute illness36. Both surrogate creatinine levels had weak agreement with the baseline creatinine level for the diagnosis of AKI (kappa value < 0.5). Researchers and readers must understand the associated bias when interpreting envenomation-related AKI studies using this surrogate creatinine measure and comparing between studies of envenomation-related AKI.

This study had several limitations. First, the differences in the venom component among the responsible snakes may have confounded the results of this study. The studies so far have reported that Gloydius species is the common cause of snakebite in South Korea. However, we did not identify the responsible snake for envenomation because of the lack of diagnostic kits to differentiate the Gloydius species from the Rhabdophis species. Envenomation by Rhabdophis tigrinus is characterized by no edema and pain at the bite site as well as disseminated intravascular coagulation with hypofibrinogenemia and hemorrhagic symptoms as opposed to the prominent symptoms of local swelling and pain associated with Gloydius species envenomation37. In this study, four patients (5.6%) showed 1 of the highest grades of local swelling and concentrations of fibrinogen that were lower than the normal lower limit (180 mg/dl). None of these four patients developed disseminated intravascular coagulation according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria during hospitalization. Gloydius species accounted for all of twenty-one (29.6%) cases, with the identification of responsible species being based on patients’ descriptions, which might be reliable due to distinct differences in shape between the two species (triangular head and brownish color in Gloydius species vs. round head in Rhabdophis species)14. Consistent with our findings, AKI was found to have a favorable outcome in a study in which the responsible snakes were Gloydius species for 82.5% and unknown for 6.5% of envenomated patients23. Second, the small number of patients is an inherent problem of clinical toxicologic research, which prevents researchers from providing reliable results. In particular, because eGFR data for the assessment of the development of CKD were available in only approximately 57.7% of patients due to the retrospective study design, we could not definitively conclude in this study that an association between envenomation-related AKI and CKD is lacking in South Korea. Additionally, there are limitations resulting from retrospective chart review. We excluded patients with risk factors for AKI and accounted for them in the regression models, but the uncollected confounding factors may not have been completely eliminated. Selection bias from single-center data impedes the generalizability of this study. Additionally, because this study involved a review of the medical records of patients over an 8-year span, several factors, including the evolution of intensive care treatment, lack homogeneity and might influence the prevalence of AKI and progression to CKD. The results obtained in this study should thus be interpreted cautiously on the basis of the various abovementioned limitations. Further prospective studies including a larger sample size are needed to investigate the relationship between envenomation-related AKI and CKD.

In conclusion, in contrast to other Viperidae envenomation, snake-envenomation-related AKI is characterized by a relatively lower incidence, lower proportion of severe AKI, and favorable in-hospital outcomes in South Korea. Additionally, snake-envenomation AKI was not correlated with coagulation disturbance, and it seems to not be related to the subsequent development of CKD. These results showed that snake envenomation-related AKI should be treated individually depending on the species of the snake. Additionally, our data suggests that future envenomation-related AKI studies would benefit from utilizing the baseline creatinine for diagnosing AKI as the creatinine surrogate under or over-estimates the AKI severity to improve the comparability of studies regarding snake envenomation related AKI.

Methods

Study design and setting

This investigation was a retrospective, case-control study performed via chart review at a single academic tertiary care center with an annual emergency department intake of 40,000 patients.

Ethic statements

The study design was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH-2023-409). Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital approved a waiver of informed consent from included patients in this study.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient selection

We included patients 18 years or older who presented at our emergency department between 1 January 2015 and 31 May 2023 with a history of venomous snakebite within 24 h as well as confirmed two-fang marks consistent with a snake bite and clinical features of snake envenomation. According to the 2016 WHO guidelines for the management of snakebites, the clinical features of local envenomation were defined as local pain at the bite site, local swelling spreading proximally up the bite limb and tender, painful swelling of regional draining lymph nodes at the bite site. The clinical features of systemic envenomation are defined as rhabdomyolysis, local or systemic bleeding, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, visual disturbances, dizziness, faintness, collapse, shock, hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, myocardial damage, drowsiness, ptosis, external ophthalmoplegia, abnormalities in taste and smell, cranial nerve palsy, and symptoms and signs of AKI1. Patients should suffer from one of the clinical features of local or systemic envenomation, and these clinical features must develop following snake bites. Additionally, the patients had to receive at least one vial of antivenom during hospitalization due to envenomation signs and symptoms and had a baseline serum creatinine concentration and at least two serum creatinine concentrations measured during hospitalization. As baseline creatinine, we used the mean creatinine between 365 and 7 days prior to hospitalization19.

We excluded patients with pregnancy, a history of CKD, which was defined as a report or laboratory evidence of renal dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2), a history of renal transplantation, an unknown time interval from snakebite to presentation at our emergency department, a history of toxic exposure with nephrotoxicity prior to snake bite or during a hospitalization due to snake envenomation, and treatment using a protocol that did not adhere to our hospital’s guidelines for snake envenomation.

We classified the patients into two groups according to the development of envenomation-related AKI: the non-AKI group and the AKI group. The severity of AKI was determined based on the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcome (KDIGO) classification (Supplemental Table 3), which utilized the magnitude of change in serum creatinine from baseline creatinine38. Urine output criteria were not considered because of the lack of data.

In South Korea, the severity of envenomation is generally assessed based on the traditional snakebite severity scale (TSSS) score (Supplemental Table 415. During hospitalization, we administered antivenom based on the TSSS score as soon as possible at our emergency department. Patients with grade I and II snakebites received 1 vial (6,000 units) of antivenom (KovaxVR, KOREAVACCINE Co., Ltd., Korea; 6,000 units/vial, total protein concentration < 30 mg/300 units, formulated as whole IgG). Patients with grade III and IV snakebites received 2 and 3 vials, respectively. If envenomation symptoms or signs persisted or recurred after the first administration of antivenom, we additionally administered an initial dose of antivenom. Dialysis indication and initiation were made by the consultant in charge of the department of kidney disease in the intensive care unit.

Methods and measurements

We extracted the following information from electronic medical records: baseline characteristics, vital signs and laboratory findings at presentation, the development of local swelling at the bitten site, venom-induced consumption coagulopathy and rhabdomyolysis as envenomation signs, antivenom use, the volume of crystalloid administered (ml/kg) and the administration of vasopressor and diuretics, the need for mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy regardless of the reason, the development of hyperkalemia (K+>6.0 mEq/L) and metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.20 and HCO3−<16 mEq/L) regardless of the reason, the duration of the hospital stay, renal recovery at discharge, in-hospital mortality, the latest eGFR data more than ≥ 1 year after discharge, and the development of CKD after discharge. The severity of local swelling as the local envenomation sign was graded as follows: grade 1 (redness and swelling around the bitten area), grade 2 (redness and swelling of the wrist or foot joint), grade 3 (redness and swelling of the elbow or knee joint), grade 4 (redness and swelling of the whole extremity), or grade 5 (redness and swelling in parts beyond the extremity)39. Rhabdomyolysis was defined as mild (serum creatine kinase activity 1000–4999 IU/L) or severe (serum creatine kinase activity ≥ 5000 IU/L)40. We defined venom-induced consumption coagulopathy as a condition characterized by a prolonged prothrombin time (PT) (recordable international normalized ratio (INR) > 1.2) and evidence of fibrinogen consumption (concentration lower than the lower cutoff concentration of the fibrinogen assay or an elevated D-dimer concentration at least 10 times the upper cutoff concentration of the D-dimer assay)41. Here, the reference ranges of fibrinogen and D-dimer were 180–350 mg/dL and 0–0.55 mg/L FEU, respectively. Hematoxic venom contributes to snake envenomation-related AKI and is associated with adverse renal outcomes in other Viperidae envenomation types4,7,12. To explore the association between the severity of venom-induced consumption coagulopathy and the incidence of AKI, venom-induced consumption coagulopathy was further subdivided into partial or complete venom-induced consumption coagulopathy. Complete venom-induced consumption coagulopathy was defined as having either an undetectable fibrinogen concentration (< 50 mg/dL) (the measurement limit of the fibrinogen assay used in our hospital) or a PT > 120 s (PT INR > 13) (the measurable limit of the PT assay). Venom-induced consumption coagulopathy lacking these two criteria was classified as partial venom-induced consumption coagulopathy. In the case of complete venom-induced consumption coagulopathy, values beyond the limit of measurement were recorded as 121 s/13.1 for PT/PT INR and 49 mg/dL for fibrinogen.

Because serum creatinine and renal biomarkers peak within 24 h post-bite in snake-related AKI patients7, we collected data related to fluid administration within the first 24 h after presentation.

Renal recovery was defined as an improvement in the serum creatinine below the creatinine concentration defined as AKI stage 1. CKD was defined as a decreased eGFR (< 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) for more than 3 months or more, irrespective of cause, in patients with an available follow-up eGFR beyond at least one year after envenomation38.

To identify the time course of serum creatinine change in AKI after presentation, serum creatinine at presentation, at presentation < serum creatinine ≤ 24 h after presentation, 24 h < serum creatinine ≤ 48 h after presentation, highest serum creatinine during hospitalization, and serum creatinine at discharge were also collected in the AKI group. Additionally, because the most frequently used surrogates are creatinine concentration at presentation and the lowest creatinine greater than 0.4 mg/dl (lowest creatinine throughout the manuscript) during hospitalization in most envenomation-related AKI studies7,8,22, we collected the lowest creatinine and evaluated the performance of these two measured creatinine values to diagnose AKI by comparison with the true AKI diagnosed by baseline creatinine.

Data analysis and statistics

The baseline patient characteristics are presented as frequencies for categorical variables and as medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables. For categorical variables, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used. For continuous variables, Mann‒Whitney U tests were used. Risk factors for AKI were determined by multivariate logistic regression analysis using the enter method. The variables that could be obtained at admission and that were significant in the univariate analysis were entered into the regression model. Before modeling, if two or more variables retained in the multivariate analysis were highly correlated in the linear regression analysis, one variable was removed to avoid collinearity. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the regression model.

The agreement between the baseline creatinine concentration and each surrogate creatinine concentration was assessed via a scatter plot of the differences between the two creatinine concentrations against the mean of the two concentrations (Bland Altman plot)42. The bias, which is the overall mean of the differences between baseline creatinine and surrogate creatinine, was calculated. Precision was defined as 1 standard deviation of the bias. The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare the surrogate creatinine distribution with the baseline creatinine distribution. The kappa statistic was used to report the level of agreement of AKI classification between baseline creatinine and surrogate creatinine. The estimated error was calculated as the percentage of patients diagnosed with AKI using surrogate creatinine with respect to those diagnosed from baseline creatinine within the total patients using the formula below. Estimated error = (estimated surrogate AKI-baseline AKI)/baseline AKI. A positive value indicated an overestimation, while a negative value indicated an underestimation.

A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).
