Gangrenous cholecystitis as a sentinel manifestation of chronic mesenteric ischemia in a hepatomesenteric trunk
Authors: Eliana S. Suárez, Felipe Camejo, Santiago González, Luis Figoli, Marcelo Diamant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic mesenteric ischemia is an uncommon and often underdiagnosed atherosclerotic condition due to nonspecific symptoms. The hepatomesenteric trunk is a rare anatomical variant with important clinical implications when its common origin is involved. We report the case of a 43-year-old heavy smoker presenting with progressive postprandial pain and significant weight loss. Imaging revealed ischemic gangrenous cholecystitis, a hepatomesenteric trunk, and complete occlusion of the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery. Following cholecystectomy, symptomatic chronic mesenteric ischemia was confirmed, and successful endovascular superior mesenteric artery revascularization led to clinical improvement and restoration of antegrade mesenteric flow.

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) is an uncommon manifestation of advanced atherosclerotic disease and is frequently diagnosed late because of nonspecific clinical presentation.1,2

The hepatomesenteric trunk is a rare anatomical variant, reported in 1% to 4.5% of cases, defined as a common origin of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and a hepatic arterial branch, most frequently the right hepatic artery.3,4 Its presence acquires clinical relevance when atherosclerotic disease compromises its common origin.

We present the case of a patient with this anatomical variant in whom gangrenous cholecystitis revealed previously unrecognized severe CMI.

The patient provided written informed consent for publication of the case details and images.

Case report

A 43-year-old male heavy smoker (30 pack-years) presented with a 1-year history of progressive postprandial abdominal pain and a 20 kg weight loss. Imaging demonstrated a distended gallbladder with absence of homogeneous wall enhancement in the portal venous phase and no evidence of gallstones, findings highly suggestive of ischemic (gangrenous) cholecystitis, as well as a hepatomesenteric trunk and complete occlusion of both the celiac trunk and the SMA (Figs 1, 2, A and B).Fig 1Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrating a distended gallbladder with absence of homogeneous wall enhancement in the portal venous phase, without evidence of gallstones, consistent with ischemic gangrenous cholecystitis.Fig 2A, Coronal computed tomography angiography demonstrating proximal occlusion of the hepatomesenteric trunk (arrow), measuring approximately 2.5 cm in length. Laboratory results at presentation demonstrated normal bilirubin levels, aspartate aminotransferase 74 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 96 U/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase 15 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 75 U/L, globulins 2.1 g/dL, albumin 2.8 g/dL, and an albumin-to-globulin ratio of 1.31. Notably, no cholestatic pattern was observed, supporting a nonobstructive etiology.

Following imaging, the patient experienced clinical deterioration with hemodynamic instability, prompting emergency exploratory laparotomy. Gangrenous cholecystitis was identified, along with absence of mesenteric pulse and no evidence of intestinal necrosis. Cholecystectomy was performed. Given the patient’s critical condition and the absence of small bowel or colonic necrosis, revascularization was deferred.

The findings were consistent with symptomatic CMI secondary to multivessel atherosclerotic disease (Fig 3). Given the patient’s relatively young age, alternative etiologies were considered, and a thrombophilia work-up was performed, which was negative.Fig 3In a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained section, findings show transmural necrosis of the gallbladder with a predominantly polymorphonuclear inflammatory infiltrate, consistent with acute gangrenous cholecystitis.

After clinical stabilization, endovascular revascularization was performed via a left brachial approach, 20 days after the initial surgery, under systemic heparinization. A 5F sheath was introduced. Initial attempts to cross the occlusion with a 0.035-inch SplashWire guidewire (Merit Medical Systems, Inc.) were unsuccessful; the lesion was subsequently crossed using a hydrophilic V-14 guidewire (Boston Scientific Corporation). A 5F Bern catheter (Cook Medical) was used to selectively engage the SMA ostium.

A 12 × 45 mm Check-Flo Flexor sheath (Cook Medical) was advanced to the proximal SMA, with a telescoped 7 × 70 mm sheath positioned at the ostium. Balloon predilation was performed using a 3 × 20 mm angioplasty balloon. A 6 × 37 mm Express SD balloon-expandable bare-metal stent (Boston Scientific Corporation) was deployed at the SMA ostium, restoring antegrade mesenteric flow (Fig 4).Fig 4Completion angiography confirmed adequate stent expansion and restoration of antegrade mesenteric flow.

A covered stent was intentionally avoided to prevent compromise of the hepatic arterial branch arising from the hepatomesenteric trunk, which was critical for hepatic perfusion. The stent was deployed just proximal to the origin of this branch, preserving hepatic arterial flow. The short ostial lesion, favorable anatomy, and need for precise deployment supported the use of a balloon-expandable bare-metal platform.

Revascularization of the SMA alone was considered sufficient, as it represents the primary arterial supply to the midgut and, in the setting of a hepatomesenteric trunk, also contributes to maintaining hepatic perfusion. Revascularization of the celiac territory was not pursued due to the complexity of the lesions involving both the trunk and its branches, with uncertain additional benefit and a potential increase in procedural risk.

The access site was closed with 6-0 Prolene sutures. The patient tolerated the procedure without complications. Computed tomography angiography performed during the first postoperative week demonstrated adequate stent expansion and restoration of antegrade mesenteric flow (Fig 5). At the 1-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, had resumed full oral intake, and duplex ultrasound guidance confirmed continued patency of the stented segment. Discussion

The patient had multivessel mesenteric atherosclerotic disease, with complete occlusion of both the celiac trunk and the SMA, a pattern associated with a high risk of acute mesenteric ischemia. According to the 2025 European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, multivessel involvement carries up to a 25% risk of acute mesenteric ischemia within a period of 1 to 6 years, and severe malnutrition should not delay revascularization. This case highlights gangrenous cholecystitis as a potential sentinel manifestation of severe mesenteric ischemia, particularly in the presence of high-risk anatomical variants.

Given that the SMA constitutes the primary arterial supply to the midgut, its revascularization was prioritized. In this case, the presence of a hepatomesenteric trunk, with the right hepatic artery arising from a common origin with the SMA, further increased the importance of restoring flow through this vessel to maintain both mesenteric and hepatic perfusion. Endovascular treatment is recommended as first-line therapy due to its lower morbidity, faster recovery, and survival comparable to open surgery.5, 6, 7 Reinterventions are generally safe and effective.8, 9, 10

Although covered stents reduce restenosis, recurrence, and reintervention rates, in this case a bare-metal stent was considered ideal to avoid compromising the hepatic arterial branch arising from the hepatomesenteric trunk. The short ostial lesion, favorable anatomy, potential for precise deployment, and brachial access supported the use of a balloon-expandable bare-metal stent, with high technical success and acceptable patency. These factors, together with malnutrition and clinical deterioration, supported an endovascular-first approach.

Conclusions

Primary endovascular revascularization of the SMA is a safe and effective strategy in multivessel CMI, even in the presence of severe malnutrition. Early recognition of high-risk vascular patterns and anatomical variants, such as a hepatomesenteric trunk, is essential for appropriate procedural planning and prevention of acute mesenteric ischemia.
