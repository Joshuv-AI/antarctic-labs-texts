Microscopy underestimates submicroscopic malaria infections in Ethiopia’s southern rift valley: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Betelihem Jima, Biniam Wondale, Nigatu Eligo, Feven Wudineh, Teklu Wegayehu, Bernt Lindtjørn, Fekadu Massebo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEfforts to control malaria face several challenges, including the presence of parasite reservoirs within the community that contribute to disease transmission. This study aimed to assess malaria prevalence among community members using microscopy and nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect parasite reservoirs in malaria-endemic settings.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in malaria-endemic districts of the Gamo Zone in the southern Rift Valley of Ethiopia. The research was conducted as part of a baseline assessment for a trial evaluating the impact of house screening and ivermectin treatment of domestic animals on malaria incidence. Capillary blood samples were collected from the study participants to prepare blood films and dried blood spots. Malaria parasite detection and species identification were performed using both microscopy and nested PCR.ResultsOf 4745 participants screened by microscopy, malaria prevalence was 2.7% (126/4745; 95% CI: 2.2–3.2%). Nested PCR was performed on 1183 samples, including 97 microscopy-positive and 1,086 microscopy-negative samples. PCR confirmed 67% (65/97) of microscopy-positive cases, yielding a PCR-corrected microscopy prevalence of 1.4% (65/4745). Submicroscopic infections were detected in 6.1% (66/1086) of microscopy-negative samples. Malaria prevalence across Kebeles ranged from 0.5–2.3% by microscopy and 4.1–9.6% by PCR. Species misclassification by microscopy was common: 19.5% of P. falciparum infections were misidentified as P. vivax, 14.0% of P. vivax as P. falciparum, and 33.0% of microscopy-positive samples were PCR-negative.ConclusionThese findings emphasize that community-based malaria diagnosis using microscopy underestimates malaria prevalence, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05890-4.

Background

Malaria remains a major public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa, where most malaria cases and deaths happen worldwide [1]. In many malaria-endemic countries, indoor residual spraying (IRS), insecticidal-treated nets (ITNs) and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), along with antimalarial drugs, have contributed to reductions in malaria-related illnesses and deaths [2].

Despite ongoing control efforts, malaria cases in Ethiopia have increased steadily over the past seven years, with a recent concerning upsurge [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 12.4 million malaria cases were reported in Ethiopia in 2024 [1]. This increase may be due to disruptions in programs caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, instability within the country, and weaknesses in the health system, particularly at the community level [4]. Additionally, factors such as widespread occurrence of insecticide-resistant malaria vectors, the emergence of drug-resistant malaria parasites, the deletion of the HRP2/3 gene in Plasmodium falciparum parasite, climate change, changes in vector behavior, and the high prevalence of asymptomatic cases in the community may also be contributing to malaria transmission and cases [5–10].

Routine case finding for malaria captures only symptomatic individuals, leading to an incomplete assessment of parasite burden and an underestimation of transmission intensity [11]. Community-based screening addresses this gap by identifying asymptomatic microscopic and submicroscopic carriers who contribute to transmission but do not seek care [12, 13]. Many asymptomatic individuals tend to have low parasitemia that can only be detected using sensitive molecular techniques [14]. Infections that are positive by PCR but negative by microscopy are classified as submicroscopic infections [15] and have been reported across all levels of malaria transmission [16]. Deploying more sensitive diagnostic tools to target these hidden reservoirs is therefore crucial for reducing the malaria burden and interrupting transmission [17].

In Ethiopia, estimates of malaria prevalence and case distribution have traditionally relied on facility-based data from microscopy and RDTs [18]. This approach, however, captures only a fraction of the actual burden, as it misses infections in the broader community, particularly among asymptomatic microscopy and submicroscopic carriers [19]. Consequently, the hidden reservoirs of infection that sustain transmission remain unquantified and are often underestimated [16]. Without accurate data on community-level prevalence, national efforts to control and eliminate malaria risk are being undermined by these undetected sources of transmission.

Microscopy enables the identification of Plasmodium species and the quantification of parasite density; however, it has notable limitations, particularly at low parasitemia levels [20]. False negatives result in untreated symptomatic patients [21] whereas false positives lead to unnecessary administration of antimalarial drugs and inefficient use of resources [22].

This study assessed the prevalence of microscopic and submicroscopic malaria in endemic Kebeles near the southern Rift Valley lakes of Ethiopia using microscopy and polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Methods

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in malaria endemic Kebeles near the southern Rift Valley lakes in the Gamo Zone, Ethiopia.

Study setting

This study was conducted in 12 malaria endemic Kebeles (the lowest administrative level in Ethiopia) of the Arba Minch Zuria and Mirab Abaya districts in the Gamo Zone in South Ethiopia, from July 2023 to February 2024 (Fig. 1). The area's main cash crops include banana, mango, and maize. Malaria transmission typically occurs twice a year, following the rainy season [23]. Hot and humid climatic conditions create an environment favorable to the malaria vector. Anopheles arabiensis is the most common and primary malaria vector in the study area [24].Fig. 1Study area map: A Ethiopia, B Southern Ethiopia, C Study site, including 12 Kebeles. The study districts have four health centers serving the local population, and each Kebele has at least one health post. ITNs and IRS are the primary methods for controlling malaria vectors. The houses are primarily built with iron roofs, earthen floors, and mud walls (Supplement file 1).

Study population

The study's target population consisted of residents living in the malaria-endemic lowlands of the two districts' Kebeles. A cross-sectional study was conducted in twelve Kebeles, in which four to six Limat Budin or sub-Kebele units comprising approximately 30–35 households were randomly selected from each. This study was done before the start of a cluster-randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate whether combining house screening with ivermectin treatment for domestic animals can reduce malaria transmission more effectively than using ITNs alone. All members of households within each Limat Budin were included in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Malaria-endemic kebeles bordering Lake Abaya and Lake Chamo were included in the study. Six Kebeles were excluded because of security concerns, long distance from the coordination center, or lack of proximity to the lakes.

The study included all household members living within each Limat Budin. Permanent residents who provided written informed consent to participate were eligible for inclusion.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was the prevalence of microscopic and submicroscopic malaria in endemic Kebeles, assessed by microscopy and nested PCR.

Variables indicating individual sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, sex, education level, marital status, occupation, and individual temperature measurement, were included as background variables.

Data collection and management

Data were collected from households using GPS-enabled Samsung tablets equipped with the Kobo Toolbox (KoboToolbox, 2023). Each household was assigned a unique identification number, and demographic information for all household members was recorded. Trained data collectors conducted the fieldwork under close supervision to ensure the quality and reliability of the data. They performed home visits and made at least two follow-up attempts when participants were unavailable before deeming them as absent. Axillary temperature was measured using digital thermometers, and field data were uploaded daily to a central data management server.

Blood sample collection

Trained laboratory professionals collected 300 μL of capillary blood to prepare blood films for microscopy and dried blood spots (DBS) for molecular testing, following standard operating procedures. Internal and external quality controls were performed as needed during analysis. The thin smear was fixed using absolute methanol and stained with a 10% Giemsa solution at the health facility until it was transported to the Arba Minch University Advanced Medical Entomology and Vector Control Laboratory. Two trained and experienced laboratory technologists examined both thick and thin blood smears. Negative results were reported after examining 100 microscopic fields [25]. If discordant results were observed, they were re-checked by senior microscopists.

DNA extraction and parasite detection by nPCR

A capillary blood sample was collected using a capillary microtainer, and three (approximately 20 µL each) drops were applied onto 3MM Whatman filter paper (Qiagen, Germany) to prepare the DBS sample. After air-drying, DBS samples were stored at −80 °C until molecular analysis. For DNA extraction, samples were cut into 6 mm circles using a single-hole punch. DNA extraction was performed using the DNase Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) on a DBS sample, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Detection of the parasite and species identification were conducted using nested PCR, targeting the 18S rRNA. Dual amplification was performed using genus-specific primers being utilized in the first step with primer sequences (5’−3’), rPLU6: (5’TTAAAATTGTTGCAGTTAAAACG3’), rPLU5: (5’CCTGTTGTTGCCTTAAACTTC3’) n PCR. In the second step, we used the first amplification product with species-specific primers as previously described: rVIV1, (5’CGCTTCTAGCTTAATCCACATAACTGATAC3’), rVIV2, (5’ACTTCCAAGCCGAAGCAAAGAAAGTCCTTA3’) for P. vivax and rFAL1: (5’TTAAACTGGTTTGGGAAAACCAAATATATT3’), rFAL2: (5’ACACAATGAACTCAATCATGACTACCCGTC3’) for P. falciparum. PCR products were visualized by ethidium bromide staining under UV light (Supplement file 3). Amplified DNA was separated on 2% agarose gels, stained with Gel Red, and visualized using a UV trans illuminator. Amplicon bands were compared to a 100-base-pair DNA ladder [26].

Participant classification

Participants with negative blood smears and parasite DNA found by PCR were classified as having submicroscopic malaria [27].

Bias

To address selection bias, all households within the selected Limat Budin were included, and all household members who volunteered to provide consent and a blood sample were enrolled. Although the study aimed to include all microscopy-positive samples, some were excluded from PCR analysis due to insufficient sample volume (Fig. 2). As a result, 97 of 126 microscopy-positive samples were analyzed. Systematic random sampling was employed to select 1086 DBS samples from 4619 microscopy-negative samples. The first DBS sample was selected by lottery method, and every fourth DBS sample was selected for PCR analysis. To evaluate potential selection bias, we compared two groups: (1) participants in microscopy screening, (2) non-sample giver (Table 1). To further minimize the measurement bias, we applied a standardized protocol and implemented strict quality control procedures to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results. Sample size

This study is part of the malaria control intervention trial evaluating the impact of house screening and ivermectin treatment of domestic animals on malaria parasite transmission. The sampling units for this study were individual participants. For the trial, we estimated the sample size to be 8117 participants living in 1741 households [28]. The sample size calculation for this cross-sectional study was performed using the single population proportion formula in OpenEpi (www.OpenEpi.com) online statistical calculation software. The assumption was a 12.2% prevalence of malaria, based on a previous study that employed a combination of RDT, microscopy and PCR [29]. A precision of 3% at a 95% confidence interval, a design effect of 1.5, and a 10% non-response rate were considered, resulting in a calculated sample size of 754 individuals. Because the sample size was smaller than that of the planned intervention study, we included all individuals and households selected for the malaria trial.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata 18 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to measure frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio‑demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 8117 individuals from 1741 households participated in the survey. However, 1094 individuals refused to provide a blood sample, and 2278 individuals could not be located during repeated visits. As a result, microscope examinations were conducted on 4745 individuals, and PCR tests were performed on 97 microscopy-positive samples and 1086 microscopy negative individuals (Fig. 2).

Table 1 outlines the socio-demographic characteristics of the study population. Participant distribution differed between those examined with microscopy and those who did not provide a blood sample. The microscopy group included fewer males (44.5%) and fewer individuals aged 15 or older (56.7%) compared with non-sample givers.

Prevalence of malaria by microscopy

A total of 4745 participants were screened with microscopy, 2.7% (126/4745; 95% CI: 2.2–3.2) were positive for malaria parasites. Of these cases, 1.4% (67/4745) were identified as P. vivax, 1.1% (51/4745) as P. falciparum, and 0.2% (8/4745) as mixed P. vivax and P. falciparum infections.

Malaria prevalence by microscopy versus study participants’ characteristics

Among the participants, the highest prevalence of malaria was seen in children between five and fourteen years old with a prevalence of 4.6% (63/1383); they had 3.51times higher odd of getting malaria compared with those aged greater than 15 years old (95% CI: 2.32–5.31). Followed by children under the age of five with a prevalence of 4.0% (27/680), they had 3.04 times higher odds of getting malaria compared with the participants older than 15 years (95% CI: 1.83–5.04) (Table 2). PCR corrected malaria prevalence and misdiagnosis

Among 4745 participants tested by microscopy, 126 were positive for malaria by microscopy. Of these, 97 underwent PCR testing, including 41 P. falciparum, 50 P. vivax, and 6 mixed infections. Of the microscopy-positive samples, 67% (65/97) were PCR positive, and the remaining were negative. The PCR-corrected malaria prevalence estimated by microscopy was 1.4% (95% CI: 1.1–1.7%). Among PCR-positive cases, 35% (34/97) were P. vivax, 31% (30/97) were P. falciparum, and 1% (1/97) were mixed infections.

PCR revealed that 19.5% (8/41) of P. falciparum cases were misdiagnosed as P. vivax, and 14% (7/50) of P. vivax cases were misdiagnosed as P. falciparum by microscopy. Additionally, 33% (32/97) of PCR-negative cases were incorrectly diagnosed as microscopy positive.

Prevalence of submicroscopic infections

PCR analysis was conducted on 1,086 randomly selected microscopy-negative samples. Submicroscopic infections were detected in 6.1% (66/1086) of these samples, including 3.8% (41/1086) with P. vivax, 2% (22/1086) with P. falciparum, and 0.3% (3/1086) with mixed infections.

Characteristics of the study participants and submicroscopic infections

The highest prevalence of submicroscopic malaria infection was observed among children aged 5 to 14 years, 9.7% (29/297), and they had 2.48 times higher odds of getting malaria compared with those aged greater than 15 years (95% CI: 1.43–4.29) as determined by PCR (Table 3). Malaria prevalence estimated by PCR

PCR detected malaria in 6.1% (66 of 1086) of microscope-negative cases. Extrapolating to all 4619 microscope-negative samples, an estimated 282 were malaria-positive. Among microscopy-positive cases, 65 of 97 tested samples were PCR-positive. Applying this proportion to all 126 microscopy-positive cases yields an estimated 84 PCR-positive cases. The overall estimated prevalence of PCR-confirmed malaria was 7.7% (366/4745).

The estimated species distribution for P. vivax was 4.4%, for P. falciparum it was 2.7% and 0.3% for mixed infection.

Microscopic and submicroscopic prevalence of malaria in the study Kebeles

Malaria prevalence across 12 Kebeles ranged from 0.5% (2/396) in Chano Chalba to 4.1% (17/410) in Molle, with a mean of 2.6%. PCR identified a larger hidden reservoir of infection among cases reported as negative by microscopy in all Kebeles. Submicroscopic infection rates ranged from 2.3% (2/88) in Chano Chalba to 9.6% (7/73) in Sile (Table 4). Discussion

This study revealed a high prevalence of submicroscopic malaria within the community, showing that microscopy significantly underestimates the actual burden of the disease. PCR testing detected malaria in approximately 6.1% of cases that were negative by microscopy. Many cases were misdiagnosed, leading to inappropriate patient management. Such diagnostic inaccuracies hinder efforts to control malaria and underscore the operational limitations of microscopy. This situation highlights the importance of implementing more sensitive testing methods for reliable diagnosis and surveillance.

Accurate estimation of malaria prevalence in the community using highly sensitive diagnostic methods is essential for reducing transmission and scaling up effective interventions [30]. In the present study, PCR detected submicroscopic malaria infections in 6.1% of individuals who were reported as negative by microscopy. This finding has important implications for malaria control strategies, particularly in countries such as Ethiopia, where routine diagnosis relies predominantly on microscopy [18]. The results indicate that a substantial proportion of malaria infections in the community may remain undetected and untreated, thereby serving as hidden reservoirs for ongoing transmission. This under-detection may be attributed to the limited sensitivity of microscopy and variability in the technical skills required for parasite identification [20]. The findings of this study are consistent with reports from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and India [19, 31, 32].

To understand the malaria parasite population within the country, it is essential to analyze the speciation of Plasmodium infections. In this study, we found that P. vivax was the dominant species by both diagnostic methods, followed by P. falciparum. Mixed infections were also observed, which aligns with findings from other studies [33, 34]. Plasmodium vivax is more frequently reported in community-based research than in facility-based studies due to its unique biological characteristics. Typically, P. vivax causes milder symptoms compared to P. falciparum, leading individuals to delay seeking medical attention [35]. Community-based studies are more effective in capturing these cases. Furthermore, the parasite's ability to form a dormant stage in the liver results in recurring infections that often present with mild symptoms, causing individuals to hesitate in visiting health facilities [36]. Our study screened individuals regardless of symptoms, enabling us to identify asymptomatic P. vivax infections that may result from relapse or new infection. This approach likely increases the reported number of P. vivax cases.

Accurately identifying Plasmodium infections through microscopy has been a challenge in controlling malaria [21]. In this study, most microscopically identified P. vivax and P. falciparum infections were misdiagnosed when confirmed with PCR testing. This misdiagnosis can lead to prolonged illness and increase the transmission of the disease because of the mistreatment [21]. The research finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia and Uganda [22, 37]. In addition, false negatives can lead to severe or fatal outcomes, while false positives expose patients to unnecessary drugs, driving resistance and wasting scarce resources. Together, these diagnostic failures intensify the burden on patients and health care systems, highlighting the urgent need for more accurate tests.

This study showed high microscopic and sub-microscopic infection among participants with the age group of 5–14 years. A study conducted in Tanzania similarly revealed an elevated risk of malaria, with a prevalence of 40.4 among school-age children aged 5 to 14 [38]. A significant prevalence of malaria was also seen in comparable age groups in studies performed in Mali and Uganda [39, 40]. This increase may be linked to high vulnerability to mosquito bites due to behavioral factors such as outdoor activities, reduced bed net usage, and greater independence [41]. The lower risk of 15 + individuals could be related to acquired immunity developed on by frequent malaria exposure [42].

The main strength of this study was the use of a sensitive diagnostic technique. Additionally, the study involved a large sample size and was conducted within the community. Despite potential selection bias and incomplete PCR testing, we consider these findings are valid. Although microscopic screening was conducted for all volunteer participants, there was a notable absence of non-sample provider males, students, and farmers. Therefore, the prevalence may be higher than reported, as these groups are at an increased risk of malaria. In addition, the exclusion of certain microscopy-positive samples from PCR analysis may affect the results.

Conclusion

Our findings show that microscopy significantly underestimates the burden of malaria; PCR revealed a high number of submicroscopic infections. It is essential to integrate sensitive diagnostic tools into community-level surveillance to determine the true extent of malaria transmission and to implement targeted control measures effectively.
