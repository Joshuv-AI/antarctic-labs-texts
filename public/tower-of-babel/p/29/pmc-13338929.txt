Structure–property relationships in subnanometric transition metal tetramers
Authors: Franklin Ferraro, Carlos Cárdenas, Tatiana Gomez, Cacier Hadad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a systematic and exhaustive exploration of transition metal tetramer low-energy minima (M, M′ = Fe, Ru, Os, Co, Rh, Ir, Ni, Pd, Pt, Cu, Ag, Au, x = 0–4), combining stochastic structural searches with density functional theory. Our results reveal a pronounced structural diversity, multiple low-lying spin states, and widespread fluxionality at the subnanometric scale. While binding energies broadly follow periodic trends, selected heterometallic combinations exhibit enhanced cohesion, identifying “magic” compositions with increased stability. Time-dependent DFT calculations predict intense UV-visible absorptions in several tetramers and distinctive and potentially useful optical signatures in a number of Ni-containing heterotetramers. Conceptual DFT descriptors uncover a wide range of electrophilic and nucleophilic behaviors across the series, offering initial, qualitative insights into potential reactivity patterns across the series. These results establish clear links between atomic-scale composition, geometry, and functionality in transition metal subnanoclusters, providing transferable insights relevant to nanocatalysis, optical nanomaterials, and the rational design of functional nanoalloys.

Introduction

Advances in experimental techniques for the generation, manipulation, and characterization of atomic-scale matter have significantly expanded interest in subnanometric metal clusters, whose properties often differ profoundly from those of bulk materials and even from larger nanoparticles.1–3 At this size regime, clusters exhibit molecule-like electronic structures, strong quantum size effects, and a pronounced sensitivity of their properties to composition, geometry, and spin state. As a result, subnanoclusters have emerged as promising building blocks for the development of novel optical, magnetic, and catalytic materials.1

From an optical perspective, the discrete electronic energy levels of subnanometric metal clusters give rise to absorption and emission features that are highly tunable with size, structure, and elemental composition.4 Unlike conventional nanomaterials,5,6 which often display size-dependent band-gap behavior, subnano systems lack crystallinity and exhibit electronic states more closely resembling those of molecules.1 Consequently, small variations in geometry or metal composition can induce substantial changes in their optical response. Notable examples include ligand-stabilized or bare gold clusters, whose luminescence properties depend sensitively on structural motifs,7,8 as well as cationic bismuth subnanoclusters displaying UV-visible-NIR emission with potential applications in sensing and bioimaging.1 Related effects have also been reported for ultra-small transition metal clusters supported on oxide nanosheets, where enhanced photoactivity has been observed relative to noble-metal-based systems.9

Small transition metal clusters have also long been investigated as models for catalytic active sites on extended surfaces or supported nanoparticles.2,10–15 Despite the inherent differences between free clusters and surface-bound species, such studies have provided valuable insights into structure–reactivity relationships and have guided the rational design of heterogeneous catalysts.16–21 It is important to emphasize that the reactivity and electronic structure of a free tetramer are distinct from those of a larger nanoparticle or a supported catalyst. Tetramers are best viewed as a distinct class of molecular systems, and their study provides fundamental insights into metal–metal bonding22 at the ultimate size limit, rather than as direct models for extended materials. However, in parallel, recent progress in the synthesis, stabilization, and characterization of nano- and subnano-clusters—both in the gas phase,1,2,14,15,23 and in solution24—has opened realistic prospects for their direct use in homogeneous catalysis. Owing to their high surface-to-volume ratio, abundance of low-coordination sites, and tunability at the atomic level, subnanometric clusters can display exceptional catalytic activity and selectivity while minimizing metal loading.15,25–27

At the same time, practical challenges remain, including cluster instability, aggregation, and difficulties associated with size selection and manipulation.2 Consequently, many technological applications currently rely on clusters stabilized on surfaces, confined within cavities,28,29 or protected by ligands.2,30–35 Nevertheless, the intrinsic fluxionality and reactivity of small clusters—which are often viewed as limitations—may also represent advantages for specific applications, particularly where adaptive or dynamic behavior is desirable.36–39 Moreover, the study of free clusters remains essential, both as a fundamental scientific endeavor and as a foundation for the rational design of supported, confined, or ligand-stabilized systems. Environmental and societal concerns associated with the large-scale extraction of transition metals further motivate the exploration of efficient, atom-economical alternatives based on small clusters.40

From a theoretical standpoint, the increasing availability of computational resources has enabled predictive and explanatory studies of metal clusters at increasingly sophisticated levels of theory. Exhaustive searches for local minima on the potential energy surfaces (PESs), combining efficient structural search algorithms with electronic structure methods such as density functional theory (DFT), have proven particularly powerful.41–46 Such approaches allow not only the identification of global minima but also the characterization of low-lying local minima that may play a role in finite-temperature behavior, fluxionality, and reactivity.46,47 This strategy has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including molecular and atomic clusters, microsolvation phenomena, and catalytic processes.45–50

In addition, it is well established that mixing different metals at the atomic scale can lead to emergent properties that surpass those of monometallic systems.51–56 Bimetallic and multimetallic clusters may exhibit enhanced stability, modified electronic structures, and improved catalytic or optical performance due to synergistic interactions between constituent elements. Transition metal tetramers, in particular, represent an attractive compromise between computational tractability and chemical richness: they are small enough to permit exhaustive theoretical characterization, yet large enough to exhibit high structural diversity and thus to observe changes in properties for the same composition,57–59 and to find functional nanomaterials.16,17,57–59

In this work, we combine a stochastic structural search using the molecular KICK algorithm,60,61 with DFT calculations at the TPSS/SDD level to perform an extensive identification of low-energy minima on the PESs of transition metal tetramers, including both homonuclear and heteronuclear species. The study encompasses possible homonuclear tetramers (we will call them “homotetramers”) of the 3d, 4d, and 5d metals from groups 8–11 (Fe, Ru, Os, Co, Rh, Ir, Ni, Pd, Pt, Cu, Ag, Au). For heteronuclear tetramers (“heterotetramers”), our focus is on alloys based on Cu, Ni, and Pd. Copper is a foundational metal in nanoalloy research and is also abundant and inexpensive, while nickel is of particular interest due to its strategic importance in catalysis and its environmental implications. Palladium is a key representative of the platinum group metals with well-known catalytic properties. This targeted approach enables a deep, computationally tractable investigation of the effects of doping these base metals with other elements from across the series. In addition to characterizing their structures and relative stabilities, we examine their optical properties using time-dependent DFT and assess their reactivity trends within the framework of conceptual DFT. Particular attention is given to identifying general patterns and exceptional cases—such as unusually stable “magic” compositions—that may inform the design of functional subnanometric materials.

Previous computational studies have investigated some of the bimetallic combinations examined here, including Cu–Pd, Pd–Ag, Pd–Au, and various Ni-based alloys (Ni–Cu, Ni–Ag, Ni–Pd, Ni–Co, Ni–Rh, Ni–Ir, Ni–Fe, Ni–Ru).1,2,18,36,45,62–82 These works have provided valuable insights into ground-state geometries, magnetic properties, and stability trends for selected compositions and cluster sizes. However, they do not offer a uniform, systematic comparison of all tetramer compositions and their low-lying isomers within a single theoretical framework. In addition to the review presented above and the one discussed later, a literature summary is provided in the SI, where the relevant bibliographic references can be found.62–82 That summary discusses how the present work complements and extends these previous efforts through an exhaustive, consistent DFT study across 12 metals and their bimetallic combinations.

Finally, we emphasize that the present study aims to provide a comprehensive and comparative description of a broad family of tetramers at a consistent level of theory. While effects such as spin–orbit coupling, higher-order relativistic corrections, and multireference character are beyond the scope of this work, the results reported here establish a robust foundation for future, more refined investigations.

Theoretical and computational procedures

The structural exploration of the energy minima on the PESs of transition metal tetramers was performed by combining a stochastic search algorithm with electronic structure calculations. Initial candidate geometries were generated with the molecular KICK code, a stochastic algorithm that efficiently samples potential energy landscapes by producing diverse structural motifs and avoiding bias toward preconceived geometries.60,61 In this regard, we proceeded as follows: the KICK procedure was employed to generate 100 initial random structures for each cluster system. All generated structures were initially optimized at the PBE1PBE/SDDALL level, without frequency calculations, and then ranked by their relative energies. Structures presenting identical or very similar relative energies were further inspected from a geometrical point of view to determine whether they corresponded to duplicate structures or to genuinely different isomers, considering variations in the relative positions of the atoms within the cluster. When structures had the same energy and no meaningful geometrical differences, they were considered equivalent. Conversely, nearly isoenergetic structures with distinguishable geometrical arrangements were retained as independent isomers. In addition, some chemically reasonable isomeric arrangements that were not obtained directly from the stochastic KICK search were proposed based on previous studies and on structural trends observed for smaller or related systems analyzed in the present work. These additional candidates were also optimized at the PBE1PBE/SDDALL and included in the energetic comparison when they represented distinct structures.

Each generated structure was subsequently optimized with density functional theory (DFT), using the TPSS exchange-correlation functional, as implemented in Gaussian.83,84 This meta-GGA functional combines TPSS exchange and TPSS correlation in a nonempirical framework that depends on the electron density, its gradient, and the kinetic-energy density, satisfying a broad set of exact constraints and providing balanced accuracy for molecular structures and energies.84 Baek et al. performed a benchmark study of DFT for a neutral small gold cluster, showing that this computational setup is among the most accurate for geometries within a diverse group of 44 exchange–correlation functionals.85 Scalar relativistic effects were accounted for via the Stuttgart/Dresden effective core potentials, and the corresponding SDD valence basis sets were applied to all atoms through the SDDALL keyword, which invokes both the ECPs and their associated contracted basis functions for the entire system. Using frequency calculations at the same “TPSSTPSS/SDDALL” level, it was verified that the structures found effectively correspond to minima in the PESs (that is, structures in which all vibrational frequencies are greater than zero). For each stoichiometry, multiple spin multiplicities were considered, allowing the identification of global and low-lying local minima across different electronic states. Duplicate or redundant geometries were removed. Calculations were carried out with the Gaussian 16 package.83 The optimizations were carried out without any geometric constraints, and the convergence criteria used were: maximum force = 4.5 × 10−4 Hartree Bohr−1, RMS force = 3.0 × 10−4 Hartree Bohr−1, maximum displacement = 1.8 × 10−3 Bohr, and RMS displacement = 1.2 × 10−3 Bohr. In addition, the final optimization steps showed very small predicted energy changes, typically of the order of 10−8–10−10 a.u., which further supports the numerical stability of the optimized geometries.

The energy calculations for the atoms were performed under the same theoretical conditions as those for the tetramers, using Gaussian's default parameters and without any additional restrictions on their electronic structure beyond those inherent to the “TPSSTPSS/SDDALL” level theory.

To investigate optical properties, linear-response time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations at the same “TPSSTPSS/SDDALL” level were carried out for global minima, yielding vertical excitation energies, oscillator strengths, and simulated UV-vis absorption spectra. The calculations were performed without any additional restrictions on the electronic structure. In all cases, 50 excited states were calculated. To validate spectral features and to account for improved relativistic effects, additional TD-DFT calculations were performed for selected systems using the TPSSh hybrid functional within the ZORA (Zeroth-Order Regular Approximation) framework and the TZVP basis set, as implemented in ORCA 6.0.1.86 Since these calculations have specific requirements, we have included the original inputs in the section where the report appears (highlighted in green) in the SI.

Results and discussion

Characterization of low-energy minima on the PESs of homotetramers (M4, M = Fe, Ru, Os, Co, Rh, Ir, Ni, Pd, Pt, Cu, Ag, Au)

Fig. 1 shows that, in general, for tetramers consisting of only one type of atom (homotetramers) the landscape of low-energy minima is quite rich with various structures and total spins within narrow energy ranges, which is striking for such a small cluster size. Especially rich in possibilities are Ni4, Pt4, Co4, Ir4, and Ru4. In this sense we can also find cases of practically isoenergetic structures of either different or equal spin multiplicities.

Each of the energy states shown corresponds to a structure and is ordered in ascending order of relative energy for each stoichiometry, with 0.0 kcal mol−1 being the global minimum. For ease of visualization, only the Cu4 geometries are shown (See SI for the other geometries).

For simplicity, only the Cu4 geometries are shown in the figure as an example. The remaining structures can be consulted in the SI, along with their coordinates and relative energies. The geometries of the global minima are shown in Fig. 2. The Cu4 species, as illustrated in Fig. 1, shows that there can be cases of different spin multiplicities with similar geometries (same geometric motif), although with different energies, and cases of different geometries for the same spin multiplicity. The tetramers Au4, Co4, Ir4, Fe4, and Ru4 show that for the possibilities of use where the global minima are the relevant ones, they can be accompanied by structures close in energy that can influence or modify the properties of the tetramer because of thermal sampling or low-lying structures. For these cases, structural and spin interconversion may also be relevant, making the fluxionality and tunability of properties for tetramers in the gaseous state significant. In the same way, we can affirm that in the cases Cu4, Ag4, Ni4, Pd4, and Rh4 the global minimum would be geometrical-electronically quite stable in the gaseous state, with properties that depend on a single structure with a fixed spin multiplicity. As indicated in the introduction, we consider that not only global minima are worthy of being searched and reported, but also other more energetic structures, which could be present in thermal equilibrium if temperature conditions allow it, or when these higher energy structures are stabilized by some external agent, such as a cavity, a surface-support, etc. In this sense, the exhaustive identification of minima made by us is relevant.

Geometries of global energy minima and their binding energies per atom, at the TPSS/SDD level, for the homotetramers studied here.

On the other hand, Fig. 2 shows that the BEs per atom (eqn (1)) or cohesion energy per atom varies considerably with the nature of the metal. Except for homotetramers of group 11 (Cu, Ag, Au), in the others (group 10: Ni, Pd, Pt; group 9: Co, Rh, Ir; group 8: Fe, Ru) the most massive atom studied in the group is the one with the highest BE, which would be in accordance with its higher number of electrons in the same group.

As a key takeaway for this subsection, the PESs of homotetramers are characterized by a high density of low-energy isomers with different geometries and spin states, indicating significant intrinsic fluxionality. A few systems, like Cu4 and Pd4, present a more isolated global minimum, suggesting greater structural rigidity at low temperatures.

Heterotetramers based on Cu, Pd, and Ni: structures and stabilities

Copper- and palladium-based heterotetramers

Fig. 3 shows the energy minima within an energy range of 60 kcal mol−1 in the TPSS/SDD PESs of the CuxM4−x (M = Ag, Au or Pd) and PdxM4−x (M = Ag or Au) heterotramers. In general, and roughly speaking, we can observe that for each composition, the energy increases with the increase in spin multiplicity, so that, in turn, structures of different energies are grouped under the same spin multiplicity. This is different from that shown for homotetramers (Fig. 1). An exception occurs in the case of Pd2Au2, where a triplet structure is situated between two singlet structures, with one of them being the global minimum.

Fig. 3 also shows that in these heterotetramers the energy difference between the respective global minimum and the next lowest energy structure is higher than in the case of homotetramers, so that, at least in this electronic energetic aspect, it is not as easy to populate the next isomer in energy by thermal means. Indeed, in Fig. 1 the smallest gap with respect to the global minimum is 0.9 kcal mol−1 for Fe4, i.e. we are dealing with, practically, two global minima of very similar energy. In contrast, for the heterotetramers of Fig. 3, the smallest gap is 3.2 kcal mol−1 for Cu1Ag3. This means that, for the possibilities of use where the global minima are the relevant ones, they can hardly be accompanied by structures close in energy, so their properties would originate from a single structure of the same spin multiplicity; the lowest possible spin multiplicity for each stoichiometry.

On the other hand, Fig. 4 shows the BEs per atom (eqn (1)) for the global minima of these heterotetramers. We see that their BEs per atom roughly follow a rule of mixtures. That is, the BEs of heterotetramers are in line with what would be expected by combining the fragments of the corresponding homotetramers in Fig. 2: the values of BEs per atom of Ag4 and Cu4 are 26.16 and 33.97 kcal mol−1, respectively, which makes the Cu–Ag combination have BEs per atom also between these two values (between 28.42 and 32.38 kcal mol−1, Fig. 4). In fact, replacing a silver atom in Ag4 by a copper atom makes its BE increase a little, since BE of Cu4 is higher. This trend is maintained by replacing more Ag atoms by Cu.

This rule of mixtures holds for the combinations Cu–Pd (a range of 35.65–40.85 kcal mol−1; Cu4: 33.97 kcal mol−1, and Pd4: 40.61 kcal mol−1), Pd–Ag (a range of 29.07–38.23 kcal mol−1; Ag4 26.16 kcal mol−1, Pd4: 40.61 kcal mol−1), and Pd–Au (a range of 34.85–39.46 kcal mol−1; Au4: 33.08 kcal mol−1, Pd4: 40.61 kcal mol−1). The Cu–Au combination is a special case because the BE per atom lies in the range of 37.02–40.16 kcal mol−1, whereas the BE per atom of the homotetramers are only 33.97 kcal mol−1 (Cu4) and 33.08 kcal mol−1 (Au4). Unlike the other combinations, mixing strengthens the cohesion per atom. Moreover, the variation pattern is not smooth: Cu2Au2 exhibits the largest BE per atom, representing a kind of “magic combination”.

The other thing we can observe in Fig. 4 is that these kinds of heterotetramers adopt the geometrical motif of the homotetramer of the metal that is in higher proportion. For example, Cu1Pd3 consists of a pyramidal structure such as Pd4, while Cu3Pd1 has a rhombohedral shape like Cu4. For Cu2Pd2 and for the remaining 2 : 2 combinations of Pd, the pyramidal Pd4 shape prevails.

As a key takeaway for this subsection, we would have that in Cu- and Pd-based heterotetramers, binding energies often follow a rule of mixtures, with the Cu–Au system being a notable exception exhibiting enhanced, “magic” stability at the Cu2Au2 composition. These systems also tend to have larger energy gaps to their first isomers, suggesting greater structural robustness compared to homotetramers.

Nickel-based heterotetramers

We are interested in researching nickel metal due to its strategic role in global technology, such as in batteries and electronics, and its promising future projections, especially in the field of catalysis.98–100 Another important reason for conducting research on its use in small quantities, such as in subnanoclusters, is that its massive extraction (nickel production is increasing and demand is expected to multiply 3 to 5 times by 2040)101 leads to serious environmental and social problems.101 Therefore, the next tetramers of interest to us are combinations of Ni with the other metals studied in this paper.

Fig. 5 and 6 show the relative energies of heterotetramers formed by Ni with Cu, Ag, Au, Pd, Pt, Co, Rh, Ir, Fe, or Ru. As in the previous cases (Fig. 1 and 3), only in the Ni1Pd3 (Fig. 5) and the Ni3Rh1 (Fig. 6) heterotetramers do we show geometries as illustrative examples of different structures for the same total spin or different total spins for the same geometrical motif. As in previous cases these landscapes of low-energy minima are very rich with several structures in narrow energy ranges, and with almost isoenergetic structures. Unlike the heterotetramers of Fig. 3, here the energy states (and their corresponding structures) are not grouped by total spin and the energy does not follow a regular pattern of increasing with spin multiplicity. In fact, only in the cases of the combinations of 1 Ni atom (group 10 of the periodic table) with 3 atoms of group 11; Ni1Cu3, Ni1Ag3, and Ni1Au3, it is given that the global minimum corresponds to the lowest possible total spin state of S = 1/2. As shown in Fig. 7, in these three cases the structures are rhomboids, as in the global minima of Cu4, Ag4, and Au4 (Fig. 2), with Ni at the least sharp vertex. Clearly in Ni1Cu3, Ni1Ag3, and Ni1Au3 the preponderant geometrical and electronic situation is dictated by the atoms of group 11, of basal configuration [noble gas]nd10(n + 1)s1), which, in conjunction with 1 Ni atom, tend to form molecular orbitals of different energy and nature among them, favoring pairing tendency and low total spins. Since Cu, Ag, and Au are slightly more electronegative than Ni, they would tend to extract electron density from Ni, so that the unpaired electron (unpaired because there is an odd number of atoms of group 11 in these heterotetramers, and because Ni has even number of electrons) would be located in the Ni atom. In the other cases the spin multiplicities of the global minima are of intermediate values between their possibilities, reaching values as high as 5 (Ni3Pd1, Ni2Pt2, Ni3Pt1, Ni2Rh2, Ni2Ir2, and Ni1Fe3), 6 (Ni3Co1, and Ni1Rh3), 7 (Ni2Co2, and Ni3Ru1), and 9 (Ni3Fe1, Ni1Ru3, and Ni2Ru2), thus projecting significant magnetic properties.

As can be seen in both (Fig. 5 and 6), it is rare to find heterotetramers with a limited number of structures within the studied energy range. Notable cases are Ni1Pd3 and Ni3Rh1 with only 3 and 4 structures, respectively in a total range of 55 kcal mol−1. Also, cases where the global minimum is quite separated in energy from the next structure occur mostly when combining Ni with atoms of its same group (10 in the periodic table) or with atoms of group 11, as shown in Fig. 5 for the cases Ni3Cu1, Ni2Ag2, Ni3Ag1, Ni1Au3, Ni2Au2, Ni3Au1, Ni3Pd1, Ni2Pd2, and Ni3Pt1. For the combination of Ni with atoms of groups 8 and 9, only the cases Ni3Co1, Ni3Rh1, Ni2Ir2, and Ni3Fe1 are in this situation. In all these structures we can expect, at least at relatively low temperatures and without any other external factor, that the properties in the gaseous state come exclusively from the global minimum. The case of Ni3Fe1 is striking; it has a spin multiplicity of 9, i.e., 8 unpaired electrons, a huge magnetism, and separated by the next structure, a septuplet, 6 unpaired electrons, by an energy of 4.5 kcal mol−1, which is not easily achievable by thermal means, at least as far as this electronic aspect is concerned. These clusters with a large magnetic moment have been previously observed and explained as a ferromagnetic bonding stabilized exchange.102–105

Similarly, Fig. 5 and 6 show several cases in which the global minimum is accompanied by structures very close in energy, and even practically isoenergetic. Extreme cases are: Ni2Pt2, with two quintuplets separated by just 0.7 kcal mol−1, but of different geometries (see SI to observe their geometries), where the global minimum is rhomboid (Fig. 7) and the one following in energy is pyramidal; Ni1Co3, with two quartets at 0.7 kcal mol−1 difference, both of the same geometrical motif, but with different geometrical parameters; Ni2Co2, two nanoplets of different geometry, at 0.4 and 0.6 kcal mol−1 above the septuplet global minimum; Ni3Ir1, with a pyramidal sextuplet at 0.8 kcal mol−1 above the global minimum pyramidal quartet; Ni1Ru3, a pyramidal septuplet at 0.1 kcal mol−1 above the global minimum pyramidal nonet; and Ni2Ru2, a pyramidal septuplet structure at 0.2 kcal mol−1 above the “boat” type global minimum nonet.

Fig. 7 and 8 show the cohesion energies per atom (BEs per atom, (eqn (1))) of the respective global minimum for the heterotetramers formed by nickel and the other metals studied. As in the tetramers in Fig. 4, almost all the values obtained are within the range formed by BE per atom of Ni4 and BE per atom of the homotetramer of the metal in the respective binary combination. For example, in Ni–Cu we expect values between 33.97 kcal mol−1 (Cu4) and 42.96 kcal mol−1 (Ni4) and, indeed, we have the progressive increase from 34.67 kcal mol−1 for Ni1Cu3, through 37.22 kcal mol−1 for Ni2Cu2, to 39.14 kcal mol−1 for Ni3Cu1. Similarly occurs for almost all other cases, with an increase or decrease of BE per atom as the number of Ni atoms in the heterotetramer increases (see Fig. 7 and 8), depending on whether it is a metal that has its homotetramer with lower BE per atom, or higher, respectively, with respect to Ni4. Similarly, as we found above for the Cu–Au combination, there are two exceptions to this: the Ni2Au2 heterotetramer, and the Ni–Pd combinations, which have higher cohesion energy values per atom than expected. For example, in the cases of Ni1Pd3 and Ni2Pd2, applying the predictive eqn (10) and comparing the results with the calculated values yields additional mixing stabilization energies per atom of 3.29 and 2.67 kcal mol−1, respectively. Again, one could say that these are sort of “magic combinations” in terms of the cohesion of their atoms.

As a key takeaway for this subsection, we would have that Nickel-based heterotetramers display a remarkably diverse range of behaviors. Their energy-minima on PESs are often highly complex, with many low-lying isomers. The spin states of the global minima vary widely, from low spin (with group 11 elements) to very high spin (with Fe and Ru), indicating tunable magnetic properties. Like the Cu–Au system, Ni2Au2 and several Ni–Pd combinations exhibit enhanced stability beyond a simple rule of mixtures, identifying them as further “magic” compositions.

Electronic properties

Optical excitations

We will start the discussion with a tetramer for which experimental UV-vis absorption spectra are available. This allows us to compare with our predictions and thereby check the validity of TD-DFT at the TPSS/SDD level as an approximated method for a first estimate of the optical properties. After that we will proceed to discuss the UV-vis absorption spectra of a few representative interesting systems for which, to our knowledge, there is no experimental information of this type. A comprehensive listing of allowed transitions, oscillator strengths, and simulated spectra for all global minima is provided in the SI.

From the optical point of view, one of the most studied ultra-small neutral metal clusters is 1Ag4. UV-vis absorption spectra have been obtained for this species with different techniques, especially by means of its isolation in rare gases cryogenic matrices to overcome the problem of the critical concentration required for the measurements.106–108 All these spectra show that two signals in the UV-violet are basically the most characteristic: the most intense is between 3.0–3.1 eV (413 to 400 nm), and the next one is between 4.15–4.23 eV (299 to 293 nm). A third signal, obtained in Ne matrix,108 that follows in intensity is located at about 5.43 eV (228 nm).

Fig. 9 shows our predicted UV-vis spectrum for the global minimum (D2h, see SI) of Ag4 within that energy region. As can be seen, our methodology is able to predict reasonably well the absorption spectrum of this species: a very strong signal at 3.0 eV (413 nm), a region between 3.5 and 5.5 eV (354 and 226 nm), which has a signal at 4.08 eV (304 nm), followed by other less intense group of signals centered at 4.38 eV (283 nm), and finally a signal at 5.11 eV (243 nm). Our signals do not exactly match the experimental ones, but they are closer compared to TD-DFT calculations from the literature with other functionals and basis sets.108 Moreover, it should be taken into account that spectra of metal clusters embedded in solid noble gas matrices are somewhat distorted by the matrix effect.108,109 For the next energy low-lying isomer, C2v, (singlet) at 5.5 kcal mol−1 higher in energy with respect to the global minimum, the spectrum is different (see SI), so it is discarded as has also been described elsewhere.107,108

As mentioned above 1Ag4 absorbs from UV to violet. The component atomic orbitals of the main transition responsible for the excitation in the violet (around 413 nm, 3.0 eV) are mainly of s (HOMO) to s (LUMO + 1) type, with smaller contributions from p-like and d-like orbitals, which is confirmed by natural transition orbitals (see SI). This excitation could be used to generate some useful optical effect. For example, Felix et al.,106 show a fluorescence peak at 2.71 eV (458 nm, blue) after exciting around that wavelength in Ar matrix at 25 K.

Excitations from closed shell ground states (S = 0)

Although, strictly speaking, 〈S2〉 is not a true observable in DFT, it can be taken as an approximate diagnostic tool. So, judging by the 〈S2〉 = 0 values (see SI) for all vertical excitations from ground state closet-shell tetramers, we can say that, in general, in these systems the singlet → singlet transitions occur in a pristine manner, with no participation/contamination of other multiplets.

We focus here on a few illustrative examples of optical behavior. For a comprehensive and detailed analysis, including the nature of the primary excitations, please refer to SI. The closed-shell group 11 tetramers (e.g., 1Cu4, 1Ag4, 1Au4) and their alloys (e.g., 1CuxAu4−x) all exhibit intense absorption features (with 0.1 ≤ f (oscillator strength) ≤ 1 as the criterion), which have similarities to the above-described 1Ag4 case: intense violet absorptions of analogous electronic origin of mainly s to s character (see SI).

In contrast, some pyramidal closed-shell systems like 1Rh4 and 1Cu2Pd2 display much weaker absorptions (f < 0.1), with, for example, 1Rh4 showing weak signal even in the red region (ca. 625 nm and 686 nm), involving d → s type transitions (see SI).

Excitations from open shell ground states (S > 0)

According to the indicator 〈S2〉 (see SI) some vertical excitations in open-shell tetramers occur with very little alteration of the total spin, but many others have significant spin contamination, which is a sign of involvement of other multiplets, revealing the multireferential nature of many of these excitations. However, as was made clear from the beginning, our aim is to make an exhaustive study in terms of the number of systems and local minima, but only preliminary in terms of the nature of the electronic states, so the treatment of multireference character, relativistic effects at a higher level of theory and spin–orbit couplings are not the focus of this paper and would be matter for further refinements based on our results.

With these limitations in mind, let us now turn to a general illustrative review of the approximate optical behavior of open-shell ground states. A comprehensive and detailed analysis is provided in the SI.

The open-shell tetramers exhibit a wide range of absorption intensities. Among those that absorb most strongly (f ≤ 1.0), 2Cu3Pd1 is notable for its absorption in the green region at 550 nm, an excitation of s → s character. Several Ni-containing systems, such as 2Ni1Ag3 and 3Ni2Ag2, absorb strongly in the UV to blue range.

A particularly interesting class of systems are those that display weak absorptions (f ≤ 0.1) extending from the visible spectrum to the near-infrared (NIR). For example, 2Pd1Ag3 absorbs between 340 and 625 nm, while 9Ni2Ru2 shows weak signals from the dark red to the IR region (700–770 nm). These low-energy absorptions, which appear in several high-spin systems (see SI for a full list), are primarily of d → d character. Such intra d-band transitions have been recently reported by Ferrari et al.110 for small cationic Co clusters. As an illustrative test, we recalculated the excitations for two tetramers with such features, 4Ni1Ir3, and 5Ni4, using the TPSSh hybrid functional within the ZORA framework with a TZVP basis set. These calculations confirmed the d–d origin of the weak NIR signals (e.g., at 1149 and 1333 nm, respectively), lending support to our initial assignments (see SI).

As a key takeaway for this subsection we would have those optical properties of tetramers are highly tunable. Closed-shell group 11 systems exhibit strong, characteristic UV-vis absorptions, while many open-shell, high-spin tetramers show additional weak d–d transitions that can extend into the NIR. This suggests potential for designing clusters with tailored optical responses across a broad spectral range.

Reactivity trends: conceptual DFT descriptors and static reactivity patterns

With a view to an initial basic inquiry concerning catalytic properties, in this subsection we report the reactivity trends predicted by conceptual density functional theory.87–91 It is important to first consider the scope of the analysis in this subsection: conceptual DFT descriptors characterize the static electronic properties of isolated species. In our case, they will provide a qualitative insight into the trends inherent in electrophilic or nucleophilic behavior, but they will not reflect the dynamic structural changes that may occur upon interaction with a reactant (or due to fluxionality). Consequently, these indices should be interpreted as a preliminary guide to possible reactivity patterns, and not as definitive predictors of catalytic activity.

Let us start the analysis with a pair of key global reactivity indices, the Mulliken electronegativity, χ (eqn (4)),111 and the hardness, η (eqn (5)).95χ indicates the greater or lesser tendency of the tetramers (as a whole) to gain external electron density, as well as their lesser or greater tendency to lose their electron density. As has been shown in conceptual DFT,87,93χ is equivalent to the negative of the chemical potential, µ, of Iczkowski and Margrave,92 and, moreover, as we have independently checked for our systems, χ follows basically the same behavior as the electrophilicity index (ω = µ2/(2η),112 so it suffices to analyze χ as an index representative of µ and ω as well. η, which is equivalent to the fundamental HOMO–LUMO gap, is a measure of the resistance of a molecular species to a change in its electron density. A large η means the system is less polarizable, which is usually interpreted as less reactive or less prone to charge transfer, while a small η implies high polarizability and reactivity, and greater propensity for charge transfer. Fig. 10 shows that among the homotetramers (from Cu4 to Ru4), those with the highest Mulliken electronegativity would be Au4, Pt4 and Ir4, while those that would behave in the opposite way in this respect would be Ni4, Rh4, Fe4 and Ru4. On the other hand, those with the greatest chemical hardness would be Cu4, Au4, Pd4 and Rh4 (Rh4 is the hardest of all). The most chemically soft, i.e., the most likely to react (distort its electron density) would be Ni4, Co4, Ir4, Fe4 and Ru4.

The key global reactivity indices of Mulliken electronegativity (a.u.) and chemical hardness (a.u.) for all the tetramers in this study.

Combining these two aspects, we observe that Au4 is the most electronegative, yet quite hard. Although it has a greater tendency to conserve or gain electron density, its relatively large hardness would render it inert, according to these criteria. Ni4, on the other hand, is the most electropositive and is relatively soft, therefore it could be labeled soft nucleophilic. Rh4 is electropositive, but at the same time is the hardest, so it could be labeled as a hard nucleophile. Meanwhile, Ir4 is electronegative and soft at the same time, and it could be labeled as a soft electrophile. Fe4 and Ru4 would be labeled as soft nucleophiles. Note that this separation between hard and soft is relative to the values of the systems studied. Indeed, all of them could be considered soft when compared to the range of values of hardness spanned by the periodic table (3 to 10 eV; noble gases excluded).94

Following this same logic of analysis, for heterotetramers we have that the most outstanding examples of possible combinations in terms of their relative behavior are:

(a) Tendency to be more electronegative and chemically hard: Cu1Au3, Cu2Au2, Cu3Au1, Ni1Au3, Ni2Au2, Pd2Au2, and Ni2Pt2.

(b) Tendency to be more electropositive and chemically hard: Cu1Pd3, Cu2Pd2, Pd2Ag2, Pd3Ag1, Pd3Au1, Ni1Pd3, Ni1Rh3, and Ni2Rh2.

(c) Tendency to be more electronegative and chemically soft: Pd1Au3 and Ni1Pt3.

(d) Tendency to be more electropositive and chemically soft: Cu1Ag3, Cu2Ag2, Ni1Co3, Pd1Ag3, Ni1Cu3, Ni3Cu1, Ni1Ag3, Ni3Ag1, Ni2Pd2, Ni3Pd1, Ni3Pt1, Ni1Co3, Ni2Co2, Ni3Co1, Ni3Rh1, Ni2Ir2, Ni3Ir1, Ni1Fe3, Ni2Fe2, Ni3Fe1, Ni1Ru3, Ni2Ru2, and Ni3Ru1.

While organic compounds tend to follow the maximum hardness rule,113,114i.e., greater hardness implies greater stability, this correlation does not appear to hold in these transition metals. In the SI, we have plotted binding energy versus the chemical hardness for 56 representative clusters, and no discernible correlation emerges.

If we were to anticipate the choice of a particular tetramer as catalyst, based on these criteria of global reactivity, we would have to consider, of course, the reagent and the reaction itself, and, if, for example, what we want to achieve is that a reagent having an electron-accepting site only adsorbs, through that site, on the catalyst, which must be electropositive, without the possibility of further modifying the catalyst (chemically hard), we would choose Rh4. For a completely opposite effect, we would choose Ir4 or Fe4, and so on.

Now, considering this discussion and the highlighted systems, let us select the following exemplifying tetramers to carry out a local reactivity analysis: Ni4, Rh4, Ir4, Cu1Au3, Pd2Au2, Cu2Pd2, Ni1Pd3, Ni3Co1, and Ni2Fe2. A couple of rather important local reactivity indices are the dual descriptor, Δf(r) (eqn (6)), and the condensed dual descriptor, fa2 (eqn (8)).87 The Δf(r), visually shows the electrophilic sites (nucleophilic attack on them), Δf(r) > 0, and nucleophilic sites (electrophilic attack on them), Δf(r) < 0, of the molecular species. The second of these, fa2, corresponds to a condensation of Δf(r) to the atoms “a”, of the molecular species. fa2 has no more information than Δf(r), but it makes quantitative analysis easier.

Fig. 11 shows Δf(r) isosurfaces and fa2 values for the selected tetramers. Global reactivity indices predicted that Ni4 is relatively more electropositive and softer. In Fig. 11, we see that, indeed, Ni4 tends to be slightly nucleophilic (i.e., able to donate electron density), as Δf(r) tends to be negative. Note that this ability is concentrated in the pair of atoms that are closest to each other in the structure (labels 1–4 and 2–3, both separated at 2.21 Å).

Δf(r) isosurfaces and fa2 = CDD table of values for selected tetramers. Blueish isosurfaces correspond to Δf(r) < 0 (nucleophilic sites), whereas the reddish ones to Δf(r) > 0 (electrophilic sites).

For its part, Rh4 is also slightly nucleophilic in all four atoms equally, due to its high symmetry. This coincides with the predictions of global indices (electropositive, but chemically hard). Ir4, which was predicted to be relatively more electronegative and softer, has two slightly electrophilic atomic sites (Δf(r) > 0 (a higher isosurface was used to make it distinguishable), which coincides with fa2 > 0 for atoms 2 and 3.

On the other hand, the Cu1Au3 heterotetramer is predicted to be globally more electrophilic (electronegative), which would happen, through the Au atom of label 2 in Fig. 11 judging by the value of fa2. It is followed in electrophilicity by the Cu atom. However, it also has nucleophilic sites Au(3) and Au(4). For Pd2Au2, which is globally more electronegative and chemically harder, is locally only slightly electrophilic through the Au atoms. The Pd atoms are slightly nucleophilic to the same extent that the Au atoms are electrophilic (according to the fa2 values). Cu2Pd2, which is relatively and globally more electropositive and chemically harder, is clearly nucleophilic through the Pd atoms, but is locally predicted to be electrophilic on the Cu atoms. On the other hand, Ni1Pd3 is locally nucleophilic through its three Pd atoms equally but is strongly electrophilic by the Ni atom. Ni3Co1 is strongly nucleophilic through the Co atom (which coincides with the fact that it is global and relatively more electropositive), but it is slightly electrophilic through its 3 Ni atoms. On the other hand, the nucleophilicity of Ni2Fe2 is due to its Fe atoms, especially the one labeled 3 in Fig. 11. The Ni atoms would be electrophilic sites.

As a key takeaway for this subsection we would have that global reactivity indices provide a qualitative, first-order classification of tetramers as nucleophiles or electrophiles with varying hardness. Local descriptors like the dual descriptor further reveal that reactivity is often site-specific, with different atoms in a heterotetramer showing opposing electrophilic/nucleophilic character. This site selectivity, as seen in Ni1Pd3 or Cu2Pd2, could be crucial for guiding multi-step or cooperative catalysis, though these predictions remain to be tested with explicit reaction models.

Finally, looking at all the results as a whole, we can say that they are valuable for basic science and may be useful in direct catalysis in the gas phase,14,15 but we would like to conclude this general section about results with a brief comment on how they may guide the design of ligand-stabilized, cavity-confined, or supported subnanometric clusters. First, the identification of “magic” heterometallic compositions (e.g., Cu2Au2 and Ni2Au2) that exhibit enhanced cohesion relative to a linear mixing rule provides a thermodynamic basis for selecting alloy compositions that are likely to remain stable once isolated from the gas phase and anchored on a support or encapsulated in a matrix.115,116 Second, the demonstration that strong visible absorptions occur only for specific combinations (e.g., Ni with Ag or Au) whereas d → d NIR transitions dominate in high-spin systems (e.g., Ni with Fe or Ru) offers design principles for optical readout or photocatalytic activation of supported clusters, since the molecular-like electronic structure of the free tetramer can be partially preserved even when confined,115,116 especially if the cavity is such that it can preserve the properties of cluster, as is the case in inert matrices, or when the cluster itself possesses a certain degree of stability.115,116 Third, the computed local reactivity descriptors reveal that different atoms within a heterotetramer can simultaneously act as electrophilic and nucleophilic sites, suggesting that bimetallic clusters confined in cavities or stabilized by ligands could function as dual-site catalysts, analogous to the synergistic effects observed in gas-phase cluster catalysis.12 The fluxional behavior and multiple near-degenerate isomers identified for many tetramers imply that, upon ligand-passivation or encapsulation, these clusters may retain a degree of structural flexibility that can be exploited for adaptive catalysis or for the stabilization of otherwise metastable but catalytically relevant configurations.

Conclusions and final remarks

In this work, we have carried out a comprehensive and systematic theoretical investigation of transition metal tetramers, encompassing both homonuclear and heteronuclear compositions across the 3d, 4d, and 5d series. By combining stochastic structural searches with density functional theory, we achieved an extensive identification of energy minima on the PESs of these subnanometric clusters, explicitly accounting for multiple spin states and low-lying isomers.

Our results reveal a remarkable structural and electronic diversity within narrow energy ranges, highlighting the intrinsic fluxionality of many tetramers and the coexistence of multiple near-degenerate minima. While general periodic trends are reflected in the calculated binding energies, several heterometallic combinations deviate from these expectations and display enhanced cohesion, identifying “magic” compositions with increased stability. Such effects underscore the non-trivial role of atomic-scale metal mixing in governing the properties of subnanoclusters. Specifically, Cu2Au2, Ni2Au2, and several Ni–Pd combinations emerge as such “magic” compositions.

Beyond structural stability, the calculated optical and reactivity descriptors point to a wide functional variability across the series. Time-dependent DFT calculations predict intense UV-visible absorption features in selected systems and distinctive spectral features in selected Ni-containing species, while conceptual DFT descriptors reveal a broad spectrum of electrophilic and nucleophilic behaviors. These optical simulations, validated against experimental data for Ag4, highlight the tunability of absorption from the UV to the NIR. The reactivity descriptors, while limited to static predictions, suggest design rules for site-specific reactivity, such as the electrophilic Ni site in Ni1Pd3 or the nucleophilic Co site in Ni3Co1. These findings emphasize that small changes in composition and geometry can induce pronounced modifications in both optical response and chemical reactivity at the subnanometric scale.

Taken together, this study provides a unified and comparative framework for understanding how composition, geometry, and spin state collectively determine the properties of transition metal tetramers. The trends and exceptional cases identified here are expected to contribute to the rational design of materials at the subnanometer scale. While further refinements incorporating relativistic and multireference effects may be required for specific systems, the present results establish a robust baseline for future investigations into subnanometric metal clusters at the molecular–nanomaterial interface. In particular, the identification of “magic” compositions, the tunability of optical transitions from the UV to the NIR, and the predicted site-specific reactivity patterns provide concrete design guidelines for the development of ligand stabilized, cavity confined, or supported subnanometric clusters with tailored catalytic and photophysical properties.
