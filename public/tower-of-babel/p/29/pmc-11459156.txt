Alveolar macrophage function is impaired following inhalation of berry e-cigarette vapor
Authors: Amelia Kulle, Ziyi Li, Ashley Kwak, Mathieu Mancini, Daniel Young, Daina Zofija Avizonis, Marc Groleau, Carolyn J. Baglole, Marcel A. Behr, Irah L. King, Maziar Divangahi, David Langlais, Jing Wang, Julianna Blagih, Erika Penz, Antoine Dufour, Ajitha Thanabalasuriar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAlveolar macrophages (AMs) are specialized resident macrophages tasked with safeguarding the lower respiratory tract. Our study reveals that AMs utilize cell division control protein 42 (CDC42)-mediated actin polymerization pathways for their movement. However, the inhalation of the popular berry-flavored e-cigarette products leads to a halt in AM movement. This impairment in AM mobility leads to diminished bacterial clearance by the cells, consequently reducing the survival rate of animals.

Positioned at the crossroads between the external environment and the body’s internal organs, the lung emerges as a vital hub of immunity. Their constant exposure to the nonsterile external air results in the development of a distinctive profile of innate immune cells compared to those found in other organs (1–4). Pulmonary innate immune cells help maintain a delicate control of inflammation, as prolonged immune infiltration can compromise lung function (5). These cells include tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (AMs), which act as the sentinels that safeguard the airways by defending against pathogens and pollutants within the lower respiratory tract (2–4, 6). They play a pivotal role in coordinating both the onset and resolution of pulmonary immune responses.

AMs represent the front line in airway protection and are directly exposed to inhaled particulates. Therefore, it is important to understand whether and how environmental exposures alter their function. E-cigarettes (e-cigs) are inhalation devices created to replace combustible tobacco cigarettes. They heat an organic liquid [consisting of propylene glycol (PG) and vegetable glycerin (VG)] containing nicotine and flavorings, generating a vapor that is inhaled (7, 8). Due to the availability of desirable fruit and candy flavors, e-cigs have become exceedingly popular among the younger population, particularly between the ages of 13 and 18 y old (8). Importantly, some adolescent users of e-cig products have displayed rapid onset of pathologies such as lung injury (9–12). While classical tobacco cigarettes’ detrimental effects typically take decades to manifest, adverse outcomes of e-cigs can occur in some individuals within months (9–12). The association between adolescent e-cig consumption and poor lung health is likely influenced by several factors. These may encompass their inclination toward binge consumption, preference for fruity and sweet flavors, and the impact of such usage on lung immunity (13–15).

The complexity of the alveolar network renders it a unique tissue environment. Each alveolus is ~38 to 80 μm in diameter, and adjacent alveoli are connected by 0.8 to 13 μm channels called the pores of Kohn (16–18). AMs traverse between and within alveolar spaces to patrol them, weaving in and out of the open alveolar environment and squeezing through the pores of Kohn (4). The mechanisms by which AMs achieve such dynamic movements are unclear; however, navigating a complex extracellular matrix requires coordinated actin polymerization (19). Immune cells have been shown to use the activation of Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome protein (WASp), an actin nucleation promoting factor, by the Rho GTPase cell division control protein 42 (CDC42) to navigate narrow or crowded environments (19, 20). CDC42-mediated pathways are required for immune cells to sense and squeeze their large nuclei through constricted environments (19). Nuclear squeezing and actin polymerization can be activated in both integrin-dependent and -independent manners (21).

AM motility is essential to maintain tissue homeostasis in the presence of inhaled pathogens. Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen and a common model organism for studying bacterial respiratory infections (22). Upon its inhalation, AMs clear the microbe without initiating inflammatory pathways. However, if AM chemotaxis is impaired, P. aeruginosa can colonize the airways, resulting in inflammatory neutrophil recruitment (4). Although healthy individuals rapidly clear the bacterium without issue, P. aeruginosa is a major cause of illness and death in those with immunosuppressive and chronic conditions (23). The ability of P. aeruginosa to colonize the lower respiratory tracts of chronically ill individuals is likely linked to compromised AM function (4).

In this study, we characterized the behavior of AMs at steady-state and following acute exposure to e-cig vapor. Our results revealed AMs encompass three distinct behaviors: probing, squeezing, and patrolling. Upon e-cig exposure, a significant reduction in AM motility was observed. Proteomic analysis of AMs unveiled alterations in cytoskeletal functions induced by berry-flavored vapor exposure, giving a mechanistic understanding of the diminished mobility of AMs. Importantly, when AMs encountered inhaled P. aeruginosa after berry-flavored vapor exposure, their capacity to clear the infection was compromised. Crucially, our observations indicate that exposure to berry-flavored vape reduces the expression of Rho GTPase CDC42, which is important for actin polymerization. Collectively, our findings highlight the intricate nature of AM movement and reveal their functions may be significantly impaired in berry-flavored e-cig users.

Results

Berry E-cig Vapes Limit AM Squeezing Motility within 3-d of Inhalation.

PKH26 is a stable membrane dye that specifically labels phagocytic cells and effectively stains AMs in vivo; therefore, it can be used to monitor AM movement by intravital microscopy (IVM) as previously described (4, 24, 25). We administered PKH26 to mice 5 days (d) before daily e-cig exposure for 3- or 10-d using a whole-body exposure system with a push-button e-cig pen containing berry-flavored vape juice with nicotine (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), then performed IVM (Fig. 1A). Control mice were treated the same but exposed to room air in the whole-body chamber. There were no differences in AM behavior between the air controls exposed for 3- and 10-d (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B); therefore, our data show the 10-d air controls. The mean net displacement [i.e., the distance traveled between the start of the 1-hour (h) video to the end] was significantly reduced in AMs from berry e-cig–exposed animals compared to AMs from air controls (Fig. 1 B and C). Tracking AMs by time-lapse microscopy for 1-h and plotting their trajectories revealed shorter cell tracks after berry e-cig exposure (Fig. 1B); accordingly, less of these AMs traveled >40 μm compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C and Movies S1 and S2) and did so at lower speeds (Fig. 1D). We next assessed whether the decreased AM movement and speed were due to an increase in cell size, hypothesizing the AMs in e-cig-exposed mice may become filled with PG/VG—a viscous organic compound—reducing their speed and movement. However, both 3- and 10-d cohort AMs appeared shrunken and smaller in size compared to air controls (Fig. 1 E and F).

We then characterized AM behaviors in air- and e-cig-exposed animals. Three distinct movement patterns were noted over the course of the 1-h video: a) squeezing—cells completely moving between alveolar spaces; Movie S3, b) patrolling—cells moving around the alveolus; Movie S4, and c) probing—cells extending protrusions into their current and adjacent alveoli without physically moving into the next alveolus; Movie S5. In air-exposed controls, we observed equal proportions of AMs squeezing, patrolling, and probing in each field of view (FOV; ~33% in each category, Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Upon exposures to e-cig vapor, probing behavior significantly increased (up to ~75%) and squeezing significantly decreased (down to ~9%; Fig. 1G, SI Appendix, Fig. S1D, and Movie S6). The patrolling phenotype remained after e-cig vape exposure but was less prevalent, decreasing to ~21% from 33% in e-cig-exposed animals (Fig. 1G, SI Appendix, Fig. S1D, and Movie S7). Intriguingly, the increase in probing behavior was also associated with longer probe lengths in e-cig-exposed animals (Fig. 1H). Additionally, the squeezing phenotype is required for AMs to move from one alveolus to the next. We observed a significant decrease in interalveolar migration in e-cig-exposed mice compared to air controls (Fig. 1 I and J). Together, these data show that e-cig exposure attenuated AM migration, and specifically their movement between alveoli.

Given the significant changes in AM behavior, we assessed AM viability by evaluating cell permeability using a live/dead staining system and observed no changes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Any proapoptotic pathway initiation was also ruled out as there were no increases in Caspase 3 cleavage (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Finally, we did not observe changes in cytokines that are indicative of pyroptosis or necroptosis, such as interleukin (IL)-1β or tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (Fig. 2 A and B) (26).

We previously demonstrated β2 integrins, specifically integrin subunit alpha L (ITGAL), were important in AM movement. In our previous work, while ITGAM and ITAGX expression increased during AM activation, blockade or deletion of ITGAL resulted in reduced AM movement (4). We thus assessed the abundance of these integrins on AMs after e-cig exposure and found that e-cig exposures did not alter ITGAL, ITGAM, or ITAGX levels (Fig. 2 C–E). The mechanisms behind e-cig-induced changes in AM behavior appear to extend beyond traditional integrin-mediated signaling and are unrelated to cell viability effects.

We next tested whether decreased AM movement had any physiological consequences and affected the cells’ ability to clear surfactant and other particulates from the airways. When AM function is perturbed, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) turbidity increases due to the accumulation of proteins in the airways (4, 27). We found that by 10-d of e-cig exposures BALF turbidity increased (Fig. 2F). We assessed whether this increase in turbidity was related to increased surfactant. Indeed, we did see an increase in surfactant A but not surfactant D in the BALF after exposure to berry vape (Fig. 2 G and H). Together, these data indicate that berry e-cig vape exposure altered AM movement and behaviors, resulting in physiological changes to lung homeostasis.

E-cig Exposure Does Not Induce AM Activation.

As we previously found that AMs reduced their movement upon activation, we assessed whether e-cig exposure caused AM activation (4). One indicator of AM activation is the reduced retrieval of these cells from the BALF, as activated AMs adhere more strongly to the alveolar epithelium, resulting in fewer cells being retrieved (4). We observed decreased AM retrieval from the BALF of e-cig-exposed mice compared to air controls, potentially indicating activation (Fig. 2 I and J). Therefore, we analyzed the levels of cytokines and chemokines AMs typically release in the BALF by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs). E-cig vape exposure did not significantly increase cytokine profiles as would be expected with increased activation (Fig. 2 A, B, K, and L). In fact, C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) was significantly decreased in the BALF of e-cig-exposed mice compared to air controls (Fig. 2K). Given that complement activation may also cause AMs to adhere to alveolar epithelium (28), we assessed complement protein C3 in the BALF. Although there was trend of increased complement C3, this did not reach significance among experiments (Fig. 2M). Finally, exposure to e-cig vape did not induce neutrophil, eosinophil, or monocyte recruitment to the airways (Fig. 2N and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F).

AMs can be classified as pro- or anti-inflammatory based on their expression of nitric oxide synthase 2, inducible (Nos2), or arginase 1 (Arg1), respectively (29). qPCR analysis of isolated AMs revealed that e-cig vape exposure led to a reduction in Nos2 without altering the expression of Arg1 (Fig. 2 O and P). These data suggest that while the AMs have decreased proinflammatory characteristics, they do not adopt a fully anti-inflammatory phenotype either. Alterations in metabolic functions can also indicate changes in the activation state of immune cells (30). We did not observe changes in the transcript level of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (Pparγ)—an important regulator of lipid metabolism—after 3- or 10-d of e-cig exposure (Fig. 2Q). However, ATP citrate lyase (Acly), which is involved in the first steps in fatty acid synthesis, was significantly increased after 3 d of e-cig exposure but decreased to similar levels as air controls by 10-d of e-cig exposure (Fig. 2R). Other markers of AM functional changes, such as expression of major histocompatibility complex 2 (MHC-II), were unaffected (Fig. 2S). Together these data demonstrate that although these AMs do not exhibit their classical switch between pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes, their homeostatic functions are altered by e-cig exposure. The reduced AM retrieval from the BALF after e-cig exposure indicates that they are likely trapped in the lungs, but the ELISA and qPCR data show this is not due to activation, rather the IVM data suggest that the defect lies in a reduced ability to squeeze through the pores of Kohn.

Berry Flavor E-cig Exposure Alters AM Cytoskeletal Signaling and Actin Organization.

To systematically identify molecular changes that could reduce AM locomotion after e-cig exposure, we performed untargeted quantitative proteomics on AMs isolated from mice exposed to e-cig vape or air for 10-d (Fig. 3A). Since recent studies have indicated that unflavored and flavored e-cigs may have different effects (31, 32), we performed proteomic analysis on both the berry-flavored vapor used in above experiments and an unflavored (flavorless) version of the same composition (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We identified 608 proteins in total in AMs isolated from mice exposed to the berry e-cig vape, of which 160 were identified as differentially expressed, with 70 proteins showing increased fold change and 90 showing decreased fold change compared to the air samples (Fig. 3 B and C). Intriguingly, flavorless e-cig exposure induced minimal changes, with only 25 proteins down-regulated and 10 up-regulated compared to air (of 1,670 total proteins identified; SI Appendix, Fig. S2A).

A comprehensive pathway analysis of the differentially expressed proteins upon exposure to berry-flavored e-cig vape revealed enrichment in various metabolic pathways, including carbon and amino acid metabolism and fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 3 D–F). These results are consistent with our qPCR analysis of metabolic activity, which revealed increased Acly expression (Figs. 2R and 3C). Of the minimal protein changes upon exposure to flavorless e-cig vape, pathways associated with metabolism were also noted in these samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). As changes in AM metabolic pathways often coincide with accumulation of lipids within them (33), we assessed lipid accumulation in AMs exposed to e-cig vapors using immunofluorescence. Accumulation of small lipid droplets in the AMs was observed as early as 3-d after vape exposure; however, there were no discernable differences between flavorless and berry e-cig–exposed groups (Fig. 3 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D), indicating that the metabolic alterations and lipid accumulation seen in AMs after e-cig exposure are not related to the presence of flavor in the products.

The enrichment analysis also revealed significant downregulation of some actin filament- and a mix of up- and down-regulated motor protein-related pathways in berry e-cig–exposed AMs compared to air controls (Fig. 3 D–F). Therefore, we examined cellular actin levels using phalloidin staining. Actin localization was significantly altered in berry e-cig–exposed AMs, with increased localization near the plasma membrane (Fig. 3I). Interestingly, actin intensity surrounding the nucleus was significantly diminished in berry but not in flavorless e-cig–exposed AMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and E). Additionally, IVM on flavorless e-cig–exposed mice revealed no discernable effects on AM displacement or behavior compared to air controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F–H). Together, these data suggest that modulations in actin cytoskeleton signaling and AM behaviors after e-cig exposure are dependent on the presence of the berry flavor.

Among the actin-related proteins altered specifically by berry e-cig exposure, we were particularly interested in CDC42, which is crucial for orchestrating actin polymerization around the nucleus during cell migration (34), and myosin heavy chain 14 (MYH14), a member of the nonmuscle myosin II family. Nonmuscle myosins play a role in nuclear positioning of cells by orienting the microtubule-organizing center and acting in concert with CDC42 to polarize the cell (35). Therefore, we assessed Cdc42 and Myh14 transcript expression in AMs from air- and berry e-cig–exposed mice. Cdc42 mRNA expression was decreased after 3- and 10-d of berry e-cig exposure (Fig. 3J), while Myh14 mRNA was significantly increased after 3-d but similar to control levels after 10-d (Fig. 3K). This is likely due to how MYH14 is regulated; the protein’s slower turnover may require only a transient increase in mRNA expression (36). As a control, we assessed expression of Itgal mRNA and found no changes (Fig. 3L), consistent with our flow cytometry data (Fig. 2C).

To identify compounds in the berry-flavored products that may be altering AM actin dynamics, we performed gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) on the flavorless and berry-flavored e-cig vape condensates. Several compounds, such as benzyl-alcohol, benzyl-acetate, and alpha-ionone, were uniquely identified in the heated berry condensate (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). Strikingly, these compounds all had chemical structures reminiscent of inhibitors of CDC42’s activity (37–39). Therefore, we performed docking-based virtual screening with these compounds at known allosteric CDC42 inhibition binding sites (40). Most flavor compounds in berry vape demonstrated affinity for residues in the allosteric inhibition site, like the noncompetitive allosteric CDC42 inhibitor (37, 38) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D and E). It is worth noting that nicotine, which is present in both the flavorless and berry vapors, also exhibits affinity for the reported CDC42 allosteric inhibition site, raising the possibility of additive effects between it and the other aromatic compounds that could intensify CDC42 inhibition. To test this, we exposed animals to single components identified in heated berry flavors and assessed AM displacement (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). Curiously, none of the single components of berry flavor was able to cause a significant reduction in AM movement similar to the combined berry e-juice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). As a further confirmation, we exposed animals to an unrelated flavor, menthol, and found no significant change of AM movement compared to air controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). From these data, it is evident that the combination of nicotine and many of the components of berry flavor is necessary to inhibit CDC42’s activity, leading to reduced AM displacement.

Functionally Inhibiting CDC42 in AMs Demonstrated Similar Behavior and Functional Alterations as Berry Vape Exposure.

In order to understand whether the effects seen after berry e-cig vape exposure were directly related to CDC42 inhibition, we delivered the noncompetitive allosteric CDC42 inhibitor ML141 into the airways and monitored AM behaviors (41). Oropharyngeal CDC42 inhibition did not alter AM viability, increase proinflammatory cytokines, or induce recruitment of neutrophils (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–E). When AM displacement was monitored by IVM in CDC42-inhibited animals, net displacement of AMs was significantly decreased compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 4 A and B). When the types of AM behaviors observed per FOV were characterized, AM squeeze was most significantly decreased from 33% in control animals to 18% in CDC42-inhibited animals (Fig. 4C). When interalveolar migration was assessed, CDC42 inhibition blocked the ability of AMs to move back and forth between alveoli (Fig. 4D).

Our previous study showed ITGAL blockade resulted in significant decrease in AM net displacement (4). We therefore compared the effects of CDC42 inhibition to blockade of integrin β1 or 2 (αCD29 or αITGAL respectively) (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). We were able to replicate previous results, but in this instance, we also assessed AM behavior phenotypes under ITGAL blockade. Blocking ITGAL resulted in AM patrolling decreasing significantly from 32 to 14%, but did not stop AMs from traversing between alveoli with no significant changes to AM squeeze phenotype or interalveolar migration (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 G and H and S5B). Blockade of CD29 (integrin β1) did not cause a significant decrease in AM movement or alter behavior (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 F and G and S5B). These data complement previous studies where integrin β1was also not shown to play a role in macrophage deformation (42).

We assessed the turbidity of BALF in CDC42-inhibited mice and found increased turbidity within the airways of CDC42-inhibited animals treated for 2-d (Fig. 4E). We performed ELISA and found that the increase in turbidity correlated to increased Surfactant D proteins in CDC42-inhibited animals (Fig. 4 F and G). In addition, an increased trend of C3 complement proteins in the BALF of CDC42-inhibited animals was observed, similar to vape-exposed animals (Figs. 2M and 4H).

We next assessed AM retrieval from the lungs via BALF after CDC42 inhibition. Inhibiting CDC42 alone significantly decreased AM retrieval from the BALF compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4 I and J). In a complementary experiment, we blocked β1&2 integrins in e-cig-exposed animals, hypothesizing that if integrins were playing a role in retaining AMs after e-cig exposure, this blockade would improve AM retrieval in BALF. However, even in the presence of integrin blockade AM retrieval remained low (SI Appendix, Fig. S4I). These data indicate that inhibition of CDC42 is a major pathway affecting AM loss of movement as well as retrieval from BALF.

In addition to our qPCR and proteomics results indicating a decrease in CDC42, we assessed CDC42 expression using western blot analysis. The western blot revealed a decrease in CDC42 abundance in AMs exposed to berry vape for both 3-d and 10-d (Fig. 4 K and L). Additionally, a size shift in the CDC42 protein was observed (Fig. 4 K and L). Both the decrease in expression and shift size of CDC42 were unique to berry vape exposure, as treatment with the CDC42 inhibitor did not alter protein abundance or size in AMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 J and K). These data indicate that likely a posttranslational modification of CDC42 is occurring after e-cig vape exposure.

We next performed western blot to assess ubiquitin and phosphorylation of the CDC42 protein. While there were no significant alterations in ubiquitination (SI Appendix, Fig. S4L), we did see a noticeable change in phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4M). Using the Phos-tag gel, we found that CDC42 was phosphorylated after 10-d of berry e-cig exposure based on the size shift of the protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S4M). Together, these data indicate the inhibition of CDC42 by berry-flavored e-cig results in the protein being phosphorylated.

Increased phosphorylation of CDC42 regulates the GTPase’s interaction of Rho GDP-dissociation inhibitor (RhoGDI), preventing plasma membrane localization of CDC42, which is required for interstitial immune cell migration (43–45). Active CDC42 localizes to the leading edge of the migrating cell, while inactive CDC42 is cytosolically localized (45). To confirm that the localization of CDC42 to the plasma membrane was impaired and validate our findings that CDC42 is phosphorylated after exposure to berry e-cigs we performed immunofluorescence staining of CDC42 in isolated AMs. We found that there was a stark difference in the localization of CDC42 in AMs from air versus e-cig-exposed animals, with CDC42 being dispersed throughout the cell in berry e-cig–exposed AMs but being localized to one node near plasma membrane in air-exposed AMs (Fig. 4M). If CDC42 is not activated, then it cannot turn on the N-WASP pathway that is required for nuclear squeezing of the cell (19). These data support that the loss of function of CDC42 in vape-exposed AMs results in the loss of the mechanism to squeeze through the pores of Kohn.

AMs from Berry Vape–Exposed Animals Are Unable to Migrate toward P. aeruginosa In Vivo.

We previously uncovered that AMs use complement component 5a receptor 1 (C5AR1) and formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) to recognize bacterial infections (4). C5AR1 is important for complement-mediated pathogen recognition, while FPR1 detects formylated peptides on components of both bacterial and dead cells (46, 47). Both Fpr1 and C5ar1 had decreased transcript levels after vape exposure (Fig. 5 A and B). We assessed the biological relevance of the decrease in FPR1 expression by measuring the in vitro migration of AMs from e-cig-exposed mice toward the ligand N-formyl methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLP). AMs from vape-exposed mice had reduced movement toward the fMLP compared to air controls (Fig. 5C). However, since AMs need to squeeze through the pores of the Transwell (8 μm) toward the fMLP, the reduced migration could be due to the impaired actin polymerization rather than the reduced FPR1 expression. We assessed the ability of AMs to phagocytose bacteria after vape exposure or CDC42 inhibition in vitro in the absence of structural barriers. AMs from animals exposed to air, berry vape, or CDC42 inhibitor were isolated and incubated with P. aeruginosa, and after 30 min, the number of intracellular bacteria was quantified. There were no significant differences between the number of bacteria phagocytosed by AMs in between the groups (Fig. 5D). Together, these data indicate that while berry e-cig exposure may alter movement to bacteria, it does not alter the phagocytic capacity of AMs in the absence of physical barriers.

We next used IVM to distinguish between defects in the squeeze function and bacterial sensing. Mice were exposed to air or berry-flavored e-cig vapor for 9-d, infected with green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing P. aeruginosa, and imaged 3-h postinfection (Fig. 5E). In previous studies, we found AMs move from a stochastic displacement throughout the airways to a slower directed movement, representing the chemotaxis to the bacteria (4). Once AMs have phagocytosed the bacteria, they become activated, stop moving, and perform intracellular killing (4). Surprisingly, we observed increased net AM displacement in the infected berry-flavored e-cig-exposed mice compared to infected air–exposed mice (Fig. 5 F and G and Movie S8). AMs of infected berry e-cig–exposed mice displayed decreased squeezing behavior and increased their patrolling activity (Fig. 5H, Movie S8, and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). This suggests the AMs of infected berry e-cig–exposed mice are not able to undergo directed chemotaxis and move to the adjacent alveolus, resulting in patrolling the alveolus blindly upon exposure to P. aeruginosa. Intriguingly, both air and berry e-cig–exposed AMs displayed increased expression of ITGAL after infection (Fig. 5I), supporting earlier results that the patrolling behavior is ITGAL mediated (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G).

The AM behavioral phenotypes observed in infected berry-flavored e-cig–exposed mice indicate that they are unable to travel between alveoli but still can patrol the alveolus they are in (Fig. 5H and Movie S8). Therefore, we hypothesized that AMs could capture bacteria in the same alveolar space but would not be able to move to another space to capture bacteria. Consistent with this, infected berry e-cig–exposed AMs showed significantly less overlap with GFP-expressing P. aeruginosa and more bacteria that were not associated with AMs, even though they had a similar number of AMs per FOV (Fig. 5 J–L). Importantly, in animals where CDC42 inhibitor was administered, we observed a similar level of reduction in overlap with GFP-expressing P. aeruginosa by IVM as well as increased AM displacement resulting from increase in AM patrolling behavior (Fig. 5 F, G, and J–L). Together these data indicate that blockage of interalveolar migration of AMs through inhibition of CDC42-mediated AM squeeze behavior is a mechanism by which berry vapes reduce ability of animals to clear bacterial infections.

Berry-Flavored E-cig–Exposed Mice Have Difficulty Clearing P. aeruginosa Requiring Increased Neutrophil Recruitment.

Thus far, we have shown that exposure to berry-flavored e-cig vape uniquely alters AM actin organization and behavior compared to flavorless e-cig exposure. We therefore sought to evaluate the biological consequences of exposure to flavorless and berry-flavored vapes after infection with P. aeruginosa (Fig. 6A). In low doses, P. aeruginosa is not typically lethal, and both healthy humans and mice clear the infection with minimal adverse effects. However, in immunocompromised individuals, it can cause severe illness, respiratory distress, and death (22, 48). We conducted bacterial colony-forming assays on the BALF 24-h post P. aeruginosa infection and found that berry e-cig–exposed mice had significantly higher bacterial counts than air-exposed mice (Fig. 6B).

When bacteria reach the alveoli of a healthy individual, AMs cloak the pathogen by rapidly phagocytosing them, avoiding the release of cytokines and chemokines that recruit other immune cells, such as neutrophils, which can cause tissue damage (4). We therefore assessed BALF cytokines, chemokines, and neutrophil numbers in e-cig- and air-exposed animals after P. aeruginosa infection. Berry e-cig–exposed animals had higher C-X-C motif ligand 1 (CXCL1) and TNF-α levels 24-h postinfection, while IL-6 levels trended down compared to both flavorless e-cig- and air-exposed animals (Fig. 6 C–E). Consistent with the increase in CXCL1, we also observed increased neutrophil recruitment 24-h postinfection specifically in berry e-cig–exposed mice (Fig. 6F). We assessed lung pathology at 7-d postinfection (16-d of vape exposure) and uncovered the berry vape–exposed animals have more immune cell infiltrates than air or flavorless (Fig. 6 G and H). These data support the inability of AMs to neutralize the infection, resulting in increased inflammation.

Finally, we assessed the outcomes of the infected mice. Infected berry e-cig vape-exposed mice had significantly reduced body weights compared to infected air-exposed controls and took a longer time to recover their baseline weights after infection (Fig. 6I). Importantly, berry vape–exposed mice also exhibited modest but significantly decreased survival, with 3 to 4 mice per replicate dying after infection, while the survival of infected flavorless vape-exposed mice was not significantly different from that of infected air controls (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these findings indicate that the components of the berry-flavored e-cig vapor induce AM dysfunction that perturbs their immune responses, decreasing their ability to clear P. aeruginosa infection.

Discussion

The motility of immune cells is inherently complex, particularly during inflammation, when inflammatory stimuli pull cells from circulation into tissues. The initial stages of extravasation from the circulation require integrins (50). However, once in the tissue, these cells can navigate through the matrix using integrin-independent mechanisms (51). As tissue resident cells, AMs do not undergo extravasation; instead, they exhibit unique behaviors tailored toward their environment, patrolling each alveolar spaces and squeezing through the pores of Kohn to enter adjacent alveoli. This squeezing behavior is crucial to effectively survey the lungs, given there are fewer AMs than alveolar spaces (4). This study indicates that the mechanisms of AM motility surpass the traditional extravasation-versus-extracellular-matrix-movement paradigms of other innate immune cells, such as neutrophils (50).

We describe three modes of AM movement at steady state: 1. patrolling, involving movement around an alveolus, 2. squeezing, enabling movement between adjacent alveoli, and 3. probing, which does not entail active motility, but rather extending protrusions to sample surrounding areas. Based on our work, while ITGAL may be pivotal for cells to move along the alveolar epithelium during patrolling, we propose that coordinated actin polymerization is necessary for AMs to squeeze between alveoli. Given the alveolar space is not as dense with extracellular matrix as the skin in combination with our data that anti-CD29 did not affect AM movement, we conclude β1 integrins are not playing a role in AM movement (42). Our study suggests that exposure to berry-flavored e-cig vape specifically affects actin polymerization by decreasing CDC42 expression.

The accumulation of lipid-laden AMs has been observed after several weeks to months of exposure to e-cig vape in both human and mouse studies (31, 52). Our study demonstrates this AM lipid accumulation as early as 3-d post e-cig vape exposure. Increased AM lipid content after e-cig exposure is likely due to shifts in metabolic function triggered by the excessive surfactant uptake. In the context of our research, the decreased turbidity of the BALF after 3-d of e-cig exposure suggests that initially, AMs likely engulf excess content in from airways. However, as they become less able to navigate between alveoli with continued exposure, surfactant clearance is reduced, causing increased BALF turbidity by 10-d. Lipid-laden macrophages are generally associated with inflammation (53); however, we observed no changes in proinflammatory cytokine release, suggesting that this alteration in AM metabolism is not driving inflammatory lung pathology in the absence of infection.

Given the long history of P. aeruginosa infection in smokers (54) and the importance of AM movement in P. aeruginosa clearance (4), we assessed the ability of air- and e-cig-exposed mice to clear it. The modest but statistically significant decrease in survival between berry vape–exposed mice versus flavorless vape–exposed and air controls is consistent with observations in human populations, as not all e-cig users experience detrimental outcomes (55). Individuals exposed to e-cig vapors could take longer to clear infections, potentially requiring antibiotics (56). As P. aeruginosa carries several antibiotic resistance genes, this raises concerns about antibiotic-resistant infections (23).

By systematically exposing animals to flavored and flavorless vapes, we were able to tease apart common pathways from those unique to the presence of flavor. Lipid accumulation and metabolic changes in AMs were altered in mice exposed to flavorless and berry-flavored vapes, indicating that the defects in AM movement seen only in berry flavor–exposed mice are unrelated to changes in lipid metabolism. Overall, our findings underscore the pronounced effect the addition of berry flavor to e-cig products can have on cellular function and host immunity.

In summary, this study unveils CDC42-mediated actin polymerization as a mechanism through which AMs survey the lung, beyond ITGAL-dependent movement. CDC42 is important for the nuclear squeeze and the movement of AMs through the pore of Kohn into adjacent alveoli. We show how the consumption of berry-flavored e-cig vapes can alter CDC42, resulting in protein phosphorylation, blocking the ability of the protein to be membrane bound. Sequestering CDC42 to the cytosol interferes with the migration of AMs and squeezing of the nucleus. The inability of AMs to move into adjacent alveoli results in the inability of the cells to effectively clear bacteria, resulting in skewed inflammatory response with increased TNF-α, CXCL1, and decreased IL-6. Together this skewed immune response and increased bacterial burden led to decreased survival of animals that were infected by P. aeruginosa during exposure to berry. Through this work we found AMs use actin polymerization pathways to move between alveoli, and that this movement modality is required for efficient clearance of bacterial invaders.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

6- to 8-wk-old C57BL/6J (WT) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (add address) and bred in-house. They were fed a standard autoclaved rodent diet and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the McGill University Animal Care Committee and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. A combination of male and female mice was initially used for flow cytometry and intravital to assess sex-specific differences. As both sexes showed similar trends we used male mice in qPCR, infection, and proteomics experiments.

E-cig Products.

Two commercially available e-liquids were purchased from Vapours Canada (vapourscanada.com). The “Berry” liquid was composed of 70:30 PG:VG, 20 mg/mL nicotine, and “Razzy3Some” (berry) flavor. This mixed berry flavor was chosen based on the popularity of fruit-flavored e-cigs among younger e-cig users (13, 15). The “Flavorless” liquid was composed of 70:30 PG:VG with 20 mg/mL nicotine.

Animal Exposures.

Parameters for e-cig exposure were determined to mimic human usage, particularly in the adolescent population (57–59). Mice were placed in SCIREQ’s inExpose compact full body inhalation exposure system for 1 h daily for 3 or 10 consecutive d and exposed to 78 mL puffs of e-cig vapor at a rate of 2 puffs/min. Control mice were placed in a separate full body chamber and exposed to room air for equivalent durations.

In Vivo AM Labeling.

AMs were labeled using the PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit for Phagocytic Cell Labeling (Sigma). A 100 μM stock of PKH26 was prepared and diluted to 0.5 μM in Diluent B. Mice were oropharyngeal administered 75 µL of diluted PKH26 directly into the lungs at least 5 d prior to imaging, using a published procedure with slight modifications (60). Briefly, mice were anesthetized under 3% isoflurane (with oxygen as a carrier), for 2 to 3 min then immediately suspended by their incisors on a 60° plane. The tongue was gently extruded using cotton tip applicators, the required volume was pipetted into the back of the throat, and the nares were gently closed with a gloved finger until the mouse took 10 deep breaths. It was then returned to its cage and monitored until ambulatory.

ML141 Administration.

CDC42 inhibitor ML141 (Sigma) was diluted in 1× PBS to a concentration of 10 ng/µL. Mice were dosed twice daily for two consecutive days by oropharyngeal administration and assays were performed on day 3. Each mouse received a total of 1.6 µg of the inhibitor. Approximately 12 h after the last dose, IVM was conducted, or the lungs were harvested to isolate AMs for western blot and the gentamicin protection assay.

IVM.

A Nikon CSU-X1 multichannel spinning-disk confocal upright microscope was used to image mouse lungs using a setup adapted from previously described work (4). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 10 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride and 200 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride and their body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad (World Precision Instruments). The right jugular vein was cannulated to administer additional anesthetic as needed. The trachea of the mouse was exposed, and a small catheter was threaded into it then attached to a small rodent ventilator (Harvard Apparatus). The mouse was placed in the right lateral decubitus position. A small surgical incision was made and the intercostal muscles between ribs 4 and 5 were gently teased apart to form a ∼1.5 cm opening. An intercostal lung window was then carefully placed between ribs 4 and 5. The lung was stabilized with a suction of ∼20 mmHg. No-delay time-lapse images were acquired using a 20× water objective with a numerical aperture of 1. Images show maximum projection intensities of z-stacks. Epithelial cell autofluorescence in the fluorescein isothiocyanate channel was used to define alveolar boundaries (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). Videos were analyzed using Imaris (Oxford Instruments) and open-access ICY software’s manual tracking plugin to produce polar plots (61). Videos were blinded and analyzed for behaviors over the 1-h video using Nikon NIS viewer and Imaris.

Flow Cytometry.

Flow cytometry panels were developed using fluorescence minus one controls and used in previous published works (62). Samples were stained with 50 µL of 1:1,000 LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen) in 1× PBS for 15 min on ice, then washed twice with 200 µL FACS buffer. The samples were resuspended in 50 µL (lung) or 25 µL (BALF) of master mix [all anti-mouse, BioLegend unless otherwise stated; BV421-Ly6G (cat. 127628), BV605-CD11b (cat. 101257), BV711-CD45 (cat. 103147), AF488-MHCII (cat. 107616), PerCP/Cy5.5-CD11a (cat. 101123), APC/Cy7-CD11c (cat. 117324), PE/Cy7-MERTK (cat. 151522), PE-SiglecF (BD Biosciences; cat. 562068), AF647-CD117 (cat. 105818), PE/Dazzle 594-CD49b (cat. 108924), BV785-Ly6C (cat. 128041), and BV650-CD206 (cat. 141723)] and incubated on ice for 30 min, then washed twice with 200 µL FACS buffer. Samples were then resuspended in 50 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After fixation, cells were washed once with 200 µL FACS buffer and stored in the dark at 4 °C for up to a week. The d the samples were run they were resuspended in 180 µL FACS buffer and 20 µL 123count eBeads Counting Beads (Invitrogen). This allows us to standardize the cell numbers analyzed during flow cytometry as follows: absolute count (cells/µL) = ((cell count x eBead volume)/(eBead count x cell volume)) x eBead concentration. For SI Appendix, Fig. S1E samples were not fixed, instead after staining with a modified master mix (same as above except replace BV711-CD45 with BV711-CD366 (cat. 119727), replace PE/Dazzle 594-CD49b with PE/Dazzle 594-MHCII (cat. 107648), replace BV650-CD206 with BV650-CD45 (cat. 103151), replace AF647-CD117 with APC-AXL (Invitrogen, cat. 17-1084-82), and remove AF488-MHCII), samples were resuspended in 180 µL of a 2 drop/mL CellEvent Caspase-3/7 Green (Invitrogen) in FACS buffer and 20 µL of 123count eBeads were added. Samples were run on a BD LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer and analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (Tree Star; see SI Appendix, Fig. S4N for the gating schemes used).

AM Isolation.

The total cell count for the single cell suspensions of lung tissue in 5 mL of FACS buffer was obtained using a hemocytometer, and then, the samples were centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 900 µL of isolation buffer [FACS buffer with 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)] and 10 µL of anti-Siglec F MicroBeads, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec) per 107 cells. The samples were incubated at 4 °C for 10 min, washed with 1 mL isolation buffer per 107 cells, then centrifuged. The pellet was resuspended in 300 µL of isolation buffer and the sample was transferred into a magnetized MACS MS column for magnetic isolation according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec). The column was washed three times with 500 µL of isolation buffer, then demagnetized to elute the isolated AMs in 1 mL of isolation buffer. Isolated cell counts were obtained using a hemocytometer. Purity of isolations was assessed using flow cytometry, isolated samples were 90 to 98% pure for AMs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G).

Integrin Inhibition Experiments.

30 µg of anti-mouse CD29 (Invitrogen) (63), CD11a (BioXCell) (4), or isotype control (BioXCell) blocking antibodies were given via oropharyngeal administration. For IVM, naïve mice were dosed with the blocking antibodies and imaging was conducted 3 h post administration. For flow cytometry of BALF to assess AM retrieval, mice were exposed to berry e-cig for 3-d, received the dose of blocking antibodies on the third day, and were sacrificed 1-h post administration.

Quantitative Shotgun Proteomics.

Isolated AMs were subjected to a quantitative shotgun proteomics analysis as described (64). At least 1 × 106 isolated AMs were used per sample, which required the lungs of 5 mice to be pooled. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each condition. The isolated AMs were lysed in 200 mM HEPES buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 10 mM EDTA, and complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), and then treated with 10 mM dithiothreitol to denature proteins, followed by incubation for 25 min with 15 mM iodoacetamide (VWR) at room temperature in the dark to alkylate proteins. Samples were then digested with Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subjected to C18 chromatography using Sep-Pak C18 96-well plates (Waters), then liquid chromatography and tandem MS. Proteomics analyses were performed by the Southern Alberta Mass Spectrometry Facility at the University of Calgary on an Easy-nLC 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer operated with Xcalibur software (version 4.0.21.10; all Thermo Fisher Scientific) in data-independent acquisition mode. Spectral matching was done using DIA-NN version 1.8.1 in a FASTA digest for library free search mode to a Mouse FASTA reference file obtained from uniprot.org on June 4th, 2021.

Bioinformatics Analyses.

Differentially expressed proteins with log2 fold change values >1.5 and <−1.5 were analyzed using Metascape (65). Physical interactions between proteins [from STRING (66) and BioGrid (67) databases] were organized into networks and their closely related components were identified with the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm (68). Pathway and process enrichment analyses were performed on each MCODE node, and the three most significantly enriched pathways were used to characterize the functions of each network. The specific functions of proteins in the enriched pathways were determined using uniprot.org (69).

Analysis of Immunofluorescence.

All immunofluorescence data were analyzed using FIJI software. Actin localization was measured based on a published method which automatically measures the expression of actin in the nucleus and cytoplasm of individual cells (70). For CDC42 localization, IMARIS colocalization was performed and validated with FIJI software (71). Mean fluorescence intensity measures were performed using FIJI plugin and analyzed per cell (72).

Bacterial Growth and Infection for In Vivo Experiments.

P. aeruginosa (strain 6077 (73) or 6206-GFP (74, 75)) was grown on agar plates overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were picked and incubated in brain heart infusion broth until they reached an OD600 of 0.5. After centrifuging 1 mL of the bacteria culture, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL PBS and diluted to a dose of 1x105 CFU/mouse of 6077 for infection studies (survival, BALF CFU) or 1x106 CFUs of 6206-GFP for intravital studies. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane, and 50 µL of the diluted bacteria was administered intranasally. The inoculate was plated on agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C to validate colony-forming units administered to mice. For survival animals are monitored twice daily and sacrificed if showing any clinical signs of distress or lose more than 20% of their body weight.

GCMS.

GCMS was performed on heated e-cig vape juice as previously described (76). A volume of 100 μL of the heated unflavored and berry-flavored e-cig condensates were dried by vacuum centrifugation with sample temperature maintained at −4 °C (LabConco). After drying, the dried condensates and nondried aliquots were extracted by adding 100 µL of n-hexane, followed by 2 rounds of vortex mixing and sonication. Each n-hexane extract was transferred to a glass vial and 1 μL was injected into a 5977C single quadrupole GC/MS (Agilent Technologies) in splitless mode with an inlet temperature of 250 °C. Compounds were separated on a DB-23 column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.15 μm film; Agilent Technologies) at a constant flow rate (0.8 mL/min) using helium as a carrier gas. For separation, the column temperature started at 50 °C for 2 min, then increased to 175 °C at 25 °C/min. The temperature was held at 175 °C for 1 min, increased to 250 °C at 5 °C/min, and held for 2 min. The column was baked out at 260 °C for 5 min between samples. Eluting compounds were ionized using positive electron impact mode with an electron energy of 70 eV. The ion source temperature was set to 230 °C. Data between 20 and 500 Da were collected in scan mode. Compounds of interest were identified using the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s MS spectral database (2020) and Mass spectral search program (v2.4 build Mar 25, 2020). The authentic standards for linalool, beta-damascone, gamma-decalactone, benzyl-acetate, and alpha-ionone were purchased from Millipore Sigma. Each standard was diluted in n-hexane and analyzed by GC-MS as above to validate their retention times and mass spectra.

Molecular Docking Virtual Screening.

To predict the interaction between CDC42 and chemical compounds detected in heated berry vapes, we conducted molecular docking virtual screening. We utilized the Mus musculus CDC42 protein homolog in complex with diaphanous homolog 1 (DIAPH1) (PDB code 3EG5, resolution of 2.70 Å). Subsequently, we performed a protein sequence comparison of M. musculus CDC42 (PDB code 3EG5) with the Homo sapiens CDC42 in complex with the CRIB domain of PAK6 (PDB code 2ODB, resolution of 2.40 Å) and identified a 99% conservation (177 out of 178 amino acids) on chain A of CDC42 protein between M. musculus and H. sapiens. M. musculus CDC42 Chain A was employed for docking analysis where a previously identified allosteric binding pocket was located (37). The structure was refined using AutoDock 1.5.7 (40), with polar hydrogen atoms assigned and charges added. The grid box of 26 × 26 × 26 Å was centered on the reported allosteric site of CDC42 Chain A (37). Chemical compound structures were obtained from PubChem, and the structure for ML141 (41) was generated using ChemDraw and Chem3D. All compounds were prepared using AutoDock 1.5.7. Finally, AutoDock Vina 1.2.0 (40) was employed for molecular docking, with an exhaustiveness of 64 and retention of 9 poses. The pose with the lowest binding energy was chosen for data representation.

Lung Histology.

Mice were sacrificed at day 7 postinfection and lungs were inflated with 4% paraformaldehyde (77). Filled lungs were excised and fixed for 24 h then embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Lungs were sliced and stained by H&E.

Histology Scores.

Five fields of view from 12 mice per group were assessed. Histology score of 5 represents increased immune infiltration. Histology scores were performed on blinded samples by lab members not involved in the project.

Statistical Analyses.

GraphPad Prism (version and source) was used to conduct statistical analysis. Unpaired Student’s t test was used for comparison of two groups, and the Brown–Forsythe one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s posttest were used for multiple comparisons. Two-way ANOVA with Uncorrected Fisher’s with individual variances computed for each comparison was performed for all AM behavior analysis (SI Appendix, Figs. S1D and S5). For nonparametric infection experiment results, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA with Dunn’s posttest was used for multiple comparisons. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve was analyzed using the log-rank test. Data represent the mean and error bars indicate the SD, except for Fig. 6 B–F where data represent the median and error bars indicate the interquartile range. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant.
