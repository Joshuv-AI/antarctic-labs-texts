Goal setting in youth sport: are we unintentionally contributing to dropout?
Authors: Scott G Goddard, Lindsey J Reece, Stewart Anthony Vella, Christian Swann
Published: 2025

Sports participation reportedly drops from 45% among children aged 5–14 to just 12% among 20–24 year-olds,1 despite well-documented benefits. This limits opportunities for children to develop confidence, resilience and social skills, while potentially increasing mental health risks.2 To counter this, extensive literature and national strategies emphasise the need to enhance the quality of sport experiences and reduce the likelihood of dropout. For example, the Australian Sport Commission’s (ASC) PlayWell strategy,3 codesigned by the sport sector, advocates for ‘high quality sports experiences for all’ as a pathway to lifelong engagement (p. 34). Similarly, Norway’s Provisions on Children’s Sports, published by the Norwegian Olympic and Paralympic Committee and Confederation of Sports (NIF), state that the guidelines ‘are designed to help children have a positive experience every time they participate in training, competition or other activities’4 (p. 4).

A prominent strategy in youth sport that influences the experience of children is goal setting, which often includes performance goals that frame instructions on a specific task outcome.5 Internationally, goal setting is advocated in youth sport by researchers,6 7 prominent media outlets8 9 and sport organisations.10 11 For example, the Australian state New South Wales (NSW) Office of Sport recommends that ‘people of all ages and experience levels should set goals to remain motivated, focused, and to monitor progress’12 (p. 2). Goals can significantly improve performance outcomes in sport but may have mixed effects on key psychological outcomes and quality of experience13—including intrinsic motivation, confidence and enjoyment. Despite widespread use, traditional goal-setting practice is being questioned in fields such as physical activity promotion, where concerns have been raised that prevailing approaches to goal setting may be leading to inadvertent negative consequences, such as exacerbated mental health symptoms,14 contributing to dropout. As such, it is important to consider whether similar adverse issues could be occurring within youth sport. Specifically, this commentary raises the important question of whether the traditional approach to goal setting negatively impacts the quality of experience for youth sport participants and unintentionally contributes to dropout.

The problems with focusing on specific performance goals

The traditional approach to goal setting in sport relies heavily on specific performance goals,15 such as to ‘win this match’ or ‘score 15 points in this game’. Such goals are recommended and used by organisations, coaches and parents—often with the implicit assumption that this is an effective approach—shaping how young participants engage with sport. The NSW Office of Sport, for instance, explicitly advises approximately 800 000 youth across NSW to use SMART goals (ie, specific, measurable, action-based, realistic, time-limited), providing an example: ‘a goal to improve your 3km run time from 15 minutes to 12 minutes is both specific and measurable’12 (p. 2). However, such specific performance goals have been described as ‘double-edged swords’, as they can enhance performance but also induce negative psychological outcomes, such as high levels of stress15 (p. 384). To illustrate, the runner encouraged to improve their 3 km run time from 15 min to 12 min may experience undue pressure, stress and/or anxiety about the goal, and if they fall short, it may lead to frustration, diminished confidence, perceptions of failure and, potentially, withdrawal from the sport. Fundamentally, young participants’ choice of goal could be sufficient to negatively impact their sport experience and, potentially, result in dropout.

There are theoretical explanations as to why specific performance goals may not always suit youth sport. According to goal-setting theory, specific performance goals are most effective when individuals have the resources and capacity to achieve them but ‘may be detrimental to performance when people have yet to acquire the requisite knowledge, skills, or abilities for a task’15 (p. 382). Since young sport participants are typically still acquiring basic motor and sport competencies, goal-setting theory suggests that specific performance goals are rarely appropriate and could even be harmful in these contexts—particularly in entry-level or early-stage contexts, where athletes are least likely to have the skills or experience needed to meet such goals. Further, the assumed motivation and benefit of such specific goals (ie, improving performance) is not necessarily valued by young athletes,16 nor is it prioritised within quality youth sport programming,17 meaning specific performance goals present these risks despite their benefits not aligning with the fundamental purpose of youth sport. Moreover, the widely accepted practice of specific performance goals may unintentionally undermine their quality of experience and could result in negative psychological outcomes that increase the risk of dropout.

Given how broadly specific performance goals are advocated in youth sport, these problems could be occurring on a large scale, potentially affecting millions of participants worldwide. For example, in the USA—where it is estimated that over 60 million youth participate in organised sport—fun is consistently rated as the top reason for participation, while the most frequently cited reason for dropping out is that it is no longer fun.18 Similarly, Australian data show that 73.5% of 15–17 year-olds cite fun and enjoyment as primary motives for participating in sport, compared with just 34.3% who cite competition or performance16—suggesting that alternative approaches to goal setting that prioritise the quality of experience are likely to be more appropriate for the majority of youth sport participants.

How can appropriate goal setting prioritise quality of experience?

Despite its proliferation as such, goal setting is not a ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy. Evidence from sport and physical activity has demonstrated that certain types of goals can optimise psychological outcomes (while achieving equivalent levels of performance compared with specific performance goals). For example, a meta-analysis13 found that there was no significant difference in sport performance between specific and non-specific performance goals. Unlike specific performance goals, which impose fixed performance outcomes, non-specific goals (such as open goals to ‘see how well you can play today’ or ‘see how much fun you can have in this session’) promote a more flexible and exploratory approach to participation, encouraging individuals to discover what they are capable of without a predetermined aim or outcome.19 Indeed, athletes associate open goals with positive experiences, such as optimal challenge, performance satisfaction and increased enjoyment, all while reducing pressure and stress19—factors that support autonomy, intrinsic motivation and long-term youth sport participation.2 Hence, it is possible to prioritise youth sport participants’ quality of experience by considering additional options, including open goals. These findings highlight the need to rethink how goal setting is applied in youth sport. Shifting the goalposts

To align with national strategies (eg, ASC’s PlayWell strategy and NIF’s Children’s Rights in Sports) and expert guidance17 on prioritising young people’s quality of experience in order to foster lifelong participation, it is time for a shift in how goals are set in youth sport. The traditional approach of specific performance goals should be reconsidered, as emerging evidence suggests that alternative goal-setting approaches are required to better support the quality of experience for young sport participants. Specific performance goals may be appropriate when tailored to a participant’s stage of development, motivations and readiness, but they should not be the default approach. By reducing the reliance on specific performance goals and incorporating other goal types (eg, open goals) and emphasising aspects outside of performance (eg, fun, helping team-mates and working hard), youth sport can better facilitate more positive and rewarding experiences that support long-term participation and reduce the risk of dropout.
