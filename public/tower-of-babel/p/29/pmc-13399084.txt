No Cost of Resistance in Wheat Gene Stack Lines Containing 10 Stem Rust Resistance Transgenes
Authors: Ming Luo, Aihua Wang, Soma Chakraborty, Shyh Yin Low, Saeid Babaei, Jian Chen, Jianping Zhang, Dhara Bhatt, Brenton Brooks, Oadi Matny, Narayana Upadhyaya, Rohit Mago, Melania Figueroa, Evans Lagudah, Peter Dodds, Brian Steffenson, Michael Ayliffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenetic resistance is the most economical and sustainable approach for crop protection, however, it is regularly overcome by pathogen virulence evolution. Polygenic resistance has greater durability, but unlinked genes are laborious to maintain in breeding programs. Introducing cloned resistance genes into the genome as gene stacks enables polygenic resistance with single locus inheritance. Fielder and Robin wheat plants were generated carrying two loci that each encode five wheat stem rust resistance transgenes, that is, Sr13c/Sr21/Sr22/Sr26/Sr33 and Sr22/Sr35/Sr45/Sr50/Sr55. Multiple transgenic events were combined and tested in the field. These lines, with unprecedented levels of transgenic resistance (i.e., 10 transgenes encoded on 90 kb of sequence), showed stable gene stack inheritance and transgene expression after eight generations and were highly resistant in the field. Importantly, in the absence of disease pressure no reproducible differences in the agronomic performance of transgenic lines that contained either one or both gene stacks was seen compared with control lines over two field trial seasons. These data confirm resistance gene stack efficacy and viability as a novel durable resistance strategy in agricultural crop production. Furthermore, this isogenic material that differs only by polygenic resistance provides significant insight into the broader question of the cost of disease resistance in host plants.

Introduction

Crop pests and pathogens cause global annual losses of 10%–30% (Savary et al. 2019) despite the breeding of resistant crops coupled with chemical and agronomic management practices. Genetic resistance is the most economical and sustainable approach for combating plant diseases; however, it is frequently overcome by pathogen evolution. Many plant disease resistance genes (R) act as immune receptors that recognise specific pathogen molecules (effectors) upon challenge, whereupon they activate a plant defence response. This is the case for nucleotide binding site, leucine rich repeat NLR proteins (NLR), which are a large class of plant immune receptors that each usually recognise either directly or indirectly a single, specific effector molecule (Contreras et al. 2023). Under disease resistance selection pressure, pathogens lose, alter or disguise recognised effector molecules to avoid plant recognition. When plant NLR genes are deployed singularly, they are particularly vulnerable to being overcome as only a single mutation in a pathogen effector gene is required for recognition avoidance. Combining NLRs to provide polygenic plant resistance is considered a more durable strategy, as multiple simultaneous mutations in the pathogen are needed for virulence (Schafer and Roelfs 1985). It is also the most responsible approach to protect these valuable genetic resources from individual breakdown caused by single gene deployment.

Polygenic resistance can be achieved via conventional breeding, however, it is laborious as plant R genes are usually dispersed throughout the genome and maintaining unlinked gene combinations in subsequent generations is difficult. To simplify polygenic resistance deployment, we have been developing plant resistance transgene stacks, whereby multiple cloned plant R genes are assembled on a single, large Agrobacterium T‐DNA sequence and inserted into the genome as a single locus. Using this approach in wheat we produced a resistance transgene stack that confers polygenic resistance against the wheat stem rust pathogen Puccinia graminis f. sp. tritici, but with single locus inheritance (Luo et al. 2021). This gene stack, called ‘Big5’, encodes 4 NLR stem rust R genes (i.e., Sr45, Sr50, Sr35 and Sr22 that each provide race specific, all‐stage plant disease resistance (ASR)) (Steuernagel et al. 2016; Mago et al. 2015; Saintenac et al. 2013). In addition, an adult plant resistance (APR) gene, Lr67/Yr46/Sr55/Pm46/Ltn3 (hereafter referred to as Sr55) which encodes a hexose transporter protein variant (Moore et al. 2015) is present in the Big5 stack. APR genes like Sr55 provide partial resistance in mature plant tissues only. However, Sr55 resistance is broadly effective against wheat leaf, stripe and stem rust and powdery mildew diseases and acts synergistically with some ASR resistance genes (McCallum and Hiebert 2022). Sr55 resistance is also associated with leaf tip necrosis (Ltn) in some environments. The Big5 gene stack conferred highly effective stem rust resistance in wheat cultivar Fielder in multiple field trials and no pleiotropic growth effects were observed under glasshouse growth conditions (Luo et al. 2021).

Plant R genes, including NLR genes, are under tight regulatory control to prevent defence response autoactivation (Borrelli et al. 2018). The precise regulation of R genes includes transcriptional and post‐transcriptional controls (Richard et al. 2018). The Arabidopsis SNC1 locus highlights the potential for NLR autoactivation whereby a natural SNC1 duplication, resulting in < 4‐fold increased expression, caused an autoimmune phenotype (Richard et al. 2018). It is well established that inappropriate expression of R transgenes can cause fitness costs when heterologous promoters are used for overexpression. Examples include the Arabidopsis Laz5 (Palma et al. 2010), RPS4 (Heidrich et al. 2013), RPS2 (Tao et al. 2000), BAL (Stokes et al. 2002) and ADR1 genes (Grant et al. 2003), potato Rx gene (Bendahmane et al. 2002), tomato Pto kinase gene (Tang et al. 1999), flax L6 gene (Howles et al. 2005), wheat Pm3b and Pm3f genes (Kalinina et al. 2011; Koller et al. 2018), wheat Lr10 gene (Romeis et al. 2007) and Arabidopsis RPS2 and RpM1 genes when overexpressed in rice (Li et al. 2019). There are, however, also numerous examples of R gene overexpression that do not cause an apparent fitness cost. For example, no obvious macroscopic growth detriments were reported in greenhouse or field experiments for transgenic wheat lines each carrying one of 995 NLRs from 18 different grass species that were each expressed using a maize ubiquitin promoter (Brabham et al. 2025).

Sometimes transgenic plants expressing R genes using endogenous promoters can also show altered resistance phenotypes and pleiotropic effects, that are often associated with increased gene expression due to either position effects or increased gene copy number. Examples include increased resistance and SAR activation by the tomato Prf gene (Oldroyd and Staskawicz 1998); increased resistance in barley transformed with the Rpg1 tandem kinase gene (Horvath et al. 2002); increased resistance in wheat transformed with Sr21 (Chen et al. 2018); extreme resistance in Arabidopsis transformed with RCY1 (Sekine et al. 2008), increased resistance in rice transformed with Xa3/Xa26 (Cao et al. 2007) and spontaneous HR‐like lesions with the Arabidopsis RPW8.1 and RPW8.2 genes (Xiao et al. 2003). SNC1 transgenes under endogenous regulatory elements can also result in autoactivate phenotypes (i.e., dwarfism, leaf curling and water soaking) with phenotype severity correlated with increased gene expression from transgene insertion sites (Li et al. 2007).

Increased expression of the wheat APR gene Lr34/Yr18/Sr57/Pm38/Ltn1 under its endogenous promoter also results in increased resistance in durum wheat seedlings (Rinaldo et al. 2017). Higher expression of this same gene under its own regulatory sequences in heterologous species like rice (Krattinger et al. 2016) and sorghum (Schnippenkoetter et al. 2017) causes pleiotropic effects, in addition to resistance. A similar observation was observed for Sr55 expression in these cereal species (Lagudah et al. unpublished).

Here we generate a second gene stack for wheat stem rust resistance called ‘Fearless5’ and develop wheat lines containing 10 rust resistance transgenes by combining both Big5 (Luo et al. 2021) and Fearless5 gene loci in the same plants. Multiple independent transgenic event combinations were used to assess if there are negative pleiotropic effects or a yield penalty associated with this unprecedently high level of transgene‐based stem rust resistance.

Results

Generation of Fearless5 Gene Stack Lines

A 43 kb Fearless5 stem rust resistance transgene (Sr) stack (GenBank accession PZ171369) was produced in binary vector pCSIRO using the reiterative Gateway cloning strategy described by Luo et al. (2021). This stack encodes five ASR stem rust R genes isolated from wheat and wild wheat relatives, with each gene encoding a NLR protein (Figure 1a). These genes are Sr33 (Periyannan et al. 2013), Sr26 (Zhang et al. 2021), Sr22 (Steuernagel et al. 2016), Sr21 (Chen et al. 2018) and Sr13c (Zhang et al. 2017; Gill et al. 2021). Each R transgene carried its own endogenous 5′ and 3′ regulatory elements and intron sequences (Figure 1a). The Sr22 gene in the Fearless5 construct is also present in the previously generated construct, Big5 (Figure 1a).

Wheat cultivar Fielder was transformed with the Fearless5 construct via Agrobacterium transformation and 11 T0 plants generated by selecting for phosphinothricin resistance. PCR analyses were used to test each T0 for the presence of the four junction sequences present between adjacent resistance genes in the Fearless5 construct, in addition to the 35Sbar/Sr33 junction and Sr13c/T‐DNA right border sequence junction (Figure 1a). All junction sequences were amplified from six of the 11 T0 plant DNAs (i.e., Fearless5‐3, 4, 5, 8, 9 and 11) indicating these plants may contain all five Sr transgenes. Each Sr gene in the Fearless5 stack was flanked by NotI restriction enzyme sites apart from Sr33 and Sr26 which were co‐located on a single 15 kb NotI fragment (Figure 1a). DNA blot analysis of the six T0 plants using NotI digestion and probes specific for either the Sr26, Sr22, Sr21 or Sr13c transgenes identified restriction fragments of the predicted sizes for full length gene insertions in all cases (Figure 1b); although an additional, smaller NotI fragment with homology to the Sr21 probe was detected in plant Fearless5‐5 DNA (Figure 1b). This smaller fragment is likely an additional truncated transgene insertion product. DNA blot analyses were also undertaken on segregating T1 progeny from T0 plant Fearless5‐11 and co‐inheritance of full‐length transgene sequences was observed for each gene carried in the Fearless5 construct (Figure S1).

Four T0 plants, Fearless5‐3, 4, 5 and 11, were taken forward and T1 families screened for resistance to Pgt isolate 98‐1,2,3,5,6,7 which is recognised by all five R genes in the stack (Pathan and Park 2007; Periyannan et al. 2013; Park 2016) (Figure 1c). In Fearless5‐4 and Fearless5‐11 T1 families, resistance segregated as a single dominant locus indicating no functional partial integrants unlinked to the primary insertion site were present (Table S1). In T1 families from Fearless5‐3 and Fearless5‐5, resistance segregated as two unlinked loci (Table S1) suggesting these lines contain either two complete loci, one complete and one partial locus or alternatively two partial loci that collectively contained all junction sequences.

The four T1 families were then screened by digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) amplification to determine copy number of the bar selectable marker gene and to identify homozygous plants. ddPCR detected only a single bar gene present in each T0 event that segregated 1:2:1 in each T1 family, respectively (Table S2). Progeny (8–12 seedlings) of a bar homozygous plant from each family were PCR screened for all six junction sequences, and all were positive for all junctions, consistent with homozygosity for a full‐length insert in each line. However, the presence or absence of an additional unlinked truncated locus in this material could not be excluded by this marker analysis. Additional backcrossing and intercrossing of the four Fearless5 T0 provided further evidence confirming the presence of a full‐length insertion in each transgenic event, including lines Fearless5‐3 and Fearless5‐5 (see below).

Transgene expression was assayed in leaf RNA from homozygous T2 families using Renseq analysis (Steuernagel et al. 2016) and expression compared with control lines carrying equivalent endogenous genes. A Fielder line containing the Big5 gene stack served as both a Fielder background control and Sr22 positive control. Full‐length transcripts of Sr22, Sr26 and Sr33 were detected in all Fearless5 lines and corresponding control lines (Figure S2), while sequence matches were only partial and limited in lines not carrying the R‐gene. Sr22, Sr26 and Sr33 transcripts were greater in Fearless5 lines compared with corresponding endogenous gene lines, suggesting sufficient transgene expression (Figure S3). In contrast, Sr13c and Sr21 RenSeq reads were numerous in control lines not carrying either gene (Figures S2 and S3), likely due to the presence of related homologues or susceptible alleles. To further quantify expression, SNPs were identified in each R gene that distinguished corresponding susceptibility alleles or closest homologues in the Chinese Spring v. 2.1 genome (Figure S4). R‐gene specific SNP sequences were only detected in Fearless5 and appropriate control RNAs for each transgene, with expression generally equal to or greater than the endogenous gene expression in control lines (Figure 2).

Triplicated T2 families from the four homozygous Fearless5 lines were screened in a disease nursery field trial in Rosemount, Minnesota in 2021. High levels of resistance to Pgt race QTHJC were observed in each Fearless5 family (0% disease severity and HR infection responses), while control lines were highly susceptible (60%–100% disease severity) (Figure 3a). Single gene control lines showed race Pgt‐QTHJC is avirulent to Sr13c, Sr22, Sr26 and Sr33 but virulent to Sr21, consistent with its known pathotype. Three Fearless5 lines were again trialled in Minnesota in 2022 using duplicated plots and similar results again observed (Figure S5).

Production of Fielder and Robin Lines Containing Both Big5 and Fearless5 Loci

Given that wheat stem rust is a major disease threat in eastern Africa, the Fearless5 transgene locus in Fielder lines 3, 4, 5 and 11 was each backcrossed into the elite African cultivar Robin, with homozygous BC7 lines produced. In BC6/BC7 generations of Robin Fearless5 material PCR analyses showed a single locus was inherited from each primary Fielder T0 plant that encoded all six junction sequences, with no unlinked partial loci detected (Table S3). Long‐range PCR amplification was used to further confirm the presence of full‐length Sr13c and Sr33 genes in homozygous Robin BC7 Fearless5 lines and Fielder Fearless5 lines, respectively. Each gene was amplified as two overlapping ca. 4 kb PCR fragments that collectively encompassed the gene in its entirety (Figure S6). These products were successfully amplified from all Fielder and Robin Fearless5 transgenic lines while no equivalent products were amplified from parental control lines (Figure S6). Nanopore sequencing of the four products amplified from Robin line Fearless5‐4 showed 100% sequence identity to the Fearless5 construct used for transformation. These data confirm the stable inheritance of the Fearless5 gene stack loci over multiple generations.

Homozygous BC7 Robin lines were also generated carrying the previously described gene stack insertions from Fielder Big5 lines 19, 52 and 60 (Luo et al. 2021). In addition, wheat cultivar Robin was directly transformed with the Big5 construct and a single homozygous transgenic line (Big5‐R) generated that contained all junction sequences that were co‐inherited as a single locus. Protoplasts were isolated from Big5‐R and transformed with a YFP gene and a plasmid encoding either AvrSr22 (Arndell et al. 2024), AvrSr35 (Salcedo et al. 2017) or AvrSr50 (Chen et al. 2017). Significantly reduced YFP expression occurred in protoplasts transformed with each effector confirming the function of these three resistance genes using this in vivo cell death assay (Figure 4a). Sr45 function in Big5‐R protoplasts could not be tested as the cognate AvrSr45 effector gene has not been cloned, nor could the Sr55 APR gene as it does not appear to function by effector recognition.

Labels on the X‐axis indicate the avirulence effector (Avr) that protoplasts were transformed with or empty vector control (EV). Data shows the average normalised YFP fluorescence of three biological repeats with standard deviations shown. Common letters above graph columns indicate no significant differences were observed (ANOVA with post hoc Tukey p < 0.01).

To determine if the transgene stacks remained functional after eight generations of crossing into Robin, protoplast cell death assays were undertaken on a homozygous Robin BC7 line containing the Big5 construct, inherited from Fielder line Big5‐19. Cell death was detected in the Robin Big5‐19 line when protoplasts were transformed with either AvrSr22, AvrSr35 or AvrSr50, confirming these three R transgenes remained functional (Figure 4b). Protoplasts from three homozygous Robin BC7/8 lines containing Fearless5 loci inherited from Fielder Fearless5‐3, 4 and 5, respectively, were also tested for Avr effector recognition. Cell death was similarly detected in protoplasts from these three Robin Fearless5 lines when transformed with either AvrSr13c (Spanner et al. 2026) or AvrSr22, although the Sr13c/AvrSr13c interaction was weaker, similar to that previously reported by Arndell et al. (2024) (Figure 4c–e). (Note AvrSr21 and AvrSr26 have not been cloned).

In contrast, no reduction in YFP fluorescence occurred in protoplasts from any Robin Fearless5 line when transformed with AvrSr33 (Spanner et al. 2026) (Figure 4c–e) or in protoplasts from wheat line CS1D5405 which contains the endogenous Sr33 gene (Figure 4f). Fielder lines Fearless5‐3, 4 and 5 were the transgene donors of these BC Robin lines and all three parental Fielder events have elevated Sr33 expression compared with the endogenous Sr33 gene present in CS1D5405 (Figure 2). In cereal protoplast cell death assays Avr genes and R genes are both typically overexpressed using a maize ubiquitin promoter (Saur et al. 2019; Upadhyaya et al. 2021; Ortiz et al. 2022; Arndell et al. 2024). Saur et al. (2019) report greater variability in barley protoplast assays when resistance genes were not overexpressed and suggest some Avr/R interactions may not be detected in protoplasts in the absence of R gene overexpression. Similarly, Arndell et al. (2024) showed higher levels of cell death in protoplasts assays when the maize ubiquitin promoter was used for resistance gene expression rather than using protoplasts containing the endogenous gene. Co‐transformation of protoplasts from a BC7 Robin F5‐4 null line with Sr33 expressed by the maize ubiquitin promoter and AvrSr33 resulted in cell death (Figure 4g), consistent with an over‐expression requirement explaining the absence of AvrSr33 induced cell death in protoplasts from the Fearless5 lines and the endogenous Sr33 control line. Formally, however, Sr33 transgene function was not demonstrated in this material, although the same transgene sequence previously used by Periyannan et al. (2013) to confirm the identity of the Sr33 gene was used in the Fearless5 gene stack. The Sr33 sequence is an 8 kb genomic fragment that was amplified by Periyannan et al. (2013) using PCR primers 5′‐TTCAAGATGTCAAATTTTAAAAGGGC‐3′ and 5′‐CTACTCATTAGGAACTCGAGCGG‐3′ (Periyannan et al. 2013—supplemental methods), with these same sequences present in the Fearless5 construct (GenBank PZ171369).

Three Fielder Big5 and four Fielder Fearless5 lines were inter‐crossed and lines homozygous for both transgene loci produced (Fielder Big5+F5 lines), with nine of the possible 12 combinations made in addition to 11 null sibs from Big5 + Fearless5 families (Table S4). Three Robin Big5 BC7 lines, Big5‐R and four Fearless5 Robin BC7 lines were inter‐crossed to produce 9 homozygous Robin Big5+F5 lines and 9 null sib lines, from the possible 16 Robin transgene combinations (Table S4). The Big5+F5 lines made by intercrossing are homozygous for 10 resistance transgenes located at two loci that collectively encode approximately 90 kb of transgene sequences. Fielder Big5+F5 and null sib lines were infected with Pgt race QTHC at Rosemount, Minnesota in 2023 and 2024 field seasons and data from each trial pooled. All Big5+F5 lines were highly resistant while null sibs and parental Fielder controls were susceptible (Figure 3b). Robin lines could not be trialled due to the unavailability of Pgt isolates virulent to Robin.

Field Trialling of Double Homozygous Fielder and Robin Lines in the Absence of Disease Pressure

Field trials were undertaken in 2023 and 2024 in the absence of disease pressure at a field site located near the township of Boorowa, NSW (Australia). Big5+F5, Big5 and Fearless5 Robin and Fielder lines were grown in block designs in 3 blocks. For some Fielder Fearless5 lines and all Robin Fearless5 and Big5 lines, multiple homozygous resistant and null sibs were trialled for each transgenic event (Table S4). Only single Big5+F5 Fielder and Robin resistant and null sibs were trialled for each cross (in replicated plots), with the exception of Fielder Big5‐60 + F5‐3, where two homozygous resistant sibs were trialled in 2024 (Table S4). In the absence of disease pressure, plots were assessed for % seedling emergence, yield per plot, yield per plant, one thousand kernel weight (TKW) and, prior to heading, normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI).

Data from the four field trials were analysed using linear mixed models (LMMs) fitted via asreml for R (Butler et al. 2017; Butler 2022), running in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025). In these models, the factor ‘genotype’ was nested within factor ‘genotype_status’, defined to distinguish between the Big5, Fearless5 and Big5+F5 transgenic line groups, their respective null sibs, and the parental controls. In the 2023 trial, only three cases showed a significant difference between the predicted best linear unbiased estimate (BLUE) mean of a transgenic group and its corresponding null sib control for any of the five parameters assessed. Robin Big5+F5 lines showed lower yield per plot values when compared with their null sib lines; however, this yield value was not significantly different to any other Robin lines in 2023 including the Robin parental control and null sib lines of both Robin Big5 and Fearless5 (Figure 5). The thousand grain weight values of Big5 lines were significantly less than their null sib controls in both Fielder and Robin lines. However, for Big5 Robin lines these values were not significantly different to any other Robin line including parental lines and other null sib control lines in the same year (Figure 5).

However, an obvious difference was seen for Big5 Fielder and null sib controls when compared with the remaining Fielder lines. The Fielder Big5 and Big5 null lines had decreased yield per plot, but increased yield per plant when compared with all the other Fielder lines, which likely resulted from reduced seedling emergence at planting. We suggest the differences between Fielder Big5 and its null lines, and the remaining Fielder germplasm (Figure 5) was likely due to poorer quality seed being available for the Fielder Big5 and null sib germplasm, resulting in reduced seedling establishment. This suggestion is supported by no significant difference being observed in either Fielder Big5 or Big5 null lines when compared with the Fielder parental line for any parameter measured in the 2024 trial.

In the 2024 trial, eight significant differences were observed between transgenic lines and their corresponding null sib controls across the two genetic backgrounds. Seedling emergence values for both Fielder Big5+F5 and Robin Big5 lines were significantly greater than their null sib lines, respectively, but did not differ to parental control lines. In Robin Big5 and Big5+F5 lines yield per plant values were significantly lower than in respective null sib control lines in both cases, but neither value differed significantly to the parental control line. Similarly, yield per plot values were lower in Robin Big5 and Big5+F5 lines compared with null sib control lines, although only the Big5 value also differed significantly to the parental control line for this parameter. In contrast, Fielder Big5+F5 lines had significantly higher values for yield per plot and one thousand grain weight compared with its null sib control line, although none of these parameters differed significantly to the parental control line.

These data provide no strong evidence of reproducible agronomic differences existing between transgenic lines and transgenic control lines. In only three cases did values differ significantly between transgenic lines, their null sib control, and the parental control in the same year. These being a reduced yield per plot for Robin Big5 lines in 2024 and both reduced yield per plot and one thousand grain weight for null sib control lines of Fielder Big5+F5 in 2024. These three parameters were not significantly different for the same genotypes in the 2023 trial.

The above analyses compared transgenic groups and control lines (Table S5). As different transgenic insertion events can show different levels of expression it was also of interest to compare individual transgenic events with their corresponding null sib control, for which Fishers Protected Least Significant Difference (LSD) tests were used at the 5% level of significance on the predicted means (BLUEs) for the genotype factor. In the Fielder background, 18 (2023 field trial) and 17 (2024 field trial) single and double transgenic events were compared with their null sib controls for % seedling emergence, yield per plant, yield per plot and thousand grain weight (Figures S7 and S8). In the Robin genetic background, 22 comparisons were made for these same four parameters in both the 2023 and 2024 trials (Figures S9 and S10). From this total of 316 pairwise comparisons, 43 pairs differed from the least significant difference at a probability value of 0.05. Nineteen of these 43 differences showed an increased agronomic performance in the transgenic line compared with the null sib control. The only reproducible significant difference was a higher thousand grain weight value in Fielder line Big5‐19+F5‐3 compared with its null sib control in both the 2023 and 2024 trials (Figures S7–S10), although this same difference was not detected in the Robin background in either trial.

In summary, within the limits of this study, these data do not identify a reproducible difference for the growth and yield parameters assessed in two field trial seasons and provide little support for an agronomic performance penalty being associated with single or double gene stack lines compared with control lines in two different wheat genetic backgrounds.

Discussion

No reproducible examples were observed in this study for an agronomic cost being incurred in wheat lines containing single gene stacks encoding five resistance transgenes or double lines containing both the Big5 and Fearless5 stacks that collectively encode 10 resistance transgenes, nine of which are unique genes. In Fielder, these field trials were undertaken using material from three independent Big5 transgenic events and four independent Fearless5 transgenic events along with nine of the possible 12 double gene‐stack combination lines that could be produced by inter‐crossing. Multiple independent insertion events have therefore been examined to determine if gene stack insertion sites influenced agronomic performance.

The transgene loci present in Fielder were also backcrossed into the elite African cultivar Robin, while a single Robin Big5 line was also produced by direct transformation. Another nine inter‐cross lines containing both Big5+F5 gene stacks were tested in the Robin background with no statistically reproducible differences identified that imply a cost for this transgenic resistance. Also noteworthy was that the Sr13c, Sr22, Sr35 and Sr50 transgenes each remained functional in the gene stack lines after eight generations of backcrossing to wheat cultivar Robin followed by homozygous line selection. Both Big5 and Fearless5 resistance gene stacks encode wheat genes containing their endogenous 5′, 3′, and intronic sequences which may reduce the overexpression potential of each gene and possibly help to prevent epigenetic silencing effects.

This highly isogenic germplasm also provides insight into the broader question of a cost of resistance genes in general, in the absence of disease pressure. In a meta‐analysis of 88 published studies, a mean resistance fitness cost of a 3.5% reduction in productivity compared to susceptible plants was calculated, with about half of all comparisons surveyed reporting a detectable effect (Bergelson and Purrington 1996). In other studies, using Arabidopsis isolines transgenic for the RPM1 (Tian et al. 2003) or RPS5 gene (Karasov et al. 2014), yield penalties of 5%–10% were observed. However, the magnitude of the effect of these latter two genes is presumably unusual and gene‐specific as there are hundreds of R genes in most plant genomes and such a cost for each gene would be an impossible genetic load.

A number of wheat genes have been suggested to have yield penalties but in most cases evidence that the penalty is due to the R gene and not associated linkage drag is unavailable. Many wheat resistance genes are sourced from wild relatives or unimproved germplasm and introgressed as large genomic fragments. The Sr26 gene present on an introgressed Thinopyrum segment was reportedly associated with a 13% yield penalty (Latter et al. 1998; Rai et al. 2019), and Sr21 was previously reported to have a 7% yield penalty (The et al. 1988) as was an Sr22 introgression from Triticum boeoticum (Olsen et al. 2010). These cloned genes are all present in the Big5+F5 lines we have tested, including two copies of Sr22. The apparent absence of yield penalties in these transgenic lines further supports that associated linkage drag is responsible for these previously reported negative pleiotropic effects and not the R genes themselves. It also highlights another advantage of stacking cloned resistance genes, that being the removal of associated linkage drag. Further breeding efforts to reduce the endogenous chromosome linkage blocks of Sr26 and Sr22 may have also overcome these negative linkage associations (Mago et al. 2005; Olsen et al. 2010).

In an interesting example, epigenetic regulation of the NLR encoding rice Pigm blast resistance locus has evolved to apparently minimise resistance costs (Deng et al. 2017). This locus consists of a gene family with one member, PigmR, being expressed throughout the plant and providing broad spectrum resistance, while a second gene PigmS is expressed only in pollen and panicles and its protein product competitively attenuates PigmR homodimerization to suppress resistance in these tissues. In the absence of PigmS expression, PigmR results in a rice yield penalty (Deng et al. 2017). In contrast, for the Arabidopsis RPS2 gene both the resistance allele and susceptible allele provided a fitness benefit compared with a deletion allele (MacQueen et al. 2016). Similarly, no consistent evidence of pleiotropic effects was observed in wheat lines carrying one, two and four pyramided Pm3 overexpression alleles (Koller et al. 2018; Camenzind et al. 2024) or Pm17 overexpression transgenes (Koller et al. 2024), although Pm3b and Pm3f single gene overexpression lines did show effects (Brunner et al. 2011; Koller et al. 2018). The cost of resistance therefore remains an enigmatic question. The material we have generated is arguably the most isogenic example of complex polygenic resistance. However, the transgene combinations used in this study do not appear to incur a yield cost when combined in wheat, within the limitations of the study design employed.

A question of interest is how durable could the resistance provided by five to nine Pgt ASR resistance genes be? Based upon mutation frequencies it is estimated that for six resistance genes to be overcome by simultaneous mutation in a Pgt population, a susceptible rust‐infected wheat crop would need to be grown over an area 4× the size of the annual USA wheat acreage to provide sufficient inoculum for a single spore to evolve with virulence to all six R genes, that is, one in 3.4 × 1023 spores (Schafer and Roelfs 1985). The counterfactual is that many of the cloned genes used in these two gene stacks have already been deployed, with virulence detected in some instances. This previous deployment potentially enables pathogen erosion of the resistance genes present in the stack in either a stepwise manner on single gene lines or via pathogen somatic hybridisation that can generate new virulence gene combinations. However, previous deployment does assist in negating concerns regarding toxicity, allergenicity and weediness potential of transgenes used in these gene stack lines.

Another consideration regarding resistance durability is the Pgt evolutionary potential in regions where its sexual cycle can occur due to the presence of species of Berberis and Mahonia which are the alternate plant hosts for completion of the Pgt sexual cycle. From an analysis of 68 Pgt isolates collected between 2013 and 2015 from Georgia, a country where sexual populations of Pgt are present, isolates of race TTRTF had the greatest virulence spectrum against the genes used in this study with virulence for Sr21, Sr35, Sr45 and Sr50 identified (Olivera et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2021; Gill et al. 2021). However, the Fearless5 stack contains four ASR genes (Sr13c, Sr22, Sr26 and Sr33) functional against this highly virulent isolate and when combined with the Big5 stack an additional APR gene, Sr55, is included. Big5+F5 lines also contain at least five resistance genes, that is, Sr13c, Sr22, Sr26, Sr35 and Sr50 (Sr45 not tested) functional against a recently emerged Pgt race, TKHBK, collected in Spain adjacent to Berberis plants, in addition to at least six genes, that is, Sr13c, Sr22, Sr26, Sr33, Sr35 and Sr50 (Sr45 not tested) functional to a TKTTF isolate also collected in Spain (Olivera et al. 2022).

In summary, combining resistance gene stacks that are effective against wheat stem rust appears to offer a stable and agronomically acceptable approach for improved disease resistance in wheat. This strategy is applicable to other crops and diseases and a similar approach has been pursued in potato to provide polygenic resistance to Phytophora infestans (Zhu et al. 2012; Haesaert et al. 2015; Ghislain et al. 2019; Webi et al. 2019). With the continued improvement of resistance gene cloning technologies and genomic platforms (Joust et al. 2023), more resistance genes will become available from many species to more broadly facilitate this approach and improve crop productivity and resistance durability whilst reducing chemical reliance in agriculture.

Material and Methods

Plant Growth Conditions

Plants were grown either in glasshouses with a 16 h light, 22°C/8 h dark, 18°C growth regime, or in growth cabinets using a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod and 22°C/16°C temperature cycle. Typically, plants were grown in a compost/soil mixture in 25 cm pots and regularly fertilised with osmocote slow‐release fertiliser (Scotts, Australia). For speed breeding, plants were grown in 15 cm pots in growth cabinets under a 22‐h light/2‐h dark photoperiod at a constant 22°C.

Nucleic Acid Extractions and Molecular Analyses

Plant DNA was extracted using a Nimbus liquid handling robot (Hamilton, Province, USA) after leaf tissue had been freeze dried and macerated. Tissue was heat treated for 1 h at 65°C in extraction buffer (0.1 M Tris pH 8.0, 0.05 M EDTA, 1.25% SDS), followed by the addition of 6 M NH3Ac and centrifugation. DNA was precipitated from the supernatant by the addition of an equal volume of isopropanol followed by centrifugation (14,000 × g for 15 min) and finally washed in 70% ethanol before resuspension in 10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA. For DNA blot analysis DNAs were restricted with NotI (or NotI plus AscI in the case of Sr33) and after electrophoresis transferred to Biodyne nylon membranes (Pal Corp.) and then hybridised with 32P‐labelled dCTP DNA probes. Digital droplet PCR was undertaken as described by Petrie et al. (2020). Long range PCR of Sr13c and Sr33 was undertaken on plant DNAs using a nested PCR strategy shown in Figure S6 and RepliQa HiFi ToughMix (Quantabio, USA). PCR amplification cycles of 98°C 7 s, 60°C 5 s, 68°C 2 min for 33 cycles were used for the first round of amplification followed by a second cycle using the same PCR conditions. PCR products from Robin F5‐4 BC7 were purified from agarose gels and nanopore sequenced (Plasmidsaurus, USA). RNA was extracted from leaf tissue using a Spectrum Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma‐Aldrich) and any remaining genomic DNA removed using an On‐column DNase I Digestion Kit (Sigma‐Aldrich). Primers used for long range PCR amplification of Sr13c and Sr33, ddPCR of the bar gene and to produce DNA probes for Sr13c, Sr21, Sr22, Sr26 and Sr33 are shown in Table S7.

Wheat Transformation

Wheat transformation was undertaken using the Agrobacterium scutellum transformation procedure described by Ishida et al. (2015) using phosphinothricin resistance for selection (Richardson et al. 2014). Briefly embryos were removed from seeds harvested 12–14 days post anthesis and sterilised in 0.8% sodium hypochlorite solution. Embryo explants were then co‐cultivated for 2 days in the dark on WLS‐AS medium (Ishida et al. 2015) with Agrobacterium strain GV3101 PMP90 carrying either the Fearless5 or Big5 construct in binary vector pCSIRO. Upon removal of embryonic axes, explants were transferred to WLS medium containing phosphinothricin. Callus was then transferred to LSZ media containing phosphinothricin in the light for shoot regeneration and once root systems had developed plantlets were transferred to the glasshouse.

Generation of Homozygous Fielder Lines Containing Fearless5 Transgene Loci

T1 families from Fielder Fearless5 lines 3, 4, 7 and 11 were screened by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) to determine the copy number of the bar selectable marker gene. Approximately 12 seedlings from T1 plants homozygous for the bar gene were then screened by PCR using the primer sequences shown in Table S6 to confirm the co‐inheritance of all junction sequences in each family member. Null sib lines for each transgenic event were also collected from T1 families.

Generation of Homozygous Robin Lines Containing Big5 or Fearless5 Transgenic Events

Fielder Big5 lines 19, 52 and 60 (Luo et al. 2021) were crossed to Robin and backcross generations accelerated using speed breeding. Inheritance of Big5 loci was followed in each generation using primer pairs that amplify all gene junction sequences present in this construct as described in Table S7 of Luo et al. (2021). BC7 Robin lines were self‐fertilised, and progeny screened by ddPCR for homozygosity of the hpt selectable marker gene. Small families (approximately 12 seedlings) from progeny plants homozygous for hpt were then screened by PCR for co‐inheritance of all junction sequences in all family members.

A similar strategy was used to develop homozygous BC7/8 Robin lines containing Fearless5 transgene loci. Fielder Fearless5 lines 3, 4, 5 and 11 were backcrossed to Robin for multiple generations. Transgene loci were followed by marker assisted selection of all six junction sequences using the primer pairs shown in Table S6. Homozygous Fearless5 Robin lines were selected after self‐fertilisation of BC7/BC8 material. Progeny from this material were screened by ddPCR for homozygosity of the bar selectable marker gene. Presumed homozygotes were then self‐fertilized and small families of approximately 12 progeny PCR screened for all junction sequences to confirm homozygosity, with junction sequence primers shown in Table S6. Null sib lines were also collected from progeny of self‐fertilised BC7/8 Robin lines.

Generation of Fielder and Robin Lines Containing Both Big5 and Fearless5 Loci

Fielder and BC7/BC8 Robin lines homozygous for either Big5 or Fearless5 loci were intercrossed. F1 plants were self‐fertilised and F2 progeny screened by ddPCR for homozygosity of the hpt selectable marker gene and the bar selectable marker gene. These double homozygous plants were self‐fertilised and approximately 12 progeny screened with primer pairs specific for the Sr50/Sr35 and Sr55/Sr50 junction sequences in the Big5 construct and Sr26/Sr22 junction sequence in the Fearless5 construct, to confirm homozygosity. In those small F2 families where double homozygous lines were not identified by selectable marker ddPCR, F2 plants were selected that were firstly homozygous for either the Big5 hpt or Fearless5 bar selectable marker gene and secondly were also heterozygous for the alternative selectable marker gene. These plants were self‐fertilised, and progeny identified that were homozygous for both hpt and bar by ddPCR. Progeny from these presumed double homozygous plants were then screened with the three junction sequences above to confirm homozygosity of the Big5 and Fearless5 loci. Null sib lines from each cross used to combine Big5 and Fearless5 were also collected.

Renseq Analysis

Renseq analysis was undertaken on RNA extracted from pooled homozygous T2 families of Fearless5 lines 3, 4, 5 and 11 and wheat lines containing a corresponding single, endogenous gene, that is, Kronos (Sr13a), Cranbrook (Sr21), Schomberg (Sr22), Avocet (Sr26) and CS1D5405 (Sr33). For each line, RNA was extracted from pooled seedlings at the 4th leaf stage and RenSeq capture and sequencing undertaken by Arbor Biosciences (Ann Abor, USA) using a Triticeae bait library and HiSeq 2500 paired end sequencing, as previously described (Luo et al. 2021). Sequence reads were aligned to transgene sequences using CLC Genomics Workbench (Qiagen) with a 100% similarity, 95% sequence length and default parameters. An average of 46 million reads per sample (range 37–55 million) were aligned to reference NLR sequences and matching reads quantified for each sample. Due to the presence of closely related allelic or homologue transcripts causing background cross homology (Figures S2 and S3), alignment was repeated for each gene using a single approximately 70 bp sequence that contained at least one diagnostic SNPs for the resistance allele used (Figure S4). In addition, previous RenSeq data from a transgenic Fielder line, Big5‐19, which carries Sr22, Sr35, Sr45, Sr50 and Sr55 transgenes was included in the analysis (Luo et al. 2021). This Big5‐19 data serves as a positive expression control for the Sr22 resistance transgene and a negative control for Sr13c, Sr21, Sr26 and Sr33 expression in the Fielder background.

Protoplast Assays

Protoplast assays were undertaken as previously described (Saur et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2021). In brief, Fielder and Robin seedlings were grown for 9 days under low light conditions and leaf peels then treated with 1.5% (wt/vol) cellulase Onozuka RS and 0.75% (wt/vol) macerozyme R10. Protoplasts were filtered through nylon mesh and harvested by centrifugation before PEG mediated co‐transformation of plasmid constructs encoding effector genes and a YFP reporter plasmid. Effector genes were placed under the regulatory control of a maize polyubiquitin (Ubi) promoter and the 3′ termination sequence from the Agrobacterium tumor morphology 1 gene while the YFP reporter gene was expressed using a Ubi promoter and dual 35S/NOS 3′ terminator sequence. After 24 h post transformation, YFP fluorescence was quantified using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG, Germany).

Three biological repeats were undertaken for each plant line assayed with three technical repeats in each assay for each effector construct. For YFP fluorescence measurements, each protoplast sample was halved and placed into two microplate wells to accommodate the volume of the sample, and each well was read individually. In each experiment, control YFP values were averaged from technical replicates, and this average control value was then used to normalise all individual YFP values obtained in the same experiment. Normalised data values from three biological repeats were then pooled for identical genotypes and averaged to give a final normalised average value. Statistical analyses were undertaken using ANOVA with post hoc Tukey.

Stem Rust Resistance Trials in the Field

Trials of homozygous Fearless5 lines and double homozygous Big5+F5 lines were undertaken in Pgt disease nurseries at the University of Minnesota, Rosemount Research and Outreach Center. Fearless5 lines and controls were trialled in the 2021 and 2022 field seasons with triplicate plots for each genotype in the earlier year and duplicate plots in the latter year. Homozygous Big5+F5 lines were trialled using duplicate plots in both 2023 and 2024. For each trial a completely randomized planting design was used with approximately 25 seeds planted in each plot. Wheat cultivars Fielder, Morocco (PI 431591) and LMPG‐6 were used as susceptible controls. When the first nodes of plants were detectable (Zadoks 31), inoculations were made with a suspension of Pgt urediniospores of race QTHJC (1 g urediniospores/1 L Soltrol 170 mineral oil; Phillips Petroleum, Bartlesville, OK) using an ultra‐low volume sprayer (Mini‐ULVA, Micron Group, Herefordshire, United Kingdom). Three additional inoculations were made in successive weeks to ensure high infection levels during the later stages of crop development. The percentage (0%–100% scale) of stem and leaf sheath tissue covered by uredinia was assessed using the modified Cobb scale (Peterson et al. 1948) from the late milk to late dough stages (Zadoks 77–85) of crop development (Zadoks et al. 1974).

Plant Growth and Yield Parameter Trials

Field trials were undertaken in 2023 and 2024 at the CSIRO Boorowa Agricultural Research site located 5 km from the township of Boorowa, NSW, Australia. Homozygous Fielder and Robin wheat lines carrying either the Big5 construct, the Fearless5 construct or both constructs, in addition to null sib and nontransgenic parental controls, were grown. Fielder and Robin lines were grown in separate sites with three replicates per genotype. To mitigate the effect of spatial variation, a spatial row–column design with partial replication was used and designed using DiGGer 1.0.0. software (Coombes 2016). A separate randomisation was used for each year. Both Fielder and Robin trial sites were surrounded on all four sides by plots of nontransgenic parental material to act as a buffer zone (Figures S11 and S12). This buffer control material was not included in the final data analysis.

In both the 2023 and 2024 trial each plot was 1.5 m long and 2 m wide and contained 10 plant rows (Figures S11 and S12). Plots were arranged in columns with 14 plots per column and each plot separated by 1 m from adjacent plots in the same column. Columns of plots were spaced 0.5 m apart (Figure S11). In the 2023 trial 14 columns of Fielder genotypes (including buffer rows) and 16 columns of Robin genotypes were planted (Figure S11). Each trial plot in 2023 contained two genotypes, with rows 1–5 containing one genotype and rows 6–10 containing a second genotype (Figure S11). In each plot, rows 1–5 and rows 6–10 were harvested separately. Thirty seeds were planted in each 1.5 m row in each plot. In the 2024 trial, due to increased seed availability, each 10‐row plot contained a single genotype, and all 10 rows were harvested together (Figure S12). Again, 30 seeds were planted in each 1.5 m row. In this trial, the Fielder site consisted of 15 columns each with 14 plots (including buffers), while the Robin site consisted of 15 columns each with 16 plots (Figure S12). Spacings between columns and plots was the same as used in the 2023 trial.

Seed emergence was assessed by manual counting of emergent seedlings in plots when at the two‐leaf stage. During the growth period, plants were sprayed with fungicide (either Tangent, Azoxystrobin or Amistar Xtra at 500 mL/ha) and herbicide (Mateno at 1 L/ha + MSPA LVE at 500 mL/ha) for weed control. Croplift 15 at 110 kg/ha, treated with flutriafol, and urea at 150 kg/ha were applied as fertilizer. Prior to flowering, the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) was measured using a GreenSeeker Variable Rate Application and Mapping System (NTech Industries Inc., USA). Plots were machine harvested using a Wintersteiger Classic plot harvester (Wintersteiger, Austria), and seed weighed using a scientific balance. One thousand grains were collected using a Contador seed counter (Pfeuffer, Germany) and again weighed using a scientific balance.

NDVI, % seedling emergence, yield per plot, yield per plant and thousand grain weight data were collected from the four field trials undertaken for Fielder and Robin in 2023 and 2024. Data for each year and each genotype were analysed using linear mixed models (LMMs) fitted via asreml for R (Butler et al. 2017; Butler 2022), running under R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025). In the models the ‘genotype’ factor was regarded as nested within factor ‘genotype_status’. Genotype_status distinguished between Big5, Fearless5, Big5+F5 transgenic line groups and their respective null sibs, in addition to parental controls. The number of individual genotypes in each genotype_status is shown in Table S5. Both genotype_status and genotype_status:genotype were fitted as fixed effects factors, yielding predicted means as best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) for these model terms. In addition to fitting ‘block’ as a fixed effects factor, residual diagnostic plots and relevant statistical tests were used to assess the presence of extraneous spatial variability across the trials and relevant model components to account for these effects were included as needed. Residual diagnostic plots were also used to assess model residuals for their distribution and the presence of potential outliers in the data. No transformations of the data were deemed necessary, nor were any potential outliers flagged in the residual diagnostics. Pairwise comparisons at the 5% level of significance between the genotype_status BLUEs were performed using the asremlPlus R package (Butler 2022); results are presented as the compact letter displays in Figures 5 and 6. To compare individual transgenic events with their respective null sib controls, a least significant difference (LSD) approach was applied to predicted means (BLUEs) using the average LSD calculated across test lines.

Results from the 2024 Boorowa field trial. Analyses and data are presented as described in Figure 5.
