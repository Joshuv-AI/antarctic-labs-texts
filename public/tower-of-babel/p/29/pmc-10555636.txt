Impedance trend from a symptomatic perielectrode cystic cavity following deep brain stimulation: illustrative case
Authors: Brooke Elberson, Hayden Scott, Rohit Dhall, Erika Petersen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDeep brain stimulation (DBS) is a well-established neurosurgical intervention for a growing number of neurological and psychiatric diseases. Patients who are affected by Parkinson’s disease may benefit from DBS of either the subthalamic nucleus or the globus pallidus internus. Patients who undergo DBS often notice a significant reduction in their clinical symptoms; however, the procedure is not without risks. Multicenter studies have reported postoperative complications such as hardware infection, intracranial hemorrhage, and perielectrode edema.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report a case of a perielectrode cyst managed conservatively. Tracking the impedance trend was a novel approach to monitor for changes within the cyst and to herald a clinical change in the patient. Perielectrode cystic formation can be a transient process that resolves spontaneously or with conservative, nonoperative management, and all diagnostic information is valuable in making clinical decisions.LESSONSImpedance values have provided an appropriate estimation of this patient’s clinical picture. The authors suggest treatment of edema and a cyst after DBS lead implantation through conservative management and observation, avoiding the removal of hardware if a patient’s clinical picture is either stable or improving and forgoing additional clinical imaging if the impedance values are trending in an appropriate direction.

Patients with implanted intracerebral devices such as shunt catheters or electrodes can develop infection, hemorrhage, or fluid cysts related to the device. These findings can be clinically silent, more so in the case of small hemorrhages or fluid cysts, or symptomatic, such as in the case of infection or a larger hemorrhage. In addition to these space-occupying lesions, there may be associated edema or even infarction. Transient hemiparesis has been reported in cases in which patients’ postoperative imaging demonstrated poorly defined hypodensities.1 Additionally, the development of edema appears to be a transient process, because the incidence was higher in earlier magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed closer to implantation.2 Mechanisms of cyst formation are unclear but may be a sequel of perilead edema.3 Management strategies in the literature are varied. Conservative treatment with steroids or observation can result in regression of the cystic lesion accompanied by signs of clinical improvement.4,5 There have also been reports of aseptic cysts occurring months after deep brain stimulation (DBS) placement that resulted in neurological deficits treated with hardware removal.6 Alternatively, a more conservative approach to cyst formation involving stereotactic aspiration of a cyst in a patient with a persistent lack of treatment efficacy has facilitated a safe outcome without the need for surgical lead removal.7 After aspiration and without steroids, DBS stimulation became more effective. Symptomatic lesions, of course, must be addressed more aggressively. A major goal of treatment for patients with implanted devices of all kinds is device retention to improve their outcomes, avoiding delays in treatment while complications are managed.1

Illustrative Case

A 53-year-old male patient with Parkinson’s disease symptoms initially experienced resting tremor of the right hand and decreased arm swing with ambulation. Further classic symptoms developed over the next few years, and carbidopa/levodopa treatment was initiated 5 years later. Despite dose optimization, the patient reported disabling symptoms leading to a cumulative 25% daily off time. He expressed goals of care that included decreasing his medication burden and daily off time. He was evaluated through the multidisciplinary movement disorders program for DBS candidacy and was scheduled to undergo bilateral DBS targeting the left and right subthalamic nucleus (STN) using a two-stage approach with compact spaced leads (Medtronic 3389) following our center’s surgical standard, and immediate postoperative images confirming lead placement did not show any cystic lesions around the electrode.8 The initial programming visit was completed 15 days after lead implantation without incident. On postimplant day 46, the patient had abruptly developed right-sided veering while ambulating, dysarthria, right-sided facial droop, and falls when moving to the right and was admitted for observation. Imaging studies including magnetic resonance angiography without contrast and brain MRI with contrast revealed the presence of a 2.0 × 1.5–cm fluid collection around the left-sided DBS lead in the left basal ganglia, with mass effect on the internal capsule (Fig. 1A–D). The DBS stimulation was turned off, and the patient was started on a 2-week dexamethasone taper. We elected to follow up with him with serial examinations and imaging studies rather than surgically intervene. Repeat MRI 2 weeks later (postimplant day 59) showed stability of the cyst, and the patient demonstrated neurological improvements. Over the next 2 months, he progressed back to his postoperative baseline. DBS stimulation remained off on the left side and was set to a low amplitude on the right side. The patient ultimately had a recurrence of his parkinsonian symptoms 4 months after the cyst was noted, and bilateral DBS stimulation was resumed, with improvement. The cyst continued to resolve, as demonstrated in Fig. 1E–H.

The relevant impedance values during the clinical decline showed a sizable impedance decrease around the left-sided STN lead, where the cyst had occurred, as compared with the right-sided control lead (Fig. 2A). The patient’s symptoms effectively resolved by postimplant day 98. Discussion

Observations

This case provides insight into the conservative treatment options for patients who experience perielectrode cystic formation following their DBS procedure. Surgical management to remove hardware may not be necessary, especially if the patient has a clinical picture that remains stable or is improving. Patients who experience perilead edema can also be treated conservatively or with a steroid course, such as dexamethasone. Aström et al.9 discussed the microenvironment modeled around the DBS lead and stated that there is often a fluid space around the DBS lead because the common targets for stimulation are enriched with Virchow-Robin spaces. As patients age, these cavities enlarge. The presence of a large cystic cavity lowers the impedance of the electrode because of the higher conductivity of fluid compared with the parenchyma. By the same mechanism, it alters the local field potential and thereby the clinical effect of stimulation.9 Typically, the normal range of impedances is between 500 and 2000 Ω, averaging around 1250 Ω, and there is a documented long-term gradual decline in impedance values during stimulation, which may be due to physical degradation of the electrode or changes to the local microenvironment from ongoing electrical stimulation.10 None of the referenced reports or reviews of DBS complications includes an analysis of the impedance trends of the DBS device over months of nonsurgical management, which may be changed by the local fluid dynamics.9

Lessons

Our case supports trending impedance values in patients to predict changes in the patient’s clinical picture. We followed a strategy of scheduled clinic visits to monitor for a sizable change in the impedance values, which would suggest the need for further work-up. Impedance measurement is a simple process that can be done in an outpatient setting, and, as we demonstrate in this paper, it may herald impending clinical change as well as indicate resolution of the offending lesion, which removes the need for frequent advanced imaging. Stimulation was halted in this patient to avoid complicating the interpretation of his clinical process. Stimulation could have been continued with altered parameters to achieve some level of clinical benefit while the cyst resolved itself. Further study is necessary to determine if stimulation affects the lesion or if it is simply the presence of foreign material. Given that many lesions are clinically silent, as discussed earlier in this work, many patients may have transient edema or lesions that are never detected and may continue to use the device. Of course, having the data from only a single patient is a major limitation, but, given the significant benefit in retaining the device without the need for removal and reimplantation, which both negates further surgical risk and allows more rapid resumption of therapy when the cyst is cleared, we believe that these data call for a larger study of the effect on impedance of asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic perihardware lesions that may be observed rather than aggressively managed.
