Relationship between glycolipid metabolism and aortic dissection in a hypertensive population: an early diagnostic model with internal training and external validation
Authors: Yong Lin, Yi-Ting Huang, Shao-Sheng Wu, Yi-Na He, Hai Lu, Wang Li, Lan Lan, Peng-Yu Qin, Liang-Wan Chen, Yong-Ping Zhu, Mei-Fang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The relationship between glycolipid metabolism and acute type A aortic dissection in patients with hypertension remains unknown.Materials and methods:All patients with hypertension from six centers over the past 5 years were enrolled in this retrospective multi-center study. Participants from the leading institution were classified into the training set and those from the participating institutions were classified into the validation set. Baseline demographic and glycolipid metabolic characteristics were collected to establish an early diagnostic model for acute type A aortic dissection. Internal training and external validation were performed to assess the efficacy of the model.Results:Younger age, higher body mass index, male sex, absence of diabetes mellitus, lower serum total cholesterol concentration, and higher serum lipoprotein(a) concentration were identified as risk factors for acute type A aortic dissection in individuals with hypertension. Incorporating these variables into the clinical early diagnostic model yielded receiver operating characteristic values of 0.789 (0.758–0.820) for the training set and 0.744 (0.689–0.789) for the validation set. The positive predictive value was 0.893 (0.871–0.916) for the training set and 0.835 (0.788–0.883) for the validation set, while the negative predictive values were 0.448 (0.401–0.496) and 0.448 (0.366–0.529) for the two sets respectively. Decision curve analysis for the training set showed that the model provided greater net benefit than the two extreme strategies for threshold values between 0.05 and 0.72. The same was true for the validation set when the threshold value was between 0.09 and 0.71. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test confirmed that the P values for the calibration curves of the training and validation sets were higher than 0.05, indicating good calibration.Conclusion:The model based on demographic characteristics and serum lipid levels was validated to have a good predictive value for acute type A aortic dissection among hypertensive individuals.

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a life-threatening medical emergency that requires prompt diagnosis and surgical intervention[1]. Chronic hypertension can damage the aortic endothelium and compromise aortic wall integrity. Increased pressure can force blood through weaknesses or tears in the aortic wall, initiating the dissection process. However, only a few patients with hypertension develop this potentially fatal disease[2,3]. Up to 41.1% of the patients had moderately or mildly elevated blood pressure measured at the time of symptom onset, and only 27.4% of the patients had a systolic blood pressure of >150 mmHg[4]. Moreover, only 40% of patients had a maximum systolic blood pressure ≥180 mm Hg, which was fewer than that of the total hypertensive population (46.0%)[4]. The absolute value of systolic blood pressure may not be significantly associated with the occurrence of aortic dissection[3]. Factors other than hypertension may also cause structural changes in the aortic wall.

Glycolipid metabolism refers to the biochemical processes involved in the synthesis, breakdown, and transport of lipids within the body that are closely associated with cardiovascular diseases[5]. Dyslipidemia is a critical factor in the development of atherosclerosis and is characterize by elevated serum total cholesterol (TC), elevated serum low-density lipoprotein (LDL), low serum high-density lipoprotein (HDL), or a combination of these features[6]. Abnormalities in lipid metabolism can impair the function of the endothelial cells lining the inner walls of blood vessels[7]. However, the association between lipid metabolic disorders and ATAAD remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the relationship between glycolipid metabolic disorders and ATAAD risk by constructing an early clinical early diagnostic model with internal training and external validation to facilitate the development of effective prevention strategies for ATAAD in a hypertensive population. This cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with STROCSS guidelines[8].

Material and methods

Data source

A multicenter cross-sectional observational cohort study was performed to explore the risk factors for ATAAD related to glycolipid metabolism from 1 May 2019 to 30 April 2024. All data were obtained from the hospital information systems.

Participants

Six cardiovascular surgical centers in China were included in this study. Consecutive patients diagnosed with hypertension were enrolled. Among these patients, those with ATAAD confirmed by computed tomography angiography of the thoracic aorta were assigned to Group AD and those without aortic disease who visited the emergency department were assigned to the control group (Group CON). Observational variables

Data on demographic characteristics (sex and age), body mass index (BMI), medical history, and serum lipid levels were obtained. The presence of underlying diseases such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus (DM) was evaluated and confirmed according to diagnostic criteria. Laboratory test results were obtained for serum lipid levels, including triglyceride (TG), TC, HDL, LDL, and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)].

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed using R version 4.3.0 (2023-04-21, www.r-project.org) and Zstats v0.90 (www.medsta.cn/software). All statistical graphs were generated using Microsoft PowerPoint 2010 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and the online data processing platform, Wenjuanxing (www.wjx.cn, Changsha, China). Continuous variables are described using mean ± standard deviation (S.D., for normal distribution) or quartiles and median (for non-normal distribution), while categorical variables are described using frequency. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using the t-test for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cases from the leading institution were classified into the training set for model establishment and those from the participating institutions were classified into the validation set for external validation of the model. Univariate regression was used to identify ATAAD predictors. Given the prominent effect of some demographic characteristics, such as BMI, age, and sex, on glycolipid metabolism, all serum lipid parameters were adjusted for these variables using multivariable logistic regression, and all suspected risk factors (P value <0.1 were included in the clinical early diagnostic model). A nomogram was subsequently constructed based on univariate logistic analysis results. Model evaluation was performed using external validation methods, specifically the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, Hosmer–Lemeshow calibration curve, decision curve analysis curve, and confusion matrix, to assess the early diagnostic model performance on both the training and validation sets.

In the sensitivity analysis, the exposure factors of lipid metabolism characteristics were redefined based on the reference standards for primary prevention[9] and the medians of serum lipid concentrations in our series. A logistic analysis was then performed using variables with new definitions. Finally, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles was applied to evaluate the trend of ATAAD risk with serum lipid concentrations, which were confirmed as independent risk factors. Both factors were adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and history of DM.

Sample size

As nine probable risk factors for ATAAD were included in the logistic regression analysis and the proportion of ATAAD in the training set was 23.5% (Table 1), the minimum sample size was calculated as 90/0.235 = 382. Missing data

Patients with any missing critical observational variables, as described above, were excluded from this study.

Ethics

This study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The medical records used in this study were obtained from previous clinical diagnoses and treatments. Therefore, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Model specification

Male sex, younger age, higher BMI, lower serum TC concentration, higher serum Lp(a) concentration, and the absence of DM were incorporated into the early diagnostic model, and a nomogram was constructed (Fig. 2). Model performance

The AUC for the training and validation sets was >0.7, suggesting good performance in detecting the occurrence of ATAAD in patients with hypertension (Fig. 3). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test confirmed that the P values for the calibration curves of the training and validation sets were >0.05, indicating good calibration (Fig. 4A and 4B). The decision curve analysis for the training set suggested that the net benefit of the model was greater than that of the two extreme strategies when the threshold value was between 0.05 and 0.72 (Fig. 4C). The same was true for the validation set when the threshold value was between 0.09 and 0.71 (Fig. 4D). The confusion matrix revealed that the positive predictive values were 0.893 (0.871–0.916) for the training set and 0.835 (0.788–0.883) for the validation set, whereas the negative predictive values were 0.448 (0.401–0.496) and 0.448 (0.366–0.529) for the two sets, respectively (Table 5). The RCS curve indicated a trend of risk for ATAAD with lower serum TC and higher Lp(a) with or without adjustment for sex, age, BMI, and history of DM, and the analysis did not reveal significant evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity > 0.05) for either of the two risk factors. Serum TC levels were positively associated with the odds ratio of ATAAD at a concentration <4.56 mmol/L (cutoff point), but the association became negative when the concentration increased to >4.56 mmol/L. In contrast to TC, the odds ratio of ATAAD increased as the concentration of serum Lp(a) increased, and the cutoff point was 125.00 mg/L (Supplementary Digital Content Figure S7, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/E916).

Discussion

In this study, an early diagnostic model for ATAAD was constructed based on multicenter, retrospective, cross-sectional cohort study data, with external validation. This model demonstrated high early diagnostic accuracy. The DCA curve from the external validation indicated that patients with a predicted risk below the lower threshold (0.09) may be considered very low risk and could be managed with routine surveillance, whereas those above the upper threshold (0.71) may be considered high risk and warrant more aggressive interventions. In addition to well-known cardiovascular disease risk factors, such as male sex[10], high BMI[11], and high levels of serum Lp(a)[12], the authors observed that more patients did not present with DM and had lower serum TC levels in Group AD than in Group CON. In addition, the regression model constructed in this study included age as a negatively associated factor; the reasons for this are discussed below. The construction of this model provides epidemiological evidence for the formulation of clinical strategies for the early detection of ATAAD in patients with hypertension while offering a theoretical foundation for elucidating the mechanisms of hypertension-related ATAAD.

ATAAD is closely associated with the aortic degeneration caused by aging and chronic hypertension[13,14]. However, the logistic regression analysis performed in this study revealed an odds ratio of 0.957 for age, indicating a mild decrease in the incidence of ATAAD of 4.3% for each year of age. The nomogram also suggests that the weighted score decreases with age, corresponding to a lower risk of ATAAD. The authors speculated that this seemingly contradictory result may be related to the fact that all the participants in this study were diagnosed with hypertension. These findings could be interpreted as indicative of younger age at the time of diagnosis of hypertension as a risk factor for future ATAAD. Data from a survey on hypertension in China showed that 74.3% of participants aged 18–44 years had mild hypertension, demonstrating a growing trend of hypertension affecting younger individuals[15]. This trend may partially explain the significantly lower average age of patients with ATAAD in China than in the European and American populations. These findings suggest that younger patients with hypertension should be more aware of the risk of ATAAD, highlighting the importance of the early identification and strict control of hypertension in middle-aged and young adults.

DM is currently recognized as a high risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, aortic aneurysm, and peripheral vascular disease[16]. However, the relationship between DM and ATAAD in the hypertensive population remains unclear[17]. A recent prospective cohort study failed to validate the causality between DM and ATAAD in middle aged adult population from ten regions of China[18]. Even more, a real-world study found that DM may be a protective factor against AD onset[19]. In 2018, a large observational cohort study based on data from the Swedish National Diabetes Register demonstrated a negative correlation between DM and AD. This study included patients hospitalized between 2010 and 2017, and a retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on patients with type 2 DM (T2DM) versus individuals without T2DM. Survival analysis adjusted for demographic factors showed no statistically significant negative correlation between T2DM and AD (HR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.753–1.004; P = 0.057). However, after adjusting for hypertension, a significant negative correlation was observed between the two conditions (HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.621–0.830; P < 0.05). This finding suggests that DM may be a protective factor against AD only in patients with hypertension[20]. The probable mechanism involves the protection of the arterial wall elastic tissue under hyperglycemic conditions, which reduces the risk of aortic tearing or dilation by improving hemodynamics[21]. Moreover, a recent study that utilized a Mendelian randomization analysis suggested that DM may have a protective effect on the aorta mediated by 3-hydroxybutyrate, indicating a prophylactic effect against AD, which is consistent with the findings of previous real-world observational studies. This study also indicated that interventions, such as ketogenic diets or exogenous ketone supplementation, could be potential preventive measures for AD in patients with hypertension[22]. Although DM was uncommon in our series of ATAAD, the possibility of missed diagnoses owing to urgent hospital admissions for surgery, vague medical histories, and diagnostic difficulties cannot be ruled out. Therefore, although numerous studies have linked DM to a reduced occurrence of AD, the specific protective mechanisms require further investigation.

Hyperlipidemia is an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis and plays a significant role in the progression of cardiovascular disease[23]. Lipid metabolism contributes to the development of atherosclerosis. Plaque formation can weaken the structural integrity of the aortic wall and increase the susceptibility to dissection[24]. Dysfunctional lipid metabolism can trigger inflammatory responses and increase oxidative stress within the vessel walls. Oxidative stress caused by an imbalance in free radical and antioxidant levels can weaken aortic wall[25]. Another possible mechanism involves the frequent association between lipid metabolic disorders and hypertension, leading to vascular stiffness and increased mechanical stress on the aortic wall[26,27]. Hence, strict control of lipid levels is strongly recommended by several clinical practice guidelines[28,29].

Nonetheless, significantly lower serum lipid levels (including TC, TG, HDL, and LDL) were observed in the Group AD compared with the Group CON. Further logistic analysis and early clinical diagnostic modelling suggested that TC and Lp(a) levels were independent diagnostic factors for ATAAD in the hypertensive population. A relatively high serum TC (>4.56 mmol/L) exerted a protective effect against ATAAD in the hypertensive population in this study, despite the associated risk of endothelial damage and aortic atherosclerosis. This suggests that lipid metabolism plays different roles in the formation of ATAAD, which appear to be different from the mechanisms of other cardiovascular diseases.

Two decades ago, decreased systemic atherosclerosis was considered a risk factor for ascending aortic dissection, which was explained by the same genetic mutations that cause aortic root pathology and inherent resistance to atherosclerosis[30]. Genetic components may be involved in glucose and lipid metabolism disorders that may influence the risk of developing ATAAD. Specific genetic mutations can impair the function of metabolic enzymes, leading to abnormalities that increase aortic wall vulnerability[31]. Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) is a non-cytochrome P450 mitochondrial aldehyde-oxidizing enzyme involved in the detoxification of alcohol-derived acetaldehydes and endogenous aldehydes[32]. ALDH2 also participates in modulating cholesterol biosynthesis by regulating 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase, which is a well-known target of hyperlipidemia therapies, such as statins[33]. A prominent elevation in serum cholesterol levels was observed in ALDH2-knockout mice fed with a normal diet, indicating an important role for ALDH2 in the downregulation of serum lipids[34]. However. an animal model of aortic aneurysm induced by angiotensin II revealed that ALDH2 deficiency may contribute to abdominal aortic aneurysm through the reactive oxygen species pathway, promoting vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis and aortic inflammation[35]. Recently, two independent case-control studies demonstrated that individuals with the ALDH2*2 allele (phenotype of ALDH2 low activity) had an approximately 50% reduced risk of developing an aortic aneurysm or dissection compared to those with ALDH2*1/1 (phenotype of ALDH2 high activity) by regulating the phenotypic transformation of vascular smooth muscle cells inside the aortic wall[36]. Moreover, ALDH2 deficiency may promote the development of aortic aneurysms by downregulating miR-31-5p expression[33]. The authors speculated that the downregulation of ALDH2 in individuals with certain phenotypes, combined with low levels of serum TC, might contribute to structural damage in the aortic wall. According to the RCS curve of TC in the odds risk of ATAAD, the control of the risk for pathological changes in the aorta was dose-dependent on the ALDH2 activation level[36,37], suggesting that the hypertensive population might not benefit from excessively low serum TC regarding protection from ATAAD. Nevertheless, an excessively high serum TC level may not necessarily be beneficial as it may increase the risk of other cardiovascular diseases. Additionally, the authors failed to verify the protective effect of high serum TC concentrations using sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/E912 and Supplementary Digital Content Table S3, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/E914), indicating that patients with hypertension will probably not benefit from hypercholesterolemia in preventing ATAAD.

Lp(a) is a special apolipoprotein biosynthesized in the liver that consists of LDL-like particles and apolipoproteins (a)[38]. Genetic factors such as race and ethnicity, rather than diet and environment, are key determinants of serum lipoprotein (a) levels[39–42]. Recently, the independent and additive nature of Lp(a) level in cardiovascular disease risk was confirmed[12]. Increased levels of serum Lp(a) were also observed in patients with ATAAD compared with other healthy controls, especially in younger individuals (<60 years old)[43]. Serum Lp(a) level has been proven to be a highly suspected risk factor for ATAAD in patients with hypertension using multivariate regression and subgroup analyses. Based on the RCS curve of Lp(a), we speculated that the association between serum Lp(a) levels and ATAAD was dose-dependent. Patients with high Lp (a) levels were more likely to developing ATAAD[44]. In addition, the prominent elevation in serum Lp(a) levels in patients with AD was accompanied by lower levels of TG, LDL, and TC, which is consistent with our findings[43], indicating that lipid metabolism plays a different role in the formation of ATAAD than in other cardiovascular diseases. The possible mechanisms of ATAAD caused by increased serum Lp(a) concentrations include mediating an inflammatory response[44], promoting monocyte trafficking[45] and altering the contents of T-lymphocyte subpopulations[46] which leads to an inflammatory response and extracellular matrix degradation in the aortic wall, ultimately promoting ATAAD[47].

In addition, the body enters a stress state after the onset of ATAAD causing severe pain, contributing to a sharp increase in catecholamine and glucocorticoid concentrations in the blood, which in turn promotes liver lipoprotein synthesis, indirectly causing disorders in glycolipid metabolism[48]. To eliminate the effect of this stress state on blood glycolipid levels, patients with hypertension who visited the emergency department were assigned to the control group. This approach may have counteracted the bias caused by acute stress on the risk of AD due to altered blood glycolipid levels.

This study had some limitations. First, the study originally intended to include a sample size exceeding 18 000. However, many patients were excluded from the final logistic regression analysis because of the lack of critical variables, such as height, body mass, and blood glycolipid levels, which may have decreased the accuracy of the early diagnostic model. Second, although the study found that the absence of DM may be a high-risk factor for the occurrence of ATAAD in patients with hypertension, the majority of the patient data lacked HbA1C test results. Thus, it was not possible to determine whether hyperglycemia or genetic susceptibility to the DM phenotype was involved in the progression of ATAAD. Third, this was a cross-sectional retrospective study, and all serum lipid level data were derived from blood tests conducted at admission, which did not necessarily reflect serum lipid concentration prior to disease onset. Lastly, owing to the retrospective design, variables such as lifestyle factors and genetic predispositions were not incorporated which may have influenced the interpretation of the results, necessitating verification of causality through further prospective investigations.

Conclusion

Low serum TC and high Lp(a) levels may contribute to ATAAD in hypertensive populations. Additionally, male sex, younger age at the time of hypertension diagnosis, absence of DM, and high BMI were closely associated with the occurrence of ATAAD in the hypertensive population. A clinical early diagnostic model for ATAAD risk was constructed based on data from a multicenter retrospective cross-sectional cohort series. This model exhibited a relatively high accuracy in detecting the occurrence of ATAAD but a low accuracy in negative detection. These findings indicate that frequent physical examinations are recommended for young men with hypertension but without DM. However, a well-designed prospective cohort study should be conducted to confirm the relationship between glycolipid metabolism and ATAAD. A series of in vivo and in vitro experiments are required to demonstrate the role of glycolipids in ATAAD formation. Although a history of DM and higher serum TC levels may be protective factors against ATAAD in hypertensive populations, strict control of blood glucose and serum lipid levels is important to prevent other cardiovascular diseases. Finally, this model demonstrated the well predictive performance in hypertensive populations, but some potential differences in risk-factor distributions within the general population – such as the roles of BMI and a history of DM require re-evaluation in future study.
