Introduction: A SEQUINS inductee recalls taking aim at inequities
Authors: Robert J. Adams
Published: 2026

The honor of this invitation to write about our work in stroke prevention in children with Sickle Cell Disease, the STOP Study, and the resulting protocol, leading to Induction into the SEQUINS Hall of Fame of Equity in Neuroscience Society, is matched only by the challenge this task represents. The STOP project entailed decades of work and collaboration, and this award allows me to reflect on this part of my life and highlight features that reflect the core values of SEQUINS. At the heart of SEQUINS is the belief that all people deserve the chance to enjoy brain health [1].

This journey started in 1973 when I left the world of professional engineering for medical research, not otherwise specified. My goal was simple and certainly naive: to work toward the reduction of suffering in those less fortunate so that at the far-off age of fifty, I would be able to think I had used my skills for good. When I made this change, I could not know that I would be given such a terrific opportunity to influence the lives of people with Sickle Cell Disease (SCD), experiencing a great inequity in life, that of also having a stroke, at a very young age.

In this paper, I will discuss various features of this effort, called STOP, which occupied most of my research effort from 1986 until 2005. The STOP Project represented a great deal of work through a large collaboration of professionals and the trust and cooperation of many hundreds of patients and families. In retrospect, even though it took over 15 years, we, in fact, did move the needle and put in motion a practical and proven strategy to significantly decrease ischemic stroke in children with Sickle Cell Disease. In this paper, I want to highlight some features that were important for this achievement to be realized.

I am reminded of a saying attributed to Dr. Ron Petersen of Mayo Clinic that goes something like this: “When asked about how our career of research developed, we end up describing a situation where the concept of a ’plan’ is superimposed onto a battlefield of hard work and serendipity”.

Taking this wisdom as template, I look back and examine the research itself (the “hard work”), some of the special “accidents” that happened along the way that increased chances for success (“serendipity”), and the “battlefield” on which the work took place. STOP grew out of a particular research and clinical environment at academic centers such as the Medical College of Georgia (MCG), and the thirteen other related participant sites, including Medical University of South Carolina, Several, like MCG, were also National Institutes of Health (NIH) funded SCD Centers.

What was important about this environment?

MCG had successfully hosted the Sickle Cell Research Center for almost 15 years, led by a world-famous hemoglobin scientist, Titus H J Huisman, DSc [2]. He himself reported dozens of new forms of this amazing molecule. As a clinical Cerebrovascular expert, familiar with blood disorders as the occasional cause of stroke, I knew a bit about hematology but very little about hemoglobin, the protein, and consequently very little about the fundamental pathophysiology of SCD.

One thing that STOP demonstrated is that one does not need to understand the pathophysiology of a disorder to intervene and reduce its impact. For example, we did not, and still do not, fully understand how red cell transfusion works so well to reduce stroke, but nonetheless, we have been able to intervene and prevent stroke because we learned how to identify those with a risk high enough to merit intervention.

The NIH via the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) had for several decades funded SCD research primarily through funding 1O Centers with obligatory cores and specific research targets. Importantly, funding for clinical care was also included. This was of critical importance because it ensured that there were at least ten tertiary centers in the United States offering high-quality care in combination with advanced clinical research. In those days, NHLBI allowed these centers to include substantial funding to underwrite the clinical care of these patients. Professor Hulsman had put together a clinical team at MCG to provide clinical care while the bulk of his scientific effort was devoted to the molecule. One of these people was pediatric hematologist Virgil McKie, MD, who was friends with the Department of Neurology Chair Thomas R Swift who hired me. At about this time, an 8-year-old patient of Dr. McKie’s had what was diagnosed as a TIA. The recommendation at the time was to put the child on monthly transfusions for a year then stop transfusion. Unfortunately, the child then had a large brain infarction. Dr McKie reached out to his friend in Neurology, recommending we meet, resulting in a 20-year partnership. Other SCD team members included in the core team at MCG were a pediatrician, Kathy McKie, MD, Abdulla Kutlar, MD, an adult Hematologist and Director of our Core Laboratory, and my colleague in Vascular Neurology, Fenwick Nichols. Having the project take place, at least at the lead clinical center, in this environment helped to pull together the supportive clinical teams with the skills and experience in which patients and families had developed trust.

A remarkable amount of trust must be mobilized and leveraged to conduct a randomized clinical tr/al (RCT). In the case of STOP the trust that patients and families had for the teams providing their care, the pediatric generalists, and hematologists, was in part shifted to the STOP Study Team.

How was this trust mobilized and leveraged to support the STOP RCT?

The simple answer is that the study teams became, at the invitation of the pediatric clinical care teams, embedded in their systems of care. Most of the foundational care provided for these children was done on an outpatient basis. MCG, the clinical lead center, had a large clinic in Augusta, Georgia, and a series of five clinics in outlying smaller towns in rural Georgia. We conducted research screenings with TCD in the clinics, including those in rural Georgia, and on this basis were able to become a part of the clinical care system, and acceptance quickly followed. We proposed this model throughout the RCT, and it worked well to execute the study.

This project was the beneficiary of many what I consider “providential” Interactions that increased its chances for success. As one example of this serendipity, early in the project in 1986, I quite by accident met the inventor of Transcranial Doppler ultrasound, Rune Aasild, Ph D. He became a valued consultant for the project that took TCD in a vastly different direction than he had imagined when he developed it for use in the neuro ICU. Learning about TCD and meeting its inventor were good examples of this serendipity that we recall looking back.

The “hard work” part of the story of the “STOP Project”, the details of the project itself, can be found in a trilogy of papers in the New England Journal of Medicine published in 1992 [3], 1998 [4], and 2005 [5].

To prevent brain Infarction, why not transfuse all the children with SCD at the highest age of risk? This unselected approach may well have worked, but the data from the Cooperative Study of SCD [7] showed we would need to transfuse 100–200 children for a year just to prevent a single stroke: unacceptable odds in most people’s view. It was necessary to stratify the risk of stroke and transfuse only those at the highest risk. Was TCD the right tool to stratify the risk of stroke?

TCD was newly introduced (1982) into the clinical world via the neuro ICU to detect subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) related vasospasm after aneurysm rupture. This pathological process, so-called vasospasm, was quite different than the vasculopathy related to SCD. However, the location of the areas of stenosis was similar to what had been reported from cerebral angiography in SCD [8]. In both cases, SAH vasospasm and vasculopathy related to SCD- TCD detected stenotic areas by documenting elevated velocity of blood flowing through the affected narrowed areas based on the Bernoulli principle of physics [6] using reflected pulsed ultrasound. We reasoned that while the mechanism causing the stenosis was different in SCD, the effect this narrowing had on the fluid dynamics of arterial flow would be the same as observed in vasospasm.

In 1985, we also began using Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) at MCG. We reported some of the first applications of MR imaging of brain infarction in SCD [9]. MR was elegant and exciting, and it would soon be clear that it was to be a significant change. In retrospect, it is fortunate for this project that we did not know how powerful it was going to turn out to be. In any case, we were faced with an operational question of the greatest significance.

“Life sometimes offers the choice between the lesser of a better thing and the better of a lesser thing”. TCD was clearly the “lesser thing” compared to the “better thing”, the MRI. But because TCD was so easy to use, portable, low-cost, and the like, we realized the “better” of this lesser thing to great advantage rather than having to settle for the “lesser” of the elegant but difficult to use MRI.

As a medical diagnostic instrument, MR was clearly the “better thing”. It measured a range of variables, allowed several ways to alter the experiment, and offered enormous growth potential. What is not to like? The problem with MR, which is why in real life the experience was the “lesser” in value, was that MRI was difficult to use. It requires several support personnel and systems. While TCD was almost comically simple by comparison because it was portable, easy to use, required no support personnel, could be placed in the back of the hematology van, and taken around rural South Georgia, the use of TCD, for this project, was vastly “the better” experience. Yes, it was the lesser thing, but how we were able to use it meant that we enjoyed the “better” of this lesser thing rather than the “lesser” of the better thing, the MRI. I am hard-pressed to find a clearer example of how this truism can play out in real life, in real clinical research life, at least.

In fact, we ended up using MRI for more select applications in this project. But the most critical test, the selection of patients for primary stroke prevention with regular red cell transfusion, the TCD was nearly perfect, and we performed this test by the thousands. With it, followed by regular red cell transfusion, we broke the back of ischemic stroke in children with SCD. When fully applied according to our evidence-based methodology, the first stroke rate was cut by 90%, making it one of the most effective primary stroke prevention protocols for any high-risk condition in medicine up to that time or since [4].

What did NHLBI get right that supported STOP? Using the ten Federally funded SCD Centers to provide the foundation for good clinical care was clearly important in setting up a program that patients and families trusted. This trust was translated over to the domain of clinical research.

How did we get funding for STOP? NHLBI had a funding mechanism designed to support multicenter high-cost RCTs. NHLBI was prepared to receive, evaluate, and support significant RCTs. They also required that the clinical administrative leadership institution (in our case, MCG) had to be separate from the Data Management and Statistical Analyses Center (New England Research Institutes in the case of STOP), providing a degree of objectivity and scientific rigor, and preventing a small group of scientists with perhaps inbred ideas from having too great an impact on the design, conduct and/or interpretation of the results of the RCT.

The Clinical Trial Review Section consisted of clinical trialists familiar with the excessive cost of good RCTs. Grants such as STOP were submitted as R01s. If accepted for funding, they were converted to U01s or so-called cooperative agreements, giving NHLBI a big seat at the table. All these factors worked in favor of increasing the chances of success and provided a much-needed way to protect these young brains placed at severe risk by genetics. Taking note of what NHLBI got right and how that helped make STOP a success could be something that SEQUINS can use to promote good science in other situations.

There were several other operational decisions involved in STOP that we were incredibly lucky to have chosen, almost reaching the point of being “serendipitous”. For example, the choice of the cut point velocity-reflecting, in part, what degree of vascular damage had been inflicted up to the time of intervention, was especially Important [10]. This velocity was called the “cut point” at or above which the children could be randomized, and it was critical. It embodied the classic tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity. We needed a cut point associated with a high enough risk of stroke but not so high as to be associated with too proximate a risk of stroke. In that situation, we would never find enough cases to approach for entry into the trial because there would not be time to enroll them before a stroke occurred. We needed a reliable point, and again, we met serendipity on the battlefield with the choice of 200 cm/sec. Not only was this a nice round number, but it turned out to be a good balance of risk versus proximity of stroke. It represented a point at which the risk of stroke was firmly established, but stroke was not imminent. That was important because we needed time to intervene, which at first involved performing a repeat TCD to confirm the findings. As confidence in TCD grew, this step was later abandoned. We also needed time to enroll the child in the transfusion program. An abnormal TCD was nonetheless a warning of stroke. There were several examples during screening of children who had a stroke in between the first and confirming TCD, which was only a matter of a few weeks.

We determined in ancillary studies that if the velocity was 200 cm/sec and not much more at the time of intervention, the child had a low risk of having severe stenosis on angiography, and subsequent angiography on treatment would show that the vasculopathy did not advance to severe stenosis [11]. So, in many ways, 200 cm/sec was a near-perfect cut point.

We were also fortunate to keep the trial protocol simple by resisting the temptation to use more complex predictors and cut points. A TCD study like the one used in STOP measured three velocity parameters (peak, systolic, and time average maximum of the mean (TAMM) at 15 points in the intracranial arterial system, with a pulsatility and resistivity index generated at each arterial point of measurement. This represented a vast number of possible ratios and other ways to formulate indicators. But we held firm to the idea that it would work best to distill the exam into a single indicator variable, as we could not reasonably expect enough endpoints to validate more complex measures. Keeping it simple [10] resulted in a strong and durable trial result of lasting consequence.

With respect to STOP RCT design, it was Important to find and preserve equipoise. What was “on trial” In STOP was, strictly speaking, red cell transfusion, but there was little doubt that transfusion would work. “Equipoise” lay in the uncertainty surrounding many questions as to whether a practical method of patient selection for transfusion could be demonstrated and tested in a multicenter format. Fortunately, we were able to execute STOP, and the observations were as expected. STOP will be the first and last study where children with SCD at risk for stroke will be randomized to a control group without active treatment. In fact, TCD has become so firmly validated that the FDA now accepts it as a surrogate endpoint for Phase 3 Registration studies for stroke prevention in children with SCD. New agents, specifically small molecules, can be tested using TCD, and if the drugs prevent worsening of TCD from normal or conditional TCD to high risk, it will be considered equivalent to observation of a clinical event [12].

Why was a second STOP RCT performed?

In 1997, STOP RCT was prematurely halted. In August of that year, the investigators were told to meet with all 130 randomized patients and their parents and explain the findings [4]. This meant that the trial itself had met its goal of recruiting enough patients, enrolling, and following them until a statistically significant and convincing result was found. In the trial control arm, there were 10 children with stroke (primary endpoint) versus one in the group with transfusion. This was highly significant (p< .001). We explained the results to all patients, regardless of the group to which they had been randomized, and asked patients/families to choose the therapy they wanted to take going forward from the closure of the STOP Study. In September 1997, the Director of NHLBI announced the trial results. We were indeed able to say with confidence how to prevent the first stroke in children with SCD, but we did not know how long the preventive treatment needed to be continued. This proved to impede the acceptance of the STOP Protocol.

We believed that a second attempt to address this question needed to be pushed forward without delay, while functioning clinical stroke research from STOP was still in place. We moved quickly to submit STOP II: Optimizing Stroke Prevention in Sickle Cell Disease and received funding to begin in 2000 [5].

In STOP II, we randomized children on preventive transfusion, with now normal TCD, into two groups: the control group consisted of children who had been on preventive red cell transfusion for at least 30 months without developing any stroke symptoms. This group continued with no change in treatment. The experimental arm consisted of similarly treated children withdrawn from transfusion. The two groups were expected to include up to sixty children in each arm and followed for 24 months. This trial, like STOP, was striking in its result. The trial was discontinued early, in this case based on safety rather than a positive outcome. Too many children had to be placed back on transfusion because they developed TCD endpoints (reversion from on-treatment normal to high risk), that we could not complete the randomization of the two groups. It was quickly clear coming off transfusion without an alternative treatment would not be a good idea.

In retrospect, it may seem ill-advised to stop transfusions, but at the time, there was a theory that the high risk of stroke, and by extension the need for RBC transfusion, was time-limited and highest only in the early years of life when demands for blood due to rapid brain development are at their highest. If we could get them past the age of this vulnerability, we reasoned, we could remove the preventive red cell therapy. But this was not borne out by the data and the experience. A second Clinical Alert issued by NHLBI at the time when STOP II was terminated advised that, as of 2004, no safe point to stop preventive red cell transfusion had been identified. The findings of the first trial, STOP, were reiterated, and the recommendation was to follow these unless the patient is participating in a properly designed RCT.

STOP II reinforced how effective TCD was at predicting stroke. Other ancillary studies showed that transfusion had many salutary benefits beyond the prevention of stroke (less pain and acute chest syndrome, better growth) [13].

An examination of the probable impact of STOP was carried out by scientists who were not part of STOP. They looked at the black/white disparity in the risk of death due to ischemic stroke in children. Comparing before and after STOP time frames, black/white disparity was reduced from 1.7 pre-STOP to 1.27 post-STOP. This suggests that the national needle had indeed been moved. The researchers found no reduction in the risk of death from hemorrhagic stroke (not affected by the STOP protocol), which supports the conclusion that the widespread application of STOP and not secular changes in healthcare explain the reduction seen in death from ischemic stroke [14].

It was a truly rewarding experience to work with so many professionals dedicated to the care of these patients and their families. It was the privilege of a lifetime and closed the circle I had imagined in 1973 when I left the telephone company and engineering to embark on a career in Medical Research, which was a step into the unknown. Much work remains, but we have taken a first key step towards achieving a Stroke Free Childhood in Sickle Cell Disease [15].
