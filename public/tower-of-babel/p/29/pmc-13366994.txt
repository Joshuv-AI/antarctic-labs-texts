Optimising support for stroke caregiver burden: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised clinical trials on occupational therapy-related interventions
Authors: Xiaolu Liu, Wendong Zhang, Zehua An, Shuaijing Wan, Wenjing Jiang, Zhexuan Yan, Yu Yin, Weibo Li, Yanning Yan, Peiyuan Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke caregivers often experience significant physical and psychological burden during long-term caregiving, leading to reduced quality of life. Traditional unidimensional support models inadequately address their complex needs. Occupational therapy interventions based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), which integrate multidimensional support across body function, activity and participation, and environmental factors, may systematically alleviate caregiver burden. However, the efficacy and optimal intervention approaches lack comprehensive synthesis.MethodsA systematic literature search was performed in the following seven databases from January 2010 until January 2025: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Science Direct, CNKI, and WANFANG. The search aimed to identify all relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) where the participants were informal stroke caregivers, the intervention was occupational therapy, and the comparator was usual care. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore heterogeneity by categorising interventions according to the International Classification of Functioning, ICF framework: body functions, activities and participation, environmental factors, and personal factors. Methodological quality was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Random-effects meta-analyses were employed to calculate mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsTwelve RCTs involving 1,636 participants were included in the analysis. The meta-analysis demonstrated that occupational therapy-related interventions significantly reduced caregiver burden (SMD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.53 to -0.10; p = 0.004). For secondary outcomes, no significant effects were found for caregiver depression or survivor functional recovery. Subgroup analyses based on the ICF framework indicated that interventions combining activity participation, environmental factors, and personal factors resulted in a statistically significant reduction in caregiver burden (SMD = -0.50, 95% CI: -0.81 to -0.20; p = 0.001). Interventions targeting other dimensions did not show significant effects.ConclusionOccupational therapy-related interventions demonstrate a small but statistically significant beneficial impact on alleviating the caregiving burden for stroke caregivers. Prioritizing multifaceted ICF-based interventions that address activities, participation, and contextual factors is crucial. Future research should evaluate long-term outcomes to advance the shift from patient-centered to family-centered rehabilitation, thereby enhancing wellbeing for stroke caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04929-y.

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of acquired long-term disability worldwide [1], characterized by significant morbidity, mortality, and recurrence. The consequences of stroke affect not only patients but also their family members [2]. Approximately 90% of Chinese stroke patients depend on familial care, with merely 10% accessing professional rehabilitation facilities, rendering stroke one of the most care-intensive conditions [3, 4]. Across diverse healthcare systems and cultural contexts, the predominant caregiving model involves informal caregivers, typically comprising immediate family members or close acquaintances. This arrangement frequently leads to pronounced physical and psychological strain among caregivers during extended care periods, compounded by insufficient caregiving expertise and knowledge. Such strain invariably affects caregivers’ occupational productivity, financial stability, physical wellbeing and emotional equilibrium [5, 6].

Caregiver burden refers to the multidimensional stress experienced by those providing care for stroke patients, encompassing emotional, psychological, physical, and social domains. Evidence shows that over 80% of caregivers experience significant burden [7], with female caregivers consistently bearing greater burden [8, 9]. Notably, caregiver burden directly correlates with diminished care quality [9]. When burden levels become excessive, caregivers may fail to provide adequate daily support, potentially leading to suboptimal functional recovery that directly compromises patient rehabilitation outcomes and quality of life [1, 10]. More significantly, caregiver burden shows a strong correlation with heightened patient readmission rates [11], establishing a vicious cycle of “declining care quality → deteriorating patient outcomes → escalating burden” [12]. Crucially, healthcare systems frequently neglect the economic burden of informal care [13, 14]. The annual indirect economic costs borne by families through caregiver work absenteeism, including productivity loss and employment adjustments, constitute approximately 23% – 45% of total stroke-related medical expenditures [4]. Consequently, developing evidence-based interventions to strengthen caregiver capacity and mitigate their burden has emerged as a critical priority for optimising comprehensive stroke management.

Occupational therapy (OT) is a client-centred health profession that enhances health and wellbeing through engagement in purposeful and meaningful activities [15]. Grounded in empirical evidence, OT offers strategies to support service recipients’ quality of life and constitutes a distinct component of holistic health interventions [16]. In this review, “occupational therapy related interventions” are defined across three dimensions: (1) interventions directly delivered by an occupational therapist; (2) interventions led or co-designed by an occupational therapist (e.g., where the OT serves as the primary intervention developer or a core member of a multidisciplinary team); and (3) interventions aligned with core OT principles—such as client-centeredness, activity analysis, and environmental adaptation (e.g., developed based on OT frameworks, applied the concept of OT, or utilized the model of OT), even when not directly delivered by an occupational therapist (e.g., interventions focusing on activity, participation, or environmental factors). In addition, interventions targeting three or more different areas within the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework -including physical function, activity and participation, environmental factors, and personal factors- are classified as “multifaceted interventions”.

The ICF framework conceptualises caregiver burden as emerging from interconnected pressures spanning physiological, psychological, social, and economic domains [17]. However, current interventions for caregiver mainly concentrate on unidimensional skills training or psychological support [18, 19], generally neglecting key areas within the ICF framework, such as social support and adapting the environment. Such interventions are difficult to effectively address the diverse burdens faced by caregivers and also differ from the multidimensional integrated intervention approach advocated by the ICF. OT can bridge the gap between patients, caregivers, and their environment by applying a multidimensional ICF framework, achieving environmental adaptation, activity participation, and role balance, effectively reducing the burden on caregivers, and improving the quality of life of the patient-caregiver dyad [20, 21].

Despite strong evidence supporting OT’s efficacy in improving patient function, its application for caregivers remains underdeveloped. Research in this area is fragmented and lacks systematic integration. It is important to clarify that this review evaluates comprehensive interventions that are led or deeply informed by occupational therapists and grounded in core OT principles, such as activity/participation-centered practice, environmental modification, and role balancing. While many intervention components (e.g., skill training) are inherently transdisciplinary, the aim here is not to assert professional exclusivity, but to systematically assess the combined effect when such services are delivered within a cohesive OT professional framework. This helps clarify OT’s unique value within interdisciplinary teams in providing caregiver support.

This meta-analysis aims to determine whether occupational therapy-related interventions, grounded in the ICF framework, can effectively reduce caregiver burden and enhance quality of life for informal caregivers of stroke survivors, when compared to usual care. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis specifically designed to evaluate the efficacy of OT-related interventions on caregiver burden in the context of stroke. By synthesizing evidence from randomized controlled trials, this review seeks to derive clinically relevant findings to inform the optimization of home-based care support systems.

Methods

Study registration and design

This study was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD42025065391) and implemented in accordance with the PRISMA Protocol [22] for systematic reviews of randomised controlled trials. No deviations from the registered protocol occurred.

Data sources and search strategy

Two independent investigators (SJW and WDZ) systematically searched seven electronic databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Science Direct, EMBASE, CNKI and Wan Fang. The search encompassed literature published from January 2010 to January 2025, with no geographical or linguistic restrictions imposed. This time frame was chosen because the ICF framework’s key dimension “personal factors” was first systematically classified in 2010 [23]. Restricting the search scope to studies published after 2010 ensures that included studies reflect the full dimensionality of the ICF framework, thereby more representatively capturing contemporary, ICF‑based occupational therapy intervention practice.

The search strategy was developed based on the PICOS framework. Two reviewers (SJW and WDZ) collaboratively identified key concepts: Population (informal caregivers of stroke survivors), Intervention (occupational therapy-related interventions), Comparison (usual care), Outcomes (caregiver burden and related measures), and Study design (randomized controlled trials). For each concept, we compiled relevant Medical Subject Headings terms and free-text keywords by examining terminology used in relevant seminal articles and previous systematic reviews. The final PubMed strategy was peer-reviewed by a second reviewer (WBL) with expertise in systematic review methodology. The full electronic search strategy for PubMed is provided in Appendix S1.

Additionally, we manually examined reference lists of all identified articles to locate potentially relevant studies not captured by the database search. The dataset presented in this study can be found in the supplementary materials. For any matters not covered, please contact the corresponding author.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were selected based on the PICOS framework to identify records that complied with the eligibility criteria and aligned with the research questions (See Table 1). The manuscript defines OT-related interventions as meeting at least one criterion: (1) directly delivered by an occupational therapist; (2) led or co-designed by an occupational therapist (e.g., OT as primary intervention developer or core member of multidisciplinary team); and (3) aligned with OT principles but not delivered by an OT (e.g., interventions focusing on activity/participation/environment, but delivered by other professionals). The classification was performed independently by two reviewers (WDZ and XLL) based on full-text information. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Exclusion criteria included non-RCTs, study protocols, abstracts, and studies with sample sizes of fewer than 10 participants. Two investigators (SJW and WDZ) independently screened records, with a third (ZHA)resolving disagreements. Interrater agreement was high (Cohen’s kappa = 0.78).

Data extraction

Two independent investigators (WJJ and ZXY) systematically extracted the data using a purpose-designed standardised form. A third researcher (YNY) verified the extracted data by comparing both forms and finalising the consolidated results. The extracted data included: study information (authors, publication year and country); sample characteristics (age, sex, educational attainment, daily caregiving hours, chronic health conditions, employment status and monthly income, relationship to patient); Intervention details (occupational therapy-related intervention type, content, specific timing and frequency, delivery format, setting, control group type); and outcome measures (assessment tools, follow-up schedule). When critical data (e.g., outcome means and standard deviations) were missing or only reported in figures, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors via email. Notably, only caregivers were included in all statistical analyses; stroke survivors were not treated as participants. For caregiver-survivor dyad studies, only caregiver data were extracted and analyzed.

ICF-based classification of interventions

To systematically categorize intervention content and facilitate subgroup analyses, we classified all interventions using the ICF framework. Classification was conducted independently by two reviewers (PYL and WDZ) with prior training in ICF for rehabilitation research.

OT-related interventions were categorized into four ICF domains:

After independent classification, the two reviewers compared their assignments. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer (YNY). The inter-rater reliability for the ICF domain classification was substantial (Cohen’s kappa = 0.83), indicating good agreement beyond chance.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and potential risk of bias were evaluated in accordance with Cochrane Collaboration guidelines [24], assessing seven key domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential biases. An independent third investigator arbitrated any discrepancies arising during this assessment process. All analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) software (version 5.4; The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). Three risk classification levels were applied: High Risk (red), Low Risk (green), and Unclear Risk (yellow). Studies lacking registration or protocol documentation were categorised as inconclusive for selective reporting assessment. A comprehensive synthesis of full-text articles and methodological quality informed the evaluation of other bias sources. The bias assessment outcomes were graphically presented. One included study [25] used a cluster-randomised design; due to unavailable intracluster correlation coefficients, we could not adjust for clustering in the primary analysis, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative synthesis was performed using RevMan 5.4 software. A random-effects model was applied to evaluate the statistical significance of combined effect sizes, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were used to allow comparisons across studies employing different outcome measures, including various caregiver burden scales and the Zarit Burden Interview, as well as different tools for assessing activities of daily living (ADL) such as the Barthel Index, FIM, and Lawton IADL. All outcomes were presented with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed through the chi-squared test (significance threshold P ≤ 0.05) and quantified using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50% and 75% indicating low, moderate and high heterogeneity respectively [26]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for moderate-to-high heterogeneity cases by sequentially excluding individual studies to identify potential outlier influences. Irrespective of heterogeneity levels, all analyses employed a random-effects model. Forest plots visually presented the pooled effect estimates, while funnel plot analyses evaluated potential publication bias. Notably, funnel plot asymmetry testing was only applied when meta-analyses contained a minimum of 10 studies. None of the included trials reported multiple ADL measures for the same stroke survivor sample; thus, no within-study selection of instruments was necessary.

Results

The database search identified 454 records, which were imported into EndNote X9. After removing 136 duplicates, 202 records were excluded based on title and abstract screening. The remaining 116 studies underwent full-text screening. Of these 104 were excluded for failing to meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, 12 studies involving 1,636 stroke caregivers were included (Fig. 1).

Caregiver demographics

Sex distribution data were available for all except three studies, documenting 584 male and 862 female participants. Caregivers had a mean age of 60.10 years (SD = 14.67). Kinship analysis revealed the majority were spouses (n = 1,038), followed by parents/children (n = 390) and other relatives (n = 91).

Reported characteristics

A limited number of studies documented specific caregiver characteristics. Merely one study reported the prevalence of pre-existing chronic conditions among caregivers, while another study recorded the daily care duration required by stroke patients. Regarding employment status, only three studies provided relevant data, indicating that 36.7% (n = 80) of caregivers were employed, with the remaining 63.3% (n = 138) being either unemployed or retired. Concerning intervention duration, four studies implemented programmes shorter than three months, eight studies conducted interventions lasting three months or more, and one study failed to specify the exact duration.

Intervention settings

The studies employed diverse intervention settings: four adopted a hybrid hospital-home approach, three implemented interventions exclusively in home environments, two utilised rehabilitation centres, one conducted session solely in hospital settings, one extended from hospital to rehabilitation centre, and one was based in a community rehabilitation centre.

Outcome Assessment: Six studies only evaluated outcomes immediately following the intervention. Six additional studies incorporated follow-up assessments after the intervention period. Merely two studies extended follow-up beyond six months.

Risk of bias in the included studies

The risk of bias for the 12 studies is shown in Fig. 2. Twelve studies described randomization methods: four used computer-generated sequences, two used random number tables, three used randomization programs, and three used simple randomization (e.g., coin toss and lottery). Five studies reported allocation concealment. Blinding of participants was not achieved in most studies. Seven studies specified that evaluators were blinded (unaware of group assignment during assessment), while the rest did not report blinding details. Eleven studies reported complete results. Potential selective reporting bias was identified in one study [33], where a positive post-hoc finding (caregiver burden) was emphasized despite null effects on all pre-specified outcomes, which may have led to an overestimation of the intervention’s true effect. Five studies contained potential risk sources, including selection bias from non-random allocation methods, unreported allocation concealment, potential allocation bias, high sample attrition rates, possible assessor awareness of group assignment, inadequate psychometric properties of assessment tools, and COVID-19-related anxiety affecting intervention adherence. Sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled effect size was not over-affected by excluding individual studies, indicating the relative robustness of the results. The funnel plot for caregiver burden was approximately symmetric. However, the power to detect publication bias or small-study effects was limited given the inclusion of only 12 studies, warranting cautious interpretation of the results. For secondary outcomes with fewer than 10 studies, funnel plots were not generated, as their validity is limited.

Quantitative summary: effect of occupational therapy on stroke caregivers’ burden

In accordance with this meta-analysis’s objectives and the PROSPERO-published protocol, we conducted a quantitative analysis of the primary outcome measure.

Primary outcome

Caregiver burden

The twelve included studies evaluated caregiver burden using the following standardised instruments: Caregiver Burden Scale (CBS): 4 studies [11, 25, 29, 33]; Caregiver Strain Index (CSI): 3 studies [27, 30, 34]; Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI): 3 studies [20, 28, 31]; List of Threatening Experiences–Structured Interview (LISS): 1 study [32]; Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI): 1 study [6]. A pooled effect size was calculated by combining all caregiver burden studies. Results demonstrated significantly lower burden in the intervention group versus controls (SMD = −0.32; 95% CI −0.53 to −0.10; p = 0.004). The forest plot revealed substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 61%; p = 0.003) (Fig. 3).

Secondary outcomes: for caregivers

Caregiver depression

Four studies evaluated caregiver depression. However, two studies either lacked or did not report the quantitative data (mean ± standard deviation) required for meta-analysis [28, 34]. Consequently, only two studies could be included in the pooled analysis of caregiver depression. One of the studies used the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale Depression Subscale (HADS-D) [27], while the other used the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [31]. No statistically significant difference in depressive symptoms between experiment and control groups (SMD = −0.00; 95% CI −0.28 to 0.27; p = 0.97). Forest plot analysis indicated no statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%; p = 0.68) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of pooled effect sizes demonstrating OT-related intervention effects on caregiver depression in stroke

Caregiver anxiety

Caregiver anxiety was assessed in three studies employing distinct measurement instruments. Quantitative data for anxiety scores were unavailable for extraction from two studies [25, 31], precluding their inclusion in the meta-analysis. The investigation conducted by Kootker et al. [27] exclusively applied the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety subscale (HADS-A) to document post-intervention anxiety levels among stroke caregivers. In that study, no significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups at any time point. Due to the limited number of studies, a pooled analysis was not performed.

Caregiver family functioning

One study assessed family function in caregivers using the Chulalongkorn Family Inventory (CFI) [28]. Family caregivers in the intervention group showed significant improvement in CFI scores from baseline to 12 weeks compared to controls (p = 0.001). Family members in the intervention group also showed significant improvement (p < 0.001). Meta-analysis was not performed due to the limited number of studies.

Caregiving benefit

One study evaluated caregivers’ caregiving capacity following the intervention, utilising the Caregiver Benefit Finding Scale (CBFS) [20]. The results showed that caregivers in the occupational guidance group reported significantly greater perceived benefit compared to the control group (mean change from pre- to post-intervention: 16.56 ± 4.69 vs. 11.42 ± 3.55, p = 0.01). Meta-analysis was not performed due to the single study.

Secondary outcomes: for stroke survivors

Activities of daily living in stroke survivor

Seven studies examined activities of daily living as a secondary outcome in stroke survivors, utilising the BI, FIM and Lawton IADL assessment tools. Among these, four utilised the Barthel Index (BI) [6, 25, 29, 35], two employed the Functional Independence Measure (FIM) [31, 34], and one study applied the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale [20]. However, in the study by Deepradit et al. [28] only statistical comparisons of BI scores were reported, rather than the raw data. An overall effect size was calculated by combining all studies involving activities of daily living. The analysis revealed no significant difference between intervention and control groups (SMD = 0.14; 95% CI −0.20–0.47; p = 0.42). Forest plot analysis indicated moderate homogeneity across trials (I² = 55%; p = 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Quality of life in stroke survivor

Seven studies evaluated quality of life in patients. Among these, the study by Forster et al. [25] did not report specific values due to negative results, Marsden et al. [30] omitted the total Stroke Impact Scale (SIS) scores, and Guidetti et al. [29] similarly failed to provide specific values; consequently, these studies were excluded. The remaining four studies were incorporated into the quantitative analysis: one utilised the Stroke-Specific Quality of Life Scale (SSQoL) [20, 33], one employed the Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale (SAQOL-39) [11], one applied the SIS [31]. An overall effect size was calculated by combining all studies involving quality of life. The analysis revealed no significant improvement in the intervention group compared to controls (SMD = 0.25; 95% CI −0.07–0.57; p = 0.13). Forest plot analysis indicated low heterogeneity across trials (I² = 39%; p = 0.18) (Fig. 6).

Stroke survivor depression

Four studies evaluated depressive symptoms in stroke patients. Deepradit et al. [28] presented results using non-parametric tests, while Forster et al. [25] did not report specific numerical data and these were not obtainable upon request. The remaining two studies employed standardised assessment tools: one utilised the Beck Depression Inventory and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale (BDI-II) [11] and the other applied the GDS [31] to measure depressive status. Meta-analysis demonstrated no significant improvement in depressive symptom severity following occupational therapy interventions compared to control conditions (SMD = −0.17; 95% CI −1.16–0.82; p = 0.73). Forest plot analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity between studies (I² = 91%; p = 0.001) (Fig. 7). No significant differences were found in the merged analysis, but the number of studies was small and the heterogeneity was high, so the results need to be interpreted with caution.

Caregiver burden

Five studies conducted post-intervention follow-up assessments. The analysis demonstrated not significantly reduced caregiver burden in the intervention group relative to controls during the 2- to 8-month follow-up period (SMD = −0.18; 95% CI −0.40–0.05; p = 0.13), with forest plot analysis indicating no significant heterogeneity (I² = 44%; p = 0.08) (Fig. 8).

Caregiver depression

Two studies monitored caregivers’ depressive status during 3- to 8-month post-intervention follow-up. The experimental group demonstrated no statistically significant improvement in depressive status relative to controls (SMD = 0.02; 95% CI −0.19–0.23; p = 0.86). No statistical heterogeneity was observed in the forest maps (I² = 0%; p = 0.84) (Fig. 9). This analysis only included two studies (Kootker et al. [27]; Ostwald et al. [31]), and the statistical power is insufficient to determine the long-term effects of intervention on depression in caregivers.

A single study evaluated caregivers’ anxiety status at 4- and 8-month post-intervention follow-up points. Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed that in terms of reducing caregiver burden, a significant effect was observed only in interventions targeting “activity participation + environmental factors + personal factors” (SMD = −0.50, 95% CI −0.81 to −0.20; p = 0.001). In contrast, interventions addressing “body functions + activity participation + environmental factors + personal factors” (SMD = −0.27; 95% CI −0.96 to 0.42; p = 0.44), “activity participation + environmental factors” (SMD = −0.32; 95% CI −0.80 to 0.16; p = 0.19), or “environmental factors only” (SMD = −0.26; 95% CI −0.60 to 0.08; p = 0.13) did not show statistically significant effects, and no significant differences were observed across the different intervention dimensions (I² = 0%; p = 0.73). Results are presented in Fig. 3.

Regarding the improvement of activities of daily living in stroke survivors, the subgroup analysis indicated that interventions focusing on “activity participation + environmental factors + personal factors” demonstrated a potential benefit that reached statistical significance at the conventional threshold (SMD = 0.50; 95% CI 0.01 to 0.99; p = 0.05). However, comprehensive interventions including body functions (SMD = −0.09; 95% CI −0.60 to 0.42; p = 0.74), interventions involving only activity participation and environmental factors (SMD = −0.23; 95% CI −0.52 to 0.06; p = 0.11), and interventions targeting environmental factors alone (SMD = 0.42; 95% CI −0.28 to 1.11; p = 0.24) all failed to show significant effects. Moreover, the differences among the various intervention dimensions were statistically significant (I² = 62.4%; p = 0.05). These results are presented in Fig. 5.

However, these findings should be interpreted with considerable caution due to methodological limitations. Only 12 studies on caregiver burden were divided into four subgroups, with very few studies in each subgroup (range: 2–4 studies). Only 6 studies on stroke survivor’ daily living activities were divided into four subgroups, with very few studies in each subgroup (scope: 1–2 studies). The apparent benefits of some ICF-based intervention combinations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Further large-scale trials are needed to confirm whether interventions targeting specific ICF domain combinations yield larger or more consistent effects.

The sensitivity analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity in caregiver burden outcomes, which was markedly reduced upon exclusion of the study by Forster et al. Second, exclusion of the study by Bunketorp‑Käll et al. [32], which was considered to carry a potential risk of selective reporting bias, led to a slight reduction in the pooled effect size (from SMD = − 0.32 to − 0.31), with the effect remaining statistically significant (95% CI: −0.54 to −0.07, p = 0.01), see Supplementary Fig. S1. This suggests that although selective reporting bias may have marginally inflated the effect estimate, it does not affect the overall conclusion that occupational therapy‑relatedinterventions are associated with a small but statistically significant reduction in caregiver burden.

In addition, after excluding the intervention measures involving the widest dimensions of “physical function + activity participation + environmental factors + personal factors”, heterogeneity significantly decreased from 61% to 32%, indicating that this study is the main source of heterogeneity. This reflects the inherent complexity of multidimensional intervention measures - the integration of different components and mechanisms introduces greater variability in different research contexts. In contrast, interventions with more focused targets may benefit from higher standardization, resulting in more consistent effects. This discovery highlights a key trade-off: while multidimensional interventions have theoretical advantages, their complexity also poses challenges to reproducibility in different contexts.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 12 randomized controlled trials to evaluate the impact of OT-related interventions on the caregiving burden of stroke caregivers. OT-related interventions based on the ICF framework significantly reduced the burden on caregivers. However, no significant overall improvement was observed in secondary outcomes such as caregiver depression, as well as patients’ activities of daily living, quality of life, and depressive symptoms. Subgroup analysis indicates that comprehensive interventions focusing on “activity participation, environmental factors, and personal factors” demonstrate the most significant effect in reducing the burden on caregivers.

Overall findings

Caregiver burden

Caregiver burden encompasses the multifaceted physical, psychological, social, and economic challenges experienced by individuals providing care. The meta-analysis demonstrated a consistent and significant reduction in burden scores among caregivers receiving OT-related interventions compared to control groups. This treatment effect was particularly pronounced for interventions that concurrently targeted the domains of Activities and Participation, Personal Factors, and Environmental Factors within the ICF framework. These findings strongly support the Grade A recommendation for caregiver interventions in the Occupational Therapy Practice Guidelines for Adult Stroke Rehabilitation [36], which underscores the efficacy of strategies such as stress management, emotional reframing, and role adaptation training in enhancing caregiver self-efficacy. OT-related interventions include home environment adaptation, tailored education, and skills training specific to the patient’s needs. Evidence confirms that home environment adaptation constitutes a core occupational therapy [37]. Simultaneously, OT delivers tailored care knowledge and skills training specific to individual patient circumstances [38].

Furthermore, OT employs a comprehensive approach that evaluates both the stroke survivor and their family caregiver as an integrated unit. Based on this assessment, it develops individualised activity plans specifically designed to alleviate caregiver burden while simultaneously enhancing the patient’s activities of daily living [39, 40]. Consequently, as patients demonstrate improved ADL performance and participation, their stroke-related burden diminishes proportionally. This effect is paralleled by significant burden reduction observed when caregivers successfully incorporate acquired knowledge into their practical caregiving competencies [20]. Existing evidence demonstrates that home-based intervention programmes incorporating knowledge transfer, skills training, family support therapy and stress management components (typically delivered through home visits and telephone follow-ups) substantially strengthen caregivers’ decision-making capacity and problem-solving competence [41].

Our results demonstrated substantial heterogeneity, indicating outcome variability across populations and intervention settings that warrants individualised analysis. As an illustration, Kootker et al. [27] did not include direct interventions for caregiver psychological support, such as stress management or respite care. Similarly, Kootker et al. [27] and Guidetti et al. [29] reported no significant reduction in caregiver burden, as their interventions focused primarily on patient rehabilitation rather than directly addressing caregivers’ needs. Heterogeneity was largely driven by the TRACS trial [25], and we could not adjust for its cluster design due to missing data, which is a limitation.

Existing OT research predominantly focuses on the home or community phase. During the critical hospital-to-home transition period, when caregiver stress peaks and support proves most essential [42]. Continuity of care should be achieved through predischarge education and sustained follow-up measures, such as telephone support or home visits, during the initial two to four weeks following discharge. Exemplary research by García-Pérez et al. [11] successfully reduced burden through three core strategies: (1) integrated hospital-to-home cycle support, (2) collaborative patient-caregiver empowerment, and (3) OT-related resource coordination, specifically addressing care gaps during transition. Future initiatives should integrate transitional occupational therapy practices into stroke rehabilitation guidelines, develop comprehensive hospital-community referral systems to ensure care continuity, and investigate implementation approaches across varied healthcare systems.

It should be noted that this study found a statistically significant reduction in caregiver burden, according to Cohen’s effect size evaluation criteria [43], the pooled effect size (SMD = −0.32) only falls within the “small” effect category. Therefore, interpretation of its clinical significance should be cautious. The various studies included in the analysis used different caregiver burden assessment tools (such as the ZBI, CSI and CBS, etc.), making it impossible to uniformly convert standardized effect sizes into the original scores of each scale. More critically, there is currently a lack of consensus on the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) values for the aforementioned scales among stroke caregivers [44]. This gap may stem from the high heterogeneity of factors such as caregiver age, relationship with the patient, duration of caregiving, and cultural background, which profoundly affect individuals’ perception thresholds for “meaningful change”. Simultaneously, a single MCID value may lack universality in such a population.

Caregiver depression

Caregiver burden in stroke shows a strong correlation with depression and anxiety [7, 45]. Descriptive research has highlighted the necessity of developing suitable coping strategies for caregivers to alleviate anxiety and related issues [46]. Our findings revealed no significant difference in depression levels between intervention and control groups following the intervention period, implying that OT-related approaches may not substantially modify caregivers’ depressive states. This outcome may stem from certain OT programmes concentrating principally on functional and activity/participation levels while failing to simultaneously target high perceived stress and low self-efficacy, which could restrict improvements in depressive symptoms [47]. Alternatively, improvements in depression and anxiety may require more intensive, longer-term psychosocial interventions that incorporate cognitive behavioural therapy. Moreover, caregiver depression frequently stems from multifaceted factors including financial pressures, diminished social support and relationship changes, which education and skills training alone may be insufficient to resolve. This vulnerability is particularly pronounced when wives assume the primary caregiver role. Their physical and mental health becomes especially susceptible, manifesting in physical exhaustion and emotional distress [48]. In contrast to our findings, Jeong et al. [49] demonstrated that client-centred occupational therapy significantly enhanced social integration, reduced stress levels, and improved community participation among depressed caregivers. Their study also highlighted how OT can alleviate both physical and psychological strain through optimised time management, provision of assistive devices, and targeted skills training.

Caregiver family functioning

Stroke significantly disrupts family functioning, particularly in the first six months, impairing the system’s capacity for adaptation and growth [50]. Only Deepradit et al. [28] assessed family functioning using the CFI. This study reported positive outcomes, with families receiving a family-based occupational therapy-related intervention demonstrating significantly higher CFI scores at four and twelve weeks compared to those receiving usual care, showing continuous improvement throughout the study period. This suggests that OT approaches hold potential, although the limited evidence precludes definitive conclusions. Stroke frequently precipitates substantial transformations in family roles, particularly as spousal relationships evolve from equal partnerships to caregiver-care recipient dynamics [51]. The positive finding from Deepradit et al. [28] may be attributed to the intervention’s explicit focus on the family as a unit, involving both caregivers and family members in supportive therapy, stress management, and skill training. This approach aligns with the notion that family functioning is a key determinant of rehabilitation outcomes [52]. However, most reviewed interventions prioritized individual caregiver or patient outcomes over dyadic or family functioning (e.g., couple interaction, role rebuilding). Furthermore, Ramachandran et al. [53] emphasise that family-centred interventions necessitate cultural adaptation. Future OT-related research should develop and test structured, culturally adapted family-centered interventions.

Caregiving benefit

Caregiving benefit, defined as the positive psychological gains caregivers experience from their role, such as personal growth, enhanced self-efficacy, and strengthened family relationships, was assessed in one study (Zhang et al. [20]) using the CBFS. This study found that caregivers in the occupational guidance group reported significantly greater perceived benefit compared to the control group. This suggests that interventions incorporating coaching strategies—such as motivational interviewing, goal setting, and positive reframing—may enhance caregivers’ ability to find meaning and growth in the caregiving experience. Caregiving benefit finding can buffer the negative effects of stress and promote adaptive coping [54]. However, the limited evidence base for caregiving benefit in stroke populations warrants further investigation. Additionally, the relational diversity among caregivers (e.g., spouses vs. adult children) may influence the experience of caregiving benefit [55]. Future interventions should consider tailoring strategies to address the unique needs of different caregiver groups.

Given that the functional and psychological status of stroke survivors are key determinants of caregiver stress, this review also examined the effect of OT-related interventions on three critical outcomes pertaining to the survivors: activities of daily living, quality of life, and depression. The meta-analysis found no significant improvements in any of these outcomes. These consistent null findings are unsurprising and highlight a critical intervention target mismatch.

Activities of daily living of stroke survivors

Limitations in survivor ADL strongly correlate with caregiver burden [56]. Consequently, in theoretical terms, improvements in survivors’ recovery may facilitate caregiver wellbeing. However, the results of this meta-analysis reveal that the included OT-related interventions did not produce significant improvements in stroke patients’ ADL performance. In contrast to our findings, Wang et al. [57] demonstrated that interventions targeting caregivers can enhance both physical performance and ADL abilities in chronic stroke survivors. Several factors may explain these null findings. First, the OT-related interventions included in this review primarily focused on addressing caregiver-specific needs, such as stress management, skills training and knowledge education, rather than being directly or systematically designed to improve patient activities of daily living performance. Consequently, the core objectives of these interventions may not have been adequately linked to patient ADL outcomes. Secondly, given that four studies assessed ADL using the BI, its limited sensitivity to detect subtle changes in mild-to-moderate impairment may have obscured true intervention effects [58].

Quality of life of stroke survivors

The analysed OT-related interventions, which focused on caregiver support, did not significantly improve survivor QoL. Theoretically, occupational therapy empowers stroke survivors and may enhance their sense of competence and agency, thereby potentially improving QoL [59]. Nevertheless, the analysed OT-related interventions primarily focused on caregiver stress management and skills training. This indirect approach may not adequately address key patient specific factors influencing quality of life, including self-perceived competence, autonomy and participation in meaningful activities. Meanwhile, QoL improvements typically demand sustained cumulative effort [60]. Regarding environment factors, rural settings elevated social isolation risks and limited service access often compound caregiver QoL reductions [61], potentially indirectly affecting survivors QoL.

Depression in stroke survivors

This meta-analysis found that OT-related interventions focus on did not significantly improve depression levels in stroke survivors. Our findings are consistent with Zachry et al.‘s systematic review of OT, very little strong evidence supporting the effectiveness of OT in alleviating depressive symptoms [62]. Psychological and social changes typically require long-term, stable, and high-quality intervention exposure to manifest. When stroke survivors continue to experience depressive symptoms, caregivers may face sustained emotional strain that could undermine the benefits of OT-related interventions support [7]. This suggests that optimising caregiver outcomes may require interventions that simultaneously address the psychological needs of both members of the dyad. Incorporating psychotherapy such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy within the OT practice framework appears essential as an adjunct for optimising depression management [63]. Therefore, collaborative care models involving occupational therapists, psychotherapists, and social workers may help address the broader psychosocial influence both stroke survivor and caregiver.

Insufficient compliance may mask the true effects of secondary outcomes, such as depression and family functioning, which did not reach statistical significance. This may be related to insufficient intervention exposure. Only 5 studies [20, 25, 29–31] reported intervention compliance with clear reporting of intervention frequency and analysis of influencing factors, and only 1 study [29] reported clear reporting of fidelity assurance measures. If caregivers are unable to complete the scheduled intervention frequency due to time conflicts, inconvenient transportation, epidemic lockdown, etc., the improvement of their depression symptoms, family functioning or caregiving benefit may not be fully reflected. On the other hand, differences in fidelity will also affect the effectiveness of interventions.

Subgroup analysis on clinical significance for caregiver

Subgroup analyses based on ICF framework categorisation revealed that interventions integrating Activities and Participation components with Personal Factors and Environmental Factors components achieved optimal caregiver burden reduction. Other studies have reported consistent findings. Gutenbrunner et al. [64] highlighted that effective caregiver burden reduction necessitates comprehensive strategies addressing personal capability enhancement, environmental support improvement and activity participation facilitation. Wang et al. [65] demonstrated that combined interventions incorporating both psychological support and practical skills training for caregivers produced significant reductions in distress, depressive symptoms, subjective burden, work-family conflict and financial stress, alongside notable improvements in self-efficacy and quality of life. Interventions exclusively targeting personal skills while failing to incorporate environmental modifications may demonstrate restricted effectiveness. Comprehensive interventions prove more effective than single component approaches in reducing caregiver stress [66]. Current research consistently demonstrates the superiority of multidimensional interventions based on the ICF framework, particularly modular approaches that combine activity participation, environmental modification and personal capacity building.

The lack of sustained statistical significance at follow-up, underscores the uncertainty regarding the long-term durability of these interventions. Similarly, Secinti et al. [67] observed that the burden reduction effects may weaken over time, requiring ongoing environmental support and individualised adaptations to sustain therapeutic benefits. Future practice should prioritise module flexibility and cultural adaptability. However, the number of studies included in each subgroup is relatively small, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Further research is needed to verify the intervention effects of different ICF combinations.

The included interventions exhibited considerable variation in duration, setting, delivery format, and components. Although we collectively categorized these as “OT -related interventions,” this umbrella term may obscure meaningful differences that influence effect sizes. While our ICF-based subgroup analysis offers preliminary insights into potentially effective intervention components, future research should further deconstruct these interventions to identify which specific elements, such as, skills training, environmental modification, or psychological support - are most critical for reducing caregiver burden. Again, these insights are based on a limited number of studies and remain vulnerable to residual confounding. They should therefore be regarded as exploratory guidance for designing future confirmatory trials, rather than conclusive evidence of superiority.

Limitations

First, current research presents several notable limitations. Methodologically, the inability to blind participants in most studies may lead to potential overestimation of effect sizes. Simultaneously, the follows up periods (≤ 6 months) in the included studies significantly limits the evaluation of long term intervention benefits, which is particularly given clinical guidelines recommending at least one year of monitoring. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for future intervention research to implement more rigorous adherence to standardised scientific methodologies to improve study credibility, reproducibility and clinical applicability.

Second, several critical limitations relate to research perspective and scope. Most studies fail to adequately consider key background variables among stroke caregivers, such as economic income, daily caregiving hours, pre-existing chronic conditions and gender, despite their well documented direct or indirect influence on caregiver stress. Reporting of caregiver baseline characteristics was incomplete. However, because such data were lacking in most included studies, we could not perform the subgroup analyses to explore their potential impact on the pooled effect estimates. For example, male caregivers may need additional counselling support to adjust to cognitive and emotional changes in their spouses following stroke [68].

Furthermore, current research-particularly in Eastern countries-while largely overlooking the potential integration of community resources. Culturally specific elements, such as China’s filial piety obligations and their potential to exacerbate caregiver burden, remain substantially under-investigated. Another notable limitation involves pervasive gaps in core outcome measurement: evaluations of caregiver social participation indicators including employment rates and community engagement are consistently omitted, while the clinical relevance of moderate improvements reported in certain studies remains unclear.

Fourth, regarding secondary outcome indicators, although the pooled effects did not reach statistical significance, these findings should be viewed as inconclusive rather than evidence of no effect. The analyses were critically underpowered due to the small number of included studies, their modest sample sizes, and short follow-up durations. Moreover, these outcomes were typically exploratory additions in studies designed and powered primarily for the primary outcome of caregiver burden. Consequently, we caution against overinterpreting these null results.

The fifth point should be pointed out that some outcome measures in this meta-analysis (caregiver depression, stroke survivor depression and follow up of caregiver anxiety) are based on only a few studies (≤ 2), with limited statistical power, and the results should be considered exploratory. These analyses did not show significant differences, which may not be due to ineffective intervention, but rather due to small sample size and insufficient testing power. More high-quality, large sample randomized controlled trials are needed in the future to validate these preliminary findings.

Finally, geographic and cultural universality is limited. The vast majority of research comes from high-income countries (Europe, North America, Australia), with limited representation of low and middle-income countries (LMICs)., severely limiting the generalizability of results to LMICs. Future research must prioritize conducting adaptive intervention trials in LMICs that are in line with local cultural, medical, and care realities.

A further limitation is that not all studies involved occupational therapists in designing, delivering, or supervising interventions. The absence of professional OT involvement may influence quality and fidelity, and this heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting findings.

Prospects and future directions

Based on our findings, we propose four key directions for future research and clinical practice:

Developing phased and integrated service models

Service delivery models should be refined to strengthen caregiver support systems, particularly during the vulnerable acute phase, where psychological crises often reduce receptiveness to structured training. A phased approach is recommended, dividing acute-phase training into two components: in-hospital foundational skills instruction (e.g., safe transfers and dressing techniques) and post-discharge community-based intensification through OT-related home visits or remote guidance. Implementing a continuous care chain, featuring 48-hour standardised pre-discharge training combined with home follow-up within four weeks post-discharge, could substantially enhance caregiver confidence.

Advancing methodological rigor and cultural adaptation

There is a pressing need for long-term studies (≥ 1 year) to evaluate the sustained efficacy of interventions. Research should employ mixed methods designs to explore individual and cultural variations in caregiver needs. Crucially, interventions must be culturally adapted; for instance, in Chinese contexts, models should account for the dual impact of filial piety, which can be both a source of obligation and a potential stressor.

Leveraging technology

OT interventions show promising potential to reduce healthcare expenditures, as demonstrated by García-Pérez et al.‘s [11] findings showing a 35% reduction in patient hospital visits. OT may serve as an effective cost-containment strategy by decreasing rehospitalisation rates, enabling caregivers to re-enter the workforce, generating economic benefits, and ultimately alleviating the often-overlooked emotional and social costs of care provision, thereby delivering comprehensive health advantages. Telehealth and digital tools (e.g., remote supervision, VR training) should be leveraged to improve accessibility and provide scalable, cost-effective solutions.

Refining outcome assessment

The refinement of assessment frameworks necessitates the incorporation of caregiver social participation metrics, such as employment rates and social activity engagement, as core outcome measures to address a significant evidence gap. Furthermore, given the established correlation between elevated caregiver distress and poorer survivor outcomes, additional research is required to clarify how late-phase rehabilitation enhances caregiver life circumstances and to examine potential bidirectional interactions between caregiver well-being and survivor prognosis [32].

Conclusions

Occupational therapy-related interventions demonstrate a small but statistically significant beneficial impact on alleviating the caregiving burden for stroke caregivers. Prioritizing multifaceted ICF-based interventions that address activities, participation, and contextual factors is crucial. Future research should prioritize the development of personalized and culturally sensitive applications of this model, focuses on evaluating long-term outcomes and integrating evidence into policy to advance the shift toward family-centered rehabilitation, thereby enhancing wellbeing for stroke caregivers.
