Effect of ultrasound-guided anterior sacral plexus block on catheter-related bladder discomfort after transurethral prostate surgery: protocol for a single-centre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Simeng Liu, Dawei Xie, Xiaofeng Duan, Yang Liu, Jia Jiang, Jianwen Wang, Anshi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCatheter-related bladder discomfort (CRBD) is a common and distressing complication after transurethral prostate surgery (TUPS). Many patients experience moderate to severe symptoms, which may delay recovery and reduce patient satisfaction. Currently, there are no standardised expert consensus statements or international guidelines for CRBD management. The ultrasound-guided anterior approach of sacral plexus block (anterior SPB) is a novel regional anaesthesia technique that may reduce CRBD through effects on multiple neural pathways. This trial is designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this technique in patients undergoing TUPS.Methods and analysisThis randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial will enrol 90 male patients aged 50–80 years who are scheduled for elective TUPS under general anaesthesia. Participants will be randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either bilateral anterior SPB with 0.25% ropivacaine (20 mL per side) or saline placebo after anaesthesia induction. The primary outcome is the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours postoperatively. Secondary outcomes include the overall incidence and severity of CRBD, postoperative pain scores, rescue analgesia requirement, opioid consumption, emergence agitation, time to ambulation, quality of recovery, patient satisfaction and length of hospital stay. Safety outcomes include block-related, surgery-related and systemic adverse events. Data will be analysed using both modified intention-to-treat and per-protocol approaches.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University on 25 April 2025 (2025-Ke-405-1). Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common condition in older men and often causes lower urinary tract symptoms that impair quality of life. For patients with persistent moderate or severe symptoms refractory to pharmacological therapy, transurethral prostate surgery (TUPS), including endoscopic resection and enucleation using monopolar, bipolar, plasmakinetic or laser techniques, remains the gold-standard surgical approach for relieving bladder outlet obstruction.1 2 After surgery, a large triple-lumen Foley catheter is routinely inserted to provide continuous bladder irrigation and prevent clot retention. Irrigation usually lasts for 12–24 hours, and the catheter is typically kept in place for 3–7 days. Although necessary, this practice often causes catheter-related bladder discomfort (CRBD), one of the most common postoperative complications.

CRBD is characterised by a burning sensation in the urethra, suprapubic discomfort and urgency to void. In severe cases, it may lead to suprapubic pain and impaired catheter drainage, a condition often referred to as bladder spasm.3 4 These symptoms are associated with increased postoperative agitation, a higher risk of bleeding, reduced patient satisfaction and a greater workload for healthcare staff.5,7 The incidence of CRBD varies widely depending on the type of surgery and patient population, generally ranging from 47% to 90%, with moderate-to-severe CRBD occurring in 27% to 55% of cases.8 9 Risk factors include male sex, age under 50 years, catheter sizes ≥18 Fr, gynaecological or perineal surgery, urologic surgery and transurethral procedures.10,12 Accordingly, patients undergoing TUPS are exposed to multiple risk factors, resulting in an overall CRBD incidence of approximately 75%, with moderate-to-severe cases accounting for up to 50%.5 6 Severe CRBD after TUPS may disrupt coagulated microvessels in the surgical field, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative bleeding. In severe cases, blood clots may obstruct the catheter, resulting in bladder tamponade and requiring reoperation.

The pathophysiology of CRBD involves involuntary detrusor contractions mediated by M3 muscarinic receptors and increased sensory input from bladder afferent nerves. The bladder and urethra have a complex nerve supply: sympathetic fibres (T11–L2) travel through the hypogastric nerves; parasympathetic fibres (S2–S4) travel through the pelvic splanchnic nerves and control detrusor contractions; somatic innervation is provided by the pudendal nerve, which originates from the sacral plexus and controls the external urethral sphincter and pelvic floor muscles. Catheter irritation activates these pathways, producing discomfort and involuntary detrusor contractions.13,17

Currently, there are no expert consensus statements or international guidelines for CRBD prevention and management.18 Drug therapy is the mainstay of treatment and includes antimuscarinic agents (eg, oxybutynin, tolterodine and solifenacin), antiepileptic drugs (eg, gabapentin and pregabalin), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and anaesthetic agents such as ketamine, tramadol and dexmedetomidine.35 9 10 15 19,22 Although these treatments can be effective, their clinical utility is often limited by adverse effects, such as dry mouth, constipation, excessive sedation, nausea, vomiting, blurred vision and haemodynamic instability. In recent years, regional anaesthesia techniques such as sacral canal block, dorsal penile nerve block and pudendal nerve block have been used to reduce CRBD.1019 23,25 These techniques have fewer systemic side effects than pharmacological treatments but also have drawbacks, including inconsistent efficacy because they primarily block somatic pathways, procedural inconvenience, longer performance time and higher infection risk because the puncture sites are close to the perineum.

In 2022, Li et al26 introduced a novel anterior approach of sacral plexus block (SPB). Compared with conventional posterior approaches, this technique can be performed with the patient in the supine position, avoiding repositioning and potentially improving procedural convenience. Anatomically, at the level of the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS), the sacral plexus lies posterior to the psoas muscle and medial to the ilium.27 Real-time ultrasound guidance allows identification of the AIIS, ilium, iliopsoas muscle and external iliac vessels, as well as visualisation of in-plane needle advancement and injectate spread in the space deep to the psoas muscle.26

This anatomical relationship is relevant to CRBD. In anterior SPB, local anaesthetic is injected at the posteromedial surface of the iliopsoas muscle for deep diffusion. The injectate may spread within the anterior piriformis space, where the sacral plexus and pelvic splanchnic nerves involved in parasympathetic innervation of the bladder and urethra are located, while the pelvic plexus lies within the adjacent urogenital fascia.17 28 Therefore, anterior SPB may potentially affect both somatic pathways, including the pudendal nerve, and autonomic pathways involved in bladder and urethral function. This provides an anatomical and mechanistic rationale for investigating anterior SPB as a potential strategy to reduce CRBD after TUPS.

Early studies have supported the feasibility and safety of this anterior approach and related anterior iliopsoas space techniques in healthy volunteers and patients undergoing hip or prostate surgery.29,32 In particular, Gong et al reported no nerve block-related complications in a volunteer study of anterior lumbar and sacral plexus blockade,29 and Shao et al reported reduced early postoperative pain and a lower incidence of CRBD after laparoscopic radical prostatectomy, also with no block-related complications.32 However, no randomised controlled trial has specifically evaluated whether anterior SPB can prevent CRBD in patients undergoing TUPS.

Objectives

This study aims to determine whether ultrasound-guided anterior SPB can reduce the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours after TUPS. In addition, we will evaluate whether this technique can lower the overall incidence and severity of CRBD, relieve postoperative pain, reduce rescue analgesia requirements and opioid consumption, decrease the incidence of emergence agitation, shorten the time to ambulation, improve recovery quality and patient satisfaction and shorten postoperative hospital stay. The study will also evaluate the safety and feasibility of anterior SPB by recording block-related and surgery-related complications.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial will be conducted at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital Affiliated to Capital Medical University, a tertiary teaching hospital in China. The study protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 statement, and the trial results will be reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The trial flow chart and participant timeline are shown in figure 1 and online supplemental file 1, respectively.

Study flow diagram. CRBD, catheter-related bladder discomfort; mITT, modified intention-to-treat; NRS, Numerical Rating Scale; PP, per-protocol; QoR-15, Quality of Recovery-15; RSAS, Riker Sedation-Agitation Scale; SPB, sacral plexus block; TUPS, transurethral prostate surgery.

Participants

From 22 May 2025 to 28 February 2026, 90 adult male patients scheduled for elective TUPS, including bipolar resection, laser techniques and plasmakinetic surgery, under general anaesthesia will be enrolled.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are age 50–80 years; scheduled for elective TUPS under general anaesthesia; body mass index (BMI) 18–35 kg/m²; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; and provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria include prostate cancer; severe urinary tract infection; overactive or neurogenic bladder; recent treatment with M-receptor antagonists or β3-adrenergic receptor agonists (within 2 weeks); preoperative suprapubic catheterisation; history of prostate or bladder neck surgery; allergy to ropivacaine or other local anaesthetics; infection at the puncture site; coagulopathy or bleeding disorders; severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; long-term opioid use; and psychiatric disorders or language barriers that prevent proper communication.

Informed consent and study withdrawal

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants 1 day before surgery. Investigators will explain the study purpose, procedures, follow-up assessments, potential benefits and risks. Participation is voluntary, and refusal to participate or withdrawal of consent will not affect the participant’s standard medical care. A copy of the Participant Consent Form is provided as online supplemental file 2.

If a participant withdraws consent, no further study-specific assessments will be performed after withdrawal. Data collected before withdrawal may be retained and used for analysis unless the participant requests otherwise, in accordance with ethical and regulatory requirements.

Predefined post-randomisation conditions include cancellation of surgery, surgical duration >180 min, blood loss >600 mL, bladder perforation during surgery, change in intraoperative diagnosis or surgical method, abnormal anatomy or inadequate ultrasound visibility preventing safe block performance, catheter removal within 24 hours, unplanned reoperation within 24 hours or other serious adverse events (SAEs). These events will be recorded and managed according to the prespecified definitions of the analysis populations.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants will be randomly allocated to either the SPB group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated list of random numbers (Excel; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). Randomisation codes will be enclosed in sequentially numbered, identical, opaque sealed envelopes. On the day of surgery, a research assistant not involved in the trial will open the randomisation envelope according to the study number, prepare the medication (40 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine or 0.9% normal saline, both colourless, odourless and transparent) in identical syringes and give it to the anaesthesiologist. Participants, anaesthesiologists, urologists and outcome assessors will remain blinded to group allocation.

Emergency unblinding will be carried out if a life-threatening SAE occurs and immediate knowledge of group allocation is required, if unexpected medical risks arise and unblinding is deemed necessary to protect the participant, or if regulatory authorities decide to terminate the trial early for safety or efficacy reasons.

Interventions

All patients will receive routine general anaesthesia and surgery. Before surgery, investigators will explain the typical symptoms of CRBD and surgical-site pain, and instruct patients on how to use the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS).

On arrival in the operating room, intravenous access will be established, and standard monitoring will begin, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) and depth of anaesthesia monitoring using bispectral index (BIS). General anaesthesia will be induced with sufentanil (0.2 µg/kg), propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). After loss of consciousness, a laryngeal mask airway will be inserted. Mechanical ventilation will be volume-controlled with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 12–16 breaths/min and a fresh gas flow of 1 L/min, aiming to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mm Hg. Anaesthesia will be maintained with total intravenous anaesthesia using propofol at 4–12 mg/kg/hour and remifentanil at 0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min. Dosages will be adjusted to maintain a BIS of 40–60 and to keep heart rate and mean arterial pressure (MAP) within ±20% of baseline values (defined as the measurements taken 5 min before induction). Vasoactive agents will be given as needed.

Sacral plexus block

Ultrasound-guided anterior SPB will be performed after induction of anaesthesia and before surgical site preparation by an attending anaesthesiologist with more than 10 years of experience in ultrasound-guided nerve blocks. The same ultrasound system (Anesus ME7P, Mindray, Shenzhen, China) will be used for all procedures.

All blocks will be performed under continuous real-time ultrasound guidance. The patient will remain in the supine position. After sterile preparation, a low-frequency curvilinear transducer (C5-1S) will be placed transversely at the level of the anterior superior iliac spine, with the scanning depth set to 5–8 cm. The probe will then be moved slightly caudally to identify the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS), ilium, external iliac vessels and iliopsoas muscle. A 22G, 90 mm needle (Tuoren, Henan, China) will be inserted in-plane from the lateral end of the probe. Under real-time ultrasound visualisation, the needle tip will be advanced through the iliacus and psoas muscles along the medial surface of the ilium until it reaches the space deep to the psoas muscle. Needle advancement will be adjusted as needed to maintain visualisation of the needle tip and avoid vascular or visceral structures. After negative aspiration for blood, a small amount of saline will be injected to confirm the needle-tip position. Then, 20 mL of study medication (0.25% ropivacaine in the SPB group or normal saline in the control group) will be slowly injected while its spread along the deep iliopsoas plane is observed (figure 2). Correct block performance will be assessed procedurally based on needle-tip placement and injectate spread. The same procedure will be repeated on the contralateral side.

Anterior approach for ultrasound-guided sacral plexus block. AIIS, anterior inferior iliac spine; ASIS, anterior superior iliac spine; EIA, external iliac artery.

Postoperative management

At the end of surgery, propofol and remifentanil infusions will be discontinued, and ondansetron 4 mg will be administered. The surgeon will insert a 22 Fr three-way silicone Foley catheter (uroVision GmbH, Bad Aibling, Germany) lubricated with lidocaine gel. The catheter balloon will be inflated with 40 mL of distilled water, and continuous bladder irrigation will be maintained for at least 12 hours with gentle traction on the catheter.

On emergence from anaesthesia, sugammadex 2 mg/kg will be administered to reverse residual neuromuscular blockade. The laryngeal mask will be removed when the patient is awake and responsive. The moment of laryngeal mask removal will be recorded as time zero (0 hour) postoperatively. Patients will then be transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) for 1 hour of observation and subsequently returned to the ward for continued postoperative monitoring.

Postoperative rescue analgesic treatment will be standardised as follows. Before rescue medication is administered, symptoms will be classified as either CRBD or surgical-site pain. CRBD grade ≥2 will be identified by spontaneously reported urgency to void or suprapubic discomfort. Surgical-site pain will be defined as sharp pain localised to the urethra and assessed using the NRS. During the PACU stay, intravenous tramadol 1.5 mg/kg will be given for CRBD grade ≥2, while intravenous sufentanil 0.1 µg/kg will be administered for surgical-site pain with an NRS score ≥4. After transfer to the ward, CRBD grade ≥2 within the first 24 hours will be treated with rectal indomethacin 50 mg as first-line rescue treatment; intravenous tramadol 1.5 mg/kg will be used instead for patients with contraindications to NSAIDs. Patients will also have access to patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) for opioid analgesia, consisting of sufentanil 1 µg/mL with a 2 mL bolus dose, a 15 min lockout interval and no background infusion.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics will include demographic and clinical information, including age, weight, height, ASA physical status, primary diagnosis, comorbidities, previous surgeries, laboratory test results (complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, electrolytes and coagulation profile), preoperative urinary catheterisation and prostate volume. Prostate volume will be measured preoperatively using ultrasound and calculated with the ellipsoid formula: length×width×height×0.52.

Intraoperative data will include the specific surgical modality or energy device used for TUPS, such as bipolar resection, thulium laser, holmium laser or plasmakinetic surgery, as well as the duration of surgery and anaesthesia, estimated blood loss, total fluid volume administered and the types and doses of anaesthetics and vasoactive drugs used. Heart rate and NIBP will be recorded every 5 min throughout anaesthesia. Any significant intraoperative events will be documented.

Postoperative assessments will be performed at predefined time points after laryngeal mask removal, which will be defined as time zero. The main repeated assessments will be conducted at 0, 0.5, 1, 4, 6, 12 and 24 hours postoperatively, as summarised in online supplemental file 1.

CRBD will be evaluated using the 4-point scale developed by Agarwal et al21 at 0, 0.5, 1, 4, 6, 12 and 24 hours postoperatively. Grade 0 indicates no discomfort even when directly questioned; grade 1 indicates mild discomfort reported only when questioned; grade 2 indicates moderate discomfort reported spontaneously with an urge to void or suprapubic discomfort; and grade 3 indicates severe discomfort accompanied by obvious behavioural responses, such as limb movement, intense verbal reactions or attempts to remove the catheter. Postoperative pain will be assessed at the same seven time points using the NRS, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 indicates the worst imaginable pain. Sedation and agitation will be evaluated using the Riker Sedation-Agitation Scale (RSAS) at 0, 0.5 and 1 hour postoperatively. The RSAS ranges from 1 (unarousable) to 7 (dangerous agitation).33 Lower limb motor function will be assessed using the modified Bromage scale, where 0 indicates the ability to move the hips, knees and ankles; 1 indicates inability to move the hips but ability to move the knees and ankles; 2 indicates inability to move the hips and knees but ability to move the ankles; and 3 indicates complete inability to move the hips, knees and ankles.34

Rescue analgesic use will be recorded, including the time of administration, drug type and dosage. Opioid consumption will be calculated for the intervals of 0–12 hours and 12–24 hours after surgery and converted to intravenous morphine equivalents, with 10 mg of tramadol or 1 µg of sufentanil equating to 1 mg of morphine. Data on PCIA use will also be collected.

Additional postoperative information will include time to first ambulation, the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score and patient satisfaction at 24 hours postoperatively, and postoperative hospital length of stay. The QoR-15 ranges from 0 (extremely poor recovery) to 150 (excellent recovery), with higher scores indicating better recovery.35 Patient satisfaction will be assessed using a 7-point Likert scale (1=very dissatisfied, 2=dissatisfied, 3=somewhat dissatisfied, 4=neutral, 5=somewhat satisfied, 6=satisfied, 7=very satisfied). Postoperative hospital length of stay will be defined as the time from transfer back to the ward until hospital discharge.

Data management and monitoring

All investigators responsible for data collection and outcome assessment will receive standardised training before the trial begins. Study data will be recorded on case report forms at predefined time points. An electronic database, protected by passwords and managed by the statistical service, will be established for data entry and analysis. After study completion, the original case report forms will be securely stored at the study centre for 5 years.

To ensure data quality, a data monitoring committee (DMC) consisting of two members independent of the study team has been established. The DMC will provide independent oversight of the trial by conducting comprehensive reviews of participant safety, data integrity and data storage throughout the study period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours postoperatively, defined as the presence of CRBD rated grade 2 or 3 at any postoperative time point within the first 24 hours.

Secondary outcomes include the overall incidence of CRBD within 24 hours postoperatively, defined as CRBD grade 1 or higher at any recorded time point, and CRBD severity at 0, 0.5, 1, 4, 6, 12 and 24 hours postoperatively. Other outcomes include NRS pain scores at the same seven time points; the incidence of emergence agitation, defined as an RSAS score ≥5; the proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesia; intravenous morphine-equivalent opioid consumption during 0–12 hours and 12–24 hours postoperatively; time to first ambulation; QoR-15 score at 24 hours after surgery; patient satisfaction score; and postoperative hospital length of stay.

Safety outcomes will include block-related, surgery-related and systemic adverse events. Block-related complications include local anaesthetic systemic toxicity; haematoma or infection involving the skin puncture site or deep needle pathway, including psoas muscle haematoma; visceral or vascular injury related to needle misplacement; and lower-limb motor blockade, defined as a Bromage score ≥2. Patients will be clinically monitored for signs suggestive of puncture-related bleeding, infection, visceral or vascular injury or prolonged motor blockade. Additional ultrasound or other imaging examinations will be performed when clinically indicated. Surgery-related adverse events include bladder perforation, postoperative bleeding requiring reoperation and hyponatraemia. Systemic adverse events include intraoperative hypotension or hypertension, defined as a MAP change of more than 20% from baseline; tachycardia (>100 bpm); bradycardia (<50 bpm); postoperative fever; nausea and vomiting; dizziness; shivering; pruritus; and excessive sedation, defined as an RSAS score ≤2.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using Power Analysis and Sample Size (PASS) software V.15 (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA) for comparison of two proportions. The assumed control-group incidence was informed by previous studies of CRBD after transurethral prostate procedures, while the expected intervention-group incidence was informed by preliminary pilot observations and re-analysis of a related institutional trial involving anterior SPB.5 6 32 Accordingly, the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours was estimated to be 50% in the control group and 20% in the anterior SPB group. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power (β=0.20), 36 patients per group were required. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final sample size was set at 45 patients per group, resulting in a total of 90 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS V.25.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables will be assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables will be expressed as numbers and percentages. Between-group comparisons will be performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Treatment effects will be reported with corresponding 95% CIs where appropriate.

The primary outcome, the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours, will be analysed as a binary patient-level outcome using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Repeated CRBD severity scores at 0, 0.5, 1, 4, 6, 12 and 24 hours will be analysed using a generalised estimating equation model for ordinal repeated-measures data, including treatment group, time point and group-by-time interaction. Time will be treated as a categorical variable. Other secondary outcomes, including repeated NRS pain scores, will be analysed using methods appropriate to their data type, distribution and repeated-measures structure.

Efficacy analyses will be conducted in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population and in the per-protocol (PP) population, with the mITT analysis considered primary. The mITT population will include randomised participants who receive the assigned intervention, undergo the target TUPS procedure and have primary outcome data available. The PP population will include mITT participants without major protocol deviations or predefined post-randomisation conditions that may substantially affect primary outcome assessment. Safety analyses will be performed in the safety set, defined as all participants who receive the study intervention and have safety data available.

Missing data will be summarised by randomised group, including the amount and reasons for missingness. The primary efficacy analysis will use available primary outcome data without routine imputation. For repeated secondary outcomes, all available observed measurements will be included in the repeated-measures models. If post hoc comparisons are performed at individual postoperative time points, Bonferroni correction will be applied. All tests will be two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Discussion

CRBD is a common and distressing complication following TUPS and may adversely affect postoperative comfort, recovery and patient satisfaction. Although several pharmacological and regional anaesthesia-based approaches have been used for CRBD prevention or treatment, their clinical application may be limited by adverse effects, technical complexity or inconsistent efficacy. Therefore, this trial is designed to evaluate whether ultrasound-guided anterior SPB can reduce postoperative CRBD after TUPS, using a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled design and multidimensional outcome assessments.

A key strength of this protocol is its randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled design, which minimises bias and enhances the reliability of the findings. This study will use a multidimensional assessment framework, with patients evaluated at seven postoperative time points using validated scales and indices, including the 4-point CRBD scale, Riker Sedation-Agitation Scale, pain scores, QoR-15 questionnaire, perioperative haemodynamic monitoring and systematic documentation of adverse events. This approach will enable a comprehensive evaluation of both the efficacy and safety of ultrasound-guided anterior SPB.

Drawing on high-quality CRBD studies,36 37 we selected the incidence of moderate-to-severe CRBD within 24 hours postoperatively as the primary endpoint, defined as the occurrence of CRBD grade 2 or 3 at any predefined postoperative assessment time point within the first 24 hours. We focused on moderate-to-severe CRBD rather than overall CRBD incidence because mild CRBD (grade 1) is usually tolerable and often reported only when patients are directly questioned, whereas moderate-to-severe CRBD is more clinically relevant and often requires active intervention. We chose the cumulative incidence within 24 hours rather than a single immediate postoperative time point because previous studies and our pilot observations suggest that some patients may develop moderate-to-severe CRBD 1–2 hours after surgery, possibly due to residual anaesthetic effects or paroxysmal catheter-induced bladder spasms.9 37 In addition, CRBD is most common within the first 6–8 hours after transurethral procedures and typically decreases to mild or no discomfort by 12–24 hours postoperatively.10 23 However, this cumulative binary endpoint does not fully capture temporal changes in CRBD severity. Therefore, CRBD severity at seven predefined postoperative time points will also be analysed as a secondary repeated-measures outcome.

A recent prospective cohort study evaluating intraoperative strategies to reduce CRBD after robot-assisted radical prostatectomy demonstrated that peri-vesical local infiltration reduced the incidence of postoperative CRBD by nearly 50% and appeared more effective than intravenous clonidine, ketamine or dorsal penile nerve block.38 The presumed targets of peri-vesical infiltration are the pelvic plexus and postganglionic parasympathetic fibres supplying the bladder. These findings suggest that modulation of pelvic autonomic neural pathways may play an important role in preventing CRBD. Anatomically, the sacral plexus, pelvic splanchnic nerves and pelvic plexus are closely related within the pelvic fascial compartments, and local anaesthetic spread via anterior SPB may potentially affect both somatic and autonomic innervation of the bladder and urethra.17 28 39 This provides an anatomical and mechanistic rationale for evaluating this technique as a potential strategy for CRBD prevention. Although spinal anaesthesia can also reduce CRBD, it is a more invasive technique with stricter contraindications, may delay early mobilisation and typically has a much shorter duration of action than single-injection regional nerve blocks. In this study, 40 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine was selected as a relatively low-concentration, high-volume dosing strategy to maximise fascial spread while minimising the risk of lower-limb motor block, supported by the findings of Shao et al.32 Future studies could further explore optimal dosing and concentrations.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this is a single-centre study including only male patients undergoing TUPS, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, the sample size assumption was informed by previous literature, preliminary pilot observations and re-analysis of a related institutional trial, but the pilot sample was small and the related trial involved a different surgical population and a combined regional anaesthesia technique. If the true treatment effect is smaller than expected, the study may be underpowered to detect a statistically significant difference in the primary outcome. Third, although the block will be performed by a single experienced anaesthesiologist using standardised ultrasound equipment and techniques, block efficacy may still vary because of individual anatomical differences or technical factors. Postoperative sensory testing will not be used as a formal confirmation method to preserve blinding; therefore, the absence of direct functional confirmation of block success remains a limitation. Finally, routine postoperative imaging will not be performed in all asymptomatic participants; therefore, asymptomatic deep needle-pathway haematoma cannot be completely excluded, although clinically suspected complications will prompt further ultrasound or other imaging examinations.
