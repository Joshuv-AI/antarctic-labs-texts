Indonesian update of urethral stricture characteristics and treatment patterns

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical profile and treatment patterns of urethral stricture (US) in Indonesia, the world’s fourth most populous country. Since 2012, Indonesia has expanded access to reconstructive urology services, supported by 19 genitourinary consultants and multiple referral centers nationwide.MethodsA multicenter retrospective study was conducted involving urologists from 38 hospitals across Indonesia. Data from patients treated for US between 2019 and 2023 were collected, including demographics, stricture characteristics (site and etiology), and treatment modalities. Stricture length was recorded but excluded from the primary analysis due to inconsistent reporting across participating centers. Surgical procedures were categorized as direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU), urethral dilatation, excision and primary anastomosis (EPA), and substitution urethroplasty.ResultsA total of 3,055 patients were included, with a mean age of 49.3 years (SD 18.8; range 1–91); 99.3% were male. The leading causes of US were iatrogenic (38.4%), trauma (38.0%), and infection (17.7%), followed by idiopathic (5.4%), and congenital (0.5%). The bulbar urethra was the most commonly affected anatomical site (24.3%), followed by membranous (13.9%), pendular (7.6%), and panurethral strictures (11.6%). Surgical reconstruction was the primary approach in 65.7% of cases, including Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) in 33.1% and urethroplasty in 32.6%. Augmentation urethroplasty using oral mucosal grafts was performed in 16.6%. Endoscopic management—primarily Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU) and dilatation—was employed in 30.5% of patients.ConclusionsReconstructive surgery, particularly urethroplasty, is now the dominant modality for US management in Indonesia. The predominance of trauma-related urethral strictures reflects epidemiological patterns commonly observed in developing and middle-income countries. However, a national registry and expanded training in reconstructive urology are crucial to further enhance outcomes and standardize care delivery across Indonesia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02082-9.Keywords: Urethral stricture, Reconstructive urology, Treatment patterns, Indonesia, Epidemiology, Urethroplasty, Multicentre

Introduction

Urethral stricture (US) is a significant urological condition characterized by the narrowing of the urethra, which restricts urine flow and often leads to a range of complications, including recurrent urinary tract infections, bladder dysfunction, and potential kidney damage if left untreated [1]. The condition primarily affects men and can severely impact the quality of life by causing painful urination, urinary retention, and in severe cases, complete urinary obstruction [2]. Globally, the etiology of urethral stricture varies widely, with common causes including trauma, infections, iatrogenic injuries (particularly from catheterization or surgical interventions), and congenital abnormalities. Indonesia, as one of the world’s most populous countries with an estimated 279 million citizens, faces unique challenges in addressing urethral stricture treatment comprehensively across its healthcare system [3]. Given the widespread nature of this condition and the varied causes across different populations, there is a pressing need to understand the epidemiology and treatment patterns of urethral stricture within Indonesia [4].

Despite the condition’s prevalence, data on urethral stricture in Indonesia remains limited. This scarcity of information can be attributed to several factors, including the historically decentralized healthcare system, limited subspecialist training in reconstructive urology, and lack of national registries for tracking the incidence and management of urethral stricture cases [1, 4]. In recent years, however, there has been substantial progress in Indonesia’s healthcare infrastructure, with the establishment of more specialized urological centers and an increasing number of genitourinary consultants who can manage complex cases. Since 2012, the country has developed multiple reconstructive urology referral centers, which are gradually improving access to specialized care for urethral stricture patients [5]. However, comprehensive data on treatment modalities, patient demographics, and etiological factors remain sparse in published literature, making it challenging to identify patterns or standardize treatment protocols across the nation [4, 5].

Internationally, urethral stricture management typically involves both minimally invasive techniques and complex surgical interventions [5]. Initial treatment often includes urethral dilatation or direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU), which are generally less invasive but have high recurrence rates [6]. In cases where strictures are complex or recurrent, reconstructive surgery, such as urethroplasty, is often preferred due to its more durable outcomes [7]. Techniques like Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) and augmentation urethroplasty using oral mucosal grafts are frequently employed in advanced cases [8]. However, these procedures require specialized skills and resources, which can vary significantly in availability and accessibility across Indonesia’s vast geography [4]. Understanding the current treatment patterns, including the prevalence of different procedures like urethroplasty and other choices of treatments, is critical to enhancing the national approach to urethral stricture management [4, 5].

To address these gaps, this study aims to analyze the clinical characteristics and treatment patterns of urethral stricture patients in Indonesia by examining multicentric data collected from 2019 to 2023. A total of 38 hospitals and 3055 patients across the country were included in the study, capturing a broad spectrum of cases from various geographic and socioeconomic backgrounds. Key parameters such as age, gender, site and length of the stricture, etiology, and the types of procedures performed were meticulously recorded and analyzed. This research provides one of the most extensive analyses of urethral stricture treatment in Indonesia to date, offering valuable insights into the prevalence and distribution of different etiological factors and treatment choices across diverse patient populations. By understanding these trends, healthcare providers and policymakers can develop more targeted strategies to optimize urethral stricture treatment nationwide.

This study aimed to provide a national overview of urethral stricture disease in Indonesia by describing patient demographics, etiologies, anatomical distribution, and contemporary treatment utilization patterns using data from a multicenter registry.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a retrospective, multicenter design to evaluate urethral stricture’s clinical characteristics and treatment patterns in Indonesia. Data were collected from 38 nationwide hospitals, representing diverse geographic regions to capture a broad range of cases. These hospitals were selected based on their affiliation with the Indonesian Urological Association and their capability to perform urethral reconstructive procedures. This study was conducted in coordination with an interest group of urethral reconstruction specialists in Indonesia. The patients’ data were reported through “URODATANESIA”, a standardized reporting data system of urological patients in Indonesia. This study follows the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. This study is a retrospective, registry-based, multicenter observational analysis utilizing data from the URODATANESIA registry, a nationwide standardized electronic reporting system for urological diseases in Indonesia.

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from medical records of patients diagnosed with urethral stricture and who received treatment between January 2019 and December 2023. Urologists from participating hospitals were invited to contribute to the study by providing data on all relevant cases within the study period. Each patient’s demographic information (age, gender), clinical characteristics (stricture site and length), etiology, and treatment method were extracted and anonymized to protect patient confidentiality. Inclusion criteria were patients with complete records that specified all required data points, while patients with incomplete records were excluded from the analysis. Data consistency across 38 centers was ensured through “URODATANESIA”, a nationwide standardized electronic reporting system that uses uniform variable definitions. Prior to data entry, all contributors received written instructions and a standardized data dictionary. Cases were included consecutively during the study period to reduce selection bias. A total of 109 incomplete cases were excluded. Stricture length was not included in the present analysis due to inconsistent reporting across participating centers, resulting in a substantial proportion of missing data. To avoid biased estimation and preserve data integrity, stricture length was excluded from the primary analysis. Both pediatric and adult patients were analyzed collectively, although their proportions are reported. Pediatric patients were included to reflect real-world national practice within a comprehensive nationwide registry. Given the distinct etiological profile and management considerations in children, pediatric cases were predefined and analyzed as a separate subgroup. This study adhered to the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Stricture classifications

Urethral strictures were classified as partial or total according to the standardized definitions used in the URODATANESIA registry. Partial strictures were defined as non-obliterative lesions with residual luminal patency, corresponding to non-obliterative strictures described in the international literature. Total strictures were defined as completely obstructive lesions without an identifiable lumen, corresponding to obliterative strictures.

Variables

The study collected various types of data to provide a comprehensive analysis of urethral stricture characteristics and treatment patterns in Indonesia. Demographic data, including each patient’s age and gender, were recorded to identify any patterns in the distribution of urethral strictures across different age groups and between genders. Etiology of the urethral strictures was categorized based on the underlying causes, which were grouped into six main categories: iatrogenic, trauma, infection, idiopathic, and congenital. This categorization helped to identify the most common causes of urethral strictures within the study population.

The anatomical site of the stricture was also documented, with specific focus on areas such as the bulbar urethra, membranous urethra, pendular urethra, prostatic urethra, bladder neck, meatal stenosis, and cases of total stricture. By detailing the stricture location, the study aimed to determine whether certain sites were more prone to strictures and to assess any correlation with etiology.

Treatment methods were categorized into surgical and non-surgical approaches. Surgical interventions included Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) as well as various types of urethroplasty, such as augmentation urethroplasty with oral mucosal graft, dorsal onlay, ventral onlay, Johanson’s approach, one-sided dissection, and tubularized urethroplasty. Other surgical procedures documented included perineostomy, ileocaecovesicostomy, and endoscopy. Non-surgical approaches included Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU) and urethral dilatation, which are commonly used for short strictures or as initial interventions.

Finally, treatment outcomes were recorded, specifically noting whether reconstruction was performed and identifying the specific procedures used in each case. Additional details on outcomes were documented where available, providing insight into the effectiveness of various treatment methods and the overall success rate of surgical and non-surgical interventions in managing urethral strictures. This comprehensive approach allowed the study to offer a detailed overview of current practices and outcomes in the treatment of urethral strictures in Indonesia.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data collected. Continuous variables, such as age, were reported as mean values with standard deviations, while categorical variables, such as gender, etiology, stricture site, and treatment type, were summarized as frequencies and percentages. This study focused on the descriptive nature of urethral stricture data in Indonesia, providing the latest update of demographic current choices of urethral stricture treatments all across Indonesia, showcasing the general and wide image of the current treatment patterns of urethral stricture in Indonesia. Main proportions and means are presented with 95% confidence intervals. Exploratory regional comparisons (Java vs. non-Java) were conducted using chi-square tests. Multivariate modeling was considered; however, given this study’s descriptive primary aim, such analyses are planned for subsequent research.

Outcomes

This study was designed as a descriptive analysis of treatment utilization patterns. Objective postoperative outcomes, including treatment success, functional improvement, or long-term durability, were not evaluated due to the absence of standardized follow-up data within the registry.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) of each participating hospital. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of patient data. All data collection followed ethical guidelines to ensure patient confidentiality and data security, complying with Indonesian health research regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 3055 patients diagnosed with urethral stricture (US) were included in this study, with data collected from 38 hospitals across Indonesia. 109 patients were excluded due to the incomplete data reported. The mean age of the patients was 49.3 years (SD = 18.8), with an age range spanning from 1 to 91 years. Among these patients, 3033 (99.3%) were male, while only 22 (0.7%) were female, reflecting the higher prevalence of urethral stricture in males (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Pediatric urethral stricture

From the national multicenter database, 157 pediatric patients aged 0–17 years were identified, accounting for 5.1% of the total urethral stricture cohort. The youngest patient was 1 year old, and the oldest was 17 years old, with a mean age of 11.7 ± 4.1 years and a median age of 13 years (IQR 9–16). Pediatric urethral stricture demonstrated a marked male predominance, with 156 male patients (99.4%) and one female patient (0.6%) included in this subgroup.

Trauma was the most common etiology of pediatric urethral stricture, followed by iatrogenic causes. Congenital strictures, including those associated with hypospadias, constituted a smaller but clinically relevant proportion. Infection-related and idiopathic strictures were uncommon, and a limited number of cases had undocumented etiologies. This etiological pattern differs from adult populations by demonstrating a relatively higher contribution of trauma and congenital factors.

Definitive reconstructive surgery represented the dominant management strategy in pediatric patients, performed in 91.7% of cases. Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) was the most frequently utilized reconstructive technique, reflecting the predominance of short-segment strictures suitable for primary repair. A subset of patients required reconstructive surgery with a pelvic approach, including inferior pubectomy and combined abdominoperineal reconstruction, which were selectively employed for complex posterior urethral strictures and pelvic fracture urethral injuries (PFUI).

The majority of remaining cases were managed using other urethroplasty techniques, encompassing a heterogeneous group of substitution and augmentation procedures, such as buccal mucosal graft urethroplasty, staged repairs, and other reconstructive variants tailored to stricture length, location, and etiology. Endoscopic management, primarily Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU), was used selectively and accounted for a minority of pediatric cases (Table 2).

Etiology of urethral stricture

The most common cause of urethral stricture was iatrogenic, identified in 38.4% of cases, followed closely by trauma, which accounted for 38% of cases. Infection-related strictures were noted in 17.7% of patients. Less common etiologies included idiopathic causes (5.4%), and congenital origins (0.5%). This distribution indicates that iatrogenic and trauma-related causes are the primary contributors to urethral stricture in the Indonesian population (Table 3 and Fig. 2).

Stricture location and grade

The predominance of partial strictures (88.4%) reflects the standardized classification in URODATANESIA, which categorizes strictures based on the presence of any maintained urethral lumen rather than radiologic obliteration. Anatomical stricture locations were classified as mutually exclusive categories. The bulbar urethra emerges as the most frequently affected site, accounting for 48.1% of cases. Other notable sites include the membranous urethra (14.4%) and the bulbomembranous junction (11.1%). Additionally, panurethral strictures are observed in 11.2% of cases. Panurethral strictures were recorded as a distinct category representing involvement of the entire anterior urethra and were not counted within individual segmental locations. Less common sites include the pendular urethra (7.6%), the external urethral meatus (4.1%), the navicular fossa (1.0%), the prostatic urethra (1.7%), the bladder neck (0.5%), the pendulo-bulbar (0.2%), and with sporadic involvement of the transsphincteric area (0.1%). Regarding the severity of strictures, the majority are classified as partial strictures (88.4%), while total strictures constitute a smaller proportion (11.6%) (Tables 4 and 5).

Treatment patterns

Table 6 presents the distribution of treatment modalities employed for urethral stricture cases in Indonesia, based on a total of 3055 documented cases. The therapeutic approaches range from minimally invasive interventions to complex reconstructive procedures. Non-reconstructive procedures include cystostomy, performed in 104 cases (3.5%), and urethral dilatation, comprising Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU) in 719 cases (23.6%) and conventional urethral dilatation in 212 cases (6.8%). Reconstructive surgery represents the most commonly adopted approach, with Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) accounting for 1,011 cases (33.1%). Various technical adaptations of EPA were documented, including bulbar mobilization (0.8%), crural separation (3.9%), inferior pubectomy (4.1%), and supracrural rerouting (0.4%). Urethral perineostomy was performed in 78 cases (2.5%), while ileocecovesicostomy, a rare and more radical option, was noted in only 2 cases (0.1%). Additional reconstructive techniques included non-transecting urethroplasty (0.8%), tubularized grafts (0.5%), vaginal flap urethroplasty (0.3%), and Johanson’s staged procedure (2.1%). Meatoplasty, often used for distal strictures, was recorded in 80 cases (2.6%). Augmentation with buccal mucosa graft (BMG) was utilized in 451 cases in total, reflecting significant application of graft-based techniques. These included dorsal onlay (6.4%), ventral onlay (2.4%), augmented anastomotic urethroplasty (2.2%), one-side dissection (2.4%), dorsal inlay (1.3%), ventral inlay (0.4%), and double-faced grafts (0.3%).

Treatment modalities of the urethral stricture cases in Indonesia

Among the 22 female patients included in this cohort, the most frequently performed treatments were reconstructive procedures, totaling 19 cases (86.4%). Within this group, buccal mucosal graft (BMG) urethroplasty accounted for several approaches: dorsal onlay BMG in 3 patients (13.6%), augmented BMG urethroplasty in 3 patients (13.6%), and ventral inlay BMG in 1 patient (4.5%). Vaginal graft–based urethroplasty was also reported, consisting of dorsal vaginal graft in 1 case (4.5%), lateral vaginal graft in 1 case (4.5%), and unspecified vaginal graft urethroplasty in 5 cases (22.7%). EPA (excision and primary anastomosis) was performed in 2 patients (9.1%). Two cases involved urethrovaginal fistula repair procedures—one managed through a ventral approach with anterior vaginal repair (4.5%), and one through a transvaginal approach (4.5%). Ileocaecovesicostomy was reported once (4.5%). Endoscopic procedures accounted for a smaller proportion, with DVIU performed in 2 patients (9.1%) and urethral dilatation with steroid injection in 1 patient (4.5%). No cases of cystostomy, perineostomy, non-transecting repairs, tubularized repairs, flap techniques, Johanson’s procedure, meatoplasty, or double-faced grafts were identified in female patients.

Among the 3,033 male patients, cystostomy was performed in 104 cases (3.4%). Endoscopic management comprised a substantial portion, including 718 urethral dilatations (23.7%) and 210 DVIU procedures (6.9%). Reconstructive surgery represented the largest treatment category, with a broad range of techniques utilized. EPA-based procedures included bulbar mobilization in 27 cases (0.9%), crural separation in 119 cases (3.9%), inferior pubectomy in 126 cases (4.2%), and supracrural rerouting in 14 cases (0.5%). Urethral perineostomy was performed in 78 patients (2.6%), while ileocaecovesicostomy was recorded in 1 patient (0.03%). Additional reconstructive techniques included non-transecting repairs in 24 cases (0.8%), tubularized repairs in 16 cases (0.5%), flap-based reconstruction in 9 cases (0.3%), Johanson’s procedure in 62 cases (2.0%), and meatoplasty in 80 cases (2.6%). Augmentation with BMG was also widely used, comprising dorsal onlay BMG in 195 cases (6.4%), ventral onlay in 74 cases (2.4%), augmented graft techniques in 63 cases (2.1%), one-side dissection in 75 cases (2.5%), dorsal inlay in 13 cases (0.4%), ventral inlay in 13 cases (0.4%), and double-faced grafts in 12 cases (0.4%) (Fig. 4).

Indonesian Urethral Strictures Treatment Pattern

Summary of findings

The results of this study indicate that reconstructive surgery, particularly EPA and urethroplasty, is the preferred treatment approach for urethral strictures in Indonesia. The distribution of stricture sites and the predominance of iatrogenic and traumatic etiologies align with international trends. However, the substantial use of dilatation treatments such as DVIU and urethral dilatation reflects the ongoing need for a range of therapeutic options to accommodate different clinical scenarios and resource availability. These findings highlight the diversity in both the causes and treatment approaches for urethral stricture across Indonesia.

Discussion

This study provides an extensive analysis of urethral stricture (US) characteristics and treatment patterns in Indonesia, contributing valuable data on the demographic distribution, etiology, anatomical sites, and therapeutic approaches used for this condition. With data collected from 3055 patients across 38 hospitals, this research is among the largest investigations into urethral stricture in Indonesia. Findings reveal that iatrogenic and traumatic causes are the primary etiologies, reconstructive surgery is the most common treatment approach, and that endoscopic procedures remain widely used. These insights highlight the progress in managing urethral strictures in Indonesia while identifying areas for future improvement.

Comparison with international data

The etiological profile observed in this study differs from Western cohorts, where iatrogenic causes predominate. The high prevalence of trauma and infection aligns more closely with patterns reported in developing and middle-income countries, reflecting differences in injury mechanisms, healthcare access, and infectious disease burden [1, 9]. Iatrogenic causes, accounting for 38.4% of cases, likely reflect the impact of medical interventions, such as catheterization and pelvic surgeries. The prominence of iatrogenic strictures may reflect catheterization practices, high-volume inpatient care, and variability in procedural training across regions. Trauma-related strictures (38%) are consistent with reports from other Southeast Asian countries where road traffic injuries remain a major contributor. Trauma-related strictures, almost equally prevalent at 38%, likely stem from the high incidence of road traffic accidents, falls, and other injuries in Indonesia. Infection-related strictures, though less common, accounted for 17.7% of cases, indicating the role of untreated or recurrent infections in urethral narrowing. This rate is higher than in many high-income countries, potentially reflecting a greater burden of infectious diseases in Indonesia and emphasizing the need for infection prevention and prompt treatment. A notable limitation regarding this specific etiology is the lack of granularity in the etiological dataset, particularly the absence of specific information regarding the infectious agents implicated in infection-related urethral strictures, thereby constraining a more refined analysis of this subgroup. This findings highlight the ongoing need for improved infection prevention, early UTI treatment, and public health education [10].

Treatment patterns and procedural preferences

Reconstructive surgery, particularly Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) and urethroplasty, emerged as the primary treatment choice, consistent with international recommendations for durable outcomes in urethral stricture management [1, 11]. EPA was performed in 33.1% of cases, a technique known for its success in short-segment strictures. Urethroplasty, performed in 32.6% of cases, is a well-regarded approach for more complex or long-segment strictures [12]. Of these procedures, augmentation urethroplasty using oral mucosal grafts was performed in 16.6% of cases, with the dorsal onlay technique as the most frequently chosen method [13]. This preference aligns with evidence favoring dorsal onlay for its lower risk of complications and high success rate [14]. The diversity in urethroplasty techniques used including ventral onlay, Johanson’s approach, and one-sided dissection demonstrates the growing expertise and flexibility in surgical approaches to urethral reconstruction in Indonesia [15].

Despite the increased use of surgical options, endoscopic procedures, such as Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU), remain commonly used, accounting for 23.6% of cases, with urethral dilatation used in 6.9%. These less invasive procedures are often chosen for shorter strictures or cases where surgery is not feasible [16]. However, their higher recurrence rates compared to urethroplasty suggest a need for selective use and regular follow-up to assess long-term outcomes [17]. Additionally, cystostomy was performed in 3.5% of cases, highlighting its role as a temporary solution for patients who cannot undergo definitive treatment immediately.

Implications for healthcare policy and training

Expanded reconstructive urology training programs since 2012—including fellowship opportunities and national workshops—have increased procedural capacity, explaining the rise of urethroplasty and complex repairs. The findings underscore the importance of standardized treatment protocols and specialized training to optimize urethral stricture management across Indonesia. While reconstructive procedures are becoming more prevalent, variability in treatment practices across hospitals suggests disparities in access to resources and expertise. Hospitals in urban areas may have more access to reconstructive urology specialists and equipment for complex surgeries, while rural hospitals may rely more heavily on endoscopic or temporary interventions. This variability highlights the need for targeted training programs in reconstructive urology and resource allocation to ensure that patients nationwide receive optimal care regardless of geographic location [3–5].

Establishing a national urethral stricture registry could address these challenges by facilitating data collection on patient outcomes, recurrence rates, and procedure efficacy. Such a registry would enhance the ability to track trends, assess the effectiveness of various treatment approaches, and support evidence-based policy decisions. Furthermore, initiatives to reduce iatrogenic strictures, such as improved catheterization techniques and post-surgical care, could help prevent the incidence of stricture in healthcare settings [3–5].

Study limitations and future research

Given the lack of longitudinal outcome data, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment effectiveness or durability. Instead, they provide insight into current real-world utilization patterns across Indonesian urology centers. While this study provides a comprehensive overview of urethral stricture management in Indonesia, several limitations should be acknowledged. The absence of standardized stricture length data limits the ability to directly correlate disease severity with treatment selection. Future registry refinements incorporating mandatory stricture length reporting may enhance clinical interpretability. As a retrospective study, findings rely on the accuracy and completeness of medical records, which may vary across institutions and limit the ability to capture long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the absence of follow-up data restricts the ability to evaluate recurrence rates and the long-term efficacy of different procedures. A key limitation of our dataset is the absence of follow-up and recurrence information, which restricts interpretation of long-term outcomes. Future research should focus on prospective studies with standardized follow-up protocols to assess patient outcomes and quality of life post-treatment. Additionally, studies examining patient and surgeon perspectives on procedural choice and satisfaction could further inform treatment practices.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed overview of urethral stricture (US) characteristics and treatment patterns in Indonesia, offering valuable data from 3055 patients across 38 hospitals. The findings reveal that iatrogenic and trauma-related etiologies are the predominant causes of urethral strictures, reflecting both healthcare-related and environmental risk factors. The bulbar urethra was identified as the most common site affected, although stricture location varied significantly, indicating the need for individualized treatment approaches.

Reconstructive surgery, especially Excision and Primary Anastomosis (EPA) and various forms of urethroplasty, has become the most frequently performed treatment, marking an important shift towards more durable and advanced surgical interventions in Indonesia. The continued use of endoscopic procedures, particularly Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy (DVIU) and urethral dilatation, underscores the demand for a range of treatment options to accommodate diverse clinical needs and varying levels of access to specialized care.

These findings suggest the need for standardized treatment guidelines and further development of reconstructive urology training programs to reduce variability in treatment practices and improve outcomes across different regions. Additionally, establishing a national urethral stricture registry would facilitate the tracking of patient outcomes, recurrence rates, and the effectiveness of various treatments, providing essential data to guide future healthcare policy and clinical practice.

In conclusion, while Indonesia has made significant progress in urethral stricture management, further advancements in specialized training, infrastructure, and data collection are essential for ensuring consistent, high-quality care nationwide. Addressing these needs will enhance the capacity of the healthcare system to effectively manage urethral strictures and improve the quality of life for affected patients.
