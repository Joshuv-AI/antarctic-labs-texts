The resected prostate weight matters in transurethral resection of the prostate in men with benign prostatic hyperplasia
Authors: Kekeli Kodjo Adanu, Mawuenyo Attawa Oyortey, James Edward Mensah, Mathew Yamoah Kyei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough a notable proportion of patients report improvement in clinical outcomes following transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), symptoms persist in about 25% of cases, resulting in dissatisfaction with the treatment. Due to this dissatisfaction, several studies have been conducted to determine the parameters that can predict outcomes. These parameters include the resected weight and the resected weight fraction. However, there is ambiguity as to what constitutes an adequate resection. Even more, the effect of the resected weight on clinical outcomes in African men with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is limited. Relevant data is needed to improve clinical practice. This study aims to determine the effect of the resected prostate weight on clinical outcomes in men with BPH at the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH).MethodsThis study uses the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), Quality of Life (QoL), International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) questionnaires, Peak Flow Rate (PFR) and Post Void Residual (PVR) urine volume to assess the clinical outcomes among men with BPH undergoing TURP at the KBTH. A cross-tabulation analysis evaluated the impact of the resected weight on the clinical outcome variables. Pearson and Spearman correlation methods ascertained the correlation between the post-operative PFR and the prostate weights, while receiver operator characteristic models interrogated the relationship between resected weight models and the clinical outcome variables. A total of 52 patients were recruited with an average of 5 recruited per month, using consecutive sampling.ResultsThe mean age was 66.83 ± 8.67 and failed medical therapy was the main indication for surgery (65%). The mean resected weight was 10.53 ± 6.98 g and the average resection speed was 0.13 g/minute. Resected weights, 20% or less of the prostate volume were much less effective. As the resected fraction doubles, the improvement in the peak flow rate also doubles. Although the resected weight and the resected weight fraction curve models were both effective in predicting voiding outcomes, the resected weight fraction had a better predictive value.ConclusionThe improvement in voiding function following TURP is dependent on the resected weight as it doubles the improvement in voiding function. Resections greater than 30% of the prostate weight are sufficient to restore normal voiding function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01939-9.

Introduction

Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is considered the gold standard in the surgical management of complications arising from Benign Prostate Hyperplasia (BPH), despite the new minimally invasive surgical interventions [1, 2]. The main indications for TURP include refractory urinary retention, obstructive uropathy, recurrent urinary tract infection, bladder calculi and failed medical therapy [3]. It has revolutionized the management of BPH since its invention [4]. However, the impact of TURP on patients’ clinical outcomes has remained controversial and uncertain. Even more, it may be associated with complications such as decreased sexual function. Some studies have reported an increase in diminished sexual function after the procedure [5, 6], while others noted significant improvement in sexual function and urinary symptom-related quality of life [7–9]. Milonas et al. [10] reported an improvement in post-void residual urine (PVR), peak flow rate (PFR) and quality of life (QoL). Similarly, in a long-term study, Kallenberg and colleagues [11] observed durable improvements in voiding symptoms several years after TURP.

Although a notable proportion of patients report improvement in clinical outcomes, symptoms persist in about 25% of cases, resulting in dissatisfaction with the treatment [12]. Due to this dissatisfaction, several studies have been conducted to determine the parameters that can predict treatment outcomes. These parameters include the resected weight and the resected weight fraction [13, 14]. As recommended by Nesbit [15], TURP should ensure the complete removal of the adenomatous tissue. This has been corroborated by Hakenberg et al. [13]. However, Antunes and associates [14] suggested that excision of < 30% of prostatic tissue may be adequate to relieve symptoms. Zhang et al. [16] also opined that the removal of at least 50% of the adenomatous tissue is required to gain optimal improvements in clinical outcomes. Due to this ambiguity, no consensus has been reached as to what constitute adequate resection and to the best of our knowledge; the effect of the resected weight on clinical outcomes in African men is unknown.

As mentioned, TURP is considered the reference standard for the management of outflow tract obstruction [1]. However, this is based on historic data mainly collected using retrospective registers and non-standardized survey instruments. Validated questionnaires only emerge in the last three decades. This makes comparison with current data invalid. In addition, TURP is a fairly new technique in sub Saharan Africa. The lack of the requisite equipment and the apparent shortage of necessary skillset has bedeviled the rollout of TURP services on the continent [17], with reported utilization rate of just 20% [18]. As a result, not many studies have investigated its effects on symptom severity among men with the condition; let alone the effect of the resected prostate weight on clinical outcomes. Relevant data is needed to improve clinical practice. The aim of this study therefore is to assess the impact of the resected prostate weight on treatment outcomes mainly the IPSS (International Prostate Symptoms Score), urinary symptom-related QoL, PVR, the erectile function and the PFR among men with BPH at the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval was sought and received from the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital Institutional Review Board, with reference number KBTH-STC/IRB/00033/2022. A written informed consent was obtained from all participants and the study was conducted with due regard to the ethical principles of the declaration of Helsinki.

Study setting

This prospective longitudinal study was conducted at the urology unit of Korle Bu Teaching Hospital from September 2021 to December 2022. The unit performs an average of five (5) TURP surgeries per month. Participants were men diagnosed with BPH who had undergone TURP.

Sampling

The sample size was based on a previous study by Hossain et al. [19]. We relied on this study because of the absence of studies on clinical outcomes post TURP from sub-Saharan Africa at the time of data collection. This study was designed to consecutively select a minimum of 44 participants assuming a 5% non-response rate using a standard deviation (δ) of 0.46 for QoL at 95% confidence interval, an expected mean difference of 20% for QoL and an 80% chance of detecting a true obtainable effect.

All consecutive patients diagnosed as having BPH and counseled for TURP were eligible to participate in this study. Each patient had a unique number for easy identification.

Method of data collection

This study used a quantitative data collection and analysis approach. The questionnaire consisted of three segments, namely 1) Socio-demographic status 2) Assessment of symptom severity and quality of life 3) Erectile function. It is important to note that the IPSS and International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) questionnaires have been validated in African populations [20, 21]. The questionnaire was administered before surgery, then 1 and 3 months post-surgery through face-to-face interviews, after a free and written consent had been obtained from the patient, after being assured of confidentiality and data security. The clinical outcome measures were categorized into three main domains, urine flow dynamics (IPSS, PFR and PVR), QoL and erectile function. Symptom severity and QoL were assessed using the IPSS-QoL questionnaire. Erectile function was assessed using the IIEF-5 validated questionnaire and the PVR determined using an Acclarix LX4 ultrasound machine with a 2 MHZ convex trans-abdominal probe.The Intelligent uroflow meter estimated the PFR. Post-surgery, the weight of the resected prostate chippings was determined using a standardized weighing scale. The time spent in resecting the prostate was recorded. The pre-operative prostate volume (PV) was also estimated using the Acclarix LX4 machine with a 2 MHZ convex and 5 MHZ linear probes at a bladder volume of about 200mls. To ensure consistency in PV and PVR estimations, the ultrasonography was performed by two qualified radiologists.

Recruitment and procedure

Overall, 52 patients were included in this study. All patients who presented to the outpatient department with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) were evaluated for potential causes as per the unit’s protocol. This involved taking a thorough history, performing physical examination including digital rectal examination (DRE), diagnostics – serum PSA, abdominopelvic ultrasonography (USG) and a full blood count. Patients were invited to participate once a diagnosis of BPH was made and a decision taken to undergo TURP. All men clinically diagnosed as having BPH, between 40 and 85 years of age, considered for TURP and willing to participate in this study were included while men with co-existing conditions such as neurogenic bladder (such as diabetic cystopathy), urethral stricture, bladder tumor/pelvic malignancies, histologically confirmed prostate cancer, diabetes mellitus and external genital anomalies were excluded. Diabetic patients were excluded because of the risk of diabetic cystopathy which may have an effect on clinical outcomes and confound study findings. Appropriate diagnostics such as fasting blood sugar (FBS), retrograde urethrogram and histopathological studies were ordered, when indicated, to rule out differentials. The unit’s protocol was also followed to evaluate patients with suspicion of neurogenic bladder. This includes clinical assessment for loss of bladder sensation and review of PVR. Urodynamic studies were performed when there was bladder sensation loss, significant PVR (>150 mls), and suspected detrusor/sphincter instability.

The bipolar TURP procedure was conducted using a Karl Storz bipolar generator with voltage set at 340 V, a cutting current of 180 watts and a coagulation current set at 80 watts. The prostate chippings were evacuated with the aid of the Elliks evacuator and weighed. The adequacy of the resection was assessed intra-operatively by withdrawing the resectoscope to the level of the distal verumontanum, to observe for an open channel (panoramic view) through the prostate. Haemostasis was secured and a three-way catheter placed for continuous drainage. Adequate analgesia and antibiotics were provided post-surgery.

Quality control measures instituted include the training of research assistants, pre-testing of the questionnaires and correction of errors. Completed questionnaires were validated and entered daily. Data collated was entered into Excel 2013 and exported to STATA, version 15.0 (StataCorp LLC) for analysis.

Primary outcome measures

Peak flow rate (PFR)– the PFR offers an objective measure of free urine flow post-surgery. This parameter provides a good measure of treatment because it is unaffected by patients’ perceptions and idiosyncrasies. Quality of life (QoL) – the QoL provides a general overview of patients’ well-being. Although it is a subjective measure, we consider this parameter as the most important patient-perception of treatment outcome.

Secondary outcome measures

The IPSS, the erectile function which was evaluated through the IIEF-5 questionnaire and PVR, in addition to the primary outcome measures, are important for a comprehensive understanding of the surgery’s impact on treatment outcome.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported using frequency tables and charts, while continuous variables were reported using means, standard deviations and median scores. Pearson or Spearman correlation methods ascertained the correlation between the resected weight and the PV (Spearman test was used for non-normally distributed data), while receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve models further interrogated the relationship between the resected weight fraction and the clinical outcome variables. Results were reported at 95% confidence interval with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The Bonferroni test was employed for post-hoc pairwise comparisons in cases where a significant difference was observed between multiple comparable groups.

Results

Sixty-three (63) patients were assessed for enrollment. A total of eleven (11) patients were excluded after seven (7) were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus (one of whom had a neurogenic bladder), two (2), on account of being above the age of inclusion and two (2), due to histologically confirmed prostate cancer. Hence, the analysis in this study included fifty-two (52) eligible patients.

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

The mean age was 66.83 ± 8.67 years. Majority were married (85%) and above 60 years of age (81%). Most participants were retired (64%) and had tertiary education (52%).

Discussion

This study assessed the impact of the resected weight on the IPSS score, QoL, PVR, PFR and erectile function. The resected weight fraction model proved to be a better predictor of voiding function compared to the absolute resected weight model, though both were effective. It also emerged that removing more than 30% of the prostate was sufficient to restore voiding function.

Prior to surgery, most patients rated their QoL as grade 5, meaning they were unhappy with their state of health. However, following surgery, a significant number were delighted (grade 0) with the intervention. The estimated mean difference in the QoL pre- and post-surgery was 88%, demonstrating a true difference in the QoL of our patients (Table S4). The improved QoL was irrespective of the resected weight fraction and the PFR. Patients with the least resected fraction, consequently the least improved PFR reported similar improvement in their QoL as those with higher resected weight fractions and PFR (Table 2). Similar findings were observed in the studies by Antunes et al. [14] and Zhang et al. [16].

Based on the study findings, it was apparent that as the resected fractions increased, the voiding function improved correspondingly. Resections less than 20% were much less effective (Tables 2 and 3). Of all the outcome measures, the primary outcome measure, the PFR appeared to be most responsive to changes in the resected fraction unlike the QoL, IPSS score and the PVR which were less responsive. Moreover, it is notable that improvement in the PFR was influenced by the degree of resection where larger resections gave rise to greater improvement in this parameter. For instance, as the resected fraction increased from 11 - 20% to 31 – 40% and then to 50%, the PFR increased from 12.5 to 24.0, representing a 92% increment. As the resected fraction doubled, the improvement in the voiding function almost doubled. These findings suggest that patients' benefits are related to the degree of adenoma resection. The more complete the resection, the greater the improvement in voiding function. This result should be contextualized against the fact that the highest resected fraction was 48%; would these results be any different had the resected fraction been much higher? The study by Zhang et al. [16] provided answers. They observed a similar trend in their study where 25–50% and >50% resections resulted in a statistically significant increase in PFR as compared to ≤25% resection rate; and just like in our study, the other parameters (PVR, IPSS and QoL) were similar in the comparative groups. Conversely, Antunes and colleagues [14] found that <30% prostate tissue resection may just be enough to improve male LUTS. Unlike this study, the study by Antunes et al. did not measure PFR. It is therefore difficult to ascertain objective differences in pre-operative obstruction, which may have accounted for the contrary findings.

The degree of pre-operative urinary obstruction is a critical factor in voiding function. Lopes and colleagues [22] found a correlation between the severity of urinary obstruction and the resected weight fraction. Patients who were obstructed (PFR < 10 ml/s) noticed more significant improvement in voiding function as more tissue was resected compared to those with equivocal uroflow (PFR : 10–15ml/s). They opined that patients with a higher PFR may do well with a less profound prostatic resection [22]. In an analysis of this study’s population, all patients were obstructed pre-operatively (PFR < 10ml/s). As observed by Lopes and co-authors, we found a statistically significant correlation between the post-operative PFR and the resected weight fraction (r = 0.45, p = 0.001). A similar trend was observed for the absolute resected weight (r = 0.34, p = 0.017). Our findings support the suggestion that obstructed patients benefit significantly from more complete tissue resection. A further categorization of the IPSS scores into mild-moderate and severe symptom scores also show a similar trend with regard to the post-operative PFR (Tables S1 and S2). We also observed a negative albeit non-statistically significant correlation between the post-operative PFR and the PV (r = − 0.06, p = 0.656). An analysis of the degree of improvement in PFR based on PV (<50g versus ≥ 50 g) affirmed this suspicion (Tables S3a and S3b). This meant that larger volume prostates achieved less than the required resection, leading to less improvement in the post-operative PFR. Surgeon fatigue may play a role, particularly as extended durations are necessary for the resection of larger prostates.

From our findings, resections 20% or less resulted in similar improvement in the PFR (Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, resections>30% of the prostate weight resulted in a greater increase in the PFR as compared to ≤ 20% resections (i.e., 24 ml/s vs ≤ 12.5 ml/s). A general trend analysis shows a rise in the PFR as tissue resected increases (16 ml/s to 24 ml/s). It is important at this stage to examine the clinical implications of these findings. As established in literature, PFR >15ml/s are considered non-obstructed and rates between 20–25 ml/s are regarded as normal [23, 24]. We infer that even as resections ≤ 20% resulted in a rise in the PFR, patients in this category still had some level of urinary obstruction (median PFR ≤ 12.5 ml/s) while resections>20% culminated in larger PFR, such that resected prostate fractions of 31–50% resulted in the restoration of normal voiding function (median PFR = 24 ml/s). Based on these findings, we recommend resections >30% of the prostate weight since they resulted in significant improvement in the PFR and the restoration of normal voiding in men post-TURP.

Previous studies have evaluated the persistence of storage symptoms post-TURP. A similar trend was observed in this study (Figure S1). Among the factors responsible for this phenomenon, the severity of storage symptoms before surgery, small bladder capacity and detrusor instability are prominent [25, 26]. Our analyses of the effect of pre-operative storage and voiding symptom sub-scores on treatment efficacy show that storage symptoms are negatively correlated with post-operative PFR (r = − 0.02, p = 0.886). The reverse was observed for the voiding symptom sub-score (r = 0.14, p = 0.392). Although these correlations were not statistically significant, it was apparent that the severity and the persistence of storage symptoms affected treatment efficacy negatively unlike voiding symptoms. The operating surgeon should therefore anticipate this phenomenon post-surgery.

Although tissue resection improved voiding, as seen with an increment in the PFR, the erectile function remained unchanged. The mean IIEF-5 score noted no significant change irrespective of the amount of tissue resected. The average IIEF-5 score recorded across fractions suggests that most patients had some form of ED before surgery, with dysfunction persisting afterward. It is argued that the relief of symptomatic BPH through TURP and the tissue resected have no bearing on erectile function. Global literature, however, remains divergent, as some authors reported an increase in ED [5, 27] others observed either an improvement or persistence of the condition [28, 29]. We must reiterate that erectile function is of great concern to patients with BPH. Previous studies have described the relationship between LUTS and erectile function [30, 31]; and others found a relationship between TURP and ED. The reasons, however, remain unclear. It has been postulated that elaborate resection compromises blood flow to the prostate and penis, thus inducing ED [6]. Others suggest that it could be of neurogenic or psychogenic origin due to its association with diminished libido [5].

Interrogation of the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve models yield interesting results. The resected weight and resected weight fraction are both sensitive to changes in the PFR. Consequently, the absolute resected weight and the degree of resection are determinants of treatment and may influence patients’ urinary symptom-related QoL. These findings are incongruous to what pertained in the study by Hakenberg et al. [13], where the absolute weight but not the resected fraction affected treatment.

The resected weight correlated positively with the resection time, meaning large resections are accomplished in longer periods. Although not evaluated in this study, Uchida et al. [32] found that longer resection times and large resected prostate weights were associated with the risk of morbidity and blood transfusion. They found that older men were more likely to receive blood transfusions which was attributed to the lower hemoglobin levels before surgery. They also noted a decline in the requirement of blood transfusion as techniques for TURP, instrumentation - including the use of the three-way catheter for continuous bladder drainage and anesthesia management improved over time. No patient was transfused in this series.

Although this study yielded very interesting results, there were some limitations. The amount of tissue lost due to heat and vaporization was not accounted for. This may have influenced the clinical outcomes measured. Secondly, the total PV was estimated by transabdominal ultrasonography which is believed to be less accurate compared to transrectal ultrasound; however, previous studies show agreement between both procedures [33, 34]. The varying levels of experience of surgeons may have also impacted the degree of tissue resection and clinical outcomes. Our reliance on the study by Hossain et al in calculating the sample size and the use of consecutive sampling may have introduced some bias in sampling. Long-term studies in larger cohorts may further strengthen the reliability and generalizability of findings. It is also noted that measurements were mainly based on free uroflow and not on pressure-flow study, which represents the gold standard method to achieve urodynamic diagnosis of bladder outlet obstruction in males.

Conclusion

The improvement in voiding function following TURP is dependent on the resected weight, such that as the resected weight doubles, the improvement in voiding function also doubles, with resections greater than 30% being sufficient to restore normal voiding function. The resected weight fraction model had a better predictive value. The resected weight has no influence on erectile function.
