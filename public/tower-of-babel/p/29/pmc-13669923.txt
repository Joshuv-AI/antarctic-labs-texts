Fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair (FEVAR) allows endograft sealing above renal and visceral branches while preserving organ perfusion by including fenestrations for these arteries.1,2 Precise alignment of the graft fenestrations with the target vessel ostia is critical.3 Diameter-reducing wire slippage may result in premature graft expansion and fenestration malalignment, potentially causing visceral ischemia and branch access failure.4

Balloon anchoring techniques have been widely used to stabilize devices during complex endovascular procedures5; however, maintaining stable catheterization under unfavorable branch angulation remains technically challenging. We describe a step-by-step endo-anchoring guidewire technique that uses balloon-assisted intraluminal fixation to facilitate catheterization and restore procedural control during complex FEVAR.

Case Summary

A 72-year-old man was referred for abdominal aortic aneurysm with a maximum diameter of 6.6 cm and involvement of both renal arteries (Figure 1A). A physician-modified endovascular graft was prepared on a 34 × 200-mm Ankura stent graft (Lifetech Scientific) according to renal arteries (left renal artery, right renal artery), superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and celiac trunk positions measured by computed tomography angiography (CTA) (Figure 1B). A stiff 0.018-inch V-18 wire was preloaded as the diameter-reducing wire (Figures 1C and 1D).

Under fluoroscopy, the modified graft was advanced until the fenestrae aligned with the respective visceral branches as planned. During this stage, the operating team noted an unexpected change: the V-18 diameter-reducing wire normally parallel to the delivery system was seen bowing outward into a pronounced arc (Figure 1E), suggesting its slippage from its intended position within the graft. Completion angiogram confirmed the wire slipped off from its normal position with partial graft deployment and fenestration malalignment (Figure 1F). Marked resistance was encountered when attempting to rotate the delivery system, raising an urgent need to adjust the position of the graft.

To salvage the situation, a right brachial artery access was introduced.6 An angioplasty balloon was positioned through the fenestra into the SMA to stabilize graft orientation, after which the remaining constraining mechanism was released (Figure 1G).

Next came branch cannulation, as the SMA was now held in a downward-angled position by the inflated balloon, catheterization from a femoral approach became difficult. The endo-anchoring guidewire technique was therefore introduced. A guidewire was advanced into the SMA and internally stabilized using a partially inflated balloon positioned within the proximal SMA. Balloon inflation was limited to the minimum extent necessary to create temporary apposition between the guidewire and the vessel wall. This intraluminal fixation enabled smooth catheterization despite the unfavorable angle (Figure 1H). Each branch was then successfully catheterized and stented over guidewires. The 3-month follow-up CTA revealed proximal aneurysm repaired and all 4 visceral arteries restored (Figure 1I).

Procedural Steps

Step 1: Recognition of wire slippage

During advancement of the physician-modified endovascular graft, the diameter-reducing wire, which normally remains parallel to the delivery system, was observed unexpectedly bowing outward into a pronounced arc. Resistance during graft rotation and completion angiography further confirmed wire slippage, partial premature deployment, and fenestration malalignment, prompting immediate bailout maneuvers.

Step 2: Establishment of bailout access

As repositioning through the femoral approach was unsuccessful, right brachial access was established to improve graft control and facilitate branch manipulation. A hydrophilic guidewire was advanced into the SMA through the upper extremity approach.

Step 3: Balloon anchoring

A 6 × 30-mm balloon (Boston Scientific) was advanced through the SMA fenestration and partially inflated within the proximal SMA to stabilize graft orientation before complete release of the stent graft. The balloon size was selected to match the SMA diameter while allowing controlled inflation without excessive vessel wall stress. The SMA was chosen because of its adequate length, straighter course, and critical importance for visceral perfusion. With the graft stabilized by the balloon anchor, the remaining constraining mechanisms were safely released without having the graft shift further.

Step 4: Endo-anchoring guidewire technique

The same partially inflated balloon was subsequently used for the endo-anchoring guidewire technique. A guidewire was advanced into the SMA and internally stabilized by the partially inflated balloon. The balloon was inflated only to the extent necessary to achieve temporary apposition between the guidewire and the vessel wall. By reducing guidewire mobility at the fenestration-vessel interface, this intraluminal fixation transformed the guidewire into a stable support rail for catheter advancement. The enhanced support enabled stable catheterization across the unfavorable downward angle.

Step 5: Branch reconstruction

All visceral branches were catheterized and reconstructed using bridging stents. Completion angiography confirmed branch patency without endoleak.

Potential Pitfalls

Beyond addressing this intraoperative emergency, equal importance lies in preventing such complications at their origin. To minimize the risk of diameter-reducing wire slippage, it is important to ensure that the hemostatic valve of the delivery sheath is not overly tightened or excessively pressurized by heparinized saline. Attention also should be paid to device-sheath compatibility and iliac stenosis that may impede wire advancement.7 In our case, the hemostatic valve was likely slightly overtightened during filling, contributing to the unintended wire slippage as the graft advanced.

When applying the endo-anchoring guidewire technique, excessive balloon oversizing or overinflation should be avoided because it may increase the risk of vessel injury, dissection, or flow compromise. The technique also may be less suitable in severely diseased, heavily calcified, or small-caliber target vessels in which safe balloon-mediated fixation cannot be achieved. Careful balloon sizing, controlled inflation, and continuous angiographic monitoring are essential to ensure procedural safety.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of rapid recognition and decisive management of diameter-reducing wire slippage during complex FEVAR. The endo-anchoring guidewire technique provided a practical bailout strategy when conventional maneuvers failed and facilitated branch catheterization under unfavorable anatomical conditions. Beyond its technical success, this experience underscores the need for meticulous intraoperative monitoring and preventive attention to sheath-wire interactions to avert such life-threatening complications. Although careful balloon sizing and controlled inflation are required to minimize procedural risks, this technique may serve as a useful adjunct in complex endovascular procedures.
