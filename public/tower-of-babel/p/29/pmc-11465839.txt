Community perspectives of Ebola Viral Disease in high-risk transmission border regions of Tanzania: a qualitative inquiry
Authors: Emmy Metta, Hussein Mohamed, Priscilla Kusena, Tumaini Nyamhanga, Shalini Bahuguna, Deodatus Kakoko, Nathanael Siril, Awet Araya, Alice Mwiru, Stanley Magesa, Lulu Makene, Allan Rwechungura, Fatimata B Kirakoya, Method Kazaura, Gasto Frumence
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTanzania faces ever-rising concerns due to the recurrence of the Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) in neighbouring Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Uganda. This necessitates a better understanding of the community perspectives in high-risk regions for effective risk communication and preparedness.MethodsThis rapid ethnographic assessment study used explorative qualitative methods to collect data. People from diverse backgrounds participated in 59 in-depth interviews, 57 Key Informant interviews, and 35 focus group discussions. Data was analysed using a thematic analysis approach.FindingThe study revealed existence of awareness of EVD and its symptoms, with radio and television being the main sources of information. However, there were varied perceptions of EVD’s cause and transmission, some attributed it to bats, monkeys, and wild animal meat, while others associated it with high fever, a dirty environment, changing dietary patterns, and the COVID-19 virus. Physical contact with an infected person’s body fluids and eating meat from infected animals were perceived as EVD transmission routes. Women, school children, boda-boda (motorcycle) riders, and fishermen were considered the most susceptible to EVD infections due to their daily activities. Preventive measures included avoiding physical contact, touching fluids, and refraining from eating wild animal meat. Prompt reporting of suspected cases to health facilities was deemed crucial for earlier outbreak identification and containment.ConclusionThe high-risk regions of Tanzania had a high level of awareness and perceived susceptibility to EVD, coupled with varying degrees of misperception about the etiology and its transmission. To improve community perspectives and preparedness in the case of an outbreak, there is a need for ongoing risk communication and participation in EVD prevention and responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20305-2.Keywords: Community perspectives, EVD susceptibility, EVD Severity, Boarder regions, Tanzania

Background

One of the worst viral infections that continues to threaten worldwide public health is the Ebola Virus Disease (EVD). In the whole equatorial region of Africa, over 38 epidemic episodes have been documented since its discovery in 1976 in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), then known as Zaire [1]. Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo ebolavirus, Sudan ebolavirus, and Taï Forest ebolavirus (sometimes called Cote d’Ivoire ebolavirus) are the four ebolaviruses that cause EVD in humans and are members of the filovirus in the family Filoviridae [2]. The virus that causes Ebola is ordinarily hosted in non-human primates and fruit bats. Humans can contact it by contacting infected animals, such as porcupines, fruit bats, gorillas, chimpanzees, monkeys, or forest antelopes. Transmission of the virus from one person to another occurs through direct contact with an infected person - the blood, organs, or other bodily fluids - whether asymptomatic or not. The EVD has an incubation period of 2 to 21 days, and with epidemics, the infections spread swiftly throughout communities. Fever, weakness, headache, soreness in the muscles, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, internal and external bleeding, and liver and kidney problems are among the symptoms. It will take a lot of work to stop the spread of EVD, including investments in risk communication and community participation strategies.

Sub-Saharan Africa has had several EVD outbreaks, mainly in Guinea, Liberia, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Mali, Ivory Coast, Nigeria, Uganda, Sudan, and the DRC. From 2014 to 2016, West Africa saw the worst epidemic on record, which claimed 11,325 lives [3]. The recent outbreak was recorded in North Kivu Province, the DRC, on August 21, 2022, accounting for 15 outbreaks nationwide. One month later, on 20th September 2022, another epidemic was proclaimed in Uganda after a verified case of the Sudan strain of the Ebola virus was discovered in the Mubende district [4]. The Sudan ebolavirus’s fatality rate ranges from 41 to 100% and is a well-known virulent and extremely contagious Ebola virus [5]. The likelihood that the EVD epidemics in Uganda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo may spread to nearby nations has increased [6]. Tanzania borders the DRC and Uganda, and cross-border socioeconomic exchanges between the three populations occur on a daily basis. Concerns and issues regarding the potential for EVD importation into Tanzania are raised in such circumstances. As a result, the Tanzanian Ministry of Health decided to bolster the nation’s epidemic readiness and response protocols in the event that EVD erupt out there.

The Tanzanian government alerted all citizens to take precautions against the outbreak as part of its preparation and prevention efforts for the epidemic. Due to their proximity to neighbouring countries and the influx of people from those regions, the government gave special attention to the border regions (Kagera, Mwanza, Mara, Geita, and Kigoma) and regions with large airports and bus terminals (Dar es Salaam, Arusha, Kilimanjaro, Songwe, Mbeya, and Dodoma). A demand was made for increased efforts to implement efficient EVD outbreak preparation measures for prompt diagnosis and control of epidemics in all risky regions. On the other hand, effective epidemic interventions must consider all facets of the disease, including the cultural perception of the illness and the social factors likely to contribute to its spread [6]. Developing relevant, flexible, and long-lasting illness preventive and control strategies should be informed by the communities’ understanding and disease perspectives [7, 8]. There is little information available on how the non-EVD-affected communities in Tanzania’s high-risk areas perceive the disease. To support risk communication, community’s participation, and focused interventions against EVD should it enter the country, gathering comprehensive data on people’s views, values, norms, and beliefs about the disease is imperative.

Research indicates that how a community views illness and its causes significantly impacts how it responds to it, especially in terms of prevention and control [9, 10].According to the Health Belief Model (HBM), the most popular model for analysing how individuals behave when adopting illness prevention measures, risk perceptions influence people’s conduct [12–14] including their responses to disease and illnesses. The hypothesis put out by the HBM proposes that a person is likely to alter their conduct if they believe they are susceptible to EVD and that the illness may have negative effects (perceived severity). The Model postulates that there is a positive correlation between the perceived severity of a condition and the likelihood of following prescribed behaviour to prevent unfavourable outcomes. The model suggests that a person’s decision to engage in EVD prevention will be influenced by several factors, such as his/her understanding of the illness, its effects, and the advantages of implementing the preventive behaviour [11]. To prevent and control the condition, and to increase community knowledge of its health risks, it is crucial to explore the existing norms, values, attitudes, and beliefs about the disease [12]. Beliefs and opinions on the disease’s causes, symptoms, susceptibility, prevention, and possible hazards influence people’s perceptions of it [13].

This study explores the community perspectives on EVD in Tanzania’s high-risk regions in light of the outbreaks experienced at different points in the neighbouring countries of Uganda and the Democratic Republic of Congo. To elicit the community perspectives, we employed the HBM constructs: Perceived susceptibility to EVD, Perceived severity of EVD, perceived benefits and perceived barriers to EVD prevention, and Perceived self-efficacy to adapt the recommended preventive measures. Studies detailing the community perspectives of Ebola in high-risk regions from a Tanzanian setting are limited [14]. To support context-specific preparedness actions for prevention and response to the potential importation of EVD cases, this knowledge is crucial for improving risk communication and community participation initiatives for EVD containment.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as part of a more extensive rapid ethnographic assessment (REA) research that employed exploratory qualitative data collection methods, such as observation, key informant interviews, focus groups, and in-depth interviews to learn more about the community’s perspectives about EVD that are likely to exacerbate or prevent the disease’s spread. REA was considered helpful in this study because of its ability to produce cultural data and use it to illuminate cultural values on the phenomenon under investigation (community’s perspectives of EVD) in a short time frame and help inform policy makers and health managers on the best ways to prevent EVD while assuring that they are respectful to the populations they will influence [15]. The advantages of REA are evident in its capacity to permit: “(a) rapid data collection and dissemination of information useful for decision making (b) use of multidisciplinary assessments teams and (c) triangulation across multiple data collection methods and sources to strengthen the validity of findings, which aim to develop practical, achievable recommendations” [15]. Because of this, REA was a desirable option for this research because of the pressing requirement for quick results to guide risk communication and community participation initiatives as well as actions as part of preparation efforts in the event of an EVD epidemic. The current article presents the community perspectives of EVD thus will share only the data from the focus group discussions, in-depth interviews, and key informant interviews. The information generated through the observations will be presented elsewhere.

Study sites

The study was conducted in four high-risk EVD outbreak regions on Tanzania’s Mainland and the Zanzibar Isles. The regions covered in Tanzania’s Mainland were Kagera, Mwanza, Kigoma, and Songwe, which had previously been identified as the most at-risk for EVD outbreaks [14]. Kagera and Mwanza regions border Uganda, whereas Kigoma and Songwe regions share boundaries with the DRC. Because of its popularity as a tourist destination, Zanzibar sees many visitors from both within and outside Tanzania, especially those from the DRC and Uganda, which increases the possibility of EVD being imported. In general, because of their socio-economic interconnections, the populations in the research locations are especially vulnerable to EVD infection, should it arise. For instance, one of the key elements tying Tanzania to the DRC and Uganda is business activities. Individuals from these nations travel by land, air, or sea, with the latter option encompassing both authorized and unauthorized ports of entry. Due to a lack of formal government travel credentials, evidence indicates that many persons select unauthorized ports of entry [14], which raises the possibility of spreading illnesses, including EVD. Cross-border movements associated with commerce, family visits, or tourism are crucial for the spread of novel illnesses similar to the socio-economic and communal connection of the East African nations [16]. The context was ideal for learning about the communities’ perspectives on EVD to develop focused reaction plans if the illness emerge because of this backdrop. To concentrate the assessment, the corresponding regional and district authorities determined the precise areas within their purview that are known to be at high risk of EVD transmission in consultation with the study team. To gain broad community perspectives on EVD and socio-cultural variables likely to prevent or aggravate the infection if it arises, special attention was also given to places with Indigenous people and higher interaction of individuals inside and beyond the nation.

Data collection methods

Data was collected through three main methods, namely, in-depth interviews (IDI), Key Informant Interviews (KIIs), and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). However, data collection started with observations to understand the real-life behaviors and risk contexts of EVD transmission in various risk areas, including hospitals, markets, funeral yards, ports, bus terminals, borders, airports, churches, mosques, and schools. The observations provided first-hand information on the behaviors in natural settings and helped identify participants for FGDs, IDIs, and KIIs. The sample size for FGDs, IDIs and KIIs were determined after reaching the data saturation point, when no more new information that was being generated [17].

In-depth interviews

A total of 59 IDIs were conducted with purposively recruited prominent adult people based on their position in the communities and it included: the influential people, traditional healers, retired teachers, religious leaders, and traditional birth attendants (see additional file 1, list of people involved in the IDIs). These individuals were recruited based on their experiences in the study settings to allow identification of patterns on their feelings and perspectives on EVD. Different angles were considered during the recruitment of the IDI participants to allow for the capture of a broader range of perspectives. The IDIs aimed to obtain detailed information on the participants’ perspectives on EVD and perceived preventive measures should the disease emerge. A predetermined interview guide was used to facilitate the interviews and were audio recorded with the permission of the participants. The in-depth interviews lasted between 45 and 60 min.

Key informants’ interviews

A total of 57 purposively recruited community leaders based on their leadership experiences in the respective communities. The KII participants included elected community leaders, the chairperson of the village health committee, village executive officers, and those in charge of ports (see additional file 1, list of people involved in the KIIs). The KIIs enabled the study to engage community leaders into a conversation aiming at finding out their opinions, views and perceptions about EVD and about the EVD preventive measures. The KII lasted between 40 and 60 min and were all audio recorded with the permission of the participants.

Focus group discussions

Purposive sampling was used to select community members who were working in EVD risk environments to participate in FGDs. A total of 35 FGDs were conducted engaging community members from different populations including fishermen, traditional healers, youths, pastoralists, commercial sex workers, and bodaboda (motorcycle-for-hire) drivers. FGDs participants were selected because they can provide deeper perspectives and understanding on social-cultural issues and practices that facilitate prevention of Ebola outbreak or contribute to the risk environment for the outbreak (see additional file 1, the people involved in the FGDs). The discussions in the focus groups aimed at gaining broader community perspectives on EVD comprising of awareness of EVD and its perceived symptoms, perceived causes of Ebola and its transmission, perceived susceptibility to Ebola, severity and perceived preventive measures. A pre-developed FGD guide was used to elicit the respective information from the discussants. Each FGD consisted of 8–12 adult community members aged 18 years and above and they were conducted in a quite and convenient environment as preferred by the study participants. Men and women FGDs were held separately to encourage participants’ freeness in expressing their views on EVD. Similar to IDIs and KIIs, the FGDs were audio recorded with the discussants consent and lasted between 55 and 90 min. Most sites reached saturation at the 7th FGDs with exception of Zanzibar that reached saturation at 10th FGD. However, across sites, one additional FGD was added to confirm the saturation.

Data collection and analysis

The data were collected by six researchers with substantial backgrounds in qualitative and ethnographic research in Tanzania and a group of well-trained research assistants. A pilot study for data collection guides was conducted during the team’s three training days. The results of the pilot study were used to improve the final guides. All data collection activities were conducted in Kiswahili, the national language. After the discussions and interviews, transcriptions were written within 72 h. The transcripts in Word files were examined and aligned with the field notes. Inter-transcript dependability was demonstrated by having researchers EM and HM complete a second transcript assessment to guarantee data trustworthiness. It is worth noting that during the analysis the authors used a realist approach to narration, which attempts to ensure objectivity in the portrayal of the community perspectives on EVD in Tanzania’s high-risk regions. A realist approach presents study participants’ voice on the real practice on what is happening on the grounds with regards to phenomenon under investigation. In this context, Van Maanen [18] refers realist approach to narration as an “institutional voice,” with a focus solely on what the research proclaims is the ‘goings on’ of the group under study. Five researchers (EM, TN, DCK, HM, and SN) selected five transcripts at random from each location prior to coding the transcripts. They scanned them to determine the original codes and to become well-versed in the data. The team members exchanged and reviewed the codes after coding the first five transcripts. Discrepancies were minimal and, when they occurred, they were resolved through discussions before finalizing the codes and coding of the data. Three researchers (EM, HM, and TN) coded the data and held several meetings to review the codes. The data coding continued until no news codes emerged. This was followed by categorizing the codes into themes and family codes, following the principles of grounded theory, until no new themes and categories were identified. After the study had linked the identified concepts and the HBM constructs, the following themes emerged: (i) Awareness of EVD, (ii) Perceived EVD symptoms, (iii) Perceived EVD causes, (iv) Perceived EVD transmission, (v) Perceived EVD Susceptibility, (vi) Perceived EVD severity, (vii) Perceived EVD preventive measures. Even though the data on the perspectives of EVD from the different participants were reasonably consistent, findings are not presented separately by the participant type. Instead, they describe different themes of EVD perceptions emerging from the data. The excerpts used to elaborate on the participants’ perceptions have been translated directly from Swahili to English “preserved as is”, thus may not follow a strict grammatical route.

Trustworthiness of the study

In this study, validity interpretations of the findings were ensured in different ways: first, was to engage a team of well-trained and skilled research assistants who are able to check personal bias from the study participants and help ensure that the responses are genuine and not influenced by what the researchers want to hear. The second way was to use deep saturation into the research by ensuring that the generated responses become more consistent across larger numbers of samples, and make the data becomes more reliable. The third way was the use of triangulation methods of data collection: FGDs, IDIs and KIIs, which helped to cross-validate their findings and mitigate the limitations inherent in any single approach. The fourth way was the involvement of five researchers from multiple perspectives to analyse the same data and inform the results from different angles, compare results, discuss and resolve the discrepancies before finalizing the emerging codes and themes.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical clearance from the institutional ethical review board of the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences (MUHAS-REC-11-2022-1442) and the Zanzibar Health Research Ethics Committee (ZAHREC/04/PR/2023/06). All the methods in the study followed the relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki). Before enrolment, we explained to them the objectives and procedures of the study. Then, we obtained their informed written consent since participation in the study was voluntary, and the participants could pull out at any time if they wished.

Findings

Awareness of EVD

Study participants expressed awareness of EVD. Many of the participants reported having heard about EVD from television and radio that Ebola affects neighbouring countries. Some even mentioned learning about the disease from their relatives who had close contact with the EVD-affected countries. In this regard, a participant in Mwanza said:

“…I had a relative who used to travel to those countries …the countries which are infected, and there was a time when we were banned from traveling to those countries; he came and told us how the Ebola virus is transmitted, and people were not allowed to participate in funerals. Also, we have heard about it (EVD) from televisions like ITV and other media…” (FGD01, Mwanza).

Several other participants treated Ebola as a disease just out there, far removed from the reality on the ground. Such participants aroused sceptical and suggested that Ebola is a disease they only hear about from the radio that it affects neighbour countries, hence inconsequential. In Zanzibar, it was common for participants to indicate that they only heard about EVD from the radio:

“Ebola… I only hear from the radio” (FGD011, Zanzibar).

Similar remarks were also evident in Mwanza. As one of the respondents explicated:

“… The Ebola virus is the disease that we have been hearing about from the television only … that there is a disease that someone bleeds in all the openings and is an infectious disease… that is what we heard…” (IDI07, Mwanza).

Throughout the interviews and discussions, views such as “Ebola is a disease that came from Uganda” or “Ebola is a disease that was first reported in Congo” were common. In Missenyi district, a participant said:

“….Ebola is a disease that came from Uganda and originated from animals… such as those gorillas….” (FGD010, Missenyi).

When probed further, it emerged that participants were aware of the condition and had heard about it through television, radio broadcasts, and from their community leaders:

“To begin with, we get information on EVD from the TV. They announce it; they also have a picture…. The district director also called and informed us that there is an outbreak of Ebola. They taught us how to inform the community members to protect themselves from that disease….” (KII 02, Bukoba).

A few participants, specifically from Zanzibar, persistently maintained that it was a new disease or had either never heard or knew nothing about Ebola or had heard about it but with constricted additional knowledge.

Perceived EVD symptoms

Many study participants identified one or several EVD symptoms. The spontaneous and frequently mentioned symptom across the study sites was the oozing of blood through the nose, mouth, ears, and skin openings:

“Ebola patients bleed from all the opening of his/her body parts from the ears, nose, mouth and the openings in the skin…” (FGD 20, Mwanza).

Others reported high temperature, flu, severe headache, weakness, bloody diarrhoea, fatigue, skin rashes, dizziness, stiffness of muscles/muscle pains, and vomiting. Some participants reflected on the symptoms that the health authorities advise people to observe before going to the health facilities for further medical check-ups and support:

“…. symptoms includes high fever, cold, headache, diarrhoea and vomiting so it is advised that if you experience these symptoms, you have to quickly go to the health facility for a check-up and further support…” (KII 05 Mwanza).

A few of the participants described Ebola symptoms.

“The first symptom is the presence of high fever but also suffering from flu and getting tired … I mean one having fever and becoming weak, looking weak abruptly…can be vomiting but also having diarrhoea. By seeing those symptoms at once and I already heard about the eruption of Ebola disease, I can guess maybe it can be Ebola” (KII 20, Bukoba).

Other participants further depicted that there are first, middle, and later symptoms:

“… first symptom which is high fever above 48 C, followed by diarrhoea, rushes, vomiting and the last symptom is bleeding in the open parties of the body….” (KII 04, Mwanza).

On the other hand, some participants said they had the slightest awareness of EVD symptoms, saying they did not have personal experience or any experience with Ebola patients. As one of them illustrated:

“I cannot lie that I have seen a person with Ebola so that I can see and get to know its symptoms…” (FGD07, Bukoba).

Perceived EVD causes

Participants had mixed opinions on EVD causes. Many explained that people catch the virus by eating meat from wild animals such as chimpanzees, gorillas, monkeys, antelopes, cats, rabbits, and bats. Some said Ebola is a disease stemming from high fever, whereas others reported bacteria cause EVD, though none mentioned the name of the causative bacteria.

“Ebola is a disease caused by bacteria; other people say it results from eating too much of that chimpanzee meat…” (IDI 04, Zanzibar).

Similar sentiments were evident in Bukoba:

“It is a disease caused by a virus, and we are informed that…. that virus results from eating the wild animal’s meat….” (FGD16, Bukoba).

Misconceptions about EVD causes were also reported as participants mentioned a variety of situations and conditions as causes of Ebola:

“…It is an outbreak of the disease caused by a prolonged dirt environment, such as stationery water and dirt in one place, especially rotten things or drinking dirt or using dirt latrines can cause Ebola disease…” (FGD08, Missenyi).

Some participants believed Ebola was linked to the food people eat, their lifestyles, and dietary changes. It was cited that many people have foregone their traditions and culture consequently, diseases such as EVD emerged:

“…Another thing causing EVD…. are foods we eat. The foods we eat cause many diseases, and there has been an increase in these diseases. When we were growing up, for example, there were no grinding machines; people used to grind food on rocks, and nothing wrong happened, but these days, there are so many health problems because we have forgone our traditions and culture…” (FGD 20, Mwanza).

In one of the sites, the participants implied that Ebola was caused by the COVID-19 virus and that it spreads through the air:

“Ebola disease is caused by the COVID-19 virus and transmitted through air…” (FGD 2, Missenyi).

Despite many participants affirming to have heard about EVD, not everyone in the study knew the causes of such predicament, as reported by one of the study participants from Zanzibar:

“…mmm I hear there is a disease called Ebola. I just heard, but I am not yet aware of how it affects and what things are causing it…” (IDI010, Zanzibar).

Perceptions of EVD transmission

The study participants commonly reported that Ebola is an infectious disease transmitted from animals to humans and between humans. However, participants had mixed reactions on how the transmission occurs. Some reported that Ebola was transmitted by touching an infected animal’s secretions, including that of antelopes, chimpanzees, monkeys, and bats. Others opined that Ebola transmission occurs when people eat the remains of animals such as antelopes, baboons, bats, and chimpanzees. Some participants also mentioned human primates as sources of Ebola infection transmission.

Perceptions that EVD is a human-to-human transmission describe the transmission arising after touching the bodily fluids or secretions of those already infected, through unsafe sex, and or touching the dead Ebola victims. A common perception across sites was that human-to-human Ebola transmission occurred through physical contact (a) touching, handshaking, and hugging a person with the Ebola virus; fluids contact; (b) contacting bodily fluids or secretions like vomits, blood, mucus, and sweat from someone with the Ebola virus and (c) through air, see Fig. 1, Perceived dynamics of Ebola transmission.

Nevertheless, participants in several interviews and discussions claimed that EVD was airborne. In a few discussions, participants pointed out that some community members dismissed Ebola as a white people’s disease, hence excluding blacks:

“… so one says first of all that disease cannot reach me because I am an African and that the spreading of Ebola to reach me… no…yah…” (FGD22, Bukoba).

Perceived EVD susceptibility

Many participants across sites reported that, usually, everyone was susceptible to contracting EVD. However, people in the regions bordering the DRC and Uganda, the countries endemic to Ebola cases, were more susceptible to such infection. During FGDs in Kagera, the following testimonies emerged:

“Eeh for us at the border, we are susceptible to EVD…our region Kagera borders Uganda …it is very easy for us to get this disease because it is already affecting our colleagues from Uganda…” (FGD 22, Bukoba).

Similarly, in the Songwe region, participants referred to the EVD alert provided to people living close to the border area accordingly:

“They announced people close to the borders. … Tanzania and Zambia…. they should be careful, there is a dangerous disease called Ebola…” (IDI 18, Songwe).

Children, women, boda-boda riders, and fishermen emerged to be the groups at most risk of EVD should the disease emerge in Tanzania. For example, youngsters typically roamed around in villages, oblivious to the illnesses. They further reported that children played in dangerous environments and ate without taking precautions. Due to space constraints, school children were more likely to contract EVD because of how they sat in their classes—they sat close to one another in a congested manner. School children were additionally at risk for infections, including EVD, due to unsanitary toilets and inadequate access to Water and Sanitation Hygiene (WASH).

In several aspects, women were singled out as a particularly vulnerable demographic for EVD. Traditionally, women have handled all business-related tasks, such as product sales, caregiving for the sick, and market operations. It raises their risk of getting infected when they carry out their responsibilities. For instance, participants during discussions in the Kigoma region indicated that most people who use public transport to and from Kigoma town are women. Additionally, they claimed that a woman can easily catch the ailment if any family member becomes infected because she is the one who looks after sick family members:

“Women are the main caretakers of the family, including taking care of sick family members. They also work hard to support their family members economically. Even at the market, most traders are women; they travel a lot to … for business…” (FGD25, Kigoma).

While explaining the boda-boda riders’ susceptibility to EVD, participants raised the issue that they ferry many passengers to and from designated destinations without taking any safety measures. It was clarified that they routinely transport multiple passengers and do not sanitize their motorcycles between journeys and passengers. They do not set a cap on the passengers they can carry simultaneously. It was claimed that this practice made it difficult for boda-boda passengers to keep a distance, let alone avoid having physical contact with their respective boda-boda operators.

“I am thinking of the bodaboda riders because they ferry people from here and there and sometimes, they carry many people at a go ‘mshikaki’…. all squeezed at the back… no distance between them and no one with protection…and where one has helmet… they sometimes have only one helmet that is used by different passengers in different trips…. this can spread diseases… it is not possible to keep physical distance when many people are boarding the bodaboda …you see it is not that there is no distance between passengers but also there is none between the passenger and the boda-boda rider….” KII 18, Mwanza.

The fishermen and fisher folks were also reported to be vulnerable to contracting EVD. In this regard, the participants explained that fishermen are highly susceptible to EVD because groups of fishermen live in camps where they congregate in small huts to sleep. Furthermore, fishermen also sell their fish in congested spaces. Generally, the study’s findings indicated that crowded spaces are more likely to transmit EVD infection than other environments.

Perceived severity of EVD

Across sites, participants perceived Ebola as a serious, potentially fatal illness. The fundamental cause of the perceived severity was the belief that EVD is a rapidly spreading outbreak and that infected persons may not live for long. The inability to treat Ebola renders it a highly dangerous illness. In Mwanza, for example, when explaining the severity of EVD, a participant said:

“…Ebola virus disease is a threat. The information we receive, it is a disease which threatens life as it spreads in a short period and kills within a short while, so it is a threat….” (KII09, Mwanza).

Ebola was also perceived as a lethal ailment, being an airborne disease with no vaccine or cure coupled with the costly nature of treating related symptoms:

“Ebola is a dangerous disease. Once it enters an area, it spreads very fast; a single person with Ebola can transmit the infection to the whole village, the disease spreads through the air, and its symptoms are not easily treated, and it can be costly to treat…” (KII 40, Bukoba).

The participants also voiced that many people with EVD will not survive and that the illness’ symptoms are challenging to cure. Perceptions that Ebola is a deadly illness were also prevalent among the sites:

“When I hear there is Ebola disease, I already know there are many deaths to come… Ebola is a disease that kills many people…” (IDI17, Songwe).

Similar perceptions were recorded in Kigoma, where participants also insisted that in communal settings, once Ebola struck, it would be devastating in terms of cumulative death toll:

“In villages like ours in Tanzania where many people live in communal life if Ebola erupts, honestly, many people will get the infection, and many will likely die because seeing our way of living here, I can say it is very few who will survive…” (FGD35, Kigoma).

Perceived EVD preventive measures

Participants also provided their views on EVD safety measures. Not eating the flesh of wild animals (a) and avoiding close contact with Ebola-infected people (b) were two spontaneously reported means to stop the spread of the disease:

“…to prevent Ebola from transmission, one needs to stop people from eating the meat of wild animals…” (IDI 31, Songwe).

“…to prevent Ebola from transmission, one needs to stop people from eating the meat of wild animals…” (IDI 31, Songwe).

“…to prevent transmission of Ebola to many people, one needs to restrict physical contact with those already infected…” (KII 21, Mwanza).

“…to prevent transmission of Ebola to many people, one needs to restrict physical contact with those already infected…” (KII 21, Mwanza).

Another method of preventing the spread of Ebola was to avoid direct contact with the body fluids of infected individuals:

“to prevent Ebola disease… one should not get direct contact with the body fluids of the infected person and when avoiding the person is not possible… people should wear protection…” (KII01, Missenyi).

The other strategy for averting the spread of Ebola was to encourage prompt reporting of suspected patients to medical facilities. Participants believed that since EVD and malaria share many symptoms, individuals should report suspected cases promptly to medical personnel as soon as possible so that the illness may be confirmed for appropriate action to be taken. They claimed that by doing so, individuals may become more conscious of the need to protect themselves; after all, because blood oozing occurs later in the disease, an individual may become infected without realizing that Ebola is present in the community:

“I think the important thing if you find someone with those symptoms and you already heard there is Ebola, ask the person to rush to the health facility for confirmation and further steps because if you keep thinking it is malaria, the person will transmit the infection to many [more] people, and they will keep thinking it is malaria because that sign of bleeding everywhere in the body comes at a later stage of the disease when you see that you should know the disease is at a critical stage….” (KII 32, Kigoma).

Other strategies suggested for reducing the spread of Ebola included regular hand washing, avoiding unnecessary travel during outbreaks, and continuing community education on the disease and its preventive measures.

Discussion

This study explored community perspectives on EVD to understand its prevailing awareness, perceived causes, symptoms, the perceived transmission of the condition, susceptibility, severity, and preventive measures. Using an exploratory qualitative data collection approach enabled the study to gain insights into the shared perspectives on the condition among the communities of high-risk Ebola transmission regions. Qualitative studies describing community perceptions of Ebola in high-risk regions from a Tanzanian setting are limited [14]. Yet, such exploration is essential to inform targeted interventions for enhanced community preparedness and response to EVD should an outbreak occur, mainly due to the prevailing risk of EVD transmission between Tanzania and the affected neighbouring countries.

Generally, the study participants expressed awareness of the Ebola viral disease. Indeed, many of them mentioned one or more Ebola symptoms. Although limited awareness of Ebola in communities that had never experienced the condition would not come as a surprise, the expressed awareness could be attributable to the timing of the study, which was conducted a few months past the confirmed EVD cases in neighbouring countries of DRC and Uganda. Moreover, soon after the cases were confirmed in those countries, the Tanzania Ministry of Health intensified risk communication and community participation activities on EVD awareness raising in high-risk Ebola transmission settings in the country, including the country’s border regions. Ebola alerts were also dispatched throughout the country, and several media and health initiatives were launched to educate the public about the warning signs and symptoms of Ebola and to promote early medical attention when it arises. The majority of the participants learned about Ebola from the mass media, including radio and television, or from relatives who had direct contact with EVD-affected nations. When the study was conducted, Tanzania had not yet reported any instances of the disease. This finding underlines the importance of mass media communication, especially the radio due to its greater reach and accessibility, in communicating health-related information in settings with limited access to other media sources. In addition, the recurrence of Ebola in the border countries demands continuous Ebola risk communication in a bid to enhance community awareness and understanding to create necessary conditions for prompt reporting and identification of cases once they are spotted in their midst.

Despite the awareness observed in the study, some of the participants perceived Ebola as a new disease that is out there, that is, far removed from their immediate reality. Some participants also expressed a limited level of awareness of the disease’s symptoms, as explicated in the following responses, “I cannot lie that I have seen a person with Ebola so that I can see and get to know its symptoms”. Lack of awareness on Ebola signs and symptoms can have significant negative implications for individuals and communities in halting the spread of the disease once it erupts. This lurking danger underscores the need for continued Ebola awareness-raising, given that Ebola is highly contagious and often deadly disease [19]; believing it as a disease that is far from the communities in high-risk communities can exacerbate the outbreak and its consequences should it enter the country.

In the study, participants had varied perceptions of what causes EVD and how it is transmitted. Whereas some participants associated Ebola causes with bats, monkeys, and wild animals, others reported the condition is caused by high fever, a dirty environment, changing dietary patterns, and the COVID-19 virus. The misconceptions about the causes of Ebola, despite its higher community awareness, were also reported in Western Nigeria, where respondents associated it with a dirty environment [20]. Such misconceptions can lead to significant problems, including adopting ineffective prevention methods should there be an outbreak [4, 21]. It is well documented that the perceptions of disease aetiologies have a massive implication for the resultant responses [20, 22, 23]. Regarding transmission, many study participants had correctly perceived that EVD is transmitted from animals to humans (through touching infected animal secretions or eating the carcasses of animals such as chimpanzees and Bats) and between human beings (through physical contact or contact with bodily fluids of affected individuals). This finding conforms with those of a previous study whose respondents mostly linked eating of bush meat with Ebola transmission [4]. Furthermore, similar to what was reported in Guinea [21], participants in the study harboured misconceptions that Ebola is an airborne disease. As part of community epidemic preparedness efforts, risk communication and community participation strategies should continue raising awareness and correcting the prevailing misconceptions about Ebola to avoid negative implications for public health efforts. Such perceptions can stigmatize and discriminate against the affected individuals or communities when an EVD outbreak occurs.

In the study, participants also perceived that everyone in the community was susceptible to contracting Ebola. Higher risk perception to contacting EVD was also reported by the study in Sierra Leone [24]. However, in this study, motorcyclists-for-hire, popularly known as “boda-boda riders”, fishermen, women, and schoolchildren were perceived as more susceptible to Ebola infection due to their daily undertakings. “Boda-boda” operators were particularly singled out as the most vulnerable group to contact EVD infections due to their daily business operations that favour interactions with sizeable social networks, including transporting people and goods to and from various communities, as was also reported in Uganda [25]. Overcrowding and higher interactions of people in the context of EVD infections are reported to escalate the spread of the disease between and among community members [26]. These practices have implications for the “boda-boda” operators and for spreading the infection to other community members. Similar to the current study, livelihoods, including transportation and fishing, were reported to raise susceptibility to contracting epidemics, including Ebola, in besieged populations [16] due to group interactions and overcrowding during their activities and transactions.

Another population group that emerged to be one of most at risk of contracting EVD in the study comprises women in line with what was also reported in the Scoping review by Nkangu et al. [27]. In a typical African setting, women are primary caregivers in their families, who also care for the sick individuals including administering medication, feeding, bathing, washing clothes - despite lacking knowledge and resources for safe patient care [28]. Such practices expose women to infections [28] and heighten their vulnerability, as evidenced in countries hit by EVD. In such countries, EVD-linked mortality among women was reportedly higher [27] than that of men [29]. This enlightenment underscores the need for mainstreaming gender in risk communication and community participation interventions for epidemics, including EVD. In this regard, special attention should aim to capacitate women and enable them to take necessary precautions to prevent infections when performing their roles. Furthermore, a detailed assessment of gender dimensions of the vulnerability of women to EVD would yield valuable insights that could inform preparedness and response mechanisms during disease outbreaks.

Similar to the study participants’ perceptions, EVD is reported as a severe and highly fatal disease for humans [30, 31]. The study participants reported that EVD spreads quickly and kills infected individuals within a short time if it is not managed well. The virulent nature of EVD and the speed at which it kills is well-documented [32, 33]. The EVD incubation period stands at 2 to 21 days with an acute onset without any carrier status [34] with a typical fatality rate of almost 50% ranging from 25 to 90% [35]. Evidence further indicates that the perceived threat of illness has a higher chance of prompting positive actions toward its prevention and management [36]. There is a further need for risk communication and participation activities to capitalize on the community’s perceived severity of EVD infection to strengthen community preparedness and response mechanisms during an outbreak.

Participants in the study further perceived Ebola infections to be preventable or whose transmission could be curtailed to prevent the majority in the community from contracting the lethal ailment. In line with this study’s findings, frequent washing of hands, avoiding contact with infected individuals, and not eating bush meat were reported as prevention mechanisms against Ebola transmission in Guinea [21] and Uganda [37]. In the current study, prompt reporting of suspected Ebola cases to health facilities emerged as an appropriate way to limit the infection’s rapid spread in the community. Such prompt reporting could enable early identification of the infection and its management to prevent further transmissions [38]. In addition, such understanding must be emphasized when an outbreak occurs to allow not only confirmation of the cases but also prompt management, early notification of the outbreak, prevention of the transmission, and containment.

Limitations and strength

This study was conducted in regions bordering the DRC and Uganda, countries that have experienced several EVD outbreaks, including the outbreak reported close to the start of the current assessment. As such, the awareness of EVD and the perceptions observed in the study might have been influenced by that context. Likely, the assessment of EVD in the regions with low EVD risk could yield different outcomes. Thus, any generalization of the study findings needs qualification and consideration of similar contexts. Nevertheless, conducting REA and using different qualitative data generation approaches did not only enable the gaining of the emic perspectives on EVD but also allowed data triangulation, which enhanced trustworthiness of the study findings. The involvement of different population groups and multiple high-risk regions also enabled comprehensive coverage of community perspectives on EVD that would otherwise not have been possible with limited coverage. The study findings also provide valuable insights that can inform risk communication and community participation activities in designing context-specific interventions for preventing and responding to EVD, among other epidemics, during outbreaks.

Conclusion

This REA study in Ebola high-risk regions of Tanzania observed awareness of EVD signs and symptoms, its causes, perceived susceptibility, severity, and preventive measures, with slight variations among settings. To enhance community perspectives and readiness to respond to EVD outbreak, sustained risk communication and community participation activities on EVD prevention and responses are needed.
