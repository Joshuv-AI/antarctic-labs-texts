Chemical intuition on bond-dissociation energies as an emergent ability of universal machine-learning interatomic potentials
Authors: Shinnosuke Hattori, Kohei Shimamura, Ken-ichi Nomura, Aiichiro Nakano, Rajiv K Kalia, Priya Vashishta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine-learning interatomic potentials have demonstrated power-law scaling in predictive accuracy as training data and model capacity increase, but it remains unclear whether models trained at scale acquire interpretable chemical concepts. Here we show that an E(3)-equivariant machine-learning interatomic potential learns local bond information without direct supervision of bond properties. To expose this information, we develop an edge-wise emergent energy-decomposition framework and apply it to an Allegro neural-network potential trained on SPICE2, a dataset composed of stable molecular structures. The framework analyzes edge-wise energy contributions obtained from the trained model and their distributions together with information entropy to examine how data size, data composition and model-training scenarios shape the internal representation of chemical bonds. The resulting bond-dissociation energy estimates for archetypal bond types agree quantitatively with literature values and are consistent across models trained on organic and inorganic datasets. We further examine a hybrid training set and find that combining complementary chemical data improves transition-state energy-prediction accuracy while reshaping the learned bond representations. These results indicate that scalable interatomic potentials can acquire transferable bond concepts without explicit bond-energy labels, providing a way to analyze the transition-state energy-prediction problem.Subject terms: Atomistic models, Molecular dynamics

Introduction

Machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) and universal machine-learning interatomic potentials (uMLIPs) have advanced computational simulation by delivering near-quantum accuracy at substantially reduced computational cost1–3. Recent progress has been driven primarily by scaling—increasing model size and dataset diversity—leading to predictable power-law improvements in accuracy4,5. This scaling success has enabled software frameworks6–13, also large and diverse datasets including SPICE14,15, MPTrj13, Alexandria16, TM2317, OMat2418, OMol2519, and MatPES20. These advances have produced increasingly generalizable MLIPs for applications ranging from battery materials21, catalysts22,23, drug discovery24,25, and nanodevices12,26–28.

While current trends of MLIP development follow a “bigger and more diverse” strategy4,5,29, it has become increasingly clear that the strategy faces challenges in the prediction of complex chemical reactions22. Recent studies have shown that scaling based on conventional loss functions (energy, force, and stress) does not ensure smooth and stable molecular-dynamics (MD) trajectories30,31. To further advance uMLIP development32, a deeper understanding of the nature and characteristics of internal representations (IRs) within uMLIPs is needed33,34. Chorna et al. have compared uMLIPs using the feature reconstruction error35 and found that IRs are stored in a distinct manner. Edamadaka et al. have demonstrated an alignment of IRs across architectures and training-data modalities using 59 MLIP models34.

Accurate prediction of chemical reactions is one of the long-sought capabilities of MLIPs. Figure 1a,b schematically presents the potential energy surface (PES) and reaction pathway learned by an MLIP. A mechanistic understanding of reaction pathways and energy barriers may help accelerate materials development and synthesis. Therefore, many studies have addressed this problem, such as large-scale data-generation initiatives, e.g., the Open Catalyst Project22,23,36, and active learning for reaction modeling including ANI potential29. Recent development in foundation models has also demonstrated predictive capability for chemical reactions. These models have shown improved performance in chemical reaction prediction through training on a hybrid dataset that combines organic and inorganic materials data37,38.

Despite these advancements, it remains challenging to interpret how models learn IRs relevant to chemical bonding and reactions during training. For example, Fig. 1c illustrates a disparity in the scaling behavior of reaction energy (ΔE) and activation energy (Ea) using Allegro39 models trained on SPICE2 dataset14 and evaluated on Transition1x (T1x) dataset40. The accuracy of ΔE improves consistently with increasing training data in all tensor sizes, consistent with previously reported scaling behavior. In contrast, the predicted Ea plateaus around the data size of 105, hitting a “scaling wall" where additional data provides little to no benefit. This disparity is observed consistently across larger model sizes, indicating limitations beyond model capacity alone.

This observation of the scaling disparity has given rise to several scientific questions: Why does the model learn to predict ΔE while struggling with Ea? The training is performed solely on SPICE2 dataset14, which consists primarily of stable molecular structures and includes isolated atoms, small radicals, and molecules spanning neutral, cationic, and anionic charge states; however, it does not contain any transition-state (TS) geometries. At the same time, we observe that the prediction accuracy of Ea continues to improve as the size of training set grows to ≈ 105. While the scaling behavior of Ea is somewhat expected, elucidating the underlying mechanism of the distinct scaling behaviors may help further advance MLIP development. Are there underlying data representations that could explain the learning behaviors? Is there a metric to capture the mechanisms behind these scaling disparities and help quantify chemical information that is internally learned by an MLIP?

To address these questions, we propose an edge-wise emergent energy-decomposition (E3D) framework based on the chemical bond and its associated bond-dissociation energy (BDE)41. Unlike the conventional approach, where the total energy is defined as the sum of atomic energies, we consider the total energy as the sum of bond energies. Because the bond energy does not explicitly appear in the loss function, it provides a way to assess whether a model has learned relevant chemical information.

E3D further leverages a set of IRs, i.e., the symmetric (Dij) and asymmetric (Aij) terms given in Eq. (7). The two-dimensional probability distribution of Dij vs. Aij and associated Shannon entropy H2D enable quantitative assessment of training progress and provide a way to track when and how an MLIP learns bond-related representations during training.

In this study, we employ the E(3)-equivariant Allegro architecture39 and use E3D to characterize bond-related information learned by the model. We also apply E3D to “the unreasonable effectiveness of hybrid data”42, where TS energy prediction may be improved by hybridizing organic and inorganic materials datasets37,38. We evaluate hybrid training and discuss its relationship to the scaling wall problem. Additional methodological details, dataset summaries, and validation analyses are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Tables 1–6).

The main contributions of this study are as follows. First, we show that a scalable model trained on large materials datasets learns consistent bond-type-dependent representations, and that this behavior is observed across different materials datasets. Second, we show that probability distributions of IRs and their information entropy provide a tool for qualitative and quantitative evaluation across model-training scenarios. Third, we examine how a hybrid dataset affects TS energy prediction and discuss its implications for the scaling wall during training.

Results

Emergent bond-dissociation energy

First, we demonstrate that the Allegro model39 can acquire knowledge of BDE without explicit supervision. Specific constraints were imposed on Allegro to achieve output decomposition as described in Eq. (4). We trained the Allegro model using SPICE214 and evaluated the generalization performance using the T1x40 dataset. Subsequently, we analyzed changes in IRs with increasing amounts of training data and verified that this monitoring functioned appropriately.

Figure 2a compares Dij against BDEs from literature. The model generates a distribution that aligns well with established chemical trends. Not only is the order of bond energies for C-H, N-H, and O-H correctly predicted, but the average bond energies also show quantitative agreement with the reference BDE. The model also appears to have learned the concept of bond order correctly, for example, single, double, and triple bonds for C-C, C-N, C-O, and N-N bonds, despite the fact that the model was trained solely on the total system energy. We refer to this model capability as ‘emergent BDE’.

BDE is a thermodynamic quantity that is often evaluated by comparing the enthalpy difference before and after bond breaking43, for example D = EAB − EA − EB where EAB, EA and EB are the total energy of the molecule AB and the separated constituent fragments A and B, respectively44. A previous study using MACE models trained on a drug-like molecule dataset demonstrated that the aliphatic C–H BDE could be learned only with near equilibrium configurations24. These results indicate that a model trained on a large-scale dataset (SPICE2) can encode BDE-related information in its IRs, with learned values that quantitatively agree with literature values across bond types and bond orders.

The decomposed Dij values and their corresponding BDEs are enumerated in Fig. 2b for four molecules: methane, ethane, ethylene, and 4-methyl-1,2,3,5-oxatriazole.

Scaling of emergent BDE with data size

Our E3D framework enables analysis of how the quality of IR changes with dataset size, providing a means to analyze the scaling wall. As shown in Fig. 2c,d, the mean difference ΔEBDE and variance σBDE relative to reference BDEs show signs of convergence in energy-error reduction trends when training structure counts exceed 105 − 106. This corresponds to the position of the scaling wall in Fig. 1, indicating that BDE emergence may be related to predictive capability for reaction tasks.

Figure 2 c,d also illustrates the effect of tensor size on the scaling wall. While increasing the tensor size to 128 produces negligible changes in ΔEBDE and σBDE, reducing it to 32 increases σBDE, indicating that a tensor size of 64 performs best among the values tested.

To further assess the dataset dependency of the emergent BDE, we also applied E3D to an Allegro model trained on the MatPES dataset, most of which consists of inorganic crystalline data. Figure 3 shows the resulting Dij distribution, where the model encodes bond-type and bond-order features consistent with the MatPES data.

Distribution of internal representation entropy

Figure 4a shows the distribution of the IRs, the symmetric Dij and asymmetric Aij terms given by Eq. (7). We have examined the distributions over archetypal chemical bond types (C-H, C-C, C-O, C-N) across varying training conditions. Here, each training set with different size is randomly sampled from the entire SPICE2 dataset. The distribution function shows progressive development and structural refinement that evolve into isolated peaks as the training size increases. This result indicates increasing development of complex and multi-modal features inside the model. Each island of the distribution corresponds to distinctive bond characteristics such as bond order; therefore, the obtained IR distribution may be seen as a fingerprint of a given atomic pair.

Using H2D, we also quantify the changes in the distribution during training as a function of the training-set size. Figure 4b shows consistent decreasing trends in H2D with respect to the size of training set. The entropy computed from the combined distribution of all examined bond types converged at ≈ 105–106 training structures, as shown in Fig. 4b. Bond-resolved analysis reveals distinct learning behaviors. For C–C, C–N, and C–O bonds, ΔH2D steadily decreases between Ndata = 103 and 105, with an approximate slope of − 0.5 with respect to log(Ndata). The trend then substantially slows down for Ndata > 105. In contrast, ΔH2D of the C–H bond remains essentially flat, indicating convergence even with Ndata < 103. These results suggest greater complexity in the C–C, C–N, and C–O bond distributions than in the C–H bond distribution.

Discussion

The E3D framework provides a way to analyze how BDE representations develop within MLIPs. The emergent BDE may help explain MLIP generalizability because an MLIP that captures basic chemical building blocks can, in principle, support stable simulations of large molecular systems (e.g., proteins)5. Currently, most MLIPs are trained on system energy, atomic force, and system stress; therefore, other scalable MLIPs10–12 may have acquired similar IRs. Representative molecular-dynamics stability trajectories for crystalline silicon and aspirin are provided as Supplementary Movies 1 and 2.

The emergent BDE is achieved neither with a specific loss function nor inductive bias but is learned from data. Unsupervised BDE learning, therefore, leaves room for improved prediction accuracy. For instance, supervised BDE models such as BonDNet41 have achieved a prediction error of 0.022 eV, while the emergent BDE differs from literature values by about 0.3 eV. Here, two approaches may be considered: (1) explicit BDE supervision in the loss function, and (2) further increasing the size of training data. For example, OMat2418 and OMol2519 contain over 100 million structures, which is over two orders of magnitude greater than the dataset used in this study. While evaluating whether these approaches outperform existing BDE models is beyond the scope of this study, it is useful to assess them as model and dataset scales increase45.

We found that the emergent BDE is observed with the two widely-used large-scale materials datasets, SPICE2 and MatPES. This result suggests that the observed behavior is not specific to the SPICE2 dataset that predominantly consists of the bond types we examined, and is also present in the MatPES-trained model.

The IR distribution and information entropy provide insights into MLIP development and enable us to inspect the process from multiple aspects. By changing the data size, the distributions exhibit the formation of domains that go through the refinement of their boundaries and the shrinkage in size. In addition, such domains can split into further smaller clusters as the training set increases (see Fig. 4a). The result indicates that a better understanding of the local atomic environment and bond energies has developed inside the model.

The change in entropy with respect to the size of the training set provides insight into model training and correlates with model scalability. It is useful for comparing several training scenarios, for example, SPICE2 versus MatPES or different training-set sizes.

A declining trend shown in Fig. 4b suggests that the IR distribution converges to narrower peaks with a smaller variance. How many peaks (possible BDE values) exist depends on the training set, for example, the C-C bond with different multiplicity. The value of entropy could remain the same when the distribution barely changes (for example, C-H bond in Fig. 4a) or increases if more complex structures emerge as model training proceeds.

We found that the hybrid dataset exceeds the scaling wall with the SPICE2 dataset on the prediction of TS energy, achieving a further reduction of the MAE loss by ≈ 25% for Ea and ΔE. The increased entropy suggests the formation of more complex multi-modal IR structures than the one with SPICE2 alone. Material datasets that consist of molecules or bulk systems usually have very different characteristics due to their symmetries, boundary conditions, and underlying quantum mechanics theory used to generate training set; therefore, a common practice is to train separate MLIPs depending on target applications.

E3D analysis indicates that SPICE2–MatPES hybrid training improves out-of-distribution performance for TS energy prediction. Although the mechanisms underlying these gains remain to be understood, several studies have reported similar observations46–49.

In summary, we have developed the E3D framework that provides a mechanism-based perspective on the generalization of universal MLIPs. Current MLIP advancements largely rely on methodologies designed for generic AI models; a physically inspired method such as E3D may help guide analyses of model scaling and generalization limits.

Model and energy-decomposition formulation

We employ the E(3)-equivariant Allegro architecture39 for our analysis due to its inherent energy decomposability shown in Eq. (4). We trained Allegro models with internal tensor representation sizes of 32, 64, and 128, spherical harmonic expansion lmax=2, and radial cutoff rcut = 5.2 Å. For most analysis, we employed a tensor size of 64, prioritizing computational efficiency without compromising accuracy. This specific size proved optimal: a smaller tensor (size 32) resulted in lower accuracy due to its limited parameter count and thus reduced representational capacity, whereas a larger tensor (size 128) demonstrated comparable performance to size 64.

Allegro decomposes total system energy Esystem into per-node (atom) energies εi and per-edge energies εij39:

where σZi, σZiZj are learnable per-species scale parameters, μZi are learnable shift parameters for atoms of species Zi and Zj, N is the total number of atoms, and N(i) represents atom i’s local neighborhood. The Allegro model, which is based on the ACE formalism9 and combined with their connection via nonlinear functions, enables the expression of Esystem in terms of the per-edge energies εij39.

Therefore, to implement an architecture that satisfies Eq. (4) within the Allegro model, we standardize the normalization parameters by setting the shift parameters μZi=0 and scale factors σZi=σZiZj=1.0 in Eq. (5). Furthermore, instead of learning Esystem, the model is trained to predict Ecoh. With these settings, Eq. (5) becomes a direct sum of edge energies εij:

which is equivalent to Eq. (4).

Importantly, εij and εji are generally not the same (i.e., εij ≠ εji) reflecting the local environments of i and j atoms independently. Thus, these energy values differ based on which atom index to be taken as the central atom. We define a set of metrics, i.e., the symmetric component Dij and asymmetric component Aij, to capture the degree of the bond-energy strength and its asymmetry:

Here, Dij represents total bond energy between ith and jth atoms. The summation of Dij for all atomic pairs in the system, i.e., ∑iN∑j∈N(i),j>iDij is equal to Ecoh. The asymmetric component Aij quantifies energy imbalance arising from the difference in the local environment of the two atoms. For example if ith and jth atoms are of the same element, Aij reflects the difference between the two local environments.

Model training

All models were trained using the NequIP framework10 with the Allegro architecture39. For models with tensor size 64, the two-body latent multilayer perceptron (MLP) had dimensions [64, 128, 256], the later latent MLP had dimensions [256, 256, 256], and the final edge-energy MLP used one hidden layer with dimension 128. All models used three interaction layers. The models were optimized using Adam with a learning rate of 1 × 10−3. Batch sizes were varied between 8 and 128 depending on the training-set size. Training was continued until convergence according to the validation loss, with at least 100 epochs for each training run.

The model parameters were optimized by minimizing a weighted sum of root-mean-square errors for per-atom cohesive energy, atomic forces and, for periodic systems, stress:

where N is the number of atoms, Epred=∑iN∑j≠iεij is the predicted cohesive energy, Fref and σref are the reference forces and stress tensor, and Fpred and σpred are the corresponding predicted quantities obtained by automatic differentiation. The stress term was used only for periodic systems. The loss weights were set to λE: λF = 1: 1 for SPICE2 and to λE: λF: λS = 1: 1: 1 for MatPES and the hybrid SPICE2 + MatPES dataset.

Datasets and pre-processing

The molecular training data were taken from SPICE214, which contains stable molecular configurations. Inorganic training data were taken from the r2SCAN subset of MatPES20. The hybrid dataset was constructed by combining SPICE2 and MatPES. For molecular structures from SPICE2 used in hybrid training, the molecules were placed in a cubic cell with a side length of 500 Å and assigned zero target stress.

T1x40 was used as an out-of-distribution benchmark for reactive properties. For the T1x reactant, TS and product structures, energies and forces were recalculated using Psi450 at the same level of theory as SPICE2, ωB97M-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVPPD.

E3D protocol

We assigned bond multiplicities (single, double, triple, aromatic) using general Amber force field (GAFF) atom types from antechamber in AmberTools 2351, followed by manual validation for ambiguous cases. For aromatic bonds, 0.5 was added to the assigned bond multiplicity for the E3D analysis. Reference BDE values from experimental measurements52 and established theoretical values53 enable quantitative comparison with learned Dij distributions, providing a direct comparison between the learned representation and reference bond energetics.

In addition we define two key metrics, the BDE distribution shift ΔEBDE and the BDE distribution width σBDE, to enable a unified evaluation metric across different model training settings. ΔEBDE and σBDE are computed from a single distribution combining the BDE distributions of all bond types, each of which is shifted by their reference BDE value (see inset in Fig. 2).

To analyze correlations between the symmetric (Dij) and asymmetric (Aij) components of learned bond energies, we generated 2D histograms of Dij versus Aij. This analysis focused on C–C, C–H, C–O, and C–N from T1x dataset structures, considering covalent bonds shorter than 2.2 Å to ensure chemical relevance. We constructed these histograms using uniform 0.1 eV energy bin widths for both axes over their observed ranges.
