Evaluation of Xpert MTB/RIF assay accuracy for diagnosing TB and detecting rifampin resistance in patients with very low bacterial loads results: a retrospective study in Hunan, China
Authors: Xuan Zeng, Peilei Hu, Xinyun Tan, Hua Bai, Jue Wang, Daofang Gong, Jingwei Guo, Hengzhong Yi, Yi Liu, Yi Wu, Yunhong Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is widespread use of Xpert MTB/RIF for tuberculosis (TB) diagnosis and rifampin resistance (RIF-R) detection. Despite this, concerns have been raised about false-positive results, which are more prevalent in patients with very low bacterial loads.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of 717 patients at Hunan Chest Hospital from January 2022 to January 2023, all of whom had Xpert MTB/RIF results indicating very low bacterial loads. The accuracy of Xpert in diagnosing TB was evaluated against a composite reference standard (CRS), which classified cases via symptom, liquid culture, imaging assessments, immunological tests, and therapeutic response. In addition, rifampicin resistance was adjudicated using a composite reference standard of pDST and rpoB sequencing, with pDST as the primary reference for Xpert MTB/RIF results and sequencing used to resolve discrepancies.ResultsOf 717 patients with Xpert detected very low bacterial loads, 175 (24.4%) yielded positive cultures, while 16 cases identified as non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM). The positive predictive value (PPV) of Xpert for bacteriologically confirmed TB was 90.8% (159/175). For 542 culture-negative patients, Xpert’s PPV was 81.7% (443/542) in clinically diagnosed TB (80.4% pulmonary TB, 88.9% extrapulmonary TB). In 121 patients with pDST, Xpert for RIF-R had 68.4% sensitivity (13/19), 88.2% specificity (90/102), respectively. Among the 6 patients who were RIF-R by pDST but rifampin susceptible by Xpert, four cases harbored mutations inside the 81-bp Rifampicin Resistance-Determining Region (RRDR) region of rpoB.ConclusionXpert demonstrates good performance in diagnosing TB among the patients with results showing very low bacterial loads, especially in the culture-positive group and extrapulmonary tuberculosis group. However, its performance in detecting rifampicin resistance is not reliable. Our findings emphasize that Xpert results showing very low bacterial loads should be confirmed when diagnosing rifampin resistance tuberculosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12933-3.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a chronic disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), continues to be the leading cause of death from a single infectious disease in the world. In 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 10.8 million new TB cases globally, with China alone accounting for 6.8%, ranking third among the 30 high TB burden countries [1]. Early diagnosis of TB is critical to improving treatment outcomes for patients and reducing the transmission of TB more effectively.

In 2009, an automated nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), Xpert MTB/RIF assay (Xpert), was developed by Cepheid in the USA [2]. The Xpert is designed to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) and rifampin resistance simultaneously by using five overlapping molecular beacon probes, spinning the entire 81-bp Rifampicin Resistance-Determining Region (RRDR) of rpoB gene [3].This assay has been demonstrated to be simple, rapid, and accurate by many published literatures [4], and has been recommended by WHO as the initial diagnostic test in active TB persons [5].

However, concerns have been raised about the clinical interpretation of positive results of very low bacterial loads results on Xpert. The study revealed that even in cases with negative culture results, Mycobacterial DNA and Xpert positivity can persist for years following the completion of treatment [6, 7]. This poses a diagnostic challenge, as it is crucial to distinguish a positive result signifying active disease from one that represents past infection [8]. For example, studies have reported its false positive results in the detection of MTBC [9], and RIF resistance [10, 11], in clinical practice. Accumulating evidence suggests that false-positive RIF-R results are frequently associated with very low bacterial loads results on Xpert [12–14]. Consequently, some patients may receive unnecessary second-line drug treatments due to false-positive RIF-R results from Xpert testing [15]. Therefore, evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of Xpert in samples with very low bacterial loads is essential.

In this study, we performed a retrospective analysis of patients at Hunan Chest Hospital who had very low bacterial load results identified through Xpert testing. The primary objective is to evaluate the positive predictive value (PPV) of Xpert in patients with very low bacterial loads, and to further assess the clinical diagnostic value of this assay for patients with TB. The findings of the study could help to reduce the misdiagnosis of RIF resistance in TB patients with very low bacterial loads.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective diagnostic accuracy study was conducted at Hunan Chest Hospital (Hunan, China). We screened 717 patients with Xpert results indicating very low bacterial load who were consecutively enrolled between January 2022 and January 2023. All clinical specimens analyzed in this study were obtained at the initial point of diagnosis prior to the administration of any anti-tuberculosis therapy. From these patients, clinical data including demographics, registration category, and laboratory results were collected from electronic medical records. To guarantee the independence and blinding of the evaluation, classification against the composite reference standard (CRS) was conducted by an independent clinician who was masked to Xpert results and had access exclusively to data pertaining to tuberculosis diagnosis. Rifampicin resistance was adjudicated using a reference standard of pDST and rpoB sequencing, with pDST as the primary reference for Xpert results and sequencing used to resolve discrepancies.

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged ≥ 18 years were included if they met the following criteria: (1) clinical symptoms suggestive of tuberculosis; (2) an Xpert result indicating a very low bacterial load; (3) complete clinical, radiological, and laboratory follow-up data available; and (4) a positive mycobacterial culture with available first-line phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) results.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded from the analysis based on critically incomplete medical records, ongoing anti-tuberculosis treatment at enrollment, co-infection with HIV, or the presence of any other condition associated with severe immunodeficiency.

Patient categories

Diagnostic classification and reference standard

To comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic performance of Xpert in patients with a very low bacterial load result, Xpert results from the pulmonary and extrapulmonary samples were compared with the CRS to assess the accuracy of the test. According to the definition of CRS by Vadwai et al. (2011) [16], CRS is just a patients-categorizing method, which combines tests and criteria (e.g., microbiological, radiological, and clinical) to overcome the limitations of any one method. Patients included in the study were categorized into four groups based on the CRS from 2024 WHO TB Case Definition [17, 18].

1. Bacteriologically Confirmed TB

A case was defined by the isolation of MTBC from any clinical specimen (pulmonary or extrapulmonary) using liquid culture.

2. Clinically diagnosed TB (Culture-Negative)

Clinically diagnosed tuberculosis (TB) (culture-negative) was defined as cases without bacteriological confirmation, but with positive evidence of TB based on symptoms and physical examination, imaging, pathology, or biochemical tests. To establish a diagnosis, patients had to meet all of the predefined criteria specific to the site of disease (pulmonary or extrapulmonary), as outlined in Supplementary Table 1. Supplementary Table 1.Composite Reference Standard (CRS) Diagnostic Criteria for Pulmonary and Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis.

3. Indeterminate TB

Patients with negative culture and other tests who present symptoms suggestive of TB but fail to respond to empirical anti-TB therapy.

4. Not TB

All investigations, including TB culture and other relevant diagnostics, were negative, and the patients improved without receiving TB treatment.

Laboratory Examination

Xpert MTB/RIF

The Xpert assay for pulmonary specimens (sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions by use of the GeneXpert Dx software (version 4.0). For extrapulmonary specimen, pus secretions, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, urine, or pericardial effusion was concentrated by centrifugation prior to treatment with the sample reagent, and then was further processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for raw sputum. The Xpert system categorized the bacterial load as four levels, high cycle threshold (Ct) (Ct < 16), medium (Ct 16-22), low (Ct 22-28), and very low (Ct > 28).

Bactec MGIT 960 liquid culture and phenotypic drug susceptibility testing

Phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) was performed using the BACTEC™ MGIT 960 SIRE kit (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) according to the manufacturer’s procedure manual [19].Briefly, at least 2 ml of sputum was decontaminated with NaOH-NALC, then mixed with phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) and concentrated by centrifugation (3,000×g, 20 min). All extrapulmonary specimens, except cerebrospinal fluid (0.5 ml) that was directly inoculated into the MGIT tube, need decontamination similar to the method used for sputum. Notably, at least 10 ml of pleural fluid, urine, or pericardial effusion was concentrated by centrifugation (3,000×g, 20 min) before decontaminating. The processed specimen (0.5 ml) was added to a mycobacteria growth indicator tube (MGIT) for liquid culture, which was monitored for up to 6 weeks or until it was flagged as positive by the machine readout [19].

For positive MGIT tubes, MTBC was identified by MPT64 antigen testing (Genesis, Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MTBC-positive cultures were subjected to in vitro phenotypic DST according to the MGIT procedure manual [19]. If the positive MGIT tube tested negative for MPT64 antigen, nontuberculosis mycobacterium (NTM) or contamination need to be identified by Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) microscopy, reinoculating on blood agar, and para-nitrobenzoic acid (PNB) inhibition test (reinoculating on Löwenstein-Jensen (L-J) medium containing PNB (500 µg/ml)).

DNA extraction and rpoB sequencing

One milliliter of bacterial solution from the MTBC-positive MGIT culture tube was transferred to a sterile capped centrifuge tube and subsequently inactivated in a thermostatic metal bath at 85 °C, 20 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was carefully removed. The resulting precipitate was washed by adding 500 µl of saline, followed by repeating the centrifugation step, and removing the supernatant. The precipitate was then suspended in 100 µl of normal saline, mixed, and subjected to pyrolysis at 95 °C for 20 min, followed by sonication for 15 min. The centrifugation steps were repeated, and the resulting supernatant was directly sent to Beijing Ruiboko Biotechnology CoLtd. for sequencing. Sequencing data were subjected to quality control to filter low-quality reads (Phred score < 20) and remove adapter sequences. Valid sequences were then aligned to the MTB H37Rv reference genome (GenBank accession no. NC_000962.3) using SnapGene software (version.6.0.2) to identify rpoB gene mutations. Finally, the detected mutations were compared against the World Health Organization (WHO) Catalog of Mycobacterium tuberculosis Resistance Mutations [20] to determine their association with RIF-R.

Species identification of Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM)

Species identification of clinical isolates was conducted using PCR-reverse dot blot hybridization with the Mycobacterial Species Identification Kit (Shenzhen Yaneng Biological, Lot No. M202207001), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from the samples and subjected to PCR amplification of the mycobacterial 16–23 S rRNA internal transcribed spacer region using biotin-labeled primers. The PCR amplicons were then hybridized to a nylon membrane strip immobilized with species-specific oligonucleotide probes. Following an enzyme-linked colorimetric reaction, the hybridization pattern was determined by comparison with the standard reference chart for species identification.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as percentages and compared using either chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, based on sample size. Statistical significance was considered at a P value ˂ 0.05. All data were analyzed using SPSS version 21.0 statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Performance of Xpert MTB/RIF in patients with low bacterial load

Between January 2022 and January 2023, a total of 717 patients with very low load of MTBC by Xpert MTB/RIF assay were included in the study (Fig. 1), 24.4% (n = 175) were culture-positive for MTBC, and 75.6% (n = 542) were culture-negative. The positive predictive value (PPV) of Xpert MTB/RIF for combined bacteriologically confirmed TB and clinically diagnosed TB was 83.9% (602/717). Among 542 culture-negative patients, 443 patients were diagnosed as clinically diagnosed TB based on the CRS, which demonstrated that the PPV of Xpert in the culture-negative group was 81.7% (443/542). Among 175 patients with positive mycobacterial cultures, culture-confirmed MTBC in 159 cases and NTM in 16 cases (10 Mycobacterium intracellulare (MIN), 6 Mycobacterium abscessus (MAB)). With MTBC culture identification as the reference standard, Xpert MTB/RIF achieved a PPV of 90.8% (159/175) for bacteriologically confirmed TB. The lower than 100% PPV was attributable to the 16 discordant results, where Xpert was positive for MTB despite culture growth of NTM.

Diagnostic performance by specimen type

The performance of Xpert MTB/RIF for bacteriologically confirmed TB (culture-based) and clinically diagnosed TB is shown in Table 1. With culture as reference, Xpert showed high PPV in both pulmonary (91.6%) and extrapulmonary (85.0%) specimens, which was higher in sputum (92.9%) than in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid(BALF) (90.0%). Among patients with culture-negative but clinically diagnosed tuberculosis, Xpert maintained a high positive detection rate, with 80.4% in pulmonary specimens and 88.9% in extrapulmonary specimens. Detection rates were higher in BALF (83.3%) than in sputum (76%), with strong performance also observed in pleural effusion (86.1%) and pus secretions (87.9%). Several specimen types, including urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and biopsy samples, achieved 100% positive predictive value. Notably, among patients with clinically diagnosed TB, pulmonary cases comprised 240 (64.7%) new and 131 (35.3%) retreatment cases, while extrapulmonary cases were predominantly new (68, 94.4%) versus retreated (4, 5.6%).

The accuracy of Xpert MTB/RIF in rifampin resistance detection

Table 2 summarizes the clinical and demographic traits of 103 cases with consistently RIF susceptibility results by pDST and Xpert. The median age of patients was 50 years, with 77 patients (74.7%) being male. Among the patients, 12 (11.7%) had a history of previous TB episodes and 18 (17.5%) had comorbidities. Of the 11 RIF-R TB patients, 5 (38.4%) cases were retreatment cases, while in 90 RIF-susceptible cases, 83 (92.2%) cases were new cases. Our analysis revealed that patients with very low bacterial load who have a history of a previous TB episode were associated with an increased risk of RIF-resistant TB ([OR (95% CI): 7.411 (1.906 ~ 28.809); P = 0.004].

Based on these cohort characteristics, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of Xpert for RIF resistance against pDST (reference standard) in 121 culture-positive TB patients (Table 3). The assay exhibited high specificity (88.2%, 90/102) and negative predictive value (NPV, 93.8%, 90/96). Notably, Xpert exhibited suboptimal performance in detecting true rifampicin resistance (RIF-R), with a sensitivity of 68.4% (13/19) and a critically low positive predictive value (PPV) of 52.0% (13/25). Consequently, 48.0% (12/25) of Xpert-positive RIF-R cases were confirmed as rifampicin-susceptible by pDST.

rpoB sequencing for cases with discordant RIF results

Discordant results between Xpert MTB/RIF and pDST for RIF susceptibility were further analyzed, with detailed data provided in Supplementary Table 2. Of the 12 patients who were RIF-susceptible by pDST but classified as RIF-R by Xpert, sequencing of the rpoB gene identified one mutation (G236D) and three synonymous mutations (A1068A) outside the rifampin resistance-determining region (RRDR). For the six patients initially classified as six RIF-susceptible by Xpert but confirmed as RIF-R via pDST, four were found to have mutations (D435V, Q432E, H445R, Q429R) within the RRDR of the rpoB gene, while the remaining two had no detectable mutations. Notably, only half of false RIF-susceptible patients received second-line drug regimens, and in 12 false RIF-R patients all patients in this group received second-line drug regimens.

Discussion

Xpert MTB/RIF offers the advantages of rapid results and high sensitivity in detecting tuberculosis and rifampin resistance. However, as the use of Xpert becomes more widespread, challenges in result interpretation have emerged. A key issue is the clinical interpretation of positive results in patients with very low bacterial loads [21]. In such cases, a positive MTBC signal, while analytically true, may not always indicate active, replicating disease and could represent residual DNA from a prior infection [22, 23]. This is particularly concerning for rifampin resistance detection, as incorrect results may lead to unnecessary treatment with second-line anti-TB drugs, contributing to the rise of drug-resistant patients [24]. The interpretation of Xpert results for rifampicin resistance is complicated by the potential for false positives, which can be caused by silent rpoB mutations, heterogeneous resistance, very low bacterial loads, delayed probe binding, and mutations at disputed resistance sites [10, 25, 26]. In this study, we systematically analyzed 717 patients with Xpert MTB/RIF test results showing very low bacterial loads. Our aim was to assess Xpert’s PPV and its clinical diagnostic value in the diagnosis of TB, thereby helping to reduce the misdiagnosis of RIF resistance in TB patients with very low bacterial loads.

According to the CRS, Xpert showed a PPV of 81.7% (443/542) in 542 culture-negative patients, with 18.3% of Xpert-positive results being indeterminate or confirmed not-TB, demonstrating that a false positive rate is present in these patients. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported false-positive Xpert results in culture-negative patients, especially those with a history of tuberculosis treatment [27, 28]. Boyles et al. [28]. emphasized that previous anti-TB treatment is a significant risk factor for false-positive Xpert results, as residual nucleic acid fragments or laboratory contamination may lead to positive signals in the absence of viable MTBC. Study showed that Xpert has the potential to detect genetic material from residual, non-viable organism [29], which highlights the need for caution when interpreting the very low MTBC load results in TB diagnosis.

Our study found that the PPV of Xpert for extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) (88.9%) was higher than that for PTB (80.4%), which can be attributed primarily to two factors. First, our analysis focused on samples with very low bacterial loads, where EPTB specimens such as pleural effusion and pus secretions generally feature a cleaner microbial background, lacking the upper respiratory tract flora and polymicrobial contamination commonly present in sputum from PTB patients, leading to higher positive rate of Xpert detection. A Meta-Analysis reviews also confirm that Xpert has reliable diagnostic accuracy for EPTB [23]. Second, in our research the proportion of retreatment patients in the EPTB group (5.6%, 4/72) was significantly lower than that in the pulmonary TB group (35.3, 131/371). Retreatment patients are prone to false positives due to residual non-viable MTB DNA detectable by Xpert, and this interference is more prominent in PTB sputum [6]. This finding highlights the need for context-specific interpretation of Xpert results, particularly for retreatment PTB patients with low bacterial loads.

In the present study, Xpert illustrated a PPV of 90.8% (159/175) in culture-positive patients, we observed 16 cases in which the specimens were culture-positive for NTM (10 MIN and 6 MAB) but tested positive for MTBC by the Xpert assay. The presence of a mixed infection is a plausible explanation for this discrepancy [30]. Liang et al. [31]demonstrated that the Xpert which targets the MTBC (entire complex)specific genetic- locus (IS6110) is specifically designed only to detect MTBC, meaning it cannot detect NTM. That means, mixed infections involving MTBC and NTM are present in these discordant cases. This occurs because conventional culture methods may fail to detect the coexisting MTBC, likely due to the overgrowth or competitive growth of NTM [32, 33]. Future studies employing metagenomic sequencing directly on primary samples, could definitively confirm this hypothesis.

One finding of our study was that Xpert has 31.6% (6/19) inaccurate results in the detection of RIF resistance among cases with very low bacterial loads. Studies have demonstrated that the discordance rate between Xpert and MGIT 960 in detecting RIF resistance ranges from 16.2% to 55.1% [14, 34, 35]. In the study by Ocheretina [36] et al., low bacterial loads may be the reason that causes this discordance. Specifically, in our study,12 patients were diagnosed as RIF-R by Xpert but RIF susceptible by pDST. Among these cases, 8 isolates have no mutation in the entire rpoB gene, while 4 isolates had mutations outside of rpoB RRDR region. False-positive results can arise from unequal probe binding efficiency across different target sequences. Additionally, probe delay [37], a widely discussed factor, has long been acknowledged by the assay manufacturer to contribute to false-positive RIF-R results, the cycle threshold (Ct) used to determine RIF-R increased from 3.5 to 5 in 2010 and then adjusted to 4 in the G4 assay [38]. Furthermore, the difference in Ct values between probes used to determine RIF resistance should be redefined, with a general threshold of > 4 cycles, to enhance the accuracy of resistance detection and minimize the risk of misinterpretation.

Another finding of our study was that, among six patients identified as RIF susceptible by Xpert but RIF resistance by pDST, four cases exhibited mutations in the rpoB gene. These included two relatively more frequent mutations (D435V, H445R) and two less common variants (Q432E, Q429R) [39]. Notably, these mutations are not common causes of phenotypic resistance in existing studies [40], and their clinical significance remains unclear [18], this explains why Xpert failed to detect RIF resistance. Such false-negative Xpert results have been rarely reported, suggesting their uncommon occurrence. Mixed infection of MTBC strains may be another contributing factor, as such infections may also lead to false-negative results. Zetola et al. [41]demonstrated in their study that the presence of both wild-type sequences, detectable by the probes, and resistant sequences in mixed MTBC populations can make the system inherently more prone to false-negative results. This occurs because the probes may preferentially bind to and detect wild-type sequences, potentially hiding the presence of resistant mutations and thereby leading to underestimation of drug resistance.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design inherently carries risks of selection bias and incomplete data capture; prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate and extend these findings. Second, the high cost of Xpert testing prevented the repeated testing on a significant number of samples with low levels of bacterial loads, which may have limited the ability to assess reproducibility or resolve borderline/indeterminate results in this paucibacillary subgroup. Finally, pDST for RIF resistance used a critical concentration of 1 mg/L rather than the current WHO- recommended 0.5 mg/L for MGIT system, which may lead to overestimation of phenotypic susceptibility and potential missed detection of borderline or heterogeneous RIF resistance.

Conclusion

Our retrospective study demonstrated that Xpert MTB/RIF results indicating a very low load of MTBC performed reliably for the detection of tuberculosis. However, in the diagnosis of RIF-resistant tuberculosis, clinicians should be cautious when interpreting the very low load results due to its limitations. The performance of Xpert MTB/RIF in detecting RIF resistance under conditions of very low bacterial load warrants further investigation across diverse clinical settings and larger specimen cohorts.
