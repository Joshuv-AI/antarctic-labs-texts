Predictive value of three insulin resistance surrogate indices for symptomatic hypoglycemia risk in hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Heng Tang, Zhifeng Chen, Shenfu Wang, Wen Li, Wen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInpatient hypoglycemia is a serious complication in hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), associated with adverse outcomes. While insulin resistance (IR) is central to hyperglycemia, its relationship with hypoglycemia risk in acute care settings remains unclear. Convenient IR surrogate indices, such as the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C), and metabolic syndrome insulin resistance index (METS-IR), warrant evaluation for hypoglycemia prediction.AimsTo evaluate and compare the predictive utility of three IR indices (TyG, TG/HDL-C, METS-IR) for in-hospital symptomatic hypoglycemia in T2DM patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 660 hospitalized T2DM patients (April 2024–April 2025) with ≥8-day stay and valid CGM data. Symptomatic hypoglycemia was defined by CGM < 3.9 mmol/L, capillary glucose confirmation, and clinical symptoms. Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence analysis, multivariable Cox regression, restricted cubic splines, time-dependent ROC, and decision curve analysis were used.ResultsDuring follow-up, 193 (29.2%) patients developed symptomatic hypoglycemia. TyG and METS-IR were inversely associated with symptomatic hypoglycemia risk (per 1-SD increase: HR = 0.680, 95%CI = 0.600–0.773; HR = 0.748, 95%CI = 0.680–0.823), with linear dose-response relationships. TG/HDL-C showed no significant association. TyG exhibited superior model fit (lower AIC), better predictive performance on hospital day 2 (AUC = 0.721), and higher net clinical benefit at 20%–30% risk thresholds. TyG may be more closely linked to symptomatic hypoglycemia risk in patients with good glycemic control, while METS-IR may show a stronger association in those with a longer diabetes duration.ConclusionTyG and METS-IR are valid predictors of in-hospital symptomatic hypoglycemia in T2DM patients. TyG demonstrates optimal predictive performance and clinical utility, supporting its integration into early risk stratification for individualized glycemic management.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the most prevalent chronic metabolic diseases worldwide, with a central management challenge being the maintenance of glycemic homeostasis to prevent long-term complications and reduce the risk of acute events [1]. However, in clinical practice, particularly in the inpatient setting, hypoglycemia has emerged as an increasingly serious problem [2]. Hospitalized patients with T2DM are at high risk of significant glycemic fluctuations due to a combination of factors, including acute illness, intensive adjustments to therapy (especially insulin intensification), concomitant medications, changes in nutritional intake, and accompanying hepatic or renal dysfunction [3]. Studies have shown that in-hospital hypoglycemic events are closely associated with prolonged length of stay, increased healthcare costs, elevated short- and long-term mortality risks, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, posing a significant threat to patient prognosis [4, 5].

From a pathophysiological perspective, insulin resistance (IR) is not only a core driver of hyperglycemia but may also influence glycemic variability and the risk of hypoglycemia by inducing fasting and postprandial hyperinsulinemia, impairing counter-regulatory hormonal responses (e.g., glucagon), and being accompanied by β-cell dysfunction [6, 7]. In recent years, a series of surrogate indices for IR derived from routine clinical biochemical and anthropometric parameters have gained attention in metabolic risk assessment due to their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ease of repeated monitoring [8]. Among the various surrogate indices for insulin resistance, the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), the triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C), and the metabolic syndrome insulin resistance index (METS-IR) possess unique advantages due to their independence from exogenous insulin administration. They have been demonstrated in multiple studies to be significantly associated with the risk of developing T2DM, the incidence of cardiovascular events, and the prevalence of metabolic syndrome [9, 10]. Some studies even suggest that the predictive performance of these indices may surpass that of the traditional homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [11].

However, existing research has not sufficiently explored the association between surrogate indices for insulin resistance and the risk of hypoglycemia in hospitalized patients. The relationship between insulin resistance and hypoglycemia risk remains unclear in hospitalized populations with complex conditions and intensive therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, studies that systematically evaluate and compare the predictive value of these convenient insulin resistance indices for hypoglycemic events in real-world inpatient settings are still relatively scarce.

Therefore, to bridge this knowledge gap, this study focused on a cohort of hospitalized patients with T2DM. Using comprehensive statistical modeling, we investigated the direct association between IR and hypoglycemia risk. The aim was to clarify the potential clinical utility of these readily available indices for inpatient glycemic risk management, thereby providing an objective basis for the early identification of high-risk patients and guiding individualized monitoring and intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participant selection This study was a retrospective observational cohort analysis based on real-world electronic clinical information. Using CGM to accurately define hypoglycemic events, it integrated multiple analytical approaches including Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence analysis, multivariable Cox regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS) models, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The study systematically evaluated and compared the associations of three IR surrogate indices—TyG, TG/HDL-C, and METS-IR—with the first occurrence of in-hospital hypoglycemia in patients with T2DM. The study population consisted of 660 patients with T2DM who were hospitalized at Tongling Municipal Hospital between April 2024 and April 2025. Participants were selected according to the following criteria:

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Diagnosis of T2DM (ICD-10 code: E11); (2) Hospital stay ≥ 8 days; (3) Continuous wearing of the AiDEX® X Microtech (Hangzhou, China) CGM device—which records glucose measurements every minute—on the upper arm during hospitalization, with valid data coverage ≥ 70% (invalid data were defined as missing values or outliers caused by sensor detachment, signal loss, temperature/motion interference, or device calculation errors); (4) Daily calibration passed (calibrated once daily before breakfast using fingertip capillary blood glucose, with a difference < 10% between CGM and capillary values) [12].

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Missing key laboratory data required for calculating IR indices; (2) Admission diagnosis including hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar state, lactic acidosis, severe trauma, or uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, undergone surgical treatment during hospitalization, and other patients requiring advanced-level care/monitoring; (3) Current glucocorticoid therapy.

In-hospital glycemic management adhered to the following protocol [1, 13]: For patients initiated on continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), the total daily dose was calculated at 0.5–0.8 U/kg body weight. Of this, 40–60% was delivered as a basal infusion, with the remaining dose distributed equally as premeal boluses (1:1:1). For those treated with a basal-bolus regimen (four daily injections) or premixed insulin (two to three daily injections), the starting dose was 0.4–0.5 U/kg/day. In patients receiving non-insulin antihyperglycemic agents (with or without concomitant insulin), the specific agents and regimens were individualized based on the patient’s previous therapy, degree of glycemic control, residual β-cell function, and the presence of cardiovascular or renal comorbidities. Treatment regimens were adjusted every 1–3 days, with a target fasting plasma glucose (FPG) of 4.4–7.0 mmol/L and non-FPG < 10.0 mmol/L. A confirmatory capillary blood glucose measurement was required within 10 minutes if CGM values were < 3.9 mmol/L or >20.0 mmol/L. A difference within 15% was considered acceptable, with the CGM value recorded; if the discrepancy exceeded 15%, the capillary value was used, and the CGM device was calibrated accordingly [14].

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongling Municipal Hospital (Approval No. K2025004). All data were anonymized, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Sample size calculation This study employed the Cox proportional hazards regression model for analysis. Based on previous studies [15] reporting an incidence rate of approximately 30% for hypoglycemia requiring medical intervention in hospitalized T2DM patients, and adhering to the principle of at least 10 events per variable (EPV) [16], the required effective sample size was calculated. Assuming an anticipated inclusion of 10 independent variables, the calculated effective sample size was approximately 345 subjects. Accounting for an estimated 10% rate of data missingness or invalid samples, the final planned minimum total sample size was determined to be 530.

Definition of exposure variablesThe primary exposure variables were three surrogate indices of IR [8, 11]: METS-IR = ln[2 × FPG (mg/dl) + TG (mg/dl)] × BMI/ln[HDL-C (mg/dl)]; TG/HDL-C = TG (mg/dl)/HDL-C (mg/dl); TyG = ln[TG (mg/dl) × FPG (mg/dl)/2]. TG = triglycerides, BMI = body mass index.

All blood samples for laboratory measurements were uniformly collected at 6 a.m. on the day of hospital admission or the next day (within 24 hours after admission), following a standardized overnight fast of at least 8–10 hours. All laboratory tests were performed by the Department of Laboratory Medicine, Tongling Municipal Hospital, in accordance with standardized operational procedures and quality control protocols.

Endpoint and observation period The endpoint was the first occurrence of symptomatic hypoglycemia during hospitalization. Symptomatic hypoglycemia was defined as a validated blood glucose level < 3.9 mmol/L, accompanied by hypoglycemia-related clinical symptoms (e.g., palpitations, tremor, sweating, dizziness, hunger) documented in nursing and progress notes. For simplicity, the events are referred to as “hypoglycemia” or “hypoglycemic events” in the subsequent text.

The observation period was defined as the first 8 days of hospitalization (from admission through day 8). Patients who did not experience hypoglycemia by the end of this period were censored. This timeframe was chosen based on the study cohort’s clinical features: the median hospital stay was 8 days (only 12.12% of patients stayed longer), and hypoglycemic events in hospitalized T2DM patients predominantly occur in the early hospitalization phase, with a marked reduction in frequency after the first week as glycemic control stabilizes.

Data sources and diagnostic criteria Data were sourced from the Tongling Municipal Hospital Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system and the Microtech glucose management platform (CGM data). Two uniformly trained researchers independently collected data using a back-to-back, double-entry method. Data collected included demographic information, laboratory results, clinical diagnoses, medication history, CGM data, and nursing records. All data were entered into a dedicated spreadsheet. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior researcher through review of the original medical records.

Comorbidity diagnoses were based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes, determined by integrating both admission and discharge diagnoses. Covered comorbidities included diabetic complications and common chronic/metabolic diseases. Admission reasons were categorized as follows: (1) Poor glycemic control: documented history of T2DM with ongoing glucose-lowering interventions (including medications, diet, or physical activity) and repeated FPG > 7 mmol/L or non-FPG > 10 mmol/L; (2) Newly diagnosed T2DM: no prior diagnosis of T2DM, no history of glucose-lowering treatment, accompanied by typical symptoms of diabetes; (3) Other: including patients admitted primarily for the management of diabetic complications or those transferred to the endocrinology department after treatment in other departments.

Statistical analysis Nine continuous variables contained missing values, with an average missing rate of 4.49%. Missing data were imputed using a multiple imputation approach. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2) and SPSS 27.0. A two-sided P-value ＜0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Kaplan-Meier analysis Each IR index was categorized into tertiles: low (Q1), medium (Q2), and high (Q3) level groups. Cumulative incidence curves for hypoglycemia were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and group differences were compared using the log-rank test. If the overall difference was significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction.

Cox proportional hazards regression models Multivariable Cox regression models were constructed to evaluate the association of each IR index with hypoglycemia risk. Potential confounders were initially screened via univariable analysis (p < 0.05). Variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) >10 were excluded to mitigate multicollinearity, forming the final set of adjustment variables. For each IR index, models were built using a stepwise adjustment strategy: Model 1 (minimally adjusted), Model 2 (partially adjusted), and Model 3 (fully adjusted). Model fit was assessed using the −2 log-likelihood (−2LL) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with lower values indicating better fit. The likelihood ratio test was used to compare differences in −2LL between nested models [17]. The discriminative ability was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index. The net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the improvement in predictive performance. Differences in Harrell’s C, NRI, and IDI were tested using the Bootstrap method (1000 resamples) [18].

Dose-response relationship and predictive performance assessment RCS with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) were fitted within the fully adjusted Cox models to analyze potential nonlinear dose-response relationships between each IR index and hypoglycemia risk. The likelihood ratio test compared the linear model with the RCS model. ROC curve analysis was used to assess the predictive ability of each index for hypoglycemia, with the area under the curve (AUC) compared using DeLong’s test. Time-dependent AUC (tdAUC) was calculated to evaluate the dynamic discriminative ability of the fully adjusted Cox models. Differences in tdAUC across time points between models were compared using the Bootstrap method (1000 resamples). Due to technical limitations in defining the risk set and computational instability at very early time points, logistic regression was used to assess predictive performance specifically for the first hospital day [19].

Clinical utility assessment DCA was employed to evaluate the clinical net benefit of prediction models based on different IR indices. Net benefit refers to the clinical gain achieved by using the model to guide decisions compared to the strategies of “intervene-all” or “intervene-none” across a range of threshold probabilities. In this study, the intervention strategy implied by the model included intensified glucose monitoring and hypoglycemia prevention education, and adjustment of glucose-lowering regimens.

Heterogeneity and robustness assessment To test the robustness of the findings, subgroup analyses were performed based on 23 predefined variables, including age, sex, diabetes duration, and CGM-derived metrics. Interaction terms (“IR index × subgroup variable”) were introduced into the fully adjusted Cox models. The significance of the interaction effect was assessed using the likelihood ratio test (P for interaction < 0.05 considered significant, indicating effect modification by the subgroup variable).

Cox proportional hazards regression VIF diagnostics indicated multicollinearity for BMI (VIF = 17.10) and FPG (VIF = 15.50). To maintain consistency in the set of confounders adjusted across all IR indices, both BMI and FPG were excluded from all subsequent Cox models. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed for each index: Model 1 (minimally adjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, eGFR, and initial antidiabetic treatment regimen), and Model 3 (further adjusted for TG and HDL-C). TG/HDL-C was not significantly associated with hypoglycemia risk in any model (all p > 0.05) and was therefore omitted from further analysis.

The regression results indicated that both METS-IR and TyG were significantly associated with hypoglycemia risk. Per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase, the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.748 (95% CI: 0.680 ~ 0.823) for METS-IR and 0.680 (95% CI: 0.600 ~ 0.773) for TyG.

Discussion

Through systematic comparison of three IR surrogate indices, this study identified both the TyG index and METS-IR as factors significantly associated with hypoglycemia in hospitalized patients with T2DM, based on multivariable Cox regression. Specifically, each 1-SD increase in the TyG was associated with an approximately 32% reduction in hypoglycemia risk (HR = 0.680, 95% CI: 0.600, 0.773), while a 1-SD increase in METS-IR corresponded to an approximate 25% risk reduction (HR = 0.748, 95% CI: 0.680, 0.823). Subgroup analyses further suggested potential differential associations: TyG appeared to have a stronger link with hypoglycemia risk in patients with apparently well-controlled glucose, whereas METS-IR may be more informative for the risk in those with longer diabetes duration. Moreover, RCS analysis indicated an approximately linear inverse relationship between both indices and hypoglycemia risk (P for nonlinearity > 0.05), supporting their use as continuous variables for risk stratification without evidence of a distinct threshold effect. Further comparison of model performance revealed that the TyG demonstrated better model fit (lower AIC), a stronger discriminative trend (higher C-index), and significantly superior risk reclassification (higher NRI and IDI) than METS-IR. Although both indices showed moderate overall discriminative ability (AUCs: 0.663 vs. 0.640), tdROC analysis provided a key clinical insight: TyG exhibited significantly better predictive performance specifically on hospital day 2, indicating its particular utility during the early, most intensive phase of therapeutic intervention. DCA further supported the clinical utility of both TyG and METSIR for hypoglycemia risk evaluation. For patients within the 20–30% intermediate hypoglycemia risk range (calculated by the Cox model), implementation of interventions resulted in greater net clinical benefit. Such interventions might include intensified care and patient education, closer monitoring of diet and physical activity combined with continuous glucose monitoring, individualized guidance on disease selfmanagement, setting relatively lenient glycemic targets, and using milder glucoselowering regimens. According to the tertile values in this study, a TyG level < 8.84 or a METSIR level < 36.09 may warrant clinical attention and prompt further hypoglycemia risk assessment, although these thresholds and interventions require future validation.

Potential mechanisms linking IR to hypoglycemia risk: Previous studies have reported that lower HOMAIR is associated with higher hypoglycemia risk at comparable HbA1c levels, consistent with our observed inverse relationship between IR indices and hypoglycemia [20]. This can be explained by differential insulin sensitivity and routine inpatient treatment practices. Patients with poorer admission glycemic control often receive immediate intensification of glucoselowering therapy (e.g., insulin initiation/titration) [21]. Severe IR buffers against rapid glucose declines due to impaired insulin signaling [22]. whereas mild IR increases susceptibility to therapeutic interventions, leading to steeper glycemic drops and higher hypoglycemia risk.

Our data support this mechanism: insulin use rose sharply after admission, and hypoglycemia peaked on hospital day 2—coinciding with the most intensive treatment phase—and accounted for over onethird of all events. This aligns with prior evidence linking aggressive inpatient glycemic control to increased hypoglycemia [21, 23], a pattern also reflected in the NICESUGAR trial [24].

It should be noted that acutephase IR indices such as TyG and METSIR integrate metabolic parameters (e.g., FPG, TG) that are influenced by both baseline IR and acute stressors [25]. Thus, they likely represent a composite of chronic metabolic resistance and acute metabolic instability [26]. Static IR measures may not fully capture dynamic glucoregulatory changes, including progressive βcell dysfunction [27]. Therefore, the observed inverse association more plausibly reflects a link between overall metabolic state—including treatment responsiveness—and hypoglycemia risk, rather than a direct physiological effect of IR per se.

Why the TG/HDL-C ratio lacks predictive utility: The TG/HDL-C ratio is a classic marker of IR-related dyslipidemia and primarily reflects chronic metabolic disturbances. Hepatic IR drives elevated TG levels, which promote very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) synthesis and inhibit glucose-stimulated insulin secretion via the glucose-fatty acid cycle, while simultaneously exerting lipotoxic effects on pancreatic β-cells [28, 29]. Concurrently, IR impairs high-density lipoprotein (HDL) metabolism through cholesterol ester transfer protein (CETP)-mediated lipid exchange and dysfunction of the ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), resulting in the formation of smaller, less functional HDL particles [30]. Crucially, these lipid alterations develop and stabilize over a prolonged timescale. As a result, lipid profiles possess considerable metabolic inertia and long biological half-lives [31]. This inherent stability means the TG/HDL-C ratio responds poorly to the rapid metabolic shifts triggered by acute illness and therapeutic adjustments in the hospital setting. Consequently, while informative of chronic dyslipidemia, it demonstrated limited short-term predictive utility for hypoglycemia in our study.

In contrast, TyG and METS-IR integrate FPG, a key variable highly sensitive to acute metabolic status. This integration allows them to capture the intensity of early metabolic derangement, establishing a direct link with subsequent hypoglycemic risks triggered by therapeutic interventions.

Superior performance of TyG vs. METS-IR: The distinct superiority of TyG in clinical utility is largely attributed to its composition of TG and FPG. Together, these markers effectively capture hepatic gluconeogenesis impairment and acute glucotoxicity [8]. This combined glucolipotoxic burden enables TyG to more precisely identify patients who are in a high-risk metabolic state and may exhibit heightened sensitivity to insulin therapy. Conversely, while METS-IR integrates BMI and HDL-C to account for body composition and chronic inflammation, these parameters may demonstrate a delayed response or functional impairment during acute metabolic stress encountered upon hospitalization [32]. Furthermore, evidence also suggests that TyG demonstrates superior performance to METSIR in predicting the onset of diabetes, which further supports its sensitivity to early metabolic changes [33, 34].

Differential clinical utility of IR indices in subgroups: Subgroup analysis further suggests potential distinct associations of these indices with hypoglycemia risk. In patients with apparently well-controlled glycemia (e.g., mean glucose ＜8.5 mmol/L), TyG showed a significant association with protection against hypoglycemia (HR = 0.19). This finding suggests that TyG, by integrating fasting glucose and triglycerides, may potentially identify individuals with “metabolic vulnerability”—a hidden state of dyslipidemia and early β-cell dysfunction that standard glycemic metrics may overlook [8, 28]. Consequently, TyG may serve as a valuable tool for risk assessment in these patients, who are at high risk due to underlying metabolic instability despite normal surface readings.

Conversely, the protective benefit of METS-IR was most pronounced in patients with a diabetes duration ≥ 10 years (HR = 0.92). This aligns with its composite nature, which incorporates BMI alongside lipid profiles. For long-standing diabetic patients, METS-IR likely reflects cumulative metabolic burden, including visceral adiposity and chronic inflammatory status, rather than acute glycemic fluctuations [35]. Thus, METS-IR may provide potential insights into the physiological reserve capacity of complex chronic conditions, which may assist in treatment decisions that balance glycemic control with the preservation of metabolic stability.

In summary, these results suggest that TyG and METS-IR may be complementary in predicting hypoglycemia during hospitalization: TyG may be particularly useful for detecting acute metabolic fragility that might otherwise be masked by stable glycemic control, while METS-IR could serve as a valuable tool for assessing long-term metabolic background in patients with a prolonged disease history. This differential performance potentially offers a rationale for selecting appropriate screening tools based on individual patient characteristics.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center, retrospective observational study, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables—such as insulin dosage, insulin type, and the use of secretagogues—may persist despite multivariable adjustment. Second, the insulin resistance indices were based on a single measurement taken early during hospitalization, and the observation period was limited to 8 days. Therefore, we could not capture dynamic metabolic fluctuations resulting from treatment adjustments or changes in clinical status, nor could we identify late hypoglycemic events. Additionally, this study only evaluated the predictive value of three insulin resistance surrogate indices for hypoglycemia risk and did not compare them with other conventional predictors (such as prior hypoglycemia history or renal function). Thus, it cannot fully assess their relative contribution within a multivariable prediction model.

Looking ahead, future studies should prioritize multicenter prospective designs to enhance generalizability and reduce selection bias. Serial measurements of insulin resistance indices, combined with continuous glucose monitoring-derived metrics, could better capture dynamic metabolic shifts. Furthermore, comprehensive predictive models that integrate these indices with conventional risk variables (e.g., prior hypoglycemia, renal function, and medication regimens) should be developed and compared to determine their relative contributions. Ultimately, translating validated indicators such as the TyG into clinical decision-support systems for real-time risk stratification, followed by interventional trials, would help evaluate their effectiveness in reducing hypoglycemia incidence and improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This study confirms that among hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes, IR surrogate indices—particularly the TyG and METS-IR—are inversely associated with symptomatic hypoglycemia risk. The TyG have the optimal discriminative performance and clinical utility, and may effectively assist in assessing high-risk patients with apparent glycemic control but underlying acute metabolic fragility. METS-IR, on the other hand, may show value in assessing the cumulative metabolic burden in patients with long-standing diabetes. In contrast, the TG/HDL-C ratio lacks predictive power in this acute inpatient setting.

These findings suggest that incorporating convenient indices such as the TyG into inpatient glycemic risk management frameworks could aid in the early identification of patients at high risk for symptomatic hypoglycemia, providing an objective basis for individualized monitoring and intervention. Future prospective studies are warranted to further validate their clinical applicability.
