Oral 5-aminolevulinic acid combined with sodium ferrous citrate prevents blood–aqueous barrier breakdown after anterior chamber paracentesis in healthy beagle dogs
Authors: Yuya OTAKA, Kazutaka KANAI, Aoi TOMATSU, Riyo SANGU, Daiki OKADA, Noriaki NAGAI, Yohei YAMASHITA, Yoichiro ICHIKAWA, Aki SAKAI, Kazuki TAJIMA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the preventive effect of 5-aminolevulinic acid combined with sodium ferrous citrate (5-ALA/SFC) on blood–aqueous barrier (BAB) breakdown induced after anterior chamber paracentesis (ACP) in beagles. 5-ALA/SFC (1/0.64 mg/kg or 3/1.92 mg/kg) or carprofen (4.0 mg/kg) was orally administered daily for 7 days prior to ACP. Then, a sample of the aqueous humor (AH) was collected from one eye via ACP (first sample) and again 60 min later (second sample). The protein and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) concentrations in both samples were measured. Compared with the control group, high-dose 5-ALA/SFC and carprofen significantly reduced the AH protein and PGE2 concentrations in the second sample. Our findings suggest that 5-ALA/SFC suppresses BAB breakdown in dogs.

The blood–aqueous barrier (BAB) of the anterior uvea of the eye acts as a defense mechanism to control the entry of substances from the blood into the eye. This barrier is formed by tight junctions that interconnect endothelial cells of iridal blood vessels, as well as nonpigmented cells of the ciliary epithelium [4], thereby preventing the entry of plasma proteins and cells into the aqueous humor (AH). BAB breakdown has been reported to induce anterior uveitis in both humans and dogs following surgical trauma (i.e., cataract surgery) or experimental procedures (i.e., anterior chamber paracentesis [ACP]) involving the anterior segment of the eye [15, 17].

ACP involves the aspiration of AH from the anterior chamber of the eye. In clinical practice, ACP is performed for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, including rapid reduction of intraocular pressure, screening for infectious diseases, and intracameral injection of drugs [6]. Since the inflammatory response to AH aspiration is predictable, this technique is useful in research, particularly drug evaluation [1, 28, 29].

ACP in dogs induces BAB breakdown [15]. Prostaglandins (PGs) are inflammatory mediators, and PGE2, the most commonly reported PG in BAB breakdown [32], can be quantified in canine AH [9]. PGE2 is involved in BAB stability and is considered a pivotal mediator in the canine ACP model [24, 25].

Anterior uveitis is a frequently observed ophthalmologic disease in veterinary clinical practice [19]. Chronic or severe anterior uveitis leads to various complications, such as glaucoma, cataracts, or retinal detachment, which can cause blindness [31]. Uveitis generally requires causative treatment. Since uveitis in dogs is often idiopathic [19], causative treatment is challenging, so uveitis is mainly treated with anti-inflammatory drugs [10, 11]. In human and veterinary medicine, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are widely used in addition to corticosteroids for uveitis treatment [2, 10, 11]. Since long-term use of steroids and NSAIDs may cause ocular and systemic side effects [10, 11], it is crucial to search for drug candidates for uveitis that have fewer side effects.

Cyclooxygenase (COX) is involved in the biosynthesis of PGs. COX-1 produces PGs necessary for normal physiological functions such as wound healing and intraocular pressure regulation. COX-2 produces large amounts of pro-inflammatory PGs during inflammation [18]. Suppression of COX-2 is important in controlling inflammation in uveitis [18]. NSAIDs, which are COX-2 selective drugs, have been used to treat uveitis in dogs and cats because COX-1 inhibition causes gastric disturbances and other side effects [13]. Carprofen, a COX-2 selective NSAID, has been shown to be effective in the prevention of BAB breakdown in the canine ACP model [24].

5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) is a natural amino acid commonly found in animals [12], and it is a frequently used agent in photodynamic therapy in humans. 5-ALA is synthesized in the mitochondria and converted by the heme synthesis pathway to protoporphyrin (PpIX). Subsequently, heme is produced by chelation of PpIX with iron [7]. It has been reported that 5-ALA is more efficiently utilized by the body when administered in combination with sodium ferrous citrate (5-ALA/SFC) [22]. 5-ALA has several bioactive effects, including antiviral [20] and immunomodulatory effects [7], and its usefulness has also been reported in veterinary practice, such as the application of PpIX in the diagnosis of malignant tumors [21] and efficacy in treating lipid metabolism disorders in dogs [27].

We previously demonstrated the anti-inflammatory effects and molecular mechanisms of 5-ALA/SFC in endotoxin-induced uveitis rats and subsequent PGE2 inhibition in the AH [23]. However, the anti-inflammatory effects of 5-ALA/SFC in dogs have not been elucidated. Thus, this study investigated the preventive effect of orally administered 5-ALA on the inhibition of BAB disruption in dogs by measuring protein and PGE2 concentrations in the AH.

This study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Kitasato University, Japan (approval no. 20-045). A total of 20 beagle dogs without ocular abnormalities (12 sexually intact males and 8 sexually intact females; age, 2–5 years; weight, 10.3–11.7 kg) were obtained from the Institution for Animal Reproduction (Ibaraki, Japan). They were housed in individual cages in a temperature-controlled environment with a 12-hr light cycle and fed dry food (CLEA Dog Diet CD-5M; CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) once a day at 8 AM.

Ophthalmic examination included slit-lamp biomicroscopy of the anterior segment (SL-17; Kowa American Corp., Los Angels, CA, USA), indirect ophthalmoscopy (20D BIO Lens; Volk Optical Inc., Mentor, OH, USA), Schirmer tear test (Merck Animal Health, Rahway, NJ, USA), fluorescein staining (I-Glo; JorVet, Jorgensen Laboratories, Loveland, CO, USA), and intraocular pressure measurement by rebound tonometry (iCare TONOVET Plus; iCare, Espoo, Finland).

The dogs were randomly divided into a control group and three treatment groups (n=5 per group; three males and two females). A low dose (1/0.64 mg/kg) or high dose (3/1.92 mg/kg) of 5-ALA/SFC (ENeALA Neopharma Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or 4 mg/kg of carprofen (Rimadyl; Zoetis Japan KK, Tokyo, Japan) was orally administered once daily at 8 AM for 7 days before ACP. The control group received only food, according to the same schedule as the treated groups.

ACP was performed on one eye using the method described in our previous work [28, 29]. Briefly, a coin toss was used for each dog to determine which eye would undergo ACP. Then, the dogs were sedated with a combination of medetomidine (Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan), midazolam (Astellas Pharma Inc., Tokyo, Japan), and butorphanol (Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) administered intravenously at doses of 0.01, 0.15, and 0.025 mg/kg, respectively. Before each paracentesis, the eye was washed out with 0.625% povidone-iodine diluted with saline, and then 0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride (Benoxil ophthalmic solution 0.4%; Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was applied. Next, 0.5 mL of AH was aspirated over 15 sec in a controlled manner using a 27-gauge needle. After 60 min, a second AH sample was obtained in the same manner as the first. Following paracentesis, atipamezole hydrochloride (0.05 mg/kg; Nihonzenyaku Industry Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan) was administered intramuscularly. Following the second paracentesis, the dogs treated with 0.05% difluprednate (STEROP ophthalmic emulsion 0.05%; Senju Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and 0.3% ofloxacin (Tarivid ophthalmic solution 0.3%; Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) three times a day for 5 days.

The AH samples were centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 5 min, 4°C) immediately after collection. The protein concentration of an aliquot of the resultant supernatant was determined, and the remainder was stored at −80°C until measurement of PGE2 concentrations. The protein concentration in the AH was measured in duplicate on the day of sampling using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a microplate reader (iMARKTM microplate reader; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

The supernatants of the first and second AH samples were diluted 2-fold and 20-fold, respectively, with sterilized saline. The PGE2 concentration was analyzed in duplicate using a commercial Prostaglandin E2 Express ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) after diluting the supernatants of the first and second samples 10-fold and 100-fold, respectively, with the ELISA kit buffer. The absorbance was measured with the microplate reader at 405 nm and then calculated the PGE2 cytokine release level.

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using the open-source software EZR v1.55 [14]. Paired t-test was used to analyze protein and PGE2 concentrations in the first and second AH samples. One-way analysis of variance and Tukey post hoc multiple comparison tests were used to analyze differences in the protein and PGE2 concentrations in the AH between groups. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

In this study, 5-ALA/SFC reduced the protein and PGE2 concentrations in the second AH sample compared with the control group. PG is an important factor in stabilizing the BAB in clinical cases and other uveitis types [9, 24, 26, 30]. In this canine ACP model, PGE2 is a major inflammatory mediator and is associated with BAB breakdown [24, 25]. Therefore, 5-ALA/SFC might have suppressed BAB breakdown by inhibiting PGE2, leading to a decrease in protein concentration in the second AH sample.

Recently, natural compounds that reduce experimentally induced uveitis have attracted attention as novel uveitis treatments [3, 8, 16]. The canine ACP model has been used to evaluate products that already exist, such as steroids and NSAIDs [1, 28]. To our knowledge, only one study, which was performed by our group, has reported the use of natural compounds in a canine ACP model. This study concluded that orally administered tyrosol (200 mg/kg) significantly suppressed protein and PGE2 concentrations in the second AH samples [29]. Although simple comparisons are difficult due to differences in ACP techniques and dosing frequency, it should be noted that 5-ALA/SFC (3/1.92 mg/kg) and tyrosol (200 mg/kg) significantly suppressed protein and PGE2 levels in the second AH samples to the same extent as carprofen.

Our study has some limitations. First, the metabolic pathway of 5-ALA/SFC administered orally to dogs remains unelucidated. However, the metabolism of orally administered 5-ALA in dogs has been confirmed [5]. Furthermore, 5-ALA has been used in dogs as a reagent for the detection of malignant tumors [21]. Orally administered 5-ALA caused vomiting in 50% and 88.9% of normal dogs at doses of 30 mg/kg and 60 mg/kg, respectively [5]. In our study, no dogs exhibited vomiting or other adverse effects. However, further investigations are necessary to evaluate the safety and toxicity of 5-ALA/SFC. Second, the canine ACP model used in this study may not replicate actual clinical cases of uveitis. In cases of canine uveitis, various cytokines are involved, not only PGE2 [30]. Although our study confirmed that 5-ALA/SFC suppressed PGE2 levels in the second AH sample, its effects on other cytokines were not assessed. Third, we have not been able to examine the mechanism of suppression of PGE2 production in the canine AH. Our previous study in endotoxin-induced uveitis rats revealed a mechanism by which ALA reduces COX-2 expression in the iris ciliary body region where BAB is present and PGE2 production in the AH via suppression of the nuclear factor-κB pathway and activation of the nuclear factor E2-related factor 2/heme oxygenase 1 pathway [23]. In the ACP model, the mechanism of inhibition of the PGE2 production is not yet understood, and further studies on COX involved in PGE2 production are needed.

In conclusion, this is the first report of orally administered 5-ALA/SFC suppressing protein and PGE2 concentrations in AH following ACP, thereby significantly inhibiting ACP-induced BAB breakdown in a canine model with an efficacy similar to that of carprofen. Thus, oral administration of 5-ALA/SFC can prevent BAB destruction in dogs and has potential for use as an adjunctive therapy in the treatment of uveitis.
