To see with new eyes
Authors: Chui Ming Gemmy Cheung
Published: 2024

Marcel Proust’s celebrated quote, “The real voyage of discovery consists not in seeking new lands but in seeing with new eyes,” urges us to perceive the world around us through different lenses to discover new wonders. The advances in ocular imaging has literally given us new eyes to make observations previously not possible and develop new understanding of many retinal conditions. These developments not only enable documentation of the pathology but also facilitate the understanding of the progression pattern and the development of various classification systems which shaped the management of common retinal diseases. Starting with color fundus photography, the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study classification guided the use of laser photocoagulation for proliferative diabetic retinopathy and clinically significant macular edema.[12] With the advent of dye-based angiography using fluorescein and indocyanine green, choroidal neovascularization could be defined and subtyped at baseline and its activity monitored following treatment with laser photocoagulation or photodynamic therapy.[34] Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) is the current go-to imaging tool in every retinal practice. The ability to observe fluid in the various compartments of the retina noninvasively has been used to guide treatment with antivascular endothelial growth factor therapy in macular neovascularization, diabetic macular edema, and retinal vein occlusion.[5] Some practices have even gone “virtual” with physicians assessing only the OCT for clinical decision-making. With the unprecedented efficiency of OCT, one may ask then “What more do we need to see for our daily clinical requirements?”

This special issue of TJO brings together a collection of highly clinically relevant reviews and original articles of how advances in imaging are bringing clinical care to the next level. Highlights include new developments in the management of geographic atrophy (GA) and inherited retinal diseases (IRDs). Cohn and Guymer comprehensively reviewed the advances in multimodal imaging in GA.[6] The review particularly highlighted the important role of fundus autofluorescence (FAF) together with SD-OCT-based definitions for diagnosing GA.[7] FAF is the most commonly used endpoint in clinical trials to evaluate GA lesion growth.[8910] In the context of complement inhibition therapy, better understanding of which patients will benefit most from therapy is urgently needed. Based on the published data to date, imaging biomarkers indicative of fast-growing GA lesions include extrafoveal location, multifocality, abnormal junctional zone FAF pattern, and the presence of reticular pseudodrusen. However, there remains an important knowledge gap in the understanding of GA, notably the limited and inconsistent data from Asian populations.[111213] This highlights the urgent need for the Asian retinal communities to address this gap by systematically collecting progression data among Asian patients with GA using FAF and SD-OCT. In addition, the limited effect size and lack of functional improvement in the latest clinical trials suggest that treatment may need to start at even earlier stage. This calls for the need to find biomarkers predictive of progression from an earlier stage.[1415] Imaging studies have suggested that photoreceptor loss may precede retinal pigment epithelial loss.[16] Biomarkers based on SD-OCT evaluating outer retinal changes such as nascent GA or ellipsoid zone attenuation are currently being evaluated. The role of choriocapillaris ischemia in GA development is also being studied, particularly using OCT angiography.

IRD is another previously untreatable disease area which has recently seen groundbreaking development. Voretigene neparvovec-rzyl (Luxterna, Novartis, Switzerland) is the first ophthalmic gene therapy targeting biallelic RPE-65-mediated Leber congenital amaurosis, and the possibilities of other gene and cell-based therapies are on the horizon. The need for precise diagnosis is highlighted in the review by Lee et al.[17] IRD encompasses a wide range of inherited condition which can predominantly affect different parts of the retina. Understanding the unique property of each imaging modality is, therefore, essential to select the most relevant tools to diagnose and monitor each condition. The ability of ultra widefield imaging further complements the traditional imaging modalities to evaluate IRDs that have a propensity to affect the peripheral retina. EYS-associated retinitis pigmentosa, one of the most prevalent causes of IRD in East Asia, was highlighted as an example with seemingly genotype-specific FAF patterns. In many other IRDs, however, a combination of phenotype and genotype pattern can often provide a more comprehensive workup. The case series by Shakeel et al. described varied presentation of bestrophinopathy exhibiting different OCT findings according to the genotype.[18] Beyond diagnosis, imaging advances can be expected to also impact on therapy for IRD. With most gene- and cell-based therapies using the subretinal injection route, improved precision with intraoperative OCT is expected to improve outcomes through more precise control of the subretinal bleb.[19] The current status of intraoperative OCT and clinical applications are discussed in two articles in this issue (Rocha et al.; Chang et al.).[2021]

Finally, the rich imaging datasets provide the perfect opportunity to deploy artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted algorithms for various diagnostic or prognostic tasks. The rapid advances in AI technology in retinal imaging are summarized in the review by Ruamviboonsuk et al., covering key developments from convolutional neural networks to vision transformers to foundation model.[22] While diagnosis of DR based on color fundus photographs has been the most advanced application studied, potential future roles may include the use of AI to identify high-risk biomarkers for disease progression and to select high-risk patients for participation in future GA interventional studies. The ability of AI to analyze large datasets such as the UK biobank which combines imaging and genetic and clinical information is expected to be able to address many key scientific questions.[23]

With the ever-increasing sophistication of our imaging technology, there is an abundance of features yet to be described or explained that may hold the key to understanding disease mechanisms and potential new management strategies. Retinal researchers and clinicians alike will make the most of these technologies by interrogating the images that pass through us daily with “new eyes,” asking not just “What is the diagnosis” but also “Why does it look this way.” Please enjoy this special issue of TJO on imaging.
