Factors associated with quiet quitting among public health specialists in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Burak Kurt, Cemal Koçak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuiet quitting, a passive disengagement involving minimal work effort, has surged post-pandemic amid workloads, burnout, and undervaluation, particularly in healthcare. Despite their critical role in health management, public health specialists in Türkiye often face role ambiguity and limited administrative authority, potentially fostering ‘quiet quitting’—a state of passive disengagement. The aim of this study is to evaluate quiet quitting attitudes and identify associated factors among public health specialists in Türkiye.MethodsA cross-sectional online study was conducted from August 1 to September 30, 2024, targeting 601 Ministry of Health public health specialists. A simple random sample of eligible public health specialists was invited to participate in an online survey, and 241 completed a questionnaire comprising a Personal Information Form (sociodemographics, work characteristics, satisfaction) and the Quiet Quitting Attitude Scale (QQAS). The questionnaire was pilot-tested, and informed consent was obtained. Chi-square, Student’s t, ANOVA, Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, post-hoc analyses, Logistic and linear regression were applied in the evaluation of the data.ResultsOf the participants, 18.2% viewed the future of public health in Türkiye as very hopeful or hopeful, 34.5% considered changing their specialty, 38.2% wanted a transfer from their current province, and 27.8% considered resigning. In the logistic regression model (QQAS ≥ 103), being aged 40 years or older, absence of an administrative position (OR = 6.73, 95% CI: 3.20–14.16), perceiving specialty training as incompatible with the field, a hopeless outlook on the specialty’s future (OR = 9.56, 95% CI: 3.17–28.82), and desires for transfer and resignation were independently associated with higher quiet quitting attitudes. In the linear regression model, the same factors were significant, with the addition of 4–6 years of specialist experience and dissatisfaction with the public health specialty. Marital status, presence of psychiatric illness, and total medical experience were significant in univariate analyses but did not retain independent associations in the multivariate models.ConclusionQuiet quitting attitudes among public health specialists were associated with professional hopelessness and role-education mismatch. These findings suggest that targeted institutional efforts to better align job descriptions with specialist training and to improve career outlooks may help sustain workforce engagement.

Introduction

Recent changes in work life have accelerated rapidly, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Alongside intense workloads and pressures, the feeling of being undervalued has driven employees toward thoughts of resignation [1]. To escape this stressful environment, workers have adopted various coping strategies, with “quiet quitting” emerging prominently among them [2].

Quiet quitting is not actual resignation but rather a form of passive protest, where employees refuse to exert more effort than strictly necessary [3]. The term is conceptually defined as a psychological withdrawal where employees restrict their effort to the minimum requirements of their job descriptions, eschewing extra-role behaviors [4]. Unlike burnout, which signifies emotional exhaustion, quiet quitting functions as a coping mechanism or a ‘strategic withdrawal’ to preserve mental health and personal resources, grounded in the Conservation of Resources theory and the Job Demands-Resources model [5, 6]. It manifests as performing only the minimum required work, avoiding extra time and energy expenditure, and declining additional tasks [7]. This attitude is closely linked to workplace stress, burnout, and communication deficiencies [8].

Factors contributing to quiet quitting encompass excessive workloads, inadequate compensation, lack of personal time, poor administrative support, unclear expectations, and communication issues [9]. At the individual level, it negatively affects job satisfaction and performance while weakening motivation, organizational commitment, increasing conflicts, and hindering teamwork [10–12]. Organizationally, quiet quitting diminishes productivity and overall workplace motivation, ultimately impairing the organization’s innovation and competitive advantage [13–15].

Healthcare workers have experienced increased levels of depression, anxiety, stress, fatigue, and insomnia amid worsening working conditions, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic [16]. Over time, as their sense of appreciation diminished, they grew disengaged and began exhibiting quiet quitting attitudes [17]. Quiet quitting among healthcare professionals adversely affects patient care, safety, teamwork, and productivity, posing challenges for institutions and service recipients [18].

In Türkiye, public health specialists earn their title after completing 4 years of specialization training in university public health departments—a program entered via a central exam following 6 years of medical education. These specialists are defined as physicians who assess societal health levels, identify problems, develop health policies, pinpoint causes, propose solutions, and manage all levels of health services [19]. As of 2025, Türkiye has 949 public health specialists, 726 of whom work within the Ministry of Health [20].

Public health specialists in the field have reported the most significant problems as unclear job descriptions, being perceived as a “wild card”, and lacking sufficient authority [21]. Additionally, they experience moderate emotional exhaustion and high burnout levels in the area of personal accomplishment [22].

Quiet quitting represents a significant concern for public health specialists, who already experience low job satisfaction. While quiet quitting among healthcare professionals has garnered recent attention, a significant void exists in understanding this phenomenon specifically within public health specialists. This gap is particularly critical given their unique roles in societal health, often characterized by unclear job descriptions, perceived lack of authority, and high burnout, which may uniquely shape their quiet quitting attitudes and associated factors in Türkiye.

This study aimed to evaluate quiet quitting attitudes among public health specialists working in the field and to identify associated factors, thereby shedding light on how their unique professional challenges in Türkiye influence this emerging phenomenon.

Materials and methods

Research design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted online from August 1 to September 30, 2024, targeting public health specialists working in the field across Türkiye. The population comprised 601 public health specialists employed under the Ministry of Health. The minimum sample size was calculated as 234, assuming a 95% confidence level and 50% expected frequency. To account for potential non-response, this was increased by 10% to 257 participants, selected via simple random sampling. Ultimately, 241 participants were included in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected using an online questionnaire distributed via messaging application. The target sample of 257 individuals was randomly selected from this group using computer-generated random numbers (Microsoft Excel RAND function), after the member list was downloaded and verified against the Ministry of Health’s official roster to ensure completeness and remove any duplicate or inactive accounts. The messaging group is administered by the Association of Public Health Specialists and membership is a routine for all currently active public health specialists, constituting a comprehensive sampling frame. Selected participants were contacted through the messaging application and requested to complete the questionnaire. To increase participation, non-respondents were followed up after two weeks, achieving responses from 94% of the targeted sample (241 / 257).

Informed consent was obtained prior to participation. A pilot test was conducted with 10 public health specialists, after which the questionnaire was revised based on their feedback; these responses were excluded from the final analysis.

Quiet Quitting Attitude Scale

Quiet Quitting Attitude Scale

The Quiet Quitting Attitude Scale, developed by Karaşin and Öztırak in 2023 specifically to assess quiet quitting attitudes—a phenomenon distinct from broader constructs like burnout or work disengagement—was selected for its direct measurement of this emerging behavior. As the first scale originally created in Turkish exclusively for healthcare workers, it is particularly suitable for the Turkish context. The scale features three relevant dimensions: Personal Reflection, Positive Attitude, and Perception of Mobbing, addressing workplace bullying prevalent in healthcare and ensuring cultural validity and nuance for public health specialists. In the validity and reliability study involving 473 healthcare workers, the scale’s Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was 0.938 overall and 0.954, 0.823, and 0.648 for the sub-dimensions, respectively. The KMO value was 0.818, indicating a sufficient sample size for factor analysis. The lowest factor loading was 0.476, while the eigenvalues explained 53.877% of the cumulative variance—a ratio acceptable for social sciences. Additionally, the CFI and RMSEA fit indices in the DFA analysis fell within acceptable ranges, confirming the scale’s adequacy for development. The scale has no cut-off point, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward quiet quitting [23]. In this study, analyses were conducted based on the total score of the scale.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 30. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and histogram inspections. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables.

To compare Quiet Quitting Attitude Scale scores across groups, independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were utilized for normally distributed data. For variables with non-normal distributions, the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied. Categorical associations were assessed using the Chi-square test.

Independent variables found to be associated with the dependent variable at the p < 0.25 from univariate analyses were evaluated for their prediction of the scale score using linear regression analysis, and for scores of 103 and above (the median value of the scale score) using logistic regression analysis. In linear regression analysis, the Durbin-Watson coefficient within the range 0 ≤ DW ≤ 4, VIF values within 1 < VIF ≤ 5, tolerance above 0.20, condition index below 30, and p < 0.05 for model fit were considered appropriate. Linear regression was performed using the Backward method. In logistic regression analysis, model fit was evaluated with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test p > 0.05. Logistic regression was performed using the Backward LR method.

Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Candidate independent variables for multivariate analyses were selected based on theoretical grounding in the Job Demands-Resources model and Conservation of Resources theory, as well as univariate association at p < 0.25; the backward elimination method was chosen to begin with a comprehensive set of candidates and systematically remove non-contributors, with a removal criterion of p > 0.10. It should be noted that the median-based threshold (QQAS ≥ 103) represents a statistical cut-point for this sample and does not constitute a clinically validated definition of quiet quitting; logistic regression findings should be interpreted as factors associated with relatively higher quiet quitting attitudes. Because the logistic regression analysis was based on dichotomization of the continuous QQAS at the median value, some information may have been lost compared with analyses using the continuous score; linear regression results should be interpreted alongside logistic regression findings.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine (02 March 2024, İ04-328-24). Permission to use the scale was obtained from its developers. Informed consent was secured from all participants, with data collected anonymously and used exclusively for scientific purposes.

No funding was utilized for this research.

Sixty-eight (68%) of the participants were female and 32% were male; however, there was no significant difference in QQAS scores between genders (p = 0.694).

The mean age was 37.7 ± 6.1 years, with a significant difference in QQAS scores across age groups (p = 0.003); post-hoc analysis showed significantly higher scores among those aged ≥ 40 years compared to the 29–34 years group.

Seventy-three percent were married, and QQAS scores differed significantly by marital status (p = 0.037), with widowed/divorced individuals having higher scores than singles (post-hoc). 20% reported a chronic disease (no significant QQAS difference, p = 0.057), but those with psychiatric illnesses (7.9%) had significantly higher scores (p = 0.048).

The mean total medical experience of participants was 12.7 ± 6.1 years, average working time as public health specialists was 5.4 ± 4.1 years, and duration in the current province was 4.6 ± 4.8 years. QQAS scores differed significantly by total medical experience (p = 0.014), with post-hoc tests showing higher scores for those with ≥ 15 years versus 5–9 years. Scores also increased significantly with longer tenure as public health specialists (p = 0.021), particularly for ≥ 7 years compared to 0–3 years. No significant differences emerged by time in current province (p = 0.269).

Thirty-seven percent of participants worked in central units (versus provincial), with no significant QQAS score differences by unit (p = 0.395). Those without administrative positions (60.6%) had significantly higher scores than those with positions (p < 0.001).

Seventy-six percent of participants had public health as their first-choice specialty in the medical specialization exam, with no significant QQAS score differences (p = 0.058). In contrast, those who did not willingly choose public health had significantly higher QQAS scores than those who did (p = 0.007). Only 21.6% perceived their specialty training as compatible with the field, and QQAS scores differed significantly across perceptions (p < 0.001); post-hoc tests showed significantly higher scores among those viewing it as very incompatible-incompatible compared to the very compatible-compatible and undecided groups.

No significant QQAS score differences emerged regarding consideration of subspecialty training (13.7% yes) or master’s/PhD programs (19.9% yes) (p = 0.300 and p = 0.322, respectively). However, 34.5% desired to change specialties, with significantly higher QQAS scores among those interested compared to those who were not (p = 0.011; post-hoc confirmed).

Discussion

This study examined quiet quitting attitudes among public health specialists working in the field and their associated factors. The findings indicate that quiet quitting is a notable concern in this group. In the multivariate models, older age, absence of an administrative position, perceived incompatibility of specialty training with the field, a hopeless outlook on the future of public health, and intentions to transfer or resign emerged as independent predictors of higher quiet quitting attitudes. Several additional factors—including marital status, comorbidities, and specialty satisfaction—were significant in univariate analyses but did not retain independent associations after adjustment.

Although QQAS scores were higher among female participants than males, this difference was not statistically significant. The literature reflects similarly mixed findings, with higher quiet quitting scores reported among female healthcare professionals in one Greek study [24] and among males in another [25], suggesting that gender effects may be context-dependent rather than consistent.

Older age was an independent predictor of higher quiet quitting attitudes, remaining significant in both regression models. While post-COVID-19 workplace changes reportedly affect younger workers more [26], our findings suggest that for public health specialists in Türkiye, accumulated experience coupled with long-standing systemic problems and unmet career expectations over time may be associated with higher disengagement attitudes among those aged 40 and over—a pattern that warrants targeted retention strategies for senior specialists.

Divorced or widowed participants had significantly higher QQAS scores than singles in univariate analyses; however, this association did not retain independent significance in the multivariate model after adjustment for other factors. This pattern is consistent with literature showing that work-family role conflict is a contextual stressor that may interact with occupational variables [27], rather than operating as a direct, independent driver of quiet quitting.

Participants with psychiatric illnesses showed significantly higher quiet quitting attitudes in univariate analyses, though this association did not persist after multivariate adjustment, suggesting they may be mediated by occupational factors captured elsewhere in the model. Nonetheless, given that burnout, depression, and sleep disturbances are recognized contributors to disengagement in healthcare workers [28], these findings underscore the value of flexible working conditions and supportive environments for employees managing health conditions [29].

Greater experience as a public health specialist was associated with higher quiet quitting scores; specifically, those with 4–6 years of experience had significantly higher scores than those with 1–3 years in the linear regression model. This finding aligns with the broader literature showing that long-term employees who perceive their contributions as undervalued and career development as unsupported experience declining engagement, may contribute to quiet quitting attitudes [30, 31]. The pattern suggests that early-career disillusionment, if unaddressed, can consolidate into entrenched disengagement over time.

The absence of an administrative position was one of the strongest independent predictors of quiet quitting in both regression models (OR = 6.73 in logistic; B = 14.39 in linear). This finding points to a structural problem: when career advancement paths are blocked, routine tasks become increasingly unrewarding, and motivation erodes [32]. The salience of this factor is further supported by Turkish field data, where lack of promotion opportunities is consistently identified as a primary source of dissatisfaction among healthcare professionals [33], and by Ministry of Health surveys from 2010 to 2017, which flagged inadequate career planning as a key driver of low job satisfaction [34, 35].

Although willingly choosing public health was associated with lower quiet quitting attitudes in univariate analyses, this association did not remain independent after adjustment. In contrast, the perceived compatibility of specialty training with the field was an independent predictor in both regression models—a finding that carries particular weight given that only 21.6% of participants viewed their training as compatible with their actual work. Existing reports highlight persistent gaps in public health residency programs, including training quality, educational infrastructure, and rapid quota expansion without commensurate improvements in field rotations [36]. That such structural deficiencies were associated with higher quiet quitting attitudes underscores the need to reform specialty training and align it more closely with field realities, rather than relying on voluntary commitment alone.

A striking discrepancy emerged between current satisfaction and future outlook: while 53.9% were satisfied with their choice of medicine and 60.2% with the specialty of public health, only 18.2% viewed the future of public health in Türkiye as hopeful. A hopeless outlook on the specialty’s future was the single strongest independent predictor in the logistic model (OR = 9.56) and remained highly significant in the linear model, suggesting that a hopeless outlook on the specialty’s future was associated with higher disengagement attitudes, above and beyond personal dissatisfaction alone. This aligns with documented organizational problems in Türkiye’s public health sector, including manpower shortages, governance deficiencies, repeated legislative changes, and a longstanding emphasis on hospital-based care over community services [37–39]. Satisfaction with the public health specialty was also independently associated with higher quiet quitting scores in the linear regression model, reinforcing that dissatisfaction at both the individual and systemic levels compounds disengagement.

Intentions to transfer or resign were independently associated with higher quiet quitting scores in both regression models, with 38.2% of participants considering a provincial transfer and 27.8% contemplating resignation. This convergence is conceptually important: although quiet quitting and actual resignation are distinct behaviors, the co-occurrence of quiet quitting with withdrawal intentions suggests that quiet quitting may represent an intermediate stage on a continuum toward turnover [40]. Addressing quiet quitting proactively through targeted institutional measures is therefore not only a matter of engagement, but of workforce retention [10]. This pattern is further supported by labor economics research demonstrating that job dissatisfaction arising from adverse working conditions intensifies job search behavior, which may contribute to actual job switches—suggesting that withdrawal intentions constitute an intermediate, yet consequential, step on the path toward actual separation [41].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified factors and quiet quitting attitudes; longitudinal studies are needed to explore temporal dynamics. Secondly, data were collected through self-report, which may introduce social desirability bias and common method bias due to measuring both exposure and outcome variables (quiet quitting attitudes via QQAS) using the same method and source. Such biases could inflate correlations between variables, although procedural remedies like ensuring anonymity were employed. Thirdly, the online survey methodology, while enabling broad reach, might have introduced non-response bias, as non-responders (e.g., those less inclined to participate) may differ systematically from responders in their quiet quitting attitudes, potentially affecting representativeness. Additionally, no established cut-off score exists for the QQAS, limiting the ability to categorize participants definitively as quiet quitters and hindering comparisons with other studies. Also, the use of the backward stepwise method in the regression analyses constitutes a limitation in terms of model stability and possible residual confounding from unmeasured variables. Variable selection in this method is sensitive to sampling, and the model structure may vary across different samples; residual confounders could bias effect estimates. Therefore, it is recommended that the obtained models be re-tested in confirmatory studies with alternative selection methods. Furthermore, the study focused solely on public health specialists primarily within Türkiye’s Ministry of Health workforce, limiting external validity outside this context, other healthcare professions, or non-Turkish settings. The quiet quitting construct and QQAS may also exhibit cultural specificity, as the phenomenon originated in Western contexts and its measurement has shown varying psychometric properties across cultures, warranting caution in cross-cultural generalizations. Future research could benefit from mixed-methods approaches, incorporating qualitative interviews to provide richer insights into the lived experiences underpinning quiet quitting, exploring additional objective measures where feasible, validating QQAS cut-offs, and testing in diverse cultural and occupational groups.

Conclusion and recommendations

These findings highlight several areas that may warrant institutional attention to address quiet quitting attitudes among public health specialists. The association between perceived training-field incompatibility and higher quiet quitting attitudes suggests that better alignment between specialist training, job descriptions, and field service responsibilities may be important for workforce engagement. The Ministry of Health may wish to consider formally defining the scope of practice for public health specialists to reduce role ambiguity. Similarly, the observed association between absence of an administrative position and higher quiet quitting attitudes suggests that more targeted selection and placement of specialists in roles that match their expertise may merit consideration.

These findings also suggest that efforts to improve the compatibility of public health specialization training with field realities—such as strengthening applied field rotations, reviewing departmental qualifications through accreditation mechanisms, and encouraging departments to meet defined standards—may contribute to reducing disengagement.

Equipping managers to recognize early signs of disengagement and respond with supportive measures may also help rebuild work commitment. Given the observed association between psychiatric comorbidities and quiet quitting attitudes in univariate analyses, flexible working arrangements for employees managing mental health conditions may warrant consideration. Finally, given that intentions to transfer or resign were among the strongest independent predictors of higher quiet quitting scores, these findings suggest that proactive institutional measures to address dissatisfaction before withdrawal intentions solidify may support workforce retention.
