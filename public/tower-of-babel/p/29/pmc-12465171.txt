Integrative analysis of mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA to investigate the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in TGF-β2-treated human trabecular meshwork cells
Authors: Jinfeng Liu, Xueping Wu, Junhong Guo, Lina Zhou, Xin Liu, Lei Zhu, Lan Ma, Jiantao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTGF-β-induced fibrogenic changes in human trabecular meshwork (HTM) cells contribute to intraocular pressure elevation in glaucoma. Silibinin, a natural polyphenolic flavonoid from milk thistle seeds, has been well investigated for its function to inhibit fibrosis. The transcriptomic changes in noncoding RNAs contributing to the pro-fibrotic effect of TGF-β2 and the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in HTM cells remain unknown.MethodsRNA sequencing was conducted to characterize the transcriptomic profiles (mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA) in TGF-β2-stimulated HTM cells, with or without silibinin treatment. Pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed RNAs was conducted. Competing endogenous RNA networks were constructed.ResultsTGF-β2 upregulated 1119 mRNAs, 319 lncRNAs, 383 circRNAs, and 30 miRNAs in HTM cells, of which 545 mRNAs, 183 lncRNAs, 265 circRNAs, and 22 miRNAs were reversed by silibinin. These rescued mRNAs, including COL1A1 and α-SMA, were enriched in pathways including cell migration, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix. Furthermore, TGF-β2 downregulated 1165 mRNAs, 212 lncRNAs, 449 circRNAs, and 15 miRNAs, of which 395 mRNAs, 54 lncRNAs, 104 circRNAs, and 7 miRNAs were reversed by silibinin. These rescued mRNAs were enriched in cytokine-mediated signaling pathway and inflammatory response. By predicting the binding of differentially expressed mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs to miRNAs via miranda (v 3.3a) software, we constructed lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks that might be involved in the effects of TGF-β2 and silibinin in HTM cells.ConclusionsSilibinin mitigated TGF-β2-induced alterations in the coding and noncoding RNA profiles of HTM cells, presumably contributing to its anti-fibrotic activity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to illustrate the lncRNA and circRNA changes induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells, and the first study to investigate silibinin’s anti-fibrotic effects in HTM cells from the perspective of noncoding RNAs. Our work deepened the understanding of gene regulation by noncoding RNAs in the TGF-β2-induced fibrogenic changes and the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in HTM cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12027-5.

Introduction

Glaucoma is characterized by progressive optic nerve head damage, retinal ganglion cell death, and subsequent vision loss [1]. As the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, it is projected to affect 111.8 million people by 2040 [1, 2]. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) remains the only modifiable risk factor for glaucoma [2]. Aqueous humour (AH), once produced by the ciliary body, flows from the posterior to the anterior chamber and then drains out of the eye primarily through the conventional outflow pathway consisting of the trabecular meshwork (TM) and Schlemm’s canal, and partly through the unconventional pathway constituted by the ciliary muscle, supraciliary, and suprachoroidal spaces [3]. Excessive production of AH or increased outflow resistance leads to IOP elevation. Lowering IOP levels in glaucoma patients via laser treatment, surgery, and topical medications can effectively delay the progression of vision loss [2]. However, current IOP-lowering medications mostly target AH production and the unconventional outflow pathway, highlighting the high demand for therapies that address the conventional AH outflow pathway [4].

TM is the primary site through which AH flows out of the eye [3]. Recent evidence has indicated a close association between IOP elevation and fibrotic changes of TM in primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) patients [5]. TGF-β2 increased in AH in 50% of patients with POAG [6]. TGF-β2-induced fibrogenic changes in human TM (HTM) cells might contribute to IOP elevation in glaucoma [7, 8]. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, previous research has thoroughly documented the transcriptomic profile of mRNAs in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells [7, 9]. The mRNA changes induced by TGF-β2 are likely tightly regulated by noncoding RNAs [10–13], necessitating the identification of differentially expressed noncoding RNAs in response to TGF-β2 in HTM cells. While TGF-β2 treatment alters miRNA expression in HTM cells [14], its effects on lncRNAs and circRNAs remain unknown.

Silibinin is the major bioactive component of silymarin, a polyphenolic flavonoid mixture extracted from the seeds of milk thistle (also known as Silybum marianum) [15]. Silibinin has been widely investigated for its ability to manage various pathological conditions, including chronic liver diseases, cancer, diabetes mellitus and its complications, and cognitive disorders [16–19]. Recent studies have demonstrated that silibinin can inhibit fibrosis in multiple organs, including the liver, heart, lung, kidney, and intestine, in diseased mouse models [20–25]. Our previous work described the notable ability of silibinin to mitigate cell migration and downregulate fibronectin, COL1A1, and α-SMA expression in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells [26]. These effects indicate the potential of silibinin to lower IOP in glaucoma by targeting on fibrotic changes in TM tissue. A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying silibinin’s anti-fibrotic activity would be helpful for future translational studies.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the roles of noncoding RNAs, including lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs, in the pro-fibrotic effect of TGF-β2 and the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in HTM cells. This was achieved through integrative transcriptomic profiling of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs in HTM cells treated by TGF-β2 with or without the addition of silibinin.

Methods

Cell culture and treatment

Cadaveric eyes (n = 3) were obtained from corneal donors without a history of eye diseases (Table 1). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shenzhen Eye Hospital (grant no. 2023KYPJ028). Primary HTM cells were isolated as previously described [26]. Briefly, the corneal rim of the eyeball was dissected, divided into small sections, and then transferred into a cell culture dish filled with low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Gibco). The tissues were maintained at 37℃ with 5% CO2 to allow the TM cells to proliferate. The cells were subcultured to the second passage upon reaching 80–90% confluence. The identity of TM cells was characterized in our previous work [26]. The human tissue experiments complied with the guidelines of the ARVO Best Practices for Using Human Eye Tissue in Research (Nov2021).

HTM cells at passage 4 were treated with recombinant human TGF-β2 protein (5 ng/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with or without silibinin (50 µM, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. DMSO treatment was used as a control.

RNA isolation

For mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA analysis, total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For miRNA analysis, total RNA was extracted using mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and quantity of RNA were evaluated with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The integrity of the RNA was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Library Preparation

The RNA samples with qualified purity, quantity, and integrity were used for library construction. For the construction of mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA libraries, 1 µg of RNA from each sample was used. First, the ribosomal RNA was removed with a Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The RNA library was subsequently prepared using a VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The small RNA library was constructed using a NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. 1 µg of RNA from each sample was used. The quality of the RNA libraries was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system. The libraries were finally sequenced via the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

RNA sequencing data analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted with an Illumina platform. The raw reads of each sample were processed with fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) to remove the low quality reads. For mRNA and lncRNA identification, the clean reads were mapped onto the reference genome using HISAT2 (http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2). The read counts of each gene were then obtained by HTSeq-count (http://www-huber.embl.de/HTSeq), and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were calculated with Cufflinks (https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/cufflinks/) to quantify mRNAs and lncRNAs. To identify circRNAs, the clean reads were processed with Find_circ (v 1.2) and CIRI2 software [27]. Circbase (http://www.circbase.org/) and CIRCpedia (http://yang-laboratory.com/circpedia/) were used to identify known circRNAs. Junction reads per billion mapped reads (RPB) were calculated to quantify circRNAs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed via R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples.

The raw reads of small RNA sequencing were filtered to remove the low-quality reads, the reads with 5’ primer contaminants and poly (A) sequences, reads without 3’ adapters and insert tags, and reads shorter than 15 nt or longer than 41 nt. The clean reads were then subjected to Bowtie [28] to determine their distribution in the reference genome. After searching against Rfam v.10.1 (https://rfam.org/), rRNA, scRNA, Cis-reg, snRNA, tRNA, and other RNAs were annotated and filtered. The cDNA sequence and Repbase [29] database of species repeat sequences were further identified by Bowtie and then removed. The remaining small RNA tags were then aligned against the miRBase v22 database (http://www.mirbase.org/) to identify known mature miRNAs. The unannotated reads were analyzed by miRDeep2 [30] to predict novel miRNAs. The transcript per million (TPM) was calculated to quantify miRNAs. PCA was performed to evaluate the biological duplication of samples.

Differential expression gene analysis and enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis of mRNAs and lncRNAs was performed via DESeq2 [31]. The p value was calculated by Wald test. A q value (adjusted p-value) of < 0.05 and a fold change of > 2 or < 0.5 were set as the thresholds for significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed lncRNAs (DE-lncRNAs). Hierarchical cluster analysis of the DEGs was conducted by R (v 3.2.0). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed to screen the significantly enriched terms via GOseq (Bioconductor, USA) [32]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed via the KEGG database (Kanehisa Laboratories, Japan). The p values of the GO and KEGG terms were calculated with R (v 3.2.0) based on the hypergeometric distribution, and a p value of < 0.05 was regarded as significant enrichment. Pearson’s coefficient was used to calculate the expression correlation between DE-lncRNAs and DEGs, and lncRNA-gene pairs with a p value of < 0.05 and a |r| value of > 0.8 were regarded as significant. Hierarchical cluster analysis of genes significantly coexpressed by lncRNAs was conducted by R (v 3.2.0). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the genes significantly coexpressed with the lncRNAs were performed to predict the functions of the lncRNAs.

Differential expression analysis of circRNAs was performed with DEGseq [33]. The p value was calculated by the negative binomial distribution test. A q value (adjusted p-value) of < 0.05 and a fold change of > 2 or < 0.5 were set as the thresholds for significantly differentially expressed circRNAs (DE-circRNAs). The parent gene of the circRNA was annotated according to its genomic position. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the parent genes of the DE-circRNAs were performed to predict their functions.

Differentially expressed miRNAs were calculated and determined as the threshold with a q value of < 0.05 and a fold change of > 1.5 or < 0.67. The q value was calculated with the DEG algorithm in the R package for experiments with biological replicates. The targets of the differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs) were predicted by miranda (v 3.3a) software [34]. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed miRNA target genes were performed to predict their functions.

Prediction of the lncRNA-RNA binding protein and lncRNA-transcription factor interactions

The potential interactions between lncRNAs and RNA binding proteins (RBPs) were predicted based on the starBase database (https://www.lncrnablog.com/starbase-v2-0-for-decoding-rna-interaction-networks/). To predict the interactions between lncRNAs and transcription factors (TFs), the motif sequences of the TFs were extracted from the JASPAR database, after which the binding of TF motif to the promoter area of the lncRNA sequences was predicted via Biopython. The representative lncRNA-RBP and lncRNA-TF pairs were plotted with Cytoscape 3.10.1 software.

Construction of the competing endogenous RNA network

mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs that share miRNA response elements (MREs) may compete with each other for the binding with the miRNAs, leading to the formation of a complex competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network [35]. To construct the ceRNA network, the binding of mRNAs to miRNAs was predicted using a previously published method [34], whereas the binding of lncRNAs and circRNAs to miRNAs was predicted by miranda software. Representative lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks were plotted using Cytoscape 3.10.1 software.

Validation of the differential expression of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs

To validate the RNA expression levels revealed by RNA-seq, 5 mRNAs, 5 lncRNAs, 3 circRNAs, and 2 miRNAs selected from the differentially expressed RNAs were detected by quantitative real-time reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) (primer sequences listed in Table 2). For the detection of mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, total RNA from the samples was reverse transcribed into cDNA with a reverse transcription kit (Cat. AG11706, Accurate Biology, Changsha, Hunan, China), followed by real time PCR using a SYBR Green PCR Kit (Cat. AG11702, Accurate Biology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the detection of miRNA, total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with a miRNA 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Premix (Stem-loop) (Cat. AG11748, Accurate Biology) followed by real time PCR. The amplification was conducted as follows: one cycle of 30 s at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles of 5 s at 95 °C and then 30 s at 60 °C. The relative expression levels of the target RNAs to the reference RNAs (GAPDH, ENST00000554988, circRNA_20317, and hsa-miR-100-5p) were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct formula.

Statistical graphs were plotted via GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad software, San Diego, California, USA). Statistical data analysis was conducted via SPSS software for Windows (version 20.0; SPSS, Inc., IL, USA). For comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s least significant difference test or Dunnett’s test for multiple comparisons were applied. The data are presented as the means ± SDs and a p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Expression characteristics of mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA

This study utilized RNA-seq analysis to identify alterations in mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA expression patterns in HTM cells treated by TGF-β2. Additionally, this study examined the impact of silibinin on the RNA profiles of TGF-β2-treated HTM cells. Three biological replicates per group (“DMSO”, “TGF-β2”, and “TGF-β2 + silibinin”) were analyzed. The patterns of transcript expression levels of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs were comparable between the TGF-β2 and DMSO treatment groups. The “TGF-β2 + silibinin” treatment group, however, presented an increased abundance of mRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs compared to the other two groups (Fig. 1A). PCA of the RNA-seq data separated the three experimental groups from each other in terms of mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA but not circRNA expression, indicating that a notable change in the transcriptome of HTM cells was caused by TGF-β2 or silibinin (Fig. 1B). In addition, mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA expression heatmaps, which depict sample-to-sample distances, demonstrated close clustering by replicate samples in each group, indicating high reproducibility. However, no clear group clustering was observed for circRNA expression (Fig. 1C).

Identification of differentially expressed genes induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells

TGF-β2 is known to induce fibrogenic changes in HTM cells [7]. A total of 15,556 genes were compared between the TGF-β2-treated and the DMSO-treated HTM cells. Compared to the DMSO control, the TGF-β2 treatment group presented 1119 significantly upregulated genes (fold change > 2, q < 0.05), including SLC15A1, LEFTY2, KANK4, LMCD1, and ACTBL2 (Fig. 2A and additional file 1). Specifically, type I collagen COL1A1 (fold change = 3.20, q < 0.001) and the biomarker of activated fibroblasts ACTA2 (fold change = 5.11, q < 0.001) were significantly increased by TGF-β2 in HTM cells. Moreover, TGF-β2 significantly downregulated 1165 genes (fold change < 0.5, q < 0.05) including KRTAP1-5, DTNA, VCAM1, CCL8, and FMO2 (Fig. 2A and additional file 1). A hierarchical cluster analysis of these DEGs was subsequently conducted. Those DEGs with similar expression patterns were grouped together and likely participated in the same biological processes (Fig. 2B).

We subsequently carried out GO and KEGG analyses to identify the molecular pathways or biological processes enriched by the DEGs. GO analysis of the upregulated genes revealed enrichment in pathways including cell migration, cell adhesion, extracellular space, extracellular matrix (ECM), ECM binding, TGF-β binding, and SMAD protein signal transduction (Fig. 2C and additional file 2), while KEGG analysis identified enrichment in focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, ECM-receptor interaction, the TGF-β signaling pathway, and the Hippo, Rap1, PI3K-AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 2E and additional file 3). These findings collectively indicated a notable fibrogenic effect imposed by TGF-β2 on the HTM cells. Furthermore, GO analysis of the downregulated genes revealed enrichment in chemotaxis, chemokine activity, inflammatory response, cellular components related to cell surface, extracellular region, and integrin binding (Fig. 2D and additional file 4), while KEGG analysis identified enrichment in TNF signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 2F and additional file 5).

Identification of differentially expressed lncRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells

A total of 19,111 lncRNAs were compared between the TGF-β2 and DMSO-treated HTM cells. TGF-β2 significantly upregulated 319 lncRNAs (fold change > 2, q < 0.05) including ENST00000650941, XR_930390.1, NR_148200.1, XR_001753551.1, and ENST00000658020, and significantly downregulated 212 lncRNAs (fold change < 0.5, q < 0.05) including NR_125715.1, ENST00000654859, ENST00000655283, ENST00000685577, and ENST00000670724 (Fig. 3A and additional file 6). Hierarchical cluster analysis of these DE-lncRNAs indicated that some might engaged in the same biological processes (Fig. 3B). We then applied the pearson correlation coefficient to predict the coexpressed genes of the DE-lncRNAs (Fig. 3C and additional file 7). GO analysis of these genes demonstrated enrichment in pathways including cell adhesion, positive regulation of cell migration, ECM, extracellular region, extracellular space, and integrin binding (Fig. 3D and additional file 8), while KEGG analysis revealed their enrichment in focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, ECM-receptor interaction, TGF-β signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, Hippo, Rap1, PI3K-AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 3E and additional file 9).

LncRNAs may directly interact with RBPs and TFs to participate in gene transcription or post-transcriptional control of mRNAs [11]. Herein, we used starBase v2.0 to decode the lncRNA-RBP interaction networks. Multiple RBPs were predicted to interact with the DE-lncRNAs (Fig. 3F and additional file 10). For example, ENST00000560029 might interact with DDX54, DGCR8, ELAVL1, FMR1, FUS, HNRNPA1, HNRNPA2B1, HNRNPC, IGF2BP2, SRSF1, TAF15, and U2AF2 (Fig. 3F). We further applied biopython software to predict the binding between lncRNAs and TFs based on the JASPAR database (Fig. 3G and additional file 11). For example, ENST00000560029 can interact with TFAP2A, SP1, FOXL1, TFAP2C(var.2), ZNF263, GSX2, CTCFL, HOXB13, FOXC1, MEF2A, LMX1B, HOXB2, FOXP1, and SPI1 (Fig. 3G).

Identification of differentially expressed circRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells

A total of 1399 circRNAs were compared between the TGF-β2 and DMSO-treated HTM cells. TGF-β2 significantly upregulated 383 circRNAs (fold change > 2, q < 0.05) including circRNA_08613, circRNA_10863, circRNA_19289, circRNA_01130, and circRNA_07396, and significantly downregulated 449 circRNAs (fold change < 0.5, q < 0.05) including circRNA_10366, circRNA_12610, circRNA_01514, circRNA_06193, and circRNA_15576 (Fig. 4A and additional file 12). The majority of these DE-circRNAs, however, were of low abundance, suggesting their identity as by-products of pre-mRNA mis-splicing. Hierarchical cluster analyses were conducted to illustrate the expression patterns of the DE-circRNAs (Fig. 4B). We continued to conduct GO and KEGG analyses of the host genes of the DE-circRNAs (Additional file 13). These genes were enriched in GO terms such as “cellular response to hypoxia”, “autophagosome assembly”, “rough endoplasmic reticulum”, and “kinase binding” (Fig. 4C and additional file 14), and in KEGG terms such as “TNF signaling pathway”, “MAPK signaling pathway”, “chagas disease”, and “Pyrimidine metabolism” (Fig. 4D and additional file 15).

Identification of differentially expressed miRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells

A total of 793 miRNAs were compared between the TGF-β2 and DMSO-treated HTM cells. TGF-β2 significantly upregulated 30 miRNAs (fold change > 1.5, q < 0.05) including hsa-miR-135a-3p, hsa-miR-135a-5p, hsa-miR-145-3p, hsa-miR-503-3p, and hsa-miR-27a-5p, and significantly downregulated 15 miRNAs (fold change < 0.67, q < 0.05), including hsa-miR-335-3p, hsa-miR-204-5p, hsa-miR-146b-3p, hsa-miR-486-3p, and hsa-miR-218-5p (Fig. 5A and additional file 16). Hierarchical cluster analysis demonstrated the expression patterns of these DE-miRNAs (Fig. 5B). We continued to predict the genes targeted by these miRNAs (Fig. 5C and additional file 17). Each miRNA can target multiple mRNAs while one mRNA can be targeted by different miRNAs. For example, hsa-miR-214-3p targeted ARVCF, GPR137, and DNM3, while DNM3 was targeted by both hsa-miR-214-5p and hsa-miR-214-3p (Fig. 5C). The target genes were subsequently subjected to GO and KEGG analyses and enriched in GO terms such as “regulation of microtubule polymerization”, “negative regulation of microtubule binding”, “extracellular matrix structural constituent conferring tensile strength”, and “pattern recognition receptor activity” (Fig. 5D and additional file 18), and in KEGG terms such as “adherens junction”, “ECM − receptor interaction”, “JAK − STAT signaling pathway”, and “protein digestion and absorption” (Fig. 5E and additional file 19).

Silibinin prevented TGF-β2-induced ECM gene alterations in HTM cells

To investigate the potential effects of silibinin on the fibrogenic changes in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells, mRNA expression following silibinin treatment was detected via RNA-seq. Among the 1119 DEGs upregulated by TGF-β2, 545 genes were inhibited by silibinin (Fig. 6A and additional file 20). These genes included COL1A1, COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A1, COL5A2, and COL16A1, which encode collagen proteins. Specifically, the expression of ACTA2, also known as α-SMA, a commonly used marker for activated myofibroblasts, was significantly decreased by silibinin (Fig. 6B). We subsequently conducted GO and KEGG analyses of the 545 genes rescued by silibinin and identified enrichment in GO terms including “cell migration”, “cell adhesion”, “extracellular space”, “ECM”, and “collagen-containing ECM” (Fig. 6C and additional file 21), and in KEGG terms including “ECM-receptor interaction”, “TGF-β signaling pathway”, “PI3K-AKt signaling pathway”, and “negative regulation of pathway-restricted SMAD protein phosphorylation” (Fig. 6E and additional file 22). Furthermore, among the 1165 DEGs downregulated by TGF-β2, 395 genes were reversed by silibinin (Fig. 6A and additional file 23), including CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL8, IL1B, IFIT1, IFIT2, and IFIT3 (Fig. 6B). Pathway enrichment analysis of the 395 rescued genes indicated their enrichment in GO terms including “cytokine-mediated signaling pathway”, “type I interferon signaling pathway”, “inflammatory response”, “SMAD protein complex”, and “extracellular space” (Fig. 6D and additional file 24), and in KEGG terms including “TNF signaling pathway”, “NF-kappa B signaling pathway”, and “MAPK signaling pathway” (Fig. 6F and additional file 25).

Construction of competitive endogenous RNA networks

An mRNA, referred to as “X,” shares its MREs with lncRNAs, circRNAs, and other mRNAs. Consequently, by competing for the same miRNAs, these RNAs can reduce miRNA targeting activity on mRNA X, thereby affecting its level or translation [36]. This effect may lead to the formation of numerous ceRNA networks some of which have been validated by previous evidence [37, 38]. Herein, we attempted to construct the potential ceRNA networks that might be involved in the activity of TGF-β2 and silibinin in HTM cells.

We applied miranda (v 3.3a) software to predict the potential binding of the DE-lncRNAs and DE-circRNAs to the miRNAs in the TGF-β2-treated HTM cells. A total of 86,059 lncRNA-miRNA pairs (total score > 180, additional file 41) and 30,859 circRNA-miRNA pairs (total score > 180, additional file 42) were identified. Notably, one lncRNA or circRNA can be targeted by various miRNAs and one miRNA can target multiple lncRNAs or circRNAs. For example, ENST00000424321 is targeted by hsa-miR-20a-5p and hsa-miR-4739, while hsa-miR-4739 targets numerous other lncRNAs such as ENST00000545709, ENST00000323380, and ENST00000562705, to name but a few (Fig. 10A). Similarly, circRNA_16521 is targeted by hsa-miR-6134, hsa-miR-32b-3p, and hsa-miR-8072, while hsa-miR-8072 targets on numerous other circRNAs such as circRNA_16540, circRNA_09967, and circRNA_06570, to name but a few (Fig. 10B).

To construct the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks, specific miRNAs (those with moderate levels) were chosen from the total 1378 miRNAs detected in HTM cells (additional file 43). The target genes of these miRNAs were then predicted. Combined with the previously identified lncRNA-miRNA and circRNA-miRNA pairs, we were then able to establish numerous ceRNA networks. In Fig. 10C and D, we presented the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA networks related to genes including ADAMTS10, COL26A1, IGFBPL1, and ANXABL1. These genes are known to contribute to the formation of collagen-containing extracellular matrix. They were upregulated by TGF-β2 and then reversed by silibinin treatment in HTM cells (additional file 20). For example, COL26A1 can be targeted by hsa-miR-331-3p; the latter can also be competitively bound by the mRNA TSPAN18, the lncRNAs, including NR_037709.1, XR_936962.1, XR_936963.1, ENST00000570309, XR_001753505.1, ENST00000689679, NR_110479.1, NR_171046.1, and ENST00000608023, and the circRNAs, including circRNA_01764, circRNA_12743, circRNA_16521, circRNA_20132, circRNA_20297 (Fig. 10C and D).

Validation of differentially expressed mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs

To validate the transcriptional profiles detected by RNA-seq, qRT-PCR was applied to measure the expression of a selection of transcripts including 5 mRNAs, 5 lncRNAs, 3 circRNAs, and 2 miRNAs. These targets were prioritized based on statistical significance and biological relevance to TGF-β2 signaling. As shown in Fig. 11A, RNA-seq demonstrated that ADAM19, LTBP1, GPC4, COL1A1, and α-SMA expressions were significantly upregulated by TGF-β2 stimulation and followingly inhibited by silibinin treatment in HTM cells, which was consistent with the qRT-PCR findings. Similarly, lncRNAs including ENST00000689376, ENST00000686037, ENST00000688126, XR_001750084.1, and ENST00000666697 were significantly upregulated by TGF-β2 and then decreased by silibinin treatment, as illustrated by RNA-seq (Fig. 11B). These changes were confirmed by qRT-PCR, although the decrease in ENST00000686037 caused by silibinin did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 11B). Further, RNA-seq revealed a significant increase in the expressions of circRNA_06605 and circRNA_14224 following TGF-β2 stimulation, followed by a significant decrease upon silibinin treatment in HTM cells. These changes were confirmed by qRT-PCR, although the decrease in circRNA_14224 expression caused by silibinin did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 11C). CircRNA_12552 was significantly decreased by TGF-β2 and then increased by silibinin according to RNA-seq. However, it was significantly increased by TGF-β2 and further increased by silibinin according to qRT-PCR. Finally, both RNA-seq and qRT-PCR demonstrated that hsa-miR-145-5p and hsa-miR-181a-2-3p were significantly upregulated by TGF-β2 and then inhibited by silibinin treatment (Fig. 11D). Overall, qRT-PCR confirmed the changes in most of the tested RNAs revealed by RNA-seq.

Discussion

TGF-β2 has been documented to induce fibrogenic changes in HTM cells, while silibinin was found to counteract these changes according to our previous work [7, 26]. Here, we demonstrated that TGF-β2 upregulated 1119 mRNAs, 319 lncRNAs, 383 circRNAs, and 30 miRNAs in HTM cells while downregulated 1165 mRNAs, 212 lncRNAs, 449 circRNAs, and 15 miRNAs. Among the RNAs upregulated by TGF-β2, 545 mRNAs, 183 lncRNAs, 265 circRNAs, and 22 miRNAs were decreased by silibinin. These rescued mRNAs were enriched in cell migration, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix. Among the RNAs downregulated by TGF-β2, 395 mRNAs, 54 lncRNAs, 104 circRNAs, and 7 miRNAs were increased by silibinin. These rescued mRNAs were enriched in cytokine-mediated signaling pathway and inflammatory response. Our study revealed coordinated alterations in both coding and noncoding RNAs induced by TGF-β2 and modulated by silibinin in HTM cells.

LncRNAs can alter the stability and translation of mRNAs by interacting with DNA, RNA, and proteins [11]. LncRNA changes have been implicated in TGF-β driven tissue fibrosis [39]. Our work identified 319 upregulated and 212 downregulated lncRNAs in HTM cells treated by TGF-β2. Pathway enrichment analysis of the coexpressed genes of these DE-lncRNAs identified terms like “cell adhesion”, “ECM”, “ECM-receptor interaction”, and “TGF-β signaling pathway”, indicating their potential involvement in the fibrogenic changes by TGF-β2. Specifically, nuclear enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1), a lncRNA reported to contribute to the progression of pulmonary, liver, and cardiac fibrosis [40–42], showed a robust downregulation post TGF-β2 treatment in HTM cells. NEAT1 is constitutively expressed in mammalian cells and is essential for paraspeckle formation [43]. The exact role of NEAT1 in fibrosis remains unclear. Whether and how NEAT1 affects the effects of TGF-β2 in HTM cells warrants further investigation. MiRNAs function by inducing mRNA degradation and/or translational repression upon binding to specific sequences within the target mRNAs [12]. The regulation of TGF-β signaling by miRNAs is well documented [44, 45]. Recently, Doyle et al. first reported the TGF-β2 induced miRNAome in HTM cells [14]. Our study identified 45 DE-miRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells, corroborating the majority of the upregulated miRNAs illustrated by Doyle et al., but differing from most of the downregulated miRNAs [14]. The observed discrepancies may be attributed to variations in cell conditions, as well as differences in detection and analytical methods. Notably, several of the miRNAs upregulated by TGF-β2 in HTM cells have previously been validated to influence TGF-β signaling in other systems. For example, miR-21 and miR-503 were upregulated in the TGF-β2-treated HTM cells. It was reported that miR-21 and miR-503 were increased in TGF-β-activated primary pulmonary fibroblasts and hepatic stellate cells, respectively, and functioned by targeting SMAD7, a negative regulator of TGF-β signaling [44, 46]. In addition to lncRNAs and miRNAs, 832 circRNAs, which were supposed to sponge miRNAs [13], were altered in the TGF-β2 treated HTM cells. Although the majority of these circRNAs were of low abundance and thus could be by-products of mis-splicing, those in high levels were supposed to be functional [47]. For example, THBS1 circRNA (hsa_circ_0103395), was expressed in HTM cells at a relatively high level and further upregulated by 2.2 folds upon TGF-β2 treatment. Similarly, THBS1 mRNA, which was abundant in HTM cells, was increased by 2.4 folds upon TGF-β2 treatment. It is likely that THBS1 circRNA sponges the miRNAs that target THBS1 mRNA thus inhibiting its degradation. The THBS1 product, TSP1, is a matricellular ECM protein and plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of fibrotic diseases [48]. THBS1 circRNA thus may play vital roles in TGF-β2-induced fibrogenic changes in HTM cells, which necessitates further investigation.

Our previously published work demonstrated the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells [26]. This finding was repeated in this study as the mRNAs changed by silibinin were enriched in cell migration, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix. Further investigation of the noncoding RNAs found that 237 DE-lncRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells were reversed by silibinin treatment. These lncRNAs could play crucial roles in the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin as their coexpressed genes were enriched in “TGF-β signaling pathway”, “ECM”, and “ECM-receptor interaction”. Notably, silibinin decreased various transcripts of CARMN. CARMN was known to promote the contractile phenotype of smooth muscle cells [49]. It is possible that CARMN downregulation contributed to silibinin effects on HTM cell contraction increased by TGF-β2 [8]. In addition, silibinin increased multiple transcripts of NEAT1. Considering the emerging evidence indicating the roles of NEAT1 in fibrosis [43], this effect could contribute to the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin. Silibinin also reversed 29 DE-miRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells, including miR-21 and miR-503. Moreover, miR-29b, which functioned to inhibit fibrosis in mouse kidney [50], was increased by silibinin, while miRNA-145, which was reported to contribute to the fibrogenic activity of TGF-β1 in fibroblasts [46, 51], was reduced by silibinin. Silibinin further reversed 369 DE-circRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells. One should keep in mind that those of high abundance were more likely to be functional. Notably, THBS1 circRNA, one of the highly expressed circRNAs in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells, was decreased to 0.084 folds by silibinin, highlighting its possible contribution to the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin.

The “ceRNA” hypothesis, first proposed by Leonardo Salmena et al. in 2011, suggests that mRNAs, transcribed pseudogenes, lncRNAs, and circular RNAs compete for the binding to miRNAs by sharing MREs thereby regulating each other [36, 52]. Since then, plenty of studies have been conducted to validate the roles of ceRNAs in various physiological and pathological conditions [36]. Based on our RNA-seq data, numerous lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA interactions in the TGF-β2 treated HTM cells could be predicted. We presented those interactions related to genes including ADAMTS10, COL26A1, IGFBPL1, and ANXABL1. These genes, which were responsible for the production of collagen-containing ECM, could be regulated by various lncRNAs, circRNAs, and other mRNAs listed in the constructed networks. However, the regulation by these ceRNAs on the expression of the aforementioned genes would not be efficient unless they were significantly changed, the levels of the sequestered miRNAs were not too low or too high, and they were accessible to the miRNAs [53]. Thus, functional studies are warranted to validate the efficiency of the ceRNA networks constructed here.

This study had several limitations. First, the study provided a broad overview of RNA profile alterations induced by TGF-β2 or silibinin in HTM cells, yet the roles of specific RNAs need to be validated in future research. Additionally, out of all the RNAs altered by silibinin in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells, we selected those affected by TGF-β2 for further analysis. However, the RNAs changed by silibinin but not by TGF-β2 were not explored and could also contribute to the anti-fibrotic effects of silibinin. Despite these limitations, our work presented comprehensive transcriptomic changes in mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells, as well as those changes induced by silibinin in TGF-β2-treated HTM cells, and predicted the interactions among different types of RNAs. Our work provides a foundational blueprint for the future investigations into the roles of noncoding RNAs in the pro-fibrotic effect of TGF-β2 and the anti-fibrotic activity of silibinin in HTM cells.

Conclusions

This study offers an integrative analysis of the changes of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs induced by TGF-β2 in HTM cells, along with the effects of silibinin treatment on these alterations. Additionally, numerous ceRNA networks likely modulating HTM cell responses to TGF-β2 and/or silibinin treatment were constructed. Our work provides a comprehensive understanding of the genetic regulation by noncoding RNAs underlying the TGF-β2-induced fibrogenic changes and the antifibrotic activity of silibinin in HTM cells.
