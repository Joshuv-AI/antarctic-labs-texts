Convergent thinking, divergent thinking, and openness to experience as predictors of academic success among high-achieving and typical-achieving medical students
Authors: Ahmed M Abdulla Alabbasi, Ahmed Mohamed Alansari, Abdelhalim Deifalla, Mark A Runco, Reginald P Sequeira, Ghazi A Alotaibi, Aseel AlSaleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCreativity-related cognitive processes and personality traits are not often examined together in educational contexts, particularly when discussing high-achieving subgroups of medical students. This study investigated these predictors of academic performance among high-achieving and typical-achieving medical students within a problem-based learning (PBL) environment of a medical school.MethodsData were collected from 274 Doctor of Medicine students at Arabian Gulf University during the 2024–2025 academic year. Participants completed assessments of convergent thinking (Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices and Watson–Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal), divergent thinking (Alternative Uses Test and Figural Divergent Thinking Test), and openness to experience (NEO-FFI). Students’ grade point averages (GPAs) were obtained from institutional records. Hierarchical regression analyses and moderation tests were performed, controlling for age and sex.ResultsConvergent thinking (β = 0.20, p < .001) and divergent thinking (β = 0.25, p < .001) emerged as modest-to-moderate positive predictors of GPA, whereas openness to experience showed a small negative association (β = −0.13, p = .026). Achievement status, defined as high-achieving versus typical-achieving GPA, significantly moderated the effects of both convergent and divergent thinking, such that the academic benefits of higher thinking skills were meaningfully stronger among high-achieving students, while these associations were weaker among their typical-achieving peers. No moderating effect was observed for openness to experience.ConclusionsConvergent and divergent thinking independently enhance academic performance in medical students, with amplified effects for high-achieving students. In contrast, openness to experience was negatively linked with GPA, reflecting the complex interplay between personality traits and structured medical training. These findings underscore the need for medical curricula that balance convergent reasoning with creative problem-solving while considering diverse student cognitive and personality profiles.Keywords: Academic performance, Convergent thinking, Creativity, Divergent thinking, High-achieving students, Openness to experience, Medical education

Background

Academic success in undergraduate medical programs is a cornerstone of physician training; however, its predictors remain multifaceted and context-dependent [1]. Successful learning in medical education, particularly in problem-based learning (PBL) environments, requires not only convergent and divergent thinking but also adaptability to novel challenges [2–5]. Convergent thinking, typified by logical reasoning and pattern recognition (e.g., Raven’s Progressive Matrices; [6]), is foundational for mastering structured medical knowledge. Conversely, divergent thinking—which involves generating multiple solutions to open-ended problems—aligns with the nature of PBL, where students navigate complex clinical scenarios [7]. Openness to experience, a personality trait associated with curiosity and intellectual exploration, may enhance learning in such environments [8, 9].

Although the role of cognitive abilities, such as intelligence and critical thinking, in the academic performance of undergraduate medical students is well documented [10], the contributions of creativity-related constructs, such as divergent thinking and personality traits like openness to experience, remain underexplored. While studies on intelligence, as measured by various IQ tests, have yielded mixed findings [11], critical thinking has been consistently positively associated with academic performance among undergraduate medical students [12, 13]. Few studies have examined the relationship between divergent thinking and academic achievement in undergraduate medical students [2]; as such, the role of openness to experience remains largely unexplored.

Creativity is integral to medical practice as medical professionals must often develop innovative solutions for diagnosis or treatment. Creative thinking enables clinicians to draw from existing knowledge while also considering a wide range of options. Supporting this view, Tiwari and colleagues [14] found that medical students value creativity and recognize that it should be emphasized in medical education. This view is consistent with the overwhelming evidence that creativity is domain-specific [15–18], suggesting that creativity in the medical profession may differ in important ways from that in other fields. Consequently, research conducted with non-medical populations may not directly inform medical curriculum design or the identification of prospective students. Moreover, creativity is not a unitary construct [19, 20] but requires both divergent thinking—related to originality and flexibility [21]—and convergent thinking, which facilitates the evaluation and refinement of ideas and options. In a recent study [2], found that both divergent and convergent thinking predict achievement among gifted students in medical school. These findings suggest that creativity in medical contexts depends not only on cognitive processes such as idea generation and evaluation [22] but also on metacognitive abilities. In the present study, metacognition is discussed as part of the broader theoretical framework rather than as a construct directly measured in the empirical model, highlighting its potential role in supporting medical students’ and professionals’ reflective and strategic problem-solving.

To date, only one study has examined the association between some predictors and academic performance among high-achieving undergraduate medical students [2]. That study focused on “gifted” education in higher education, although most research on gifted education programs focus on K-12 students [23, 24]. One of the most cited definitions of giftedness comes from the National Association of Gifted Children [25]:

Students with gifts and talents perform—or have the capability to perform—at higher levels compared to others of the same age, experience, and environment in one or more domains. They require modification(s) to their educational experience(s) to learn and realize their potential.

The NAGC definition [26] allows for a parallel to be drawn between giftedness and exceptional academic achievement. For comparison purposes with the extant literature, in this study, high-achieving undergraduate medical students are characterized, similar to gifted students in other published research, as having a grade point average (GPA) of 90% or higher. Although the categorization follows that from existing research [2], in this study, we use the terms high-achieving and typical-achieving students instead of gifted versus nongifted students. Studies show that high-achieving individuals frequently exhibit heightened cognitive flexibility and metacognitive strategies, potentially amplifying the benefits of convergent and divergent thinking [27]. However, no prior studies have tested whether high achievement moderates the interplay between divergent and convergent thinking and academic success in medical education, which is one of the current study’s contributions.

The present study extends research on predictors of academic success among medical students [2] by making five distinct contributions. First, unlike the existing research [2], it includes both high-achieving and typical-achieving students, allowing for ability-group comparisons. Second, the sample size is substantially larger (n = 274 vs. n = 84), enhancing the robustness of the findings. Third, the study incorporates a figural divergent thinking test not used previously. Fourth, it introduces a measure of creative personality—openness to experience. Finally, the present study controls for age and sex in the regression model. Including typical-achieving students further enabled us to examine whether GPA outcomes differ by achievement status (high vs. typical-achieving).

This study addresses two central research questions: (a) To what extent do convergent thinking, divergent thinking, and openness to experience predict the academic performance of medical students; and (b) does students’ achievement status moderate these relationships?

Methods

Participants

Data were collected in the 2024–2025 academic year from 278 students enrolled in the Doctor of Medicine (MD) program (Years 1 to 6) at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences (CMHS), Arabian Gulf University in the Kingdom of Bahrain. After excluding four students who provided incomplete responses, the final sample comprised 274 students (mean age = 20.15; SD = 1.69). Of these, 64 (23.4%) were men, and 210 (76.6%) were women. The MD curriculum at the CMHS follows a PBL approach, emphasizing problem-solving and open-ended tasks. Most participants (90.1%) were pre-clinical students (Years 1–4), while 9.9% were clinical students (years 5–6). Given the small proportion of clinical students, we did not include this dichotomous variable in the regression analyses.

Participants categorized by academic performance: 83 (30.3%) were classified as high achievers (GPA ≥ 90%), and 191 (69.7%) were classified as typical achievers (GPA < 90%). The overall mean GPA score was 85.13 (SD = 6.84), ranging from 65.91 to 96.90. Because subgroup membership was derived from GPA, which also served as the outcome variable, moderation findings should be interpreted as descriptive of differential associations rather than causal subgroup effects. Sex and age were included as control variables in all analyses. Version 29.0.2.0 of SPSS was used for conducting data analysis.

Instruments

Alternative uses test

Verbal divergent thinking was measured using the Alternative Uses Test (AUT; [28]). Based on recommendations by Wallach and Kogan [28] and meta-analytic findings on time-on-task [29, 30], both verbal and figural divergent thinking tests were administered in a game-like format, using the term “activities” instead of “exam.” For the test, alternative uses for a brick, a toothbrush, and a newspaper were obtained. Hybrid instructions were used, encouraging participants to be both fluent and creative [31, 32]. The verbatim instructions were as follows:

Now, in this game, I am going to name an object—any kind of object, like a light bulb or the floor—and it will be your job to tell me many, different, and unique ways that the object could be used. Any object can be used in a lot of different ways. For example, think about string. What are some of the ways you can think of that you might use string? Let’s begin now. And remember, think of all the different and original ways you could use the object that you will see on the next page.

Now, in this game, I am going to name an object—any kind of object, like a light bulb or the floor—and it will be your job to tell me many, different, and unique ways that the object could be used. Any object can be used in a lot of different ways. For example, think about string. What are some of the ways you can think of that you might use string? Let’s begin now. And remember, think of all the different and original ways you could use the object that you will see on the next page.

Responses were scored for fluency, flexibility, and originality. Fluency was defined as the total number of unrepeated responses per stimulus (i.e., brick, toothbrush, and newspaper). Participants received 1 point for fluency for each new idea related to the stimuli. Flexibility was defined as the number of shifts in ideas or categories [33]. Participants received 1 point for each distinct category of idea/response. Originality was scored based on statistical infrequency with a 5% cutoff [32, 34]. One point was given for ideas that were relatively rare (given by 5% or less of the samples). The fluency scores ranged from 2 to 23 (M = 9.48; SD = 4.33), flexibility from 2 to 8 (M = 5.02; SD = 1.40), and originality from 1 to 21 (M = 7.55; SD = 4.20).

Previous research with Arab samples using the AUT has shown good reliability (between 0.63 and 0.93) [33, 35, 36]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients in the current study were 0.85 (fluency), 0.71 (flexibility), and 0.74 (originality). The discriminant validity between the verbal divergent thinking test and Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices (APM-III) were − 0.01, -0.04, and − 0.02 for fluency, flexibility, and originality, respectively.

Figural divergent thinking test

Figural divergent thinking was assessed with the Runco Creativity Assessment Battery (r-CAB [37](. This test, like Wallach and Kogan’s Line Meaning Test [28], presents participants with three abstract figures and asks them to generate as many creative ideas as possible about what the figures represent. These tasks were also administered in a game-like format, which minimizes evaluative anxiety and time stress; therefore, it encourages students to use their curiosity, enjoyment, and persistence. The hybrid “be fluent and creative” instructions were used. Research has indicated that explicitly asking students to generate as many ideas as possible and think of ideas that no one else can think of increases fluency, flexibility, and originality scores compared with standard instructions [30, 31, 38].

As with the verbal divergent thinking test, the figural tasks were scored for fluency, flexibility, and originality using the same scoring method. Reliability and validity of the r-CAB have been assessed with Arab participants representing different age groups [7, 31, 35, 36], showing good internal consistency and inter-rater reliability coefficients (between 0.69 and 0.82). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.87 (fluency), 0.78 (flexibility), and 0.75 (originality). The discriminant validity between the figural test and Raven’s APM-III were − 0.19, -0.26, and − 0.15 for fluency, flexibility, and originality, respectively. The directions given to participants were as follows:

Look at the figure below. What do you see? List as many things as you can that this figure might be or represent. This is NOT a test. Please think of this as a game and have fun with it! The more ideas you list, the better.

Intelligence

Raven’s APM-III was used to assess participants’ intelligence. This test assesses lateral thinking and the ability to learn new concepts quickly and solve new and complex problems without drawing on prior knowledge [39, 40]. Raven’s APM-III includes 23 multiple-choice items that are answered within a 40-minute time limit. The mean score is 50, with an SD of 10. Extensive validity and reliability data are reported in Raven’s APM-III Manual (see [2] for additional details).

Critical thinking

The Watson–Glaser Critical Thinking Appraisal, Third Edition (WG-III) [41] was used to assess three subskills: recognizing assumptions, evaluating arguments, and drawing conclusions. The 40-item multiple-choice test was administered in its timed version (30 min), with a mean score of 50 and an SD of 10. The WG-III Manual includes detailed validity and reliability data (see [41]). Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.72.

Openness to experience

Openness to experience is one of the five core personality traits of the Five-Factor model of personality and refers to “the extent to which a person actively seeks and appreciates different experiences and tolerates and explores novel situations” [9]. This trait was measured using the NEO Five-Factor Inventory [42] (12 items). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = totally disagree to 5 = totally agree). Examples included “I often enjoy playing with theories or abstract ideas” and “I have a lot of intellectual curiosity.” Higher scores indicated greater openness to experience. Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.89.

Procedure

This study was supported by the Arabian Gulf University (grant #G02-AGU-5-22) and approved by the Ethical Committee at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences (#E23-PI-5-22). Following approval, participants received four separate test links via email in the following order: (a) the verbal and the figural divergent thinking tests, (b) the Raven’s APM-III test, (c) the WG-III, and (d) the NEO Five-Factor Inventory (Openness to Experience subscale). A short break was provided after the students completed the verbal and the figural divergent thinking tests. All assessments were administered on the same day. The first page of each assessment included information about the study’s authors and objectives and a consent box, followed by demographic questions (e.g., sex, age, pre-clinical vs. clinical student, study year). GPAs were obtained from official institutional records through the Admission and Registration Unit. GPA reflects cumulative academic performance on a 0–100 scale at the end of the same academic year in which the cognitive and personality assessments were completed.

To encourage students to participate in this study, the authors informed all students that they would receive their results of the five assessments. Additionally, a certificate of participation was awarded to each participant who completed all assessments.

Results

A hierarchical regression analysis was performed to address the first research question. Composite scores for divergent and convergent thinking were used instead of subscales to reduce the number of regression analyses, thus minimizing the risk of Type 1 error, and to focus on the primary constructs of interest (i.e., divergent and convergent thinking). In Step 1, age and sex were entered as control variables, followed in Step 2 by convergent thinking, divergent thinking, and openness to experience. GPA served as the dependent variable. All variables were centered based on the recommendation of Cohen et al. [43].

The Step 1 model was not statistically significant (R = .08, ΔR2 =0.01, F(2, 271) = 0.91, p = .403), whereas the Step 2 model indicated that convergent thinking, divergent thinking, and openness to experience were significantly related to GPA (R = .37, ΔR2 =0.13, F(5, 268) = 13.31, p < .001). As shown in Table 1, both convergent (β = 0.20, t = 3.40, p < .001) and divergent thinking (β = 0.25, t = 4.30, p < .001) were positively and significantly related to GPA. In contrast, openness to experience was negatively and significantly associated with GPA (β = − 0.13, t = 2.24, p = .026). Multicollinearity was not a concern, as all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were within the acceptable range (VIFs < 2.1) [44]. The full regression model is presented in Table 1.

To address the second research question—whether achievement status moderated the relationship between the dependent and independent variables—we used the PROCESS macro in SPSS [45] to test the moderation effect. The results indicated that achievement status significantly moderated the relationship between convergent thinking and GPA (β = 0.99, t = 2.36, p = .02). Specifically, the positive association between convergent thinking and GPA was stronger for the high-achieving GPA-based sample. Similarly, achievement status significantly moderated the relationship between divergent thinking and GPA (β = 0.77, t = 2.33, p = .02). Again, the association between divergent thinking and GPA was stronger for high-achieving students. In contrast, achievement status did not moderate the relationship between openness to experience and GPA (β = -1.28, t = − 0.97, p = .33).

Discussion

This study investigated the predictive roles of convergent thinking, divergent thinking, and openness to experience in medical students’ academic performance, with a focus on the moderating role of achievement status. The findings underscore the multifaceted nature of academic achievement in medical education, particularly in a PBL environment.

Consistent with prior research [10, 46], convergent thinking emerged as a significant predictor of GPA, supporting the view that structured reasoning and pattern recognition skills are foundational to mastering the dense factual content of medical curricula. Moreover, divergent thinking positively predicted GPA, highlighting the critical role of creative problem-solving in navigating the open-ended clinical scenarios and case-based discussions typical of PBL [7]. Together, these results suggest that academic success in medical school depends not only on knowledge acquisition but also on the ability to adapt and apply knowledge creatively to novel contexts.

Interestingly, openness to experience was negatively associated with GPA. While openness typically supports curiosity and intellectual exploration [9], its benefits may be constrained in the structured, high-pressure environment of medical school, particularly within a PBL framework. As Vedel [47] noted, high openness may inadvertently divert attention from the focused and systematic study habits required for high-stakes assessments. This suggests a potential trade-off: while openness may enrich long-term clinical reasoning and enhance patient care, it could temporarily hinder performance in rigidly structured academic settings.

It is important to note that GPA represents a relatively specific and short-term indicator of academic performance, emphasizing efficiency and assessment alignment rather than broader developmental outcomes. Accordingly, openness to experience may exert more positive effects on longer-horizon outcomes—such as professional identity formation, clinical adaptability, innovation, and competence development—that were beyond the scope of the present study.

The moderation analyses further clarified the role of achievement status. High-achieving students—operationalized in this study as those with a GPA at or above 90%—demonstrated stronger associations between both convergent and divergent thinking and GPA. This suggests that high-achieving students may be better able to leverage their cognitive flexibility and metacognitive strategies [27] to translate creative and logical reasoning skills into academic success. These findings are consistent with the literature on the metacognitive and motivational advantages of gifted learners [27]. In contrast, openness to experience did not interact with achievement status, reinforcing the idea that its negative association with GPA may be a general feature of medical training, irrespective of prior academic ability.

Implications

Several implications follow from these findings. First, the results highlight the need for balanced pedagogical approaches in medical education that nurture both convergent and divergent thinking. Strategies like differential diagnoses and interpretations of findings might enhance both types of thinking. Curricula that overemphasize rote memorization over creativity may fail to equip students with the full range of skills needed for clinical practice. Second, the negative association between openness and GPA suggests that while fostering curiosity and broad intellectual interests is important, medical educators should also support students in developing strategies to channel these traits productively. Using simulations (high-fidelity simulators, virtual reality, augmented reality, etc.) in medical student training may improve procedural skills, knowledge retention, and students’ satisfaction and self-efficacy [48]. Furthermore, implementing team-based learning in undergraduate medical education may contribute to improvement in academic performance for lower-performing students [49], knowledge retention, and engagement [50]. These strategies could help students tolerate uncertainty and ambiguity during their medical school journey and improve academic performance for those who have a high level of openness to experiences but require additional work on academic achievement. Finally, the enhanced benefit of convergent and divergent thinking among high-achieving students suggests the potential for differentiated instruction that recognizes and nurtures individual cognitive profiles.

Limitations of the study

Various limitations warrant caution in interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; longitudinal studies should be conducted to clarify how these cognitive and personality factors evolve and impact academic outcomes over time. Second, the study was conducted at a single medical school in the Kingdom of Bahrain, and cultural or institutional factors may limit generalizability. Replication in diverse educational settings is needed to validate these findings. Third, while we employed validated measures of the constructs, self-report instruments for openness may be subject to response biases. Incorporating behavioral or peer-report measures could provide a richer assessment of personality.

Future research could also explore additional moderators, such as motivation, learning strategies, or stress resilience, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how these factors interact in the context of medical education. Moreover, qualitative approaches could further illuminate how medical students perceive and navigate the balance between creativity and structured learning in their day-to-day studies.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence that both convergent and divergent thinking independently and positively contribute to academic success in medical students, particularly within a PBL-based Arab medical curriculum. However, openness to experience was negatively associated with GPA, suggesting the complex interplay of personality traits in high-stakes academic environments. Achievement status emerged as an important moderator, strengthening the positive association between cognitive abilities and performance. These findings highlight the importance of integrating both convergent and divergent thinking skills into medical curricula while also recognizing the potential pitfalls of unmoderated openness to experience. By acknowledging and supporting these diverse cognitive and personality profiles, medical educators can better prepare students for the dual demands of academic excellence and clinical competence in the modern medical landscape.
