Genomic characterization of novel lytic bacteriophages targeting carbapenemase producing multidrug resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae from Ethiopia: a molecular study
Authors: Assefa Asnakew Abebe, Alemayehu Godana Birhanu, Molalegne Bitew, Dawit Hailu Alemayehu, Abaysew Ayele, Yimer Demssie, Keyru Tuki, Lanchisl Tsegaye Mulualem, Tesfaye Sisay Tessema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is a critical pathogen responsible for a wide range of severe infections. Its escalating resistance to frontline antimicrobials, particularly carbapenems, severely compromises existing therapeutic options and undermines current treatment strategies. This clinical challenge is further exacerbated by the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains and hypervirulent lineages, which substantially complicate infection management and worsen patient outcomes. Ultimately, this alarming convergence of resistance and virulence presents a severe public health threat that necessitates the urgent development of novel therapeutic approaches; in this regard, phage therapy is emerging as a promising strategy. Bacteriophages and their derivatives have garnered increasing recognition as targeted antibacterial agents, demonstrating potent activity against carbapenemase producing and MDR K. pneumoniae in vitro models. They effectively lyse MDR strains, disrupt biofilm formation, and have shown promising therapeutic efficacy across various in vivo models. To ensure safety in clinical applications, genome sequencing is essential to identify and exclude undesirable genes, thereby determining therapeutic suitability. Here, we have sequenced and analyzed the genomic characteristics of seven lytic bacteriophages targeting carbapenemase-producing MDR K. pneumoniae. Genomic evaluation confirmed that these phages are strictly lytic and revealed the presence of distinct lysis modules across the genomes. Phylogenetic analysis showed that these phages cluster within four genera of Caudoviricetes: Taipeivirus, Drulisvirus, Przondovirus, and Webervirus. The phage genomes ranged from 15,773 to 166,437 base pairs (bp) in length and encoded core structural and replication modules. Furthermore, distinct lytic modules predicted to have function in bacterial capsule degradation were identified across the genomes. Importantly, the absence of known virulence or toxin genes guarantees therapeutic safety and satisfies rigorous genomic criteria for clinical translation. These findings underscore the potential of these phages as a promising alternative for combating MDR and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae infections. Future in vivo investigations and clinical trials are essential to validate their therapeutic efficacy, safety profile, and optimal treatment protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05410-z.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global public health threats, rendering currently available antibiotics ineffective [1, 2]. ESKAPE pathogens epitomize this crisis, as they are the most formidable multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, rendering currently available antibiotics ineffective. Among these, K. pneumoniae emerges as a leading cause of MDR infections. K. pneumoniae is an opportunistic Gram-negative encapsulated bacillus and a key member of the ESKAPE group of critical MDR pathogens, posing a major global public health threat [3, 4]. K. pneumoniae commonly found in the gastrointestinal tracts, upper respiratory tracts, skin of humans, livestock, and wildlife, as well as environmental niches like soil, water, plants, and sewage, this clinically important pathogen causes a diverse range of severe infections including pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), bloodstream infections (BSIs), septicemia, and wound infections [5, 6]. Its remarkable capacity to acquire and disseminate antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-driven by mechanisms such as production of antibiotic-inactivating enzymes (e.g., beta-lactamases and carbapenemases), efflux pumps, and plasmid-mediated gene transfer-has transformed this treatable commensal into a deadly pathogen [7]. This renders last-resort antibiotics, such as carbapenems, ineffective. The emergence of hypervirulent strains further exacerbates this threat, significantly increasing the challenge of effective treatment [8].

The incidence of MDR K. pneumoniae strains continues to rise alarmingly. Although multiple molecular pathways underpin this resistance, the production of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases remains the dominant mechanism driving therapeutic failure in this pathogen [7, 9, 10]. Notably, K. pneumoniae strains producing ESBLs and carbapenemases pose major clinical challenges due to their extreme resistance profiles and severely limited treatment options [11–13]. This bacterium also poses significant pathogenicity concerns, driven by the synergistic action of multiple virulence factors that promote host colonization, immune evasion, and systemic dissemination. Key contributors include lipopolysaccharides, iron acquisition systems, polysaccharide capsules, mucoviscosity-associated exopolysaccharides, and adhesins.

Evidence shows that K. pneumoniae isolates are increasingly exhibiting MDR profiles, accompanied by rising global rates of carbapenem resistance [7, 14]. This convergence of escalating virulence and resistance to existing antibiotics underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies [15, 16]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has classified carbapenem resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) as a pathogen of top priority. This designation is attributed to its significant resistance to a broad spectrum of antibiotics, its prevalence in both healthcare settings and community environments, and the limited treatment options available for infections caused by this organism [14, 16, 17].

Alongside ESβ-producing strains, CRKP poses considerable challenges in clinical environments worldwide, contributing to heightened morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures. The WHO’s 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List highlights carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae as the most critical pathogen, emphasizing the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies to address this threat [18–20]. In this regard, phages have shown promising therapeutic potential. Recent research indicates that phages can effectively target MDR K. pneumoniae, including strains resistant to carbapenems and those producing carbapenemases and ESβLs [16, 21]. This growing body of evidence highlights phage therapy as a valuable tool for managing infections caused by these challenging pathogens [11–13]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that bacteriophages can effectively target multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae strains in both in vitro and in vivo contexts [15, 22–24].

Whole-genome sequencing ensures the safety and efficacy of bacteriophages for clinical applications by verifying lytic cycles, identifying key host-targeting proteins, excluding undesirable genes, and facilitating taxonomic classification. This comprehensive genomic characterization ensures safety and therapeutic suitability and meets regulatory standards for therapeutic use. In this study, we present the whole-genome sequences and detailed functional annotations of lytic bacteriophages isolated from diverse environmental sources in Ethiopia. These phages specifically target multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae, providing essential molecular data for the development of locally optimized phage therapies to address the antimicrobial resistance crisis in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Lytic bacteriophages targeting K. pneumoniae were isolated from wastewater, hospital sewage, and soil samples (Table 1). These phages exhibited significant in vitro lytic activity and therapeutic efficacy against MDR strains of K. pneumoniae. A comprehensive biological characterization, encompassing molecular identification, isolation, and classification, is provided in our preceding studies [25]. The molecular characterization and AMR profile of the bacterial isolates used in this investigation are described in [9].

Phage genome extraction and quality analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted following a previously established protocol [22]. Briefly, the phage lysates were treated with DNase and RNase (1 h, 37 °C) to degrade extracellular nucleic acids, followed by enzyme inactivation with 20 mM EDTA. Proteinase K digestion (3 mg/mL) was performed at 56 °C for 90 min, followed by phage DNA purification using the EZ-10 Spin Column Bacterial Genomic DNA Miniprep Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA). The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000/2000 C Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, USA), and its integrity was confirmed by running a 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

DNA libraries were prepared from purified phage DNA using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted in paired-end mode (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform at the Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI) in Addis Ababa. The quality of raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.0) [26], and low-quality bases and adapters were trimmed using fastp (v0.23.0) [27, 28]. The quality of raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.0), and low-quality bases and adapters were trimmed using fastp (v0.23.0) [27]. Taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken2 (v2.1.2) [29] to categorize reads at the genus and species levels and to filter out potential contaminants. De novo assembly was performed with MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) [30], and contigs from each sample were scaffolded to improve assembly quality. QUAST (V5.0.2) [31] was used for assembly quality assessment, while CheckV (v1.5.0) [32] was used to evaluate genome completeness. Phage-specific downstream bioinformatics analysis, including completeness assessments, lifestyle predictions, structural gene annotations, and taxonomic classification, was performed using PhageScope [33], which provides comprehensive annotations including completeness assessments, phenotypic annotations, structural annotations, taxonomy, and functional annotations. The nucleotide sequences have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and are available under the accession numbers provided.

Molecular phylogenetics

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the nucleotide sequences of the major capsid protein (MCP) and the terminase large subunit (TerL) to evaluate the evolutionary relationships among the bacteriophages [34]. Additional sequences, including isolates from various countries, were retrieved from NCBI. For each query, MCP and TerL sequences with the best hits (e-value near zero and ≥ 90% sequence identity) were downloaded. To avoid geographic bias, no more than four phages per country were included when available. Sequences were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm in MEGA 12 [35]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method in MEGA 12, employing the Tamura-Nei substitution model to accurately estimate evolutionary distances by accounting for multiple substitutions and varying transition/transversion rates. MCP sequences were employed as the primary phylogenetic marker for six phages. For one phage lacking adequate MCP sequence data, the terminase large subunit (TerL) sequence was used as an alternative. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated with metadata using iTOL (V.6) [36].

Results

Genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Seven phages were sequenced for further characterization. The quality of the sequencing data was assessed and verified using FastQC to ensure the suitability of reads for downstream analysis. A total of 829,073 sequence reads were generated, with GC contents varying from 41 to 51%. Taxonomic classification using Kraken2 confirmed the presence of the target phage sequences while removing contaminant reads. The complete genome sequences of all phages were deposited in NCBI GenBank under accession numbers PV588648, PV588649, PV588650, PV588651, PV588652, PV588653, and PV588654. All the sequenced bacteriophages had a linear double-stranded DNA genome (Table 2). The bacteriophages belong to the genera Taipeivirus, Drulisvirus, Przondovirus, and Webervirus (Table 2). The genomes of the bacteriophages ranged in size from 15,773 to 166,437 bp, and the G + C content ranged from 37.71 to 52.85% (Table 2).

Genome annotation

Comprehensive annotation through PhageScope provided detailed functional categorization of predicted genes (Fig. 3). All phage genomes contained multiple proteins essential for various stages of the viral life cycle, including those involved in infection, assembly, replication, packaging, lysis, regulation, and several proteins with hypothetical functions. While the number and identities of these transmembranes differed among phages, several membrane-associated hypothetical proteins were identified. All phages encoded a range of infection-related proteins, particularly tail and tail spike proteins, which are essential for host recognition and adsorption. Structural components, such as capsid proteins and head scaffolding proteins, were universally present. None of the predicted open reading frames (ORFs) encode lysogenic phage-related proteins, such as transposases or integrases. Additionally, no genes associated with antibiotic resistance or bacterial virulence were detected.

Discussion

In the current study, seven lytic bacteriophages targeting MDR K. pneumoniae were sequenced, revealing circular double-stranded DNA genomes, consistent with those of many tailed phages known to infect gram-negative bacteria [37]. All phages were confirmed to be strictly lytic, exhibiting no detectable genes associated with virulence factors or antimicrobial resistance. This finding is promising for their therapeutic potential, as temperate phages carry risks such as horizontal gene transfer and lysogenic conversion, which are not ideal for phage therapy. The phages were classified into the genera Przondovirus, Webervirus, Drulisvirus, and Taipeivirus. The sequence-based classification was in agreement with PCR-based identification [22]. Consistent with previous studies, the diversity of strictly lytic phages supports the genetic variability of bacteriophages targeting K. pneumoniae [38].

Whole-genome analysis of sequenced phages revealed conserved infection-related proteins, including tail and tail spike proteins, which are critical for host recognition and adsorption. These findings are consistent with previous research on phage host specificity [39]. We also identified fundamental structural elements, consistently observed across samples. Their ubiquitous presence underscores a conserved phage architecture essential for proper assembly and structural integrity. Additionally, the small terminase subunit, crucial for DNA packaging, was consistently annotated, supporting established roles in phage assembly [40]. The variable presence of endonucleases and exonucleases indicates diverse DNA processing strategies, similar to those found in other virulent phages. Importantly, no virulence factor or antibiotic resistance genes were detected, indicating the safety of these phages for therapeutic applications. This is in line with previous studies that emphasize the importance of strictly lytic phages, free from undesirable genes, for safe phage therapy. Similar genomic profiles have been reported by earlier studies in K. pneumoniae phages used against MDR strains [41, 42].

Sequence analysis of the lysis module indicated that the phages encode a diverse array of lytic enzymes, including holins, endolysins (lysozymes), spanins, peptidases, and lytic transglycosylases. These enzymes collectively contribute to the efficient degradation of bacterial cell walls and the subsequent lysis of host cells. Consistent with previous studies on Drulisvirus and Taipeivirus genera, which encode multiple putative depolymerases and lytic enzymes capable of degrading the polysaccharide capsule and peptidoglycan of K. pneumoniae [38], our phages harbor multiple ORFs with lyase, lytic transglycosylase, and peptidase activities. This supports the presence of enzymatic depolymerases targeting the bacterial cell wall, aligning with the known depolymerase profiles within these genera [43]. This correlation supports efficient and coordinated gene expression, consistent with previous studies showing regulatory elements scale with genome complexity [44].

The hierarchical clustering analysis revealed a modular organization within the bacteriophage genomes, highlighting conserved functional groups across diverse phages. The high sequence identity (65–92%) among major capsid proteins suggests a shared icosahedral architecture, consistent with their fundamental role in capsid formation [45]. Conversely, tail fiber proteins displayed significant divergence (25–55%), reflecting adaptations for different host recognition and specificity, a pattern typical in phages that accommodate host variability [46]. The presence of canonical lysis modules, including lysozymes/endolysins and holins. Structural protein modules associated with assembly and infection showed moderate to high homology, indicating functional conservation despite sequence differences. The observed mosaicism, particularly in tail fiber genes, underscores the dynamic evolution of phage genomes driven by host adaptation and horizontal gene transfer, influencing host range and infection efficacy [47].

The identification of Rz-like spanins in our phages highlights a conserved lysis mechanism characteristic of Klebsiella phages. Spanins disrupt the outer membrane following peptidoglycan degradation by endolysins, thereby completing the lysis process in Gram-negative hosts. Additionally, the detection of lysis inhibitors (RIIB and RIIA) reflects regulatory elements previously reported to modulate lysis timing, optimizing phage progeny release while preventing premature host cell lysis [48]. Notably, the putative depolymerase enzymes identified, particularly those containing lyase and lytic transglycosylase domains, represent promising candidates for novel antimicrobial agents. Prior research has demonstrated that Klebsiella phages encoding multiple depolymerases can degrade diverse capsule types, thereby enhancing infectivity and therapeutic efficacy [49].

Our findings suggest these phages may exhibit broad capsular specificity or be engineered to target specific K. pneumoniae capsule types, a strategy supported by the successful use of engineered phage depolymerases to overcome capsule-mediated resistance [49–52]. In the current study, the capsular type of the K. pneumoniae strains was not determined, which restricts our ability to assess the capsular specificity of the identified depolymerases. Future work should focus on characterizing the capsule types of the bacterial strains and evaluating the depolymerase activity against diverse capsule variants to better understand and optimize their therapeutic potential.

All phages encoded numerous lysis-associated ORFs, including holins, endolysins, spanins, and peptidases, consistent with their virulent lifestyles. Genus-level differences were observed: Taipeivirus phages (KFM-TASP06, TTM-TASP117, and GKM-TASP92) encoded multiple redundant RIIA/B lysis inhibitors and diverse lysozyme variants, indicating sophisticated regulation of lysis timing. This feature parallels Ackermannviridae phages such as phiAS5, which optimize host exploitation through lysis timing genes [53]. Conversely, the Przondovirus phage exhibited a minimalist lysis system with few core ORFs, consistent with the streamlined genomes of Studiervirinae phages that favor rapid replication over complex regulation [53]. Taipeivirus phages also encoded multiple Rz-like spanins (2–3 copies), exceeding the single-spanin systems typical of model phages like T4 [54, 55]. This multiplicity may enhance the disruption of the complex outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria.

The presence of both RIIA and RIIB lysis inhibitors in KFM-TASP06 is unusual. These proteins are typically encoded separately and function independently in many phage systems. Their co-occurrence suggests novel regulatory mechanisms in lysis control. RIIA and RIIB have previously been reported to regulate lysis [48]. The presence of transmembrane (TM) domains in holin and hypothetical proteins in the current Klebsiella phage sequences supports their roles as membrane-associated lysis factors. Holins, characterized by TM domains, form pores in the bacterial membrane to enable endolysin access for cell wall degradation, facilitating phage release. Additionally, hypothetical proteins with transmembrane (TM) regions likely represent other lysis components. These may include spanins or Rz-like proteins. Such proteins disrupt the outer membrane in Gram-negative hosts like Klebsiella. This clustering of multiple TM-containing proteins within phage lysis cassettes reflects a coordinated lysis mechanism, underscoring their critical function in efficient bacterial membrane disruption and phage replication [56].

Ackermannviridae phages consistently encoded lytic transglycosylases, whereas Drexlerviridae (e.g., AKM-TASP09) relied primarily on holin-endolysin systems, reflecting distinct lysis strategies that align with prior reports. Several ORFs predicted to encode lysozymes with conserved hydrolase activity were identified, specifically ORFs 26, 118, and 128 in KFM-TASP06; ORFs 25, 26, 115, and 127 in TTM-TASP117; and ORFs 45, 46, and 55 in ZHS-TASP18. These enzymes likely play a vital role in peptidoglycan degradation, a crucial step in the phage lytic cycle, thereby facilitating the release of progeny [55, 57].

Phage-encoded cell wall hydrolases, including lysozymes and lytic transglycosylases, facilitate the degradation of bacterial cell wall polymers, thereby compromising structural integrity and promoting bacterial lysis [58]. The presence of multiple hydrolase genes suggests a synergistic enzymatic system targeting different peptidoglycan bonds for efficient host cell wall breakdown, a hallmark of virulent phages [58]. Their identification underscores the crucial role of phage-encoded enzymes in bacterial cell wall degradation and highlights their promising potential as antibacterial agents for phage therapy and as valuable tools in biotechnology, given that these modules can be cloned and harnessed for various applications.

The variation in intermediate terminator counts among different phages, such as GKM-TASP92 with 11 terminators and ZHS-TASP18 with only 2, underscores the diversity in transcriptional regulation mechanisms employed by these viruses. Previous studies have shown that phages with more complex regulatory regions often possess a higher number of transcription factors and terminators, which facilitate precise control of gene expression [21, 59]. This suggests that GKM-TASP92’s numerous terminators may allow for fine-tuned transcriptional switches, enabling the phage to adapt to different stages of infection or host responses. Conversely, the lower number of terminators in ZHS-TASP18 indicates a simplified transcriptional architecture, potentially favoring rapid gene expression with less regulatory complexity, as observed in simpler phage genomes [59, 60]. These differences imply that transcriptional complexity is evolutionarily tailored to optimize phage survival and infectivity within specific ecological niches [61]. Understanding these regulatory variations not only provides insights into phage biology but also has implications for engineering phages for therapeutic purposes, where precise control of gene expression is crucial.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic evaluation of seven novel, strictly lytic bacteriophages targeting carbapenemase-producing MDR K pneumoniae, reinforcing their potential as targeted biotherapeutic agents. The phages exhibit conserved structural and functional modules, including depolymerases and lytic enzymes, essential for efficient bacterial lysis and capsule degradation. Importantly, the absence of virulence factors and antibiotic resistance genes confirms their safety profile for clinical application. Variations in lysis regulation mechanisms, such as the presence of multiple spanins and lysis inhibitors, suggest sophisticated control of lytic timing and membrane disruption strategies, which may enhance their efficacy against diverse K. pneumoniae strains. These findings support the continued development of these phages as targeted biotherapeutic agents, addressing the urgent need for innovative treatments against multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae infections. In vivo studies and clinical evaluations are essential to validate their safety, efficacy, and potential for therapeutic deployment.
