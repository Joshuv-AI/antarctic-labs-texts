Molecular characterization of antimicrobial resistance genes and plasmid profiles in enterobacterales isolated from urinary tract infections in rural outpatient women in Otavalo, Ecuador
Authors: Fernanda Hernández-Alomía, Carlos Bastidas-Caldes, Antonella Zambrano, Mario Narváez, Pablo Castillejo
Published: 2025

Background

The ongoing emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria represents a substantial and expanding risk to public health. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are the second most prevalent ambulatory illness, after respiratory tract infections, with the potential to cause significant complications [1]. UTIs are predominantly caused by bacteria belonging to the order Enterobacterales, including species such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter cloacae [2, 3]. Global data indicate that antimicrobial resistance is responsible for nearly 5 million deaths annually, with E. coli and K. pneumoniae being major contributors due to their involvement in common infections like UTIs [4, 5]. The advent of antibiotic resistance, particularly to beta-lactams, carbapenems, and cephalosporins, has complicated the treatment of these infections and increased associated morbidity and mortality rates [6, 7].

In South America, research findings have indicated a mounting apprehension about the emergence of antibiotic resistance among Enterobacteriaceae. Of particular concern are genes associated with resistance to beta-lactams and colistin. The blaTEM, blaSHV, and blaCTX−M cluster genes have been linked to high levels of resistance in strains isolated from UTIs in the region [8, 9]. Likewise, the mcr-1 gene, which is responsible for colistin resistance, has emerged as an important marker for the detection of resistance, indicating a concerning increase in strains that are resistant to last-line antibiotics [10]. Furthermore, this gene has been associated with rural environments and human-animal interface [11].

Additionally, numerous clinically significant clones associated with UTIs have been identified in South America, including ST131 and ST10 of E. coli [12, 13]. These are known to exhibit high levels of beta-lactam resistance and the capacity to cause recurrent infections on a widespread basis [14–16]. In other Enterobacterales species, such as K. pneumoniae, so-called hypervirulent clones, including ST258 [17] and ST5370 [18], have been identified for their virulence capacity and load of multiple resistance genes.

In addition to the typing of circulating clones, knowledge of plasmid incompatibility groups (Inc) provides additional information for understanding the spread of bacterial resistance [19, 20]. Identification of incompatibility groups, based on their ability to coexist in the same strain, allows one to track the horizontal spread of resistance genes and better understand how they are disseminated in the bacterial population [21, 22]. Twenty-seven major plasmid incompatibility groups are associated with ARGs in Enterobacterales, with IncF, A/C, and X being the most prevalent in beta-lactamase production compared with the other Inc groups [23]. Understanding Inc widespread could help us develop effective control and prevention strategies.

In Ecuador, research on antibiotic resistance in urinary tract infections has traditionally focused on hospitalized patients in urban areas, using tertiary care hospitals as a point of reference. Recent international surveillance reports highlight the alarming scale of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). According to the World Health Organization’s 2022 GLASS report, UTIs caused by multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales are increasingly common and challenging to treat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) where diagnostic and therapeutic capacities are limited [24]. These populations often face distinct epidemiological conditions due to unequal access to healthcare and high rates of unregulated antibiotic use, which may contribute to spreading resistance in ways not captured by hospital-based surveillance [25]. Cultural, social, economic, and geographic barriers limit access to appropriate health services, often leading to late diagnosis and inappropriate treatment [26, 27]. Healthcare practices and antibiotic use in these communities may be poorly regulated, contributing to the spread of resistant strains [28].

A comprehensive understanding of the complexities of bacterial resistance, encompassing both genetic and non-genetic elements, is essential for effective public health surveillance in rural and underserved regions. In this context, the present study aimed to characterize the molecular epidemiology of antibiotic resistance in outpatient urinary tract infections (UTIs) by: (i) screening for prevalent antimicrobial resistance genes using PCR, (ii) identifying plasmid incompatibility groups involved in resistance dissemination, and (iii) determining dominant sequence types through multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of Enterobacterales isolates from rural outpatient women in Ecuador. The findings aim to generate context-specific evidence to support equitable and targeted interventions in historically underrepresented populations.

Methods

Bacterial strains

A total of 381 urine samples were collected from outpatients presenting to CEMOPLAF, Otavalo, Ecuador, between October 2021 and February 2022. Only samples that showed evidence of infection upon microscopic examination—specifically the presence of bacteria and white blood cells—were selected for bacterial isolation [29]. From these, 154 Enterobacterales strains were successfully recovered and are currently preserved in the bacterial strain collection of Universidad de las Américas in 15% glycerol aliquots at − 20 °C. The isolates included five bacterial species: Escherichia coli (n = 137), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 14), and one isolate each of Klebsiella aerogenes, Klebsiella oxytoca and Enterobacter hormachei respectively.

Genomic DNA extraction and quantification

From an aliquot of −20 °C glycerol-preserved bacteria, DNA was extracted using the Chelex-10 method described by Suenaga & Nakamura [30], followed by proteinase K digestion. The supernatants were recovered in microtubes and stored at −20 °C. DNA samples were analyzed using a NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA, ND-2000) to determine DNA quantity and purity. The samples were then stored at − 20 °C for further analysis.

Antibiotic Resistance Genes (ARGs) Screening

For β-lactamase-encoding genes detection, a single-endpoint polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed for six extended-spectrum betalactamases (ESBL) type genes (blaTEM, blaSHV, blaCTX−M−1, blaCTX−M−2, blaCTX−M−8/25, and blaCTX−M−9) with the primers as described by Hoa et al. [31]. Furthermore, for detection of the carbapenemases genes blaOXA−48, blaKPC, blaNDM, and blaVIM, and colistin resistance (mcr-1) a single-endpoint PCR was performed according to previously described procedures [32, 33]. Amplification was performed in reactions of 15 µL containing 2X GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega), 0.3 µM of each primer, and 0.33 ng/µL of extracted DNA. The positive controls for each assay were donated by the Osaka Institute of Public Health from Japan.

Incompatibility groups (Inc) screening

Plasmid identification was carried out using PCR-based replicon typing targeting incompatibility groups HI1, HI2, I1-ly, X, L/M, N, FIA, FIB, W, Y, P, FIC, A/C, T, FIIAs, F, K, and B/O. Each reaction was performed in a 15 µL volume containing 1X GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega), 0.3 µM of each primer, and 0.33 ng/µL of extracted DNA. All the Inc groups were subjected to the same PCR conditions, with the exception for IncF. The thermocycling conditions were performed as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, then 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. For Inc F PCR, an annealing temperature of 52 °C was used [22].

Multi-Locus Strain Typing (MLST)

The MLST technique was performed according to the guidelines of the Pasteur MLST database for 14 Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates, and following the PubMed database for 56 Escherichia coli isolates previously selected for carrying two or more antibiotic resistance genes (TMARG). The PCR conditions of seven housekeeping genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, recA for E. coli, and gapA, tonB, rpoB, phoE, mdh, infB, pgi for K. pneumoniae) were amplified as described by Wirth et al. [34] and Diancourt et al. [35], respectively. Amplification was performed in reactions of 15 µL containing 2X GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega), 0.3 µM of each primer, and 0.33 ng/µL of extracted DNA. The allelic profile and strain-type determination were obtained with the use of the previously mentioned databases. The analysis was performed by constructing minimum-spanning trees (MSTs) with the PHYLOViz software and goeBURST algorithm [36].

Sequencing

PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger technique on an ABI 3500xL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with BigDye 3.1® capillary electrophoresis matrix. Sequences were edited using MEGA X software [37].

Phylogenetic tree

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the NGPhylogeny online platform [38], based on concatenated 16 S rRNA, recA, and rpoB sequences previously identified in a prior study [39]. Sequences were concatenated and aligned using MAFT algorithm and a best-fit model was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Tree topologies and branch lengths were computed using the maximum-likelihood method (ML) with 100 bootstrap replicates. The model employed was General Time Reversible (GTR) with invariant sites. The iTOL software [40] was employed for tree visualization and annotation.

Dot plot analysis of resistance genes across plasmid types

The ggplot2 package was then used to visualize antibiotic resistance genes across plasmid incompatibility groups, where resistance genes were plotted along the x-axis and plasmid incompatibility groups along the y-axis. Each dot represented the presence of a specific resistance gene in a given group, with its size scaled according to the number of occurrences (range: 3 to 10). R version 4.3.3 was employed for this purpose.

Results

Antibiotic resistance genes screening

A total of 154 isolates of Enterobacterales (137 E. coli isolates, 14 of K. pneumoniae, 1 of K. oxytoca, 1 K. aerogenes and 1 isolate of E. hormaechei) were tested for the presence of 11 antibiotic resistance genes. Only four types of these genes were identified in all samples. The most predominant ARG was blaTEM type, representing 87.01% (n = 134) of the total. Subsequently, blaCTX−M−1 type was identified in 44.16% (n = 68) of the samples, while blaSHV type was present in 18.83% (n = 29) and blaCTX−M−9 type in 13.64% (n = 21). No carbapenemases or mcr-1 genes were found.

The isolates exhibited a range of ARGs profiles, with some isolates lacking any ARGs and others displaying up to four ARGs. Most isolates exhibited the presence of one or two ARGs, with a prevalence of 35.71% (n = 55) and 37.01% (n = 57), respectively. The remaining isolates demonstrated no resistance (9.09% [n = 14]), exhibited resistance to three (17.53% [n = 27]), or four ARGs (0.65% [n = 1]). The most common coexistence of two ARGs genes were blaTEM + blaCTX−M−1. Likewise, two types of coexistence of three genes in the same isolate were detected: blaTEM + blaSHV + blaCTX−M−1, and blaTEM + blaCTX−M−1 + blaCTX−M−9, with a prevalence of 25.32% (n = 39), 9.09% (n = 14), and 7.14% (n = 11), respectively.

A total of 13 beta-lactamase gene variants were identified, including both narrow-spectrum beta-lactamases (NSBLs) and extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs). TEM-type genes included blaTEM−1, blaTEM−116, blaTEM−213, and blaTEM−246; among them, blaTEM−1 was classified as NSBL, while the remaining variants corresponded to ESBL-associated types. SHV-type genes detected were blaSHV−11, blaSHV−28, blaSHV−203, and blaSHV−2, with blaSHV−11 classified as NSBL and the others as ESBLs. One LEN-type gene, blaLEN−39, was also identified and classified as NSBL. Additionally, four CTX-M-type genes were detected: blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−15, blaCTX−M−55, and blaCTX−M−65, all corresponding to ESBLs (Supplementary Table 1).

In terms of bacterial species, one isolate of E. coli presented a coexistence of four ARGs (blaTEM, blaSHV, blaCTX−M−1 and blaCTX−M−9), followed 19 isolates with three ARGs, 50 isolates with coexistence of two ARGs, 54 isolates with only one ARGs, and 13 isolates with no ARGs (Supp. Table 2). For K. pneumoniae seven isolates presented three ARGs, followed by six isolates with two ARGs and only one isolated with one ARG. K. aerogenes presented three ARGs (blaTEM−1, blaSHV, and blaCTX−M−1). Finally, K. oxytoca and E. hormaechei did not show any ARGs (Supplementary Table 2).

Plasmid incompatibility groups screening

Of the 18 incompatibility groups tested, eight were identified in the samples. The most prevalent incompatibility groups were IncFIB, IncF, and IncY, occurring in 90.1% (n = 140), 71.43% (n = 110), and 13.63% (n = 21) of the samples, respectively. The lowest prevalence was observed for the groups IncN, IncB/O, IncA/C, IncFIC, and IncL/M, with a presence of between 0.65 and 6.5% (n = 1–10). Furthermore, it was observed that certain isolates demonstrated the capacity to store up to four incompatibility groups. The most prevalent occurrence was the presence of one or two Inc groups, with a frequency of 55.8% (n = 86) and 23.4% (n = 36), respectively (Fig. 1). The coexistence of FIB + F was the most prevalent, occurring in 51.30% (n = 79) of cases (Supplementary Table 2).

The coexistence of Inc-ARGs revealed a remarkable heterogeneity in the combinations detected in outpatients’ isolates. The most prevalent combination was FIB + F + blaTEM, identified in 20.13% (n = 31) of the isolates, followed by FIB + F + blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1 with a frequency of 10.39% (n = 16) and FIB + F + blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1+blaCTX−M−9 with 5.19% (n = 8). Furthermore, the FIB + blaTEM combination was identified in 5.84% of the isolates (n = 9).

Of the 23 Inc-ARGs combinations evaluated, isolates carrying blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1 exhibited the greatest diversity in terms of incompatibility groups, representing 52.17% (n = 12) of cases, while isolates with TEM alone accounted for 39.19% (n = 9). More complex ARGs combinations, such as blaTEM+blaSHV+blaCTX−M−1 and blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1+blaCTX−M−9, were less prevalent, accounting for 26.08% (n = 6) and 17.39% (n = 4), respectively. This data indicates a high level of plasmid-mediated genetic plasticity in the isolated Enterobacterales, which facilitates the dissemination of multiple resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, ARGs were also observed in the absence of detectable plasmids. Of the isolates, 0.65% (n = 1) carried the blaTEM gene, while more complex combinations of ARGs, such as blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1 and blaTEM+blaSHV, were also observed in the absence of plasmids, albeit with low prevalence (Fig. 2).

Multi-locus sequence typing of utis isolates

A total of 56 TMARG Escherichia coli isolates were analyzed, revealing the presence of 33 distinct sequence types (STs). The predominant sequence types identified were ST10 and ST3944, each accounting for 8.93% of the isolates (n = 5). Notably, ST10, recognized as an extraintestinal E. coli clone, was found to function as a central node within the minimum spanning tree (MST). Other prevalent sequence types were ST69, ST131, and ST1193, each representing 7.14% of the isolates (n = 4). Sequence type ST101 was observed in 3.57% of the isolates (n = 2). Additionally, 4 isolates did not align with any known STs, suggesting the presence of novel clones. The remaining sequence types were less frequently encountered, with each occurring at a prevalence of 1.7% (n = 1), as illustrated in Fig. 3. The entire sequences and allelic profiles of the isolates are available in Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

The relentless rise of antibiotic resistance poses a severe threat to global health, disproportionately impacting marginalized and indigenous populations. This first study utilizes genomic insights into UTIs among rural women in Ecuador. Our findings reveal a striking prevalence of ESBL genes among Enterobacterales isolates, with the blaTEM group being dominant in 87.01% of cases. Previous studies from Ecuador have reported ESBL genes identified in clinical isolates such as blaCTX−M (91.13%), blaTEM (65.82%), and blaSHV (26.6%) [41]. Furthermore, among CTX-M variants, CTX-M-15 was found to be predominant in clinical isolates with a presence of 19.51% [42]. However, ambulatory patients could have a different dynamic of transmission, and their distribution varies across different settings and populations [39]. The surveillance of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Ecuador tends to be focused on data from urban healthcare centers, particularly from reference and tertiary hospitals. In this context, our study highlights a disparity in the prevalence of ESBL genes between urban and rural areas, underscoring the importance of rural-focused research and surveillance efforts.

Historically, the most prevalent ESBL enzymes globally were TEM and SHV variants, especially in hospital-associated outbreaks caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae before the 1990 s [43]. However, starting in the 2000 s, the CTX-M enzyme became the predominant ESBL globally, particularly associated with community-acquired infections. Among these, those coded by blaCTX−M−15 have emerged as the most common ESBL type, particularly in Escherichia coli, a significant uropathogen in community settings [44]. This trend has been especially prominent in developing countries, where CTX-M enzymes now dominate community-acquired urinary tract infections with a 36.55% of prevalence [45].

Interestingly, recent studies conducted in rural Ecuador report that blaTEM and blaSHV continue to be the most prevalent beta-lactamase genes, surpassing blaCTX−M in prevalence [25]. These genetic differences may reflect the distinct patterns of antimicrobial use in urban versus rural regions of developing countries. Urban areas often benefit from more regulated and controlled antibiotic use, with limited human-animal interaction that could otherwise facilitate bacterial transmission. In contrast, rural areas in Ecuador tend to have less regulated antimicrobial practices, with empirical treatments being common and the human-animal interface in constant interaction, promoting the spread of resistant bacteria.

The widespread presence of these resistance determinants in vulnerable populations—who face limited access to healthcare and appropriate antimicrobial therapies—exemplifies the profound disparities driving the global antibiotic resistance crisis. The identification of multiple ARGs within numerous isolates, particularly the coexistence of blaTEM (87.01%) and blaCTX−M (44.16%), is alarming. Ambulatory UTIs in the indigenous population deserve special attention since human-animal interface is evident with backyard animals raised for personal consumption. Previous studies mentioned that Ecuadorian poultry industry appears to be a hotspot for antimicrobial resistance, with 94.3% of broiler flocks harboring ESBL or AmpC E. coli [46]. This suggests that these communities are increasingly at risk of multidrug-resistant infections, highlighting the need for urgent and context-specific public health interventions. In response, the upcoming 2025–2030 National Antimicrobial Surveillance Plan from the Ministry of Public Health aims to strengthen awareness and understanding of antimicrobial resistance through educational and communication strategies, as well as effective training programs to promote the rational use of antibiotics.

A positive finding was the absence of KPC and other carbapenemase genes in the isolates, which is important for epidemiological surveillance and helps establish a baseline to detect future resistance threats. On the other hand, the observation that more complex combinations of ARGs were less common may indicate that, while the coexistence of multiple resistance genes is possible, biological or ecological constraints could influence the stability and maintenance of these plasmids in bacteria [47, 48].

The detection of thirteen beta-lactamase gene variants, encompassing both NSBLs and ESBLs, reveals a high genetic diversity of resistance mechanisms. Notably, the predominance of ESBL-associated CTX-M-type genes—specifically blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−15, blaCTX−M−55, and blaCTX−M−65—mirrors the distribution trends observed across South America, where CTX-M enzymes represent the most prevalent ESBL group among E. coli and other Enterobacterales [25]. The detection of blaCTX−M−15 and blaCTX−M−14 aligns with previous findings from Colombia [49] and Peru [50], while the presence of blaCTX−M−55 and blaCTX−M−65, typically reported in East Asia, may suggest potential dissemination through trade or recent migratory flows, as previously hypothesized in southern Ecuador [41]. Additionally, the frequent occurrence of blaTEM−1, a classic NSBL, along with ESBL-related variants such as blaTEM−116, blaTEM−213, and blaTEM−246, supports the hypothesis of point mutations in ancestral TEM genes leading to expanded resistance phenotypes. Similarly, the identification of blaSHV−2 and blaSHV−203 among SHV-type ESBLs reflects their historical association with nosocomial strains, while blaSHV-11, a non-ESBL variant, remains prevalent, consistent with earlier reports in the region [41].

Regarding the capacity for genetic transfer, this study reinforces previous findings indicating the prevalence of certain incompatibility groups, such as IncFIB and IncF, in Enterobacterales strains isolated from UTIs. These incompatibility groups are recognized as key vectors for the transfer of plasmids carrying antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [51]. The high prevalence of the IncFIB group in 90% of isolates and of IncF in 71.43% underscores their ability to be maintained in a wide range of bacterial hosts, facilitating the spread of resistance genes, which poses a significant challenge to antimicrobial treatment. These results are consistent with studies that identified IncI1, IncF (FIB, FIA) and IncB/O as the major incompatibility clusters in uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) plasmids in UTIs [52, 53]. Similarly, IncFIB, IncFIA, and IncB/O plasmids have been associated with ESBLs producing strains in university communities [54], while multiple plasmids, especially those with IncF replicons, have been associated with MDR in UPEC isolates from veterinary urinary tract infections [55].

Additionally, less frequently detected plasmids in this study such as IncA/C (1.94%) and Inc N (6.5%), have been reported to carry resistance to ESBLs, carbapenemases, sulfonamides, and quinolones [56], which are relevant to the treatment of UTIs. Moreover, IncN and IncFIB were found to be capable of storing up to four variants of the fos gene, which is crucial for conferring resistance to fosfomycin, while IncB/O (2.6%), IncFIC (1.3%) and IncY (13.63%) were able to store one or two variants [57].

Furthermore, the capacity of specific isolates to harbor multiple incompatibility groups, up to four in some instances, is indicative of remarkable genetic plasticity, which enables the acquisition of diverse plasmids within a single bacterial cell [58]. This phenomenon enables bacteria to adapt expeditiously to the selective pressures that result from antibiotic usage, thereby accelerating the dissemination of resistance within the community. Moreover, the heterogeneity observed in the combinations of incompatibility groups and ARGs in the samples evaluated reflects the complexity and dynamics of the resistance landscape in these pathogens. The predominance of FIB + F + blaTEM combinations, followed by FIB + F + blaTEM+blaCTX−M−1, suggests intense selective pressure favoring the persistence and dissemination of these genes in the bacterial population analyzed.

The MLST analysis of E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates indicates significant clonal diversity across both species, which reflects the complex dynamics of transmission and evolution of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. In the case of E. coli, a total of 33 distinct type sequences (ST) were identified among the 56 analyzed isolates, with ST10 representing the most prevalent (8.93%). This is a well-documented extraintestinal clone that served as a central node in the MST. Previous Ecuadorian studies from both environmental and animal sources confirm ST10 as an E. coli extraintestinal clone [15]. The identification of other emerging clones, such as ST131 and ST1193, which are traditionally linked to MDR nosocomial infections, highlights the increasing adaptability of these strains to disseminate to non-hospital settings. The identification of four isolates that lack sequence type (ST) matches indicates the emergence of novel clones, underscoring the necessity for comprehensive and sustained molecular surveillance. Furthermore, ST48, ST69, ST95, ST131, ST1193 are related to cases of neonatal meningitis according to Khanh Nhu et al. [59], these strains represent the 25% (n = 14) E. coli of the isolates.

In the case of K. pneumoniae, the MLST analysis of the 14 isolates revealed the prevalence of clones ST15 and ST25, which were responsible for 28.57% and 21.43% of infections, respectively. These clones, which have been frequently associated with healthcare-related infections and multidrug resistance in various regions — such as ST15 KPC-producing K. pneumoniae reported in clinical settings in Bulgaria [60] and Norway [61], and the carbapenem-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (CR-hvKP) ST25 identified in China [61] — may also have epidemiological relevance in Ecuador. Although local phylogenetic data are limited, their detection in ambulatory UTI cases suggests a potential role in the regional dissemination of high-risk clones. Furthermore, clones such as ST45, which were also identified in this study, have been previously described among tigecycline-resistant K. pneumoniae samples from swine slaughterhouses in China [62]. These findings suggest the possibility of animal-to-human transmission.

In contrast, ST462 and ST298 are clones that have recently been described in databases and/or have no epidemiologic relevance [63]. Conversely, the detection of three isolates not assigned to known STs, as in E. coli, suggests the potential emergence of novel genetic variants in this region. Nevertheless, the limited number of research studies on K. pneumoniae highlights the necessity for molecular investigations to elucidate the mechanisms of MDR clones’ dissemination and the imperative to adapt therapeutic strategies to align with these genotypic profiles.

Molecular studies such as ours are crucial for unraveling the complex dynamics of AMR in underrepresented and underserved contexts. Despite their relevance, there remains a significant paucity of molecular investigations focused specifically on rural and vulnerable populations. By generating evidence from a non-urban setting, this study helps fill that gap and supports the design of more inclusive national AMR control and surveillance strategies, including the forthcoming Ecuador’s Plan Nacional RAM 2026–2030. Integrating a framework grounded in territorial, gender, and population equity is essential to ensure that future interventions are not only effective but also socially inclusive and contextually appropriate.

Moreover, our findings underscore the urgent need to incorporate rural populations into national AMR surveillance frameworks. As current policies remain largely focused on urban, hospital-based data, integrating rural and ambulatory settings is essential to achieving a more accurate and equitable picture of resistance dissemination across the country. Doing so will not only improve early detection and targeted response strategies but also ensure that future public health interventions reflect the social and geographic diversity of affected communities.

Limitations and recommendations

Although this study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of antibiotic resistance in Enterobacterales isolated from rural outpatient women, particularly within a marginalized indigenous population, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, despite employing specific and sensitive molecular techniques, the absence of whole genome sequencing (WGS) limited a deeper characterization of the isolates. WGS would have enabled more accurate detection of resistance determinants, virulence factors, and plasmid content, as well as a more robust inference of clonal relatedness. Consequently, the classification of certain non-determinate (ND) clones remained incomplete due to unavailable sequence types in the databases used for MLST analysis. Secondly, the study’s geographic scope was limited to Otavalo, which, while representative of the Quechua nationality in Ecuador, may not fully reflect the genetic or epidemiological profiles of Enterobacterales circulating in other indigenous or rural communities with distinct sociocultural and healthcare contexts.

Despite these limitations, the study offers a critical perspective on AMR in an underrepresented setting and population. Based on our results, we recommend the implementation of strengthened antibiotic stewardship programs and routine molecular surveillance strategies in rural healthcare systems. Furthermore, we advocate for targeted public health interventions that integrate culturally adapted diagnostic and treatment protocols for indigenous women. Policymakers should consider expanding AMR surveillance beyond urban centers and incorporating genomic tools, such as WGS, into national monitoring frameworks to improve the early detection and containment of high-risk clones and resistance genes in underserved regions.

Conclusions

This study provides critical insights into the genomic landscape of antibiotic resistance among Enterobacterales isolated from urinary tract infections in indigenous outpatients in Ecuador. The dominant presence of the blaTEM gene in 87.01% of the isolates, alongside significant occurrences of blaCTX−M−1, blaSHV, and blaCTX−M−9, and underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions in rural areas. The analysis identified multiple clones of E. coli, notably ST10 and ST3944, which are associated with extraintestinal infections, reflecting their adaptability to non-hospital environments. Additionally, the presence of emerging clones like ST131 and ST1193 indicates the evolving nature of resistance mechanisms within these populations.

In K. pneumoniae, ST15 and ST25 were the most prevalent, linking these strains to healthcare-associated infections and multidrug resistance. The identification of novel untyped sequences further highlights the potential emergence of new resistance clones. The high prevalence of plasmid incompatibility groups, particularly IncFIB and IncF, facilitates the horizontal transfer of resistance genes, posing a significant challenge to treatment efficacy. These findings emphasize the necessity for ongoing molecular surveillance and tailored public health strategies to address the dynamics of antibiotic resistance in these neglected communities, where factors such as the human-animal interface and empirical antibiotic use are prevalent.
