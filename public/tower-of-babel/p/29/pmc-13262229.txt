Progressively complex exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias unveiling catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia associated with a novel RYR2 variant in a competitive athlete: a case report

Introduction

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a potentially lethal inherited arrhythmia syndrome caused by dysfunctional intracellular calcium handling, most often due to pathogenic variants in the RYR2 gene.1 It typically affects children and young adults with structurally normal hearts and normal resting ECG.2 Ventricular arrhythmias are triggered by catecholaminergic stimulation and classically appear during exercise or emotional stress.2

Diagnosis can be challenging when early arrhythmias are mild or intermittently reproduced on exercise, particularly in athletes.3 We report the longitudinal diagnostic journey of a competitive athlete with a progressively emerging CPVT phenotype.

Case presentation

A young female competitive footballer with no previous cardiac symptoms, no history of syncope, and no family history of sudden cardiac death was followed annually with pre-participation screening (PPS) for sports eligibility since age 11. During follow-up, the patient was systematically questioned regarding symptoms including palpitations, presyncope, or syncope, none of which were reported. Training tolerance was always excellent. From 2016 to 2017, all PPSs including physical examination, resting ECG, and exercise stress testing (EST) were normal.

Emergence of simple ventricular arrhythmias (2018–2021)

During serial PPSs between 2018 and 2021, isolated monomorphic exercise-induced PVCs with right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology and inferior axis were intermittently observed. These findings prompted further cardiac evaluation, including echocardiography and 24-h Holter ECG monitoring with training session, which never confirmed ventricular arrhythmias and consistently demonstrated only a trivial mitral valve prolapse with a continent valve.

Intermittent repetitive arrhythmias (2022)

Resting ECG showed some not previously identified small but diffuse T-wave abnormalities in inferior and anterolateral leads. Exercise stress testing revealed the first significant abnormal findings, consisting of PVCs occasionally occurring in polymorphic couplets and triplets, exclusively during high-intensity exertion. Subsequent exercise evaluations performed in the following days did not show significant ventricular arrhythmias (Figure 1). Laboratory tests, including electrolytes, thyroid function, troponin, and CK-MB, were normal.

Baseline ECGs were performed in both parents and were normal. Given the patient’s asymptomatic status, unremarkable laboratory findings, and the absence of reproducible arrhythmias on the most recent 24-h ECG monitoring, temporary sports eligibility was granted. Prolonged monitoring with an external loop recorder was planned, and cardiac MRI was proposed but initially declined by the patient due to claustrophobia.

Progressive phenotype (2023)

The 14-day ECG recording, which included multiple exercise sessions documented in the diary, showed only a few isolated exercise-induced PVCs and no repetitive arrhythmias. Cardiac MRI revealed no abnormal findings. Sports eligibility was therefore extended.

Annual PPS with EST confirmed exercise-induced PVCs, still predominantly RBBB with inferior axis. In this case, ventricular repolarization abnormalities appeared to persist during exercise. Therefore, coronary CT angiography was planned to investigate a possible ischaemic cause, despite the absence of anginal symptoms, including the possibility of a myocardial bridge or anomalous coronary origin. Coronary CT scan demonstrated normal coronary origins and course, with a coronary tree free of lesions.

At a subsequent new EST, rare, isolated PVCs and two monomorphic ventricular couplets were again observed at moderate workloads. A new temporary competitive sports eligibility was granted.

Definitive arrhythmic expression (2024)

A new 24-h Holter ECG with an exercise session showed only rare PVCs, whereas a maximal EST demonstrated exercise-induced PVCs including ventricular couplets. The subsequent evaluation included a maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test, which revealed excellent functional capacity and polymorphic PVCs in a fixed bigeminal pattern, including complex forms such as couplets and triplets (Figure 2), with complete suppression at peak exercise. Considering these findings, genetic testing was considered appropriate given the suspicion of CPVT.

Polymorphic exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias during cardiopulmonary exercise testing (2024). Twelve-lead ECG recording obtained during maximal cardiopulmonary exercise testing showing polymorphic premature ventricular complexes occurring in a fixed bigeminal pattern. The arrhythmias include repetitive forms with ventricular couplets and triplets (highlighted), consistent with a catecholamine-dependent arrhythmic pattern. Notably, ventricular ectopy progressively decreased and was completely suppressed at peak exercise.

The patient was initially treated with nadolol as first-line therapy, but the drug was discontinued due to poor tolerability (reported fatigue). Treatment was therefore switched to metoprolol (50 mg daily). A repeat cardiopulmonary exercise test performed under therapy showed only isolated exercise-induced infundibular-type PVCs, without repetitive ventricular arrhythmias.

Genetic confirmation (late 2024/early 2025)

Genetic testing identified a heterozygous single-nucleotide substitution [c.14197C>T] in exon 99 of the RYR2 gene, resulting in the amino acid change p.(His4733Tyr). This missense variant was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS, Class III), according to ACMG criteria (PP3 moderate, PM2 supporting, PP2), as it has not been previously reported in association with CPVT.

Given the identification of a variant in a gene of interest without an established pathogenic role, maximal EST was performed in first-degree relatives. The mother and the younger sister did not show ventricular arrhythmias during exercise testing, whereas the father and the paternal uncle developed repetitive exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias. Genetic testing was therefore extended to other family members and identified the same RYR2 variant in the father, the paternal uncle, and the younger sister, despite the absence of exercise-induced arrhythmias in the latter (Figure 3).

(B) Exercise stress test of the paternal uncle demonstrating exercise-induced monomorphic ventricular bigeminy. Beta-blocker therapy resulted in satisfactory control of the arrhythmic burden. However, in accordance with national protocols for competitive sports participation, the diagnosis of CPVT is considered incompatible with competitive athletic activity, with the exception of sports involving low cardiovascular demand. Therefore, a personalized exercise prescription was implemented under beta-blocker therapy to allow safe physical activity below the identified arrhythmic threshold, together with structured psychological support to address the impact of sport restriction and facilitate long-term adherence to lifestyle recommendations.

Discussion

This case illustrates several diagnostic challenges of CPVT, particularly in competitive athletes. The most striking feature is the long-standing intermittent and slowly progressive phenotype, which significantly delayed diagnostic certainty despite close cardiological follow-up.3

In the present case, repeatedly normal findings over several years paradoxically contributed to diagnostic uncertainty, as the initial arrhythmic burden appeared benign.4 Indeed, the key peculiarity of this case is the dynamic evolution of ventricular arrhythmia. Early findings consisted of rare, isolated, monomorphic, exercise-induced PVCs, intermittently absent on serial Holter ECG recordings, even when exercise sessions with vigorous intensity were included.5 The morphology of the ventricular ectopy suggested a left ventricular origin, possibly from the left ventricular outflow tract or the mitral annulus. At this stage, the arrhythmia could still be interpreted as a form of idiopathic ventricular ectopy, a common and generally benign finding in athletes. Only longitudinal follow-up with repeated maximal EST documented the progression toward repetitive polymorphic arrhythmias, consistent with adrenergically mediated calcium-handling instability.6 This reinforces the concept that CPVT may remain concealed for years and that a single negative exercise test does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in young athletes, whereas repeated longitudinal exercise-based screening may facilitate phenotypic unmasking over time.3 From a screening perspective, this case illustrates both the strengths and limitations of periodic PPS. Repeated annual evaluations allowed longitudinal observation of the evolving arrhythmic phenotype and ultimately raised diagnostic suspicion. However, the diagnosis was established only after several years of follow-up, reflecting the limited sensitivity of PPS for detecting inherited arrhythmia syndromes with intermittent expression and variable penetrance such as CPVT.

Another relevant aspect is the suppression of ventricular arrhythmias at peak heart rate observed during some ESTs. Although counterintuitive, this phenomenon has been previously described in CPVT, where ventricular arrhythmias may peak at submaximal workloads and attenuate or disappear at maximal heart rates, possibly reflecting transient exhaustion or modulation of abnormal calcium release under extreme adrenergic stimulation.7,8 Exercise protocols that achieve higher workloads early during testing may increase the likelihood of unmasking ventricular arrhythmias and revealing more complex patterns.9

From a genetic standpoint, this case highlights the importance of family-based evaluation. The initially unclassified RYR2 missense variant was reclassified as pathogenic only after demonstrating phenotypic cosegregation in affected relatives. This underscores the limitations of variant interpretation based solely on databases and the central role of clinical correlation and family screening in inherited arrhythmia syndromes.10 Importantly, one carrier (the younger sister) did not demonstrate exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias at the time of evaluation. However, incomplete penetrance, age-dependent expression, and variable phenotypic expressivity are well-recognized features of RYR2-related disease. Therefore, the absence of a clinical phenotype in a young carrier cannot exclude future manifestation and should not be considered evidence against pathogenicity. Recent high-impact data indicate that reclassification of genetic variants through family segregation analysis may substantially influence diagnosis, risk stratification, and clinical management in CPVT.11

Beta-blocker therapy represents the cornerstone of CPVT management and resulted in satisfactory control of the arrhythmic burden in the present case. In our patient, additional antiarrhythmic therapy was not required given the adequate control of exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmia. Implantable cardioverter–defibrillator therapy was also not indicated, as the patient remained asymptomatic and had no history of aborted sudden cardiac death.

Finally, the implications for athletic participation are substantial. Recent North American consensus documents propose a more individualized strategy, suggesting that carefully selected patients under appropriate therapy and with controlled exercise testing may be considered for return to play within a shared decision-making framework.12 In contrast, European recommendations adopt a precautionary approach and advise against competitive sports participation in patients with CPVT because of the persistent risk of exercise-triggered malignant arrhythmias.13 Similarly, our national eligibility protocols for competitive sports participation adopt a precautionary approach, excluding athletes with CPVT from high-intensity competitive activities. Early recognition in athletic populations is therefore crucial, not only to prevent sudden cardiac death but also to avoid prolonged exposure to high-risk activities during the diagnostic window.

Consistent with recent evidence emphasizing individualized risk stratification in CPVT,14 a personalized exercise prescription was implemented to maintain safe physical activity below the arrhythmic threshold.15
