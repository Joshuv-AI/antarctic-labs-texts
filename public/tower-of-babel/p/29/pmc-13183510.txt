The nurturing ability of PVG-RT7b rats is strongly affected by the fetal environment
Authors: Hiroe KON, Hisashi UETA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our strain of inbred PVG-RT7b (PVG.7b) rats was at risk of failing to be maintained owing to frequent neonatal death. Only 23.5% of mothers succeeded in nurturing their offspring at the first parturition. Furthermore, mothers failing to nurture their offspring at first parturition also failed in the second one. Here, we investigated the cause of frequent neonatal deaths in PVG.7b rats. Exchanging newborn offspring between PVG.7b and Wistar-Imamichi (WI) rats. resulted in 80.0% of WI mothers succeeding in nurturing PVG.7b offspring, whereas only 18.2% of PVG.7b mothers succeeded in nurturing WI offspring. These findings indicate that the neonatal mortality of PVG.7b is due to maternal factors rather than inherent offspring issues. Additionally, we examined the mammary glands of PVG.7b immediately after the second parturition. PVG.7b rats, which initially failed to nurture, exhibited normal mammary gland development and milk production after the second birth. These results suggested that neonatal death is due to maternal behavior. Moreover, we investigated the nurturing ability of PVG.7b rats nurtured by WI foster mothers (PVG.7b Infant/WI) or born and nurtured from WI recipients after embryo transfer (PVG.7b ET/WI). Only 26.1% of PVG.7b Infant/WI mothers succeeded in nurturing their offspring, compared to 63.6% of PVG.7b ET/WI mothers. However, only 20.0% of PVG.7b mothers born and nursed by PVG.7b ET/WI succeeded in nurturing their offspring. These results suggest that the fetal environment influences the nurturing ability of PVG.7b rats.

INTRODUCTION

PVG-RT7b (PVG.7b; Fig. 1Fig. 1.Photograph of PVG.7b rats. (A) Adult female. (B) Mother and offspring (arrow) at 1 day after birth.) is an inbred rat strain created by S.V. Hunt at the University of Oxford, UK, in the 1980s and used as a donor or recipient in tissue transplant experiments [1, 11]. It was introduced to Kumamoto University, Japan, from Harlan Olac Ltd. (Bicester, UK) in 1995 and transferred to Dokkyo Medical University, Japan in 2001. Since then, it has been maintained through brother–sister breeding. In the colony at Dokkyo Medical University, there have been frequent neonatal deaths since approximately 2015; in 2016, all females of the 33rd generation failed to nurture their offspring. Therefore, to prevent strain extinction, we separated the offspring from their biological mothers immediately after birth and had them nurtured by Wistar-Imamichi (WI) foster mothers.

Photograph of PVG.7b rats. (A) Adult female. (B) Mother and offspring (arrow) at 1 day after birth.

There are three possible causes of neonatal death in rats. Issues related to the offspring, lactation, or maternal behavior. To elucidate the cause of neonatal death in PVG.7b rats, we investigated the following: 1) weaning rates from cross-fostering between PVG.7b and WI rats, 2) weaning rate of PVG.7b offspring born and nurtured by WI rats via embryo transfer, and 3) milk production of PVG.7b mothers. Additionally, because maternal behavior in rats is influenced by the nurturing mother [4], we examined whether the nurturing ability of PVG.7b rat improved with WI foster mother care and embryo transfer to a WI recipient.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Generations 33–41st of PVG.7b rats bred in our facility were used for this study. PVG.7b rats were maintained by sibling mating. WI rats, either obtained from the Institute for Animal Reproduction (Kasumigaura, Japan) or bred in our facility, were used as foster mothers and/or embryo recipients.

Animals were kept in cages made of cyclo-olefin resin (width × depth × height: 270 × 430 × 200 mm) with pulp bedding (Paper clean, Japan SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan) under standard animal housing conditions (lights on 07:00–19:00 hr, temperature 25 ± 3°C, and humidity of 50 ± 10%). Cages were changed once a week, and the animals had ad libitum access to food (CE-2, CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and water.

Adult female rats were used as donors and embryo transfer recipients. Vasectomized adult male WI rats were used to induce pseudopregnancy in female recipients. The experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Dokkyo Medical University (Nos 1124, 1171, and 1173), and all experiments were conducted following the Guidelines for Animal Experimentation of Dokkyo Medical University.

PVG.7b breeding

PVG.7b rats were paired with littermates at 10 weeks of age for 2 weeks. Pregnant females, identified by abdominal distension, were then housed individually for birth and nursing. The offspring was weaned at 21 days after birth. A mother rat was considered to have successfully weaned if she did it for ≥1 offspring. The ratio of successful mothers to the total number of observed mothers was considered the successful weaning mother rate.

Mammary gland observation

Twelve PVG.7b female rats were used for this experiment. After pairing with their littermates and delivering their offspring, they were divided into two groups, success or failure, according to the results of weaning after their first parturition. Their mammary glands were dissected immediately following the second parturition. Female rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of medetomidine (0.4 mg/kg), midazolam (2.0 mg/kg), and butorphanol (2.5 mg/kg) and euthanized by decapitation and phlebotomy. After removing the abdominal hair, the development of mammary glands was observed from above the skin. Then, mammary glands were dissected and milk extraction from the cross-section observed.

Cross-fostering

Eleven PVG.7b and 10 WI female rats were used for this experiment. WI rats, known for their strong nurturing ability, were used as foster mothers. PVG.7b mothers at first parturition or which had failed to nurture at a previous parturition were used as foster mothers for WI offspring. The foster mother exchange was performed between the day of foster PVG.7b mother’s delivery and 2 days postpartum and between the day of birth and postnatal day 2 for WI offspring. WI mothers at first parturition or which had succeeded in nurturing at first parturition were used as foster mothers for PVG.7b offspring (PVG.7bInfant/WI). In this case, the foster mother exchange was performed between the day of the foster WI mother’s delivery and 4 days postpartum and between the day of birth and postnatal day 1 of PVG.7b offspring. After the exchange, offspring were nurtured until weaning on the 21st day after birth. The number of offspring was controlled so that the total number of offspring did not exceed 8. PVG.7b nurtured by cross-fostering was designated as PVG.7bInfant/WI (Fig. 2Fig. 2.Schematic showing cross-fostering and exchange embryo transfer experiment protocol. Wistar-Imamichi (WI) rats were used as foster mothers and embryo transfer recipients. PVG.7b rats nurtured by cross-fostering were designated PVG.7bInfant/WI whereas PVG.7b rats born and nurtured by exchange embryo transfer were designated PVG.7bET/WI.). To assess PVG.7b maternal care, we paired PVG.7b mothers with WI offspring, similar to the experiment described above.

Exchange embryo transfer

Five PVG.7b and five WI female rats were used for this experiment. Female PVG.7b rats at proestrus were placed in cages along with male rats for one night. Mating was confirmed the next morning by the presence of spermatozoa in vaginal smears. Female rats were euthanized the following afternoon for embryo collection. The oviducts were excised and embryos flushed out with the human tubal fluid (HTF) medium (ARK Resource, Kumamoto, Japan). Then, the collected embryos were washed with HTF, and normal 2-cell stage embryos were selected under a stereoscopic microscope (Leica MZ125; Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and transferred into the oviducts of recipient WI rats mated with a vasectomized male rat. Eight to thirteen embryos were transferred into both oviducts of each recipient. After parturition, the offspring was nurtured until weaning by the recipient WI. PVG.7b rats born and nurtured by exchange embryo transfer were designated as PVG.7bET/WI (Fig. 2). The ratio of delivered offspring to the number of transferred embryos was defined as the delivery rate. The ratio of weaned offspring to the number of delivered offspring was defined as the weaning rate.

Intergenerational transmission of the nurturing ability in PVG.7b rats

To assess the nurturing ability of cross-fostered or embryo-transferred daughters, female PVG.7bInfant/WI and PVG.7bET/WI rats were mated with male PVG.7b rats and allowed to give birth and rear their offspring until weaning. In addition, daughters born and nurtured by PVG.7bET/WI were mated with male PVG.7b rats and allowed to give birth and nurture their offspring until weaning.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between groups were analyzed using a χ2 test. A P<0.05 was considered significant. To evaluate differences between fail and success nurturing groups at the time of complete litter loss, a survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method. This event was defined as the day when the number of pups reached 0, and dams retaining ≥1 pups until day 6 were considered censored observations. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 30.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a P<0.05 was considered significant.

DISCUSSION

The successful weaning mother rate of PVG.7b rats was significantly lower than that of WI rats (Table 2). In cases where the offspring died, most deaths occurred by P2 (Fig. 3), and no milk band was observed in their abdomens. This suggests either that the mother did not produce enough milk or that the offspring had an abnormal milk intake. To determine whether mother PVG.7b rats were producing milk, we observed mammary glands immediately after second parturition. Mammary gland development and milk production were confirmed even in females that failed to nurture in the first parturition (Fig. 4 and Table 4). In the second parturition, although 11 out of 12 mothers failed to nurture (Table 3), milk leakage was confirmed in 6 out of 7 mothers (Table 4). These results indirectly suggest that the mother’s lactation ability is not the primary cause of offspring mortality. In this study, we first hypothesized that the nurturing performance of a mother would be consistent between 1st and 2nd parturition. However, the nurturing performance differed between the first and second parturitions in the same mothers (Table 3). Therefore, using the nurturing outcome of the first parturition as an indicator for interpreting the mammary gland function at the second parturition may not be appropriate. To reliably assess the relationship between mother’s lactation and offspring survival, other approaches, such as anesthetizing the mother while pups of a different strain suckle her milk robust and examining weight gain may be necessary [9].

Next, we conducted a cross-fostering experiment where WI mothers with high nursing skills nurtured PVG.7b offspring, and PVG.7b mothers nurtured WI offspring. The successful weaning mother rate of WI was significantly higher than that of PVG.7b (Table 5). Furthermore, in an embryo transfer experiment, wherein PVG embryos were transferred to WI recipients which then gave birth and nurtured the offspring, we found that 4 out of 5 WI recipients (80.0%) successfully weaned 18 out of 23 PVG.7b offspring (78.3%) (Table 6). These results indicate that the cause of the nurturing failure in PVG.7b rats is failing maternal care. Since maternal behavior was not directly observed in this study, future research should directly observe maternal behavior. Additionally, this study observed mammary gland development and milk secretion but not whether milk is ejected during suckling. The abnormality in milk ejection cannot be ignored, so it is also necessary to conduct a suckling experiment to find out whether milk is being ejected normally.

We investigated the weaning success mother rate of PVG.7b Infant/WI and of PVG.7b ET/WI to ascertain whether the environment of the fetal and infant periods affects the nurturing ability of female rats. We observed that the weaning success mother rate at first parturition of PVG.7b ET/WI was significantly higher than that of PVG.7b Infant/WI (Table 7). These results suggest that the nurturing ability of PVG.7b rats is strongly influenced by the environment. We also investigated the successful weaning mother rate at first parturition of PVG.7b ET/WI’s daughters, which was only 20.0% (Table 7). This result appears to support and reinforce the aforementioned suggestion; however, the sample size is insufficient for statistical comparison.

PVG.7b rats were introduced to Dokkyo Medical University in 2001 and maintained through sibling mating. In 2016, all females of the 33rd generation failed to nurture their offspring, resulting in a critical threat to the maintenance of the strain, which contributed to this study. The cause was likely the lack of nurturing ability in the mother rats. The breeding performance data of PVG.7b prior to the frequent offspring deaths are shown in Table 1. The mean weaning number of four litters in four generations used for strain maintenance was 7.0 ± 0.8. Although there are no records of the number of offspring born, we consider that they had normal nursing abilities based on the number of offspring weaned. This is because we typically reduce litters with more than 10 offspring to around 8. In PVG.7b rats, it had been previously observed that some females did not nurture their offspring; however, the frequency of this phenomenon had not been recorded. Breeders continued to maintain the strain by selecting individuals that nurtured their offspring normally, but with the increase in the number of generations, the number of females that did not nurture their offspring increased, and by the 33rd generation, all females failed to nurture their offspring. A genetic mutation, either present from the outset or arising at some point during strain maintenance, might have spread over generations, and is responsible for the failing pup weaning. Nonetheless, the precise mechanism underlying the observed nurturing deficiency in PVG.7b rats remains unclear. Detailed phenotypic and genetic analyses are needed to clarify these aspects.

In rats, maternal behavior comprises several elements, including nest building, nursing, licking/grooming of the offspring, and retrieval behavior to carry the offspring back to the nest [8]. Regarding licking/grooming, offspring born to mothers who groom infrequently but reared by adoptive mothers that groom frequently exhibit high grooming behavior in adulthood [4]. This is the result of transmission of behavioral traits not dependent on genes. In prolactin knockout (PRL-KO) mice, the offspring die because the mother mice do not exhibit retrieval behavior to the nest [10]. PRL-KO mice born by embryo transfer to wild-type recipient mice develop retrieval behavior in adulthood, whereas wild-type mice born from PRL-KO recipients do not express retrieval behavior, so their offspring dies. Accordingly, receiving prolactin during the fetal period appears necessary for the expression of retrieving behavior in adulthood [10]. It is possible that PVG.7b rats also fail to nurture because of failing prolactin signaling during the fetal period, but to clarify this point, it is necessary to examine maternal behavior more closely as well as performing measurements of blood prolactin concentration, and the mechanism whereby prolactin controls retrieval behavior.

Embryo transfer can cause other behavioral changes in rats. Particularly, Tokai high avoider (THA) rats were bred by selection in an avoidance behavior learning test [3]. While the learning ability of THA rats born by embryo transfer to Wistar recipients was considerably reduced, when THA rats were nurtured by Wistar foster mothers, their learning ability was not impaired. Furthermore, when these low-learning ability THA rats were allowed to mate, their offspring retained the high learning ability of the original THA rats [3]. Although the mechanism underlying the decreased learning ability in THA rats remains unclear, these results suggest that the learning ability of THA rats is strongly influenced by the environment during the fetal period. This pattern of intergenerational transmission of learning ability parallels the transmission of nurturing ability observed in PVG.7b rats here.

A phenotype is formed by the genotype inherited from both parents and the influence of the developmental environment. Regarding behavioral traits in rats, the influence of the developmental environment during the infant period in rats has been analyzed in detail in foster mother exchange experiments [2, 4,5,6,7, 12]. For example, when a mother rat exhibits frequent grooming behavior, her offspring exhibit a higher grooming frequency after growth. In contrast, offspring raised by mothers with low grooming behavior exhibit reduced grooming behavior after growth [2, 4]. The extent of maternal care determines glucocorticoid receptor expression and stress responses [5, 6]. Despite the difficulty in investigating the influence of the developmental environment during the fetal period, advances in developmental engineering technology allow using genetically modified animals and embryo transfer to produce mother–offspring combinations with different genotypes and phenotypes. This will likely shed light on previously unknown effects of the fetal environment on the behavioral traits of offspring and enable analysis of the effects of the developmental environment on offspring.

In conclusion, based on our results, the cause of neonatal death in PVG.7b rats is attributable to maternal factors. Furthermore, the nurturing ability of PVG.7b rats is affected by the intrauterine environment and can be restored by embryo transfer to WI recipients.
