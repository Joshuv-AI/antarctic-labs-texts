Early detection of construction project risks in Saudi Arabia: a mixed-methods study on warning signs and mitigation
Authors: Badr T Alsulami, Ali M Al‑Shaery, Mukhtar A Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of construction risk management in Saudi Arabia, this research identifies and triangulates the Early Warning Signs (EWS) for construction project failure. Two mixed-methods approaches were used. First, a questionnaire survey of construction professionals was conducted to elicit EWS and identify mitigation actions. Second, participants were invited to participate in a Nominal Group Technique (NGT) session and prioritize the EWS and identify mitigation actions. The survey was developed from existing literature and a pilot study to include 41 items, and was found to have high internal reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). Descriptive statistical analysis was used to analyze data from the survey, and the qualitative phase of the research was used to achieve consensus in prioritization of EWS and to describe mitigation options. The findings of this research provided converging evidence that the following EWS exist for construction failure: management’s failure to respond effectively, weak project definition, ineffective leadership, grossly inaccurate costs, and a lack of project experience. The study provides context-sensitive mitigation options, including improving the tendering and cost engineering functions, improving the front-end definition and resource planning of construction projects, establishing effective leadership and reporting procedures, and institutionalizing risk registers and lessons learned. Overall, the findings of this research will inform early warning signs for risk management and provide a foundation for subsequent case study evaluation of the effectiveness of mitigation measures in Saudi Arabia.Keywords: Early warning signs, Project failure, Mitigation measures, Construction project management, Risk managementSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In the building sector, project performance is an important consideration for with respect to determining if a project has been successful. The criteria upon which all projects are evaluated are: time/cost/quality. Even with improvements in project management practices, there are continuing examples of failures within transportation construction projects in the form of delay or schedule overruns, cost overruns, quantity defects found during construction, or project cancellations. Current research continues to suggest that while there have been improvements through digital solutions, lean methodologies or integrated project delivery system (IPDS) approaches, there continues to remain a substantial chance that a construction project will have underperformance. Reasons why construction projects may have underperformance are inadequate planning or ineffective stakeholder involvement (such as contractors not having enough time for pre-bid/pre-construction) and poor risk management. Additionally, many bottled-up project risks exist due to external shocks (such as the economy or government regulations)1,2.

The construction sector plays an important role in the socio-economic transformation of Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia is expected to allocate significant funds to the country’s Vision 2030 program, which includes several large-scale projects (NEOM, Qiddiya, the Red Sea Development, and Diriyah Gate) and nationwide housing and infrastructure development. As a result, the Saudi construction market was valued at $141 billion at the end of 2023 and is expected to continue to grow, driven by the government’s plans to encourage economic diversification and the levels of public and private investment in the Kingdom3. However, due to the rapid growth of the construction industry, many significant challenges will hinder growth, such as: supply chain disruption; increasing cost of imported materials; labour shortage; regulatory environment; and the overall uncertainty of the global economy4. All of these issues ultimately lead to a higher risk of projects underperforming and illustrate the need for the identification of early warning signs of potential project failure (EWS).

Early warning systems (EWS) provide organizations with early (and therefore, proactive) indications or signals of emerging areas of risk that could develop into significant project failures if not managed effectively (i.e., addressed). EWS can be viewed conceptually as evidence-based indicators of imminent failure within each of the various failure-related theories of project failure through the detection (reading) of evidence of emergent failure(s), ill-defined risk allocation, lack of effective communication, or ineffective controls. Project failure is seldom an instantaneous occurrence; rather it is a process that does not occur overnight but rather an accumulation of many early misalignment events; project misalignment events including; unreasonable or inaccurate cost estimates, improper risk allocation, ineffective communication, etc., which accumulate over time will significantly decrease the level of organizational control and ultimately will develop such that the lagging results (i.e., breach of schedule and/or budget) will eventually develop into project failure5,6. Understanding EWS in this way allows for an understanding of project failure consequences unidentifiable until their onset, and will enable timely intervention prior to project failure gravitating toward an inevitability. Nevertheless, there remains very little published research regarding EWS that is specific to the unique cultural, institutional, and economic environments in Saudi Arabia, as well as a void in the available empirical evidence on EWS (i.e., leading indicators of project failure) based on previous or current empirical cases in the region; most of the available literature addresses the factors contributing to project failure post-facto and discusses project failures but does not facilitate the development of a systematic process for identifying potential leading indicator(s) of failure and/or developing corresponding mitigation strategies7.

Literature review

Causes of project failure

Construction projects are commonly deemed failures when they experience time delays, cost overruns, or quality shortfalls relative to contractual expectations. A growing body of research has examined the underlying causes of these failures across different countries and project types. In Saudi Arabia, risk mismanagement, budget overruns, and poor interdepartmental communication have been highlighted as dominant factors undermining infrastructure projects8. Similarly, Maity9 emphasized that poor risk management, inaccurate cost estimation, and scheduling delays remain critical causes of failure. Studies in other regions echo similar concerns. For instance10, reported that construction projects often fail due to cost overruns, safety deficiencies, and quality shortfalls11. found that underground water projects were most affected by financing issues, economic volatility, material shortages, and adverse natural conditions.

Company-level studies also demonstrate that organizational weaknesses are a major source of project underperformance. Reference12 identified economic instability and lack of managerial experience as key causes of contractor failure, while13 reported that engineering firms are often undermined by fluctuating material costs, cash-flow delays, and intense competition driving profit margins to unsustainable levels. Likewise14, showed that failures often stemmed from weak planning, frequent design changes, financial constraints, corruption, and poor contractor performance. Finally15, found that delays in staged payments and lack of structured project planning were central obstacles.

Across these studies, risk management, cost control, planning, and communication emerge as recurring causes of project failure. However, much of the evidence is derived from non-Saudi contexts, leaving a gap in understanding the specific causal factors driving failure in Saudi Arabia’s construction sector under Vision 2030 dynamics. Most prior work reports causes/risk factors; fewer studies operationalize leading indicators that can be monitored during execution.

Early warning signs vs. risk factors and outcomes: conceptual framing

Although modern project management tools and digital solutions have advanced considerably, construction projects worldwide continue to experience delays and cancellations. A promising approach to mitigating these outcomes lies in the identification of Early Warning Signs (EWS), proactive indicators that alert stakeholders to potential problems before they escalate16. illustrated how monitoring early warning signals in the railway project allowed decision-makers to intervene at the front-end phase, reducing the risk of overruns. Similarly17, developed EWS indicators for mechanical and electrical projects, enabling project teams to predict outcomes with greater accuracy18. analyzing projects in Norway, the UK, and Australia, concluded that EWS must be context-specific, as each sector (e.g., construction, oil and gas, ICT) experiences distinct failure patterns.

More recently19, emphasized that weak managerial capacity and failure to proactively manage risks are themselves early warning signals of failure in Nigerian projects. Other studies have highlighted uncertainty in early project phases, ineffective communication, poor leadership, stakeholder conflicts, and financial instability as strong predictors of project underperformance20,21.

These factors consistently reappear across global contexts, suggesting their importance as leading indicators of failure. Despite these insights, the literature lacks a systematic investigation of EWS specific to Saudi Arabia. While international studies provide valuable benchmarks, no research has empirically examined how EWS manifest in the Saudi construction sector, especially in the context of giga-projects, regulatory reforms, and economic diversification under Vision 2030. This gap underscores the need for empirical, mixed-methods research to identify, validate, and contextualize EWS in Saudi construction projects, and to propose mitigation strategies tailored to local realities. Given these conceptual and methodological gaps, a mixed-methods approach is required to both prioritize EWS and contextualize mitigation.

Early Warning Signs (EWS) of construction project failure reported in literature.

As shown in Table 1, prior literature identifies 53 distinct early warning signs (EWS) of construction project failure. These EWS cluster into five categories: (i) cost and time overruns, such as inaccurate estimation and persistent delays; (ii) planning and scope deficiencies, including absence of feasibility studies, poor documentation, and scope redefinition; (iii) leadership and management weaknesses, such as poor communication, lack of risk management, and weak project commitment; (iv) stakeholder-related issues, including unresponsive clients, conflicts, cultural misalignments, and inadequate stakeholder engagement; and (v) organizational instability, such as leadership turnover, resource diversion, and lack of reporting systems.

Risk factors, early warning signs, and failure outcomes are terms that many researchers have previously used interchangeably. Dividing these concepts into clearer categories enhances theoretical clarity and improves practicality. In the present study, risk factors are defined as causal conditions that increase the likelihood of underperformance (such as weak governance, low capability, and external volatility), while EWS are indicators that provide evidence of the risk materialisation process and therefore the need to intervene before the risk further escalates. Lastly, failure outcomes can be defined as lagging indicators of underperformance (such as total cost overrun at completion, substantial schedule delay at completion, non-conformance to quality expectations, and anticlimactic cancellation). These distinctions will improve interpretation of shoreline items that may appear temporary or final product results but instead reflect continuous evidence of progressive deviation (i.e., “time differentials”) measured through ongoing monitoring processes (i.e., repeated milestone slippage) relative to both period length and level of performance. Theoretical, EWS function when they provide actionable signals of escalation behaviour (i.e., small deviations will demonstrate dwindling organisational control, diminishing team integration, and/or failure of proper governance before total impact will be marginalized).

In addition to representing generic challenges (e.g., ineffective reporting systems, stakeholder misalignment, weak leadership, and a lack of project readiness), these examples illustrate early signs of decreased decision quality, delayed corrective action, and impaired feedback loops, which serve to increase the rate at which latent risk transitions to realized failure. Thus, EWS are classified within this research as leading indicators that may commence timely intervention and derail pathways for escalation, rather than post-hoc descriptions of failed events. The literature reviewed highlights considerable variation in the identification and use of EWS: Several studies employ conceptual checklists only; others limit their analysis to a specific sector or type of project; while a significant number report on individual elements without providing a ranking of their relative importance or linking them to options for mitigation. This methodology diversity (along with the lack of localised Saudi data under the challenges imposed by Vision 2030) supports the need for a mixed-methods approach. Specifically, the quantitative component will rank the priority level of EWS for each reported stakeholder group, while the qualitative NGT component will obtain expert consensus regarding the meaning of, and the linkages between, the identified priority EWS and practical options for mitigating actions that fit with local project governance and delivery realities.

Recent Saudi-based studies reinforce these patterns24. revealed that public-sector projects continue to experience cost and schedule overruns25. identified 83 risk factors for Saudi building projects, classifying them across client, contractor, and external dimensions. Ref3. demonstrated that machine learning models can detect early indicators of delays in Saudi construction projects6. reported that COVID-19 disrupted supply chains and labor, exacerbating delays. Similarly28, highlighted leadership, stakeholder alignment, and institutional support as critical success factors for sustainable projects, while29 noted that regulatory bottlenecks and financial instability remain significant barriers. Taken together, these findings confirm that although EWS are widely recognized internationally, their manifestation and relative importance in Saudi Arabia under Vision 2030 remain underexplored. In particular, few studies in the Kingdom have explicitly framed project underperformance in terms of leading indicators (EWS), rather than ex post risk factors. This study aims to fill this gap by systematically identifying and validating EWS for Saudi construction projects and proposing mitigation strategies tailored to the local context.

Research methodology

This section outlines the research methodology employed to investigate early warning signs of construction project failure, detailing the research design, data collection methods, and data analysis procedures.

Research design

This study employed a mixed-methods research design, integrating both quantitative and qualitative approaches to evaluate early warning signs (EWS) of project failure in construction. The quantitative component involved a questionnaire survey, while the qualitative component utilized the Nominal Group Technique (NGT). This dual-method approach ensured a comprehensive investigation by combining numerical data with deeper qualitative insights through structured group discussions.

According to30, mixed-methods research enhances the reliability of findings by triangulating data from multiple sources, thereby strengthening validity. Similarly31, emphasized that integrating qualitative and quantitative data allows for broader generalizability while preserving contextual depth. In this study, the quantitative phase provided statistical rigor by ranking crucial early warning signs, whereas the qualitative phase offered rich insights into contextual challenges and mitigation measures. This triangulation increased the credibility of the research outcomes. The research targeted a purposive sample of accredited professionals and specialists in construction project management. Selection was based on expertise and certification, ensuring that the data reflected well-informed perspectives aligned with the research objectives.

Questionnaire survey

The initial pool of early warning signs (EWS) items was developed from an extensive literature review and exploratory research, generating 53 candidate items (Table 1). To ensure content and face validity, a pilot study involving 15 experts with extensive experience in large-scale construction projects within the Saudi context was conducted. Participants evaluated each item for clarity, relevance, and redundancy, and suggested any additional EWSs. Based on their feedback and pilot data analysis, several items were refined for clarity, overlapping items were merged or removed, and the instrument was reduced to 41 items, forming the final questionnaire for the main survey. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.86, confirming its reliability for further analysis. Designed to collect quantitative data on early warning signs of project failure, the final 41-item questionnaire employed a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important to 5 = highly important), allowing respondents to rank the perceived significance of each EWS. The survey was distributed online to targeted participants, and responses were collected over three months.

Nominal Group Technique (NGT)

Nominal Group Technique (NGT) Panel Composition and Process. Following the survey analysis, a Nominal Group Technique (NGT) session was conducted to validate and refine the top early warning signs and to develop consensus-based mitigation strategies. The NGT panel comprised four experts, purposively selected to represent diverse stakeholder perspectives critical to Saudi construction projects. The panel included: (1) a senior project director from a major government client organization involved in Vision 2030 megaprojects (19 years of experience); (2) a project manager from a leading international contracting firm operating in Saudi Arabia (15 years of experience); (3) a senior consultant specializing in project controls and risk management (15 years of experience); and (4) a regulatory/quality assurance expert with oversight experience across multiple large-scale public projects (20 years of experience). Each expert met the minimum inclusion criterion of 20 years of senior-level experience in the Saudi construction sector and demonstrated direct involvement in projects exceeding SAR 500 million in value. While NGT panels typically range from 4 to 12 participants, smaller panels are considered appropriate when participants possess deep, specialized expertise and represent distinct stakeholder categories, as was the case here. The structured NGT process involved: (i) silent generation of ideas; (ii) round-robin sharing; (iii) clarification and discussion; and (iv) ranking and consensus-building through iterative voting rounds. The session was facilitated by the lead researcher and lasted approximately four hours34. To ensure transparency and rigor in the qualitative analysis, the NGT discussions were subjected to an inductive thematic analysis, following an iterative coding procedure to identify key themes and mitigation strategies from the experts’ collective insights. The single NGT session, lasting approximately four hours, allowed for comprehensive discussion and consensus-building, leading to thematic saturation within the panel as no new significant ideas emerged in later stages. Experts were rigorously recruited based on stringent criteria of over 20 years of senior experience across diverse construction roles, and potential conflicts of interest were managed by focusing on broad industry expertise rather than specific project affiliations. While the initial coding was performed by one researcher, the thematic framework was validated through iterative expert review and consensus during the NGT session itself, and subsequently cross-checked by a second researcher, ensuring the trustworthiness of the findings.

Demographic analysis

Participants were selected using a purposive sampling strategy from a recruitment frame of accredited professionals listed in the Saudi Council of Engineers (SCE) directory and senior contacts within major Vision 2030 project organizations. The inclusion criteria required a minimum of 5 years of senior-level experience in the Saudi construction sector, and professional accreditation was verified against the SCE’s official registry. A total of 133 invitations were sent to qualified individuals, from which we received 96 valid responses, yielding a strong response rate of 72%. To assess potential nonresponse bias, we conducted a wave analysis comparing early and late respondents, which revealed no significant statistical differences, suggesting the sample is representative of the target group. Analysis of key findings

The survey results indicate that “bad project setup” is perceived as the most critical early warning sign, with the highest mean score of 4.5667/5, reflecting a consensus among respondents that inadequate initial planning and project scoping significantly increase the likelihood of failure. This finding aligns with prior research highlighting the importance of early-stage project definition in mitigating risks57. The second-highest ranked indicator is the inability of management to detect and proactively address emerging issues (4.4667/5), underscoring the critical role of managerial vigilance and proactive risk management in maintaining project performance. Closely following this is poor leadership (4.4556/5), which highlights that ineffective leadership can exacerbate project challenges and hinder timely resolution of problems.

Other highly ranked indicators include cost inaccuracy (4.3778/5), emphasizing the impact of budget misestimation on project performance, and lack of experience in managing similar projects (4.2333/5), reflecting the importance of expertise in avoiding common pitfalls. The indicator project awarded below estimated cost (4.2333/5) suggests that underbidding may compromise project quality and increase the risk of failure.

Notably, schedule-related issues also ranked highly. Constant schedule slippages (4.2111/5) and time overruns (4.2000/5) indicate that ineffective time management is a persistent challenge in Saudi construction projects. Similarly, undefined roles and responsibilities among team members (4.1667/5) and lack of top management support (4.1222/5) highlight the importance of organizational structure and executive backing in project success. The remaining indicators, while moderately ranked, provide valuable insights into additional risk areas such as client unresponsiveness, inadequate communication, and lack of formal risk management processes. Collectively, these findings emphasize that project failure is multi-dimensional, arising from a combination of technical, managerial, financial, and organizational factors. By ranking these EWS, this study provides practitioners and stakeholders with a prioritized list of indicators that can be used to implement early interventions, develop monitoring frameworks, and guide strategic decision-making to prevent project failure in Saudi construction projects.

Narrative synthesis

The ranking analysis reveals that technical, managerial, and financial factors dominate the top early warning signs. “Bad project setup” received the highest average score (4.5667), indicating that inadequate initial planning, unclear project scope, and poor preparation are the strongest predictors of project failure. Close behind, management inefficiency and inability to proactively address issues (4.4667) and poor leadership (4.4556) emphasize the importance of competent leadership and active risk monitoring in project success.

Financial mismanagement is also critical, with cost inaccuracy (4.3778) and projects awarded below estimated cost (4.2333) ranking among the top six. Lack of experience (4.2333) highlights that expertise in similar projects is a key determinant for avoiding early-stage risks. Schedule management issues, including constant schedule slippages (4.2111) and time overruns (4.2000), were consistently highlighted, reflecting the global trend of delays as a major contributor to project underperformance.

Organizational and team-related issues, such as undefined roles (4.1667) and lack of top management support (4.1222), further underscore the importance of clear accountability and senior-level commitment. Lower-ranked indicators, including cultural conflicts, attendance issues, and stakeholder disputes, although less critical, point to contextual and behavioral factors that may exacerbate risk if unaddressed.

Collectively, the results confirm that project failure is multi-dimensional, arising from technical deficiencies, managerial weaknesses, financial misestimations, and organizational challenges. This prioritized list of early warning signs can serve as a practical tool for proactive monitoring, enabling project managers and stakeholders to implement early corrective actions, strengthen risk management frameworks, and improve project success rates in Saudi construction projects.

Nominal Group Technique (NGT) results

The Nominal Group Technique (NGT) was employed to identify the most significant early warning signs (EWS) of construction project failure and to explore mitigation strategies based on expert opinion. NGT is a structured method for small-group discussion aimed at reaching consensus. It systematically collects individual ideas, facilitates discussion, and ranks priorities, ensuring that all participants have an equal opportunity to contribute.

The NGT process followed in this study involved the following steps:

Idea Generation: The moderator posed a research question to the experts, who independently recorded their ideas in brief phrases or statements.

Discussion

This study examines early warning signs (EWS) of construction project failure as perceived by construction professionals through a questionnaire survey and triangulated with expert consensus using the Nominal Group Technique (NGT), followed by the identification of mitigation measures for the most critical EWS. Although prior research has reported a wide range of project failure indicators, there remains a need to prioritize EWS and connect them to actionable responses that can be implemented before risks escalate into major underperformance36. Consistent with this motivation, the present findings indicate that a relatively small set of signals dominates stakeholders’ attention, suggesting that governance and control weaknesses tend to appear early and then propagate into downstream outcomes if not addressed.

The survey results show that “bad project setup” was the highest-ranked EWS, highlighting the decisive role of front-end definition (scope clarity, baseline schedule realism, resource readiness, and governance arrangements) in shaping execution performance. This was followed by the “inability of management to detect and proactively respond,” indicating that the absence of timely escalation routines, effective monitoring, and corrective decision-making is itself an early signal that risks may intensify. Other highly ranked EWS—“poor project leadership,” “inaccuracy of project costing,” and “lack of experience”—further reinforce that early vulnerabilities are often rooted in capability, planning discipline, and control systems rather than isolated technical issues37. These findings are especially salient in the Saudi context, where projects are increasingly delivered under accelerated timelines, complex stakeholder interfaces, and evolving regulatory and supply-chain conditions, which amplify the consequences of weak front-end readiness and slow managerial response to emerging deviations4.

Comparison with prior studies supports both the robustness of these dominant indicators and the importance of context in their relative ordering. For example58, reported a closely related set of top EWS but with a different rank order, placing “inability of management” first and “bad project setup” second, which is consistent with the view that front-end deficiencies and weak managerial response form a coupled escalation pathway rather than independent problems. Similarly39,40, identified “bad project setup” and “management inefficiency” as critical indicators, aligning with the present study’s central argument that governance and control breakdowns operate as leading signals across multiple project environments. While variations in ranking across studies may reflect differences in sector, procurement arrangements, and measurement approaches, the recurring presence of poor setup, weak management, and inadequate leadership suggests these EWS are structurally important predictors of project vulnerability41.

The mixed-method triangulation offers additional insight beyond ranking alone. The survey provides breadth (N = 96) in prioritizing EWS, whereas the NGT phase contributes depth by clarifying the practical meaning of high-ranked signals and translating them into mitigation actions. Importantly, convergence between the survey and NGT outputs—particularly for top-ranked items such as “bad project setup,” “cost inaccuracy,” and “lack of experience”—increases confidence in the stability of the key signals despite the small expert panel size (NGT, N = 4). At the same time, where differences emerge between the two phases, they should be interpreted as stakeholder- and role-sensitive perspectives rather than inconsistencies; these divergences highlight that early warning systems may need to be operationalized with shared baselines, clear decision rights, and transparent reporting to reduce perception gaps and shorten response time when warning signals arise.

With respect to mitigation, this study positions the proposed actions as consensus-based and context-dependent responses rather than universal prescriptions. This is particularly important in Saudi Arabia, where feasibility is shaped by procurement route and contract form (e.g., the extent of client authority over staffing decisions, enforcement of liquidated damages/penalties, and change-order governance), as well as labor availability and HSE requirements, especially for night work4. For example, schedule recovery actions such as double shifts may reduce delay risk but can increase direct cost and elevate fatigue-related safety risk, requiring enhanced supervision, lighting/logistics plans, and explicit compliance with labor and HSE policies. Likewise, applying penalties or replacing underperforming team members depends on contract clauses, HR processes, and documented performance management to avoid dispute escalation. Accordingly, mitigation should be operationalized through explicit thresholds (e.g., milestone slippage or cost variance triggers), defined accountability (who decides and who implements), and documented approval pathways to ensure actions are practicable and auditable.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the results may be influenced by sample composition and the contextual concentration of respondents, which may affect generalizability beyond similar project environments46. Second, although the study triangulates survey evidence with expert deliberation, the mitigation measures were not quantitatively evaluated for cost impact, safety implications, contractual enforceability, or effectiveness across Saudi project types; therefore, they should be treated as expert recommendations to be tailored to delivery method, governance structure, and contract conditions. Future research should examine the predictive validity of the identified EWS through longitudinal designs that link early signals to eventual project outcomes37, explore how cultural and organizational factors shape stakeholder prioritization of EWS45, and integrate quantitative performance metrics with qualitative assessment to strengthen early warning systems and enable more data-driven monitoring16. Further work is also needed to evaluate which mitigation strategies are most effective under different Saudi procurement models and project typologies, thereby supporting evidence-based selection of interventions and improving project success rates4,53.

Conclusion

This study identified and prioritized early warning signs (EWS) of construction project underperformance in Saudi Arabia using a mixed-methods design that combined a questionnaire survey with expert consensus elicitation through the Nominal Group Technique. The results indicate that a concentrated set of EWS consistently signals heightened project vulnerability, particularly weak front-end project setup, limited capability and experience, ineffective leadership, cost-estimation weaknesses, and management’s limited ability to detect and respond proactively. Rather than claiming full statistical validation, the study offers triangulated, context-specific evidence that these signals are perceived as critical by practitioners operating under Vision 2030 delivery pressures and complex governance arrangements, thereby providing a practical basis for earlier detection of emerging risk and more timely intervention.

Beyond prioritizing EWS, the study links these signals to mitigation actions that emphasize improving tendering and cost-engineering practices, strengthening project definition and baseline control, enhancing leadership, communication, and reporting routines, and institutionalizing the use of risk registers and lessons learned. These mitigation measures are best interpreted as expert consensus–based options whose feasibility and effectiveness depend on project delivery method, contractual authority, approval pathways, resource availability, and HSE requirements. As such, the proposed framework should be applied with careful adaptation to specific projects and organizational contexts, and used to support, rather than replace, established governance and risk-management processes in Saudi construction projects.

The work on EWS can be pursued in several ways by future research. First, longitudinal studies and case studies are needed to determine the predictive validity of EWS and to quantify how well the timing of EWS emergence relates to the resulting costs, time, quality, and cancellations. Moreover, more studies that look at different factors and how they relate to EWS signals, as well as how these signals change in different purchasing and management situations, could be done. Third, we need to test the suggested ways to reduce risks (like by doing studies that compare different projects, testing interventions, or running simulations) to find out which methods work best and in what situations, considering their costs, safety, and contract details. Finally, research could look into how to put EWS frameworks into action by using data to spot warning signs, including using predictive analysis and digital technology (like automated monitoring with AI) to help make informed decisions in both Saudi and international construction.
