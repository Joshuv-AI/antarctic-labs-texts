TRMT6-directed m1A modification initiates lung squamous cell carcinoma via YTHDF3-stabilized cell cycle genes
Authors: Weihao Xue, Liqiang Zhu, Xiaolong Wei, Jinqi Sun, Weihao Lin, Shaomin Huang, Lianpin Wu, Donghong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N1-Methyladenosine (m1A) is a prevalent RNA modification that governs RNA metabolism, structure, stability, and translation. Yet its cancer-wide landscape and functional impact remain largely unexplored. Here, we delineate the m1A regulatory network in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). The dominant m1A writer of TRMT6 emerged as the most up-regulated regulator in LUSC through a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis; both TRMT6 expression and global m1A levels significantly distinguished LUSC from normal tissue, serving as potent diagnostic biomarkers. Functionally, TRMT6 installs m1A marks and facilitates their cellular export. Both the in vitro and in vivo experiments reveal that TRMT6 accelerates LUSC proliferation by orchestrating cell-cycle gene expression. Mechanistically, TRMT6 binds cell-cycle transcripts, most notably TOPBP1 and DSN1, promotes the formation of m1A, and stabilizes these mRNAs via the YTHDF3 reader pathway. A single, critical m1A site in each target mRNA is sufficient to boost TOPBP1 and DSN1 expression. Using dCasRx–TRMT6, we further show that site-specific m1A deposition on DSN1 mRNA is a potent strategy to modulate its expression and drive proliferation. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized m1A-dependent regulatory axis that underpins LUSC diagnosis and progression.

Introduction

A universal hallmark of human cancer is the systematic dysregulation of RNA and protein networks driven by aberrant transcriptomic and proteomic reprogramming. Recently, the emergence of the epitranscriptome, characterized by chemically altered RNA bases, has emerged as a central driver of this reprogramming1. To date, more than 170 distinct RNA modifications have been characterized, many of which modulate RNA stability, localization, or translation and are recurrently disrupted in malignancy2,3. The most abundant RNA modification is the N⁶-Methyladenosine (m6A), which decorates 0.4–0.7% of adenosines4. The m6A modification not only affects various facets of mRNA metabolism by being enriched in its 3′ UTRs and near stop codons5, but also controls the maturation and expression of microRNAs6. The second most methylated adenosine, N1-Methyladenosine (m1A)4, is typically found in abundant noncoding RNAs7–10, but is also observed around the start codon and in the 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNAs11,12. The location and function of m1A are key complements to m6A13.

M1A is a dynamic and reversible RNA methylation that is installed and removed by dedicated “writers” (TRMT6/TRMT61A, TRMT61B, TRMT10A/B/C), “erasers” (ALKBH1, ALKBH3)8,14–16. The “readers” of m1A, including YTHDF1-3 and YTHDC1, can directly bind to m1A17 and influence RNA functions by impacting self-assembly, translation, and degradation of downstream RNA14. Growing evidence suggests that m1A modification plays a significant role in tumorigenesis and progression. TRMT6 and TRMT61A, which form the m1A methyltransferase complex, are highly expressed in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues and are negatively correlated with HCC survival18. The elevated TRMT6/61A-dependent base methylation of tRNA-derived fragments regulates gene-silencing activity and the unfolded protein response in bladder cancer19. ALKBH3 is reported to be highly expressed in a number of human cancers, including liver cancer20, lung cancer21, and breast cancer22. Knockdown of ALKBH3 has been shown to increase m1A levels in tRNA and inhibit tumor growth, colonization, migration, and invasion23. In addition, loss of ALKBH3 results in an increase of m1A in the transcriptome of Hodgkin lymphoma cells and is linked to poor clinical outcomes24. Previous studies have primarily focused on individual m1A regulators or specific cancers, but specific cancer progression relies on the interplay between multiple m1A regulators. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of multiple m1A regulators could enhance our knowledge of oncogenesis and tumor progression across different types of cancer.

Our current study first revealed that m1A writers and readers are broadly up-regulated at both the mRNA and protein levels across cancers, with the most pronounced elevation in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). TRMT6, the dominant writer, and the concomitant global m1A increase serve as robust diagnostic markers for LUSC. Mechanistically, TRMT6 installs m1A marks on key cell-cycle transcripts, such as TOPBP1 and DSN1, and thereby is recognized by YTHDF3, which stabilizes their mRNAs and accelerates proliferation. Importantly, a site-specific engineered m1A deposition in TOPBP1 and DSN1 is sufficient to elevate gene expression and drive proliferation. These findings illuminate a new layer of RNA-epitranscriptomic control in tumorigenesis and nominate TRMT6–m1A –YTHDF3 as a therapeutic axis for LUSC.

Results

TRMT6-related m1A modification mainly elevates in LUSC

To understand the expression pattern of m1A regulators in pan-cancer, we accessed the UALCAN website for proteomics and found dynamic changes of m1A regulators at protein levels in all 11 types of cancer (Fig. 1A). TRMT6, the core m¹A writer, was markedly elevated in 10 of 11 cancers, with LUSC displaying the greatest fold increase. Concomitantly, all other writers (TRMT61A, TRMT61B, TRMT10A, TRMT10C, RRP8) and all four readers (YTHDF1–3, YTHDC1) were up-regulated in LUSC, whereas the principal eraser ALKBH3 remained unchanged. Of note, TRMT6 protein was significant higher in LUSC than in either unmatched or paired normal lung (Fig. 1B, C) and was uniformly up-regulated across every clinical stratum—stage, tumor size, gender, lymphovascular invasion and survival status (Fig. 1D; Table 1). IHC on our independent clinical cohort validated these findings (Fig. 1E–G; Table 1). Functionally, elevated TRMT6 in LUSC cells drove global m¹A release into the circulation, a phenomenon absent in LUAD patients (Fig. 2C). The increasing plasma m1A concentration could distinguished LUSC patients from the normal health individuals regardless of tumor stage, lymphovascular invasion, differentiation, and tumor size (Fig. 2D and E). TRMT6 drives m¹A formation and export in LUSC

As the catalytic core of the methyltransferase complex, TRMT6 installs m¹A on mRNAs bearing the GUUCRA motif25. We therefore investigated its role in LUSC. CRISPR dependency data (DepMap 25Q2, Chronos) revealed negative gene-effect scores for TRMT6 across all 20 LUSC lines, indicating that its loss impairs proliferation and/or viability26,27. TRMT6 mRNA expression were screened in the top ten most TRMT6-dependent lines (Fig. 3B). Consistent with TRMT6 expression level, m1A modification share the same trends in both mRNA and tRNA fractions among three LUSC cell lines of EBC-1, CALU-1 and NCI-H1869 cells (Fig. 3C). To understand the role of TRMT6 on m1A mRNA modification in LUSC, we transfected EBC-1 and CALU-1 cells (high level of TRMT6 and m1A) with two independent siRNAs against TRMT6 and achieved 75–87% knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Those depletion reduced global m¹A mRNA abundance by 30 ~ 40% in both LUSC cell lines (Fig. 3D, E). Furthermore, extracellular release of m1A methylation was observed in both cell medium, and silencing TRMT6 also reduced this release (Fig. 3F). Further more, as shown in Fig. 3G, we successfully over-expressed TRMT6 in NCI-H1869 by transfected with pcDNA3.1-C-(k)DYK-TRMT6 cDNA ORF. Conversely, ectopic TRMT6 expression in NCI-H1869 cells elevated both intracellular and extracellular m¹A levels by ~2-fold relative to controls (Fig. 3H, I). TRMT6 accelerates LUSC cell growth

To verify whether TRMT6 regulates cell growth in LUSC, we firstly performed in vitro experiment of CCK8 assay and found that TRMT6 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability in both cell lines at 48 hours post-transfection, and the effect persisted and became stronger over the next 48 hours (Fig. 4A, B). The colony formation for both cells also inhibited by TRMT6 knockdown (Fig. 4C, D). Similarly, flow cytometry assay showed that TRMT6-depleted EBC-1 cells accumulated in G0/G1 (66.26% vs 50.56% control) at the expense of S and G2/M phases (Fig. 4E). For in-vivo validation, we established a TRMT6 stable silenced EBC-1 cell line (shTRMY6) with sh-RNA targeting TRMT6 transfection (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Consistent with the in vitro results, xenografts with silenced TRMT6 expression exhibited slower tumor growth than control group, yielding a 54% reduction in tumor weight at 34 days post-injection (Fig. 4F–H). Conversely, TRMT6 over-expression promoted NCI-H1869 cell viability, colony formation and DNA synthesis by promoting G0/G1 → S phases transition (Fig. 4I–L). Additionally, proteomic profiling further linked elevated TRMT6 to activation of HIPPO, WNT, SWI/SNF, MYC, NRF2, and mTOR pathways (Fig. 4M). We then investigated potential downstream targets involved in m1A-regulated cancer cell growth in LUSC. An overlapping analysis revealed 366 m1A sites within 294 genes (Fig. 5A) with high confidence between the RMVar (293 T cell) and RMBase v3.0 (human_m1a_ associated SNP) databases28,29. Then, functional annotation revealed that these m1A-marked transcripts are enriched for processes governing cell growth and death, such as mitotic cell-cycle control, apoptosis regulation, nuclear division, and telomere maintenance (Fig. 5B). Strikingly, GO terms also highlighted “positive regulation of gene expression”. Therefore, we conducted a correlation analysis in LUSC and found that TRMT6 gene expression correlated positively with 60% of m¹A-modified genes and negatively with only 16% (Fig. 5D). Of note, both mRNA and protein expressions of ten m1A-modified cell cycle genes, showed a strong positive correlation with TRMT6 protein (Fig. 5E). It is worth noting that the ten-gene with high confidence was also confirmed in mouse tissue from the RMVar database. We then validated that the top three TRMT6 correlated cell cycle genes, including DSN130, TOPBP131 and KPNB132 protein were significantly increased in LUSC tissue compared to their normal controls by IHC staining assay (Fig. 5F). Similar strong positive correlations were also found between TRMT6 and three proteins expression (Fig. 5G–I). Collectively, these data position TRMT6 as a master epitranscriptomic coordinator of m1A-marked cell-cycle genes that fuels LUSC proliferation.

RMDisease v2.0 and RMVar2.0 consistently predict two m¹A sites in the TOPBP1 and DSN1 genes, respectively. We designed one m1A-RIP-qPCR primer for TOPBP1 gene and two primers for DSN1 gene according to their m1A locals (Fig. 6D). Custom m1A-RIP-qPCR analysis revealed robust m1A enrichment at both DSN1 sites, with the first site showing 4.12-fold higher signal (Fig. 6E) and was therefore used for all subsequent assays. Further RIP-qPCR analysis demonstrated that m1A, TRMT6, and YTHDF3 could co-occupy the m1A regions of TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNA (Fig. 6F, G), which consistent with previous epitranscriptome sequencing data (GSE90963 and GSE70487)33,34. Simultaneously, TRMT6 knockdown abolished the enrichment of TRMT6, m1A, and YTHDF3 on both genes. In contrast, another two m1A readers, YTHDF1 and YTHDF2 associated with m1A-mediated translation and decay23,25, did not bind to the two transcripts.

Subsequent treatment with actinomycin D, a gene transcriptional inhibitor, revealed time-dependent decay of mature TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNAs in EBC-1 cells. Interestingly, TRMT6 depletion accelerated their degradation after 2 h treatment (Fig. 6H, I). Reversely, TRMT6 over-expression significantly block actinomycin D induced mRNA degradation of TOPBP1 and DSN1 in NCI-H1869 cells (Fig. 6J). In addition, knockdown YTHDF3 could mimic the effect of TRMT6 loss and accelerate TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNA decay with actinomycin D treatment for 4 hours in EBC-1 cells (Fig. 6K). Collectively, these data establish that TRMT6 deposits m¹A on key cell-cycle transcripts, which are then stabilized through selective YTHDF3 binding, thereby sustaining LUSC proliferation.

Discussion

M1A occurs across diverse RNA species, shaping RNA metabolism and gene expression, yet its pathogenic roles in tumorigenesis remain elusive. Here we show that m1A “writers” and “readers” are globally up-regulated in multiple cancers, with LUSC displaying the most marked dysregulation. TRMT6—the principal m1A writer—emerges as an oncogenic driver and a highly accurate diagnostic biomarker. Mechanistically, TRMT6 installs m1A marks on cell-cycle transcripts, which are stabilized via the YTHDF3 reader and thereby accelerate LUSC proliferation. A single, site-specific m1A residue in TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNA is sufficient to enhance their expression and fuel malignant growth. Moreover, tumors actively export m1A-modified RNAs into the circulation; elevated plasma m1A levels robustly discriminate LUSC patients from controls (Fig. 9). Collectively, this study establishes the oncogenic function and clinical utility of m1A RNA modification in LUSC development.Fig. 9Proposed model of TRMT6-mediated m1A on cell cycle gene during LUSC development.Up-regulated TRMT6 installs m1A on key cell-cycle mRNAs (e.g., TOPBP1, DSN1), which are stabilized via YTHDF3 binding, thereby driving LUSC proliferation. Although current sequencing technologies and informatics assays allow us to understand the abnormally expressed m6A regulators across cancers35. Little is known about the biological function of m1A in multiple cancers. We now found that the writers and readers of m1A are markedly up-regulated, whereas erasers remain unchanged—indicating a global gain in m1A abundance and functional diversity in pan cancer. The most dramatic changes TRMT6, a key m1A writers, was in LUSC, which could be used as a good diagnostic marker with an AUC of more than 0.92, especially in early stages of cancer. Comparable TRMT6 signatures could also distinguish ovarian, colon, and oral squamous cell carcinomas from controls7,24,36.

The rapid development of epitranscriptomics is revealing novel functions and mechanisms of m1A modifications that contribute to both physiology and pathological processes. m1A and its regulators have been implicated in cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, migration, self-renewal of cancer stem cells (CSCs), and metabolism by either regulating the stability or translation of oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes7,18,22,24,37,38. One specific example is the TRMT6/TRMT61A complex sustains liver CSC self-renewal and hepatocellular carcinoma growth through m¹A-mediated tRNA modification and enhanced PPARδ translation18. In current study, we also observed that m1A mRNA-modified genes, with high confidence, are enriched in processes such as mitotic cell cycle, regulation of cell cycle, regulation of apoptosis, nuclear division, and telomere maintenance according to GO and KEGG pathway analyses. This finding was further supported by CRISPR knockout screens, which revealed that knockout of TRMT6 resulted in cell growth inhibition and/or death in the majority of LUSC cell lines. Additionally, knockdown of TRMT6 directly reduced cell growth in EBC-1 and CALU-1 cells in vitro and inhibited tumor growth in vivo. This effect may be related to multiple cell cycle pathways, including HIPPO, WNT, SWI-SNF, MYC, NRF2, and mTOR pathways.

Sequencing datasets studies have indicated that the TRMT6/TRMT61A complex could serve as a methyltransferase not only on cytoplasmic tRNA, but also on thousands of mRNAs in human cells11,16,39. Indeed, the majority of these m1A sites in mRNA are within the 5′ UTR and affect their stability, transcription, and translation. For example, ALKBH3-dependent m1A demethylation of Aurora A mRNA promotes its stability and translation, resulting in the inhibition of ciliogenesis14. m1A methylation on ATP5D mRNA at A71 of exon 1 can significantly increase the expression of ATP5D, cell glycolysis, and tumor growth during cancer progression23. In our study, through an integrated analysis of the databases RMVar and RMBase v3.0, we identified two cell cycle genes with high confidence of m1A modification sites, confirmed by RT-qPCR and RIP-qPCR analysis. In addition, m1A modification could stabilize mRNA expression of two cell cycle genes, most notably TOPBP1 and DSN1, mediated by YTHDF3 binding, which is involved in TRMT6-mediated cell growth of LUSC. We also identified a single, critical m1A site in TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNA is sufficient to boost mRNA expression, and cell proliferation. This observation reveals a correlation between m1A sites in the 5′ UTR and mRNA stability, consistent with previous reports11,16.

Four YTH-domain proteins, including YTHDF1-3, and YTHDC1, have been identified as readers of m1A RNA modifications. Among them, YTHDF1-3 directly bind to m¹A-modified mRNA transcripts and regulate their stability and translation40–42. Here, we demonstrate that YTHDF3 functions in an analogous manner toward m1A: it binds m1A-modified cell-cycle transcripts and extends their half-lives. This role appears to contrast with a prior report showing that YTHDF3 promotes the degradation of insulin like growth factor 1 receptor mRNA during trophoblast-associated pregnancy disorders39. Together, these observations suggest that the influence of YTHDF3 as an m¹A reader on mRNA fate and ultimately on cellular functions, may depend on cellular context and the specific transcripts it engages.

In conclusion, LUSC accounts for the major subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, with high recurrence, pronounced heterogeneity, and limited treatment options. Sequencing studies have elucidated the complex genetic landscape, multiple altered genes, and pathways involved in LUSC development. Therefore, in contrast to lung adenocarcinoma, few targetable driver mutations have been established so far, and targeted therapies for LUSC remain unsuccessful. Our data, for the first time, shows the biological function of m1A mRNA modification during LUSC development. The elevated m1A writers not only serve as an effective diagnostic biomarker for LUSC, but also promote m1A modification and cell growth by YTHDF3-stabilized mRNA expression of cell cycle genes. Precision editing with dCasRx–TRMT6 demonstrates that site-specific m¹A deposition is sufficient to activate these genes and drive proliferation. Collectively, our work provides new insights into the dynamic and regulatory role of m1A modification in cell cycle-related mRNA and the potential of TRMT6 as a drug target.

Methods

The clinical study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013), and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (No. 2024KY2024139) and the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (No. 2025-11-55). The written consent for tissue donation was obtained from each patient.

m1A regulators expression in pan-cancer and LUSC

The protein expression of m1A regulators was examined in the online pan-cancer proteome database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html). A comprehensive analysis of mRNA expression levels of m1A regulators and readers was conducted using the Sangerbox database (http://Sangerbox.com/Tool). The UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was utilized to analyze TRMT6 mRNA expression in different pathological stages, tumor lymph nodes, and metastasis of LUSC. Spearman correlation analysis between individual m1A regulators was performed.

Plasma sample collection from Lung cancer patients

It was a randomized, hospital-based, case-control study. The newly diagnosed non-small cell lung cancer patients, including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and LUSC, confirmed by histopathology, were recruited from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China. Patients with hypertension, diabetes, myocardial infarction, stroke, renal failure, aneurysm or other serious diseases were excluded. 84 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (NC) were used as the control group. Two milliliters of venous blood samples were collected in BD Vacutainer Plus K2 EDTA from each participant. After removing whole blood cells by centrifuge, the plasma was harvested and stored at -80°C until use.

This study protocol was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (KY2024139). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.

Paraffin-embedded samples collection from LUSC patients

The paraffin-embedded samples, including LUSC tissue and paired normal lung tissue were collected from 44 cases of LUSC patients. All subjects underwent biopsy at the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Shantou University Medical College from 2024 to 2025. Dr. XL Wei and WH Lin double confirmed the histopathological scrutiny of LUSC and corresponding control tissue by Hematoxylin and Eosin-staining. The determination of tumor stage and grade was defined according to the 8th edition of the TNM staging scheme for lung cancer. The study garnered ethical approval from the Ethics Review Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (2025-11-55). All subjects gave their written informed consent to participate in the study.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining assay

IHC staining was performed as detailed in our previous literature43. Briefly, the paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 5 mm sections. Then the slides were dewaxed in xylene, dehydrated in graded alcohols, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After treating with 3% H2O2 and 5% goat serum, all samples were incubated with antibodies targeting TRMT6 (16727-1-AP, Proteintech), DSN1 (17742-1-AP, Proteintech), TOPBP1 (23340-1-AP, Proteintech), KPNB1 (10077-1-AP, Proteintech), and m1A (ab208196, Abcam). After washing, the slides were incubated with secondary biotinylated antibody and DAB stain (GeneTech, USA). Images were captured using a Motic EasyScan microscope (Motic, USA) under ×400 magnification. Two trained pathologists (XL Wei and WH Lin) independently assessed the results of IHC staining by a Histoscore (H-score) system to evaluate protein expression.

Diagnosis analysis

The expression levels of m1A regulators and clinicopathological parameters in LUSC patients were obtained from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena browser (http://xena.ucsc.edu/) using our previously established method44. Diagnostic value was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculation of the area under the curve (AUC) value.

Cell culture and transfection

EBC-1 (NM-F23), CALU-1 (NM-F11), and NCI-H1869 (NM-F25) cells were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center and maintained per the supplier’s protocol in a humidified 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. All lines were confirmed mycoplasma-negative by the supplier and re-tested monthly. All cell lines were authenticated as negative for mycoplasma contamination by providers and maintained mycoplasma-free by monthly testing.

For loss-of-function studies, MedChemExpress pre-designed siRNA sets against human TRMT6 (HY-RS15094) and YTHDF3 (HY-RS15939) were transfected into EBC-1 or CALU-1 cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Invitrogen). For gain-of-function studies, the TRMT6 ORF cloned into pcDNA3.1-C-(k)DYK (pcDNA3.1-TRMT6; OHu02263, GenScript) was transfected into NCI-H1869 cells; the empty vector served as control. Knockdown and over-expression efficiency were verified by qRT-PCR and western blot.

For dCasRx effector plasmids, catalytically inactive dCasRx (EF1a-dCasRx-2A-EGFP, #109050) and active CasRx (EF1a-CasRx-2A-EGFP, #109049) were obtained from Addgene. TRMT6-ORF was inserted downstream of the dCasRx and CasRx coding sequence via NheI/BamHI cloning to generate dCasRx-TRMT6 and CasRx-TRMT6 fusions, respectively. Three gRNAs (Supplementary Table 1) targeting m1A sites in DSN1 exon 1 were designed (CHOPCHOP), synthesized, and cloned into the gRNA scaffold plasmid (#103854). Co-transfection of CasRx-TRMT6 or dCasRx-TRMT6 with individual gRNA constructs into NCI-H1869 cells was used to identify the most effective gRNA.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation assay was performed by cell counting kit-8 (CCK8), cell cloning, and cell cycle assay. For the CCK8 assay, 1000 cells were seeded into 96-well plates for 24 hours and then transfected with siRNA-TRMT6 or pcDNA-TRMT6, as well as their controls. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 20 μL of CCK8 (96992, Sigma) for 0, 24-, 48-, 72-, and 96-hour post-transfection. Cell viability was determined by reading the plates at an optical density of 450 nm (OD450). For the cell cloning assay, 300 treated cells were cultured in six-well plates for 1 ~ 2 weeks. Then, the cells were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Visible colonies (>50 cells) were counted and pictured. For the cell cycle assay, EBC-1 and NCI-H1869 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and knocked down and overexpressed TRMT6 for 24 h, respectively. The treated cells were washed, fixed, and stained with 0.5 mL propidium iodide (PI, Sigma, USA). Then, the distribution of DNA content was determined by the Flow cytometer BD FACSCalibur and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA in treated cells was isolated using the TRIzol Reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific). One µg of purified total RNA was reverse transcribed using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (4368814, Applied Biosystems). Subsequently, the fast SybrGreen PCR mastermix (4385612, Applied Biosystems) was used for mRNA quantification. All RT-qPCR experiments were conducted on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Inc.). The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2 △△Ct method. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR analyses are listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Cells with or without TRMT6 knockdown were lysed with RIPA buffer. The total proteins were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay, and 20 μg of them were subjected to an SDS-PAGE gel and electrotransferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. After incubation with primary antibodies against TRMT6 (16727-1-AP, Proteintech), DSN1 (17742-1-AP, Proteintech), TOPBP1 (23340-1-AP, Proteintech), β-ACTIN (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech), and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C, the membrane was then incubated with anti-mouse IgG-HRP secondary antibodies (1:5000, sc-2005, Santa Cruz) for 1 hour. The immunoreaction signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence HRP substrate. The relative protein levels were calculated using ImageJ by normalizing to GAPDH or β-ACTIN protein as described in our previous study26,45.

RNA stability

To study the mRNA stability mediated by m1A modification, 5 μg/ml of actinomycin D (A9415, Sigma) was used to inhibit the synthesis of new mRNA. Cells were collected at the indicated times, and total RNA was isolated for an RT-qPCR assay. The mRNA expression of TOPBP1 and DSN1 was calculated using the 2 △△Ct method.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RIP assay was conducted according to our previously described protocol with a slight modification43. Briefly, total RNA was extracted and purified from EBC-1 cells, and then incubated with DNase I (M0303S, NEB) treatment. RNA was chemically fragmented to 150 nucleotides using RNA fragmentation buffer (Ambion, AM8740). After setting aside 50 ng of RNA fragment as input, the remaining RNAs were used for RIP by incubating with m1A antibody (ab208196, Abcam), TRMT6 antibody (16727-1-AP, Proteintech), YTHDF1 antibody (ab220162, Abcam), YTHDF2 antibody (ab220163, Abcam), and YTHDF3 antibody (ab220161, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight, respectively. Subsequently, 40 μl of protein A/G beads were added to the mixture of RNA/antibody and incubated for an additional 4 hours at 4 °C. After washing the beads, the precipitated RNA was purified and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The enrichment of m1A-containing mRNA was then quantified using qRT-PCR (The primer sequences shown in Supplementary Table 1).

Global m1A measurement

We measured the global m1A levels using the m1A ELISA kit (EIA-m1A, RayBiotech) and the m1A dot blot kit (DB-m1A, RayBiotech) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the m1A ELISA assay, 50 µl of plasma or cellular medium was incubated with m1A-coated plates and competed with m1A antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. After washing, the complex was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG. The signals were then developed and measured using a multiwell plate reader. In the m1A dot blot assay, the poly(A)+ mRNA and tRNA fractions were purified from LUSC cell lines by the Magnetic Beads method from New England Biolabs (NEB, #S1550S) and BioDynami (40054S) as their protocol, respectively. The denatured 400 ng of mRNAs or tRNAs were spotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane and crosslinked with UV light. The membrane was blocked and then incubated with anti-m1A antibody and secondary antibody. The chemiluminescent signal was captured by an imaging system, with methylene blue (MB) staining serving as an internal control.

Luciferase reporter assays

The effect of m1A modification on gene activation was measured by the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase miRNA target expression system43. We used the PCR amplification and Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (E0554, NEB) to generate the wild type or mutant (A → C) m1A methylation site of TOPBP1 and DSN1 mRNA. Then, the serial DNA sequences were inserted into the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase vector (E1330, Promega) to construct the plasmids of pmirGLO-TOPBP1-m1A-WT, pmirGLO-TOPBP1-m1A-mutant, pmirGLO- DSN1-m1A-WT, and pmirGLO-DSN1-m1A-mutant. All constructs were co-transfected with Renilla luciferase (hRluc-neo) reporter into the EBC-1 cells for 24 hours. The firefly luciferase (luc2) and Rinella luciferase (R-luc) were measured by the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay system (E2920, Promega) per the manufacturer’s protocol. The luc2 activities were used as the primary reporter to monitor m1A regulation by Rluc activity normalization. All sequences of primers were in Supplementary Table 1.

Subcutaneous xenograft model

Female nude mice aged six weeks old were purchased from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center (SLAC) in China. After being raised in a pathogen-free animal laboratory for two weeks, the mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 EBC-1 cells stably infected with shRNA targeting TRMT6 (shTRMT6) and shCtrl. Both types of cells were suspended in 100 µl of serum-free medium and Matrigel (BD Biosciences) at a 1:1 ratio. Tumor sizes were recorded every 4 days starting on day 10 after the injection of tumor cells. After 34 days, the mice were sacrificed under anesthesia with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg/kg body weight), and the depth of anesthesia was monitored by loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex. The xenografts were removed to assess tumor size and weight. The tumor volumes were measured with a Vernier caliper every four days and calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institutional Authority for Laboratory Animals Care of the Wenzhou Institute, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences (WIUCAS25111305). All procedures were confirmed to the guidelines of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes or the NIH guidelines.

Statistical analysis

In the database assay, the expression of m1A regulators in pan-cancers was compared using the Wilcoxon test. Spearman’s coefficient was used to assess the correlation of gene expression. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were calculated for the diagnosis assay.

Statistical analyses for in vitro experiments were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.2. Continuous and normal distribution were taken into account, with an unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t-test used when appropriate. For comparing multiple samples, either a one-way or two-way ANOVA test was applied. Data is represented as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Pearson’s correlation was calculated for normally distributed samples, while Spearman’s correlation was used for non-normally distributed samples. A minimum of three technical replicates were included. In all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
