Dynamics of BCMA expression in patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma receiving BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy
Authors: Masooma Shifa Rana, Sebastian Fernandez-Pol, Alexandria Jensen, Vanna Hovanky, Arash Velayati, Jean S Oak, Oscar Silva, Erik Ames, Lori S Muffly, Bita Sahaf, Sally Arai, Vanessa E Kennedy, Sushma Bharadwaj, David J Iberri, Michaela Liedtke, Yasodha Natkunam, Parveen Shiraz, Wen-Kai Weng, Melody Smith, Matthew J Frank, Crystal L Mackall, Saurabh Dahiya, Lekha Mikkilineni, David B Miklos, Hitomi Hosoya, Surbhi Sidana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is limited systemic data on the dynamics of BCMA-target antigen expression with BCMA CAR-T at relapse. We analyzed 76 patients receiving standard-of-care BCMA-directed CAR-T who underwent real-time BCMA expression evaluation at baseline (n = 50), relapse (6), or both (20) using flow cytometry (FC) and/or immunohistochemistry (IHC). BCMA was universally expressed at baseline with significant heterogeneity in expression level. No concordance was seen between FC and IHC in categorizing high vs. low expression (Spearman: 0.07, Cohen kappa: 0). Plasma cell BCMA expression by FC correlated with clinical outcomes, whereas IHC did not. High BCMA expression by FC was associated with increased likelihood for VGPR/CR (p = 0.007) and longer time to progression (p = 0.005), including the ciltacabtagene autoleucel cohort (median: 23.0 vs. 7.7 months, p = 0.02). Relapsed patients retained BCMA expression by FC, though 29% (5/16) had BCMA loss by IHC, with 4/5 showing concurrent positive BCMA expression by FC. BCMA expression at relapse by FC was significantly lower than baseline (p = 0.04); downregulation (≥25% decrease) occurred in 50% (8/16) with paired samples. Higher BCMA expression by FC correlated with higher likelihood of deep, durable responses following BCMA-directed CAR-T. While BCMA loss is rare, decreased expression is common at relapse, with implications for sequencing BCMA-directed therapies. Subject terms: Myeloma, Prognosis, Immunotherapy

Introduction

B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, bispecific antibodies (BsAb) and antibody drug conjugates (ADC) have resulted in deep and durable responses in patients with relapsed and/or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) [1]. Currently, two BCMA-directed CAR-T therapies—ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel) and idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel)—are commercially available, and several additional constructs are in advanced clinical development [2–4]. Despite their efficacy, BCMA-directed therapies are not considered curative, and relapse is common, though one-third of patients have now been observed to have durable disease control at 5 years with cilta-cel [5–7]. Given that many patients relapse, there is a critical need to understand mechanisms of resistance and optimize therapeutic strategies. Multiple factors may contribute to relapse post-BCMA CAR-T therapy, including disease burden and biology, CAR-T cell exhaustion and limited persistence, and emergence of antigen-negative or antigen-low clonal variants [8–12].

The dynamics of plasma cell BCMA expression, its impact on efficacy, and extent of BCMA loss or downregulation at relapse remains poorly characterized. As BCMA-directed therapies rely on persistent expression of the target antigen, variations in BCMA expression levels or antigen loss may contribute to therapeutic resistance [9, 13]. Antigen density at baseline has been associated with efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy in other hematologic malignancies [14, 15]. Furthermore, both decreased antigen expression (antigen downregulation/antigen low relapse) and antigen loss are distinct and well-established mechanisms of resistance to CAR-T therapies, though antigen downregulation/antigen low relapse is harder to capture with need for more sophisticated techniques [14–19]. Antigen downregulation has been observed for CD19 and CD22 in patients with large cell lymphoma [15, 16, 19], and G protein–coupled receptor, class C, group 5, member D (GPRC5D) downregulation in MM [13, 20, 21]. Further, CD19 loss is a well-described in both B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [22] and large B-cell lymphoma [15, 23]. Although BCMA loss is considered rare, it has been reported in patients refractory to BCMA-targeted therapies [8, 24–26]. However, systematic data on BCMA loss or downregulation following BCMA CAR-T therapy remain limited.

A comprehensive understanding of BCMA expression dynamics is essential to predict clinical outcomes and optimize treatment strategies at relapse, including the potential use of subsequent BCMA-directed therapies. In this study, we aimed to evaluate BCMA expression on bone marrow plasma cells before standard-of-care (SOC) CAR-T therapy and at relapse using flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry (IHC), with the goal of assessing the prognostic significance and extent of BCMA downregulation or loss at the time of relapse.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center analysis conducted at Stanford University evaluating patients with RRMM who received SOC BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy between May 2021–November 2024. Patients received one of the two commercially available CAR-T cell products: cilta-cel or ide-cel. All patients were treated according to institutional protocols. The study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board.

Clinical data, including demographics, disease characteristics, laboratory values, and treatment history and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records (EMR). Treatment responses were assessed according to International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) response criteria [27]. Disease progression was determined by the primary treating physician based on IMWG criteria, including radiographic and pathologic findings. Triple-class–refractory disease was defined as disease refractory to at least one immunomodulatory agent, one proteasome inhibitor, and one anti-CD38 antibody. Penta-refractory disease was defined as disease refractory to lenalidomide, pomalidomide, bortezomib, carfilzomib, and daratumumab. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were defined by the American Society for Transplantation and Cell Therapy (ASTCT) criteria [28]. Toxicity management followed institutional guidelines. The date of last contact was recorded as the most recent healthcare interaction documented in the EMR, including clinic visits or communications outside of the institution’s health system.

BCMA expression testing

BCMA expression was evaluated on plasma cells (PC) from bone marrow (BM) biopsies performed prior to CAR-T infusion and at relapse. Expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry. For this analysis, BCMA expression data from both techniques were retrospectively reviewed centrally by a hematopathologist specializing in plasma cell pathology and antigen measurement techniques (SFP).

BCMA expression by immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on four-micron sections using standard automated methods, including instrument-based deparaffinization, peroxidase blocking, antigen retrieval, primary and secondary antibody incubation, detection, and counterstaining. Assays were performed on Roche Ventana Ultra instruments (Ventana Optiview detection; Tucson, AZ) or Leica Bond III instruments (Leica Polymer Refine Detection; Buffalo Grove, IL). Antibodies used included CD138 (clone B-A38, Ventana/Cell Marque, CC1 antigen retrieval) and BCMA (clone D-6, Santa Cruz Biotech, ER2 antigen retrieval). Per the vendor’s description, clone D-6 used for IHC was raised against amino acids 108-161, a region located in the cytoplasmic domain of BCMA. Our hematopathologist (SFP) evaluated all cases, scoring both staining intensity (0 = no signal, 1+ = weak, 2+ = moderate, 3+ = strong) and proportion of positive myeloma cells (0–100% in increments of 10%). An H-score was calculated by multiplying the intensity by proportion of positive cells. Cases with fewer than 50 cells were deemed unscoreable unless plasma cells formed aggregates without reactive background plasma cells, as confirmed by flow cytometry or in situ hybridization for kappa and lambda light chains.

BCMA expression by flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed with a clinically validated assay on a BD FACSLyric 12 color flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Antibodies included anti-Kappa light chain-FITC (TB28-2, BD Biosciences), anti-Lambda light chain-PE (1-155-2, BD Biosciences), anti-VS38c-PerCPCY5.5 (VS38c, Dako), anti-CD117-PE-CY7 (104D2, BD Biosciences), anti-CD138-APC (MI15, BD Biosciences), anti-CD38-APC-H7 (HB7, BD Biosciences), anti-CD56-R718 (B159, BD Biosciences), anti-BCMA-BV421 (19F2, Biolegend), anti-CD45-V500-C (2D1, BD Biosciences), anti-CD27-BV605 (L128, BD Biosciences), anti-CD81-BV711 (JS-81, BD Biosciences), and anti-CD19-BV786 (SJ25C1, BD Biosciences). Based on the vendor’s description, clone 19F2 is reportedly raised to the extracellular domain. Data were analyzed using FCS Express version 7 by the hematopathologist (SFP) to identify the abnormal PC populations. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) for BCMA was recorded for both background lymphocytes and abnormal PC population. Normalised MFI was calculated by subtracting lymphocyte MFI from abnormal PC

MFI. Results were evaluated for both normalized and unadjusted MFI for response rates (primary efficacy outcome) and given concordance, subsequent analyses used normalized MFI. Our flow cytometry protocol can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Not all patients were evaluated by both techniques. Initially, BCMA expression was assessed by IHC alone; flow cytometry was subsequently introduced on May 18, 2022. In the transition period, some patients were evaluated by both flow cytometry and IHC. Thereafter, flow cytometry became the primary method.

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified as having positive or negative BCMA status based on normalized MFI or H-Score (positive was defined as any detection by flow cytometry or H-score > 0). BCMA-positive patients were further categorized as low-positive or high-positive, based on whether their BCMA expression was below or above the median baseline value of normalized MFI or H-score, respectively. BCMA downregulation was defined as a ≥ 25% decrease in normalized MFI or H-Score at relapse compared to baseline. Given that there is lack of published data on change in BCMA expression by FC, we assigned an exploratory cut-off to quantitate directionality of BCMA expression. BCMA upregulation was defined as a ≥25% increase in normalized MFI or H-Score at relapse as compared to baseline. All other patients were considered as BCMA-stable.

Demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized and stratified by CAR-T therapy in only patients with evaluable BCMA expression by flow cytometry. Continuous variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U-tests, and categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact tests. Time-to-progression (TTP) and overall survival (OS) were the primary outcome measure. TTP was preferred over progression-free survival (PFS) as PFS includes non-relapse mortality (NRM), which may not be influenced by antigen expression. TTP was defined as the time from CAR-T infusion to disease progression or last follow-up. OS was defined as time from CAR-T infusion to death or last follow-up.

Statistical significance was determined at the P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1).

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, our study cohort included 76 patients who underwent SOC BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy for RRMM and had data on BCMA expression at baseline and/or relapse. Overall, 110 patients underwent SOC BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy during this time-period. Patients with no baseline biopsy performed (n = 2), no PC burden in the bone marrow (n = 13) or where BCMA expression was not evaluated (n = 25) were excluded from analysis. Baseline, i.e., pre-CAR-T BCMA expression, data were available in 70 patients, at relapse in 26 patients (of 52 patients who relapsed at last follow-up), and at both timepoints in 20 patients. Therefore, the final study cohort included patients with either baseline or relapse BCMA expression (n = 76). The median follow-up duration for the study cohort was 16.4 months.

Baseline BCMA expression

Seventy patients had available BCMA expression at baseline, with 43 (61%) assessed using flow cytometry alone, 13 (19%) using IHC alone, and 14 (20%) using both methods. The baseline median bone marrow PC percentage was: 15% (range: 1–95%). All patients with evaluable data demonstrated positive BCMA expression at baseline by both flow cytometry and/or IHC.

The median normalized MFI on flow cytometry was 1,512 (range: 40–9299), while the median H-score was 200 (range: 10–300). The mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation are provided in Supplementary Table 1. BCMA expression was distinguished into high or low using the median baseline values for flow cytometry and IHC, respectively. Amongst patients with low baseline FC BCMA expression, 7 (25%) had high disease burden (plasma cells ≥50% in the bone marrow), while for those with a high baseline FC BCMA expression, 3 (10%) had a high disease burden; high disease burden was not found to be significantly different by baseline FC BMCA expression via Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.2). A multivariable linear regression was fit to test for potential associations between baseline BCMA expression by flow and important prognostic clinical factors, including baseline ferritin, high-risk cytogenetics, extramedullary disease, and high disease burden (Supplementary Table 2). No significant associations were found between these clinical factors and baseline normalized MFI (all p > 0.1).

Baseline BCMA expression measured by flow cytometry was not significantly different between patients with or without prior BCMA-directed therapy, though there were only four patients in FC group who had prior BCMA exposure (median normalized MFI: 1512 vs 1518; p = 0.6).

BCMA expression at relapse

Forty-four of 52 relapsed patients underwent bone marrow biopsies at point of disease progression; 18 either did not have sufficient PCs to evaluate BCMA expression or did not have IHC or flow cytometry performed (Fig. 1). Amongst the remaining 26 patients, BCMA expression was evaluated by flow cytometry alone in 10 patients, IHC alone in 6 patients and both flow cytometry and IHC in 10 patients. The median PC percentage in BCMA expression evaluable patients at relapse was 13% (range: 1–95%). The median BCMA expression at relapse was as measured by normalized MFI on flow cytometry was 1027 (range: 192 – 4418) and by H-score on IHC was 100 (range: 0–300). All patients with sufficient PCs who underwent flow cytometry had intact BCMA expression (Fig. 2).

Amongst these five patients, external control tissue slide was normal, indicating no stain failure. Four of these five patients also had BCMA expression evaluated by flow cytometry. Using cut-offs from baseline evaluation of high vs low BCMA expression, 15% had high BCMA expression by flow cytometry and 25% had high expression by IHC. When considering either of the methods, 27% of patients had high BCMA expression at relapse.

Of the 26 patients who relapsed, 6 (23%) had a high disease burden at baseline. Four of these patients with high disease burden had available FC BCMA expression and all were classified as having low FC BMCA expression at relapse.

Concordance between flow cytometry and IHC

Among patients with both flow cytometry and IHC data available at baseline (n = 14), discrepancies in the level of BCMA expression between the two methodologies were observed in 50% (n = 7) (Fig. 3). Most discrepancies arose in cases where the H-score on IHC was categorized as high, whereas normalized MFI on flow cytometry was classified as low (n = 5). Median PC percentage positive for BCMA by flow cytometry was 98% for concordant patients and 89% for discordant patients (p = 0.4). The Spearman correlation coefficient for the two modalities was 0.007, indicating a lack of correlation between the methodologies. Furthermore, Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, calculated using binary classification based on median values, was 0.0, suggesting no agreement between the two methods.

We also evaluated agreement for all paired IHC and FC BCMA evaluations combined baseline and relapse samples and observed. Agreement in BCMA expression by IHC and flow cytometry was higher at relapse (n = 10 patients) as compared to baseline, with only one patient having differing IHC and flow categorizations (Fig. 3B, D).

We also evaluated agreement for all paired IHC and FC BCMA evaluations combined baseline and relapse samples and observed agreement in 67% of all paired samples, with a near equal distribution in disagreeing samples between those as “FC high, IHC low” and “FC low, IHC high.” However, due to the small sample size of patients with both FC and IHC BCMA expression available, there is not sufficient power to formally conclude the discordance of these two methods.

Change in BCMA expression at relapse relative to baseline

In the entire cohort of patients who relapsed (n = 26; Fig. 1), BCMA expression was overall reduced at the time of relapse by both flow cytometry (n = 20) and IHC (n = 16). Figure 2A shows change in BCMA expression by flow cytometry in patients with paired samples at baseline and relapse (n = 16 with both baseline and relapse BCMA; n = 4 with only relapse BCMA). Figure 2B shows the overall distribution of FC BCMA expression at relapse (n = 20) vs baseline (n = 57), with a Wilcoxon test indicating an overall significant difference (p = 0.02). When accounting for the paired samples, there is not a significant difference in FC BCMA from baseline to relapse (paired Wilcoxon; p = 0.3).

Amongst the 16 patients with paired flow cytometry data, 8 (50%) had downregulation (≥25% decrease) (Supplementary Table 3). TTP from CAR-T was not associated with degree of change in BCMA expression at relapse (log-rank test; p = 0.2), suggesting that duration of response to CAR-T is not associated with degree of change in of BCMA antigen from baseline to relapse. Using a cut-off of progression within 6 months, there was no difference in FC BCMA expression at relapse (p = 0.5) between early (n = 23) vs late progressors (n = 16).

Due to the limited sample size, statistical analysis was not feasible for H-score comparisons for relapse vs baseline, though three out of five patients showed a reduction in BCMA expression at relapse and two had complete loss (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Immune adverse events

The proportion of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and Immune Effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS) toxicities was similar when compared between patients with high vs. low baseline BCMA expression by FC. Within the 29 high baseline FC BCMA patients, 28 (97%) developed CRS and 9 (31%) developed ICANS. For the 28 low baseline FC BCMA patients, 23 (61%) developed CRS and 7 (25%) developed ICANS. One patient with low baseline FC BCMA died before toxicities could be assessed. Neither the rate of CRS (p = 0.1) nor ICANS (p = 0.8) was statistically significant. No patients with baseline FC BCMA expression developed ≥ grade 3 CRS, while three patients developed ≥grade 3 ICANS, all from the low baseline FC BCMA group.

Response rate

Overall response rate, VGPR, and CR rates for patients with baseline FC BCMA expression were as follows: 86%, 8%, and 66%, respectively. Patients with CR or VGPR following CAR-T cell therapy had significantly higher baseline BCMA expression by flow cytometry (CR: p = 0.01; CR or VGPR: p = 0.002) than patients achieving PR or less as shown in Fig. 4A–C.

Time to progression and overall survival

After a median follow-up of 16.4 months of the 76 patients who had baseline BCMA expression analyzed by either IHC or flow cytometry, 39 patients (51%) experienced disease progression and 29 patients (38%) died, including 7 due to non-relapse mortality (9% of cohort).

Patients with high BCMA expression measured by flow cytometry (and expressed as normalized MFI) had longer time to progression (median not reached vs 7.7 months, p = 0.005) (Fig. 5A). There was a trend towards better overall survival (median not reached vs 25.7 months, p = 0.06) in the patients with high vs low FC BCMA expression as shown in Fig. 5B.

We then evaluated survival outcomes in ide-cel (n = 15) and cilta-cel (n = 42) cohorts separately as significant differences have been noted in survival with the two constructs, and noted consistent results based on baseline BCMA expression by flow cytometry. Median follow-up for patients receiving ide-cel was 25.5 months (IQR: 18.0–28.5 months) and for those receiving cilta-cel was 14.5 months (IQR: 6.2–22.8 months). In the cilta-cel cohort, patients with high baseline BCMA expression by flow cytometry had significantly longer TTP (median: 23.0 vs 7.7 months, p = 0.02) and numerically better OS (median not reached vs 17.7 months, p = 0.1). Due to the limited sample size in the ide-cel group (n = 15), definitive conclusions regarding the association of BCMA expression with efficacy and survival cannot be drawn, though we observed numerically longer TTP and OS amongst patients with higher baseline BCMA expression (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest study evaluating dynamics of BCMA expression at baseline and relapse in contiguous patients undergoing SOC BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy for RRMM and its association with clinical outcomes. BCMA expression was evaluated by IHC and/or flow cytometry, with flow cytometry correlating with clinical outcomes. BCMA expression was universal at baseline—though there was significant heterogeneity, consistent with prior data [29–34]. We observed that higher BCMA antigen expression by flow cytometry is associated with deeper response and longer TTP following CAR-T therapy. Our findings suggest that BCMA downregulation/antigen low relapse is more common than previously described, though complete loss of BCMA expression is exceedingly rare by FC. Loss of BCMA expression was observed in 29% of patients with IHC.

We observed a lack of correlation between flow cytometry and IHC in determining BCMA expression (high vs low). The inability to find a cutoff for stratifying outcomes by IHC (due to a low sample size and the semi quantitative nature of the H-score) and the lack of correlation between IHC and FC can result from several factors. Clone D-6 in IHC targets a cytoplasmic domain of BCMA, while clone 19F2 in FC targets an extracellular epitope. Since the FC antibody is theoretically closer to the extracellular CAR-T cell BCMA epitope, FC may better reflect changes affecting that epitope. IHC and FC also differ in assay properties. FC has a greater dynamic and linear range, allowing it to more accurately detect subtle changes in BCMA expression compared to IHC. Greater sensitivity of FC is consistent with prior reports [30, 35].

High BCMA antigen density, measured semi-quantitatively by FC, was significantly associated with higher likelihood of deep response and TTP following CAR-T, particularly cilta-cel. The ide-cel cohort was small, and no conclusions can be drawn for that cohort from our data. Low target antigen density may limit CAR-T efficacy, as seen in other CAR-T therapies such as CD19 and CD22-directed CAR-T therapy [15, 23]. Perica et al., using in vitro modeling, found that low BCMA expression impaired CAR-T-mediated tumor lysis and cytokine production, suggesting that reduced expression leads to treatment resistance [31]. However, clinical trial data have not demonstrated correlation of BCMA expression with clinical outcomes, though these studies have typically reported BCMA expression as a percentage and not quantitative antigen density or MFI, while in our study we report antigen density using normalized MFI. In the clinical trial of LCARB38M (subsequently cilta-cel) and in CARTITUDE-1, baseline BCMA expression by flow cytometry did not correlate with outcomes, though it was measured as a percentage [36, 37]. In the initial bb2121 (ide-cel) phase 1 trial, BCMA expression was evaluated by IHC, and there was no association with response [38].

It is critical to understand the dynamics of BCMA expression at relapse from BCMA-directed therapy to guide treatment sequencing. We and others have reported that prior BCMA-directed therapy exposure results in lower efficacy with subsequent BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy and BsAb [39–42]. This may be due to BCMA loss or downregulation/antigen low relapse, along with T cell exhaustion. Our institutional data also clearly describe that antigen loss and antigen downregulation/antigen low relapse are clear mechanisms of resistance with CD19 and CD22 directed CAR-T therapy [14–17, 19]. Previous in-vitro studies form our institution suggest that T cell exhaustion is associated with a higher antigen density threshold for CAR-T cells, and these two factors may be related. Less exhausted CAR-T cells showed greater antitumor activity in tumors with low antigen density compared with more exhausted CAR- T cells [43]. Additionally, emerging data suggests that antigen mutations are common at relapse and may play a role in decreasing surface antigen density or in some cases measured antigen density if antigenic mutations are in the same area as the binding antibody of the assay. This was elegantly shown by Lee et al., where BCMA was detected at low levels by a polyclonal antibody after loss from BCMA bispecific antibody, but not detected by the monoclonal anti-BCMA antibody [13]. Since the flow cytometry antibody is closer to the CAR-T epitope, mutations may be more common in the epitope targeted by CAR-T and may potentially reduce the binding of the antibody used in FC. In fact, this may be the reason why, in some cases, we noted discrepancies where the BCMA expression was low by FC, but high by IHC, which uses a different antibody. Future investigation is needed to evaluate the relationship between mutations and BCMA expression.

Clinical data on correlates of response with BCMA-targeted CAR-T is limited. We observed a significant decrease in BCMA expression at the time of relapse after BCMA-directed CAR-T compared with baseline. BCMA downregulation (defined as >25% decrease) was observed in 50% (8/16) of paired samples on flow cytometry. This supports the hypothesis that BCMA downregulation may play a role in sub-optimal response to therapy in patients with prior exposure to BCMA-directed therapy. It is possible that BCMA expression is regained after a period, as seen with CD38 expression after daratumumab [44], and should be evaluated in future. If confirmed, this may explain why an interval of least 6 months between BCMA-directed therapies may improve outcomes with subsequent BCMA-directed CAR-T therapy [40]. BCMA expression by IHC was also lower at relapse vs baseline, with loss seen in 29% (5/16) patients, including one patient with genomic TNFRSF17 loss. Four of the five patients with BCMA loss on IHC had concurrent flow cytometry that demonstrated retained BCMA expression, again highlighting that flow cytometry is a more sensitive technique. BCMA expression by flow cytometry was not evaluated in the one patient with confirmed genomic loss, as that was prior to flow cytometry being developed in our laboratory. In the phase II KarMMa trial investigating ide-cel, BCMA loss at relapse was identified in 3/71 patients (4%) through IHC, with one patient exhibiting biallelic TNFRSF17 loss [24, 26].

There have been case reports of genomic loss of BCMA as the mechanism of resistance after BCMA CAR-T [25, 26, 45]. Our group has recently shown that using cell-free circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), 17% (4/23) patients undergoing CAR-T had an emergent copy number loss of BCMA/TNFRSF17 at the time of relapse from CAR-T, though we did not observe any SNVs or frameshift mutations in BCMA [46]. It is important to note that the technique used in the study was genomic-level evaluation, and we did not compare it with functional BCMA expression in bone marrow. Moreover, bone marrow represents a single point of disease assessment, and ctDNA analysis may capture a more global state of genomic change. Nevertheless, while ctDNA is a sophisticated, emerging technique, bone marrow evaluation remains the standard-of-care and flow cytometry-based evaluation of target expression can be widely adopted in nearly all clinical laboratories—these two techniques may provide complimentary data. Lee et al. recently reported that 8 of 16 (50%) patients progressing after BCMA-directed CAR-T (n = 2) and BsAb (n = 14) had genomic events at the TNFRSF17 locus at time of progression. One patient progressing after CAR-T had TNFRSF17 loss in most tumor clones, but TNFRSF17 was preserved in 11% of plasma cells and retained BCMA expression, suggesting that decrease of BCMA expression may represent a mix of clones: some that retain BCMA expression and others that no longer express due to TNFRSF17 mutation/deletion [13]. Firestone et al. observed that three patients previously treated with BCMA-directed ADCs or CAR-T therapy exhibited BCMA loss by IHC, with one case showing biallelic loss of TNFRSF17. Notably, all three patients were refractory to subsequent teclistamab therapy [8]. Maura et al. have described loss of TNFRSF17 in 5/96 (5%) patients, three of which were treated with prior BCMA therapy and were refractory to subsequent BCMA-directed CAR-T [47]. Strategies to mitigate genomic loss may include using dual-targeted CAR-Ts and BsAb [48–53]. Other strategies are in development for increasing BCMA surface expression, including the use of gamma secretase inhibitors [54–57] and all-trans retinoic acid [58].

While further research is needed, BCMA expression has potential for guiding clinical decision-making at relapse. Measuring BCMA expression remains challenging particularly in distinguishing biologically relevant downregulation from technical variability. Advanced techniques, such as whole-genome sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing, can provide deeper insights into the biological mechanisms underlying BCMA loss, providing complementary data to protein expression and enabling more precise monitoring and personalized treatment strategies. Lastly, longitudinal studies with larger patient cohorts are essential to better characterize the temporal evolution of BCMA expression and its role in treatment resistance.

Strengths of our study include a large patient cohort of contiguous patients treated with standard-of-care CAR-T. BCMA expression was evaluated by flow cytometry and/or IHC in real-time, mitigating any issues with sample viability especially for flow cytometry. Limitations include lack of data on both IHC and flow cytometry in all patients, as we transitioned to flow cytometry only, given better sensitivity and lack of genomic data to evaluate mutations and/or loss of TNFRSF17. The cut-off for 25% decrease as downregulation was arbitrarily selected given lack of prior published data to define optimal cut-offs, and the limited sample size of our cohort to allow a more robust statistical approach to determine the cut-offs. Nevertheless, this study demonstrates a proof of concept that antigen density is critical for therapeutic benefit from antigen-targeted therapies like CAR-T cell therapy. Our group has previously shown that quantitative flow cytometry is more discriminatory for clinical outcomes with CD19 and CD22 directed CAR-T than semi-quantitative measures evaluated in this study. Future work building upon these findings using flow cytometry more specifically designed to precisely and quantitatively measure antigen density (e.g., using quantitative standards and fluorescence minus one control) is likely to provide more insights into the actual level of expression associated with resistance.

In summary, we observed that higher BCMA expression, measured by semi-quantitative antigen density via fluorescence intensity on flow cytometry correlated with higher likelihood of deep and durable responses following CAR-T therapy, and while BCMA loss is rare at the time of relapse, decreased expression is common, which may have implications for sequencing of BCMA-directed therapies. Future work should focus on more quantitative methods to measure antigen density and change over time and also evaluate the dynamics of BCMA expression with other BCMA-directed therapies.
