Platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio predicts postoperative overall survival in hepatocellular carcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiong Tang, Lu-Yun Zhang, Jia-Peng Liao, Xin Jiang, Yao-Ming Zhang, Gao-Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic value of the Platelet-to-High-Density Lipoprotein Ratio (PHR) in HCC patients who underwent hepatectomy remains unclear. This study aims to explore the clinical association between PHR and HCC, as well as the prognostic value of PHR in HCC patients after hepatectomy.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study included 446 HCC patients who underwent curative-intent hepatectomy. The association between PHR and overall survival (OS) was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression methods. A prognostic nomogram incorporating PHR was developed and validated internally.ResultsDifferent levels of PHR show a significant correlation with the clinicopathological characteristics of HCC. Preoperative low PHR is associated with better overall survival (OS) (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.43–0.91, P = 0.013) in post-hepatectomy HCC patients, and the nomogram incorporating PHR demonstrates a modest net benefit in predicting OS.ConclusionThe PHR could independently predict HCC OS after hepatectomy. The nomogram incorporating the PHR may aid in the prognostic management of HCC.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Hepatectomy, PHR, Survival, Nomogram

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the six most common malignant tumors worldwide and ranks fourth in mortality among all cancers globally [1]. In recent years, significant progress has been achieved in surgical and systemic treatment approaches, as well as multidisciplinary treatment (MDT); however, surgical resection remains the preferred curative intervention. Nevertheless, the 5-year recurrence and metastasis rates remain relatively high following such curative treatment [2]. For the whole-course management of liver cancer, multiple treatment modalities and multidisciplinary, multi-step management strategies are currently available [3, 4]. Thus, it is critical to identify effective predictive biomarkers to assess HCC patient prognosis and develop individualized treatment plans.

Traditional metabolic indicators have been shown to be associated with various cancers [5–7].The diagnostic and prognostic challenges in oncology often stem from insufficient standardization of biomarkers. Notably, findings from Filippini et al. in head and neck cancer underscore the cross-disciplinary need for reliable, integrative biomarkers to improve clinical stratification [8]. In HCC, a similarly robust and composite marker is needed. Systemic inflammatory and metabolic indices, such as the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), Advanced Lung Cancer Inflammation Index (ALI), and Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), have been validated as prognostic markers in HCC after hepatectomy [9–13]. However, most of these indices primarily reflect systemic inflammation or immune status. Beyond inflammation, both platelets and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) play crucial roles in tumor biology. Platelets contribute to angiogenesis and immune evasion [14, 15], while HDL-C exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor effects [16, 17]. In contrast, the Platelet-to-HDL-C Ratio (PHR) is a novel composite inflammatory-metabolic marker that integrates pathways related to both coagulation (platelets) and lipid metabolism/inflammation (HDL-C). This dual-pathway nature may provide incremental prognostic value by capturing a broader spectrum of tumor microenvironment dysregulation.Researchers have investigated the predictive value of PHR in gastrointestinal cancers and confirmed that elevated PHR is independently associated with increased risk of gastrointestinal cancers [18]. However, its prognostic value specifically in HCC patients undergoing curative-intent hepatectomy remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between preoperative PHR and clinicopathological features in HCC, and to evaluate its prognostic value for overall survival following hepatectomy.

Methods

Study population

Clinical data from 1,389 liver cancer patients who underwent hepatectomy at Meizhou People’s Hospital between May 2011 and February 2023 were extracted and analyzed. Per the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 446 adult Han Chinese patients with HCC were enrolled in the study cohort. All surgical procedures were performed with curative intent (R0 or R1 resection). Patients with gross residual disease (R2 resection) or those who received palliative treatments were excluded. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Underwent hepatectomy as initial treatment; (2) Postoperative pathological results confirmed HCC; (3) No prior history of anticancer treatment, including radiotherapy, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), or targeted therapy; (4) Complete availability of all clinicopathological data. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Incomplete clinicopathological data; (2) Overall Survival (OS) of less than 90 days; (3) Pathological diagnosis of non-HCC liver cancer or a history of other malignant tumors; (4) Received palliative treatment(which would include R2 resections). The process of patient selection was visualized in Fig. 1 through a flowchart.

The flow chart of the study

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and approved by the Ethics and Indications Committee. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All personal identifiers—including names and hospital admission numbers—were removed prior to analysis to ensure patient confidentiality. Data were securely stored and managed per institutional guidelines.

Data collection

All data were retrieved from the Cancer Database of Meizhou People’s Hospital. Data collection was completed preoperatively and included: (1) Demographic data: age, gender, weight, height, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, etc.); (2) Biochemical data: preoperative routine tumor markers (alpha-fetoprotein [AFP], carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA], carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 [CA19-9]), complete blood count (CBC), liver function, renal function, blood glucose, blood lipids, myocardial enzymes, inflammatory markers, and immunological markers; (3) Tumor-specific data: tumor number, size, grade, capsular status, vascular invasion, microvascular invasion (MVI), Edmondson-Steiner grade, and TNM stage; (4) Survival data: Overall Survival (OS). Patients were staged according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM Classification System. PHR was calculated using the following formula: PHR = Platelet count (×10⁹/L)/HDL-C level (mg/dL)18. The unit mg/dL for HDL-C was used as it is the standard reporting convention in our clinical laboratory and the referenced literature.

Follow-up and survival

Patient follow-up was conducted from admission until death or the follow-up cutoff date (July 7, 2023). Follow-up assessments included physical examination, evaluation of liver function and complete blood count (CBC), measurement of AFP and HBV DNA levels, and at least one imaging modality (e.g., abdominal ultrasound, contrast-enhanced computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging). The primary outcome was Overall Survival (OS). OS was defined as the time from the date of primary tumor resection to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up. Given the disease setting, a secondary endpoint of Cancer-Associated Death (CAD) was also defined, encompassing death from HCC progression or liver-related complications. In this cohort, all observed deaths met this criterion. Management upon recurrence (e.g., transarterial chemoembolization [TACE], systemic therapy, or re-resection) was based on multidisciplinary team decisions and contemporary guidelines. Detailed data on specific post-recurrence treatments were not systematically analyzed in this retrospective study due to heterogeneous clinical practices over the extended study period.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R software (Version 4.3.1; http://www.r-project.org). The R package “survminer” (v. 0.5.0) was used to determine the optimal cutoff value for PHR in this cohort. In the analysis: Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR); Categorical variables were reported as frequency (n) and percentage (%). Statistical comparisons of quantitative variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test or independent samples t-test, while categorical variables were assessed using the Pearson χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for survival analysis, and group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The R package “survival” (v. 4.5.1) was used to perform analyses and generate Kaplan-Meier curves for survival rates. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed using a forward stepwise procedure to identify independent prognostic factors. A nomogram was constructed using variables selected from the multivariate Cox regression model to predict OS [19]. A calibration plot was used to evaluate the calibration of the nomogram. The C-index was used as a measure of model discrimination to assess the performance of the PHR-based nomogram for OS, and nomogram calibration was evaluated using the bootstrap method with 1,000 resampling iterations. The TNM model, PHR model, and combined model were compared using time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), correlation index, and decision curve analysis (DCA) [20].

Results

PHR and clinicopathological baseline data

A total of 446 patients were enrolled in this study, with ages ranging from 23 to 85 years (median age: 57 years), including 396 males and 50 females. Among them, 326 cases (73.10%) were at stage Ⅰ+Ⅱ, and 120 cases (26.90%) were at stage Ⅲ+Ⅳ. For this cohort, the mean follow-up duration was 44.17 ± 29.59 months, with a median follow-up duration of 29.65 months (range: 3.02–144.88 months). The 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year overall survival (OS) rates were 92.6%, 78.8%, 70.2%, and 61.9%, respectively. The mean PHR was 155.18 ± 107.10. The optimal cutoff value of PHR for this cohort, calculated using the R package “survminer”, was 179.82. Based on this cutoff value, patients were divided into the high PHR group (135 cases) and low PHR group (311 cases). Associations between PHR and clinicopathological baseline data of the two groups are summarized in Table 1. The results showed statistically significant differences in multiple clinical characteristics between the two groups (P < 0.05), including preoperative HBsAg, albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (Tbil), liver cirrhosis, portal hypertension, tumor size, TNM stage, and intraoperative blood loss. Specifically, high PHR was associated with low ALB, larger tumors, advanced TNM stage, and increased intraoperative blood loss; low PHR was associated with high Tbil, positive HBsAg, liver cirrhosis, and portal hypertension. However, other known adverse prognostic factors such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), tumor number, capsule integrity, histological grade, and vascular invasion showed no significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses

The optimal cutoff value of advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) for this cohort, calculated using the R package “survminer”, was 27.89. All variables were subjected to univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, and a forest plot was generated (Fig. 2). Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that high PHR, low ALI, high AFP, advanced TNM stage, high Child-Pugh score, tumor size ≥ 5 cm, multiple tumors, incomplete capsule, vascular invasion, extensive resection, and open surgery were associated with poor OS. After incorporating these prognostic factors into multivariate Cox regression analysis, the results indicated that low PHR (HR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.43–0.91, P = 0.013) and single tumor (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.36–0.78, P = 0.001) were independent prognostic factors for favorable OS; whereas advanced TNM stage (HR: 3.86, 95% CI: 2.64–5.64, P < 0.001) and low ALI (HR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.28–2.71, P = 0.001) were independent prognostic factors for poor OS. Further subgroup analysis of PHR (Fig. 3) showed that low PHR was a significant protective factor for OS in most subgroups (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.45–0.75, P < 0.001), with no significant interaction between groups (P for interaction > 0.05), which further confirmed that low PHR was significantly associated with better OS.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic impact of PHR and ALI on OS in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. As shown in Fig. 4, patients with low PHR had significantly better OS than those with high PHR (HR: 0.466, 95% CI: 0.313–0.695, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A); patients with high ALI had significantly better OS than those with low ALI (HR = 2.231, 95% CI: 1.481–3.361, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4D). Stratified analysis by TNM stage showed that there was no significant difference in OS between different PHR levels in patients with TNM stage Ⅰ+Ⅱ (P = 0.235) (Fig. 4B); whereas in patients with TNM stage Ⅲ+Ⅳ, those with low PHR had significantly better OS than those with high PHR (P = 0.003) (Fig. 4C). For ALI, patients with high ALI had significantly better OS than those with low ALI in both TNM stage Ⅰ+Ⅱ and Ⅲ+Ⅳ (P = 0.012 and P = 0.008, respectively) (Fig. 4E, F). Stratified analysis by ALI level showed that patients with low PHR had better OS than those with high PHR across different ALI levels (P = 0.043 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 4G, H). Finally, analysis based on the combination of PHR and ALI in TNM stage Ⅰ+Ⅱ and Ⅲ+Ⅳ showed that the OS of the low PHR-high ALI group was better than that of the high PHR-low ALI group (Fig. 4I, J).

Nomogram and decision curve analysis (DCA)

A nomogram model was constructed by integrating four independent prognostic factors: tumor number, TNM stage, ALI, and PHR (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, a dynamic nomogram was developed for convenient query of specific scores and prognosis (URL: https://tangxiong.shinyapps.io/DynNomapp/). The calibration plot showed high consistency between the predicted probability and actual probability of the nomogram model, with the best performance in predicting 1-year OS (Fig. 5B). The ROC curves for predicting 1-year, 3-year, 5-year, and 7-year OS using the nomogram model are shown in Fig. 5C, indicating good discriminative ability. Comparative analysis of different models showed that the C-indices of the nomogram, TNM stage, ALI, PHR, and tumor number models were 0.757, 0.701, 0.603, 0.592, and 0.576, respectively; the AUCs for 1-year OS were 0.788, 0.747, 0.644, 0.554, and 0.552, respectively; the AUCs for 3-year OS were 0.800, 0.745, 0.617, 0.615, and 0.581, respectively; the AUCs for 5-year OS were 0.794, 0.736, 0.607, 0.607, and 0.585, respectively; and the AUCs for 7-year OS were 0.725, 0.690, 0.579, 0.577, and 0.545, respectively. Time-dependent AUC curves and Concordance Index curves (Fig. 5D) showed that the predictive performance of the nomogram was significantly better than that of the other four individual models, followed by the TNM stage model. DCA curves showed that the nomogram model had the highest net benefit rate in predicting OS, particularly in 5-year and 7-year OS predictions (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

This study investigated the correlation between the PHR and baseline clinicopathological characteristics of patients with HCC, as well as the prognostic value of PHR in HCC patients undergoing potentially curative hepatectomy. We found that high PHR was associated with larger tumors, more advanced TNM staging, and increased intraoperative blood loss, whereas low PHR correlated with liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension. We also observed that preoperative low PHR was correlated with better OS in HCC patients after hepatectomy. Additionally, a nomogram prediction model integrating multiple factors demonstrated certain net benefits in predicting OS.

In recent years, platelets and HDL-C have received increasing attention as novel participants in the tumor microenvironment. Our finding that a high PHR is associated with larger tumors and advanced TNM stage aligns with the known pro-tumor roles of its components. Platelets are established facilitators of HCC progression, promoting proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion [14, 21–24]. Conversely, HDL-C exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor effects [16, 17, 25, 26]. Thus, PHR integrates these opposing pathways, potentially offering a composite readout of the pro-tumor microenvironment. Interestingly, we also found that low PHR was associated with liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension, contexts often linked to thrombocytopenia due to reduced thrombopoietin production and splenic sequestration [27, 28]. This suggests PHR may also reflect the underlying severity of liver disease.Therefore, the biological mechanism underlying the association between PHR and HCC is multifaceted, reflecting the dynamic balance between systemic coagulation and inflammatory lipid metabolism. Recently, Tong et al. reported that PHR can be used as a biomarker for predicting gastrointestinal cancers [18]. Their results showed that the risk of gastrointestinal cancers increases sharply when PHR exceeds a certain threshold, highlighting the potential value of PHR in cancer risk stratification. However, there is limited data regarding the clinical significance of PHR in HCC patients and its prognostic value in those undergoing initial hepatectomy. Our study provides important evidence that PHR can offer crucial prognostic value for this patient population.

Notably, when exploring the subgroup differences in the impact of PHR on OS, we found that the protective effect of low PHR was attenuated in patients with hypertension and even increased the risk (hazard ratio [HR] 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.19). However, the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the HR was wide and lacked statistical significance. Nevertheless, studies have shown that elevated PHR levels are closely associated with an increased risk of hypertension [29], which is consistent with the trend of potential interaction between PHR and hypertension observed in our study (despite the lack of statistical significance). On the one hand, this may be attributed to insufficient statistical power resulting from a small sample size, which requires validation with an expanded sample cohort. On the other hand, hypertension in these patients may reflect lipid metabolism disorders and variable HDL-C levels, whereas platelet function and hyperlipidemia exhibit a synergistic interaction [30]. Further research is needed to fully understand this interaction. In addition, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that PHR had no significant prognostic value in patients with TNM stage I + II (Fig. 4B, P = 0.235), whereas its prognostic discriminatory ability was significantly enhanced in patients with advanced-stage HCC (Fig. 4C, P = 0.003). To explore a potential mechanistic hypothesis for this observation, we turn to existing literature. Platelets can promote HCC metastasis via mechanisms including the release of TGF-β124. Intriguingly, TGF-β1 is described as having a dual role in carcinogenesis, acting as a tumor suppressor early on and a promoter in later stages [31, 32]. We therefore hypothesize that the lack of prognostic value of PHR in early-stage HCC may relate to this complex, context-dependent biology of factors like TGF-β1 within the tumor microenvironment. In advanced stages, where the pro-tumor effects dominate, a high PHR (reflecting a pro-coagulative, anti-HDL state) becomes a clearer marker of adverse biology and poor survival. Similar results were observed in the high ALI group (Fig. 4H) and the combined PHR-ALI group in advanced-stage HCC tumors (Fig. 4J). Specifically, when the hepatic tumor microenvironment (i.e., coagulation and inflammation) is severely imbalanced, the “amplifying effect” of PHR on the pathological status becomes more pronounced, and its predictive value becomes more significant. Thus, high PHR is a comprehensive manifestation of “coagulation activation-inflammatory imbalance.” The synergistic interaction between platelets and HDL-C supports the biological plausibility of our findings and highlights PHR’s comprehensive capacity as a biomarker to capture the dynamics of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory processes.

Currently, the main causes of death in HCC patients are cancer recurrence or metastasis. Early recurrence is primarily driven by initial tumor-related factors, whereas late recurrence is associated with factors that alter the hepatic tumor microenvironment. PHR not only serves as an indicator for evaluating initial tumor-related factors (e.g., tumor size, invasion, and metastasis) but also reflects changes in systemic inflammation and the metabolic status of underlying liver diseases [14, 15, 23–25]. The prognostic value of PHR essentially reflects the synergistic interaction between coagulation dysfunction and inflammatory lipid metabolism disorders in HCC progression. Via subgroup interaction analyses and internal validation of the nomogram, we further confirmed the consistency and reliability of this association, indicating that PHR can serve as a novel biomarker for assessing HCC risk and prognosis. Currently, a growing number of second-line treatment options (e.g., antiplatelet agents and immunotherapies) have demonstrated promising therapeutic efficacy in HCC [24, 33]. The nomogram prediction model we constructed by integrating PHR, ALI, and other factors demonstrated a certain net benefit in predicting OS, with higher net benefit rates for 5-year and 7-year post-hepatectomy OS. This finding is particularly important for the long-term management of HCC patients: patients with advanced-stage HCC have a high risk of postoperative recurrence and metastasis, necessitating long-term follow-up and monitoring. The nomogram can assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and considering more aggressive maintenance therapies (i.e., targeted therapy and immunotherapy), thereby enhancing patients’ survival benefits. To further improve its clinical utility, we developed a dynamic nomogram that enables direct calculation of OS prediction probabilities via input of individual indicators, thereby providing an objective tool for clinical decision-making. These findings hold important implications for the stratified management and comprehensive treatment of HCC.

Although we have preliminarily demonstrated the clinical utility of PHR in HCC patients undergoing initial hepatectomy, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the exploration of the underlying mechanisms was inadequate. We did not measure biomarkers related to platelet activation, HDL subfractions, or specific inflammatory pathways (e.g., TGF-β1) due to the unavailability of relevant tissue or serial blood samples in this retrospective design. Future prospective studies should incorporate such translational analyses to elucidate the mechanisms linking PHR to HCC progression.Furthermore, due to the unavailability of data, other well-established important inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], C-reactive protein [CRP], and plasma viscosity [PV]) were not included in the analysis. Integration of these data would further help clarify the prognostic value of PHR in post-hepatectomy HCC patients. Second, the study design may introduce inherent biases. As this was a single-center retrospective study that exclusively enrolled Han Chinese patients, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities or healthcare settings is limited.To control for confounding from early postoperative mortality, we applied strict exclusion criteria (notably, the requirement for a minimum of 90 days of OS) to minimize potential interference from perioperative complications, residual post-resection tumors, and non-standardized follow-up on the interpretation of our findings. It should be noted that all mortality events in this study were related to HCC, strengthening the interpretation of our survival findings as being cancer-specific.However, this criterion may itself introduce selection bias and limits the interpretation of PHR for predicting very short-term outcomes. Furthermore, a key limitation is the unavailability of Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS) data for this cohort, preventing analysis of this critical endpoint in post-hepatectomy HCC.Third, several aspects of this study remain unclear and require further validation, including the correlation between PHR and key clinicopathological parameters (e.g., alpha-fetoprotein [AFP] and microvascular invasion [MVI]), as well as the prognostic role of PHR in patients with TNM stage I + II. Furthermore, the established nomogram lacks external validation to further assess its predictive performance. Extrapolation of our findings requires validation in large-sample, multi-center prospective studies (e.g., those involving diverse ethnic populations and longer follow-up periods) to enhance the robustness and validity of these findings. Finally, this study did not address several aspects, including exploring the clinical significance of dynamic PHR changes during treatment, elucidating the potential mechanisms underlying the association between PHR and HCC progression, investigating the impact of PHR on patient-reported outcomes (PROs), and examining its prognostic relevance in patients receiving comprehensive treatment modalities beyond hepatectomy. Therefore, the associations between PHR, systemic inflammation, the tumor microenvironment, and treatment response remain to be elucidated. Future research directions should include prospective, multi-center validation of the PHR and the proposed nomogram. Investigating dynamic changes in PHR during the perioperative period or in response to adjuvant therapies (e.g., TACE, immunotherapy) could further refine its utility. Integrating PHR with emerging molecular biomarkers (e.g., ctDNA) or advanced imaging features may also enhance predictive accuracy for personalized HCC management.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that different PHR levels are significantly correlated with the clinicopathological characteristics of HCC. Preoperative low PHR is associated with better OS in post-hepatectomy HCC patients, and the multi-factor nomogram prediction model incorporating PHR demonstrates a certain net benefit in predicting OS—particularly long-term OS. This finding suggests that monitoring preoperative PHR levels may enable clinicians to assess patients’ prognosis at an early stage, thereby facilitating more effective comprehensive treatment decision-making and enhancing patients’ survival benefits. However, these findings require further validation via large-scale prospective multi-center studies.
