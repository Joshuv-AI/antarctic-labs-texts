Early childhood exposure to general anesthesia and subsequent risk of learning disabilities: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies
Authors: Huimin Zhou, Yadong Jin, Hao Zhou, Xin Chen, Hoi Yan Mak, Hamdan Hamdan, Chaoxuan Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPotential neurodevelopmental consequences of early childhood exposure to general anesthesia (GA) remain uncertain, with existing studies reporting inconsistent findings regarding later learning disabilities (LDs). This meta-analysis assessed whether early childhood exposure to general anesthesia is associated with subsequent LDs and whether risks differ by exposure frequency, duration, or LD subtype.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Ovid, CINAHL, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to September 1, 2024. Cohort studies reporting LD outcomes after early childhood GA exposure were included. The primary meta-analysis was restricted to independent cohorts to avoid double-counting participants, whereas overlapping cohorts and studies reporting non-clinical educational outcomes were analyzed separately in sensitivity or secondary analyses. Random-effects models were used to pool risk ratios (RRs), study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE.ResultsSeven cohort studies comprising 167,848 children were analyzed. The primary meta-analysis, restricted to three independent cohorts reporting clinically diagnosed learning disabilities (LDs), showed that early childhood exposure to general anesthesia (GA) was associated with an increased risk of subsequent LDs (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.43). Exploratory subgroup analyses showed no significant association following a single GA exposure (RR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.89–1.16), whereas multiple GA exposures were associated with a significantly increased risk of LDs (RR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.72–3.05). Similarly, no increased risk was observed for GA durations shorter than 60 min, whereas exposures longer than 60 min were associated with progressively higher risk estimates. The certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, with low-certainty evidence for the primary outcome.ConclusionLow-certainty evidence suggests that early childhood exposure to GA is associated with an increased risk of subsequent LDs, with larger pooled effect estimates observed following repeated or prolonged exposures. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because residual confounding related to underlying disease, surgical complexity, and hospitalization cannot be excluded. In addition, all included cohorts comprised children born in 2000 or earlier; therefore, the findings may not fully reflect contemporary pediatric anesthesia practice. Further well-designed prospective studies are required.Trial registrationPROSPERO, CRD42024603831.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04096-0.

Background

Concerns regarding the potential neurotoxic effects of general anesthesia (GA) on the developing brain have persisted for more than two decades. Since the seminal report by Ikonomidou and colleagues in 1999 demonstrated anesthetic-induced apoptotic neurodegeneration in the immature brain [1], extensive experimental work has shown that early-life or repeated exposure to commonly used anesthetic agents can disrupt synaptogenesis, neurogenesis, and neuronal survival in rodents and nonhuman primates, with long-lasting consequences for learning and behavior [2–6]. Despite these robust preclinical findings, translation to human populations has been challenging. Clinical studies have yielded mixed results, with several large prospective and retrospective investigations suggesting that a single, brief exposure to GA in early childhood may not be associated with measurable neurodevelopmental harm [7–9], whereas repeated or prolonged exposures appear to confer greater risk for later neurodevelopmental disorders, including learning disabilities (LDs), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and intellectual impairment [10–13].

Learning disabilities represent one of the most prevalent and consequential categories of neurodevelopmental disorders in school-aged children, affecting approximately 5% to 15% of the population worldwide, with a higher incidence in males than females (ratio of 2:1 to 3:1) [14]. LDs are characterized by persistent difficulties in acquiring core academic skills, such as reading, writing, or mathematics [14], and are commonly classified into dyslexia, dysgraphia, and dyscalculia [15–18]. Beyond academic underachievement, LDs are associated with adverse long-term outcomes, including impaired social functioning, increased risk of mental health disorders, and substantial educational and economic burden for affected individuals and their families [19–22]. Although the etiology of LDs is multifactorial such as encompassing genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, and early-life brain injury, the identification of potentially modifiable risk factors remains a public health priority [14, 23–27].

Several population-based cohort studies have suggested an association between early childhood exposure to GA and the subsequent diagnosis of LDs [10, 13, 28, 29], however, the magnitude and clinical relevance of this association remain uncertain. Existing evidence is limited by heterogeneity in exposure definitions, outcome assessment, and adjustment for confounding factors, as well as by inconsistent findings across studies. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have addressed neurodevelopmental outcomes following early childhood anesthesia exposure, often incorporating learning-related measures within broader cognitive or behavioral domains [30–32]. However, LDs have not consistently been examined as a distinct primary outcome across these reviews. The present study therefore aims to complement existing literature by focusing specifically on LDs and exploring potential variation in associations by exposure frequency, duration, and LD subtype. Given the widespread use of GA in early childhood and the lifelong implications of learning disabilities, a rigorous synthesis of the available evidence focused explicitly on this outcome is warranted.

Therefore, this study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies to examine the association between early childhood exposure to general anesthesia and the subsequent development of learning disabilities. In addition, subgroup analyses were performed to assess the potential associations of exposure frequency and duration, and to explore differences across specific LD subtypes.

Methods

Given that this study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published data, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The study adhered to MOOSE reporting guidelines and followed the PRISMA 2020 framework [33] (Additional file 1). Two investigators independently conducted the literature search, study screening, data extraction, and quality assessment. Any discrepancies at each stage were first resolved through discussion and consensus between the two reviewers. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated the disagreement. This procedure was applied consistently throughout the review process. The protocol for the meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; identifier: CRD42024603831).

Study design and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to examine the association between early childhood exposure to GA and the subsequent development of LDs. To ensure comprehensive coverage, search strategies incorporated LDs-related keywords from previous meta-analyses (Clausen et al., Ing et al., and Zhou et al.) [34–36]. Six databases-PubMed, Embase, Ovid, CINAHL, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library-were searched from inception to September 1, 2024. Detailed search terms and strategies are provided in Additional file 2. All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for reference management. Duplicate records were removed using the software’s automated duplicate-identification function, followed by manual verification to identify any remaining duplicate citations before study screening.

Study selection

Study eligibility was determined according to PICOS criteria as following: Population (P): School-aged children (kindergarten through 12th grade); Intervention (I): Exposure to single or multiple episodes of GA; Comparison (C): Control group without exposure to general or local anesthesia; Outcome (O): Diagnosis or reporting of learning disabilities; Study design (S): Cohort studies.

Exclusion Criteria for the study are as following: (1) Participants with central nervous system disease or significant congenital heart defects; (2) Case reports, animal studies, editorials, reviews, or studies reporting only short-term outcomes; (3) Non-English publications; (4) Studies lacking sufficient data to estimate a risk ratio (RR); (5) Studies without formal diagnosis or risk assessment of LDs; (6) Participants with intellectual disability, sensory impairments, or movement disorders; and (7) Studies involving children without access to formal education.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted the following data from each eligible study: first author, publication year, country, birth year of participants, age at GA exposure, method and criteria for LDs assessment, sample size, number of exposed cases, effect estimates (risk ratio [RR], hazard ratio [HR], or odds ratio [OR]) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and adjustment for confounders. When necessary, different effect measures were harmonized before meta-analysis. Studies reporting HRs were pooled with RRs, with HRs treated as approximations of RRs because all HR-based studies were cohort studies reporting time-to-event outcomes. Although HRs and RRs are not mathematically identical, they are generally expected to be numerically similar when the proportional hazards assumption is reasonable and event rates are relatively low over the follow-up period. Pooling HRs with RRs is a commonly accepted approach in epidemiological meta-analyses of cohort studies with time-to-event outcomes and has been adopted in previous meta-analyses [37–39].For studies reporting ORs, ORs were converted to RRs using the method proposed by Zhang and Yu (1998): RR = OR / [(1 - P₀) + (P₀ × OR)], where P0 is the incidence of the outcome of interest in the non-exposed group [40]. The P0 value used for the conversion was extracted from the original study and is provided in Additional file 3.

Quality assessment

Study quality and risk of bias were independently assessed by two reviewers using the NewcastleOttawa Scale (NOS) (Additional file 4), which evaluates three domains: (1) selection of study participants; (2) comparability of groups; and (3) outcome assessment. NOS scores range from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating better quality. Studies scoring 0–3 were considered low quality, 4–6 moderate quality, and 7–9 high quality [34]. Only studies of moderate to high quality were included in the quantitative synthesis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P < 0.05 was set for statistically significant. Because of the potential clinical heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of population characteristics and exposure definitions, a more conservative random-effects model was applied to calculate the pooled effect estimates [41, 42]. Binary outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and the I² statistic. A P-value ≤ 0.10 for the Cochrane Q test indicated a statistically significant level of heterogeneity. For the I² statistic, values between 75% and 100% were interpreted as high heterogeneity, values greater than 50% as moderate heterogeneity, and values less than 25% as low heterogeneity.

To avoid double-counting participants, the primary meta-analysis was restricted to independent cohorts, whereas overlapping cohorts were evaluated in sensitivity analyses. When correlated effect estimates from the same cohort required combination into a single study-level estimate, the method described by Borenstein et al. was used [43]. Sensitivity analyses assuming within-study correlation coefficients (ρ = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8) were performed to evaluate the robustness of the combined estimates.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to exposure frequency, exposure duration, and learning disability subtype. These analyses were considered exploratory and interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Publication bias was planned to be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s and Begg’s tests only when at least 10 independent studies were available.

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for each prespecified outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach [44]. The certainty of evidence was evaluated across five domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, and was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Search results

A total of 108,533 records were identified through a comprehensive literature search up to September 1, 2024. After removing 12,704 duplicates by automated and manual screening, 95,721 records were excluded based on title and abstract review against the predefined eligibility criteria. The full texts of 108 articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 101 were excluded for predefined reasons (Additional file 5). Ultimately, seven cohort studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [10, 13, 28, 29, 45–47] (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The seven study cohorts spanned birth years from 1976 to 2000. Four studies were conducted in the United States, two in Australia, and one in Singapore. Notably, four U.S. studies conducted in the United States were based on the Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP), including two studies of children born between 1976 and 1982, one study of children born between 1996 and 2000, and one secondary analysis including both birth cohorts.

Exposure definitions varied: one study assessed exposure before age 1, one before age 2, three before age 3, and two before age 4. The age at outcome assessment varied across studies, ranging from 8 to 9 years to 19 years. Six studies identified LDs using clinical or administrative diagnostic criteria (including medical records, school records, DSM-IV criteria, or ICD-9 codes), whereas Schneuer et al. [45] evaluated academic underperformance using the National Assessment Program-Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN) and reported separate effect estimates for dyslexia and dyscalculia without an overall summary estimate. Six studies reported covariate-adjusted effect estimates and reported hazard ratios (HRs) or risk ratios (RRs), whereas Bong et al. [28] reported only unadjusted estimates (Table 1).

Quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) showed generally good methodological quality, with all studies scoring above 6, indicating the reliability and relative low risk of bias (Additional file 6).

Study selection for quantitative synthesis

Although seven cohort studies met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review, the primary quantitative synthesis was restricted to independent cohorts reporting clinically diagnosed learning disabilities. Accordingly, only one non-overlapping cohort from each Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP) birth window was retained, whereas overlapping REP cohorts were evaluated in sensitivity analyses. In addition, because Schneuer et al. [45] assessed academic underperformance using NAPLAN rather than clinically diagnosed LDs, this study was analysed separately as a secondary outcome.

Primary analysis (clinically diagnosed LDs)

The primary meta-analysis included three independent cohort studies (Table 2). Pooled analysis showed that early GA exposure was associated with an increased risk of developing clinically diagnosed LDs later in childhood (RR = 1.23, 95% CI, 1.06–1.43; P = 0.005) (Fig. 2). The pooled estimate yielded a Q statistic of 0.16 (P = 0.921) and an I² value of 0.0%. Because only three independent studies were included, these heterogeneity estimates should be interpreted cautiously. (Fig. 2). When the unadjusted estimate reported by Bong et al. was additionally included, the pooled effect estimate (RR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.05–1.67, P = 0.020; I2 = 49.3%) was slightly attenuated and between-study heterogeneity increased to a moderate level; however, the direction and statistical significance of the association remained unchanged (Additional file 7).

Another sensitivity analysis were performed to evaluate the influence of overlapping cohorts from the Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP). When the overlapping REP-derived studies were included in the pooled analysis, the association between early-life GA exposure and subsequent LDs remained statistically significant (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.04–1.29, P = 0.010), with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (Additional file 8). The effect estimate was slightly attenuated compared with the primary analysis, but the direction and statistical significance of the association remained unchanged, indicating that the findings were robust to the inclusion of overlapping REP cohorts.

Publication bias was not formally assessed because fewer than 10 independent studies were available for the primary meta-analysis, making funnel plots and statistical tests for small-study effects unreliable.

Exploratory subgroup analysis

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to exposure frequency, exposure duration, and LD subtype. Because exposure-specific estimates were not consistently available from the independent cohorts included in the primary analysis, all eligible exposure-specific estimates were used where appropriate to maximize the available information. When multiple exposure-category estimates were available from the same cohort, only one prespecified exposure estimate was retained within each subgroup analysis to avoid multiple inclusion of participants. Therefore, these subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Because most subgroup analyses included only a small number of studies (two to four studies), heterogeneity estimates should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of true between-study homogeneity.

Secondary analysis (academic underperformance)

Schneuer et al. [45] assessed academic underperformance using the National Assessment Program-Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN) rather than clinically diagnosed LDs. Because only dyslexia- and dyscalculia-specific estimates were reported, these were combined into a single study-level effect estimate using the method proposed by Borenstein et al. [43]. Sensitivity analyses assuming within-study correlation coefficients (ρ) of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 yielded highly consistent results; therefore, the estimate based on ρ = 0.5 was presented as the primary result (RR = 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.35) (Additional file 10).

The original study further reported similar patterns for dyslexia and dyscalculia across exposure categories. Compared with single exposure, repeated GA exposure (two exposures and ≥ 3 exposures) was associated with progressively larger effect estimates for both outcomes (Additional file 10). Because these findings were derived from a single cohort and represent academic performance rather than clinically diagnosed LDs, they should be interpreted as complementary evidence rather than pooled estimates.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each prespecified outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach (Additional file 11). The certainty of evidence for the primary outcome (clinically diagnosed LDs) was rated as low, primarily because all included studies were observational cohort studies. The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for the associations with multiple GA exposures, dyslexia after multiple exposures, dyscalculia after multiple exposures, and dysgraphia after multiple exposures because of the relatively large effect estimates. In contrast, the certainty of evidence for single-exposure analyses and several duration-specific subgroup analyses (1–30 min and 31–60 min) was rated as very low owing to serious imprecision related to the small number of contributing studies and wide confidence intervals. The certainty of evidence for secondary analysis (academic underperformance) was also rated as very low because this outcome was assessed using the National Assessment Program-Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN), which represents educational achievement rather than clinically diagnosed LDs, resulting in indirectness.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized the available evidence from independent cohort studies evaluating the association between early childhood exposure to general anesthesia and subsequent clinically diagnosed LDs. To avoid double-counting participants from overlapping cohorts, the primary meta-analysis was restricted to independent cohorts reporting clinically diagnosed LDs, whereas overlapping cohorts and studies reporting academic underperformance were evaluated separately in sensitivity and secondary analyses. The primary analysis demonstrated that early GA exposure was associated with an increased risk of later clinically diagnosed LDs. Exploratory subgroup analyses further suggested larger effect estimates following multiple exposures or prolonged exposure durations, whereas no clear association was observed after a single exposure. A separate secondary analysis based on academic underperformance showed directionally similar findings, although this outcome represents educational performance rather than a clinical diagnosis of LDs. However, according to the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence for the primary outcome was low, and the certainty for subgroup analyses ranged from very low to moderate. These findings should be interpreted as observational associations rather than evidence of causality.

Some previous meta-analyses have examined the association between early childhood exposure to GA and neurodevelopmental disorders [30–32, 35, 36, 48–50]. Among these, Wang et al.’s study demonstrated, based on existing clinical evidence, a modestly increased risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children exposed to anesthesia/surgery during early childhood, particularly those with multiple exposures [31]. Zhang et al.’s study further demonstrated a modestly increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children undergoing GA before age 3, while a single GA after age 3 appeared relatively safe [32]. Reighard et al.’s study demonstrated that the association between childhood anesthesia exposure and subsequent neurodevelopmental deficits varies across different neurodevelopmental domains [30]. Despite the inclusion of LD-related articles in these studies, none of them conducted a distinct meta-analysis exclusively for LDs, which distinguishes our current investigation. Despite differences in study selection and analytical strategies, our findings are broadly consistent with previous meta-analyses suggesting that early childhood exposure to GA is associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, particularly following repeated or prolonged exposure. However, unlike previous reviews, the present study focused specifically on clinically diagnosed LDs and explicitly accounted for overlapping study populations.

Given potential clinical heterogeneity in population characteristics and exposure definitions, a conservative random-effects model was applied to ensure the robustness of the pooled estimates, despite the absence of statistically significant between-study heterogeneity. Because the primary meta-analysis was restricted to three independent cohorts, the subgroup analyses according to exposure frequency and exposure duration incorporated all eligible exposure-specific estimates and were therefore considered exploratory. Within these exploratory analyses, repeated or prolonged GA exposure was associated with larger effect estimates than single or shorter exposure. Specifically, no statistically significant association was observed after a single GA exposure, whereas multiple exposures were associated with an approximately two-fold higher risk of clinically diagnosed LDs. Likewise, progressively larger effect estimates were observed with increasing anesthesia duration, particularly for exposures exceeding 60 min. Although these findings may suggest an exposure-response pattern, they should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup analyses were exploratory rather than confirmatory. Moreover, several subgroup comparisons were based on only two or three studies. Under these circumstances, an I2 value of 0% should not be interpreted as evidence of true homogeneity, and greater emphasis should be placed on the magnitude and precision of the pooled estimates than on statistical significance alone.

Another important consideration when interpreting these findings is the variation in the age at outcome assessment across the included cohorts. Because LDs often become clinically apparent only after increasing academic demands during schooling, differences in assessment timing may influence case ascertainment and, consequently, the estimated magnitude of the association between early GA exposure and later clinically diagnosed LDs. Earlier assessments may not identify children whose learning difficulties emerge only during more advanced stages of education, whereas later assessments may additionally reflect the cumulative influences of educational experiences and remedial interventions.

Given the clinical heterogeneity among different LD subtypes [15, 31, 51–53], subgroup analyses were further performed to examine associations between early childhood exposure to GA and the risks of developing specific LD subtypes. Although repeated GA exposure was associated with higher estimated risks of dyslexia, dyscalculia, and dysgraphia, these analyses were likewise exploratory and based on only two to three independent cohorts. Consequently, they provide limited evidence regarding potential differences among LD subtypes and should not be interpreted as demonstrating that the strength of the association differs across specific LD phenotypes.

One included cohort [45] evaluated academic underperformance using the National Assessment Program-Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN) rather than clinically diagnosed LDs and was therefore analysed separately as a secondary outcome. The secondary analysis showed a directionally similar pattern, with larger effect estimates following repeated GA exposure, suggesting that the observed association was generally consistent across different outcome definitions. Nevertheless, the effect estimates were more modest than those observed for clinically diagnosed LDs, which may reflect the broader nature of educational performance outcomes. Academic achievement is influenced not only by neurodevelopment but also by socioeconomic, educational, family, and environmental factors. Therefore, the secondary analysis should be regarded as complementary evidence supporting the consistency of the overall findings rather than direct confirmation of the primary outcome.

Importantly, factors related to hospitalization itself may constitute a major source of confounding when interpreting the association between early childhood exposure to GA and subsequent LDs. Previous studies have shown that frequent or prolonged hospitalizations are associated with adverse developmental and academic outcomes in children, including poorer performance in literacy and numeracy domains [54, 55]. Moreover, a case-control study indicated a potential association between exposure to anesthesia and the development of LDs, but the correlation between these two factors may be weaker than that the correlation observed between hospitalization and LDs [56]. These findings suggest that the association between early childhood exposure to GA and LDs cannot be interpreted in isolation from underlying clinical complexity. The process of hospitalization often involves multiple potential confounding factors, all of which may exert some impacts on children’s neurological development and cognitive function.

Repeated or prolonged exposure to general anesthesia in children may be associated with greater clinical complexity, including more severe underlying conditions, complex surgical procedures, and repeated hospitalizations, which may introduce substantial confounding by indication or disease severity. Furthermore, although experimental studies have suggested potential anesthetic neurotoxicity [2–6], the observational design of the included studies does not permit the independent contributions of anesthesia exposure, the underlying disease process, surgery, hospitalization, perioperative inflammation, and anesthetic neurotoxicity to be disentangled. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as a causal dose-response relationship between early childhood exposure GA exposure and subsequent LDs, but rather as an association that may reflect underlying clinical complexity. Nevertheless, these findings have potential clinical implications. Although causality cannot be inferred from observational evidence, these findings support careful consideration of the timing and necessity of procedures requiring repeated or prolonged GA exposure in young children whenever clinically feasible. Future high-quality prospective cohort studies with rigorous adjustment for important confounding factors, including disease severity, surgical complexity, hospitalization-related factors, anesthetic agents and dosages, perioperative inflammation, and exposure duration, are required to clarify the independent contribution of anesthesia to subsequent LDs. Such studies would provide a more robust scientific foundation for evaluating pediatric anesthesia safety and informing clinical decision-making.

This meta-analysis has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, only seven studies met the inclusion criteria, with sample sizes ranging from 206 to 153,025 cases. Although the overall sample size was large (n = 167,848), all included subjects were born in 2000 or earlier. This limitation reflects the extended observation period required for the reliable diagnosis of learning disabilities, which typically manifest several years after school entry. As a result, long-term follow-up studies based on contemporary anesthetic techniques could not be included. This is an inherent limitation of evidence synthesis in this field because all currently available primary studies require follow-up of approximately 5 to 10 years after early childhood exposure before learning disabilities can be reliably assessed. Consequently, the direct applicability of these findings to modern pediatric anesthesia practice is limited, and extrapolation to current clinical settings should be undertaken with caution. This also highlights the need for future studies based on more recent birth cohorts with long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up. Furthermore, anesthesia practices in some studies such as the widespread use of halothane and the non-routine use of pulse oximetry and carbon dioxide monitoring, differed significantly from contemporary clinical practice, further limiting the applicability of the results to recent cohorts.

Second, although most included studies adjusted for multiple confounding factors, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Children requiring surgery and anesthesia often differ systematically from unexposed children with respect to underlying disease severity, surgical complexity, hospitalization burden, socioeconomic status, genetic susceptibility, and perioperative inflammatory responses. Because adjustment for these factors varied substantially across studies, and only a limited number of studies adjusted for indicators of disease severity or hospitalization, residual confounding by indication remains an important limitation. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies adjusting for these factors was not feasible because of the limited number of eligible studies.

Thirdly, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to examine associations according to exposure frequency, exposure duration, and LD subtype. Because these analyses included only a limited number of studies and were based on exposure-specific estimates, some subgroup comparisons involved small sample sizes and unstable estimates. In addition, overlapping REP-derived cohorts were retained in certain exploratory subgroup analyses because restricting these analyses to independent cohorts alone would have resulted in insufficient data. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Fourth, although statistical heterogeneity was generally low in the primary analysis, clinical heterogeneity remained. Differences in study populations, exposure definitions, outcome ascertainment, adjustment strategies, and diagnostic criteria for learning disabilities may have contributed to variability in the pooled estimates. Because relatively few studies were available within each category, further subgroup analyses according to exposure window, anesthetic agents, or surgical procedures were not feasible. Fifth, publication bias could not be formally assessed because fewer than 10 independent studies were available for the primary meta-analysis, making statistical tests for small-study effects unreliable. Consequently, the possibility of publication bias cannot be excluded. In addition, only English-language publications were included, which may have introduced language bias.

Taken together, although this systematic review suggests an association between early childhood GA exposure and subsequent learning disabilities, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate according to the GRADE framework. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and well-designed prospective studies based on contemporary pediatric anesthesia practice are needed before firm clinical conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found low-certainty evidence that early childhood exposure to general anesthesia is associated with an increased risk of subsequent learning disabilities, particularly with higher association observed following repeated or prolonged exposures. However, these findings are derived from observational cohort studies and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. Because the comparison was between children with and without anesthesia exposure, the observed associations may also reflect differences in underlying medical conditions, surgical procedures, hospitalization, and other sources of confounding by indication. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, because all included cohorts were born in 2000 or earlier, these findings may not be fully generalizable to contemporary pediatric anesthesia practice. Further large scale, prospective studies with rigorous control of confounders and standardized assessment of learning outcomes are needed to more clearly delineate the relationship between early-life anesthesia exposure and subsequent learning disabilities.
