Consent for anaesthesia: guidelines from the Association of Anaesthetists
Authors: Andrew McLeod, David Bogod, James Brooks, Michael Devlin, Natalie Joseph‐Williams, Rehana Iqbal, Nadine Montgomery‐Allam, Felicity Plaat, Alex Ruck Keene, Cliff Shelton, Vinnie Sodhi, Michael Swart, Emma Wain, Hugo Wellesley, Jenny Westaway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntroductionGuidelines on consent for anaesthesia were first issued by the Association of Anaesthetists in 1999 and last revised in 2017. These new guidelines respond to the changing ethical, legal and clinical landscape in which anaesthetists practice. Since 2017, the Montgomery judgment has changed medical consent practice. The General Medical Council standards on decision‐making and consent and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidance on shared decision‐making also reinforce the need for high standards of patient advice and counselling.MethodA Working Party, comprising representatives from professional societies, clinical and legal experts and public voice representatives, was established. Relevant new law and professional guidance was identified. A consensus process was not followed as the guidelines are based in law rather than academic opinion.ResultsThe guidelines include a new section on peri‐operative medicine and updated advice on the timing, mode, conduct and content of consent discussions. We emphasise the role of healthcare organisations in enabling good practice. We have aligned this guidance with contemporary advice from key organisations.DiscussionThese guidelines focus on the law in England and Wales, but we also highlight the key differences in law across the devolved nations of the UK and Republic of Ireland. Although UK law and professional guidance have influence in other English‐speaking nations, a limitation of these guidelines is that they cannot be strictly applied outside the jurisdiction of the UK and Ireland. A second limitation is that they do not evaluate the resource implications that accompany them, such as clinical time and resources as well as costs of litigation and complaint.

What other guidance is available on the topic?

The most recent guidance by the Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland was published in 2017 [3]. Updated guidance on consent to examination and treatment was published by the General Medical Council (GMC) in 2020 [4]. The Department of Health published guidance in 2009 [5] and guidance is available on the NHS website which was last reviewed in December 2022 and was due for further review in December 2025 [6]. The British Medical Association and Law Society published guidance on the assessment of mental capacity in 2015, most recently updated in 2025 [7]. Guidance on information and consent for anaesthesia is contained in the Royal College of Anaesthetists (RCoA) Guidance for the Provision of Anaesthetic Services (GPAS) guidelines [8]. Subspeciality guidance on consent has also been developed for and with specific groups e.g. patients living with obesity [9] and by the Difficult Airway Society [10]. Guidance on consent for blood transfusion has been published in 2025 by the Committee on the Safety of Blood, Tissues and Organs (SaBTO) [11].

Why were these guidelines developed?

There have been several changes in the ethical and legal context around delivery of healthcare since the last Association guidance, in particular new case law and increasing emphasis on patient‐centred care and engagement with decision‐making.

How and why does this statement differ from existing guidelines?

The previous Association guidelines have been updated and fresh input received from legal experts, patient groups and lay members as well as anaesthetists. The guidelines include specific recommendations for institutions and departments as well as individual anaesthetists. A new section for anaesthetists working in peri‐operative medicine has been added. Previous sections on pain and intensive care medicine have been discontinued, as these specialities have developed their own guidance. Links are provided to guidance produced by relevant UK bodies.

Methods

A Working Party was established by the Association of Anaesthetists in 2023 comprising representatives from: the Association of Anaesthetists; RCoA; Obstetric Anaesthetists Association; Association of Paediatric Anaesthetists; relevant clinical and legal experts; and public voice representatives. The 2017 version of the guidelines [3] was reviewed by the members of the working party individually and as a group. Relevant new law and professional guidance relevant to consent for anaesthesia was identified based on reviewing guidance from the professional bodies represented, plus specialist societies, statutory bodies (e.g. the GMC), healthcare system and governments, as well as case law and legal statute.

Between October 2023 and January 2025, the Working party met on five occasions over videoconferencing (Zoom, Zoom Communications Inc, San Jose, CA, USA). Between meetings, correspondence was by email.

The 2017 guidelines were comprehensively redrafted to reflect the updated legal and ethical context relevant to consent. A vote‐based consensus process (e.g. Delphi) was not used because the guidelines are based in law rather than opinion. Where the opinion of the Working Party is articulated in these guidelines, it is intended to help readers to work within the legal and ethical frameworks.

The re‐drafting process was led by the chairperson of the Working Party (AM) with sub‐specialty clinical input from society representatives (VS and HW), and legal oversight from ARK and MD. Public voice input was led by NM‐A and JW. The other Working Party members made contributions based on their clinical and academic expertise.

The draft from the Working Party was reviewed by a patient representative panel and shared with the membership of the Association of Anaesthetists for consultation between 7 and 21 August 2025. Suggested modifications were incorporated providing that they added clarity and did not render the guidelines incompatible with the legal and ethical frameworks that govern the conduct of consent.

Results

The importance of consent

Ethical aspects

Clinicians have an ethical obligation to respect patients' autonomy, and patients have a right to be involved in, and fully informed about, decisions that affect them. Ultimately, a decision about a healthcare intervention is for a patient to make. This is reflected in the requirement to obtain consent for treatment, which can only be valid if adequate information is supplied and the patient has the capacity to understand it and make a balanced decision, free from coercion [12]. Patients may change their minds and withdraw consent at any time, providing these conditions still apply.

Involving patients fully in decisions about their care and treatment can lead to better engagement and improved outcomes. However, the need to respect autonomy sometimes conflicts with other ethical duties such as the principle of beneficence (doing good) and non‐maleficence (doing no harm). For example, patients may request treatment with very high risks or minimal prospects for benefit. Anaesthetists may need to participate in difficult ethical decisions at times.

Professional aspects

Respect for autonomy and the requirement for consent is central to the doctor–patient relationship. The guidance on decision‐making and consent from the GMC is binding on all doctors practicing in the UK [4]. It states that “all patients have the right to be listened to, and to be given the information they need to make a decision and the time and support they need to understand it.” Patients also have an interest in knowing what is going to happen to them and what they should expect during a course of treatment or another medical encounter. It is a professional obligation and good clinical practice to explain such things to patients, to give them the opportunity to ask questions and to provide accurate understandable answers.

Consent in law

Consent is a critical legal concept, reflecting its fundamental ethical value. It must be given voluntarily by an appropriately informed patient where they have capacity to do so. We include sections below which address situations where the patient cannot consent, either because of immaturity, or temporary or permanent cognitive impairment. Here, we deal with the situation where the patient is, in principle, able to give – or refuse – consent. We do not seek to duplicate guidance given, for instance, by the GMC [4], but highlight the critical points and apply them in the context of anaesthesia. The main text of this guidance is based on English and Welsh law; the legal frameworks in Northern Ireland, Scotland and Ireland are summarised in Online Supporting Information Table S1.

Consent does not exist in a vacuum, but in the context of treatment or management options being proposed by the clinical team. In deciding what options to propose, anaesthetists must act in accordance with the ‘professional practice test’, i.e. in a way accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion. If there are several alternative treatments, anaesthetists have a duty of care to inform the patient not only of the option which they recommend, but also of other reasonable alternative treatment options (plus the option of no treatment/no anaesthetic management if that is a reasonable alternative) indicating their respective advantages and disadvantages and the material risks involved. The doctor is not under a duty to inform a patient about possible alternative options if, applying the professional practice test, they do not consider them reasonable [13].

When discussing any recommended and reasonable alternative options, and the risks which may be involved, respect must be shown for the right of patients to decide on the risks to their health which they are willing to take. The doctor must therefore be careful to consider and inform the patient of risks that the particular patient would be likely to consider significant [1]. There are only two exceptions to this rule: first, when the patient has expressed a fixed desire not to know of the risks; and second, when discussion of the risks would pose a serious harm (beyond merely causing distress) to the patient. It may be wise to seek legal advice in such situations.

The treating doctor is responsible for ensuring that the patient has consented to the treatment; in anaesthetic practice the ‘treatment’ is usually surgery or an interventional procedure. For patients referred for investigations requiring anaesthesia, (e.g. magnetic resonance imaging under general anaesthesia), consent for the investigation should be sought by the referring doctor or doctor responsible for the investigation (e.g. radiologist), as they will be best placed to discuss the indications, potential benefits, risks and alternatives, including that of not doing the investigation. Consent for anaesthesia should be sought by the anaesthetist responsible for providing care.

The obligation to seek consent is a continuing one: if the patient's clinical condition changes such that a different option is now being considered, it is necessary to consider again the steps set out above and to seek consent to that new option [14].

An adult patient who has capacity to decide whether to accept or refuse a treatment option may do so even if refusing presents a risk to their life (subject to specific limitations in the context of medical treatment for mental disorder – see further information below) [15]. Such a decision should be documented carefully, including a confirmation that the patient had the requisite decision‐making capacity [16].

Sharing risk and providing information

General considerations

Patients should receive sufficient information to enable them to make authentic decisions about their care. Providing this may require time and engagement, particularly during a period that can be stressful. The process of consent requires a two‐way flow of information and should be based on a relationship of trust and partnership.

Shared decision‐making is a process by which healthcare professionals provide information and explanations to allow patients to make decisions about care that are right for them. The workload is shared, but the decision is the patient's [17]. Information and risk sharing should focus on: what matters to the patient; what decisions they will need to make; and what complications they might face. The BRAN (Benefits, Risks, Alternatives and the option to do Nothing) model can be a helpful aide‐mémoire.

The information provided to the patient should be relevant, accessible and timely. Patients should be enabled to ask any questions about the treatment options and risks, to enable them to engage with the consent process. All information, whether in written or verbal form, should make clear to patients that they are able and encouraged to ask questions. For instance, leaflets can have blank space marked for patients to note down concerns or questions; this can reassure patients that a planned discussion with a clinician will focus on what matters to them. Online Supporting Information Appendix S1 presents further information on sharing risk.

Relevance

The Montgomery judgement confirmed what was already understood as good clinical practice [1]: clinicians must ensure that patients are aware of “any material risks involved in any recommended treatment, and of any reasonable alternative or variant treatments”. A risk is deemed to be ‘material’ if the patient “would be likely to attach significance to the risk, or the doctor is or should reasonably be aware that the particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it” [1].

This guidance provides lists of suggested risks and information and links to other resources which we believe will be relevant to patients undergoing a routine anaesthetic intervention. These are aides‐mémoire and not intended as compulsory lists. They are not exhaustive but should be relevant to a typical patient undergoing anaesthesia. The duty remains on the anaesthetist to establish which risks would be significant to the individual patient they are advising.

The only way to establish what patients wish to know is through ‘meaningful dialogue’ – this is included in the seven principles of decision‐making and consent from the GMC. This must be a genuine two‐way conversation to explore what options, outcomes and risks would be important for a patient [4].

Risk information should be up to date and reliable, and applicable to the specific patient's circumstances as far as possible. Various scoring tools and resources can be used if they are applicable to an individual situation. However, uncertainty regarding outcome and risk for the individual patient will always exist and should be acknowledged. Resources include: Surgical Outcome Risk Tool (SORT) [18]; RCoA Anaesthesia and Risk web pages [19]; National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) risk calculator [20]; and speciality specific risk scoring tools (listed in Online Supporting Information Appendix S2).

Anaesthetists are unlikely to have data on procedural and other risks for their personal practice, particularly for rare complications. However, national datasets such as the National Audit Projects may be a reliable source of information for risks such as nerve injury after neuraxial anaesthesia [21]. A wealth of additional resources is available to provide risk estimates and peri‐operative information, but as the Montgomery judgment notes, patients should not be “bombarded with information” [1].

There is no statistical threshold which defines whether a risk should be disclosed. It is the importance of that risk to the individual patient that matters. The significance of any given risk should not be assumed but established through dialogue. Patients must also be supported to weigh risks and not consider one risk in isolation.

Accessibility

Medical information should be presented in a way that is intelligible and appropriate for the patient's numerical and language skills. Care should be taken to avoid jargon and acronyms. Independent translation services should be used as routine when needed. Further information on sharing risk information effectively with patients is provided in online Supporting Information Appendix S1.

The literacy proficiency of the relevant population should be borne in mind. An Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) study found that approximately 20% of adult UK and Ireland residents have low literacy proficiency, meaning that they can understand short, simple sentences and organised lists when information is clearly indicated [22, 23]. Evidence‐based visual resources can be an effective strategy to support consent discussions. For example, the RCoA has information leaflets (some translated into 20 languages), videos, and infographics summarising risks for both adults and children (www.rcoa.ac.uk/patients/patient‐information‐resources [19]).

Patients' understanding of risk and other information should be confirmed during the consent process. Patients should be enabled and encouraged to ask questions and made aware of the alternatives that they may have, for example regarding different analgesic techniques. Follow up opportunities should be provided, particularly where results from investigations are still awaited.

Many complications, such as pulmonary infection, venous thrombo‐embolism or myocardial infarction, are as much a consequence of undergoing surgery as they are attributable to the anaesthetic. These are better termed ‘peri‐operative risks’ rather than ‘anaesthetic risks’. Some risks are linked to patients' lifestyles and comorbidities, but emphasising these contributory factors during the consent discussion can risk upsetting and stigmatising the patient. Risks should be shared without becoming judgmental or exhibiting bias. Much risk information can be linked to positive opportunities to reduce risk. For example, the risk of venous thrombo‐embolism may be reduced by advice for early mobilisation, hydration and reporting symptoms early.

Timeliness

It is commonplace for anaesthetists to first meet their patients on the day of surgery. Patients may feel pressured to proceed and risks shared at this late stage may not be properly understood and/or contribute to pre‐operative anxiety. Overall, this approach may not meet the GMC requirements for informed consent, which states: “information should be shared at a place and at a time when patients are most likely to understand and retain it, and they have time and opportunity to consider the information before and after making a decision” [4]. Healthcare organisations and anaesthetic services should therefore ensure that patients have appropriate information and access to anaesthetic pre‐assessment and advice before significant decisions are made [8].

Information can be provided by anaesthetists or other appropriately trained healthcare professionals, via telephone or video consultations or in person. Adequate time for these discussions must be allocated by healthcare organisations. The amount of time required is not necessarily related to the complexity or duration of the proposed anaesthetic or surgical procedure, but rather to the patient's need for information and discussion.

Consent is a process, not a single event. Information delivered well in advance will alleviate pressure on patients. This will give time for reflection and further questions, paralleling the ‘cooling off period’ afforded routinely in the context of other high‐stakes decisions such as financial agreements. This process can be supported in the design of information delivered in advance. Patient information leaflets can have blank space marked for the patient to note down worries and questions. In such ways, patients are enabled to engage actively in shared decision‐making.

For certain patients, the setting and time of day may be important to their ability to understand information and make decisions. Patients may wish to have relatives, friends or advocates present for support, and in some cultures the presence of someone of the same gender may be important. These wishes should be accommodated whenever possible.

Consent is event‐specific and there is no defined length of time for which it remains valid. Where patients have been seen before the day of surgery and discussion of anaesthetic conduct has taken place, the anaesthetist on the day of surgery should reconfirm that the patient still understands and agrees with the peri‐operative plan.

General anaesthesia will render the patient temporarily unconscious and unable to make further decisions for themselves for its duration. Consent for anaesthesia must therefore include future treatments or procedures that may foreseeably become necessary. It is recommended that consent for potential blood transfusion is sought if there is a reasonable possibility of this becoming indicated [24]. Other procedures such as postoperative ventilation, tracheostomy and renal replacement therapy might also be important to discuss after certain procedures.

Warning of death and serious complications

The Working Party supports the increasing emphasis on warning patients (or parents or consultees) of the risks of serious complications including death.

For most patients, the risk of death arises from a combination of underlying comorbidities, the anaesthetic, and the procedure itself, rather than being purely a complication of anaesthesia. Regardless of the cause, patients should be made aware of these risks when they decide whether to give consent for a procedure. For elective procedures, this is clearly best done before the day of treatment to allow time for reflection.

The Working Party acknowledges that anaesthetists will encounter patients who are unaware of the risk of serious complications on the day of their procedure. Last‐minute warnings could cause distress and anxiety and may not provide helpful information for patients. However, by not sharing serious risks, anaesthetists may fall short of their duties or could be subject to criticism if any complication did occur.

The Working Party considers it is not acceptable for patients and anaesthetists to be placed routinely in this situation on the day of elective surgery. Healthcare organisations should ensure that all patients have comprehensive information about anaesthesia, including the risks of serious complications or death, provided pre‐operatively before they decide about surgery or investigations under anaesthesia.

Patients who decline to learn of risks

Patients must know and understand a minimum level of information for their consent to be valid. This includes the nature and purpose of a procedure, and the likely outcome. Beyond this, patients have the right (if they have the capacity to do so) to decline to hear further risks.

It is important to explore patients' reasons for wishing to remain unaware of information. Patients may come to clinics overwhelmed by risks and statistics and may need more time or different circumstances to discuss all relevant peri‐operative information. A patient's unwillingness to learn of risk might also raise concerns over their capacity to decide, or whether their decision is fully voluntary. Both these possibilities should be explored if indicated.

It should be explained that not wishing to hear all of the information may affect their ability to make the best decision for themselves, and that they should be aware that there may be information that in hindsight they regret having been unaware of.

Patients could be offered a second appointment, and/or the involvement of a trusted family member or friend. Referral to a senior colleague for a second opinion should be considered and careful documentation is recommended. Legal advice may need to be sought in some cases.

Patients who make specific stipulations or refuse certain aspects of care

A patient may either make their consent conditional on certain stipulations or refuse certain aspects of care linked to the proposed procedure. In either case, there should be a discussion to explore the patient's reasoning, with careful explanation of the potential risks of taking the course of action desired by the patient. Options should be explored and patients may need time to reflect.

If the anaesthetist considers that, overall, the relevant procedure can be carried out consistent with the patient's wishes, a record should be made in the hospital notes indicating that the patient has been informed of the likely consequences of their choices. The anaesthetist should also make a note of the precise nature of any conditions or restrictions that have been imposed by the patient and the explanation of risks that took place.

If an individual anaesthetist does not feel capable of providing proper care consistent with the patient's wishes, seeking further senior opinions should be considered as to whether the relevant procedure can be performed or what alternative arrangements can be made. In the case of an elective procedure, this may mean that the procedure or treatment cannot be offered, if offering it based on the patient's stipulations or restrictions would not serve their needs [25]. It may, on rare occasions, be necessary to seek a legal opinion.

Refusal of blood products

Patients may refuse the administration of blood components or products. Stipulations of this sort are relatively common among practicing members of the Jehovah's Witness community. Interpretations of what is acceptable may differ between patients and time should be taken to explain the difference between the various blood components; artificially derived recombinant products; cell salvage; cardiopulmonary bypass; and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Patients making such decisions must be given information about potential harms that may result if severe anaemia or coagulopathy ensues, and the discussion should be fully recorded. Most patients who are practicing Jehovah's Witnesses will have documentary evidence of a prior advance decision to refuse treatment which should be copied for their hospital records. Institutions and departments should develop appropriate documentation to record these conversations and decisions. Guidance is available from the Association [26] (see also the paediatric section).

Advance care plans, including treatment limitations, in the peri‐operative period

Wherever possible, anaesthetists should check whether a patient has made an advance decision to refuse treatment. Association guidance for discussion of the operation of advance care plans, including do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (DNACPR) recommendations and treatment escalation plans such as the Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment (ReSPECT) is available [27].

Documentation

The view of the Working Party continues to be that a separate signed consent form is not required for anaesthetic procedures where these are done to facilitate another treatment or investigation. Patients should be able to give consent fully aware of all the surgical, peri‐operative and anaesthetic information that could be relevant to their decision. The quality of information, discussion and counselling is the basis of good practice in consent. It is essential, however, for anaesthetists to document clearly both a patient's agreement to anaesthesia and the discussions that led up to that agreement, including the patient's significant questions and the responses given.

Anaesthetic consent discussions can be documented on the anaesthetic record, separately in the patient's notes or on a consent form. Anaesthetic departments should design anaesthetic records to facilitate documentation of discussions and agreement to specific modes of anaesthesia and interventions. Electronic anaesthetic records should also be designed to facilitate this process. Departments of anaesthesia and healthcare organisations should periodically audit the quality of documentation of consent [8].

Letters and notes from pre‐operative clinics may be an important part of the consent documentation and increasingly should be shared with patients in language that can be understood. This allows patients to further reflect on the information they have learnt and will hopefully make the consent process more memorable for them.

Documentation provides patients with relevant routine information, but also specific information generated by the consent process. This may include: significant risks discussed; options for anaesthetic and analgesic techniques; specific pre‐operative information e.g. medication adjustments; and strategies and suggestions to reduce peri‐operative risk.

Patients increasingly wish to record their consultations, either by taking notes or by making audio or video recordings. These records may help patients recall the information and instructions they were given and the risks that were shared. However, concerns have been raised as to the security of these recordings and the extent to which they might compromise the performance and privacy of the clinician. Current guidance recognises that patients may have legitimate reasons to make recordings but proposes that it is reasonable to request that patients do not share such recordings publicly [28, 29]. Legal remedies for clinicians may be available in case of misuse of such recordings, but this is outside the scope of this guidance. Healthcare organisations should have policies in place to manage this issue.

Documentation is particularly important in circumstances when the patient's decision goes against the anaesthetist's advice. Where a patient wishes to reverse a previously documented decision, it may help to protect the anaesthetist from later dispute if the patient is asked to countersign the written details of the discussion that led up to this decision.

Anaesthesia as the primary intervention

An anaesthetic procedure may sometimes be the primary therapeutic intervention, for example an epidural blood patch for the treatment of post‐dural puncture headache. In these circumstances, and, in contrast with the position set out above where anaesthesia is being used as part of another treatment, the view of the Working Party is that consent should be recorded in writing.

Responsibility for seeking consent

Patients will encounter many different members of a team during their consent process. Anaesthetic and peri‐operative information may be provided by different healthcare professionals. For purposes of consent, these guidelines differentiate between ‘autonomously practicing’ anaesthetists i.e. consultant and senior specialty, associate specialist and specialist (SAS) doctors on the one hand, and those where some degree of supervision or support is required, such as resident anaesthetists and physician assistants in anaesthesia on the other [30].

Responsibility for seeking consent for anaesthesia will ultimately lie with the anaesthetist responsible for delivering care for the patient. This will routinely be the consultant or autonomously practicing SAS doctor allocated to the operating theatre in which the patient is anaesthetised. The process of seeking consent can be delegated to other colleagues, and GMC guidance sets out recommendations for ensuring that colleagues to whom this role has been delegated have appropriate training and support [4].

All healthcare professionals who are not ‘autonomously practicing’ must have access to a senior colleague who can provide support and advice. Departments of anaesthesia are responsible for providing a ‘named’ consultant or senior SAS doctor for all anaesthetic activities, including when working with distant supervision. Reciprocally, non‐autonomously practicing health professionals have a duty to request help if elements of the anaesthesia consent process are beyond their competence.

Partnership with surgical, obstetric and medical teams is essential to establish responsibility for sharing respective risks and ensure that patients receive all the information that is relevant to them. The responsibility lies with healthcare organisations to ensure that information provision is consistent and comprehensive between shared services. Departments of anaesthesia and healthcare organisations are also responsible for providing training in seeking consent and ensuring knowledge of relevant legislation, such as the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [8].

Education, training, and non‐physician anaesthesia providers

Membership of the anaesthesia team

Patients should be fully informed who is involved in their anaesthetic care, and what their respective grades and roles are. All members of the anaesthetic team should introduce themselves by job title and qualifications, and patients should be made aware of which autonomously practicing doctor has overall responsibility for their anaesthetic care [30].

Training in seeking consent

Seeking consent for anaesthesia is part of the training curriculum for anaesthetists, and this process should be supervised until competence is achieved. If the patient has questions or concerns, they should be enabled to discuss these with the responsible autonomously practicing anaesthetist.

Training and education

All healthcare professionals need to develop and maintain their clinical skills, and this is a duty set out by the GMC in its Good Medical Practice guidelines [31]. Whenever anaesthetics and procedures are undertaken by those who are developing or refreshing their skills, this must be under the supervision of an appropriately qualified and experienced supervisor. In this context, consideration should be given as to whether prior training opportunities on manikins or part task simulators have been utilised and whether any additional risks or burdens for the patient are reasonable.

Patients may vary in their wish for information but should know the broad level of experience of those who are providing their anaesthetic care. Documentation of the discussion regarding procedural risks should include the level of supervision. Procedures with low risk (e.g. holding a face‐mask) require different levels of discussion to more invasive procedures (e.g. central venous cannulation).

Procedures performed must be indicated for clinical reasons, not undertaken purely for educational purposes. The Working Party does not endorse the practice of other healthcare professionals coming into anaesthetic rooms or theatre simply to perform individual procedures. Rather those seeking additional procedures for training and experience should be introduced at the start of the session and discuss their role with the patient. Meaningful dialogue and taking a patient‐centred approach are required to fulfil anaesthetists' ethical duties and preserve trust. Further guidance is available from the RCoA [30].

Obstetrics

Guidance for obstetric anaesthetists

Drugs, fatigue, pain or anxiety may impair the cognition of a parturient, but this does not equate with lack of capacity to make decisions regarding their peripartum care. Birth plans which have been documented in the antenatal period often include references to analgesia and anaesthesia. Patients who request regional analgesia during labour despite recording a refusal in their birth plan must have their more recent request respected, and the decision documented.

If a patient loses capacity, for example due to eclampsia or major haemorrhage, the birth plan should be treated as representing an advance statement of their wishes and feelings to consider, as part of determining whether administration of analgesia or anaesthesia is in their best interests. If the birth plan meets the criteria for a valid advance decision to refuse treatment (including, where relevant, those applicable to life‐sustaining treatment), it should be treated and respected as such. For the refusal of life saving treatment, an advance decision to refuse treatment should be written, signed and witnessed, and include words to the effect that the stipulations apply even if life is at risk. See the section on mental capacity for further details.

Some patients may have a pre‐existing mental health condition which gives rise to a concern that the risk of them losing capacity is heightened. At that point, it is important to consider whether an application to court is required, to endorse any contingency planning that is required to ensure that the necessary arrangements are in place to address the consequences [32].

It should also be noted that in law, a pregnant person with capacity can refuse any treatment for any reason, even if this puts the unborn child at risk of harm or death. An emergency court order to authorise treatment may be requested in such circumstances but will only be granted if the court concludes that this person lacks the relevant decision‐making capacity and that the treatment is in their best interests.

Guidance for departments

Labour is the wrong time to burden patients with excessive information. In early pregnancy every obstetric unit should provide advice about options for pain relief and anaesthesia during labour and delivery. An anaesthetist should be involved in preparing this information and approve the final version. Any patient who wishes to discuss techniques with an anaesthetist should be able to do so. Nevertheless, the patient must still be provided with appropriate information at the time of the procedure, the details of which should be documented. Patients at increased risk of morbidity or mortality related to the pregnancy, delivery or anaesthesia itself should be identified and ideally be seen in a high‐risk obstetric anaesthetic clinic by a consultant anaesthetist. Units should have guidelines in place to ensure that patients aged < 18 y receive age‐appropriate information and advice and access to an anaesthetist for antenatal counselling if needed. Consent in emergency work

Guidance for anaesthetists

In emergencies, there is often less time to seek a patient's consent, but every effort should be made to do so. Patients undergoing emergency surgery have limited recall of the consent process and often feel that consent is sought poorly in emergency surgery [34].

Consent should be sought at the earliest opportunity, preferably by the anaesthetist who will be carrying out their care. It is not ideal to obtain patients' consent in the anaesthetic room or operating theatre before their operation, and every effort should be made to avoid this, except in the most pressing of emergencies.

Dialogue with the patient will help to establish whether there are any concerns regarding their capacity. Impairment to capacity is more likely in the emergency setting due to the possible effects of acute illness or injury, or drug and alcohol intoxication. All reasonable steps should be taken to support patients and promote their ability to make decisions; however, clinical urgency must be a factor in this process. The risks of further deterioration and risk to life should therefore be incorporated into any best interests decision.

As emergency surgery is generally higher risk, a multidisciplinary approach should be taken to counselling patients about the risks of surgery from anaesthetist, surgeon and where appropriate, an intensivist. Discussions regarding cardiopulmonary resuscitation should be actively considered [35]. Use of the NELA score for patients undergoing emergency laparotomy can help all parties including the patient in the counselling and consent process [20].

Guidance for departments

Departments should allow anaesthetists time to see patients that require an emergency operation, with adequate staffing to allow time for them to counsel and seek consent from patients. Processes should be set up to monitor the quality of consent in emergencies in the form of patient feedback.

Pre‐operative clinics

Pre‐operative anaesthetic clinics have a central role in patient optimisation and care planning. They also play a key part in supporting decision‐making for patients who have high‐risk medical comorbidities, and/or are considering complex surgery. Patients often resolve to proceed with surgery before having a full understanding about their anaesthetic and peri‐operative risks. Early review and anaesthetic involvement with pre‐operative clinics can greatly contribute to the overall quality of consent for surgery.

Early assessment, investigations and functional testing, such as cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), can establish risks and suggest opportunities for optimisation. Information shared early with patients can give time for optimal decision‐making, modification of behaviours and engagement with prehabilitation programs.

Referrals have traditionally been made for patients undergoing complex surgery with higher predicted mortality. However, the need to provide good information before the day of surgery for all patients will require increased provision of these clinics to support the anaesthetic consent process.

Guidance for anaesthetists working in pre‐operative clinics

Identifying a patient's priorities and personal preferences is a key consultation skill. The outcome of the consultation needs to be communicated to the patient, clinicians and multidisciplinary teams. This can be verbal or written and can be by telephone, email, letter, video conference or face‐to‐face. Clear written summaries shared with patients can help with recall and reflection.

There are several models to assist predicting postoperative mortality [18, 36], but it is important to consider the patient population from which the model was derived and when the model was updated. Information must be provided in a way that helps patients make decisions but does not over‐burden them.

Where a mortality prediction is given, this should guide a conversation, rather than be a determinant for decision‐making. Common metrics such as 30‐day mortality are not ideal for individuals to use in isolation to make decisions. The risks of life‐changing complications, disability and poor long‐term outcomes are usually much higher than standard mortality estimates and are often more meaningful to patients. Guidance for departments

Departments should implement processes and services that provide relevant information about anaesthesia at pre‐operative clinics and have a system to triage and enable high risk, complex and higher need patients, including those who wish to discuss specific issues or concerns (i.e. material risks from their own perspective), to meet with anaesthetists in advance.

Information can be derived from reliable online resources but should ideally be based on institutional data as far as practical. Electronic patient record systems can be configured to deliver pre‐operative information and notifications to patients.

Decision‐making for adults and young people who lack capacity

The Mental Capacity Act 2005 provides the legal framework in England and Wales for protecting and supporting adults and young people aged ≥ 16 y whose capacity may be impaired [2]. It reinforces that capacity should be assumed unless proven otherwise (Section 1(2)) and sets out how to make decisions where a person does not have capacity to make their own decisions (Sections 1(5) and 1(4)) (online Supporting Information Table S1).

The law gives a specific definition of what it means to lack capacity for purposes of the Mental Capacity Act 2005. It is a legal test, not a medical test, and is set down in Section 2(1) [2], which states that “a person lacks capacity in relation to a matter if at the material time he is unable to make a decision for himself in relation to the matter because of an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or the brain”.

To apply the test, as the Supreme Court has confirmed in A Local Authority v JB [2021] UKSC 52 [37], it can be broken down into three questions (Box 4).

Mental illness may impair a patient's capacity to provide valid consent for treatment. However, it should always be remembered that a person receiving treatment for mental illness (even if they are detained under the Mental Health Act 1983) should not be assumed to be incapable of providing valid consent for medical, surgical or dental treatment [38].

Best interests, advance decisions to refuse treatment and lasting power of attorney

It is a legal requirement to provide the person with all practicable support to enable them to make their own decision [2]. However, if even with that support, they cannot make the decision, it will be necessary to proceed based on what is in their best interests.

‘Best interests’ is – deliberately – not defined in the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [2]. However, section 4 sets out a series of matters that must be considered when determining what is in a patient's best interests. Ultimately, as the Supreme Court emphasised in Aintree University NHS Hospitals Trust v James “the purpose of the best interests test is to consider matters from the patient's point of view” [39]. The purpose of the process is therefore to arrive at the decision that professionals reasonably believe is the right decision for the person themselves, as an individual human being – not the decision that best fits with the outcome that the professionals desire.

If an adult (age ≥ 18 y) has made a valid and applicable advance decision to refuse a specific treatment (or treatments), then they have made their own decision as to what they consider to be in their best interests. For the refusal of life saving treatment, an advance decision to refuse treatment should be written, signed and witnessed, and include words to the effect that the stipulations apply even if life is at risk. If doubts exist as to the authenticity, validity or applicability of an advance decision to refuse treatment, particularly where it relates to life‐saving treatment, treatment can be carried out whilst steps are taken (including where relevant seeking legal advice) as to how to resolve those doubts; however, spurious doubt should not be used as an excuse to avoid the effect of an advance decision to refuse treatment.

Adults can also register a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare decisions, which, subject to certain formalities, grants the power to decide what is in their best interests to an attorney. In some cases, a deputy may have been appointed by the Court of Protection to take decisions in the best interests of the person, although a deputy can never refuse life‐sustaining treatment on behalf of a person.

Although the Mental Capacity Act 2005 covers young people aged 16–17 y [2], they are not allowed to make legally binding advance decisions to refuse treatment or register a lasting power of attorney until they are aged 18 y.

An independent medical capacity advocate should be consulted to assist with decision‐making when a patient aged > 16 y lacks capacity and there are no family or friends to consult. The independent medical capacity advocate acts to represent the patient and will decide if the best interests framework has been appropriately followed. Independent medical capacity advocates cannot make decisions themselves, however. More information can be found at http://www.scie.org.uk/publications/imca/information/doctors.asp.

Guidance for anaesthetists

Any clinician can make a valid assessment of capacity. Anaesthetists should be able to draw reasonable conclusions about their patient's capacity during their dialogue with them; they should be alert to signs that the patient may lack capacity. If they believe that a patient may lack capacity to make a decision, they must assess their capacity using the test set out in the relevant legislation, taking account of the advice in the relevant guidance [4]. On occasion, the input of liaison psychiatrists, a safeguarding team or other professionals may be of value [4], although it remains the responsibility of the clinician proposing the procedure to satisfy themselves based on that input, whether or not the patient has the necessary capacity. Efforts to restore temporary loss of capacity should be made (time permitting) before reaching a determination as to whether a patient has capacity to make the decision at hand.

Any requirement for sedation or restraint to facilitate anaesthesia or postoperative care should be factored into the overall best interests decision. Anaesthetists may therefore need to be represented or indeed present at any best interests decision meetings.

In the absence of a valid advance decision to refuse treatment or an authorised proxy decision maker (such as a lasting power of attorney holder or court appointed deputy), a process to determine best interests must be followed. Precisely what form that process will take will depend, in part, on local protocols, but also on the circumstances of the patient's situation. It may include a meeting, but holding a best interests meeting is only sensible if it furthers the purpose of the process required under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [2]; a meeting for the sake of it will not serve as a substitute for that process. The purpose of the process is to allow a decision to be reached as to what course of action is in the patient's best interests, taking into account both clinical perspectives and the views of those who are able to convey what the patient's wishes and preferences would have been in the circumstances (e.g. family members or close friends). For patients lacking anyone to represent their views, an independent medical capacity advocate should be appointed when serious medical decisions are being contemplated [40].

Guidance for departments

Departments should have guidance in place to support anaesthetists when making capacity assessments and best interests decisions. This would include appropriate documentation, and access to experienced professionals with knowledge of mental capacity law, and the deprivation of liberty safeguards (DOLS) framework [2, 41]. Institutions should make a patient's advance decisions to refuse treatment and refusal of blood products clearly visible in the patient's medical records. Departments should also make legal support available to clinicians engaged in serious medical decisions on behalf of patients who lack capacity. This should include protocols for emergency access to the Court of Protection if needed.

Consent in children and young people

This section discusses the concept of parental responsibility and the thresholds that determine who can give consent for children and young people.

Parental responsibility

Parental responsibility refers to the duties and legal powers given to parents to enable them to act on behalf of a minor (up to the age of 18 y) and includes the right to give consent to interventions. Anyone can be given parental responsibility for a child by a court order, or have it taken away, but in general a mother always has parental responsibility. A father also has parental responsibility if he was married to the mother at the time of birth, if he marries her after this or if he is named on the birth certificate. A father does not lose parental responsibility following a divorce. Grandparents, stepparents and foster parents do not have parental responsibility unless it has been given to them by a court [42]. Some care orders also give the local authority parental responsibility, but the parents almost always retain it despite this; where this is the case and you are seeking consent from the parents, it is good practice to also request it from the local authority. Those with parental responsibility must always act in the best interests of the child.

Information about further resources and the situation in other parts of the UK and the Republic of Ireland can be found in Online Supporting Information Appendix S3.

For simplicity, we use ‘parents’ in the rest of this section to mean the holders of parental responsibility.

Children aged < 16 y

Most children aged < 16 y will not be competent to give consent for interventions. Some ‘older’ children aged < 16 y may be, and this should be explored, but they need to be able to show competence (see below). The legal presumption, and therefore the fallback position if there is any doubt, is that a child aged < 16 y is not competent to give consent.

Parents can give consent to interventions on behalf of a child who is not competent. Consent usually only needs to be given by one parent; however, if it is known that the other parent disagrees then this should be explored. If an agreement cannot be reached, then legal advice should be sought. A court can override a refusal of consent from one or both parents.

A child aged < 16 y who shows that they have sufficient intelligence and maturity to understand fully what is proposed is said to have ‘Gillick competence’ [43]. This gives them the right to give consent themselves. The level of intelligence and maturity needed to achieve the understanding required for Gillick competence will depend on the complexity of the decision being made. For example, a child may be competent to give consent for peripheral venous cannulation but not for epidural analgesia.

A competent child aged < 16 y can decline to make a decision and defer to their parents, but a parent cannot override a competent child's decision to have treatment. A competent child should be encouraged to inform their parents about medical decisions, but their right to confidentiality should be respected if they refuse to permit disclosure to their parents. If a competent child refuses consent, then this cannot be overridden by their parents. If the procedure is felt to be in their best interests despite their refusal, legal advice should be sought as their refusal can be overridden by a court.

If a child who is not Gillick competent does not want an intervention, then their wishes should be considered and given appropriate weight according to their level of understanding. This, and the potential for psychological harm (either secondary to having been overruled or from any coercion that might be required to undertake the relevant intervention), should be factored into the best interests assessment. If the intervention is still felt to be in their best interests, then consent can be sought from their parents. Every effort should obviously be undertaken to minimise the need for coercion, and the risk of psychological harm.

16‐ and 17‐year‐olds

Often referred to as young people, 16‐ and 17‐year‐olds should be presumed to have capacity unless it can be shown that they do not and can give consent to interventions just as an adult can. When it is felt that a young person may not have capacity, then the Mental Capacity Act 2005 has a formal test to help establish whether they do or not (see above section on decision‐making for adults and young people who lack capacity) [2].

If it is established that a young person does not have capacity, then there are two options. The usual route is to seek consent from the parents, as they retain the right to give consent to interventions until the young person reaches adulthood at 18 years of age (under the powers of the Children Act 1989 [44]). The alternative route is to seek consent in the same way as for an adult with incapacity under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [2], as this act also applies to 16‐ and 17‐year‐olds.

If a 16‐ or 17‐year‐old with capacity refuses consent, then this cannot be overridden by their parents. If the procedure is felt to be in their best interests despite their refusal, then legal advice should be sought as their refusal can be overridden by a court. Courts have used their power to do so repeatedly where a child or young person's refusal will endanger their life (e.g. refusal of blood transfusion by patients who are practicing Jehovah's Witnesses).

If a young person who lacks capacity does not want an intervention, then their wishes should be considered and given appropriate weight according to their level of understanding in the same way as for children aged < 16 y, along with the same consideration of potential for psychological harm. If the intervention is still felt to be in their best interests, then consent can be sought in one of the two ways described above.

Deprivation of liberty

This is a special case in law. Although parents of a 16‐ or 17‐year‐old who lacks capacity can give consent for medical interventions, they cannot give consent to confinement of their child. Authority to confine the child, and hence to make the deprivation of liberty lawful, must be obtained from a court, unless (unusually) the child meets the criteria for admission and treatment under the Mental Health Act 1983 [38]. Admission for immediate life‐saving treatment requiring no unusual arrangements to restrain the child will not normally count as deprivation of liberty [45].

Parents of a child aged < 16 y who lacks the competence to consent to their confinement can authorise that confinement, so long as doing so is a proper exercise of their parental responsibility (considering such factors as medical advice and the extent to which the confinement is objected to by the child). Legal advice should be sought where there is doubt.

A detailed description of what amounts to deprivation of liberty and how to manage it is beyond the scope of this guidance, but further information is available from both the British Medical Association and The Law Society [41, 46].

Emergencies

In life‐threatening situations, consent should still be sought as normal, if possible. However, treatment that is immediately essential to safeguard the child's life or health should not be denied in the absence of parental authorisation or consultation with parents (for a 16‐ or 17‐year‐old who lacks capacity where reliance is being placed on the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [2]). Emergency treatment undertaken without consent should be limited to whatever is immediately necessary, and further interventions that can reasonably be delayed, should be. If parents are not contactable and time allows, it may be possible to get court authority – it is usually possible to find a judge within an hour.

Patients with psychiatric illness

Anaesthetists may encounter patients with mental illness requiring anaesthesia, sedation or critical care. The Mental Health Act 1983 regulates admission and treatment for mental illness, and some sections of the Act can authorise treatment for mental disorder against the patient's wishes, even if they have capacity to refuse [38].

For a patient detained under the Mental Health Act 1983 [38], but whose treatment is not governed by the treatment framework (for instance, treatment for unrelated physical disorder), the provisions of the Mental Capacity Act 2005 should be followed [2]. A patient with the relevant decision‐making capacity can refuse such treatment, although careful consideration of that capacity is likely to be necessary.

Patients having electroconvulsive therapy will require anaesthesia. Patients with capacity can consent to or refuse electroconvulsive therapy and they can also make an advance decision to refuse treatment. When patients lack capacity to make decisions about electroconvulsive therapy, specific safeguards and protocols exist if electroconvulsive therapy is to be given in their best interests. Electroconvulsive therapy can be given in emergencies under the Mental Health Act 1983 without consent if life is deemed to be at risk, but this requires specific authorisation procedures to be followed [38, 47].

Anaesthetists may also be asked to participate in acute patient restraint teams, and the Royal College of Psychiatrists has issued a position statement on the involvement of anaesthetists in restraint teams [48].

Intensive care medicine

Information on the likelihood of postoperative intensive care and related treatments should be part of the pre‐operative discussions before major or emergency surgery. Further guidance on consent and decision‐making has been jointly produced by the Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine at the RCoA and the Intensive Care Society [49].

Pain medicine

Guidance specific to pain medicine practice has been produced by the Faculty of Pain Medicine at the RCoA [50].

Discussion

The purpose of these guidelines is to state the law and professional guidance as it applies to consent for anaesthesia and describe how best to fulfil their requirements. These guidelines build on previous work [3], incorporating changes in law and related professional guidelines as well as current clinical practice, the proliferation of technology and the increasing role of anaesthetists in pre‐operative assessment and peri‐operative medicine.

The Association of Anaesthetists is an international organisation whose primary focus is on Great Britain and Ireland. While these countries have similar legal systems and ethical norms, there are important differences between nations. Bearing in mind that the guiding principles are similar throughout the devolved nations of the UK and in Ireland, the Working Party made a pragmatic decision to continue to focus the body of this guidance on the law in England and Wales. However, we highlight the key differences in law and include further information in the online Supporting Information Appendix S3.

Health professionals must familiarise themselves with, and follow, the relevant laws of the country in which they practice. Although UK law and professional guidance have influence in other English‐speaking nations, a limitation of these guidelines is that they cannot be strictly applied outside the jurisdictions of the UK and Republic of Ireland. A second limitation is that these guidelines do not evaluate the resource implications that accompany them, such as clinical time and resources as well as costs of litigation and complaint. We anticipate that in a resource‐constrained system, some healthcare organisations may struggle to fully comply with these guidelines (e.g. the provision of time and facilities for anaesthetists and patients to have comprehensive consent conversations in advance of the day of elective surgery). Although we recognise the present challenges of delivering a service, the guidance is reflective of legal and governance requirements, and we encourage readers to work with organisations to determine how they can be implemented.

Many professional guidelines are produced based on vote‐based consensus approaches such as Delphi studies and nominal group techniques [51, 52]. These approaches are designed to place panel members on an even footing and are ideally suited to situations where there are no definitive recommendations available. This is not the case when dealing with the law, and we therefore resolved to write the guidance with continuous input from legal experts, focusing on what current law requires. We accept that this approach may have limited free discussion, but we nevertheless feel it is the most appropriate technique for this subject.

In summary, this guidance provides anaesthetists working in the UK and Ireland with updated guidance for consent in anaesthesia, which focuses on the need for patient‐centred decision‐making, and following contemporary legal frameworks.
