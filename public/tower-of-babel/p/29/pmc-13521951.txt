Integrated air-coupled ultrasonic transducer with front-end preamplification for structural health monitoring
Authors: Zhangsheng Sun, Ruiyan Wang, Hanlin Zhao, Ying Wang, Zhen Jin, Xiuquan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Air-coupled ultrasonic testing provides non-contact structural evaluation for scenarios in which liquid or gel couplants are impractical. However, its field deployment is limited by severe acoustic impedance mismatch, microvolt-scale received signals, electromagnetic interference, and cable-induced signal degradation. To address these coupled limitations, this study developed an integrated air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head that combines a manufacturable hollow-glass-microsphere epoxy matching layer, a conformal conductive shielding layer with controlled grounding, and a closely coupled low-noise front-end preamplifier. The integrated architecture was evaluated through long-range weak-signal reception experiments and a preliminary wall-climbing robotic tile-debonding inspection. Under shielded conditions, integrated front-end preamplification improved the signal-to-noise ratio by 23.70 to 34.80 dB relative to the unamplified baseline over propagation distances of 400 to 2000 mm. It also outperformed downstream external preamplification by 10.43 to 12.92 dB. After tapping-based zoning of the inspection regions, the robotic experiment showed that the sensing-head could acquire non-contact through-transmission signals and distinguish region-level feature differences under mobile inspection conditions. These results support a compact sensing-head strategy that improves acoustic coupling, EMI immunity, and weak-signal preservation for air-coupled ultrasonic inspection.

Introduction

Air-coupled ultrasonic testing is a non-contact nondestructive testing technique that avoids liquid or gel couplants, making it suitable for inspections requiring cleanliness or operation in harsh or inaccessible environments. In recent years, its application scope has expanded markedly1,2, spanning structural health monitoring of aerospace composite structures3–5, internal imaging of highly attenuative natural media such as wood,6 and nondestructive evaluation and in-line monitoring of lithium batteries for new energy systems7–9. Despite this breadth, air-coupled ultrasonics remains constrained by a fundamental physical barrier: an acoustic-impedance mismatch of roughly five orders of magnitude between piezoelectric materials (≈ 30 MRayl) and air (≈ 0.0004 MRayl)9. The resulting reflection at the solid–gas interface limits transmission efficiency and, in turn, the achievable sensitivity and bandwidth.

A primary route to improve transmission efficiency is the use of acoustic-impedance matching layers10. Passive matching-layer configurations remain widely adopted for air-coupled ultrasonic transducers because they are robust and straightforward to integrate11–13. However, matching-layer design involves a coupled trade-off among impedance, attenuation, and manufacturability: conventional polymers are easy to process but often cannot achieve both sufficiently low impedance and low attenuation, whereas aerogels or metamaterials can offer ultralow impedance but may be fragile or difficult to fabricate at scale. Hollow glass microsphere (HGM) composites provide a practical compromise by enabling impedance tuning through microstructure control. Reported studies show that optimizing the packing density and particle-size distribution of HGMs in a polymer matrix can yield impedances approaching theoretical targets while maintaining adequate mechanical strength, thereby improving air-coupled transducer performance14,15. Nevertheless, acoustic transmission enhancement alone does not guarantee usable receive-side signal quality. Because propagation in air is highly attenuative, the converted electrical response after transmission can remain at the microvolt level, and subsequent signal transfer and processing become increasingly vulnerable to interference and loss.

Given the weakness of air-coupled signals, the receive chain is particularly sensitive to electromagnetic interference (EMI) and transmission loss. Experiments indicate that unshielded transducers (e.g., CMUTs) can acquire substantial common-mode noise, reducing the effective signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)16. Although metal housings are often used for shielding, their density, corrosion susceptibility, and manufacturing burden can hinder miniaturization and system-level integration17. To address these limitations, lightweight polymer-matrix composite shielding strategies have advanced significantly in recent years18,19; for example, nozzle-less ultrasonic spray coating and 3D-printed carbon-nanotube architectures can both achieve shielding effectiveness exceeding 60 dB19,20. Even with improved shielding, long coaxial cables can introduce parasitic capacitance and voltage-divider effects that severely attenuate weak received signals. Consequently, the noise figure and gain–bandwidth characteristics of the preamplifier, as the first stage of the receive chain, are decisive for the final SNR21. To satisfy the “high-voltage transmit–low-voltage receive” requirement in air-coupled applications, integrating interface electronics as close as possible to the sensing element has become a key approach for preserving weak-signal integrity22. Current studies on improving SNR by integrating JFET buffer stages, analog front-end architectures, or transformer-coupled low-noise preamplifiers23–25 further demonstrate the importance of low-noise circuit design for extracting microvolt-level signals.

Although acoustic-matching, electromagnetic shielding, and low-noise preamplification have each been investigated in previous studies, their separate use addresses only part of the weak-signal challenge in air-coupled ultrasonic testing26. In this work, the contribution is defined at the sensing-head level. A manufacturable HGM/epoxy matching layer, a conformal conductive shielding layer with controlled grounding, and a low-noise front-end preamplifier are integrated to improve acoustic coupling, reduce EMI pickup, and preserve weak received signals before cable transmission. This combined acoustic, electrical, and electromagnetic design is intended to address the coupled limitations of air-coupled ultrasonic reception, without attributing the novelty to any individual component alone27.

Controlled long-range experiments were conducted to quantify the effects of shielding, downstream external preamplification, and integrated front-end preamplification on SNR. A preliminary wall-climbing robotic experiment was then performed to evaluate whether the integrated sensing-head could acquire non-contact through-transmission signals and reveal region-level feature differences during tile-debonding inspection under mobile deployment conditions28,29. In this experiment, tapping-based acoustic screening was used only to establish three reference regions with different debonding severity levels, following the logic of previous percussion-based studies such as Kong et al. and Chen et al. This robotic experiment is treated as a preliminary feasibility validation, with full quantitative debonding inversion left for future work30.

Design and characterization of the air-coupled transducer

Principles of acoustic-impedance matching

Acoustic impedance is fundamentally defined as the product of material density and acoustic velocity. A pronounced impedance mismatch between a piezoelectric element and the target medium induces significant interfacial reflection, thereby attenuating the transmitted acoustic energy. Consequently, an acoustic-matching layer with strategically tailored impedance and thickness is introduced to mitigate this reflection and enhance overall acoustic coupling.

Matching-layer design and theoretical analysis

While the geometric-mean model facilitates the estimation of single-layer impedance targets, Mason, KLM, and series–parallel equivalent models provide more comprehensive electroacoustic characterizations when the requisite material and frequency-response parameters are accessible32,33. Given that the current system operates as an air-coupled, mid-to-low-frequency narrowband device, and considering the preliminary objective of expedited material screening, the geometric-mean model was employed to establish the target acoustic impedance, followed by subsequent finite-element analysis.

The geometric-mean model is concise and low-cost to implement, facilitating rapid material selection and preliminary parameter estimation; For mid-to-low-frequency narrowband systems with relatively low structural complexity, its predictions are typically stable, making it highly feasible and practically valuable in engineering applications. Given that this study targets an air-loaded mid-to-low-frequency narrowband system and prioritizes design efficiency and implementation, the geometric-mean model is adopted to determine the matching-layer impedance in the subsequent analysis.

Finite-element-based simulation analysis and parameter optimization

As noted above, adjusting the acoustic impedance of the matching layer can effectively alter the acoustic-intensity transmittance of the transducer. In theory, if an ideal, lossless material perfectly matched to the adjacent media were available, the transmittance could reach unity; however, such materials are difficult to obtain in practical engineering because optimal intermediate layers often require gradient or metamaterial-like structures that are hard to realize experimentally34.

To quantitatively evaluate the influence of the matching layer on ultrasonic propagation performance, parametric simulations were conducted in COMSOL. The simulation model was established using the Solid Mechanics interface and comprised a two-dimensional air domain (200 mm in height and 100 mm in width), as shown in Fig. 2a. The ultrasonic transducer consisted of a PZT disk and an acoustic-impedance matching layer. The PZT had a thickness of 2 mm and a diameter of 10 mm, while the matching layer had a thickness of 6.625 mm and a diameter of 10 mm. To highlight the role of the matching layer in wave propagation, all PZT boundaries except the interface in contact with the matching layer were set as low-reflection boundaries to emulate the effect of an absorbing layer. Materials and methods

Matching layer modeling and fabrication

Because the acoustic impedance of air (≈ 0.0004 MRayl) differs drastically from that of piezoelectric ceramics (≈ 30 MRayl), direct coupling causes strong interfacial reflections, preventing ultrasonic energy from being efficiently radiated into air and thereby severely reducing the transducer’s operating efficiency. Therefore, introducing an acoustic-matching layer is a key measure for achieving efficient energy transfer in air-coupled ultrasonic transducers.

In this study, a single-layer matching configuration based on quarter-wavelength theory is adopted, and the acoustic impedance transition between the piezoelectric element and air is realized by tailoring the composition of an epoxy-resin-based composite35. Prior studies have shown that incorporating an appropriate amount of low-density hollow inclusions into an epoxy matrix can effectively tune its acoustic impedance while maintaining low mechanical loss and good processability, making it suitable for impedance matching in air-coupled ultrasonic systems.

With properly designed matching-layer parameters, this structure can substantially suppress interfacial reflections, enhance ultrasonic radiation efficiency, and effectively broaden the transducer’s operating bandwidth, thereby maintaining stable performance within the typical low-frequency range of air-coupled ultrasonic applications.

Fabrication of a manufacturable low-impedance matching layer

Matching layers are commonly realized as particulate composites whose effective acoustic impedance is tailored through microstructural design. In this work, a low-impedance epoxy matrix is filled with hollow-glass-microspheres (HGMs), which reduce the composite’s effective density and elastic stiffness, thereby enabling controlled impedance reduction and tuning for high-performance air-coupled ultrasonic transducers. The constituents are mixed at prescribed ratios and subsequently cured to form a solid matching-layer. The acoustic properties of the individual materials are summarized in Table 3.

Electromagnetic shielding process

During weak-signal measurements, system performance can be degraded by environmental electromagnetic interference and electrostatic coupling noise. Because the transducer housing is made of nonconductive epoxy, it provides no inherent electromagnetic shielding. A conformal conductive copper-paint shielding layer was therefore introduced using conductive copper paint. After the PZT electrodes and lead connections were electrically insulated, the conductive copper paint was sprayed onto the outer surface of the insulated PZT element, the electrode lead region, and the adjacent cylindrical sidewall of the sensing-head to form a continuous conductive shell. The nominal coating thickness was approximately 50 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu\:m$$\end{document}, and the surface resistance of the dried coating was below 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varOmega\:$$\end{document}36. The acoustic radiation surface of the matching-layer was kept unobstructed, and no conductive coating was applied to this surface. This design was intended to reduce EMI pickup while avoiding an additional wrapped copper-foil layer that could introduce interfacial gaps in the acoustic path.

The coating thickness and surface resistance were recorded to improve process reproducibility, but they were not varied as independent optimization parameters in this study. In addition, coating-induced changes in bandwidth and center frequency were not separately isolated from the complete transducer response. Therefore, the shielding effect was evaluated at the transducer-system-level through controlled waveform and SNR comparisons between shielded and unshielded configurations, as described in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” section. To avoid overstating the scope of material characterization, the term “shielding performance” in this study refers to the measured system-level EMI suppression of the complete sensing-head, rather than independently measured material-level shielding effectiveness or acoustic transparency.

Experimental setup.

To prevent the shielding structure from forming an unintended ground loop, the conductive copper paint coating was connected to the coaxial cable shield through a short, low-inductance path. A star-type single-point grounding scheme was then used to establish a common reference between the sensing-head and the external detection unit. At the transducer interface, a shielded BNC connector was adopted, and a common-mode choke was incorporated into the receiving chain to suppress low-frequency and radio-frequency interference37. The same conductive copper paint coating process, grounding connection, and receiving chain configuration were used for all shielded transducers evaluated in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” and “Comprehensive validation of electromagnetic shielding and integrated front-end preamplifier” sections.

Integrated front-end preamplifier design and analysis

Air attenuation reduces the received electrical signal to the microvolt range, making it highly susceptible to electromagnetic interference (EMI) and cable-related degradation. To mitigate these issues, the preamplifier was integrated directly within the transducer and positioned as close to the PZT element as possible. This physical arrangement shortens the unamplified, high-impedance signal path, while the use of a high-input-impedance interface limits the electrical loading of the piezoelectric element prior to cable transmission.

With a low-noise transistor topology, the front-end circuit was designed to reduce input-referred noise and improve the signal-to-noise ratio of the receive chain24,25. As shown in Fig. 7a, the receiving chain consists of an integrated front-end preamplifier circuit and an external signal-conditioning circuit. The integrated front-end preamplifier is housed inside the transducer, and the signal-conditioning circuit is located in the external detection system.

Frequency response and acoustic bandwidth characterization

To confirm that the fabricated air-coupled ultrasonic transducer operated within the intended frequency range, its response was characterized using electrical impedance measurement and normalized acoustic amplitude response. The impedance magnitude and phase were measured over 60 to 160 kHz, as shown in Fig. 11. A distinct resonance region appeared near the designed operating frequency of 100 kHz. Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique

To verify the capability of the electromagnetic shielding structure adopted in this work to suppress environmental electromagnetic interference (EMI) in practical measurements, a controlled comparative experiment was designed in this section. In air-coupled ultrasonic testing, the signal becomes extremely weak after long-distance propagation, and the receiving chain is highly susceptible to ambient electromagnetic noise contamination, resulting in a pronounced reduction in SNR. Therefore, in this study, the shielding effect was evaluated at the system-level by comparing the received waveforms and SNRs of shielded and unshielded transducer configurations under identical air-coupled weak-signal reception conditions. This comparison was designed to quantify the practical EMI suppression achieved by the complete sensing-head, including the conductive coating, grounding path, shielded connector, and receiving chain.

The transducer was rigidly mounted on a slider using a 3D-printed bracket and aluminum profiles. The separation between the transmitting and receiving transducers was adjusted by moving the slider along a rail, with distances set to 400, 800, 1200, 1600, and 2000 mm. To emulate a complex electromagnetic interference environment, a mobile phone was placed near the receiving transducer during testing. Because ultrasonic waves attenuate strongly in air, the transmitting side was driven by a power amplifier providing a voltage gain of 25. The excitation signal was a five-cycle Hann-windowed sinusoid with an amplitude of 4 V and a center frequency of 100 kHz. The received signals were sampled at 1 MHz and recorded on a host computer.

Comprehensive validation of electromagnetic shielding and integrated front-end preamplifier

Building on the preliminary validation of electromagnetic shielding effectiveness in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” section, this section further evaluates the influence of preamplifier placement on weak-signal reception under shielded conditions. The comparison was performed at different inspection distances to determine whether the proposed integrated shielding and integrated front-end preamplifier design can improve SNR for long-range air-coupled detection.

The experimental setup was the same as that used in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” section. The transducers were mounted on a slider using a 3D printed fixture and aluminum profiles, and the transmit-receive spacing was adjusted along a rail. A power amplifier with a voltage gain of 25 was used on the transmitting side to compensate for the severe attenuation of ultrasound in air. The excitation signal was a five-cycle Hann-windowed sinusoid with an amplitude of 4 V and a center frequency of 100 kHz. The received signals were sampled at 1 MHz and recorded on a host computer.

All three receiver configurations were shielded and defined as follows: shielded without preamplification (Case 1), shielded with external preamplification (Case 2), and shielded with integrated front-end preamplification (Case 3). The SNR calculation procedure and window definitions were identical to those described in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” section. After excluding invalid records, five valid repeated acquisitions were retained for the statistical SNR calculation. The resulting waveforms are presented in Fig. 13 as independently normalized traces, whereas the corresponding SNR values, calculated from the original unnormalized signals, are reported in Table 7.

As depicted in Fig. 13, the received pulses exhibit progressive morphological clarity following the application of external and integrated preamplification. Relative to the shielded baseline lacking preamplification, the introduction of an external preamplifier yielded a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) increase of 13.27–22.67 dB across the 400–2000 mm measurement range (Table 7). In addition, the implementation of integrated front-end preamplification afforded an additional SNR enhancement of 10.43–12.92 dB compared to the external preamplifier setup, culminating in a cumulative improvement of 23.70–34.80 dB over the unamplified baseline.

Air-coupled tile-debonding detection experiment

To assess mobile deployment feasibility, a preliminary tile-debonding inspection was conducted with a lightweight wall-climbing robot carrying the integrated air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head. The experiment evaluated whether the sensing-head could acquire stable non-contact through-transmission signals and capture region-level feature differences under robot induced disturbance and environmental interference. Full quantitative debonding inversion and defect sizing were outside its scope. The system and inspection scenario are shown in Fig. 14. In ultrasonic nondestructive testing, transmitted-wave amplitude and frequency related features are commonly used to characterize changes in structural condition38. In this experiment, these features were extracted from the received through-transmission ultrasonic signals to assess whether the integrated sensing head could distinguish the three preselected inspection regions under mobile robotic conditions.

Before ultrasonic testing, manual tapping was used only as a preliminary contact-acoustic screening method to identify three regions A to C with different debonding severity levels on a single tiled wall, as shown in Fig. 14b. This step provided a qualitative reference for the subsequent robotic ultrasonic experiment and reduced the influence of inter-wall variability. The impact-induced acoustic signals were recorded with a mobile device at 48 kHz. In MATLAB, the effective impact transients were extracted and normalized in the time domain. A Hann window and fast Fourier transform (FFT) were applied to obtain the single-sided amplitude spectrum, and STFT was used to generate the power spectrogram39. The time-frequency ridge was then obtained by tracking the peak-energy frequency at each time step. This tapping-based characterization was used only to define the qualitative A to C debonding hierarchy before the robotic air-coupled ultrasonic experiment and was not used to validate the proposed ultrasonic sensing-head.

Tapping results were used only to rank the three inspection regions qualitatively. Better bonded regions generally show higher characteristic frequencies, whereas debonded regions show lower characteristic frequencies and stronger low-frequency energy40. In Fig. 15, the characteristic frequency decreases from region A to C, while low-frequency amplitude and power spectral density increase. Accordingly, regions A, B, and C were defined as areas of increasing debonding severity for the subsequent robotic ultrasonic experiment.

After the three inspection regions were identified, the air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head was mounted on the wall-climbing robot and aligned with the target position. Debonding detection used a non-contact through-transmission configuration. The excitation was a 64 bit coded pulse with a center frequency of 100 kHz and an amplitude of 150 V. Unlike the long-range bench tests in “Validation of the EMI immunity of the electromagnetic shielding technique” and “Comprehensive validation of electromagnetic shielding and integrated front-end preamplifier” sections, the robotic experiment used pulsed coded excitation without a separate broadband power amplifier. A shielded transmitting transducer and an integrated front-end preamplified receiving transducer were used to preserve weak transmitted signals near the sensing element before cable transmission, which benefits mobile deployment. The received signals were sampled at 1 MHz and recorded by the host computer. Because robot motion may introduce mechanical interference, a Butterworth band-pass filter was applied to the raw data to improve signal quality.

Figure 16 presents the received waveforms in each inspection region. Based on the transmission-wave features from Case A to Case C, a quantitative evaluation of transducer performance was conducted, and the results are summarized in Table 8. As shown in Table 8, the spectral centroid, peak voltage, bandwidth, and RMS amplitude decreased monotonically from region A to region C. By contrast, the dominant frequency changed only slightly, from 54.20 to 55.66 kHz, and did not show the same decreasing trend. Therefore, dominant frequency was less discriminative than the other reported features in the present dataset41. Among them, Case C showed the largest decrease in spectral centroid, whereas the dominant frequency exhibited only a slight shift. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the obstruction of acoustic-wave propagation by the debonded structure, which intensifies the attenuation of high-frequency components, thereby shifting the spectral centroid toward lower frequencies and reducing signal amplitude42. In comparison, the variation in dominant frequency is relatively small, indicating lower sensitivity to structural changes than that of the spectral centroid. Meanwhile, the three transmitted waves in Fig. 16 show overall similarity in their time-domain morphology; however, as the debonding severity increases, the waveform amplitude decreases and the effective duration shortens, consistent with the frequency-domain analysis results42.

Collectively, spectral centroid, peak voltage, bandwidth, and RMS amplitude distinguished the three designated regions under non-contact robotic inspection. Manual tapping served only as preliminary zoning and rapid screening because it depended on manual excitation, mobile phone acoustic recording, impact segment extraction, and qualitative spectral comparison, making it sensitive to impact force, microphone position, and ambient noise. Therefore, tapping outputs were not directly correlated with the quantitative ultrasonic features. In contrast, the robotic ultrasonic experiment used 100 kHz coded excitation, fixed through-transmission geometry, 1 MHz sampling, and uniform preprocessing, enabling consistent feature comparison across regions. These results provide deployment-oriented feasibility evidence for the integrated air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head, without establishing a calibrated model for quantitative debonding sizing.

However, within the confines of this preliminary validation, the current dataset merely demonstrates region-level separability rather than comprehensive diagnostic classification; critical statistical metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, receiver-operating characteristics, and rigorous defect-area calibration remain undetermined. Subsequent validation efforts must quantify sensor alignment tolerances and the repeatability of robotic kinematics, as these operational variables can independently modulate the received signal amplitude irrespective of actual debonding severity. In addition, the current experimental scope was limited to three regions on a single specimen, with regional classifications relying on qualitative acoustic responses rather than destructive testing or an equivalent quantitative ground-truth methodology. Therefore, the incorporation of additional diverse test specimens, independently verified defect geometries, repeated robotic scans, and comprehensive uncertainty analyses is imperative before these ultrasonic features can be reliably calibrated to specific debonding severities or extrapolated to large-scale structural façade inspections42.

Conclusions

This study developed and evaluated an integrated air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head for non-contact structural inspection under weak-signal and interference-prone conditions. The principal contribution is framed at the sensing-head level. Acoustic-matching, electromagnetic shielding, and low-noise preamplification have each been investigated in previous studies. In the present work, these functions are coordinated within a compact sensing-head architecture to address three coupled limitations of air-coupled ultrasonic testing: inefficient acoustic coupling, susceptibility to electromagnetic interference, and degradation of microvolt-level received signals before amplification.

At the acoustic-matching level, a manufacturable matching-layer was developed using an epoxy matrix filled with hollow glass microspheres, which reduced acoustic impedance while preserving a simple single-layer structure. The design was guided by the theoretical single-layer impedance target and finite-element parametric analysis, although the fabricated HGM/epoxy composite did not reach the ideal target impedance. Therefore, the formulation with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:=62.8\mathrm{\%}$$\end{document} should be regarded as the lowest-impedance and best-performing formulation within the tested material and processing range. Experimental comparisons among different matching-layer configurations showed that reducing the composite impedance within this manufacturable range increased the first-arrival response in air-coupled transmission. These results provide a practical route for improving coupling efficiency while recognizing the limitations of the current single-layer HGM/epoxy matching design.

At the receiving system-level, controlled long-range experiments showed that the integrated design improved weak-signal reception under the tested conditions. Shielding provided its largest benefit when combined with preamplification, increasing SNR by 6.49 to 13.30 dB relative to the corresponding unshielded amplified configuration. Under shielded conditions, integrated front-end preamplification further improved SNR by 23.70 to 34.80 dB relative to the shielded unamplified baseline and by 10.43 to 12.92 dB relative to downstream external preamplification. These results support the system-level value of placing the preamplifier close to the sensing element before cable transmission. These comparisons provide sensing-head-level evidence under the tested conditions, but they do not constitute an independent material-level shielding-effectiveness measurement and do not fully isolate every cable-related attenuation mechanism.

The integrated sensing-head was further assessed in a preliminary wall-climbing robotic tile-debonding experiment using non-contact through-transmission and coded-pulse excitation. Before ultrasonic testing, tapping-based acoustic screening was used only to define three regions with different debonding severity levels, a step that could also be performed with a microphone or mobile device. The robotic experiment then used the proposed air-coupled ultrasonic sensing-head to acquire 100 kHz through-transmission signals without liquid or gel couplants. The extracted ultrasonic features showed systematic region-level differences, supporting the feasibility of robotic air-coupled inspection. These results indicate deployment-oriented feasibility, while quantitative damage-sizing still requires additional specimens, independently verified defect geometries, repeated robotic scans, and uncertainty analysis.
