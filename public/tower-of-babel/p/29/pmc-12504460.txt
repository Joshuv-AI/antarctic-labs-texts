The study of dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT-based models in predicting malignant solitary pulmonary lesions
Authors: Libo Zhang, Xing Wan, Kai Ji, Kun Chen, Xiang Zhu, Qian Su, Shudan Zhai, Wengui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The morphology of benign and malignant solitary pulmonary lesions sometimes overlaps, making the differentiation difficult. This research aimed to create a radiomics-based prediction model using dual-phase 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) for noninvasive classification of these lesions. A total of 132 patients with solitary pulmonary lesions were included. CT, routine PET (PET1), delayed PET (PET2) and clinical data were acquired. Five combinations of radiomic features (CT, CT + PET1, CT + PET2, CT + PET1 + PET2, CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1) were analyzed. Feature selection used eight methods, and the top ten ranked features were retained based on their weight coefficients. Seven classifiers were used to construct models. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of the five optimal radiomics models for solitary pulmonary lesions were compared. The optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model achieved the highest AUC of 0.898 (95% CI: 0.828–0.968), compared to the optimal CT (0.828, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.754–0.902), CT + PET1 (0.858, 95% CI: 0.785–0.931), CT + PET2 (0.867, 95% CI: 0.796–0.938), and CT + PET1 + PET2 (0.868, 95% CI: 0.798–0.939) models. Based on dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT for radiomic analysis, the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model demonstrated promising diagnostic efficacy and can be a clinical diagnostic tool to distinguish between benign and malignant solitary pulmonary lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18677-5.

Introduction

Lung cancer has one of the highest incidence and mortality rates worldwide, with these rates continuing to rise annually1,2. It is often detected at advanced stages due to the lack of early symptoms, concealed tumor development, and inadequate health screening awareness. Consequently, most patients miss the optimal treatment window, which results in poor therapeutic outcomes3. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment are key to improving lung cancer outcomes.

Lung cancer diagnosis primarily relies on imaging examinations. Traditional chest X-ray examination has low sensitivity for detecting small, specially located, or atypical lung cancers, making it prone to oversight and misdiagnosis4,5. CT (computed tomography) employs high-resolution, three-dimensional imaging technology to clearly display the microstructure of lung tissues and lesions, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing mortality6,7. However, CT’s misdiagnosis rate remains high for solitary pulmonary lesions due to overlapping CT features of benign and malignant lesions. More frequent nodule identification using CT hasn’t increased cancerous nodule diagnoses8. New imaging methods are needed to improve solitary pulmonary lesion diagnosis.

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) has been widely used in diagnosing solitary pulmonary lesions9. There are over 80 etiologic possibilities for pulmonary lesions10. However, 18F-FDG is not specific for malignant tumors, and some benign lung lesions like tuberculosis, granulomatous inflammatory lesions, sclerosing hemangiomas, and fungal infections, may have higher standard uptake values (SUVs), while some malignant lesions may have lower SUVs11,12. Therefore, the accurate diagnosis of solitary pulmonary lesions remains a major challenge for 18F-FDG PET/CT. Dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT, involving delayed localized imaging of the lesion at intervals after routine 18F-FDG PET/CT, may aid in diagnosing solitary pulmonary lesions13,14.

Radiomics involves extracting features and modeling from image data using computational methods for disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment15–19. Researchers have explored the use of 18F-FDG PET/CT combined with radiomics analysis for solitary pulmonary lesions20. However, previous radiomics analyses have been limited to routine 18F-FDG PET/CT, which include only CT data and one set of PET data, thus restricting analysis.

In this study, we aim to use three sets of data (CT, routine PET [PET1], and delayed PET [PET2] images) from dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT in patients with solitary pulmonary lesions for radiomics analysis. We seek to improve the efficiency of diagnosing benign and malignant solitary pulmonary lesions by developing a radiomics model through dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients with solitary pulmonary lesions who underwent dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT at the Department of Molecular Imaging and Nuclear Medicine, Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital between January 2015 and March 2021 were included. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee, and informed consent was waived. Inclusion criteria: (1) 18F-FDG PET/CT performed before pathological examination such as puncture biopsy or surgery; (2) no prior surgery or antitumor treatment; (3) 18F-FDG PET/CT images showed lesions that were solid or predominantly solid as a single nodule or mass; and (4) higher metabolic activity than surrounding normal lung tissue. Exclusion criteria: (1) suspicious metastasis on PET/CT; (2) unclear pathological diagnosis; and (3) unclear 18F-FDG PET/CT images. A total of 132 patients were retrospectively included (84 malignant, 48 benign). Figure 1 shows the inclusion and exclusion workflow. Solitary pulmonary lesion is defined as a single occupying nodule or mass in the lungs, with no size limitation. Clinical information included gender and age. CT image features included maximum diameter, CT number, spiculation, lobulation, pleural traction, calcification, and vessel convergence sign.

PET/CT imaging

Patients fasted for 4–6 h, with blood glucose levels maintained below 11.1 mmol/L before 18F-FDG injection. A dose of 3.70–4.81 MBq/kg was injected intravenously. Whole-body scanning was conducted after 50–60 min. The PET/CT scanner used was the GE Discovery PET/CT Elite (American), and the scanning range extended from the top of the skull to the upper end of the femur (approximately 6 to 8 bed positions). First, a spiral CT scan was performed with a voltage of 120 kV, using automatic exposure control technology (30–210 mA, noise index of 25), a layer thickness of 3.25 mm, and a pitch of 0.75:1. Subsequently, PET acquisition was performed using 3D + TOF + spot expansion function (SharpIR) for acquisition and reconstruction technology, with a matrix size of 192 × 192, and a duration of 2 min per bed position. Delayed imaging was performed 50–70 min after the initial scan, with PET/CT localized scanning under the same conditions as the initial scan. SUV was calculated as the ratio of tissue radioactivity concentration to the administered dose, divided by body weight in kilograms. The maximum SUV (SUVmax) was the highest pixel SUV within the tumor. Tumor metabolic volume (MTV) represented the volume of tumor with FDG uptake exceeding 42% of SUVmax. Total lesion glycolysis (TLG) was calculated by multiplying MTV with the mean SUV (SUVmean). The PET VCAR software (GE Healthcare) automatically computed SUVmax, MTV, and TLG on the GE Advantage Workstation 4.7 (AW4.7). Patient maintains shallow breathing while performing 18F-FDG PET/CT image acquisition.

Image segmentation and pre-processing

DICOM images were obtained and manually segmented the regions of interest (ROIs) by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians using 3D Slicer software (Version 5.2.2, www.slicer.org). CT images are manually outlined layer by layer. PET1 and PET2 images are semi-automatically outlined, i.e., the outlining based on the thresholding of 42% SUVmax is used first, which may need to be adjusted manually later if it is necessary. They then reviewed each other’s work, and in the event of disagreement, they negotiated to reach a consensus. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by a third physician with over 10 years of experience if necessary. We measured PET traditional metabolic parameters from the outlined lesions, including those for PET1 (SUVmax1, MTV1, TLG1), and PET2 (SUVmax2, MTV2, TLG2). CT, PET1, and PET2 images were resampled to uniform isotropic voxels of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. Radiomics processing was conducted on the CT, PET1, and PET2 images using the Pyradiomics module in Python (Version 3.7.3). Figure 2 illustrates the radiomic analysis workflow.

Radiomics feature extraction

Radiomics features were extracted from CT, PET1, and PET2 images using five combinations (CT, CT + PET1, CT + PET2, CT + PET1 + PET2, and CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1). The extracted radiomics features included: first-order features; shape-based features; gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM); gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM); gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM); gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM); and neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM).

Radiomics features selection

Features were normalized, and consistency was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC > 0.75). Pearson correlation analysis was used to remove redundant features (correlation > 0.9). Then, to further remove irrelevant and redundant features, we employed the following eight machine learning algorithms: analysis of variance (ANOVA), mutual information (MI), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), ridge regression (RR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB). For each algorithm, the top ten ranked features were retained based on their weight coefficients resulting in a total of 40 feature subsets.

Construction of radiomics model

Initially, due to the imbalance between the number of patients in the malignant and benign lesion groups (7:4), which could affect the predictive performance of the machine learning model, we oversampled the ROI data from the benign lesion group, using the widely used synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) to balance the sample sizes of both groups to 1:1. We used seven classifiers to construct machine learning models: random forest (RF), support vector classifier (SVC), stochastic gradient descent (SGD), extremely trees (ET), decision tree (DT), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB). Each feature subset was used to train seven classifiers, resulting in a total of 280 classification models. Within each feature category, the best-performing model was selected, yielding five optimal models. Finally, these five optimal models were compared to determine the globally best model. In constructing the radiomics model, we randomly divided 70% of the patients into the training cohort and the remaining 30% into the test cohort. This process was repeated 1000 times to mitigate the potential impact of sampling variability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Python (version 3.7.3). Comparisons between two or more groups of normally distributed data were conducted using the independent samples t-test or ANOVA test, while non-normal distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. The diagnostic performance of each model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each radiomics model. Two-by-two comparisons between different AUCs were performed using the Delong test. Calibration curves were drawn to assess the agreement between the radiomics models and pathology evaluations. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the clinical value of the radiomics models. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Clinical data

A total of 132 patients with solitary pulmonary lesions were enrolled in this study, including 67 males and 65 females. Of these lesions, 84 were malignant and 48 were benign. Patient ages ranged from 17 to 86 years, and the lesion sizes on CT ranged from 0.9 cm to 6.5 cm. Detailed CT features, PET metabolic parameters and additional information for the malignant and benign groups are provided in Table 1. In the malignant lesion group, 43 patients were male and 41 were female, with a mean age of 63.27 ± 11.14 years. The pathological types of malignant lesions included adenocarcinoma (n = 62), squamous carcinoma (n = 13), small-cell carcinoma (n = 4), large-cell carcinoma (n = 2), typical carcinoid (n = 1), sarcoma (n = 1), and malignant melanoma (n = 1). For benign lesions, there were 24 males and 24 females, with a mean age of 54.91 ± 14.16 years. The pathological types included tuberculosis (n = 21), inflammatory pseudotumor (n = 17), pulmonary sclerosing pneumocytoma (n = 5), cryptococcal infection (n = 3), chronic inflammation with focal alveolar epithelial mild hyperplasia (n = 1), and fibrillation with charcoal deposition (n = 1). We also compared PET traditional metabolic parameters and found that SUVmax1, SUVmax2, TLG1, and TLG2 were statistically different between the benign and malignant lesion groups (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between the malignant and benign lesion groups regarding gender distribution, age, and CT features (maximum diameter, CT number, spiculation, lobulation, pleural traction, calcification, and vessel convergence sign) (P > 0.05). Radiomics feature extraction and screening results

After segmentation and preprocessing of CT, PET1, and PET2 images from 132 patients, a total of 1262 radiomics features were extracted from each imaging modality. Based on five different combinations of radiomics features, the initial selection using ICC and Pearson correlation analysis retained 232 features from CT, 567 from CT + PET1, 551 from CT + PET2, 956 from CT + PET1 + PET2, and 512 from CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1. These features were further selected using eight machine learning algorithms (ANOVA, MI, LASSO, RR, SVM, RF, DT, and XGB). The top ten features by weight coefficient were selected from each of the five radiomics feature combinations. The categorization of features across the different combinations is presented in Table 2. The top ten radiomics features and their weight coefficients are listed in Supplemental Figure S2, and the heat map of feature correlations is shown in Supplemental Figure S3.

Construction and efficacy evaluation of radiomics models

The top-performing radiomics models for each of the five feature combinations and their corresponding AUCs are presented in Table 3(a). Figure 3 displays the optimal radiomics models for the five feature combinations and their corresponding ROC curves. The optimal model among the CT radiomics models was selected using the RF method and constructed with the RF classifier, with an AUC of 0.828 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.754–0.902). The optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model had an AUC of 0.898 (95% CI: 0.828–0.968), which was the highest compared to the optimal CT + PET1 (0.858, 95% CI: 0.785–0.931), CT + PET2 (0.867, 95% CI: 0.796–0.938), and CT + PET1 + PET2 (0.868, 95% CI: 0.798–0.939) models. The optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model was selected using the RF method and constructed with the XGB classifier. A two-by-two Delong test of the AUCs of the five optimal radiomics models revealed statistically significant differences between the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model and the other four optimal models. The Delong test results showed significant statistical differences between the optimal CT model and the optimal CT + PET1, CT + PET2, and CT + PET1 + PET2 groups (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found in the AUCs between the CT + PET1, CT + PET2, and CT + PET1 + PET2 groups (P > 0.05). Calibration curves are shown in Fig. 3(b), demonstrating that the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model had the highest fit to the ideal prediction curves. DCA, detailed in Fig. 3(c), indicated that the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model had the highest overall net clinical benefit compared to the others. Examples of the cases are shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the ability to differentiate between malignant and benign solitary pulmonary lesions using radiomics based on dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT. By combining heterogeneity features from CT, PET1, and PET2 (CT, CT + PET1, CT + PET2, CT + PET1 + PET2, and CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1), using eight machine learning algorithms and seven classifiers, the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model, with RF for feature selection and XGB for modeling, achieved high diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.898, 95% CI [0.828–0.968]). The improved diagnostic performance demonstrated may reduce unnecessary invasive examinations and enable early detection of malignant lesions.

Many studies have utilized CT features and clinical features to assess the malignancy of lung lesions. One study screened for malignancy based on low-dose CT and used age, nodule diameter, smoking history, and duration of smoking cessation to predict lung malignancy21. A recent study showed that a logistic regression model based on plain CT and enhanced CT features showed superior efficacy in assessing malignancy in solitary solid pulmonary nodules compared with a model based on plain CT alone22. These studies then showed limited efficacy in detecting large lung nodules, and more optimized diagnostic models are needed. Our study also explored clinical information and CT features such as maximum diameter, CT value, spiculation, lobulation, pleural traction, calcification, and vessel convergence sign, which were not significant in our dataset. In addition, we explored PET traditional metabolic parameters such as SUVmax1 and TLG1, which were statistically significant in differentiating benign and malignant lesions (all p < 0.05). However, SUVmax represents only the single hottest pixel, and TLG varies by MTV, which is dependent on a threshold-based approach, making these parameters of limited diagnostic utility in diagnosing benign and malignant lung lesions.

In recent years, the value of 18F-FDG PET/CT -based dual-time imaging in tumors has received increasing attention from researchers. However, dual-time imaging based on 18F-FDG PET/CT has been less studied in lung lesions. A study reported that in regions with prevalent sarcoidosis, delayed PET models (AUC = 0.9) and combined early and delayed PET/CT models (AUC = 0.91), developed using support vector machine and texture features from delayed 18F-FDG PET/CT images, showed a significant improvement in differentiating between benign and malignant solid pulmonary nodules, compared to clinical indicators and visual interpretations23. An additional study reported that the highest AUC (0.98) was achieved using a combination of parameters extracted from dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT to differentiate between benign and malignant lung lesions24. However, these studies have limitations, such as defining the tumor volume based on a fixed threshold SUVmax greater than 2.5. SUV values can vary between hospitals and equipment, which may affect both the reproducibility and accuracy of the results. In our study, we used the 42% SUVmax threshold method to define lesion volume, compared five different radiomics feature combinations horizontally, and employed eight machine learning algorithms and seven modeling methods vertically. The optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model demonstrated superior efficacy in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions. Dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT provides a set of delayed PET images that are additional to those obtained from routine 18F-FDG PET/CT, allowing for various combinations of multiple image datasets to explore supplementary modalities for radiomics analysis. Handcrafted features depend on radiologists’ expertise, while deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), enables automatic extraction of complex imaging features. Deep features outperform handcrafted ones by capturing higher-order, abstract information, reducing human bias, and improving analysis accuracy. However, they lack interpretability and require large, annotated datasets. In pulmonary lesion diagnosis, combining PET/CT radiomics with deep learning enhances early malignancy detection, histological grading, and treatment planning, supporting clinical decision-making25,26.

Preclinical research is widely regarded as the gold standard for translational studies. The application of radionuclides in murine models serves as a critical bridge linking experimental findings to human disease states. In biomedical sciences, radionuclides are frequently employed to label biomolecules or therapeutic agents for molecular imaging purposes27. The integration of advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning has further expanded research capabilities in this field28. Currently, preclinical multiphase radiomics has emerged as an active area of investigation. In a recent study, PET imaging was performed to assess the biodistribution of a 64Cu-labeled chelator in nine nude mice at 1, 4, and 24 h post-injection, and radiomic features were extracted from seven different organs. This approach demonstrated potential in optimizing biodistribution evaluation and advancing the development of novel radiopharmaceuticals29. In preclinical applications, molecular imaging technologies have also been utilized for therapeutic monitoring. Another study leveraged PET radiomics to accurately identify T cell exhaustion states, dynamically tracking changes in CD8+ T cells following ablative irradiation in a tumor-bearing mouse model, and developed a noninvasive method for assessing T cell exhaustion to inform the optimal timing of combination therapies30. Furthermore, radionuclide-labeled nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems hold promise for achieving more precise targeted therapy while minimizing systemic toxicity28.

In this study, the top 10 features in the optimal CT+(PET2-PET1)/PET1 model included one logarithmic CT feature, one wavelet CT feature, and eight wavelet PET0 ((PET2-PET1)/PET1) features. We propose that PET0, reflecting the rate of uptake pattern, strongly correlates with the benign or malignant nature of the solitary pulmonary lesions when combined with CT and PET0 features. This strong correlation indicates that malignant solitary pulmonary lesions exhibit distinct changes in PET features before and after delay compared to benign lesions. The analysis of the rate of change of PET features before and after delay showed advantages in diagnosing benign and malignant solitary pulmonary lesions. This method can be used as an adjunct to improve the accuracy of clinical diagnosis.

Our study has several limitations. First, image segmentation and feature extraction require manual intervention, without a standardized protocol31, leading to variability and potential bias in lesion analysis. Second, PET and CT were resampled to the same isotropic voxel size, but image voxel upsampling may introduce image interpolation effects, affecting radiomic features in measuring image information. Third, we did not explicitly analyze correlations between radiomics parameters and tumor biology. More studies are needed to explore this further. Additionally, there is debate about the reproducibility of radiomics study32–37. Deep learning, which require minimal human intervention38–40, offers more objective results but demands larger datasets. However, a widely accepted guideline suggests at least 10 samples per feature for binary classification15. By this calculation, we need at least 100 samples, so our sample size is likely sufficient for our study.

In conclusion, the accurate diagnosis of solitary pulmonary lesions remains a clinical challenge. Traditional imaging methods offer limited diagnostic accuracy, but radiomics analysis using dual-phase 18F-FDG PET/CT has the potential to improve this. The advancement of life sciences, computer technology, and the expansion of medical image databases enable the development of more sophisticated radiomics models. These models, integrating multimodal image data, may significantly enhance clinical applications. Future studies are expected to further improve diagnostic accuracy, providing enhanced support for treatment and prognosis.
