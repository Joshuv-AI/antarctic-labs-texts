RGD-grafted dextran methacrylate hydrogel incorporating osteogenic peptide and Mn3O4 nanozymes for enhanced bone defect healing
Authors: Yuxuan Pang, Xin Wang, Huan Zhang, Xin Li, Min Lin, Liang Cheng, Ying-Wei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAggressive inflammation triggered by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) seriously disrupts intercellular communication between macrophages and tissue regenerative cells, such as bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and vascular endothelial cells, thereby delaying the healing process. Modulating the immune microenvironment through biomaterial-based strategies is crucial for effective healing. In this study, we constructed a composite hydrogel (denoted as RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4) by co-loading manganous-manganic oxide (Mn3O4) nanozyme and 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine-modified osteogenic peptide P24 (DOPA-P24) into arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD)-grafted dextran methacrylate (DEXMA) hydrogel. The Mn3O4 nanozymes effectively regulated the immune microenvironment by scavenging intracellular ROS and inducing M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization. This immunoregulatory capability synergistically enhances angiogenesis, providing essential metabolic support for improved osteogenesis. The RGD sequence endowed the hydrogel with excellent adhesive properties for BMSCs, promoting cellular proliferation. In combination with DOPA-P24, the DOPA moiety facilitated the sustained release of P24, inducing the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs over time. By alleviating inflammation, promoting neovascularization, and enhancing bone formation, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel accelerated the regeneration of bone defects in vivo. This work presents a novel strategy to enhance bone healing through synergistic immunomodulatory and osteogenic effects mediated by multifunctional bone scaffolds.Keywords: Hydrogel, Nanozymes, Anti-inflammatory, Bone defect healing, Immunoregulation

Introduction

The clinical management of bone defects caused by infection, trauma, and tumors remains a significant challenge. When the extent of the defect reaches a certain degree, the self-healing capacity of the human body is poor, posing considerable harm to the physical and mental health of patients [1]. Thus, artificial implants need to be reasonably designed to facilitate bone defect healing.

In recent decades, bone tissue engineering (BTE) has undergone rapid development, and various scaffolds have been constructed for the treatment of bone defects [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Among them, hydrogels have garnered significant attention owing to their advanced properties and demonstrated desirable therapeutic efficacy in promoting bone regeneration [[6], [7], [8]]. Hydrogels possess high porosity, resembling the extracellular matrix and facilitating cellular proliferation and adhesion [[9], [10], [11]]. Notably, such porous structures enable the immobilization of drug molecules and proteins, thereby ensuring the stability of drugs for achieving considerable therapeutic effects [[12], [13], [14]]. Additionally, cell adhesion, particularly that of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), plays a crucial role in bone tissue regeneration [15]. The arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequence serves as a specific integrin-binding ligand, facilitating cellular adhesion and proliferation [[16], [17], [18]]. RGD modification has been demonstrated as an effective approach to endow BTE scaffolds with the desired cell payload capacity and accelerated tissue regeneration potential [19,20]. Therefore, incorporating RGD into hydrogels could be an applicable method to facilitate cell growth.

In addition to cellular adhesion and growth, the induction of osteogenic differentiation-related agents is critical for bone healing. Growth factors have demonstrated robust therapeutic effects on the regeneration of bone defects when integrated into BTE scaffolds [21,22]. Among these, BMP-2 establishes a favorable microenvironment for the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, thereby triggering a series of osteogenic-related factors [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. However, the complicated configuration and high production costs of BMP-2 limit its widespread application. Therefore, a BMP-2-derived osteogenic peptide (OP) was synthesized (P24), exhibiting robust osteoinductive activity comparable to that of BMP-2. Like other exogenous growth factors (EGFs), P24 requires a well-designed platform to enhance stability and promote sustained release for a long-term effect on bone healing [27]. Inspired by the adhesive capabilities of marine mussels, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) is considered a suitable moiety due to its adhesive catechol groups, which can establish stronger non-covalent interactions with hydrogels for long-term function [28,29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the DOPA modification significantly enhances peptide materials, thereby prolonging their biological activity [28]. The speculation arises that DOPA-modified P24 could improve the stability and sustained release properties within a hydrogel scaffold.

Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation at the bone defects results in tissue oxidant stress injury and intensive inflammatory responses, which compromise angiogenesis and osteogenesis by disrupting the communication between macrophages and mesenchymal stem cells [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Therefore, it is highly essential to design the immunoregulation strategies. Artificial nanozymes, such as cerium dioxide (CeO2) and manganous-manganic oxide (Mn3O4), mimic natural antioxidant enzymes to maintain the redox balance, thereby preventing the occurrence of oxidative stress injury [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Specifically, owing to the double oxidation states of Mn2+ and Mn3+, Mn3O4 nanozymes present excellent ROS-scavenging capabilities, which facilitate redox cycling and result in the pro-inflammatory macrophage (M1) polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (M2), ultimately mitigating inflammation [38]. With the improvement of immune microenvironments in defect sites, bone tissue can exhibit an elevated level of neovascularization and osteogenesis, resulting in high-quality bone regeneration [39].

In this study, a multifunctional hydrogel (RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4) was developed to promote cell proliferation, enhance osteogenesis, and modulate the immune microenvironment (Scheme 1). The hydrogel was synthesized via photopolymerization of RGDfKAC and methacrylate dextran (DEXMA), successfully grafting the RGD sequence onto the hydrogel to improve its cell viability, metabolic activity, and adhesion capacity. After DOPA modification, osteogenic peptide P24, i.e., DOPA-P24, exhibited strong binding ability and sustained release behavior compared to unmodified P24. Importantly, Mn3O4 nanozymes in the hydrogel played a vital role in modulating the immune microenvironment through their catalytic activity in scavenging intracellular ROS, leading to the M1-to-M2 transition of macrophages and promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel significantly promoted bone regeneration at defect sites. Notably, in this “immunomodulation–osteogenesis coupling” strategy, Mn3O4 nanozymes alleviate inflammation via scavenging ROS and promoting M2 polarization. Moreover, the paracrine effects of M2 macrophages and DOPA-P24 synergistically accelerate bone repair. This design overcomes the single-function limitation of conventional dextran- or peptide-based hydrogels and proposes a promising strategy to enhance bone healing through the synergistic immunomodulatory and osteogenic effects within a multifunctional bioactive hydrogel.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Mn3O4 nanozymes

Mn3O4 nanozymes were prepared using a previously reported method [40]. A mixture of MnAc2 (2 M, 30 mL) and NaOH (0.2 M, 30 mL; Haoran, China) solutions was stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, glucose (5.6 mmol; Macklin, China) was added and the mixture was stirred for an additional 20 min. The mixture was then reacted at 180 °C for 21 h, and cooled to room temperature to collect the Mn3O4 nanozymes.

Synthesis of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel

DOPA-P24 osteogenic peptide (sequence: DOPA-(S[PO4] KIPKASSVPTELSAISTLYLDDD; 200 μg/mL; Sangon Biotech, China) and Mn3O4 nanozymes (100 μg/mL) were added to the lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphonate (LAP) solution (0.25 % w/v; EFL, China) containing DEXMA (5 % w/v; EFL, China). Further, 405 nm ultraviolet (UV) light was used to crosslink DEXMA to form hydrogel networks. Then, the RGDfKAC solution (5 % wt; EFL, China) was spread on the surface to conduct the modification [16]. After being exposed to 405 nm UV light for an additional 30 s, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 composite hydrogel was prepared. The irradiation conditions were light source power 3 W, illuminated panel diameter 2.5 cm, corresponding to a power density of 0.611 W/cm2.

Materials characterization

The morphological characteristics of different materials were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Gemini300, Zeiss, Germany). Element composition and distribution were scanned via an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; HORIBA EMAX mics2, HITACHI, Japan). The crystal structure of the Mn3O4 nanozyme was analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer instrument (D8 ADVANCE, Brucker, Germany). Functional groups of different materials were characterized via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; 6700, Nicolet, America). Universal testing instrument (INSTRON, England) was used to assess the compressive resistance property of hydrogels.

Degradation rate calculation

Different hydrogels (10 × 10 × 6 mm3) were immersed in the PBS solution containing α-dextranase (2 U/mL) at 37 °C for 21 days. Illuminated panel diameter 2.5 cm, corresponding to a power density of 0.611 W/cm2. The initial weight (W0) was recorded on day 0, and the remaining weights (Wt) of the hydrogels were recorded at different time points [41]. Release performance of peptide and Mn ions assessment

RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogels were immersed in PBS at 37 °C under gentle shaking [27]. The upper layer of liquid was collected at different time points, and the bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime, China) was employed to quantify the cumulative release of DOPA-P24. Further, RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 was incubated under the same condition and upper layer of liquid was collected at different time points. ICP-MS (ICPOES7200, ThermoFisher, USA) was utilized to detect Mn ions release.

Cell live/dead staining

RAW 264.7 cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on the hydrogels with different concentrations of DOPA-P24 (100, 150, 200, and 250 μg/mL) and Mn3O4 (50, 75, 100, and 125 μg/mL), respectively. After culturing with high glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (HG-DMEM; Solarbio, China) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10 %; Solarbio, China) and penicillin-streptomycin (1 %; Solarbio, China) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 24 h, the cytotoxicity was evaluated using a Calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide assay kit (Calcein-AM/PI; Beyotime, China). The reacting agent containing Calcein-AM and PI was incubated with the samples in the dark for 30 min. Cell images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (Bioteck, America). Then, BMSCs from male Sprague-Dawley rats (80–100 g) were seeded on the different hydrogels and cultured for 7 days. Detailed experimental procedures were consistent with those mentioned above.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

RAW 264.7 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were co-cultured with the DEXMA hydrogels with different concentrations of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes for 24 h. After washing with PBS (pH 7.4, 0.1 M, three times), culture medium (100 μL) and CCK-8 solution (10 μL) were added. After incubating in the dark for 2 h, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bioteck, America). Besides, BMSCs (3 × 104 cells/well) were cultured with different hydrogels in 24-well plates for 1, 4, and 7 days, and the corresponding absorbance values were also monitored at 450 nm.

Cytoskeleton staining

BMSCs (3 × 104 cells/well) were cultured with different hydrogels for 4 days. Then, BMSCs grown on different hydrogels were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4 %; Shangbao, China) for 30 min. After being treated with Triton-X (0.5 %; Beyotime, China) and BSA (5 %; Beyotime, China), the rhodamine-phalloidin cytoskeleton staining reagent (Beyotime, China) was diluted 1:100 (v/v) and added for incubating with samples in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, nuclei of BMSCs were stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime, China). The fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany) was employed to obtain the images. The BMSC attachment areas were calculated via ImageJ.

Hemolysis assay

Red blood cells (RBCs) from rabbit blood samples were incubated with Triton-X, NaCl (0.9 %) solution, and different hydrogels at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, the absorbance of centrifuged supernatants at 540 nm was monitored to calculate the hemolysis ratio.

Hydroxyl radical (•OH) scavenging assay

The hydroxyl radical (•OH) assay kit (Jincheng, China) was utilized to evaluate the scavenging capacities of different hydrogels. The agent was prepared, and subsequently, different hydrogels were added and incubated for 20 min. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 550 nm to calculate the scavenging rate. Then, RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel was incubated in PBS at 37 °C for 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 days, and then assessing •OH scavenging property. The procedure was same as abovementioned details.

1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging assay

The DPPH assay kit (Solarbio, China) was used for assessing the nitrogen radical scavenging capability. In brief, different hydrogels were incubated with the reacting agents in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the absorbance at 515 nm was measured to calculate the scavenging rate. Then, RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel was incubated in PBS at 37 °C for 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 days, and then assessing DPPH scavenging property. The procedure was same as abovementioned details.

Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining assay

DHE staining was employed to assess the anti-oxidative stress capacity of different hydrogels in vitro [42]. RAW 264.7 cells (1.5 × 105 cells) were cultured for 24 h, followed by the addition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 100 μM) and subsequent co-incubation with different hydrogels for 2 h. Then, DHE solution (5 μM, Beyotime, China) was added and incubated for 30 min. Then, the images were obtained, and quantitative analysis regarding DHE staining intensity was conducted through ImageJ.

2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining and flow cytometry analysis

The establishment of H2O2-induced RAW 264.7 cells and the intervention procedures were consistent with the previously mentioned protocol. Then, a DCFH-DA (Beyotime, China) staining kit was employed to detect the intracellular ROS. In brief, the DCFH-DA was diluted with culture medium at a 1:1000 (v/v) ratio and added to different samples for a 20-min co-culture. Then, the reagent was removed, and the samples were washed three times with the medium. The fluorescence images of the cells were recorded, and the corresponding positive areas were analyzed via ImageJ. RAW 264.7 cells underwent the abovementioned treating procedure was collected for flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, cells samples were collected and then loaded with DCFH-DA probe. Subsequently, flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, America) was employed to analyze positive cells for DCFH-DA positive cell rate calculation.

Immunofluorescence staining

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in HG-DMEM (Solarbio, China) supplemented with FBS (10 %) and penicillin-streptomycin (1 %) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 environment. Then, the cells were induced with lipopolysaccharide (1 μg/mL; LPS; Solarbio, China) and cultured with different hydrogels [43,44]. LPS-induced RAW 264.7 cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4 %). Then, HIF-1α, CD86, and CD206 primary antibodies (Affinity, America) were diluted at a 1:500 (v/v) ratio and cultured with the cells overnight. The secondary antibody (Affinity, America) was incubated with these samples for 60 min. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were obtained and subsequently analyzed via ImageJ.

Transwell assay

HUVECs (1.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded on the upper chamber supplied with basic HG-DMEM and macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) from different hydrogels. After co-culturing for 24 h, the upper chamber was stained with crystal violet solution (0.1 %) to observe HUVEC migration. Migration images were obtained via an optical microscope (Leica, Germany), and the number of migrated HUVECs in the upper chamber was calculated via ImageJ.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs (2 × 104 cells) were seeded into a 96-well plate coated with Matrigel matrix (50 μL/well; Corning, America), and subsequently treated with different hydrogel MCM for 4 h. Then, tube formation of HUVECs was observed, and the number of nodes was calculated via ImageJ.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity analysis

The osteogenic medium containing LG-DMEM (80 %), FBS (10 %), penicillin-streptomycin (1 %), ascorbate (50 μM); β-sodium glycerophosphate (10 mM), and dexamethasone (100 nM) was mixed with MCM of different hydrogels at a volume ratio of 2:1 for subsequent experiments [43]. After culturing with the osteogenic medium for 14 days, BMSCs were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4 %, 30 min). Then, BMSCs were stained in the dark for 30 min using 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate/nitro blue tetrazolium (BCIP/NBT) for quantitative analysis of ALP activity. Additionally, the protein concentration was normalized via the bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Beyotime, China). All procedures were concluded according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Alizarin red-S (ARS) staining

To observe mineralization, BMSCs (3 × 104 cells/well) were stained with ARS (Solarbio, China) in the extracellular matrix, and corresponding images were obtained on an optical microscope. Then, mineralized nodules were dissolved in cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC; 10 %; Solarbio, China) for monitoring the absorbance at 562 nm via a microplate reader.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed to detect the expression of inflammation-related genes, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) in LPS-induced RAW 264.7 cells, as well as osteogenesis-related genes, including runt-related transcription factor-2 (RUNX-2), ALP, Osteocalcin (OCN), and collagen-I (COL-I) in BMSCs. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIZOL (Yeason, China), followed by reverse transcription into cDNA. Then, RT-qPCR assay was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China). Gene expression levels were quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the reference housekeeping gene. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Bone defect model establishment and therapeutic effect assessment

The critical calvarium defects (5 mm diameter) of male Sprague-Dawley rats were established to evaluate the healing efficacy in vivo. Twenty model rats were randomly divided into 4 groups: DEXMA, RGD@DEXMA, RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24, and RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4. After implanting different hydrogels for 8 weeks, Micro-CT (SCANCO, Switzerland) was employed to observe the bone regeneration processes at the defect sites, and the volume ratio between bone tissue (BV/TV) was quantitatively analyzed. Then, the samples were fixed using paraformaldehyde (4 %) and decalcified using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (15 %; EDTA; Servicebio, China). To assess the bone regeneration performance, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was employed. Additionally, the collagen deposition levels in the newly formed bone were evaluated using Masson staining. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to detect the TNF-α and IL-10 expressions in vivo. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect OCN and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression. Positive staining areas of these cytokines were evaluated via ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed and represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical analyses were calculated using GraphPad Prism software 8.0.1. Student's t-test was performed when only two groups were being compared. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Turkey's multiple comparison tests was conducted for comparisons involving more than two groups. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, ns: no significant difference.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel

Dextran, with excellent biodegradability, serves as an ideal polymer matrix to ensure biosecurity. To avoid the use of toxic crosslinking agents such as glutaraldehyde, the hydrogel was synthesized by DEXMA upon 405 nm UV light irradiation for 30 s. SEM images revealed that the hydrogel possessed porous structures with an average pore size of 96.8 ± 21.8 μm (Figs. 1A and S1), which is favorable for cell adhesion. As shown in Fig. S2, Mn3O4 nanozymes exhibited a zeta potential of −9.33 ± 0.27 mV and an intensity-weighted hydrodynamic diameter of 103 ± 18.8 nm as determined by DLS, suggesting that they remained well dispersed and homogeneous in aqueous medium after sonication. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) analysis showed a characteristic C=O vibration at 1732 cm−1, along with a moderate intensity signal around 1646 cm−1 corresponding to the unreacted C=C stretching vibrations, indicating the unreacted methacrylate groups of DEXMA hydrogels (Fig. 1B). This offered the feasibility of grafting RGD onto DEXMA (RGD@DEXMA) hydrogels through an additional photochemical reaction. Amide I (1646 cm−1) and amide II (1540 cm−1) of DOPA-P24, and the O-Mn-O vibration (609 cm−1) from Mn3O4 suggested the successful incorporation of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 into the hydrogel [45,46].

To fabricate the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel, the functional components DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes were premixed with DEXMA, followed by UV light irradiation. As illustrated in Figs. 1A, 1E, and S1, the synthesized composite hydrogels maintained an ideal three-dimensional (3D) network with uniform pore structures (96.8 ± 17.8 μm), suggesting that the incorporation of functional components had an almost negligible effect on the porosity of the hydrogels. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealed the distribution of elements, such as C, N, O, P, and Mn (Figs. 1C, S3, and S4). Among them, P originated from the DOPA-P24 peptide (S[PO4] KIPKASSVPTELSAISTLYLDDD), and Mn was solely from the Mn3O4 nanozymes. Mn3O4 exhibited an integrated hexagonal nanoplate morphology with a particle size of 80.9 ± 15.9 nm (Fig. S4D). The featured X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the tetragonal system of Mn3O4 nanozymes [JCPDS card No. 24-0734, I41/and (141)] (Fig. 1D). These results confirmed the co-loading of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes in the composite hydrogels.

The release behavior of osteogenic agents within the scaffold is directly related to its biological role in bone defect healing. Therefore, the release of DOPA-P24 from the hydrogel was investigated. As shown in Fig. 1F, direct loading of P24 in the DEXMA hydrogel (DEXMA/P24) exhibited burst release during the initial 3 days, with approximately 20 % being released by the 15th day. To modulate the release behavior, P24 was replaced by DOPA-P24, whose catechol groups establish non-covalent interactions with the polymer matrix [28]. Thereby, the release of DOPA-P24 was significantly decreased. The gentle release behavior, providing prolonged duration, is more suitable for the osteogenic process. Furthermore, in the presence of α-dextranase, both DEXMA and RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogels degraded gradually over time. No obvious difference in the degradation rate was observed, and approximately 85 % of hydrogels degraded after 21 days (Fig. 1G). As shown in Fig. 1H, Mn ions released continuously from the hydrogels and stabilized after day 7, indicating controlled degradation without burst release. Fig. 1I and 1J, the compressive strength followed the trend: RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 (0.057 ± 0.004 kPa) > RGD@DEXMA (0.047 ± 0.003 kPa) > DEXMA (0.035 ± 0.001 kPa). The improvement in RGD@DEXMA may be attributed to extended UV irradiation during RGD grafting with increased crosslinking [47,48]. Further enhancement in RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 is likely due to Mn3O4 forming hydrogen and coordination bonds with hydroxyl groups in DEXMA and acting as a nanofiller to effectively disperse stress and enhance mechanical properties [49,50].

Biocompatibility assessment

For bone healing, BMSCs must undergo recruitment, adhesion, and proliferation onto the scaffolds, and ultimately differentiate into osteoblasts [51,52]. Each stage requires high biocompatibility for BTE scaffolds. To preliminarily determine the optimal concentrations of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes in the hydrogel, live/dead staining was performed to observe their cytotoxicity. As depicted in Figs. S5 and S6, cytotoxicity effects began to emerge at concentrations of 250 μg/mL for DOPA-P24 and 125 μg/mL for Mn3O4, with an increased mortality rate of dead RAW 264.7 cells on the hydrogel surfaces. The CCK-8 assay exhibited a similar trend (Figs. S7 and S8). Based on the results, the concentrations of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 were set at 200 μg/mL and 100 μg/mL, respectively, for subsequent experiments. Furtherly, hemolysis and cytoskeleton staining assays were also carried out to confirm the biocompatibility of the hydrogels. Briefly, BMSCs were seeded on the surfaces of different scaffolds, and the live/dead cell staining with predominant living cells indicated negligible cytotoxicity of all hydrogels (Fig. 2A and C). Moreover, the hemolysis assay demonstrated rates below 5 % for all hydrogels, which is well within the safety limitations (Fig. S9).

Cytoskeleton staining was performed to further detect the effect of hydrogels on BMSCs adhesion. As shown in Fig. 2B and D BMSCs spread better in the RGD@DEXMA than in pure DEXMA hydrogel. Cellular adhesion was further enhanced in the presence of DOPA-P24. Increased cellular number and adhesion area of BMSCs were observed for both RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24 and RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogels. This improvement is attributed to the grafted RGD sequences binding integrin receptors, thereby initiating adhesion-, growth- and proliferation-related pathways, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) [53]. On the other hand, P24 exhibited a stronger effect on the adhesion and proliferation properties of BMSCs than BMP-2, due to its smaller molecular weight and exposed activity sites [27,54]. The CCK-8 assay validated the cytoskeleton staining outcomes, exhibiting continuous increases in cellular metabolic activity over 1, 4, and 7 days (Fig. 2E). The increase in metabolic activity of RGD@DEXMA hydrogels was higher than that of DEXMA hydrogels, with the highest increment of hydrogels containing both RGD and DOPA-P24, with or without Mn3O4 nanozymes. Therefore, the grafted RGD and the sustained release of DOPA-P24 synergistically promote the adhesion and proliferation of BMSCs, providing a favorable platform for further osteogenic differentiation.

Anti-oxidant stress injury property assessment

The accumulation of ROS at bone defect sites induces oxidative stress injury and exacerbates inflammation [55]. To assess the ROS-eliminating capacity of different hydrogels, •OH and DPPH scavenging were performed. As shown in Figs. S10-S13, there was no significant difference in the radical scavenging rates between the DEXMA and RGD@DEXMA groups. However, the incorporation of DOPA-P24 slightly increased the scavenging rate, likely due to the catechol groups in DOPA, which can eliminate ROS by converting into quinone groups [43,56]. More importantly, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel demonstrated the highest •OH and DPPH scavenging rates, exceeding 70 %. Mn3O4 nanozymes have been proven to scavenge ROS through the redox cycling between Mn2+ and Mn3+, suppressing the intracellular ROS levels [44,57]. As shown in Figs. S14 and S15, Mn3O4 exhibited dose-dependent catalase (CAT)-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities (0–100 μg/mL). Regarding the particle size, we have already provided the statistical analysis in the Supporting Information (Fig. S4A and S4D). The size distribution of Mn3O4 based on SEM images was 80.9 ± 15.9 nm. DHE staining was employed to detect the intracellular ROS elimination capacity of different hydrogels. Consistent with previous results, both the positive staining area and the fluorescence intensity according to flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 3A and C) were sharply decreased in H2O2-induced RAW 264.7 cells treated by the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel (Fig. 3A and C). As shown in DCFH-DA staining, the obvious decrease in positive staining area and intensity in RAW 264.7 cells incubated with the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel indicated the significant reductions in total intracellular ROS levels under the same treatment (Fig. 3B, 3D, and S16). These results demonstrate that the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel can alleviate oxidative stress injury by efficiently eliminating intracellular ROS, thereby possessing the potential to protect bone tissue from severe inflammatory damage.

Immunoregulation property assessment in vitro

Immunoregulation is crucial for bone tissue healing, as reducing inflammatory responses prevents tissue from damage [55]. The transition of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype is commonly regarded as a hallmark of inflammation relief. To investigate the immunoregulation through macrophage polarization, the expression of CD86 (M1 marker, Fig. 4A) and CD206 (M2 marker, Fig. 4B) was observed via immunofluorescence staining. Consistent with the gene expression results, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group exhibited the lowest positive expression area of CD86 (Fig. 4C), while significantly increased CD206 expression (Fig. 4D).

Moreover, RT-qPCR was conducted to assess the expression of M1 biomarkers (TNF-α and IL-1β) and M2 biomarkers (TGF-β and IL-10). The expression levels of TNF-α (Fig. 4E) and IL-1β (Fig. 4F) in the LPS-induced RAW 264.7 cells exhibited negligible differences between the DEXMA and RGD@DEXMA groups. While the incorporation of DOPA-P24 partially suppressed these pro-inflammatory biomarkers, and the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group exhibited the lowest expression levels. In contrast, anti-inflammatory biomarkers TGF-β (Fig. 4G) and IL-10 (Fig. 4H) were significantly upregulated in the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group compared with the other groups. Previous studies have demonstrated that LPS stimulation induces excessive intracellular ROS in macrophages, which promotes macrophage polarization towards pro-inflammation M1 phenotype by upregulating HIF-1α and activating the glycolysis pathway [43,58]. Furthermore, the expression level of HIF-1α in LPS-induced RAW 264.7 cells was significantly down-regulated when treated with the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel (Fig. S17). This demonstrated the ROS scavenging capability of Mn3O4 nanozymes in hydrogels through the Mn2+/Mn3+ redox cycle, potentially generating sufficient intracellular O2 to improve the hypoxic microenvironment and suppress HIF-1α expression, thereby alleviating inflammation [44,57]. These results indicate that the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel exhibits excellent immunoregulatory capability through efficient macrophage polarization, thereby providing a favorable microenvironment for angiogenesis and osteogenesis.

Enhancement of angiogenesis and osteogenesis via immunoregulation in vitro

The polarization of macrophages towards M2 is crucial for coordinating angiogenesis and osteogenesis [39,59,60]. Bone tissue is highly vascularized, requiring angiogenesis to support the process of osteogenesis. To reveal this interaction, HUVECs were employed to evaluate the angiogenic effect of MCM treated via different hydrogels. Transwell assay demonstrated that the number of migrated HUVECs in the upper chamber was the highest in the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group (Fig. 5A). It may result from the secretion of angiogenesis-related cytokines such as VEGF from M2 macrophages [38], facilitating the migration, proliferation, and subsequent angiogenesis of HUVECs (Fig. 5C). As shown in Fig. 5A and D, the tube formation assay also confirmed that the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group formed the highest amount of branch nodes (marked as a yellow arrow), approximately 4 times greater than that in the DEXMA group. We further assess the enhanced osteogenic effects via immunoregulation of different hydrogels, ARS staining, and RT-qPCR. As depicted in Fig. 5B, the blue-purple positive ALP staining areas were significantly larger in hydrogels incorporating DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4. The RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel exhibited a nearly 3.5-fold increase in ALP activity compared with bare DEXMA hydrogel (Fig. 5E). Additionally, ARS staining exhibited the highest quantity of orange-red calcium nodules in the same group, suggesting enhanced mineralization in extracellular matrix (Fig. 5B and F). According to western blot (WB) assay, both RUNX-2 and COL1a1 in BMSC were significantly up-regulated when treated with RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 (Fig. S18), which was consistent with RT-qPCR results.

In addition to angiogenic cytokines, osteogenesis-related factors, particularly BMP-2, are simultaneously secreted within the local environment during M2 polarization [39]. BMP-2 triggered the expression of osteogenic-related genes, such as RUNX-2, ALP, and OCN, facilitating the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts [12]. Moreover, P24, an osteogenic peptide enriched in aspartic acid and phosphorylated serine, significantly enhances the osteogenesis of BMSCs and promotes the biomineralization of hydroxyapatite [61]. Hence, we evaluated the expression of osteogenic-related genes, including RUNX-2, ALP, OCN, and COL-I via RT-qPCR. These genes were significantly up-regulated in BMSCs treated with MCM from the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group (Fig. 5G–J). This evidence confirmed that the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel could activate the enhancement of angiogenesis and osteogenesis via immunoregulation, which may be conducive to guiding bone regeneration in vivo.

Therapeutic effect on bone defect in vivo

In vitro experimental results have demonstrated the capability of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel to modulate the immune microenvironment, promoting its osteogenic effect on BMSCs and angiogenic effect on HUVECs. It should be a novel candidate to accelerate bone healing. Bone defect models were established, and different hydrogels were implanted into the defect sites. Micro-CT was employed to observe new bone formation at the defect sites (Fig. 6A). After 8 weeks, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group exhibited more newly forming bone (the gray area in the red circle) than the DEXMA, RGD@DEXMA, and RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24 groups. The BV/TV ratio calculated from micro-CT suggested a slight increase in the RGD@DEXMA hydrogel group compared with the DEXMA hydrogel (Fig. 6B and C) [62]. The incorporation of DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes significantly increased the BV/TV ratio, consistent with the in vitro results. To evaluate bone healing at the histological level, H&E staining was conducted with newly formed bone staining as pink-red. As depicted in Fig. 6C, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 group displayed the highest amount of newly formed bone tissue and the narrowest defect gap compared with other groups, consistent with the imaging evaluation results. Collagen deposition, another vital indicator for bone healing, was also assessed through Masson staining. As shown in Figs. 6B and S19, collagen deposited in bone tissue was stained blue, and the deposited area at the defect site was significantly increased in the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel group. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, bone trabecular number (Tb.N) and thickness (Tb.Th) were significantly increased at the defect site after implantation of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel, approaching the levels of the Sham group. These results are consistent with the imaging and BV/TV data, indicating that newly formed bone possessed more denser structures after RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel treatment. In addition, H&E staining of major organs (Fig. S20A) and analysis of residual Mn ions (Fig. S20B) were investigated to confirm the efficiently metabolism and favorable biocompatibility of Mn3O4 nanozymes.

Inflammation and tissue regeneration-related cytokines expression in vivo

To further investigate the regeneration mechanism, we evaluated the in vivo expression of inflammation and tissue regeneration-related cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-10, VEGF, and OCN (Fig. 7A and B). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that the positive area of TNF-α in the bone defects was significantly decreased by approximately 80 % after being treated with the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel (Fig. 7C), while the IL-10 expression represented a converse tendency and increased by about 400 % (Fig. 7D). These in vivo results further verified the immunoregulation property of Mn3O4 nanozyme incorporated hydrogel. Then, the expression of angiogenic factor VEGF and osteogenic factor OCN was detected via immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7E and G). The immunofluorescence positive staining area and intensity level of VEGF were significantly enlarged for the defects implanted with the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel (Fig. 7E). Accordingly, the expression of CD31 (Fig. 7F) and OCN (Fig. 7G) in bone defect was remarkably up-regulated via the treatment of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel. These in vivo data confirmed the effectiveness of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel in immunoregulation, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis effects. Thus, the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel has been proven to be a novel BTE scaffold with great potential for bone regeneration.

Conclusions

In this study, a multifunctional composite hydrogel was constructed by incorporating DOPA-P24 and Mn3O4 nanozymes into DEXMA hydrogel, followed by RGD modification. This composite hydrogel exhibited effective therapeutic effects on the bone defect healing process. The enhanced osteo-inductive property, resulting from the RGD sequence, facilitated cellular adhesion and proliferation of BMSCs, while the optimized sustained release of P24 provided long-term induction of osteogenic differentiation. Moreover, the immunoregulation mediated by Mn3O4 nanozymes and residual DOPA efficiently scavenged intracellular ROS and polarized macrophages from M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which led to a favorable immune microenvironment for enhanced angiogenesis, further supporting osteogenesis, and establishing a robust foundation for accelerated critical bone healing in vivo. This work revealed the immunoregulatory properties of the RGD@DEXMA/DOPA-P24/Mn3O4 hydrogel in activating bone healing, presenting a promising strategy for synergistic immunomodulation and osteogenesis in bone regeneration.

Despite these encouraging findings, several challenges remain to be addressed for future clinical translation. First, large-scale preparation often suffered from batch-to-batch inconsistency due to process variations and environmental sensitivity. A feasible approach is transforming hydrogels into powders after large-scale production and reconstituting them when needed. Due to the light-dependent preparation of DEXMA hydrogel, strict control and optimization of conditions are essential. Second, DOPA-P24 exhibits strong osteogenic efficiency, and its applications could be further expanded if production costs can be reduced. Finally, the good biocompatibility and low metabolic toxicity of Mn3O4 nanozymes have been confirmed in vitro and in vivo. Future validation in more clinically relevant disease models will be critical to substantiate therapeutic efficacy and promote clinical translation.
