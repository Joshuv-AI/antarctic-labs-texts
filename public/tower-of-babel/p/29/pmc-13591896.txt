Meeting the primary care needs of communities where they are: mobile primary care clinics, test and try evaluation, Nova Scotia Canada

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary care is crucial for reducing health disparities and enhancing population health. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated long-standing challenges for Canada in maintaining robust primary care; however, it also presented opportunities for health system transformation. One such transformation was the rapid implementation of Mobile Primary Care Clinics (MPCCs) in the Atlantic Canadian province of Nova Scotia to enhance healthcare accessibility and alleviate pressure on emergency departments (EDs). This evaluation aimed to assess the impact, implementation, and partner experiences of MPCCs as another point of primary care access for Nova Scotians.MethodsThe evaluation involved a mixed-methods approach (September 2022–October 2023) guided by implementation outcomes of effectiveness, efficiency, acceptability, and feasibility. Data sources included clinic utilization and cost records, administrative data, patient and provider surveys, and a questionnaire from the implementation team. A scenario-based economic analysis with probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was performed from a third-party payer perspective. ED diversion estimates were derived by combining survey-reported counterfactual care-seeking behaviour with observed ED utilization within 72 h of an MPCC visit, identified through linkage to provincial administrative data.ResultsAcross 157 clinic days, 13,019 visits were recorded. The most common presentations were respiratory or dermatological complaints and prescription renewals. Among 1,888 patient survey respondents, 23.7% would have sought ED care, and 7.2% would have gone untreated had an MPCC not been available. ED utilization within 72 h of an MPCC visit occurred in 13.7% of visits. The mean cost per visit was CAD $103 for general primary care clinics and $236 for upper respiratory infection clinics. Scenario-based estimates of potential fiscal offsets from survey-reported ED diversions were $1.95 million (95% CI $0.77–$3.64 million). Surveyed patients (97%) and providers (99%) rated the clinics favorably, citing timely access and interdisciplinary teamwork. Implementation enablers included adequate staffing, clear roles and strong interagency partnerships; key barriers were limited EMR integration and inconsistent public awareness.ConclusionsPreliminary findings show MPCCs have made a positive impact by enabling access to primary care in multiple communities across the province. Estimates of ED diversion-related fiscal offsets are illustrative and should be validated with linked administrative data. As operational planning and integration with primary care continue, ongoing work should address continuity of care, efficiency, equity, and sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03442-y.

Background

Primary care stands as the most significant factor in diminishing health disparities and enhancing the overall health of populations, serving as the core of effective health systems [1]. Evidence indicates that having access to primary care is linked to more equitable health outcomes across different nations [2]. However, the Canadian context reveals the challenges of maintaining robust primary care services: 16% of Canadians lack a regular family physician—a statistic unchanged since July 2020. Those without consistent access to a family physician are subject to fewer diagnostic tests, reduced specialist referrals, and an increased risk of mortality from serious illnesses compared to individuals with regular healthcare access [1, 3].

Various factors hinder access to primary care in Canada [4]. A survey by The Commonwealth Fund revealed that 39% of Canadians resorted to emergency departments (EDs) for issues that could be treatable by a primary care physician, had they had access to one [5]. The growth rate of family physicians in this country is also alarmingly low, especially considering over one-third of current practitioners are above 60 years old [1, 3].

The COVID-19 pandemic further complicated matters, causing staffing shortages and reducing chronic care management. EDs, overwhelmed during the pandemic, have faced unprecedented pressure [6–8]. However, the pandemic also revealed opportunities for health system transformation [9]. Internationally, health systems are employing creative strategies to enhance primary care access. These include technology integration whereby consultations and triage can be offered through virtual or digital platforms and new care models that move services closer to home [10–13]. Expanded pharmacy, physiotherapy, and team- or mobile-based services are emerging [10, 14]. Still, workforce, financial, technological, and political sustainability remain important considerations for these new approaches. [11, 12].

In the Canadian context, it is important to distinguish between “attachment” and “access.” Provider-patient attachment refers to the confirmed and documented affiliation between a patient and a primary care provider, care team, or practice [15]. Within the Atlantic Canadian province of Nova Scotia (population approximately 1 million), a significant proportion of citizens remain unattached to a regular primary care provider, with system strain arising when individuals with healthcare needs are unable to access timely care in the most appropriate setting. This was particularly evident during the pandemic, when challenges in accessing primary care for acute or chronic conditions pushed many patients toward EDs.

Mobile clinics are a model of care that can promote first-contact, accessible, and coordinated care, particularly for populations facing barriers to traditional primary care; evidence supports that mobile clinics provide access to fundamental primary care services [16, 17]. Mobile clinics are acknowledged by the World Health Organization as, “a good illustration of the tension between equity of access and the efficient utilization of scarce human resources” [18].

Since the pandemic, there has been renewed interest in using mobile clinics to bring care closer to communities. Malone et al. found that mobile clinics in the United States serve as an important link between clinical facilities and the community, and by doing so, can improve access [19]. By helping reduce obstacles to healthcare (e.g., transportation challenges, time constraints, and system complexity) and enhancing accessibility beyond traditional healthcare settings, mobile clinics were deemed valuable in advancing health equity and reaching the most vulnerable and disenfranchised populations [19–21]. There is promise for mobile clinics to provide individualized, accessible healthcare services at less cost than those at EDs, reducing unnecessary ED visits, and overall healthcare costs. Despite the promise mobile clinics have to reduce costs, some studies show that, in contrast to other delivery methods, mobile clinics can be costly [22]. They can be difficult to operate, take a lot of time to plan and deploy, and may be unsuitable for treating chronic illnesses due to challenges with continuity of care [18, 23–25].

Building on international evidence and local experience with mobile public health clinics deployed during the COVID-19 pandemic, Nova Scotia implemented Mobile Primary Care Clinics (MPCCs) as an additional access point to address persistent gaps in primary care. This paper presents early findings from a mixed-methods evaluation of the MPCC model, examining its performance in practice and its potential to improve timely access to care and reduce avoidable emergency department use.

Nova Scotia context

Enhancing primary care access in Nova Scotia continues to be a priority for the province [26]. As of December 1 st, 2023, there were 148,512 unattached Nova Scotians (15.0% of the Nova Scotia population) for myriad reasons, which include being new to the area (34.7%); their provider having moved (24.8%), having retired (20.2%), or retiring (10.4%), or not requiring a provider in the past (9.7%) [26]. As of May 2025, the percentage of unattached Nova Scotians was 8.6% or 91,474 Nova Scotians.

In Nova Scotia, there are many strategies being implemented to enhance access to primary care. The Nova Scotia MPCCs were designed and implemented as one such targeted response to the access to primary care problems. Other initiatives include Virtual Care Nova Scotia, which enables residents to consult with providers through online platforms, and YourHealthNS, a mobile application designed to improve navigation, access, and continuity of care. Together, these initiatives, alongside the MPCCs, form part of a broader provincial strategy to provide timely and flexible access to primary care services while alleviating pressure on emergency departments and traditional family practice settings [26]. MPCCs were initially implemented in September 2022 as a “test and try” initiative (i.e. a trialed initiative with an evaluation) through the Nova Scotia Health Innovation Hub to bridge a gap in primary care services in communities impacted by Hurricane Fiona [27]. Since fulfilling this initial purpose, mobile primary care clinics have scaled across the province. MPCCs are offered in communities across all four Nova Scotia Health (NSH) management zones: Central, Eastern, Northern, and Western. MPCCs in NSH provide an immediate, flexible solution to communities where unmet healthcare needs were generating avoidable ED visits and exacerbating health system pressures for both attached patients and patients without a regular primary care provider. The underlying hypothesis was that MPCCs would expand timely access to essential primary care in communities of need, including underserved communities and thereby alleviating ED burden while improving patient and community experiences of care.

Operations

The term “mobile” refers to the deployment of care teams who temporarily operate in different communities using existing infrastructure such as community health centres, schools, or municipal buildings. They operate primarily during weekends and evenings at existing clinical and community spaces (such as schools, community centers, and health facilities) (Fig. 1). These stationary, short-term clinics are rapidly set up and disassembled based on local need, and aim to relieve pressure on EDs, particularly during respiratory surges. Services provided include assessment and treatment of minor illnesses, prescription renewals, chronic disease management, basic assessments, and referrals when indicated. While point-of-care testing (e.g., rapid strep, COVID-19) was available, MPCCs are not equipped for onsite diagnostic imaging or routine bloodwork. Urgent or life-threatening concerns are not addressed at these clinics. Patients who visit MPCC may be attached or unattached to a primary care provider. Attached patients visit these clinics if they are unable to visit their health care providers in a timely manner. No structured follow-up is provided by the MPCCs themselves, and patients do not have ongoing access to a dedicated clinic phone line; instead, patients are advised to seek ongoing care through their primary care provider (if attached) or to access alternative provincial programs such as Virtual Care Nova Scotia or community pharmacies. To support the continuity of care, patient care records are shared with relevant providers post-visit for MPCC patients who are attached.

A variety of options for appointments, including drop-in and pre-booked, have been employed at MPCCs. Information on MPCCs, including appointment types, operating hours, and location is available to the public online [28]. To support broader engagement, a variety of communication strategies have been used including public service announcements and announcements through local community trusted channels.

The MPCCs operate in two main formats: general Primary Care Clinics (PCCs) and Upper Respiratory Infection (URI) Clinics. While both aim to alleviate pressures on EDs and improve access to care, they differ in scope, services, and use cases. PCCs provide non-emergent care for minor ailments (e.g., rashes, infections, musculoskeletal issues), prescription refills (excluding controlled substances), basic musculoskeletal care, and mental health check-ins. They serve both attached and unattached patients and may offer health education or referrals. URIs focus on acute respiratory symptoms and seasonal surges (e.g., cold, flu, COVID-19). Services include rapid testing and symptom management. Public health staff may be integrated based on local need. The decision to assign a clinic as a PCC or URI clinic is based on population needs, seasonal trends, and health system pressures. Decisions are made collaboratively by NSH, with input from public health, EDs, and community health leadership. Clinic type and focus may vary across sites and weeks, with URI clinics deployed more often during respiratory virus surges.

MPCCs are typically staffed by an interprofessional team composed of family physicians, nurse practitioners, emergency support aides, paramedics, and administrative and clerical support. A unique feature of the MPCC model is the inclusion of on-site clinical team leads to support operations and management along with the integration of public health and mental health professionals based on community need. While MPCCs are primarily focused on non-urgent primary care, they may occasionally integrate public health services such as offering COVID-19 rapid tests, measles testing, or health education materials based on local needs and in collaboration with the public health team. However, routine public health services like vaccinations are generally not provided through MPCCs. Providers were remunerated through sessional stipends arranged by NSH, rather than fee-for-service billing. This model was designed to encourage participation by providers such as physicians and nurse practitioners and ensure predictable compensation regardless of patient volume.

The population sizes of communities served by MPCCs ranged from very small rural sites such as Canso (population < 1,000) and Neil’s Harbour (< 500) to larger urban centres such as Dartmouth and Kentville (populations > 20,000) (See Fig. 1 and Table 4 in the Results for more details). Presenting results by community is a key aspect of the current evaluation’s scope because MPCCs were purposefully created to be responsive to local variation in access and system pressures throughout Nova Scotia. Community-level differences in uptake and utilization can show how small rural sites (e.g., Neil’s Harbour, Canso) compare to larger urban centres (e.g., Dartmouth, Kentville) and frame the reach of MPCCs in relation to population size and geography. The inclusion of these results also demonstrates how the model was responsive and adaptive to various local contexts, whether per-capita impacts were more pronounced in certain rural communities, and allows policy-makers to consider effectiveness in the context of multiple settings. Although summary results are important to the evaluation’s goal of demonstrating provincial impact, results by community are also responsive to its aim of determining whether MPCCs improved access equitably and responsively across diverse settings.

Methods

Study design and objectives

This mixed-methods evaluation aimed to assess the impact, implementation, and interest-holder perspectives of MPCCs in Nova Scotia. Specifically, the evaluation aimed to determine whether MPCCs achieved their intended purpose of alleviating access challenges in primary care and reducing avoidable ED use by (1) assessing their impact on healthcare access, service utilization, ED diversion, and economic outcomes; and (2) examining implementation processes, operational feasibility, and interest-holder experiences across diverse community contexts to identify barriers, facilitators, and lessons for sustainability and potential scale-up. This mixed-methods evaluation thus sought not only to measure outcomes but also to critically assess whether MPCCs addressed the core access problem they were designed to solve. The evaluation was conducted across all four NSH management zones from September 2022 to October 2023, unless otherwise specified.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected through on-site research assistants using patient surveys, daily clinic summaries, and provincial health card information. Data elements included de-identified demographics, clinic zone, patient attachment status, reason for visit, number of patients seen, COVID-19-related information, self-reported ED diversion, and follow-up ED use. These data were aggregated into monthly reports by a PhD-trained researcher and used to assess trends in access and utilization.

Qualitative data were collected through surveys administered to patients, providers, and implementation team members. Patient surveys focused on satisfaction and perceived access, explicitly to determine whether the MPCC model achieved its intended access-related objectives. Patient and provider surveys were administered immediately after clinic visits and shifts, respectively, allowing real-time monitoring and improvement of MPCC operations. Implementation team surveys were conducted later in the evaluation period (January 2024). Both provider and implementation team surveys explored experiences, barriers, and enablers to clinic delivery. Together, these data sources enabled triangulation across evaluation domains.

All data were collected using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure online survey platform, with strict protocols to ensure confidentiality. Participation was voluntary, and completion of surveys implied consent. This project was reviewed by the NSH Research Ethics Board (File #: 1030321) and deemed a quality improvement initiative, therefore exempt from full research ethics review in accordance with institutional policy.

Measures

To ensure alignment with evaluation objectives, measures were organized across two domains: 1) impact, and 2) implementation and interest-holder perspectives. Multiple data sources were used to enable triangulation.

Impact

Impact measures assessed the extent to which MPCCs improved access to care and influenced health system utilization. Quantitative indicators included patient volumes, attachment status, reasons for visit, follow-up ED usage, and self-reported likelihood of ED use in the absence of MPCC services, and economic outcomes. These measures were derived from patient surveys, administrative clinic data, and linked provincial administrative data where available.

Implementation and interest-holder perspectives

Implementation and interest-holder perspective measures examined the feasibility, consistency, operational characteristics, and perceived value of MPCC delivery. Indicators included clinic setup and delivery processes, staffing models, service volumes over time, patient satisfaction, perceived quality and timeliness of care, provider-reported experiences related to workflow and collaboration, and implementation-team perspectives on barriers and enablers. These measures were derived from patient, provider, and implementation-team surveys and were used to assess how the model functioned across diverse contexts and to identify factors influencing successful delivery.

Economic analysis method

A cost–benefit analysis was conducted. The analysis was conducted from a third-party payer perspective. This perspective aligns with the structure of Canada’s publicly funded health care system, in which provincial governments are responsible for financing physician and primary care services. Under this model, insured patients access mobile clinic services without direct out-of-pocket costs, and the analysis focuses on costs borne by the health system. The analysis proceeded in two stages using data up to October 2023 [29]. The analysis included two components:Cost per Visit Estimation: Costs were calculated based on staffing (salaries and benefits), supplies, space rental, and travel. Staff categories included nurse practitioners, registered nurses, emergency support aides, administrative assistants, and clinical team leads. Wage data were sourced from publicly available Nova Scotia compensation schedules, with a 24% benefit rate applied. Visit volumes were drawn from administrative records managed by Nova Scotia Health’s Performance and Analytics team, a department that serves as the steward of the province’s health administrative data.ED Diversion Savings: ED diversion estimates were generated by applying a survey-derived proportion of respondents who reported they would have sought care in the ED in the absence of MPCC services to total MPCC visit counts, producing an estimate of potential (counterfactual) ED demand. Observed ED utilization within 72 h of an MPCC visit was identified through linkage of MPCC visit records to the provincial ED database and subtracted to estimate net ED diversions. Because the counterfactual component is derived from patient self-report and not validated using administrative data, these estimates should be interpreted as scenario-based (illustrative) cost offsets rather than definitive system-level savings. This approach assumes that survey respondents are representative of the broader MPCC visit population. The cost of an ED visit was based on standard NSH costs; this was compared to the MPCC per-visit cost to estimate savings, using 2022 Canadian dollars.

All cost and utilization estimates were subjected to probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to quantify uncertainty [30]. Input variables were modeled using appropriate distributions: gamma for right-skewed cost and visit data, and beta for proportions. Monte Carlo simulation with 1000 iterations was performed to generate distributions of outcomes. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (95% CI) were constructed using the percentile method from the results of the PSA.

Results

Results are presented according to two evaluation domains: impact and implementation/interest-holder perspectives, aligned with the study objectives. Each section synthesizes key findings from quantitative and qualitative data sources to provide a focused assessment of MPCC performance. The findings are also intended to inform future planning and growth of these clinics in Nova Scotia and to generate broader lessons for Canada.

Impact

Clinic activity and outreach

Between September 2022 and October 2023, MPCCs were deployed throughout the province as shown in Fig. 1. The results encompass data from a total of 157 clinic days, accounting for 13,019 patient visits. It is important to note that the total number of visits does not represent unique patients.

Trends of when and how many clinic days occurred are summarized in Fig. 2. Clinics have been held on a regular basis since they started in September 2022 and on average there have been 12 clinics held each month. Each clinic had an average of 80 visits per day; the number of visits varies greatly based on location (see Table 4 under Economic Findings). The Central Zone clinics had the highest visit volume per clinic with Cobequid and Tantallon having an average of 172 and 153 visits per clinic day, respectively, from September 2022 to October 2023 (n = 13,019). ED diversion and unmet needs

Patients were asked in the survey where they would have sought care if the MPCC had not been available (Table 2). Most commonly, respondents stated they would have gone to the local ED for care instead (23.7%, n = 447). Of interest, 137 (7.2%) respondents stated they would have gone untreated, and an additional 168 (8.9%) indicated they did not know where they would have sought care. These results indicate that the MPCCs have successfully created a place of care for unattached Nova Scotians. Economic findings

Overall, the mean cost per visit for primary care clinics was $103 (95% CI $66—$153), and $236 for URI clinics (95% CI $154—$345), but this varied significantly by site and population size (Table 4). From Table 4, three patterns emerge. Primary-care clinics handling ≥ 100 encounters per day (Cobequid, Kentville, Mumford, Tantallon) recorded the lowest costs, clustered around $81 per visit (95% CI $53–$118). Mid-volume clinics (45–60 visits/day) had a mean cost of $120 (95% CI $80– $176), while very low-volume rural sites (< 40 visits/day; Amherst, Canso, Inverness, Neil’s Harbour, Springhill, Strait Richmond) incurred $187 (95% CI $120– $272). Second, the two dedicated URI clinics (Sydney and Yarmouth) exhibited the highest mean costs of $236 (95% CI $154– $345). Figure 3 situates each site against its catchment population size. The high-cost, low-volume clinics predominantly serve sparsely populated coastal or rural communities. The analysis indicates that urban sites with larger populations benefit from economies of scale and greater resource availability, leading to lower per-visit costs. Implementation and interest-holder perspectives

Implementation was assessed primarily through patient, provider, and implementation team experience surveys. Results from these surveys are presented here, including implementation barriers and enablers affecting operational feasibility from the experiences of those who delivered, planned, and used the clinics.

Out of 13,019 total visits, 1,888 patients engaged in the survey, yielding a response rate of 14.5%. Of this subset, 42.6% reported that they were unattached to a primary care provider, while 57.4% reported being attached, suggesting that MPCCs served as an important access point for both groups. High patient satisfaction was observed, with 97.4% of respondents rating their experience as “good” or “excellent.” Patients highlighted the convenience, timeliness, and accessibility of MPCC services, particularly as alternatives to emergency department use for non-urgent concerns. These findings align with the original rationale for MPCCs, to provide timely access to care in communities where patients would otherwise face significant delays or seek care in emergency departments. Satisfaction was strongly linked to rapid access and respectful interactions, reinforcing the value of MPCCs as a mechanism to address access barriers. For unattached patients, MPCCs provided an essential entry point to the health system; for attached patients, they offered timely episodic care when regular providers were unavailable.

These comments underscore the importance of situating MPCCs within a broader primary care strategy, as they are not designed to replace longitudinal care but to complement existing services. These findings also suggest that while MPCCs are meeting a significant need for accessible and timely care, additional communication strategies may enhance patient awareness and understanding of clinic capabilities and availability.

Provider experience and satisfaction

A total of 343 MPCC provider responses to the structured post-clinic survey were collected over 157 clinic days. Respondents included family physicians, nurse practitioners, registered nurses, emergency support aides, paramedics, administrative and clerical staff, and clinical team leads. A small number of public health and mental health professionals also completed the survey, reflecting the interdisciplinary nature of MPCC staffing. Of this group, 98.8% of providers reported that they were satisfied or very satisfied with their overall experience.

Survey responses indicated a strong sense of professional fulfillment and positive workplace experience among providers. Many reported that MPCCs made a meaningful contribution to primary care access, particularly for unattached patients, and that their work was impactful and valued. Respondents highlighted interdisciplinary teamwork, effective collaboration, and the ability to deliver timely, efficient care as key strengths of the model. Technology and system integration were also central themes. Limited access to EMR and other digital infrastructure constrained documentation, follow-up, and continuity of care, an issue with broader relevance for mobile and community-based care models internationally. Discussion

Overview of key findings

Since September 2022, Nova Scotia’s MPCCs have provided 13,019 low‑acuity visits across 157 clinic days, were associated with scenario-based estimates of 2,684 potential ED diversions and approximately CAD $1.95 million in potential fiscal offsets and achieved > 97% patient‑ and > 98% provider-reported satisfaction. These results suggest that the MPCC model may extend primary healthcare access efficiently in higher-volume settings when patient volumes are sufficient and interagency partnerships are robust.

Interpretation and comparison with prior work

While healthcare systems often prioritize transactional interactions, growing evidence underscores the value of sustained, trust-based relationships in improving care quality, access, and system navigation—particularly in primary care [31, 32]. The MPCC initiative reflects this shift by emphasizing relationships between providers, patients, and communities as central to successful service delivery. These joint efforts have played a significant role in the success of the MPCC initiative, highlighting the necessity for ongoing support and development. Based on feedback received, these include sufficient staffing, continuous staff training, and a commitment to continuous quality improvement.

Although mobile health clinics have been implemented in various contexts [16, 17, 19, 20], the peer-reviewed literature remains limited in capturing detailed evaluations of their operational models, cost-effectiveness, patient outcomes, and integration within broader healthcare systems. This evaluation of the MPCCs in Nova Scotia helps address these gaps by providing real-world evidence on clinic utilization, patient and provider experiences, economic impact, and implementation challenges. These findings contribute valuable insights into how temporary, site-based clinics can be scaled as sustainable access points in primary care systems and offer a replicable model for jurisdictions facing similar access issues.

Implementation enablers and barriers

Implementation team survey data identified the importance of having a skilled and adequately staffed workforce, as well as effective communication channels, in ensuring the smooth operation of the MPCC. This highlights the role of leadership and collaborative relationships in driving the MPCC’s success. Survey data also reflected on challenges in leveraging digital tools and resources, like EMRs, to support clinic operations and continuity of care. This underscores the importance of aligning the MPCC with established practices and ensuring sufficient resources for implementation to maintain a standard of care and patient-centricity across primary care access points.

However, the evaluation has also highlighted areas for improvement, such as enhancing appointment management, optimizing supply and equipment availability, and strengthening targeted clinic services such as adding referral pathways for diagnostic and laboratory services and providing follow-up care. Addressing these areas will not only improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the clinics but also enhance patient satisfaction and overall provider experience. Additionally, the need for better communication strategies, both with the public and within the clinic teams, was identified across interest-holder groups, highlighting the importance of communication and consistency for successful clinic implementation. These insights will function as guidance for the continuous improvement and modification of MPCCs. It will be crucial to incorporate these findings into future planning and decision-making strategies to maintain the long-term success and efficiency of the clinics.

Success of mobile primary care clinics and the rapid scaling across the province is evident in the high uptake, and positive experience ratings by patients and MPCC staff, and demonstrate the value of these clinics. An important consideration when interpreting these findings is the relationship between MPCCs and ED utilization. While the underlying rationale for establishing MPCCs was to provide an alternative to ED visits for patients facing access challenges, this evaluation did not include linked patient-level data to directly measure reductions in ED presentations. As such, it remains possible that MPCCs generated additional encounters for conditions such as upper respiratory infections or minor dermatologic and musculoskeletal concerns, rather than substituting for ED visits. International evidence suggests that urgent and episodic care models can reduce some lower-acuity emergency department presentations, although the magnitude of this effect is modest and variable across settings [33–36]. At the same time, these models expand access and may generate additional utilization, reflecting both substitution effects and the identification of previously unmet need [37]. The availability of reliable alternatives such as telephone advice lines, online resources, or virtual care platforms also shapes whether patients choose to seek in-person care. In Nova Scotia, these options coexist alongside MPCCs, highlighting the importance of situating the model within a broader ecosystem of primary care access. Future research using linked utilization data is needed to determine the extent to which MPCCs functioned as true ED alternatives versus additional service capacity.

When interpreting the economic analysis, it is critical to note that patients who visited an MPCC and then presented to an ED within 72 h were treated as an additional cost to the system, rather than as direct substitutions for ED care. Although ED utilization within 72 h was identified through administrative linkage, the available data could not determine whether those ED visits were for the same condition addressed at the MPCC or for a new or unresolved concern. Therefore, the analysis estimates the possible economic consequences of patients’ reported counterfactual care-seeking intentions, rather than providing definitive evidence of system-level ED avoidance or cost savings. Future research using more detailed linked administrative and clinical data is needed to validate whether MPCC visits directly reduce ED utilization.

Patient satisfaction findings are relevant to assessing whether MPCCs fulfilled their intended purpose of improving timely access to care. While high satisfaction suggests early success in meeting this objective, the evaluation was limited to the initial years of implementation (2022–2023). Future evaluations should therefore assess whether satisfaction and utilization patterns are sustained as MPCCs mature, and whether patient expectations shift toward continuity of relationships that the MPCC model is not designed to provide. The focus in the province at the present time is to ensure these clinics are strongly positioned within primary care services and support timely continuity of care. Primary care teams and relevant leads are also looking into the optimal model of care based on the preliminary findings and a provincial program office to support ongoing operations of these clinics. Considerations for planning also include evaluation from other models of mobile care in the province, including the more recently implemented mobile mental health services. The findings from these and sustainability planning considerations will be shared in upcoming publications. Although MPCCs were established primarily to provide timely alternatives to ED visits for acute or urgent concerns, findings from this study and the utilization data revealed that patients frequently sought care for issues such as prescription refills and minor dermatologic conditions. While these encounters were not the central focus of the MPCC mandate, they highlight an unmet demand for accessible primary care and represent an important, if unintended, outcome of the model. They also highlight the importance of this study to inform planning for future enhancements to the service as part of the primary care suite of services including better education to Nova Scotians on the use of MPCCs and other access enhancement services in the province.

The sustainability and expansion of mobile clinic services are closely linked to the current health policy landscape in Nova Scotia. As of 2025, MPCCs are hosted regularly at eight sites, with additional clinics offered across the province as needed [28]. Our findings must be interpreted within the context of Canada’s single-payer system, where mobile clinic services are publicly financed by the province. This means the estimated costs and savings accrue directly to the health system, rather than individual patients. The provincial government has prioritized improving access to primary care, especially for unattached patients, and has invested in mobile health units as a key strategy. However, long-term funding remains subject to broader fiscal pressures and competing system priorities. Additionally, workforce availability, especially NPs and RNs, may constrain future scalability.

Strengths

The province-wide, mixed-methods evaluation design strengthens confidence in both the generalizability and practical relevance of the findings. By capturing 13,019 encounters across 157 clinic days in all four NSH zones, the study supports meaningful comparisons across urban and rural contexts, as well as between clinic types. Real-time encounter data collected via REDCap were triangulated with patient, provider, and implementation team surveys, integrating utilization, experiential, and operational insights. Economic analyses were grounded in transparent costing inputs and reinforced by a 1,000-iteration Monte Carlo simulation to quantify uncertainty around key parameters.

Limitations

Several limitations of the evaluation should be noted given the nature of this study that reviewed preliminary impacts from the implementation of MPCCs in Nova Scotia. First, the analysis was limited by the absence of detailed clinical data on diagnostic testing and investigations, which constrained our ability to assess the full scope of clinical decision-making or identify conditions that may have required further diagnostic follow-up. Second, outcomes beyond the initial visit could not be assessed, as linked data on follow-up care and longer-term patient trajectories, including repeat visits to the MPCC within the evaluation period, were not available within the scope of this evaluation. Third, while providers were remunerated through stipend-based models, the evaluation did not include access to detailed billing or compensation data, limiting the ability to examine variations in cost structures or provider participation patterns. These limitations reflect constraints in available data and linkage for the purposes of this evaluation, rather than the operational design or intent of the MPCC model.

Patient surveys only had a 14.5% response rate, which may not accurately reflect the full spectrum of patient experiences at the mobile clinics. This limited participation could reflect selection bias so caution should be made in interpreting patient satisfaction and experience feedback. Variability within clinic models may hinder our ability to draw firm conclusions about their consistent operational model of care and effectiveness. Differences in how data is collected and reported among clinics affect the reliability and comparability of the information, reducing the accuracy of our evaluation. Due to this variability, future evaluations may benefit from incorporating direct observation of patient flows and informal ethnographic-style engagement. Having unbiased observers present to watch clinic operations and patient journeys could help identify operational strengths and areas needing improvement from an external perspective, supplementing feedback from immediate interest-holders. The interpretation of the clinic data was also limited as the data platform grouped together clinics or removed clinic data after our initial analysis, making it difficult to run confirmatory analyses with the same group of data. Furthermore, this evaluation analyzed MPCC activity at an aggregate level and did not stratify results by presenting complaint or by comparison with urgent referral/respiratory clinics. While dermatological or minor episodic visits may not directly substitute for ED care, they do reflect patient demand for accessible primary care alternatives. The extent to which such visits represent avoided ED utilization versus unmet primary care needs warrants further investigation, which is the focus of ongoing work.

Additionally, the economic analysis incorporates survey-derived estimates of respondents’ reported care-seeking behavior in the absence of MPCC services, suggesting that some ED visits may have been avoided. However, because the counterfactual component is based on self-reported data and not directly validated using administrative data, these diversion estimates should be interpreted cautiously as scenario-based (illustrative) fiscal offsets rather than definitive evidence of cost savings or system-level substitution.It is important to recognize that this evaluation could not conclusively link follow-up ED visits to the patient’s choice of either attending an MPCC, going to an ED, or using ED services for a clinical issue that was unable to be resolved at the MPCC. This underscores the necessity for a better follow-up system and enhanced data tracking to understand the long-term effects of MPCCs on reducing unnecessary ED visits and improving patient outcomes.

Conclusions

MPCCs have demonstrated measurable reach and high patient and provider satisfaction, and they may help improve access to primary care and reduce some avoidable ED use Positive patient and provider feedback underscores the clinics’ value as a promising model of care delivery. The MPCCs have made a positive impact on the community and can be seen as a vital component of Nova Scotia’s healthcare infrastructure. Future work should focus on EMR integration, clearer referral and follow-up pathways, consistent public communication, and ongoing evaluation of equity, cost, and continuity outcomes.
