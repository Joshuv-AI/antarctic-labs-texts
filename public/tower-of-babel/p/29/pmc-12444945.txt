Current Practice and Barriers to Using High‐Flow Nasal Cannula Among ARDS Patients in Saudi Arabia: A Cross‐Sectional Study of Physicians
Authors: Mohammed M. Alyami, Abdulelah M. Aldhahir, Abdullah A. Alqarni, Amal A. Alqahtani, Sara M. Homadi, Mohammed A. Almeshari, Nowaf Y. Alobaidi, Jaber S. Alqahtani, Rayan A. Siraj, Saeed M. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHigh‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) is increasingly utilized in clinical practice; however, the perspectives of physicians on its use and associated barriers in managing acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in Saudi Arabia remain unexplored. This study aims to evaluate HFNC practices and identify the barriers faced by ICU and respiratory physicians in utilizing HFNC for ARDS patients.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was distributed between December 22, 2022, and July 15, 2023, to respiratory and ICU physicians in Saudi Arabia. Data are presented as frequencies and percentages.ResultsA total of 987 responses were analyzed, revealing that physicians perceive HFNC as beneficial for relieving shortness of breath (686, or 69.5%), helping with speech and eating (649, or 65.8%), and potentially preventing intubation (629, or 63.7%). In terms of HFNC practice, 445 respondents (45.1%) reported that its use is governed by institutional protocols, while 679 (68.8%) indicated that the decision to initiate therapy is made by physicians. Additionally, 458 (46.4%) stated that a flow rate of ≥ 15 L/min is necessary to start HFNC therapy. When considering initial settings, nearly half (455, or 46.09%) chose a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) between 61%–80%, 442 (44.8%) chose a flow between 30 and 40 L/min, and 476 (48.23%) chose a temperature of 34°C. Reducing gas flow by 5–10 L/min every two to 4 h was the most appropriate way of weaning, according to 407 (41.24%), and both FiO2 < 35% and gas flow < 20 L/min should be achieved to disconnect HFNC therapy. Lack of knowledge about HFNC therapy was the most commonly reported barrier (494, or 50.1%).ConclusionPhysicians demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of the potential benefits associated with HFNC therapy. However, variations in initial settings and the absence of standardized guidelines present significant challenges to its optimal application in patients with ARDS.

Introduction

High‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) has recently emerged as a novel modality of oxygen therapy capable of delivering heated and humidified oxygen‐enriched gas at a maximum flow rate of 60 L/min [1, 2]. HFNC has proved to be superior to conventional oxygen therapy devices since its setting parameters are independently adjusted, which contributes positively to meeting the patient's oxygen demand [3]. Current evidence has demonstrated that offering warmed oxygen is intimately associated with greater cilia function, which facilitates mucus clearance, thus optimizing patient comfort and decreasing the work of breathing [4].

Previous literature has shown that HFNC can effectively enhance oxygenation status and alleviate respiratory distress by providing constant friction of inspired oxygen, substantially reducing anatomical dead space, and improving alveolar ventilation [5]. More importantly, early application of HFNC significantly reduces the risk of endotracheal intubation, mortality rates, and the length of intensive care unit (ICU) stays among hypoxemic patients [6, 7]. Indeed, HFNC is beneficial in various clinical settings, including acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, obstructive sleep apnea, post‐extubation oxygenation, and during bronchoscopy [8, 9, 10].

Despite the increasing popularity of HFNC use in current practice, clinicians still lack evidence‐based guidelines for the application of HFNC [11]. Subsequent studies of physicians and respiratory therapists have provided limited evidence that HFNC is widely used in clinical settings, resulting in a lack of consensus on the criteria for HFNC initiation [11, 12, 13]. Accordingly, a cross‐sectional study of ICU physicians in France revealed that the current practice of HFNC, including the criteria for HFNC initiation and weaning, was highly inconsistent [14]. Additionally, a global survey of ICU healthcare providers found that the daily practice of HFNC in terms of initial settings and management criteria for HFNC parameters was relatively heterogeneous [15]. These significant variations in clinical practice can lead to HFNC failure, which is presumably attributable to certain barriers, such as the scarcity of institutional protocols and training programs [16]. Increasing evidence has suggested the urgent need for developing standardized guidelines for optimal use of HFNC [13].

To the best of our knowledge, there is an extreme paucity of research exploring the awareness, clinical practice, and barriers to using HFNC in patients with ARDS among physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, this national study primarily aimed to assess the current practice of HFNC and, secondarily, identify barriers to its use in patients with ARDS among physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds in Saudi Arabia. Although this study primarily focuses on HFNC use in patients with ARDS, it is important to acknowledge that many of these patients may have coexisting chronic respiratory conditions such as COPD. The application of HFNC in COPD populations, particularly in acute and stable phases, warrants further exploration, especially given the regional variability in disease prevalence and etiology.

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study design was employed to assess the current practices and barriers related to the use of HFNC in ARDS patients. Data were collected via an electronic survey distributed using SurveyMonkey, a widely used online platform that supports anonymous participation, customizable logic, and secure data export. The survey was disseminated between December 22, 2022, and July 15, 2023.

Instrument

The questionnaire consisted of five sections with closed‐ended questions and free text fields for additional comments. The questionnaire was developed by a multidisciplinary panel of experts, including ICU physicians, pulmonary physicians, respiratory therapists, and ICU nurses. It was adapted from validated instruments in previously published literature [14, 15, 17, 18, 19, 20]. The content validity was ensured through expert review. Before launch, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing with 10 ICU physicians to evaluate clarity and comprehensibility. Based on their feedback, language adjustments were made to improve understanding and flow before final distribution.

The five sections of the questionnaire included: Section one consisted of questions about participants' demographic backgrounds, including gender, assigned role in the department, geographical location, years of clinical experience, and usual clinical practice area, and four questions about previous training, use, existing managing protocols for HFNC, and who was responsible for initiating high‐flow nasal cannula in ARDS patients. ARDS was clearly defined in the survey by the Berlin definition, which was developed in 2012 [20].Section two consisted of four statements about whether HFNC is a tolerable device for patients, helps patients with conversations and eating, improves shortness of breath, and can be used to avoid or prevent intubation, using a five‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree; 5 = strongly disagree).Section three consisted of six questions about the criteria required for switching to HFNC, initial settings, management, and weaning.Section four consisted of four statements assessing participants' agreement about HFNC failure and when intubation is needed.Section five consisted of one question asking about the most commonly reported barriers to using HFNC.

Data Collection and Sampling

A convenience sampling technique was employed. Eligible participants included physicians in Saudi Arabia with professional backgrounds in respiratory and intensive care medicine. Physicians without ICU or respiratory specialization were excluded. The survey was distributed via professional societies, including the Saudi Thoracic Society and the Saudi Critical Care Society, as well as through regional data collectors who shared it at their respective hospitals. The goal was to capture one response per physician. However, this approach may have resulted in multiple responses from the same hospital using a standardized HFNC protocol, which could contrast with other hospitals lacking such protocols.

After data collection, responses were exported from SurveyMonkey in Excel format. Initial data cleaning was performed in Microsoft Excel, including removal of incomplete responses, duplicates, and verification of participant eligibility.

Informed consent was obtained electronically. Participants were informed about the study's purpose, procedures, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were anonymized. Duplicate entries were avoided by limiting survey access to a single submission per device. The estimated completion time was approximately 8 min.

Power Calculation

According to the Saudi Ministry of Health report published in 2024, there are a total of 18,064 physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds [21]. Based on this report, a total sample of 984 physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds with 99% confidence level and 4% margin of error is required.

Data Analysis

Cleaned data were imported into IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentages) were used to summarize the data. A χ 2 test was used to compare responses between physicians with existing HFNC protocols and those without. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained before its commencement from the Bioethical Committee at Batterjee Medical College (Reference Number RES‐2022‐0077).

Results

Demographic Data of Study Respondents

The survey was distributed to a cohort of 1645 respiratory and ICU physicians, with 987 responding, resulting in a response rate of 60%. Of these, 572 (58.0%) were males. Half of the physicians were from the Central Region (500, or 50.7%), followed by the Western Region (312, or 31.6%). Half of the respondents were clinical specialists (535, or 54.1%), followed by staff therapists (383, or 38.8%). Almost half of participants had between one and 5 years of experience (477, or 48.3%), followed by those with between 6 and 10 years of experience (324, or 32.8%). Table 1 reports the respondents' demographics and characteristics. Physicians usually worked in the medical‐surgical ICUs (535, or 53.0%); 312 (31.6%) usually worked in non‐ICU settings, and 337 (34.1%) usually worked in emergency room settings. Most respondents had been formally trained in using HFNC (714, or 72.4%), and 698 (70.7%) had used HFNC at least once on ARDS patients (See Table 1).

Perceived Advantages of HFNC

When asked about tolerability, the majority of respondents indicated that HFNC had high tolerability (711, or 72%). Moreover, most of the respondents agreed that HFNC improved shortness of breath (686, or 69.5%), helped patients maintain speech and eating abilities (649, or 65.8%), and might prevent the need for intubation (629, or 63.7%). Nearly half of respondents (458, or 46.40%) said that an oxygen flow of ≥ 15 L/min was needed to switch an ARDS patient to HFNC therapy, followed by 293 (29.70%) who chose an oxygen flow of ≥ 12 L/min (See Table 2).

Initial Settings of HFNC

When initiating HFNC therapy for ARDS patients, nearly half of respondents (455, or 46.09%) said an FiO2 between 61% and 80% was indicated, followed by 283 (28.7%) who indicated an FiO2 between 21% and 40% was indicated. Nearly half of respondents (442, or 44.8%) said that an initial HFNC flow should be set between 30 and 40 L/min, followed by 291 (29.5%) who chose a flow between 41 and 50 L/min. Almost half of respondents (476, or 48.23%) said that an initial HFNC temperature should be set at 34°C, followed by 423 (42.86%) who said the temperature should be set at 37°C (See Table 2).

Management of HFNC

When respondents were asked how strongly they agreed or disagreed with the statement “HFNC fails and intubation is needed if an ARDS patient pauses or stops breathing”, the majority of respondents agreed that the statement was correct (700, or 70.9%). When asked how strongly they agreed or disagreed with the statement “HFNC fails and intubation is needed in cases of refractory hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90% with FiO2 100% and flow rate of 60 L/min),” the majority of respondents agreed that the statement was correct (732, or 74.2%). When asked how strongly they agreed or disagreed with the statement “HFNC fails and intubation is needed in cases of severe respiratory acidosis (pH is unacceptably low ( < 7.25) and the PaCO2 is elevated)”, most of the respondents agreed that the statement was correct (703, or 71.2%). When asked how strongly they agreed or disagreed with the statement “HFNC fails and intubation is needed if ARDS patients experience reduced levels of consciousness (GCS score ≤ 8)”, the majority of respondents agreed that the statement was correct (710, or 71.9%). The complete report of responses appears in Supporting Information S1: Table 2.

Weaning and Discontinuation of HFNC

Two‐fifths of respondents (407, or 41.24%) stated that when weaning ARDS patients from HFNC, clinicians should first reduce gas flow by 5–10 L/min every 2–4 h. This was followed by 353 respondents (35.76%) who said that the FiO2 should first be reduced by 5%–10% every 2–4 h. When discounting HFNC, half of respondents (567, or 57.45%) stated that both FiO2 < 35% and gas flow of < 20 L/min should be achieved. This was followed by 274 (27.76%) who stated that a gas flow < 20 L/min was suitable to discontinue ARDS patients from HFNC (See Table 2).

Barriers to Using HFNC

Several barriers to using HFNC were identified by respondents, with the lack of knowledge about HFNC being the most frequently mentioned (494, or 50.1%). This was followed by the availability of devices in hospitals (466, or 47.2%), and work overload (389, or 39.4%). All reported barriers are illustrated in Figure 2.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first national study that assesses the current practice of using HFNC in ARDS patients among physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds in Saudi Arabia and the barriers to using this modality. The study findings provide valuable insights into the perceptions, benefits, disagreements, and barriers regarding the use of HFNC in ARDS patients.

HFNC offers multiple benefits in terms of improving oxygenation status and ameliorating respiratory distress [5, 22]. In the present study, we found a high level of agreement among respondents that HFNC is highly tolerable, improves shortness of breath, helps in maintaining speech and eating abilities, and may prevent the need for intubation in ARDS patients, similar findings to those of Koyauchi et al. [17]. Additionally, the recent ESICM guidelines have demonstrated that HFNC is well‐tolerated by patients and has a significantly greater beneficial effect compared to conventional oxygen therapy in preventing intubation [23]. This indicates that physicians recognize the potential benefits of HFNC in improving patient outcomes and reducing the need for invasive mechanical ventilation.

The current study identified areas of disagreement among respondents regarding the initial settings of HFNC on patients with ARDS, which was similar to a previous study [14]. Our study participants were found to favor initiating HFNC with a FiO2 ranging between 60% and 80%, a flow rate of 30–40 L/min, and a temperature setting of 34°C. These clinical practices contradict the recommended protocols, which suggest initiating therapy with a FiO2 of 100%, a flow rate of 60 L/min, and a temperature of 37°C [24, 25]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in individual clinical experiences, preferences, or the lack of standardized guidelines for HFNC [18]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated a potential beneficial effect of HFNC in reducing the necessity for endotracheal intubation and decreasing the length of stay in the ICU when utilizing a higher flow rate of 50–60 L/min. Future research and consensus guidelines should address these areas of disagreement to ensure appropriate and evidence‐based initiation of HFNC therapy in ARDS patients.

In our study, the level of consensus among physicians was high concerning identifying scenarios where HFNC fails and intubation becomes necessary. The majority of respondents ( > 70%) agreed that if a patient pauses or stops breathing, experiences refractory hypoxemia, or suffers from severe respiratory acidosis, the use of HFNC may not be sufficient, and intubation is warranted. Our findings are in line with previous studies [14], although we found a lower percentage of agreement. This significant finding indicates a shared understanding of when escalating from HFNC to invasive mechanical ventilation in ARDS patients is necessary. Consistency in decision‐making in this regard is vital for ensuring patient safety and achieving the best possible outcome.

The current study found disagreement among respondents on the weaning process of FiO2, the flow rate, and the discontinuation of HFNC. While the majority of respondents favored reducing flow by 5–10 L/min every 2–4 h, a significant proportion also suggested reducing FiO2 by 5%–10% during the weaning process. Moreover, the responses regarding the criteria for discontinuing HFNC varied, with a considerable number of respondents emphasizing the achievement of both a FiO2 < 35% and a gas flow < 20 L/min. Previous studies also found disagreement about the weaning [14, 15] and the discontinuation of HFNC [14]. These findings suggest a lack of consensus, highlighting the need for standardized protocols and guidelines to facilitate a systematic approach to weaning and discontinuation of HFNC in ARDS patients. Such guidelines would enable physicians to manage the transition confidently and uniformly from HFNC to other modalities of respiratory care.

When examining the barriers to HFNC use, respondents identified a lack of knowledge, the unavailability of the devices, and work overload as the most significant challenges. In light of these challenges, previous studies have indicated that inadequate training and a lack of standardized protocols for the application of HFNC therapy can result in suboptimal clinical practices [26, 27]. This reflects the broader issues faced by healthcare professionals in resource‐limited settings. To address these barriers, the findings suggest the need for investment in infrastructure, training, and education to promote the appropriate use of HFNC therapy. This could involve increasing the availability of HFNC devices in hospitals, providing comprehensive training programs for healthcare professionals, and working towards establishing standardized protocols and guidelines for HFNC application in ARDS patients.

Previous studies have demonstrated that HFNC can enhance exercise tolerance and quality of life in stable COPD patients by alleviating carbon dioxide retention, reducing respiratory rate, and decreasing the frequency of acute exacerbations and hospitalization durations [28, 29]. However, despite these advantages in stable COPD, evidence supporting HFNC's clinical benefits during acute exacerbations is limited [30]. Evidence suggests that in cases of moderate hypercapnic acute respiratory failure, noninvasive ventilation (NIV) may provide superior outcomes compared to HFNC [31]. In the context of our study, a substantial portion of patients with ARDS may have pre‐existing respiratory conditions such as COPD, which may influence how HFNC is perceived or implemented. Importantly, COPD in the Arabian Peninsula differs from that in Western countries in both prevalence and phenotype. While tobacco smoking remains a major contributor globally, studies from Saudi Arabia and Gulf countries indicate a significant proportion of COPD cases among non‐smokers, particularly women [32]. These cases are often associated with biomass fuel exposure, indoor air pollution, and genetic predispositions [33, 34]. This contrasts with populations in Europe or South America, where the smoking‐COPD link is stronger and the clinical profile of patients may differ [35]. These regional differences highlight the need to consider population‐specific characteristics when evaluating the utility of HFNC.

The findings of this study have significant implications for the clinical practice of HFNC in ARDS patients in Saudi Arabia. Firstly, the high level of agreement among physicians on the indications for HFNC use indicates a good understanding of its potential benefits for various respiratory conditions. By understanding the indications for HFNC, physicians can avoid unnecessary use of this modality in conditions where it may not be effective or appropriate. This ensures that resources are allocated optimally and reduces the risk of potential adverse effects associated with unnecessary treatment. Moreover, when physicians are aware of the conditions in which HFNC has demonstrated efficacy, they are more likely to consider this therapeutic option early in the management process. Promptly initiating HFNC as part of the treatment plan can lead to improved outcomes. Secondly, several areas of disagreement have been identified, including the initial settings and weaning protocols, which point to the need for standardized guidelines in these areas. Developing consensus guidelines will ensure consistency and facilitate optimal practice, resulting in better outcomes for patients. The medical community should work to establish evidence‐based protocols that guide the initiation, adjustment, and discontinuation of HFNC therapy in ARDS patients. Moreover, the findings from this study may have broader relevance for the management of respiratory failure beyond ARDS, particularly in patients with coexisting COPD. Given the distinct characteristics of COPD in the Saudi population, including a higher prevalence of nonsmoking‐related etiologies. Further research should explore how HFNC can be optimized in these overlapping clinical scenarios.

Strengths and Limitations

This study represents the first national cross‐sectional investigation of current HFNC practices in patients with ARDS among physicians with respiratory and ICU backgrounds in Saudi Arabia. It offers valuable insights into existing discrepancies in HFNC application and underscores the urgent need for standardized clinical guidelines and protocols. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self‐reported data, which may be prone to recall and reporting biases. Although self‐reports provide useful perspectives, they may not fully capture actual clinical behaviors. Second, the sample was limited to physicians specializing in respiratory and critical care medicine within Saudi Arabia, which may restrict the generalizability of findings to other healthcare professionals and international contexts. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on ARDS patients, limiting its applicability to other patient populations. Future research should aim to quantify self‐reported data using validated instruments or structured clinical vignettes to enhance measurement precision and subgroup comparability. Longitudinal study designs are also recommended to evaluate the long‐term outcomes of HFNC therapy, such as hospital readmission rates, mortality, and functional recovery, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of its sustained effectiveness—particularly in patients with ARDS and comorbidities such as COPD. Finally, future studies should incorporate more analytical methodologies and objective data collection strategies, including direct clinical observations or audits of electronic health records, to ensure a more accurate assessment of HFNC practices in real‐world settings.

Conclusion

This national study provides valuable insights into the current practice of HFNC use in ARDS patients among physicians in Saudi Arabia with respiratory and ICU expertise. The findings emphasize a strong understanding of the potential benefits of HFNC, such as improved patient comfort, enhanced oxygenation, and reduced reliance on invasive mechanical ventilation. However, barriers such as discrepancies in initial settings and the absence of standardized guidelines hinder its optimal implementation. Addressing these challenges through future research and the development of consensus guidelines will be essential to bridging the gap between its recognized benefits and practical application, ultimately promoting evidence‐based practice and better patient outcomes.
