4D injection molding

ABSTRACT
Injection molding is a foundational manufacturing process for the large-scale production of plastic parts and is also applied to rubber, metal, ceramic, glass, wood, and composites. Regular injection molding focuses on minimizing warpage and ensuring dimensional uniformity and high repeatability, as non-uniform material shrinkage in complex mold cavities causes undesirable deformations. In contrast, we reverse this traditional paradigm by leveraging controlled solidification shrinkage to induce purposeful, programmable deformation. We propose 4D injection molding, utilizing localized thermal activation and selective in-mold bonding to create non-uniform temperature and pressure distributions in spatiotemporal dimensions during the molding cycle. This enables the generation of complex, customized geometries from a single mold. Integrated with predictive modeling and multi-objective response optimization, our approach transforms warpage from a defect into a design feature, yielding functional parts with tunable shapes and performance characteristics. This method supports rapid, scalable, and cost-efficient mass customization across various materials. By reimagining shrinkage not as a limitation but as a design opportunity, the method represents a transformative shift in manufacturing science, with promising applications in biomedical, aerospace, and responsive consumer products.

Introduction

Injection molding (IM) stands as the cornerstone of innovation in batch molding plastic parts1,2, known for its efficiency, repeatability, and versatility, while also extending its transformative reach to rubber3, metal4, ceramics5, glass6, wood7, and composites8. The growing global demand for polymer components in food, medical, transportation, packaging, and electronics highlights the need for highly efficient IM. Injection molds, typically crafted from metal to endure high pressure and clamping forces, represent a significant investment but can produce tens of thousands of parts9. These parts are usually required to be “cast in the same mold” with high repeatability and minimal dimensional deviation as the key metrics for precision IM10. A persistent challenge in IM is mitigating warpage, the unwanted deformation arising from non-uniform volumetric shrinkage (Δv). This shrinkage anisotropy originates from spatiotemporal gradients in temperature (T) and pressure (p), compounded by material-specific and geometric factors within the mold cavity (Fig. 1a)2,11,12. Extensive efforts have been devoted to reducing warpage and dimensional deviations. Existing strategies can be broadly categorized as: (1) Simulation-driven compensation, in which mold geometry is pre-distorted to counteract predicted shrinkage14; (2) Mold design innovations, such as conformal cooling channels to homogenize temperature fields15,16; and (3) Advanced process control, using sensors and adaptive systems to optimize packing pressure and cooling profiles17,18. While these approaches effectively minimize warpage, they fundamentally aim to suppress the manifestation of inherent material shrinkage to produce a single, static geometry from a given mold. They treat shrinkage differentials as a problem to be solved, not a potential tool. Moreover, absolute zero warpage remains theoretically unattainable due to the inherent spatiotemporal thermomechanical behavior and shrinkage anisotropies of materials19–21 in the complex structure of the mold cavity.

In contrast to regular IM, which seeks to eliminate deformation, we propose a transformative approach that leverages differential shrinkage to program predictable, purposeful deformations. This concept, termed 4D Injection Molding (4D IM), enables the fabrication of multiple distinct geometries from a single, unmodified mold—“various parts from the same mold”. 4D refers to shapes that evolve over time (t) after ejection, driven by controlled release of process-induced stresses. The foundation principle is the deliberate generation of spatiotemporal Δv differentials by manipulating processing parameters (T, p, t) (Fig. 1b). In the spatial dimension, Δv includes shrinkage in flow (Δvsl), transverse (Δvsw), and thickness (Δvst) directions. In the temporal dimension, Δv comprises in-mold shrinkage (Δvt1) and post-shrinkage (Δvt2): Δvt1 occurs during solidification under pressure, constrained by the mold walls, generating internal stresses and in-plane restricted deformation; Δvt2 occurs after ejection, when constraints are removed, producing out-of-plane free deformation with releasing residual stress generated from Δvt1 (Fig. 1c).

Deformation in IM parts is attributed to spatiotemporal Δv differences driven by uneven thickness, T and p differentials, and anisotropic effects from molecular chain, fiber, or filler orientation (Fig. 1d). From regulation perspective, these Δv variations are influenced by material properties, part design, mold structure, and process conditions1,11,17,21–23. When raw materials and mold designs remain unchanged, 4D IM enables shape customization by adjusting process conditions (Fig. 1b). However, the achievable deformation remains limited unless time (t) is introduced as an additional design dimension. Expanding spatiotemporal Δv differentials enables broad control over final shapes and functionalities. Numerous activation techniques including thermal24,25, light26–28, bonding29,30, electric31, magnetic fields32, ultrasonic33, acoustic34, and mechanical35,36 have been extensively explored in additive manufacturing (AM), including 3D and 4D printing37,38. However, AM is intrinsically limited in production speed, cost, and material compatilibity. Its serial, layer-by-layer nature inherently limits throughput and inflates cost-per-part in mass production39. Producing a morphing structure may require hours, with significant overhead from machine time and support material. 4D IM combines these activation techniques with the inherent capabilities of IM to regulate part deformation in line, facilitating the mass production of diverse parts from a single mold without modification to the IM machine. A full 4D IM cycle, including the activation step, typically completes in around 30 s for a medium-complexity part, independent of geometric complexity. After mold costs are amortized, the cost-per-part becomes orders of magnitude lower than AM9. Material waste is minimized because no support structures are required. Therefore, 4D IM transitions programmed shape-morphing from a prototyping method to a practical mass-production technology, enabling scalable customization and functionalization.

We demonstrate the implementation of 4D IM using two key techniques: localized thermal activation and selective in-mold bonding (Fig. 1e). These methods enable controlled deformation by modulating Δv(x,y,z,t) of plastics in specific regions of the mold cavity through localized T gradients, curing t differentials, thickness variations, fiber orientation, and ejection elastic and integration effects. Using response surface methodology (RSM), we achieved programmable control over part shapes and dimensions. Furthermore, 4D IM was extended to composites and elastomers, enabling fiber-directed deformation, process-induced deformation, and tailored mechanical properties and functional requirements.

Results

Localized thermal activation 4D IM

Creating T gradients can drive shape-transformable materials for special applications40,41 and can also be used in IM for parts with permanent deformations. While non-uniform T distributions commonly occur in regular IM, T gradient-induced deformations are typically constrained by the mold and process. To overcome this limitation, we developed a localized thermal activation system for 4D IM, combining a regular IM machine and an experimental mold (Fig. 2a). The demonstration part, a three-bladed polypropylene (PP) impeller (Supplementary Fig. 1a), was molded using this setup. The thermal activation system employed multiple circular ceramic heating elements on the back of the mold cavity (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2), with eight heating elements (No. 1–8) assigned to each impeller blade. Under regular IM process without localized heating, the displacement between Point 2 (blade edge) and datum Point 0 (blade root) in the axial Z-direction of the impeller presented a limited range from 0.079 to 0.5 mm, with blade warping towards the gate side due to the higher T effect of the bulky runner (Supplementary Tables 1,2, and Supplementary Fig. 3). Through orthogonal simulations, we optimized process parameters—melt temperature (Tmelt) of 250 °C, packing pressure (ppack) of 24 MPa, packing time (tpack) of 2 s, and cooling time (tcool) of 6 s—to maximize warpage. Subsequently, we incorporated the localized thermal activation system to enhance deformation capability. Activating individual heating elements at different positions induced diverse deformations, including bending, twisting, and reverse warping, surpassing the deformation capability of regular IM without localized heating (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Tables 3,4, and Supplementary Figs. 4,5). Simultaneous activation of multiple heating elements at varying voltage levels (0–24 V) reversed the warpage direction (Supplementary Movie 1), expanded the displacement range, and enabled programmable deformation. Using face-centered central composite design (FCD) and response surface methodology (RSM)42,43, 90 experimental runs were conducted (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Fig. 6). Axial displacement at Point 2 (blade edge) served as the control target. Experimental displacements ranging from −0.15 to 4.22 mm confirmed the capability to achieve a broad spectrum of programmable deformations. Quadratic response surface models for simulation and experimental results (Supplementary Equations (1) and (2)) achieved predicted R2 values of 99.3% and 96.36%, respectively (Supplementary Tables 6–10). These models enabled inverse calculations using desirability functions42 to determine optimal heating element voltages for achieving specific target displacements. Five solutions were derived for target displacements of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 mm, respectively (Supplementary Table 11). Validation simulations and experiments confirmed high accuracies, with coefficients of determination (R2) of 98.9% (simulations) and 99.88% (experiments) (Fig. 2c–f). A maximum target displacement of 4 mm, equivalent to 14% of the part’s radius, was achieved, with an IM cycle completed within 9 s.

Selective in-mold bonding 4D IM

Integrated components are typically fabricated via insert-IM or injection over-molding44,45, where inserts are placed in the mold cavity and bonded with injected molten plastic. This technique is widely used for bonding inserts of metal, polymer, and fiber-reinforced polymer composites in automotive and electrical components46,47. Thermal processes at the bonding interface induce Δv differentials and residual stresses, which drive warpage deformation—a phenomenon leveraged in 4D IM. We developed selective in-mold bonding using molds with three cavity structures: cross-shaped, hand-shaped, and caterpillar-shaped (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). PP sheets with a thickness of 0.4 mm were fabricated via hot pressing, cut into smaller parts, and bonded at selective positions during IM. This technique created localized T and p differentials (ΔTmax = 7 °C, Δpmax = 23 MPa, Fig. 3b) by leveraging thickness variations and interfacial bonding. Bonded regions accelerated heat dissipation, reduced local Δv, and induced deformation toward high T regions. Both simulations and experimental results demonstrated that bonding near the gate (Bond 1) created greater ΔT and Δp, resulting in more pronounced deformations compared to mid-blade (Bond 2) and edge (Bond 3) bonding. Programmable deformation was achieved by coupling selective in-mold bonding and IM process control. A Box-Behnken RSM design (29 experimental runs, Supplementary Table 12) was employed with the response variable of displacement between Point 2 (blade edge) and datum Point 0 (root) in the Z-direction of the cross-shaped part (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Four critical IM parameters (Tmelt, ppack, tpacck, and tcool) were optimized for Bond 1 (Supplementary Fig. 7f), achieving a maximum experimental displacement of 3.68 mm (12.27% of the part’s radius). A quadratic model (Supplementary Equation (3)) was derived with a prediction accuracy of 82.74% (Supplementary Tables 13, 14). For Bond 2 and Bond 3 with small deformation ranges, RSM with simulation results yielded two models (Supplementary Equations (4) and (5)). Using desirability functions, inverse calculations derived IM process conditions (II–V) for target displacements (3.6, 3.2, 2.2, 1.8, 0.5 mm) across bonding positions (I). Five optimal solutions were validated experimentally, achieving high accuracy (R² = 99.36%, Supplementary Table 15, and Fig. 3d).

The method’s versatility was demonstrated in hand-shaped (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Equation (6), Supplementary Tables 16–19, Supplementary Movie 3) and caterpillar-shaped parts (Fig. 3g, h, Supplementary Table 20). Multi-insert bonding enabled complex deformations in caterpillar-shaped parts under identical IM conditions (Supplementary Table 21, Supplementary Fig. 9). Torsional deformations were achieved by varying bond part geometries (Supplementary Fig. 10). Integrating selective in-mold bonding with localized thermal activation enhanced 4D IM capabilities for intricate geometries (Supplementary Fig. 11).

4D IM for composites

Insert-IM with continuous fiber-reinforced thermoplastic prepreg enables high-strength, lightweight components and has already been commercially applied44,45,48. However, mismatched properties between fibers and the matrix often induce residual stresses, causing warping and reducing dimensional accuracy45. By leveraging fiber orientation to induce controlled deformations, we achieve 4D IM while tailoring mechanical properties. We integrated selective in-mold bonding with fiber-reinforced composites, using continuous glass fiber-reinforced polyamide 6 (CGF-PA6) prepreg tapes as bonding inserts and short-glass-fiber-reinforced PA6 (SGF-PA6) as the injection material. A mold with two plate-shaped cavities was employed, with three bonding positions and two fiber orientations—weft (parallel) and warp (perpendicular) to the flow direction (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 12). Sufficient interfacial bonding was ensured using the same PA6 matrix and optimizing process conditions44. Bonding positions and fiber orientations resulted in diverse deformations (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Figs. 13,14,15, Supplementary Movie 4). CGF restricted Δv of the bonding part and induced warpage toward the bonding side (positive Z-direction). The combined SGF orientation, T and p gradients, and thickness variations induced Δvt1 and Δvt2, causing final parts to warp toward the negative Z-direction while simultaneously bending along the CGF orientation. Given the 0.3 mm thickness of the bonded CGF-PA6 relative to the 2 mm cavity thickness (a mold-to-bond ratio of 6.7:1), the achievable deformation is constrained by both the cavity geometry and the inherently low Δv of SGF-PA6 (0.5–0.7%), with the maximum measured displacement of 0.96 mm representing the minimum deformation potential under these molding conditions. The maximum warpage deformation occurred in parts with warp fibers bonded at the middle position, attributed to localized T retention (Fig. 4c). Mechanical properties were enhanced locally (Supplementary Fig. 16). Parts with weft fibers bonded farthest from the gate exhibited the highest bending strength (229.54 ± 4.14 MPa) and modulus (6.68 ± 0.19 GPa) but reduced fracture strain. The bonding position affected thermal stress distribution, creating warpage, while fiber orientation dictated both structural anisotropy and warpage direction (Supplementary Fig. 17). Through full factorial RSM (Supplementary Table 22), we achieved programmable control over warpage, strength, modulus, and fracture strain. The developed models (Supplementary Equations (7)–(10)) exhibited solid predictive performance (predicted R2 = 79.5%, 68.09%, 99.21%, and 54.37% for warpage, strength, modulus, and failure stain) and strong linear relationships49, as indicated by the Pearson correlation coefficient (r = 0.892, 0.825, 0.996, and 0.737) (Supplementary Tables 23,24, Supplementary Note 3).

4D IM for elastomers

Soft robotics, which employs flexible and adaptable elastomers, mimics biological systems to navigate complex environments, handle delicate objects, and perform tasks requiring flexibility50,51. IM offers an efficient method for mass-producing soft robotic components52. 4D IM extends this capability to fabricate elastomer parts with controlled shapes, enhanced flexibilities, and embedded functionalities from a single mold. Leveraging the processability and elasticity of thermoplastic elastomers, we produced soft parts with diverse deformations by adjusting IM process conditions. Experiments with thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) demonstrated the fabrication of various shapes (Fig. 5a). TPU exhibits pronounced viscoelastic behavior and low stiffness during IM. The controlled release of molded-in stresses during elastomer relaxation enabled predictable deformation patterning, with strain fields dictated by processing history and geometric constraints. Lower ppack and shorter tpacck resulted in insufficient p to compensate for Δv, leading to higher residual stresses. Similarly, higher Tmelt and shorter tcool created uneven thermal gradients and incomplete solidification, exacerbating internal stresses. Upon ejection, the sudden release of these stresses caused significant warpage, while insufficient cooling (shorter tcool) prevented material stabilization, making it more susceptible to deformation under external forces. These factors collectively contributed to the observed curling in TPU parts (Fig. 5a). Through process control, the same mold produced hand-shaped grippers with varying curled fingers and gripping forces (maximum 0.7 N) (Fig. 5b), suitable for handling objects of different weights and shapes or functioning as robotic gripers for object transfer (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Movie 5, Supplementary Note 4).Fig. 54D IM for elastomers.a TPU parts exhibiting different deformations. b Tensile force measurement of hand-shaped TPU samples. The over-molding capability of elastomers with rigid substrates (polymers/metals) facilitates the fabrication of multi-material components with integrated flexibility and functionality. While analogous approaches using thermoset elastomers have been demonstrated29, their implementation in IM with thermoplastic elastomers remains largely unexplored. Our work advances this field through selective in-mold bonding 4D IM to enhance the functional capabilities of elastomeric components. We fabricated magnetically responsive composites by blending Fe3O4 particles with TPU, followed by hot-pressing (Supplementary Fig. 18). The resulting Fe3O4/TPU sheets were cut into pieces for selective in-mold bonding 4D IM to produce soft robotic parts with magnetic responsiveness (Fig. 5d). The structural integrity was maintained through interfacial adhesion with homogeneous TPU (Supplementary Fig. 19). These components exhibited environmentally tunable deformation control (Fig. 5e), facilitating applications such as magnetically actuated flexible switches (Fig. 5f) and object handling/transfer (Fig. 5g) (Supplementary Movies 6, 7).

Quantitative comparison of scalability

To evaluate the scalability of 4D IM relative to established manufacturing technologies, we performed a quantitative comparison against regular IM, 3D printing, and 4D printing across key production and economic metrics (Supplementary Table 25). Regular IM remains the benchmark for mass manufacturing, achieving cycle times of 2–60 s and production rates of 50–1000+ parts per hour per machine1,9,53–57. These capabilities are retained in 4D IM, with cycle-time extensions limited to 20% due to localized thermal activation and selective in-mold bonding. This enables typical production rates of 40–850+ parts per hour. In contrast, 3D printing processes such as fused deposition modeling (FDM), stereolithography (SLA), and selective laser sintering (SLS) operate at 0.1–20 parts per hour, constrained by layer-wise deposition and limited build volume27,28,32,55–59. Stimuli-responsive 4D printing further reduces throughput to 0.1–10 parts per hour due to slow material response, complex paths, and narrow material compatibility37,38,51. These rate limitations result in significantly higher per-part cost. IM and 4D IM achieve $0.06–10 per part at scale, while AM and 4D printing remain at $1–250, even at high utilization37,38,55,57. In addition, 4D IM exhibits < 1–3% material waste, consistent with standard IM due to near-net-shape molding and recyclable runners. AM and 4D printing typically exhibit 10–45% material loss through support structures, incomplete fusion, and post-processing removal55,56.

Discussion

Based on pressure-specific volume-temperature (pvT) behaviors of thermoplastic polymers and the solidification shrinkage mechanism23, thin-sectioned parts exhibit greater susceptibility to warpage deformation under process-induced stresses60. Increasing melt temperature (Tmelt), reducing packing pressure (ppack), and shortening packing time (tpack) and cooling time (tcool) induce high residual stress, thermal gradient, insufficient cooling, and final warpage deformation. This process-induced deformation is limited due to the stiff properties of regular plastics and mold constraint, while it becomes more achievable on elastomers. Exploiting the elastic recovery (relaxation) of the molded elastomers after ejection, curling deformations were directly achieved by injection molding (IM) process control, which would otherwise require complex and costly molds.

Localized thermal activation generates temperature (T) gradients to induce targeted deformation in specific areas. Selective in-mold bonding creates non-uniform T and pressure (p) fields that lead to non-uniform shrinkage and deformation and enables multi-material parts to be bonded at selective positions with tailored mechanical properties and functionalities. Combining selective in-mold bonding and localized thermal activation enhanced 4D IM capabilities. By leveraging the orientation of continuous fibers and optimizing bonding positions, we achieved parts with fiber-directed deformations and enhanced mechanical properties. By joining elastomers with magnetic elements, we produced soft grippers and switches with magnetically driven capabilities. Given the reduced softness and recovery capability of elastomers with added magnetic content, the amount of magnetic material should be tailored to specific functional requirements. By systematically modeling the relationship between 4D IM parameters—such as the heating element’s position and voltage, the bonding part’s position and shape, and process parameters (e.g., Tmelt, ppack, tpacck, tcool)—and deformation outcomes, response surface methodology (RSM) enables programmable deformations. RSM models based on face-centered central composite design (FCD), Box-Behnken, and full factorial designs demonstrated high prediction accuracy. Inverse calculations based on desirability functions were successfully applied to determine the 4D IM parameters required for target displacements. This data-driven approach enhances deformation control and reduces the need for trial-and-error experimentation, making 4D IM a more efficient and scalable manufacturing solution.

Despite these advances, several methodological constraints remain. The magnitude and spatial resolution of deformation depend strongly on part geometry: thin sections allow large shape changes, whereas thicker regions inherently restrict actuation. Localized thermal activation requires tooling with integrated heating elements, which increases mold complexity, energy consumption, and cost. Selective in-mold bonding currently relies on adhesive application, introducing potential variability in bond strength and surface finish, and bond displacement under high temperature and high-shear melt flow remains a critical challenge. Future developments of the 4D IM framework will target these limitations. One direction is the adoption of non-contact thermal activation methods (e.g., directed infrared heating61) to generate spatial T gradients without permanent mold modification, thereby improving flexibility, energy efficiency, and cost-effectiveness. Another is the exploration of alternative bonding strategies, such as temporary high-performance adhesives62 that form mechanical interlock interfaces between the bond part and cavity surface during molding and can be removed afterward to preserve surface quality. For composites and elastomers, greater material variability and more complex fiber/filler–matrix interactions may reduce model robustness for programmable deformation. Future studies will incorporate additional microstructural descriptors and hybrid machine-learning-assisted metamodels to enhance predictive accuracy and reliability. Performance validation under realistic operating conditions, including higher loads, multi-cycle actuation, and integration with automated or electronic systems, will also be required. Moreover, systematic evaluation of long-term durability, functional certification, and compliance with industrial standards will be essential for transitioning 4D IM from controlled laboratory demonstrations to robust industrial deployment.

Overall, 4D IM merges the high throughput of IM with programmable deformation, enabling the production of customized, functionally graded parts from a single mold. By generating multiple geometries without tooling changes, 4D IM significantly reduces tooling amortization and accelerates product development. This technology is applicable to a wide range of materials, including polymers, composites, elastomers, and potentially metals, ceramics, and biodegradable materials, paving the way for adaptive, functionally integrated manufacturing. This capability positions 4D IM as the only technology that unifies industrial-scale manufacturing efficiency with the design freedom of 4D functional transformation. These advantages open opportunities for mass-customized, adaptive components in deployable consumer products, morphing wearable devices, automotive and aerospace systems, and stimuli-responsive soft robotic structures.

Methods

Localized thermal activation 4D IM

Polypropylene (PP 579S, SABIC), a semi-crystalline homopolymer with a melt flow rate (MFR) of 47 g 10 min−1 (230 °C, 2.16 kg load, ISO 1133) and a density of 905 kg m−3 (room temperature, ISO 1183), was used as the primary material for the localized thermal activation 4D IM.

The IM process was performed using a Haitian ZE1200 machine equipped with a 32 mm screw diameter, a maximum screw stroke of 170 mm, an injection volume of 157 g, a maximum injection pressure of 253 MPa, and a clamping force of 122 tf.

The demonstration part is a three-bladed impeller (Supplementary Fig. 1a) with a diameter of 70 mm. The blade thickness tapered from 1 mm at the root to 0.5 mm at the edge. The blade surface adjacent to the fixed mold plate was flat, with the root section as the reference plane for deformation measurements. The gate design included a draft angle to facilitate demolding.

A localized thermal activation system (Supplementary Fig. 2a) was developed using ceramic heating elements (MCH, Φ9 mm × 1.5 mm, Supplementary Fig. 2b) for temperature control. A 500 W switching power supply (LRS-500-24) converted 220 V AC to 24 V DC to power 24 heating elements. Voltage regulation was achieved using DC regulators (XL4016), which supported an input range of 4–40 V and an output range of 1.25–36 V, enabling real-time voltage monitoring and adjustment. Each regulator controlled three heating elements, with eight regulators managing eight elements per impeller blade.

The experimental mold featured a modular cavity design with replaceable components, including a moving plate, fixed plate, insulation plate, and cavity plate (Fig. 2a). The cavity plate, attached to the moving template via threaded connections, included a square groove (70 × 80 × 4 mm³) for embedding heating elements and wiring. Thermal conductive silicone (Pinsai GK-920, resistant to 300 °C) secured the heating mold cavity plate, with a 2.5 mm gap for insulation plate installation.

The system’s heating capacity was evaluated using an infrared thermal imager (TUi260B, −20 °C to 550 °C range, 0.1 °C accuracy, < 500 ms delay). To minimize reflection artifacts, black tape was applied to the mold cavity wall (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Each heating element group (No. 1–8, corresponding to Zones 1–8) was tested individually for 180 s using a 24 V power supply. Temperature profiles exhibited a rapid initial increase followed by stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Numerical simulations using Moldex3D63 confirmed similar trends, with faster initial heating. Infrared thermal images at 150 s (Supplementary Fig. 2e) revealed localized temperature peaks at activated heating zones, with heat spreading to adjacent areas due to the mold’s high thermal conductivity. Elements No. 7 and 8, near the center, reached the highest temperatures due to reduced heat dissipation, while elements No. 1, 2, and 3, farthest from the center, recorded the lowest temperatures.

Finite element simulations were conducted using Moldex3D. The impeller geometry was modeled, and finite element meshing (tetrahedral mesh) was applied with a surface mesh size of 0.25 mm for the blade (minimum thickness: 0.5 mm) and 4 mm for other components (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The total mesh comprised 1,495,229 elements, including 212,037 for the part, 10,471 for the cold runner, and 1,272,721 for the mold. PP 579S material properties were sourced from the software database and characterized using the Cross-WLF viscosity model and a modified Tait pvT model63. IM machine parameters matched the Haitian ZE1200 specifications. The boundary conditions were defined to match the experimental setup. The melt temperature (Tmelt), injection pressure (pinj), packing pressure (ppack), packing time (tpack), and cooling time (tcool) were set according to Supplementary Table 1. Orthogonal design with 5-factor and 4-level was conducted to evaluate process parameter effects and identify optimal conditions. Local heating was excluded from this orthogonal investigation. The mold walls were assigned an initial temperature of 20 °C with a thermal conductivity of 29 W m−1 K−1. For the warpage simulation, the part was assumed to be fully constrained by the mold until ejection at the calculated ejection temperature of 90 °C, after which it was allowed to deform freely under the calculated residual stress field while cooling to an ambient temperature of 20 °C. The injection filling time of 0.19 s (Supplementary Fig. 3b), agrees well with the theoretical value derived from the specified injection speed (15 mm s−1), cavity volume (2.28 cm3), and screw diameter (32 mm). The analysis focused on maximum and minimum warpage deformation in the Z-direction and the Z-direction distance between measurement Point 2 and the datum Point 0. Under the regular IM orthogonal conditions, the impeller exhibited warpage displacement toward the gate side (negative Z-direction, Supplementary Fig. 3c), driven by the T gradient, with higher T near the gate. Sample No. 14 exhibited the smallest displacement (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The displacement had significant correlations with the maximum and minimum warpage values (Supplementary Fig. 3e). The range value (Supplementary Table 1,2) reflects the influence of each process parameter on the warpage deformation, ranked in descending order: tpack, pinj, ppack, tcool, and Tmelt (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Increasing tpack and tcool, as well as decreasing Tmelt, reduced warpage deformation; as pinj increased, warpage deformation initially increased and then decreased. Similarly, as ppack increased, warpage deformation first decreased and then increased (Supplementary Fig. 3g). Optimal parameters for maximum warpage deformation included Tmelt = 250 °C, pinj = 24 MPa, ppack = 24 MPa, tpack = 2 s, and tcool = 6 s.

Following parameter optimization, simulations incorporating the localized thermal activation system were performed using Moldex3D. Each heating element was activated at 16 W to match experimentally determined temperatures (Supplementary Table 3). The simulation results corresponding to warpage deformation caused by the single activation of heating element No. 1 were specially analyzed (Fig. 2b). The final Z-direction displacement for the 9 runs (No. 0–8) varies from each other (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Validation experiments were conducted using PP 579S pellets and the Haitian ZE1200 IM machine under the same process parameters as the simulations. The nozzle temperature was identical to Tmelt for all IM experiments. The injection speed was set to 15 mm s−1, and the screw rotation speed during plasticizing was maintained at 30 rpm. Heating elements were individually activated at 24 V (Supplementary Table 3). Ten impeller parts were produced for each experiment (No. 0–8, Supplementary Fig. 4b). After completion of the IM process, all molded parts were allowed to equilibrate at ambient conditions for at least 24 h prior to deformation measurement. Z-direction warpage deformation was measured using a laser displacement sensor (HG-C100, Panasonic, accuracy: 0.01 mm). A measurement frame was prepared by fixing three steel nails onto a glass slide with a leveling device and adhesive (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The impeller was placed with its parting surface facing upward. The blade root (1 mm thick) served as the reference plane. Each impeller inherently contained a mold-defined datum feature at the blade root (near the runner root), allowing consistent positioning of all samples. The primary datum point (No. 0) was defined at the midpoint of the blade root (Supplementary Fig. 1a), providing a fixed reference for locating all subsequent measurement points. To transfer measurement-point coordinates accurately onto the curved blade surfaces, we printed full-scale CAD drawings showing all measurement point locations. Benefiting from the thin and semi-transparent PP blade walls, each part was aligned directly over the drawing, enabling precise manual marking of the measurement points with a fine-tip marker. These marked points were then used for laser-distance measurements. Warpage displacement was calculated as the Z-direction difference between measurement points (Positions 1–8) and the datum point (Position 0). Although manual marking introduces some inevitable experimental variability, this was mitigated by robust replication. For each experimental condition, ten samples were molded, providing ten independent measurements at every designated point. The resulting mean values and deviation ranges (Supplementary Fig. 4d) confirm consistent measurement. In addition, a one-way ANOVA performed across the nine sets of ten consecutive molding cycles showed small pooled standard deviations, demonstrated a high measurement precision and a good production repeatability (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Note 1). The detailed results of measured displacement and comparison between simulation and experiment (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e) are provided in Supplementary Note 1, and the simulation data of p, T, and v distribution and profiles in the special runs of No. 0 and No. 8 (Supplementary Fig. 5) are also analyzed.

Selective in-mold bonding 4D IM

PP 579S and Haitian ZE1200 IM machine were utilized for selective in-mold bonding 4D IM. Experimental molds with three cavity structures—designed for cross-shaped, hand-shaped, and caterpillar-shaped parts—were employed (Fig. 3a).

A hot compaction machine (TY-7006, Jiangsu Tianyuan Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd) was used to fabricate PP sheets measuring 100 × 60 × 0.4 mm3. PP pellets were compacted at 180 °C and 6 MPa for 5 min. The prepared sheets were cut to the required dimensions for in-mold bonding. The small component piece was positioned at selected bonding locations in the mold and temporarily fixed using double-sided tape. During IM, the polymer melt encapsulated the piece, and the high melt temperature softened its surface, enabling fusion and solidification with the surrounding matrix. After demolding, residual adhesive on the part surface was removed with ethyl alcohol.

Numerical simulations of the IM process for the cross-shaped part were conducted using Moldex3D. The cross-shaped part geometry was modeled, and the total mesh comprised 337,197 elements, including 125,595 for the part, 17,645 for the runner, 70,869 for the insert, and 123,088 for the mold. The pinj was maintained at 35 MPa, and the mold temperature (Tmold) was set to 25 °C. The initial process conditions of Tmelt = 250 °C, ppack = 24 MPa, tpack = 4 s, and tcool = 13 s were initially used to analyze the IM process and warpage deformation of the root-position bonded part (Bond 1, Fig. 3b). A comparative analysis of three bonding positions (Bond 1–3) was subsequently conducted under identical process conditions: Tmelt = 220 °C, ppack = 18 MPa, tpack = 4 s, and tcool = 13 s (Supplementary Fig. 7a–e, Supplementary Movie 2, Supplementary Note 2). Experimental validation parts (Supplementary Fig. 7b) were fabricated using the Haitian ZE1200 IM machine under the same process parameters as the simulations. Part deformations were qualified in the Z-direction displacements using the HG-C100 laser displacement sensor with the measurement platform, relative to the pre-marked datum and measurement points on the parts (Supplementary Fig. 1b). A very strong linear correlation was observed between the simulated and experimental warpage displacements (R2 = 99.79%, Pearson r = 0.9989)49 (Supplementary Fig. 7d).

The Tmelt, ppack, tpack, and tcool were varied according to the RSM-designed conditions (Supplementary Table 12) to analyze their effects on the warpage deformation of the cross-shaped parts with Bond 1 (Supplementary Fig. 7f, g). The effect of ppack was most significant, followed by Tmelt. The influence of other single factors is insignificant. Higher Tmelt resulted in stronger interfacial bonding, leading to reduced warpage. Increasing ppack improved melt compensation and enhanced material consolidation at the bonding interface, which lowered shrinkage gradients and reduced warpage. However, at higher ppack (35 MPa), the melt became over-compressed, creating larger thermal gradients and higher frozen-in residual stresses, especially near the bonding interface, which increased warpage after demolding. The minimum in the curve therefore reflects the balance between these two competing effects. Increasing tpack and tcool slightly reduced displacement.

The local interfacial strength of the cross-shaped part was assessed using a nanoindenter (G200, KLA Corporation) in compliance with ISO 14577. To meet the equipment’s size requirements, the bonded portion of the part was cut into samples measuring 14 × 10 × 1 mm3. The embedded part’s surface area was tested in load-displacement mode, with a maximum displacement of 2.5 μm, a holding time of 20 s, and a loading speed of 10 nm s−1. Seven test points were randomly measured on each of the three samples (Bond 1–3). The load-displacement curves (Supplementary Fig. 8a) at Bond 1 for different test points show significant deviations, indicating sensitivity to T and p changes and residual stress. In contrast, Bonds 2 and 3 exhibited smaller deviations, indicating more uniform bonding. Elastic modulus and hardness were highest for Bond 2, while Bond 1 showed significant deviations, and Bond 3 demonstrated moderate and stable values (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The modulus distribution of Bond 3 was most uniform, slightly lower than Bond 2, and higher than Bond 1 in several results, indicating sufficient bonding from effective melting and resolidification despite its distance from the gate (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

RSM modeling for programmable part shapes of selective in-molding bonding 4D IM

The Box-Behnken design within the RSM framework was employed, following the six-stage modeling procedure outlined (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The bonding position was defined as the variable I. Three bonding positions (Bond 1, 2, and 3) were defined along one blade of the cross-shaped part, spanning from the root to the edge (Fig. 3a). Modeling was conducted separately for each bonding position. Four IM process parameters (Tmelt, ppack, tpack, and tcool) were considered as variables II, III, IV, and V, respectively. Each variable was assigned three levels, and a total of 29 experimental runs were designed (Supplementary Table 12).

The displacement, defined as the difference in the Z-direction between the measured value at measurement point No. 2 (part edge) and the datum point No. 0 (blade root) (Supplementary Fig. 1b), served as the response variable and regulation target. RSM experiments were first conducted for Bond 1 under the 29 designed conditions. Given the high coefficient of determination (R2 = 99.79%) between experimental and simulation results, and the smaller displacements observed for Bond 2 and 3, data collection for these bonding positions was conducted using simulation results.

The quadratic model, demonstrating the best predictive ability, was selected to fit the collected data. All terms were retained in the final equations (Supplementary Table 13), which exhibited high accuracy (Supplementary Table 14).

The predictive models (Supplementary Equations (3)–(5)) were used to determine the optimal process parameters for achieving target displacement values (3.6, 3.4, 3.2, 2.2, 1.8, and 0.5 mm). The desirability function was employed to identify variable combinations that satisfied the target. Validation experiments demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and actual values (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Table 15), confirming the model’s robustness and applicability.

Validation of selective in-molding bonding 4D IM for other parts

Selective in-mold bonding 4D IM was conducted on the hand-shaped part with four bond parts. Four bonding positions were used together, Bond 1 to 4, respectively located at four positions of the four “fingers” (Fig. 3e). Numerical simulation of the IM process for the hand-shaped part were conducted using Moldex3D. The simulation model comprised 493,095 elements, including 150,164 for the part, 34,561 for the runner, 115,042 for the insert, and 193,328 for the mold. The pinj was maintained at 35 MPa, and the Tmold was set to 25 °C. The Tmelt, ppack, tpack, and tcool were varied according to the designed conditions (Supplementary Table 16). The displacement was collected from simulation results according to the measurement positions (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Bond 1 led to the largest displacement range (3.027 to 3.768 mm), while Bond 4 led to the smallest displacement range (−0.421 to −0.541 mm). RSM model (Supplementary Equation (6)) for Bond 1 was fitted (Supplementary Table 17,18) with a predicted R2 of 98.75%, and further applied for programmable shape control with the reverse derivation of parameter values with the desirability function. Three parts for each targeting experiment were fabricated. Results showed a high accuracy (R2 = 96.94%) to the target values (Fig. 3f).

Selective in-mold bonding 4D IM was applied to caterpillar-shaped parts (Supplementary Fig. 1d) with four bonding positions (Bond 1–4, Fig. 3g). Simulations and experiments were conducted under identical process conditions: pinj = 35 MPa, Tmold = 25 °C, Tmelt = 250 °C, ppack = 18 MPa, tpack = 4 s, and tcool = 13 s. The simulation model for the caterpillar-shaped part included 173,217 elements, with 42,943 for the part, 34,460 for the runner, 25,742 for the insert, and 70,072 for the mold. Results illustrate various shapes in “–”, “v”, “n”, “u”, “N”, “M”, “W”, etc., and the displacement ranged from −2.98 to 3.65 mm (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e).

Experiments were conducted to investigate the influence of bond part geometry on deformation. The cross-shaped parts with bond parts of six shapes in “/”, “\”, “<”, “>”, “s”, and “x” were fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The Z-direction distances between three measurement points (1,2,3) and the datum points (Supplementary Fig. 10b) were measured using the HG-C100 laser displacement sensor. Bending and torsional deformations in different directions occurred at the blade edges of the six samples (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

The selective in-mold bonding was integrated with localized thermal activation. A “C”-shaped bond part was cut from the PP sheet for selective in-mold bonding, while the localized thermal activation system was activated with voltages H1 (11.1 V), H2 (18.8 V), H3(10.2 V), H4 (22.3 V), H5 (2.8 V), H6 (22.3 V), H7 (23.8 V), and H8 (23.6 V), corresponding to the target displacement of 3 mm (Supplementary Table 11). Combined geometry with bending and torsion was obtained, and the largest displacement of 5.48 mm in Z-direction was realized. The three blades exhibited different deformations, highlighting the effect of bonding positions (Supplementary Fig. 11). One “C”-shaped bond part was pushed to the blade’s end, primarily exhibiting bending, while the other two “C”-shaped bond parts remained relatively stationary but in different positions, resulting in reversed torsional deformations.

Fiber-directed deformation and property regulation of 4D IM for composites

Polyamide 6 (PA6) granules reinforced with 30 wt% short glass fibers (SGF-PA6, A1G6) were used as the primary material. The short glass fibers had an initial length of 3 mm, and the SGF-PA6 exhibited a density of 1.55 g cm−3 and a contraction rate of 0.5–0.7%. The flexural strength and modulus of SGF-PA6 were 180 MPa and 7.5 GPa, respectively, measured under a loading speed of 2 mm min−1 according to ISO 178.

For the bond part, a prepreg of PA6 reinforced with 60 wt% unidirectional continuous glass fibers (CGF-PA6, CFRT-GA6030) was employed. The CGF-PA6 bond part had dimensions of 0.3 mm (thickness) × 20 mm (width) × 40 mm (length) and a density of 1.72 g cm−3. Its flexural strength and modulus were 1050 MPa and 45 GPa, respectively, measured under a loading speed of 2 mm min−1 according to ISO 14125. Both SGF-PA6 and CGF-PA6, purchased from CGN JUNER New Materials Co., Ltd., shared the same PA6 matrix, ensuring strong interfacial bonding (42).

A dual-cavity flat part mold and the Haitian ZE1200 IM machine were used. Samples (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 12) were prepared with three local bonding positions and two fiber directions (weft: aligned with the flow direction; warp: perpendicular to the flow direction) under consistent IM process conditions: Tmelt = 270 °C, pinj = 120 MPa, ppack = 96 MPa, tpack = 4 s, and tcool = 13 s. SGF-PA6 granules were dried at 105 °C for 5 h before molding. Three samples were prepared for each experimental group, and warpage deformation was measured using the HG-C100 laser displacement sensor relative to pre-marked points (Supplementary Fig. 12, Fig. 4b).

Numerical simulations of the IM process for the CGF/SGF-PA6 part were conducted using Moldex3D. The model comprised 633,589 solid mesh elements, including 255,382 for the part, 53,504 for the runner, 68,836 for the bond part, and 255,867 for the mold. Material properties for SGF-PA6 and CGF-PA6 were approximated using PA6 Kepamid 1500SFU (30 wt% glass fiber) and PA6 Kepamid 1350GF (50 wt% glass fiber) from the software’s database (51). Fiber orientation, crystallization effects, and solid-state shrinkage were incorporated in the simulations. Fiber alignment was defined according to the weft and warp directions of the CGF-PA6. Simulation results (Supplementary Figs. 13,14,15, Supplementary Movie 4) illustrated the displacement of the parts and the effects of fiber orientation and random fiber orientation combined with the effects of in-plane Δvt1 and out-of-plane Δvt2.

Flexural tests (three-point bending) were conducted using an electronic universal test machine (WDT-W, Chengde Precision Testing Machine Co., Ltd., displacement resolution: 0.001 mm, force accuracy: ± 0.5%) in compliance with ISO 14125:1998. The bond part faced upward during testing, with a support span of 16 mm and a crosshead speed of 1 mm min−1. Stress and strain values were calculated from force and displacement data (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Three samples were tested for each type of bonding (Bond 1–3 and Bond 1’–3’). In comparison, the samples without bonding (No bond) were also prepared and tested. Failure sections were examined using an optical microscope (CX40MRT, Shenzhen RUNXING Optical Instrument Co., Ltd.) (Supplementary Fig. 16b).

A full factorial RSM design was applied, with two independent variables (bonding position and fiber direction) at three levels each (Supplementary Table 22). Response variables included the average values of the maximum warpage, strength, modulus, and failure strain. The effects of bonding positions and fiber orientations on the four response variables were analyzed (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 17). A linear model was selected for strength and failure strain, while a quadratic model was used for warpage and modulus. Final predictive models (Supplementary Equations (7)–(10)) were derived (Supplementary Table 23), demonstrating predictive accuracy (predicted R2 = 79.5%, 68.09%, 99.21%, and 54.37% for warpage, strength, modulus, and failure stain, respectively) (Supplementary Note 3). The corresponding Pearson r values were 0.892, 0.825, 0.996, and 0.737, indicating strong ( ≥ 0.7) and very strong ( ≥ 0.9) linear relationships49. The difference between adjusted R2 and predicted R2 was less than 0.2, and adequate precision values exceeded 4, indicating robust model performance43 (Supplementary Table 24).

Process-induced deformation and functional element bonding of 4D IM for elastomers

Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU-6085F, Baoding Bangtai Polymer New Materials Co., Ltd) pellets were used. The TPU had a density of 1.11 ± 0.02 g cm−3 (ASTM D792), a tensile strength of 25 ± 5 MPa (ASTM D412), an elongation at break of 650 ± 150% (ASTM D412), a tear strength of 100 ± 30 kgf cm−1 (ASTM D624), and grey scale ≥ 4.5 after 24 h ultraviolet (UV) exposure (ISO 105-A02). Pellets were dried at 105 °C for 5 h before processing. The experimental mold, featuring cross-shaped, hand-shaped, and caterpillar-shaped cavities, was used to produce TPU parts under varying IM conditions (Fig. 5a). Part shape regulation was directly achieved by adjusting IM process parameters. To create large deformations, higher Tmelt = 220 °C, lower ppack = 28 MPa, and shorter tpack = 3, 5, and 10 s and tcool = 6 s were applied. For reduced deformation, the IM conditions were set to Tmelt = 210 °C, ppack = 28 MPa, tpack = 10 s, and tcool = 20 s.

The tensile forces withstood by the hand-shaped parts with curling fingers were measured using a digital force gauge (SMF-20, resolution: 0.001 kg, Dongguan Jingru Measuring Instrument Technology Co., Ltd). The tensile forces on each finger of two hand-shaped TPU parts varied according to the degree of curling (Fig. 5b). The maximum force reached 0.7 N, with greater curling corresponding to higher tensile force resistance.

Leveraging TPU’s inherent elasticity, hand-shaped parts with curled fingers were fabricated, enabling their use as grippers for objects such as pens, clippers, and balls (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Movie 5, Supplementary Note 4).

To enhance functionality, selective in-mold bonding was integrated with TPU, incorporating magnetic bonding parts. Fe3O4 powders (CY-Fe304-02, Shanghai CHENGYUE) with a particle size of 1 μm, 99.9% purity, density of 5.18 g cm−3, and bulk density of 1.9 g cm−3 were blended with TPU at weight ratios of 7/3, 6/4, 5/5, and 4/6. Blending was performed using a torque blender (RM-200C, Harbin HAPU Electric Technology Co., Ltd) at 180 °C and 80 rpm for 5 minutes. The blended TPU/Fe3O4 was hot-compacted at 180 °C and 6 MPa for 5 minutes, followed by cooling for 5 min, to produce 0.4 mm thick sheets (Supplementary Fig. 18a).

The cross-sectional morphology of the TPU/Fe3O4 sheets was analyzed using the optical microscope (CX40MRT) (Supplementary Fig. 18b). Agglomeration of magnetic particles increased with higher Fe3O4 content.

Magnetic induction was tested using a permanent magnet (N35, 35 × 35 × 15 mm3). A Gauss meter (GS-25, AIKESI) measured the magnetic field strength along the central axis of the magnet, with measurements taken at 5 mm intervals from the magnet’s surface. Magnetic induction decreased from 1535.5 Gs to 0 as a function of distance (Supplementary Fig. 18c).

The surface magnetic induction intensity of the TPU/Fe3O4 sheets was measured using the Gauss meter. The sheet (100 × 60 × 0.4 mm3) was divided into 16 regions, with five measurements taken per region (Supplementary Fig. 18d). The average magnetic induction intensity initially increased and then decreased with higher Fe3O4 content (Supplementary Fig. 18e). The highest average intensity, 1.201 Gs, was observed for the 6/4 ratio, followed by 1.157 Gs for the 5/5 ratio. The reduction at higher Fe3O4 content was attributed to particle aggregation, which compromised magnetic field uniformity.

TPU/Fe3O4 strips (6 × 60 × 0.4 mm3) with varying Fe3O4 content were tested for driving performance. Each strip was clamped over a 40 mm length using double-sided tape, leaving a 20 mm free segment for testing, with the free end positioned beneath the permanent magnet (Supplementary Fig. 18f). Vertical displacement under magnetic actuation was recorded (Supplementary Fig. 18g). Increasing Fe3O4 content enhanced magnetic responsiveness but also induce particle aggregation, reducing magnetic field uniformity and increasing composite hardness and density. Consequently, actuation performance declined at the 4/6 TPU/Fe3O4 ratio compared with the optimal 5/5 ratio. The 5/5 ratio was finally selected for subsequent IM experiments.

The 5/5 TPU/Fe3O4 sheet was cut into pieces for selective bonding 4D IM with cross-shaped, hand-shaped, and caterpillar-shaped molds (Fig. 3a, Fig. 5d). Process conditions included Tmelt = 210 °C, ppack = 28 MPa, tpack = 10 s, and tcool = 20 s.

The interfacial bonding properties of the hand-shaped TPU part bonded with 5/5 TPU/Fe3O4 were characterized using the nanoindenter (G200, KLA Corporation) in compliance with ISO 14577. The bonded portion was cut into measuring 10 × 5 × 1 mm3 and tested in load-displacement mode, with a maximum displacement of 2.5 μm, a holding time of 20 s, and a loading speed of 10 nm s−1. For comparison, the pure TPU part was also measured. The elastic modulus and hardness of the samples were obtained (Supplementary Fig. 19). The magnetic sample showed an average elastic modulus of 0.078 GPa and hardness of 0.01 GPa, higher than those of the pure TPU sample. The elastic recovery rate (Rr) was calculated as the ratio of the recovered displacement (Δx) to the maximum displacement (xmax). The magnetic performance of the hand-shaped part was tested by positioning the magnetic tip beneath the permanent magnet.
