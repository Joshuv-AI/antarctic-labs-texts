Bound by tradition: severe pediculosis capitis in a breastfeeding mother with religious headwear
Authors: Varun H, Hanumanthaiah H C, Adarshlata Singh, Bhushan Madke, Lamiya Latheef, Prerit Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 25-year-old breastfeeding woman from rural India presented with a 2-month history of intense scalp pruritus, formication and fever. Examination revealed a severe Pediculus humanus capitis infestation involving her scalp, face and religious headscarf, with extensive live lice, nits, pustules, excoriations, crusted papules and dermatopathic lymphadenopathy. Treatment with permethrin 1% lotion and weekly oral ivermectin for 4 weeks, in conjunction with broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive care, resulted in near-complete eradication. This case represents one of the most severe presentations of pediculosis capitis documented, demonstrating how cultural practices can impede early detection and complicate management.

Case report

A 25-year-old woman from rural India who was breastfeeding a 4-month-old infant and who routinely wore a niqab (a religious garment that covers the head, body and face except for the eyes) presented with a 2-month history of unremitting scalp pruritus and formication. She also reported a 5-day history of fever refractory to self-administered medications. Similar symptoms were reported in her husband and her infant.

Clinical examination revealed numerous live lice on the scalp and face, with several clinging to her niqab [Figure 1; Video S1 (see Supporting Information)]. In addition, multiple nits, pustules, excoriations and crusted papules were noted, along with palpable, nontender lymphadenopathy of the anterior jugular and the occipital nodes.

Clinical image taken before treatment, illustrating the extensive presence of head lice and nits attached to the hair shaft.

A diagnosis of extensive pediculosis capitis infestation with secondary bacterial infection and dermatopathic lymphadenopathy was established. Given the extensive infestation and pyoderma, HIV serology was advised, which returned a nonreactive result. Baseline complete blood count showed neutrophilic leucocytosis (78%), without features of immunosuppression. All other routine blood parameters were within normal limits. The patient reported no long-term medications, significant weight loss or chronic fatigue. Culture and sensitivity from the pus and crusts revealed a growth of methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus.

Despite no overt psychiatric symptoms, the patient was referred for psychiatric assessment due to observed ­self-neglect and poor hygiene. The evaluation revealed distress attributable to pruritus and social stigma, with no evidence of significant psychopathology.

The patient was treated with permethrin 1% lotion along with oral ivermectin at a dose of 200 μg kg–1 administered once weekly for 4 weeks, for parasite eradication. Antipyretics were provided for fever management, and oral amoxicillin 500 mg was prescribed three times daily for 7 days, guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing for the secondary pyoderma. All household members and contacts were concurrently treated. Environmental decontamination included hot-water laundering (>60 °C) of headwear, clothing and bedding, followed by drying in direct sunlight. Additionally, thorough vacuuming of floors, carpets and upholstered furniture (if any) was recommended. Head shaving was discussed as a rapid method to reduce parasitic load but was declined by the patient and family due to religious restrictions. Two weeks after initiating therapy, a marked reduction in the parasitic load was observed, with only nits visible upon close inspection (Figure 2). A hair mount examination revealed empty nits attached to the hair shaft (Figure 3).

Discussion

Pediculosis capitis, caused by Pediculus humanus capitis, is a common parasitic infestation globally, affecting all socioeconomic groups.1 While most patients present with pruritus alone, severe infestation can be complicated by secondary pyodermas, eczematization and dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, potentially mimicking primary bacterial infections.2–4 Topical pediculicides remain the first-line treatment; however, severe, recalcitrant infestations may require systemic therapy with oral ivermectin or cotrimoxazole.5,6 While shaving the head is generally deemed culturally unacceptable, it can serve as an effective and immediate means of reducing parasitic load.7

Ivermectin, a semi-synthetic derivative of avermectin B1 (originally isolated from Streptomyces avermitilis), acts by binding to parasitic glutamate-gated chloride channels and, to a lesser extent, γ-aminobutyric acid receptors. This results in an uncontrolled influx of chloride ions, resulting in flaccid paralysis and death of the parasites.8

In this patient, oral ivermectin was considered appropriate, despite ongoing breastfeeding. Although excreted into breast milk, available evidence indicates that infant exposure is minimal. In a small pharmacokinetic study by Ogbuokiri et al., involving four women, their infants’ ivermectin intake via breast milk was estimated to be approximately 2.75 μg kg–1, equivalent to a relative infant dose (RID) of 2.75%.9 These values are substantially below commonly accepted safety thresholds (an RID <10% is widely considered acceptable), indicating minimal risk of adverse effects.10,11 Similarly, Rodari et al. described a case of a woman with Strongyloides stercoralis and human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 co-infection, treated with a single 200-μg dose of ivermectin. The estimated infant exposure in breast milk this case was approximately 1.1–1.39 μg kg–1, amounts considered too low to induce adverse effects.12

A multicentric observational study by Levy et al., including 170 infants and children aged 1–64 months (weighing <15 kg) were treated for scabies and received a mean dose of 223 μg kg–1 of oral ivermectin. Mild adverse effects such as eczema flare-ups and single episodes of diarrhoea and vomiting were seen in only 4% of the participants, reinforcing the safety profile of systemic ivermectin in infants.13

Furthermore, Morand et al.14 described recommendations from the Centre of Evidence of the French Society of Dermatology, developed by a 17-member working group comprising dermatologists, gynaecologists, infectious disease paediatricians, pharmacotoxicologists, parasitologists and methodologists. After a thorough review, the group proposed permethrin, benzyl benzoate and ivermectin as first-line therapies for scabies in women who are breastfeeding. While treatment guidelines for scabies may not be directly applicable to pediculosis, these findings can allow a cautious conclusion that ivermectin use during breastfeeding is likely to be safe for the infant. Consequently, when clinically indicated, systemic ivermectin may be considered in patients who are breastfeeding without interruption of breastfeeding, provided infant monitoring and counselling are performed.14–16

Religious headwear carries profound historical and cultural significance across multiple faiths, both in India and globally. Examples include ghoonghat and Mysore peta (Hinduism), dastar (Sikhism), the hijab and niqab (Islam), and tichel/sheitel (Judaism), among others. When treating patients who observe such practices, it is essential to approach medical care with cultural sensitivity, offering alternative treatment modalities that accommodate and respect their lifestyles.

In communities where women are required to wear such coverings at all times, infestations and other conditions may remain undetected for prolonged periods.17,18 Furthermore, the social stigma associated with lice infestations often leads families to conceal their condition and resort to ineffective – or even harmful – home remedies instead of seeking appropriate medical care.19

In this case, the apparent self-neglect was attributable to cultural constraints on headwear removal, barriers to healthcare access and social stigma, rather than any underlying psychiatric pathology. Throughout treatment, the patient displayed consistent concern for her infant’s wellbeing, further supporting this conclusion.

In this instance, the patient’s autonomy in determining her course of treatment was constrained by societal customs and religious norms. The option of head shaving, despite its clinical benefit, was declined by the patient and her family, who prioritized religious adherence over immediate medical relief. As a result, she underwent prolonged pharmacological treatments, emphasizing the importance of health education and empowering women in medical decision-making. This case highlights the complex interplay between a common parasitic condition complicated by culturally driven treatment limitations, emphasizing the need for culturally sensitive dermatological care and patient-centred approaches to improve adherence and clinical outcomes across diverse populations.
