Insight into the genetic diversity of Hyalomma scupense, Ixodes persulcatus, Haemaphysalis punctata and Argas persicus in Kazakhstan
Authors: Madina Awulibieer, Zhumanov Kairat Toksanbaevich, Daerhan Maidanniyati, Sansyzbay Abylay Rysbayuli, Khizat Serik, Sándor Hornok, Meihua Yang, Kassen Aigul Zhenisbekkyzy, Yuanzhi Wang, Wurelihazi Hazihan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyalomma scupense, Ixodes persulcatus, Haemaphysalis punctata and Argas persicus have a broad distribution range across the Palearctic. However, their phylogenetic relationships are rarely reported. In this study, a total of 19,861 nymphs and adult ticks were collected from 41 sampling sites in 22 districts/cities across eight oblasts in the eastern, southern and western parts of Kazakhstan. Thirteen tick species, originated from 608 representative ticks, were identified. Regarding to Hy. scupense, I. persulcatus, Ha. punctata and A. persicus ticks from all sampling sites, 100 representative ticks were further phylogenetically analyzed based on cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene. The results showed that (i) Hy. scupense is the most widespread and abundant tick species in Kazakhstan, having ten haplotypes, of which one from East Kazakhstan was the progenitor of that from Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (XUAR, northwestern China); (ii) I. persulcatus ticks from Almaty Oblast (eastern Kazakhstan), clustered with those from Burqin County (XUAR), were the progenitors of those from Japan; (iii) Ha. punctata specimens analyzed here had three distinct lineages, and the lineage in Almaty Oblast was originated from ticks reported in Visegrad (Hungary); and (iv) A. persicus ticks in Turkistan Oblast were the progenitors of ticks in Algeria and Egypt. These phylogenetic relationships indicate larger scale genetic exchange between populations of tick species that frequently associate with migratory birds (as exemplified by I. persulcatus and Ha. punctata) or are inadvertently transported with poultry during animal trading (i.e., A. persicus).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10493-025-01078-8.

Introduction

Ticks are blood-sucking arthropods classified into three families (the hard ticks: Ixodidae; the soft ticks: Argasidae; and Nuttalliellidae) with about 994 known species globally (Khan et al. 2025). Each tick species exhibits distinct and characteristic biological attributes. Among these, the number of host individuals utilized as the source of blood meal during the whole life cycle of hard ticks will be of special importance, because the larva, the nymph and the female suck blood only once. For instance, Hyalomma scupense is a one-host ixodid tick species that completes its whole life cycle on a single host individual, infesting mainly large domestic animals such as cattle, horses, sheep and camels (Apanaskevich et al. 2013). On the other hand, Ixodes persulcatus is a three-host tick species, because its larva, nymph and female parasitize three different individuals of mammals or birds in its distribution range in the Eurasian temperate continental zone (Pfäffle et al. 2017a). Haemaphysalis punctata is another three-host ixodid tick species that is highly ecologically adaptable and can tolerate different climatic environments (Pfäffle et al. 2017b). However, Argas persicus is a soft (argasid) tick species, and therefore can repeat its blood-sucking within the nymph or adult stage when parasitic on poultry and wild birds (Tian et al. 2025). The unique biological characteristics of different tick species determine their geographical distribution and host preferences, which in turn influence the global and local spread of tick-borne pathogens (Zhang et al. 2019; Kürschner et al. 2021).

Kazakhstan, covering 2,724,900 square kilometers and bordering Russia, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and China, is the ninth-largest country in the world. Its territory is higher in the southeast and lower in the west and southwest (Tursunova et al. 2022). To date, 45 tick species have been recorded in Kazakhstan, belonging to seven genera including Ixodes, Hyalomma, Dermacentor, Rhipicephalus, Argas, Ornithodoros and Haemaphysalis (Perfilyeva et al. 2020). Previous studies have analyzed four tick species native to Kazakhstan, including Hyalomma asiaticum, Dermacentor marginatus, Rhipicephalus turanicus and Haemaphysalis erinacei (Zheng et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2021).

As the core genomic marker for DNA barcoding, the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene not only exhibits the strongest species discrimination ability when evaluating ticks of the genus Hyalomma using three molecular markers (16 S rRNA, 12 S rRNA and COI), but also effectively clarifies the genetic relationships among species of this genus (Jomli et al. 2025). In the present study, we used the COI gene to analyze the phylogenetic relationships and intraspecific genetic diversity of another four tick species—Hyalomma scupense, Ixodes persulcatus, Haemaphysalis punctata and Argas persicus.

Materials and methods

Tick sampling and morphological identification

From 2016 to 2024, a total of 19,861 nymphs and adult ticks were collected from 41 sampling sites in 22 districts/cities across the eastern, southern and western parts of Kazakhstan (Fig. 1). The sampling sites included different habitats like high mountains, wetlands, forests, semi-deserts, gobi deserts, lakes, mountainous areas, and meadows. In these places both questing (off-host) and feeding (on host) ticks were collected. Tick-infested hosts included 6 species of mammals and one species of chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus). All ticks were morphologically identified according to standard taxonomic keys (Estrada-Peña et al. 2017) and then placed into tubes with 75% ethanol for storage prior to DNA extraction. A total of 608 representative ticks were selected according to their sampling sites and tick species (shown in Appendix Table 1).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

Regarding Hy. scupense, I. persulcatus, Ha. punctata and A. persicus, 100 representative ticks were further used to analyze genetic diversity (shown in Appendix Table 2). First, the DNA was extracted from these ticks individually using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This was followed by PCR amplification of an approximately 710-bp-long part of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) encoding gene with the primers LCO1490 (5’- GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G −3’) and HCO2198 (5’ - TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA- 3’) (Folmer et al. 1994). PCR products were purified using the TIANgel Midi Purification Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), and sequenced with the Sanger method.

Sequence and phylogenetic analysis

Sequences were aligned and trimmed using MEGA 11, and the resulting alignment was exported in the appropriate format. Genetic diversity was estimated using DnaSP ver. 5.10.01 (Librado and Rozas 2009), with calculated indices including the number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (π) (Nei and Li 1979). Median-joining (Bandelt et al. 1999) networks were constructed using Network ver. 10.2.0 to visualize haplotype configurations and their genetic relationships (Wetjen et al. 2020).

Results

A total of 19,861 nymphs and adult ticks, representing 13 species and 6 genera, were collected from eight oblasts in the eastern, southern, and western parts of Kazakhstan. Hy. scupense was the most prevalent [8757 (2128 nymphs and 6629 adults), 45.49%], followed by D. marginatus [3987 (7 nymphs and 3980 adults), 20.38%], Hy. asiaticum (2760 adults, 12.49%), A. persicus [1651 (615 nymphs and 1036 adults), 8.31%], Hy. anatolicum (1191 adults, 5.99%), R. turanicus [922 (63 nymphs and 859 adults), 4.64%], Hyalomma excavatum [325 adults, 1.63%], Hyalomma marginatum [147 adults, 0.74%], Rhipicephalus sanguineus [39 adults, 0.19%], Ha. erinacei [32 adults, 0.16%], Ha. punctata [24 adults, 0.12%], D. reticulatus [16 adults, 0.08%] and I. persulcatus [10 adults, 0.05%] (shown in Appendix Table 1). The most important characters that served for morphological identification of ticks are shown in Appendix Fig. 1.

Regarding the genetic diversity of Hy. scupense, I. persulcatus, Ha. punctata, and A. persicus, network diagrams were constructed from haplotypes of the COI gene. These revealed the following relationships. First, Hy. scupense had ten haplotypes, six (H-1, H-2, H-5, H-7, H-9 and H-10) of which were found in Kazakhstan. Its haplotype diversity was 0.970, and nucleotide diversity was 0.01065. The H-3 and H-2 haplotypes were the most dominant. Interestingly, the H-4 and H-6 haplotypes from XUAR evolved as new from the H-7 haplotype (East Kazakhstan Oblast), while H-3 and H-8 haplotypes from Pakistan emerged directly from the ancestor (Fig. 2A). Secondly, I. persulcatus had seven haplotypes. Its haplotype diversity was 0.964, and nucleotide diversity was 0.007. The H-5 haplotype from Almaty Oblast and XUAR of China was the most dominant. Genetic exchange occurred between the H-1 haplotype from Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (northern China) and the H-7 haplotype from the Far East Sakhalin Island (Russia). The H-2 haplotype from Japan and the H-3 haplotype from Western Siberia (Russia) newly evolved from the H-7 haplotype (Russian Far East, Sakhalin Island) and the H-6 haplotype (Northern Altai, Russia), respectively (Fig. 2B). Thirdly, Ha. punctata had six haplotypes, which evolved into three lineages. Its haplotype diversity was 0.952, and nucleotide diversity was 0.00707. The H-2 haplotype from Visegrad (Hungary) assumed an ancestral position in the phylogeny. The H-6 haplotype from Almaty Oblast originated from the H-1 haplotype from XUAR (Fig. 2C). Finally, A. persicus had five haplotypes. The haplotype diversity was 0.9333, and the nucleotide diversity 0.00773. The ancestry of H-2 haplotype from Egypt and of the H-3 haplotype from Setif (Algeria) can be traced back to the H-4 haplotype (Turkistan Oblast). The H-5 haplotype (Gansu Province, northwestern China), H-4 haplotype (Kazakhstan: Turkistan) and the H-1 haplotype (Almaty Oblast) represented independent branches (Fig. 2D).

Discussion

Kazakhstan, the largest Central Asian country, has specific geographical characteristics (Kairanbayeva et al. 2022). The Altai Mountains, Tarbagatai Mountains, Alatau Mountains, and Tian Shan Mountains stretch in its eastern and southeastern regions, and its altitude ranges from 1300 to 3400 m above sea level (Chlachula 2020; Gillespie et al. 2021). At the same time, plains, semi-deserts, and hills are located in the central region, while lakes and wetlands are distributed around the Caspian Sea in the southwestern region (Hu et al. 2020a). In this study, a country-wide surveillance on tick species was carried out in east, south and west regions of Kazakhstan, including 41 sampling sites in 22 districts/cities of eight oblasts. A total of 19,861 nymphs and adult ticks, belonging to 13 tick species, were collected.

Hyalomma scupense is commonly found in semi-arid regions (Gharbi and Darghouth 2014). In Kazakhstan, natural semi-desert and desert zones occupy almost 70% of the territory (Hu et al. 2020b). There are six major deserts including the Kyzylkum Desert (which extends to Uzbekistan), the Moyunkum Desert, the Ryn Desert, the Sary-esik Atrau Desert, the Greater Barsuki Desert, and the Atrau Desert. In this study, Hy. scupense was found widely distributed in 21 sites, 13 districts/cities in eight oblasts in the eastern, southern, and western parts of Kazakhstan. Furthermore, Hy. scupense shows high haplotype diversity (0.970), high nucleotide diversity (0.01065), rich population genotypes and significant base variation, suggesting that this species has strong adaptability to different environments (Xu et al. 2019). The network analysis showed that i) Hy. scupense compromised six haplotypes (H-1, H-2, H-5, H-7, H-9 and H-10) in Kazakhstan, which were respectively found in Zhambyl District (Almaty Oblast, from cattle), Shymkent City (Turkistan Oblast, from cattle) and Aral District (Kyzylorda Oblast, from sheep), two districts (Koksu and Alakol) and Taldyqorghan City in Jetisu Oblast from cattle, Zaysan District (East Kazakhstan Oblast, from cattle), Oskemen City (East Kazakhstan Oblast, from sheep), Aktobe City (Aktobe Oblast, from cattle). This study found that the haplotype diversity of Hy. scupense is mainly associated with its geographical distribution rather than parasitic hosts. Two haplotypes (H-3 and H-8) were found in Pakistan. Interestingly, the H-7 haplotype sampled in East Kazakhstan was the progenitor of H-4 and H-6 haplotypes in XUAR of China. This finding can probably be explained by connections between populations via wildlife migration (Yang et al. 2021). Data of the present study support the hypothesis that Hy. scupense likely originated in Central Asia, but ultimate confirmation of this may require more background data (especially from Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan).

Ixodes persulcatus has a broad geographical range across 14 Eurasian countries, including Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan in Central Asia (Wang et al. 2023). It mainly inhabits broad-leaved forests, spruce forests, and mixed coniferous-broadleaved forests, and can be found at altitudes up to 3000 m (Pfäffle et al. 2017a). In Russia, this species is thought to have a more limited spectrum of habitats, restricted to the steppe and forest steppe (Yasyukevich et al. 2009). In this study, I. persulcatus, found in mixed coniferous broad-leaved forests and forest-steppe regions in Zhambyl District (Almaty Oblast, from cattle), harbored high haplotype diversity (0.964) but moderate nucleotide diversity (0.007), which is associated with population expansion and the occupation of a stable ecological niche (habitat mixed deciduous-coniferous forest) (Zhang et al. 2014; Gollner et al. 2016). The network analysis demonstrated that i) I. persulcatus had three mitochondrial lineages. Two out of them, distributed in Russia, included three haplotypes (H-3, H-4 and H-6). The last lineage also included a haplotype (H-7) from Russia. The H-5 haplotype was shared in Almaty Oblast and XUAR of China, while H-2 haplotype from Japan evolved from lineages in Kazakhstan and China. Previously, I. persulcatus was documented to parasitize birds from families including Corvidae, Fringillidae, Hirundinidae, Motacillidae, Muscicapidae, Sylvidae, and Turdidae (Sormunen et al. 2022; Keve et al. 2022; Laaksonen et al. 2018; Hyland et al. 2000). Given the seasonal migration of these birds, I. persulcatus is likely transported by multi-regional migratory birds in Japan, and this might facilitate its dispersal while preserving genetic connectedness over large geographical distances (shown in Appendix Fig. 3).

Haemaphysalis punctata has a broad Palearctic distribution (Guglielmone et al. 2023). However, it is not a dominant tick species in this region and has a relatively small population size. This tick species can survive in extremely diverse habitats, including humid, temperate, cold and arid environments (Pfäffle et al. 2017b). In this study, Ha. punctata also harbored high haplotype diversity (0.952) but moderate nucleotide diversity (0.00707), which reflects its ability to adapt to diverse environments despite its relatively small overall population size (Kojima et al. 2000). The network analysis revealed that i) Ha. punctata had three mitochondrial genetic lineages. One lineage was reported from Hungary (H-3 haplotype), while another was found both in Iran (H-4 haplotype) and Romania (H-5 haplotype). The last lineage occurred in Almaty Oblast (H-6 haplotype) and XUAR (H-1 haplotype). Interestingly, all the three lineages appear to originate from Visegrád in Hungary (H-2 haplotype). Ha. punctata is a three-host tick species with high ecological adaptability. Animal migration could thus allow its spread to and establishment in various regions, where it eventually evolved to different lineages and haplotypes (Liebisch et al. 1976; Medlock et al. 2018).

The soft fowl tick, A. persicus also has a broad distribution range in the Palearctic, with its habitats ranging from desert to Mediterranean temperate and rainforest climates (Rahmani et al. 2023; Monti et al. 2023). In this study, A. persicus compromised high haplotype diversity (0.9333) and moderate nucleotide diversity (0.00773), which was associated with population expansion tied to chicken distribution, while its preference for arid environments limited nucleotide divergence (Zhang et al. 2014). The network analysis demonstrated that i) A. persicus had two haplotypes (H-1 and H-4) in Kazakhstan, and H-4 haplotype collected from Shymkent City (Turkistan Oblast) was the progenitor of ticks from Algeria (H-3 haplotype) and Egypt (H-2 haplotype). This finding is consistent with the conclusion that A. persicus originated in Central Asia (Pfäffle et al. 2017c).

Central Asia covers more than 4 million square kilometers, including Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan (Bindi et al. 2012). Our findings represent a significant step forward in understanding the genetic diversity of ticks in the largest country of the region, Kazakhstan (especially considering Hy. scupense and A. persicus). At the same time, joint sampling efforts will be necessary among Central Asian and adjacent countries to uncover the complete geographical distribution and genetic relationships of these tick species, interrelated with the epidemiology of tick-borne diseases.

Conclusions

This study surveyed 19,861 ticks from 41 sites across 22 districts/cities in eastern, southern, and western Kazakhstan. It is the first to confirm that (i) Hy. scupense, the most widespread and abundant tick species in Kazakhstan, its H-2 haplotype from eastern Kazakhstan is the progenitor of those in XUAR; (ii) I. persulcatus, its H-5 haplotype from Almaty Oblast clustered with those from Burqin County (XUAR), is the progenitors of those from Japan; (iii) Ha. punctata compromises three lineages, and the ticks from the Almaty Oblast lineage are originated from Visegrad (Hungary); and (iv) A. persicus ticks from Turkistan Oblast (H-4 haplotype) are progenitors of those in Algeria and Egypt.
