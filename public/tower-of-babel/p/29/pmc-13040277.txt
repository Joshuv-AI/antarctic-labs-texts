Remnant cholesterol inflammatory index as a novel predictor of cardiovascular disease: Cross-sectional, longitudinal, and cumulative exposure analyses
Authors: Qiheng Li, Jiazhang Si, Qi Jin, Sixue Li, Yunfei Guo, Ping Han, Fuchun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional cardiovascular risk assessment using individual biomarkers may inadequately capture the complex interactions between atherogenic lipids and systemic inflammation. The remnant cholesterol inflammatory index (RCII) represents a novel composite biomarker integrating these pathways, but comprehensive evaluation of its cardiovascular predictive value remains limited.MethodsWe conducted secondary analyses of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), utilizing three complementary approaches: cross-sectional analysis (n = 10,459), longitudinal cohort analysis (n = 6,028), and cumulative exposure analysis (n = 3,515). RCII was calculated as remnant cholesterol × high-sensitivity C-reactive protein ÷ 10. Primary outcome was cardiovascular disease (CVD), defined as heart disease or stroke. Associations were quantified using logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression models with progressive adjustment strategies.ResultsRCII demonstrated consistent associations with CVD across all analytical approaches. In cross-sectional analysis, the highest RCII tertile showed 34% increased odds of prevalent CVD (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.12–1.61). Longitudinal analysis revealed 26% increased hazard for incident CVD (HR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.07–1.47) over 7-year follow-up. Cumulative exposure analysis demonstrated the strongest association, with 32% increased hazard (HR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.04–1.67). Dose-response relationships were linear without threshold effects. Associations were particularly pronounced for stroke compared to heart disease.ConclusionsRCII represents a valuable integrated biomarker for cardiovascular risk assessment, with cumulative exposure providing superior predictive performance compared to single-point measurements. These findings support RCII’s potential clinical applicability for identifying high-risk individuals requiring intensified prevention strategies targeting both metabolic and inflammatory pathways.Keywords: Remnant cholesterol inflammatory index, Cardiovascular disease, CHARLS, Composite biomarker, Risk assessment, Cumulative exposure

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) represents the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually and constituting nearly 31 % of all global deaths [1]. CVD primarily encompasses coronary heart disease, stroke, and other major cardiovascular conditions that collectively contribute to this substantial disease burden [2]. The global burden of CVD continues to escalate, particularly in developing countries where rapid urbanization and lifestyle transitions have dramatically increased cardiovascular risk factors [3]. In China, CVD affects approximately 290 million individuals and accounts for more than 43 % of all deaths, imposing substantial economic and social burdens on healthcare systems [4]. Despite significant advances in primary and secondary prevention strategies, including widespread adoption of statin therapy and evidence-based interventions, many individuals continue to experience cardiovascular events after achieving guideline-recommended treatment targets [5,6]. This persistent residual cardiovascular risk underscores the complexity of CVD pathogenesis and demonstrates the urgent need for enhanced risk assessment tools to better identify high-risk individuals and guide personalized prevention strategies [7,8].Fig. 1..

Traditional cardiovascular risk assessment has relied predominantly on individual risk factors, including low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood pressure, and diabetes status [[9], [10], [11]]. However, this single-parameter approach may inadequately capture the multifaceted nature of cardiovascular risk, particularly the complex interactions between different pathophysiological pathways [12]. Emerging evidence suggests that integrated biomarker approaches, which simultaneously reflect multiple disease mechanisms, provide superior risk stratification compared to conventional single-biomarker methods [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Among the various biomarkers under investigation, remnant cholesterol (RC) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) have demonstrated independent associations with cardiovascular outcomes, representing distinct yet interconnected pathways of atherogenic lipid burden and systemic inflammation [17,18]. The recognition that these pathways may interact synergistically in promoting atherosclerotic disease progression has led to the development of composite indices that integrate both parameters [19,20]. The remnant cholesterol inflammatory index (RCII), which combines RC and hs-CRP into a single comprehensive metric, has emerged as a promising tool for integrated cardiovascular risk assessment [21]. This integrated approach may overcome the limitations of evaluating individual biomarkers in isolation and provide a more holistic assessment of cardiovascular risk profiles [22].

The RCII represents a recently developed composite biomarker that quantifies the combined burden of atherogenic lipids and systemic inflammation [21]. While preliminary studies have demonstrated promising associations between RCII and stroke risk, comprehensive evaluation of its relationship with broader CVD outcomes remains largely unexplored. Current evidence is primarily limited to single-endpoint analyses [22], with most investigations focusing exclusively on cerebrovascular events rather than comprehensive CVD assessment. Furthermore, existing studies have predominantly utilized cross-sectional designs or short-term follow-up periods [23], leaving critical questions about the long-term predictive value of RCII and the potential clinical benefits of monitoring cumulative exposure over time unanswered. To address these substantial knowledge gaps, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to comprehensively evaluate the association between RCII and CVD in a large, nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. Our investigation aimed to establish whether RCII provides clinically meaningful cardiovascular risk stratification and to assess its potential utility for CVD prediction in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study conducted secondary analyses of data from CHARLS, a nationwide prospective cohort study examining the economic, social, and health conditions of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. The baseline survey was conducted from June 2011 to March 2012, initially enrolling 17,705 individuals. For our analyses, we utilized data from the 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018 waves to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between RCII and CVD. The CHARLS study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

We conducted three complementary analyses to provide a comprehensive assessment of the RCII-CVD relationship: (1) a cross-sectional analysis including participants with prevalent CVD to examine the association between RCII and CVD prevalence at baseline; (2) a longitudinal cohort analysis excluding baseline CVD patients to assess the predictive value of baseline RCII for incident CVD risk over 7 years of follow-up (2011–2018); and (3) a cumulative exposure longitudinal analysis to investigate the relationship between long-term RCII exposure and CVD incidence using repeated measurements from 2011 and 2015.

For the cross-sectional analysis (1), from the original 17,705 participants, we excluded participants with missing gender data (n = 12), age data or those aged < 45 years (n = 775), missing variables required for RCII calculation (n = 5,718), missing outcome variables (n = 120), cancer patients (n = 111), and those with hs-CRP levels > 10 mg/L (n = 510), resulting in a final sample of 10,459 participants.

For the longitudinal cohort analysis (2), from the original 17,705 participants, we excluded those with missing follow-up data (n = 6,079), baseline CVD or missing CVD data (n = 1,751), baseline cancer (n = 102), missing age data or aged < 45 years (n = 389), missing gender data (n = 4), missing variables required for RCII calculation (n = 3,093), and CRP levels > 10 mg/L (n = 261), yielding 6,028 participants for analysis.

For the cumulative exposure longitudinal analysis (3), based on the longitudinal cohort, we further excluded participants with missing cumulative RCII calculation variables (n = 2,194), those who developed CVD before or during 2015 (n = 304), and those without follow-up data after 2015 (n = 276), resulting in 3,515 participants for final analysis.

Assessment of RCII index

RCII was calculated by multiplying RC by hs-CRP: RCII = RC (mg/dL) × hs-CRP (mg/L) ÷ 10(21). RC was calculated using the formula: RC = TC − (HDL-C + LDL-C). Due to the markedly skewed distribution of RCII in the population, we performed natural logarithmic transformation for statistical analyses. To handle potential negative or zero values, we first applied a shift transformation when necessary (RCII_shifted = RCII + |minimum value| + 1 if minimum ≤ 0), followed by natural logarithmic transformation (RCII_log = ln(RCII_shifted)), yielding log-transformed RCII for analysis. RCII was also categorized into tertiles for group comparisons and dose–response analyses.

For the cumulative exposure longitudinal analysis, the cumulative RCII (CumRCII) was calculated using the formula: CumRCII = [(RCII2011 + RCII2015) ÷ 2] × (2015–––2011). CumRCII was also natural log-transformed using the same approach (CumRCII_log) for statistical analysis.

Assessment of CVD outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was CVD, defined as heart disease or stroke. Consistent with previous studies[[24], [25], [26]], the definition of CVD was determined by responses to two questions: (1) “Have you been diagnosed with a stroke by your doctor?” (2) “Have you been diagnosed by your doctor with congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, heart attack, angina pectoris, or other heart problems?” In this study, a confirmed diagnosis of CVD was considered if the answer to either of the two questions was “yes.” The time of CVD occurrence was defined as the difference between the time point of the follow-up and the baseline year of 2011. For participants without reported CVD events, follow-up duration was calculated from baseline assessment to their final survey date.

Data collection

At baseline assessment, trained interviewers administered structured questionnaires to collect comprehensive sociodemographic and health-related data. Demographic variables included age (continuous), gender (male/female), marital status (married/unmarried), residence (urban/rural), and education level (illiteracy, primary school, junior high school, high school or above). Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status (never, former, current) and drinking status (never, former, current).

Clinical measurements included systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared). Laboratory parameters encompassed TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, TC, fasting blood glucose (FBG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, uric acid, and cystatin C. Self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions included hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, liver disease, and kidney disease. Hypertension was defined by systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, use of antihypertensive medication, or self-reported physician diagnosis. Diabetes was identified by fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5 %, use of antidiabetic medication, or self-reported physician diagnosis. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) method.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test.

For the cross-sectional analysis, the association between RCII and CVD prevalence was quantified using multivariable logistic regression models. For longitudinal analyses, the relationship between RCII and incident CVD was quantified through multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models RCII was analyzed both as a continuous variable (natural log-transformed) and as categorical tertiles. Three progressive adjustment strategies were employed across all analyses to control for potential confounding. Model 1 (crude model) included only the exposure variable. Model 2 (minimally adjusted model) incorporated basic demographic factors: for cross-sectional analysis, this included age, gender, and residence; for longitudinal cohort analysis, this included age, gender, and education level. Model 3 (fully adjusted model) additionally included relevant covariates selected using a systematic approach. Covariate selection for Model 3 followed specific criteria: covariates were included if they met any of the following conditions: (1) P-value < 0.05 in univariate analysis, (2) changing the regression coefficient of the exposure variable by > 10 % when added to the basic model, or (3) changing the regression coefficient by > 10 % when removed from the complete model. Specifically, for the cross-sectional analysis, Model 3 adjusted for gender, age, marital status, residence, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and liver disease. For the longitudinal cohort analysis, Model 3 adjusted for gender, marital status, education level, hypertension, diabetes, age, BMI, SBP, and cystatin C. For the cumulative exposure longitudinal analysis, Model 3 adjusted for gender, education level, smoking status, drinking status, liver disease, age, BMI, FBG, creatinine, and cystatin C.

Restricted cubic spline curves were constructed for the cross-sectional analysis to examine potential non-linear relationships between RCII and CVD risk, adjusting for the same covariates as Model 3. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed for both the longitudinal cohort analysis and cumulative exposure longitudinal analysis to compare CVD-free survival across RCII tertiles, with log-rank tests used to assess differences between survival distributions. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of associations across different population characteristics, including gender, age groups (≤65 vs > 65 years), BMI categories (≤25 vs > 25 kg/m2), education levels, hypertension status, and diabetes status. Each subgroup analysis also adjusted for the respective covariates identified for Model 3 in each sub-study.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.0 and Stata 17.0. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout all analyses.

Results

Cross-sectional study (Study 1)

Baseline characteristics and CVD prevalence

The cross-sectional analysis included 10,459 participants categorized into T1 (RCII_log 2.54 ± 0.06, n = 3,487), T2 (RCII_log 2.65 ± 0.05, n = 3,486), and T3 (RCII_log 3.08 ± 0.34, n = 3,486), (P < 0.001). CVD prevalence was 11.70 %, 13.83 %, and 16.32 % in the three groups, respectively (P < 0.001).

The baseline characteristics across RCII tertiles are shown in Table 1. Demographic characteristics showed significant differences between the groups, with higher RCII tertiles being associated with older age, higher proportion of urban residence, and different smoking and drinking patterns (all P < 0.05). Regarding clinical parameters, SBP, DBP, and BMI all significantly increased as the RCII tertile increased (all P < 0.001). Laboratory parameters exhibited notable differences across the tertiles (P < 0.001). With increasing RCII levels, total cholesterol, triglycerides, CRP, FBG, HbA1c, creatinine, and uric acid increased progressively. In contrast, HDL-C and BUN decreased significantly. Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia all increased significantly with higher RCII levels (all P < 0.001).

Association between RCII and CVD

The association between RCII and CVD evaluated using logistic regression analysis was shown in Table 2. When analyzed as a continuous variable, each unit increase in log-transformed RCII was associated with progressively higher odds of CVD across all models. In the crude model (Model 1), the odds ratio was 1.72 (95 % CI: 1.46–2.01, P < 0.001). After demographic adjustment (Model 2), the association remained robust with an OR of 1.65 (95 % CI: 1.34–2.04, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the association was slightly attenuated but remained statistically significant (OR: 1.51, 95 % CI: 1.22–1.88, P < 0.001). Categorical analysis revealed a dose–response relationship across RCII tertiles. In the fully adjusted model, compared to T1, participants in T2 showed a non-significant 15 % increased odds of CVD (OR: 1.15, 95 % CI: 0.95–1.38, P = 0.156), while those in T3 had a significant 34 % increased odds (OR: 1.34, 95 % CI: 1.12–1.61, P = 0.002). The test for linear trend across tertiles was statistically significant (P for trend = 0.002).

Component-specific analyses revealed differential associations between RCII and individual CVD outcomes. For heart disease, each unit increase in log-transformed RCII was associated with a 33 % increased odds (OR: 1.33, 95 % CI: 1.06–1.68, P = 0.014), though tertile comparisons showed marginal significance. For stroke, the association was markedly stronger, with each unit increase in log-transformed RCII associated with 145 % increased odds (OR: 2.45, 95 % CI: 1.64–3.66, P < 0.001), and T3 participants having more than twice the odds of stroke compared to T1 (OR: 2.51, 95 % CI: 1.59–3.98, P < 0.001).

Fig. 2 displays the restricted cubic spline analysis exploring the dose–response relationship between RCII and CVD risk. The curve demonstrated a consistent positive association between RCII_log and CVD risk throughout the observed range (P for overall < 0.001), with the relationship appearing approximately linear (P for nonlinear = 0.531). This supports the appropriateness of treating RCII as a continuous predictor and confirms the absence of threshold effects.

Longitudinal cohort study (Study 2)

Baseline characteristics and follow-up

The longitudinal cohort comprised 6,028 participants without baseline CVD, categorized into T1 (RCII_log 1.57 ± 0.08, n = 2,010), T2 (RCII_log 1.83 ± 0.10, n = 2,009), and T3 (RCII_log 2.55 ± 0.47, n = 2,009), (P < 0.001). During the 7-year follow-up period (2011–2018), incident CVD occurred in 13.73 %, 16.82 %, and 20.96 % participants in the three groups, respectively (P < 0.001).

The baseline characteristics of the longitudinal cohort are shown in Table 4. Demographic characteristics showed significant differences between the groups, with higher RCII tertiles being associated with older age and different smoking and drinking patterns (all P < 0.05). Regarding clinical parameters, SBP, DBP, and BMI all significantly increased as the RCII tertile increased (all P < 0.001). Laboratory parameters exhibited notable differences across the tertiles (P < 0.001). With increasing RCII levels, total cholesterol, triglycerides, CRP, FBG, HbA1c, creatinine, and uric acid increased progressively. In contrast, HDL-C decreased significantly. Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia all increased significantly with higher RCII levels (all P < 0.001).

Association between RCII and incident CVD

The association between baseline RCII and incident CVD was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, as shown in Table 5. When analyzed as a continuous variable, each unit increase in log-transformed RCII was associated with progressively higher hazard of incident CVD across all models. In the crude model (Model 1), the hazard ratio was 1.37 (95 % CI: 1.23–1.53, P < 0.001). After demographic adjustment (Model 2), the association remained significant with an HR of 1.12 (95 % CI: 1.07–1.17, P < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the association was attenuated but remained statistically significant (HR: 1.16, 95 % CI: 1.03–1.30, P = 0.014). Categorical analysis revealed a dose–response relationship across RCII tertiles. In the fully adjusted model, compared to T1, participants in T2 showed a non-significant 11 % increased hazard of CVD (HR: 1.11, 95 % CI: 0.94–1.30, P = 0.211), while those in T3 had a significant 26 % increased hazard (HR: 1.26, 95 % CI: 1.07–1.47, P = 0.005). The test for linear trend across tertiles was statistically significant (P for trend = 0.005).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of RCII as a CVD predictor using multiple analytical approaches in a nationally representative Chinese cohort. RCII demonstrated consistent associations with cardiovascular disease across three complementary analyses. In cross-sectional analysis, the highest RCII tertile showed 34 % increased odds of prevalent CVD. Longitudinal analysis established RCII as an independent predictor of incident CVD (26 % increased hazard over 7 years). Cumulative exposure analysis revealed that sustained RCII elevation conferred the strongest cardiovascular risk (32 % increased hazard), supporting the value of long-term biomarker monitoring.

Our findings align with recent investigations demonstrating RCII's predictive value for cardiovascular outcomes. Chen et al. established RCII's association with stroke risk (HR: 1.106 per standard deviation increase) in the same CHARLS population [21]. Our study extends this evidence in two important ways. First, we evaluated composite CVD outcomes (heart disease OR stroke) and performed component-specific analyses across different CVD subtypes, which was not examined in prior stroke-focused research. Second, our component-specific analyses revealed differential predictive value: RCII showed markedly stronger associations with stroke (cross-sectional OR: 2.45; longitudinal HR: 1.49) compared to heart disease (cross-sectional OR: 1.33; longitudinal HR: 1.17, non-significant), demonstrating enhanced specificity for cerebrovascular events. Additionally, our three complementary analytical approaches (cross-sectional, longitudinal, and cumulative exposure) provide more comprehensive evidence than previous single-design studies. The finding that cumulative exposure provides superior risk discrimination adds important evidence supporting temporal integration approaches to composite biomarkers.

The robust associations between RCII and cardiovascular disease are biologically plausible and supported by extensive mechanistic evidence. RC represents the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and their remnants, including very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL), and chylomicron remnants. Unlike LDL cholesterol, remnant lipoproteins penetrate the arterial wall directly without prior modification, making them particularly atherogenic [27]. Mendelian randomization studies have provided compelling evidence for the causal role of RC in coronary heart disease, with genetic variants associated with higher RC levels conferring increased cardiovascular risk independent of LDL cholesterol [28]. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have further demonstrated that elevated RC is associated with increased risks of CVD (RR: 1.53), stroke (RR: 1.43), and cardiovascular mortality (RR: 1.83), independent of traditional risk factors [29]. Moreover, elevated RC has shown superior predictive accuracy for cardiovascular events compared to LDL-C in high-risk populations [30]. CRP, the inflammatory component of RCII, serves as a sensitive marker of systemic inflammation and has been extensively validated as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events [18]. Elevated CRP levels reflect underlying inflammatory processes that directly contribute to atherosclerotic plaque formation, progression, and destabilization [31]. The CANTOS trial demonstrated that anti-inflammatory therapies targeting interleukin-1β significantly reduce cardiovascular events, providing direct evidence for the causal role of inflammation in cardiovascular disease [32]. Given the established roles of both components, the biological rationale for combining these parameters stems from their synergistic pathophysiological interaction. Importantly, studies have demonstrated that dual residual risk of cholesterol and inflammation confers significantly higher mortality risk (HR: 1.75) compared to either factor alone, even after standard lipid-lowering therapy [33]. Mechanistically, remnant lipoproteins activate inflammatory pathways through toll-like receptor signaling [34], while inflammatory mediators enhance remnant particle atherogenicity through oxidative modification. This bidirectional feedback loop—where RC promotes inflammation and inflammation enhances RC atherogenicity—underscores RCII's value as an integrated assessment of atherogenic lipid burden and vascular inflammation.

This investigation demonstrates several methodological innovations that advance cardiovascular risk assessment. Our comprehensive analytical framework employing three complementary approaches—cross-sectional, longitudinal, and cumulative exposure—provides progressive validation across different study designs, creating a complete evidence chain that strengthens confidence in RCII's clinical applicability. The dose–response analysis using restricted cubic splines revealed a linear association without threshold effects, indicating that even modest RCII elevations contribute to cardiovascular risk. This continuous relationship enables precision risk stratification as a quantitative predictor, with direct therapeutic implications for controlling and reducing RCII levels in CVD prevention. The cumulative exposure assessment represents a particularly significant methodological advancement. The stronger associations observed with cumulative RCII exposure align with the chronic nature of atherosclerotic disease development, building upon established “cholesterol-years” concepts [35]. The improved statistical significance from longitudinal analysis (P = 0.014) to cumulative exposure assessment (P = 0.003) demonstrates enhanced predictive performance through temporal integration of atherogenic burden.

Our findings reveal differential associations with specific cardiovascular endpoints, with consistently stronger effect sizes for stroke compared to heart disease across all analytical approaches. This pattern suggests enhanced sensitivity for cerebrovascular events [21], potentially explained by RC and inflammatory processes contributing more significantly to stroke risk through small vessel disease and endothelial dysfunction pathways [36]. Subgroup analyses revealed age-related interactions, with stronger associations in participants ≤ 65 years, suggesting enhanced risk discrimination in younger adults where cardiovascular risk is often underestimated by traditional calculators [37]. Practical implementation is facilitated by RCII's calculation from routinely available laboratory parameters—standard lipid panels and CRP—enabling immediate clinical application without specialized assays.

While this comprehensive investigation provides robust evidence for RCII's clinical implementation potential, several important methodological limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the observational nature of our study precludes definitive causal inferences about the relationship between RCII and cardiovascular disease, although the biological plausibility and dose–response relationships support a causal interpretation. Second, our study population consisted exclusively of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups or younger populations. Third, the cardiovascular disease definition relied on self-reported physician diagnoses rather than standardized clinical criteria or medical record verification, though this approach is consistent with large-scale epidemiological studies and has been validated in similar populations. Fourth, the CHARLS questionnaire assesses heart disease and stroke as composite outcomes without distinguishing specific subtypes (e.g., heart failure vs. myocardial infarction; ischemic vs. hemorrhagic stroke), preventing subtype-specific analyses that could provide insights into differential associations across disease phenotypes. Fifth, our study did not include comparisons with cardiac-specific biomarkers such as troponin or NT-proBNP, as these measurements were not available in the CHARLS database. Sixth, despite comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible.

Future research priorities should focus on validation studies in diverse populations to establish broader generalizability across ethnic groups and age ranges. Randomized controlled trials are needed to demonstrate whether RCII-guided therapeutic interventions improve clinical outcomes beyond standard care. Additional investigations should determine optimal RCII-based risk assessment strategies, including measurement intervals and therapeutic thresholds. Cost-effectiveness analyses comparing RCII-based stratification with traditional approaches would inform healthcare policy and guideline development. Mechanistic studies elucidating pathophysiological interactions between RC and inflammatory pathways could identify therapeutic targets within this integrated pathway.
