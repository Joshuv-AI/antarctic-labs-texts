Progressive Decline in Interferon-γ Release is Associated with Cirrhosis Progression and Severity: A Prospective Preliminary Report
Authors: Marie-Charlotte Delignette, Muzhda Haem Rahimi, Morgane Gossez, Teresa Antonini, Arnaud Riff, Estelle Peronnet, Franck Berthier, Fabienne Venet, Fanny Lebossé, Guillaume Monneret
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cirrhosis progresses through distinct stages, with increasing severity and susceptibility to infections due to cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction. Lymphocyte-based functional assays are considered the gold standard for assessing immune function, although none have been evaluated across different cirrhosis stages. We conducted a pilot study to assess whether performing interferon-γ (IFN-γ) release assays (IGRA), in response to a non-antigenic mitogen (phytohemagglutinin [PHA]), could help classify immune function and disease severity in 70 patients with cirrhosis awaiting liver transplantation. Disease severity ranged from chronic cirrhosis to acute-on-chronic liver failure. We measured IFN-γ release in response to PHA using a fresh heparinized whole-blood protocol. Progressive reduction in IFN-γ release correlated with increased disease severity parameters (with respect to total bilirubin level and number of organ failure instances). A moderate but highly significant correlation was observed between IFN-γ release and CD8+ T lymphocyte count (r=0.52, P&lt;0.001). As a non-antigen-dependent mitogen, PHA enables the global assessment of lymphocyte IFN-γ-releasing capacity. Our whole-blood results suggest that PHA-IGRA testing may provide a useful parameter for characterizing immune dysfunction in patients with cirrhosis. These findings require further validation in larger cohorts to better define the clinical applicability of PHA-IGRA testing.

Cirrhosis progresses through distinct stages that are marked by increasing severity: compensated cirrhosis (CC), non-acute decompensation (NAD), acute decompensation (AD), and acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF, i.e., AD associated with multiple organ failures). In addition to being a major risk factor for mortality in patients with cirrhosis, bacterial infections represent the primary trigger of cirrhosis decompensation and, ultimately, ACLF [1]. This heightened susceptibility to infections is because of altered immune function [2], known as cirrhosis-associated immune dysfunction (CAID). Similar to sepsis, cirrhosis is characterized by systemic inflammation and progressive development of profound immunosuppression [2, 3]. In sepsis, decreased mHLA-DR expression is a biomarker of disease severity and has been proposed as a potential predictor of patients with cirrhosis prone to developing infections [4, 5]. However, to our knowledge, although lymphocyte alterations have been described in the pathophysiology of CAID [6, 7], no lymphocyte-based functional testing has been evaluated across different stages of cirrhosis. Such testing has recently offered insights into the extent of immunosuppression in patients with sepsis admitted to intensive care units [8–10].

Several techniques and protocols have been described for in vitro testing of T lymphocyte functionality, including lymphocyte proliferation assays, intracellular cytokine detection via flow cytometry, and enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) analysis. Although these methods yield clinically relevant results, they have various analytical challenges (e.g., lengthy procedures, need for cell purification, and limited sensitivity) and remain confined to clinical research settings. The development of interferon-γ release assays (IGRAs) for latent tuberculosis enabled the creation of whole-blood assays that are more readily applicable to routine clinical care [11].

Using samples from the EDMONGH cohort, we conducted an ancillary investigation into ex vivo interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) release as a functional test for T lymphocytes in patients on a liver transplant waiting list with varying stages of cirrhosis. We evaluated the association between functional lymphocyte alterations and cirrhosis severity.

This work is an ancillary study of the EDMONGH study [12] at Lyon University Hospital (Hospices Civils de Lyon, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03995537). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by our institutional review board (Comité de Protection des Personnes Île de France XI, protocol number ID-RCB: 2019-A00954-53, approved on June 28, 2019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients awaiting liver transplantation (LT) with diagnoses of CC, NAD, AD, or ACLF (detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria in [12]). Patients were sampled once before LT (median time: 11 days). The main clinical characteristics related to liver disease classification are provided in Supplemental Data Table S1. Blood samples were collected before LT.

We assessed T cell function in IGRAs using fresh heparinized whole-blood samples. All samples were analyzed within 4 hrs of collection (i.e., no blood storage). This antigen-independent testing method measured IFN-γ production in response to phytohemagglutinin A (PHA) stimulation. We incubated 300 µL of whole blood with 300 µL of PHA solution (STIMM T RUO Kit, bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) for 16 hrs at 37°C. Simultaneously, we incubated 300 μL of whole blood with 300 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (negative control) to measure IFN-γ production under baseline conditions. Quantitative results (i.e., IFN-γ concentrations in supernatants after incubation) were obtained using enzyme-linked immunofluorescent assays on a VIDAS 3 system (bioMérieux) and expressed as reference fluorescence values (RFVs).

In addition to IGRAs, we analyzed mHLA-DR expression and lymphocyte subsets [12]. Additional details are provided in Supplemental Methods.

Results were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) or as the number and percentage (%). Further details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

We included 70 patients who were categorized into the following groups: CC (N=15), NAD (N=18), AD (N=13), and ACLF (N=24). Their clinical characteristics are detailed in Supplemental Data Table S2. Overall, patients with decompensated cirrhosis showed lower IFN-γ release than those with CC (Fig. 1A). The magnitude of this reduction correlated with disease severity. The median IFN-γ concentrations (IQR) in different cirrhosis stages (in RFVs) were as follows: CC: 7,633 (3,073–10,986); NAD: 4,467 (1,582–10,401); AD: 2,670 (497–6,479); and ACLF: 1,806 (278–3,866) (Fig. 1A). This decrease in RFVs was especially pronounced in the AD and ACLF groups. Both the AD and ACLF groups showed significant differences compared with the CC group (P<0.01, Mann–Whitney test); the ACLF group showed a significant RFV difference compared with the NAD group (P<0.05, Mann–Whitney test). In contrast, the absolute counts of CD8+ T cells (main contributors to IFN-γ production) differed to a lesser extent between the disease severity groups (Fig. 1B). Consistently, significant negative correlations were observed between IFN-γ release and severity parameters (Fig. 1C–E), including total bilirubin (r=−0.54, P<0.001), sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores (r=−0.37, P=0.002), and the number of organ failure instances (r=−0.36, P=0.002).

To further study the association between altered IFN-γ release and disease status, we categorized the patients into two groups based on whether their IFN production was below or above the median. Supplemental Data Table S2 shows that patients with low IFN-γ release levels had significantly higher disease severity, increased model for end-stage liver disease scores (26 vs. 17, P=0.009), increased SOFA scores (6 vs. 4, P=0.04), and elevated bilirubin levels (150 vs. 37 μmol/L, P<0.001), compared with patients with higher IFN-γ release levels.

Regarding cellular markers, we identified positive correlations with different lymphocyte subsets, with the strongest and most significant correlation observed with CD8+ T cells (r=0.52, P<0.001; Fig. 2). Additionally, as an exploratory step (reflecting the relatively low number of patients), we examined the association between IFN-γ release, infections (at the time of sampling), and mortality rate (assessed in case of absence of LT) (Supplemental Data Fig. S1). Infected patients at the time of sampling exhibited significantly lower IFN-γ release (P<0.05). Regarding mortality, although the difference was not significant (likely because of the small sample size), patients who later died also showed lower IFN-γ release (Supplemental Data Fig. S1B; Supplemental Data Table S2). Finally, we compared the results for a subgroup of patients (N=8) with those obtained using an automated IGRA protocol [13], as described in Supplemental Methods. A strong correlation was observed between the results obtained with both methods (r=0.9, P=0.005; Supplemental Data Fig. S2).

Previous findings demonstrated that cirrhosis progression to increasingly severe stages is accompanied by CAID, a condition characterized by profound alterations in immune function that lead to increased susceptibility to infections and poorer clinical outcomes [2].

Among the immune alterations reported in cirrhosis, lymphocyte dysfunction is particularly notable. These dysfunctions have been primarily described in terms of lymphopenia, reflecting fewer circulating lymphocytes due to apoptosis, and checkpoint inhibitor overexpression, which contributes to immune exhaustion [2, 14, 15]. Despite these observations, lymphocyte functionality, crucial for immune defense, remains poorly studied in patients with cirrhosis. Our principal findings revealed a significant association between the magnitude of diminished IFN-γ release and liver disease severity. Additionally, we observed a correlation between ongoing infection and mortality without LT, consistent with previously reported findings [16]. Another key result is the correlation between IFN-γ release and CD8+ T cell counts, which is plausible given that CD8+ T cells produce IFN-γ [17]. Because IGRA was performed with whole-blood samples, the absolute lymphocyte count potentially influences IFN-γ release independently of functional changes. Our results reflect the combined effects of lymphocyte count and cellular functionality. However, as observed in sepsis [9], functional testing can provide more relevant information regarding disease severity (Fig. 1A–B). Collectively, these findings suggest that IGRA analysis using non-antigen stimulation, which provides a global assessment of T lymphocyte functionality, may serve as a biomarker associated with cirrhosis severity and immunosuppression.

Although functional testing is considered the gold standard for assessing immune function, its clinical implementation faces several analytical challenges. In this preliminary comparative study, we observed a correlation between the results obtained using automated and non-automated protocols for a subgroup of patients. Automated testing was performed with whole-blood samples, requiring no cell sorting or technician intervention, and provided results within 4 hrs, suggesting novel opportunities for immune monitoring.

This study has some limitations. These include a relatively small sample size and the fact that most patients later underwent LT, limiting the assessment of long-term clinical outcomes without transplantation. Furthermore, the study was monocentric, which could affect its generalizability. Lastly, the very small number of samples assessed using the automated protocol prevents any definitive conclusions regarding the reliability of this approach.

In conclusion, we demonstrate an association between ex vivo IFN-γ release, used to assess antigen-independent T lymphocyte functionality (in response to PHA), and liver-failure severity stages. Considering its potential clinical utility beyond liver failure [18–20], this approach could open new avenues for functional immunological testing in clinical laboratories, a field historically constrained by difficult-to-implement protocols. Nonetheless, these preliminary findings require validation in larger, well-defined patient cohorts.
