Mentalistic attention orienting triggered by android eyes
Authors: Wataru Sato, Koh Shimokawa, Shota Uono, Takashi Minato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The eyes play a special role in human communications. Previous psychological studies have reported reflexive attention orienting in response to another individual’s eyes during live interactions. Although robots are expected to collaborate with humans in various social situations, it remains unclear whether robot eyes have the potential to trigger attention orienting similarly to human eyes, specifically based on mental attribution. We investigated this issue in a series of experiments using a live gaze-cueing paradigm with an android. In Experiment 1, the non-predictive cue was the eyes and head of an android placed in front of human participants. Light-emitting diodes in the periphery served as target signals. The reaction times (RTs) required to localize the valid cued targets were faster than those for invalid cued targets for both types of cues. In Experiment 2, the gaze direction of the android eyes changed before the peripheral target lights appeared with or without barriers that made the targets non-visible, such that the android did not attend to them. The RTs were faster for validly cued targets only when there were no barriers. In Experiment 3, the targets were changed from lights to sounds, which the android could attend to even in the presence of barriers. The RTs to the target sounds were faster with valid cues, irrespective of the presence of barriers. These results suggest that android eyes may automatically induce attention orienting in humans based on mental state attribution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75063-3.

Introduction

The eyes have been described as the “window to the soul”1and play an indispensable role in human interactions. The perception of eye gaze can induce an observer to look in the same direction as the observed person; it also can prompt the observer to infer the observed individual’s mental state2. Neurophysiological studies of monkeys have suggested that, in primate brains, the eyes are prioritized for coding over other directional cues, including head and posture cues3. A comparative anatomical study further indicated that the morphology of the human eye is unique among primates, having a highly exposed and horizontally elongated white sclera that reveals the gaze direction4. Based on these anatomical data, combined with the behaviors of humans and non-human primates, it was proposed that the human eyes uniquely evolved to support communicative and cooperative social interactions5.

Consistent with these ideas, previous psychological studies have demonstrated that another individual’s eyes automatically trigger attention orienting (e.g6,7.,; for reviews, see8,9). These studies applied Posner’s10cueing paradigm in computerized experiments. For example, Driver et al7. presented a gaze cue in the center of a computer screen (the eye gaze direction of a human face in a photograph) followed by a target in the peripheral visual field. The participants’ reaction time (RT) to identify a target was faster when the target was preceded by a valid versus an invalid gaze cue. Another study reported identical results using head direction stimuli11. Gaze-cueing effects were also reported for both visual and auditory targets12, suggesting that changes in spatial attention do not occur only in response to visual stimuli. In these studies, attention orienting occurred when the cue was not predictive of the target location. In addition, some studies demonstrated that attention orienting occurred even when gaze cues were presented unconsciously13,14. These data suggest that attention orienting can be triggered automatically. Other studies investigated the gaze-cueing effect in human confederates in live, face-to-face situations and confirmed that attention orienting was triggered by real humans, in a manner similar to photographs15,16.

Furthermore, studies have shown that the gaze-cueing effect is elicited by the eyes or head direction of face stimuli, but only when the eyes are not closed or occluded17–20. These manipulations drastically affected the mental state attribution (also called “mentalizing” or “theory of mind”) of the individual serving as the cue, in terms of whether he/she could see versus not see targets. These results were interpreted as evidence that the participants automatically attributed mental states to the individual serving as the cue, and an attentional shift was triggered only if the target was regarded as visible to that individual21. Taken together, these findings suggest that real human eyes, as well as head direction, automatically induce shifts in spatial attention based on mental state attribution.

Some studies have found that automatic attention orienting can be triggered by humanoid robots’ gaze directions in live interactions. Social robots that interact and communicate with humans have recently become popular to support human activity at home and in various industries worldwide22. Social robots must interact and communicate with humans in a natural way23. Social robots are also useful for research purposes as they allow for better control of live interactions compared with human confederates24,25. Studies have reported that the direction of robots’ heads26,27, eyes28, or both29–32, induced automatic attention orienting in live gaze-cueing experiments24. Furthermore, a recent study tested the effect of barriers on the gaze-cueing effect triggered by a robot’s head, with the results suggesting that the attention orienting triggered by the robot head direction depended on mental state attribution27.

However, it remains unclear whether robot eyes can trigger attention orienting during live interactions in a manner similar to human eyes. Most studies using robots tested the effect of the head or the joint effects of the head and eyes. Only one study tested the effect of the eyes alone28, which were round-shaped and thus different from humans’ more elongated eyes33. Several previous studies reported differences in gaze-cueing effects between schematic and realistic human faces9. It has been argued that these stimuli are different in complexity34and ecological validity35, and attention orienting to schematic gaze cues might be mediated by non-social mechanisms, such as motion cues35. Furthermore, whether robot eyes can evoke attention orienting based on mental attribution remains untested. One reason for the lack of investigation of robot eyes may be that most humanoid robots do not have human-like eyes. Robots with human-like movements and appearance are called androids36. We hypothesized that android eyes similar in appearance to human eyes could induce attention orienting in humans based on mental state attribution.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a series of experiments using a gaze-cueing paradigm and an android (Fig. 1; Movie S1). We used the android Nikola, which has human-like, horizontally elongated eyes with a white sclera surrounding the darker-colored iris25. In Experiment 1, placing the android so that its head and eyes were in front of human participants before the target light-emitting diode (LED) lighting appeared in the periphery served as the non-predictive cue. We predicted that the RTs to localize the targets would be faster under valid versus invalid cue conditions for both the head and eyes. In Experiment 2, the direction of the android eyes changed before the peripheral target lights appeared, with and without barriers that made the target non-visible, such that the android did not attend to it (Fig. 1; Movie S1). Based on the expectation that attributing a mental state to Nikola would modulate gaze-triggered attention orienting, we predicted that the RTs to the targets would be faster with valid cues only when there were no barriers. In Experiment 3, the targets were changed from LED lights to a buzzer sound, which the android could attend to even in the presence of barriers. This experiment was conducted to confirm that the results of Experiment 2 were not due to the barriers preventing perception of the android’s eye gaze, and to further test the effect of mental state attribution on gaze-triggered attention orienting. We predicted that the RTs to the target sound would be faster with valid cues, irrespective of the barrier condition.

Results

Experiment 1

The RTs in Experiment 1 are shown in Fig. 2A. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with cue validity (valid versus invalid) and cue type (head versus eyes) as within-subjects factors revealed a significant main effect of cue validity (F[1, 14] = 51.99, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.79), where the RTs were faster for validly cued targets than for invalidly cued targets. The main effect of cue type and the cue type × cue validity interaction were not significant (F[1, 14] = 0.10, p = 0.756, η²p = 0.01; and F[1, 14] = 2.00, p = 0.179, η²p = 0.13, respectively). To confirm that the cueing effects were induced by both cue types, follow-up analyses were conducted for the non-significant interaction. The results showed that the simple main effect of cue validity (faster RTs for validly than for invalidly cued targets) was significant in both the head and eyes conditions (F[1, 28] = 43.44, p < 0.001; and F[1, 28] = 23.77, p < 0.001, respectively).

Experiment 2

The RTs in Experiment 2 are shown in Fig. 2B. Repeated-measures ANOVA with cue validity (valid versus invalid) and visibility (visible versus non-visible) as within-subjects factors revealed a significant cue validity × visibility interaction (F[1, 17] = 4.91, p = 0.041, η²p = 0.22) and a significant main effect of cue validity (F[1, 17] = 11.17, p = 0.004, η²p = 0.40). The main effect of visibility was not significant (F[1, 17] = 2.91, p = 0.106, η²p = 0.15). Simple effect analyses revealed that the simple effect of cue validity was significant under the visible condition, with faster RTs seen for validly than for invalidly cued targets (F[1, 34] = 16.01, p < 0.001), but not under the non-visible condition (F[1, 34] = 1.73, p = 0.197).

Experiment 3

The RTs in Experiment 3 are shown in Fig. 2C. Repeated-measures ANOVA of the same factors as in Experiment 2 only revealed a significant main effect of cue validity (F[1, 15] = 45.14, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.75), where the RTs were faster for validly than for invalidly cued targets. The main effect of cue type and the cue validity × visibility interaction were not significant (F[1, 15] = 0.00, p = 0.980, η²p = 0.00; and F[1, 15] = 2.43, p = 0.140, η²p = 0.14, respectively). Follow-up analyses were conducted for the non-significant interaction to confirm that both cue types induced attention orienting. The results confirmed that the simple main effect of cue validity (faster RTs for validly than for invalidly cued targets) was significant in both the visible and non-visible conditions (F[1, 30] = 45.97, p < 0.001; and F[1, 30] = 30.56, p < 0.001, respectively).

Discussion

The results of Experiment 1 indicated that valid directional head and eye cues shortened the RTs to localize targets relative to invalid cues. These results are consistent with those of previous studies that manipulated the direction of the head26,27or eyes28of robots in a gaze-cueing paradigm in live, face-to-face situations. However, the previous study used a robot with round eyes that differed from humans’ horizontally elongated eyes33. Tests of human-like eyes are important because debate persists regarding whether gaze-cueing effects are triggered by both schematic and realistic eyes via the same mechanisms35. Neuroimaging37and electroencephalography38studies also showed that non-realistic and realistic facial stimuli are processed differently in the brain. Our results are also in accordance with studies that tested the effects of human eyes on attention orienting16. Confirmation of these effects is important because a previous study revealed that, when the head and eye directions of stimuli were incongruent, humans followed the gaze direction of the eyes, suggesting that humans are especially attuned to the eyes5. Our results extend the previous findings by showing that human-like eyes in androids can induce automatic attention orienting.

Furthermore, in Experiment 2, valid eye cues induced faster RTs when the target lights were visible to the android but not when the targets were not visible due to barriers, such that the android did not attend to them. These results are consistent with those of studies that visually occluded on-screen human eye cues17,19. These findings suggest that participants can attribute a mental state to an android (e.g., “he is unable to see and attend to the visual targets”), which suppresses the gaze-cueing effect. Moreover, the results of Experiment 3 revealed that the valid eye cues shortened RTs regardless of the presence of barriers when the targets were sounds. A previous study also demonstrated that the gaze-cueing effect was induced by auditory targets12, suggesting that gaze cues orient observers’ spatial attention in a modality-independent manner. The gaze-cueing effect to sounds that we observed under both the with- and without-barrier conditions does not support the idea that barriers around the android affect the observer’s attention to the extent that the gaze-cueing effect disappears in Experiment 2. It was reasonable to assume that the android could hear and attend to the auditory targets, even when there were barriers, because the android was not completely isolated. This mental state attribution is in line with the function of the gaze cue, i.e., to transfer information in the environment to others. Thus, the gaze-cueing effect was not decreased. To the best of our knowledge, our results provide the first evidence that mental state attribution modulates the automatic attentional shift triggered by android eyes.

However, it should be noted that gaze-triggered attention orienting could be modulated by several contextual factors8, which may explain the results of Experiments 2 and 3. One such factor is the task goal. A previous study has demonstrated that participants followed the cue’s gaze direction when the gaze was directed toward stimuli relevant to the task goal but not when the gaze was directed toward stimuli irrelevant to the task goal39. Based on this finding, the setup for the non-visible condition in Experiment 2 may have implicitly indicated to participants that the android eye gaze, which was occluded by barriers, could be irrelevant to the task goal. In Experiment 3, it may have been obvious that the android’s eye gaze was relevant to the task goal because the barriers did not prevent the detection of sound targets. Further studies are warranted to increase the feasibility of mental state attribution-based interpretations of the attention orienting triggered by android eyes.

Our findings could have theoretical implications, assuming that they reflect mentalistic attentional orienting triggered by android eyes. First, the findings provide insights regarding whether humans attribute mental states to robots40. A previous study assessed participants’ mental state attributions to various agents and found that humanoid robots were considered to have no subjective experience, i.e., their subjective experience was rated below that of a deceased woman41. However, another study using implicit mentalizing tasks, such as explaining agents’ behaviors in mentalizing terms, found that participants attributed mental states to humanoid robots similar to those of humans42. Our findings are consistent with the latter study and demonstrate that attributions regarding the attention state of an android can occur in an implicit task43not directly assessing mental state attribution. Second, our findings provide insight into processing that involves the eyes in real-life situations. A psychological model has been proposed wherein the detection of seeing eyes activates shared attention44. The model has been extended to provide a psychological and neural framework45. The rapid detection of eyes was confirmed by previous intracranial electroencephalography data indicating that the visual cortices distinguish between eyes and other stimuli46, as well as between opened and closed eyes47, within 200 ms. Although previous studies have provided supportive evidence for the model17–20, all of these studies used screen-based stimuli, resulting in limited ecological validity. By using an android, which offers high ecological validity and experimental control24,25, our findings demonstrated that the perception of the seeing eyes of another individual results in rapid and automatic attention allocation in real life.

Our findings may also encourage the use of social robots in real-world applications. These robots have become popular worldwide, supporting human labor both at home and in the workplace22. To fulfill this purpose, it is important that social robots are able to interact and communicate with humans naturally23. Because the eyes are important in human communication48, social robots should have eyes that appear natural to humans. A neuroimaging study has revealed that artificial agents that are more humanlike both in appearance and motion more strongly activate the brain regions associated with mental state attribution49. In the context of such findings, our results suggest that robot eyes that are humanlike both in appearance and motion can automatically induce social attention allocation and mental state attribution in a similar manner to human eyes, pointing to the potential for optimized collaborations between humans and robots in future societies.

In addition to the possible confounders discussed above, there were several other limitations in this study. First, we tested only Japanese participants. Some studies have suggested that individuals from Eastern cultures have superior mental state attribution abilities, including taking the perspective of others, compared with those from Western cultures (e.g50.,; for a review, see51). Thus, whether the modulatory effect of mental state attribution on the effect of android’s eyes applies to Western cultures remains to be determined. Second, because we used only one android, the generalizability of the present findings remains unknown. Future research should test gaze-triggered attention orienting and the effect of mental attribution by using different types of androids.

In summary, we found that valid android eye and head cues presented during live interactions shortened RTs to localize peripheral light targets, relative to invalid cues in Experiment 1. In Experiment 2, the RTs to targets were faster with valid eye cues when the targets were visible to the android but not when they were occluded by barriers. In Experiment 3, the RTs to the target sounds were faster with valid eye cues, irrespective of the presence of barriers. These results suggest that android eyes, like human eyes, may automatically induce attention orienting in humans based on the mental state attribution.

Methods

Experiment 1

Participants. Experiment 1 included 15 volunteers (5 females and 10 males; mean ± standard deviation [SD] age, 40.7 ± 7.9 years). The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power software ver. 3.1.9.252. For repeated-measures ANOVA with cue validity (valid versus invalid) and cue type (eyes versus head) as within-subjects factors, an f-value of 0.4 (as in a previous study13), an α-level of 0.05, and a power (1 − β) of 0.80, the power analysis showed that 10 participants were required. The participants were recruited from the local community through advertisements. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity. They received an explanation regarding the experimental procedure and provided written informed consent. The Ethics Committee of RIKEN approved this study. The experiment was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Design. The experiment used a within-subjects two-factorial (cue validity [valid versus invalid] and cue type [eyes versus head]) design.

Apparatus.The android Nikola25 was used to provide gaze cues. Nikola was developed at RIKEN for the purpose of studying human–robot emotional interactions. Only the head part was used in our study; a mannequin body wearing a hat, scarf, and clothes was positioned on a chair. The robot head is human-like in appearance, similar to that of a male human child. It is approximately 28.5 cm in length and weighs approximately 4.6 kg. It has 35 actuators: 29 for facial muscle actions, 3 for head movement (roll, pitch, and yaw rotations), and 3 for eyeball control (panning of the individual eyeballs and tilting of both eyeballs). The movements are driven by pneumatic (air) actuators, and the entire surface, except for the back of the head, is covered in soft silicone skin. An extended system comprising control valves, an air compressor, and computers controls the actuators and sensors. Nikola was positioned about 1 m in front of the participant.

Two LEDs (Grove LED Module [white; SKU 104030009]; Seeed Technology, Shenzhen, China) were used as target stimuli. The LEDs were located on the left and right sides, about 0.5 m from the center and were set on microphone arrays shaped like potted plants. The lights were controlled using Arduino Uno Rev3 (Somerville, MA, USA) and in-house programs created with Arduino IDE 2.2.1 (Somerville, MA, USA).

Experiments were controlled using PsychoPy 2022 − 2.453 running on a Windows 11 computer (Alienware Aurora R13; Dell, Round Rock, TX, USA). Additional in-house programs created using Python (Python Software Foundation, Fredericksburg, VA, USA) and ROS (Open Robotics, Mountain View, CA, USA) were used to control Nikola. The participants’ responses were obtained using a response box (RB-530; Cedrus, San Pedro, CA, USA) with a 2–3-ms resolution.

Stimuli. As gaze cues, the eyes and head of Nikola were oriented toward the left or right LED (Fig. 1; Movie S1). The LED lights served as the target signals.

Procedure. The experiments were conducted in a chamber on an individual basis. Each participant was seated comfortably across a table in front of Nikola on a chair that could be adjusted in height to allow eye contact with the android.

The participants completed a total of 96 trials, presented in four blocks of 24. Each block comprised an equal number of valid and invalid trials for each cue type condition. Cue validity and cue type were randomized within each block. A break was provided after each block. At the beginning of the experiment, participants completed eight practice trials.

In each trial, Nikola looked up from its initially slumped position, opened its eyes, and said, “Let’s go.” The gaze cue was then presented, i.e., the eyes or head moved toward the left or right over 100 and 430 ms, respectively. One of the LEDs was lit up until a response was made, with a stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA; i.e., the time between cue movement onset and the onset of the light) of 600 ms. A meta-analysis of the gaze-cueing effect according to SOA found that the magnitude of the effect did not vary with an SOA up to 800 ms7. Other studies revealed that dynamic gaze cues based on the same images, but with different presentation durations, produced similar gaze-cueing effects54–57; thus, we did not expect cue motion duration to have a large impact on the gaze-cueing effect. After the response, Nikola slumped and closed its eyes during intertrial intervals of 3,000–5,000 ms. During the intertrial and rest periods, Nikola continuously made subtle motions (e.g., breathing58) to enhance its human-like impression.

As in previous studies (e.g6.,), participants were instructed to indicate whether targets appeared on the left or right side as quickly as possible. Participants were told that the gaze cues of Nikola were not predictive of the target position. Responses were made by pressing the appropriate key on the response box using the left or right index finger. The time between the target onset and the response was recorded as the RT.

Data analysis.The data were analyzed using JASP 0.14.159. The mean RT for correct responses was calculated for each condition and participant, with RTs outside of the range of ± 3 SD being classified as artifacts. The RTs were analyzed by 2 (cue type) × 2 (cue validity) repeated-measures ANOVA. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. In preliminary analyses, the numbers of errors were calculated. The mean ± standard error [SE] percentage of errors was low in all conditions (100.0% ± 0.0%, 98.9% ± 0.5%, 100.0% ± 0.0%, and 99.7% ± 0.3% for the head–valid, head–invalid, eyes–valid, and eyes–invalid conditions, respectively), and there was no evidence of a speed–accuracy trade-off.

Experiment 2

Participants. Experiment 2 tested 18 volunteers (11 females and 7 males; mean ± SD age, 37.6 ± 8.2 years). The conditions of participants were the same as in Experiment 1.

Design. The experiment used a within-subjects two-factorial (cue validity [valid versus invalid] and visibility [visible versus non-visible]) design.

Apparatus and stimuli. Experiment 2 used identical apparatus and stimuli to Experiment 1, except that only the eyes were used as cues and two barriers were used to manipulate visibility. Each barrier was made by connecting two document boxes (FB-A4TB; UNIV. CO-OP, Tokyo, Japan).

Procedure. The procedure in Experiment 2 was identical to that in Experiment 1, except that the cue type factor was replaced with the visibility factor. The visibility conditions (visible versus non-visible) were manipulated across blocks (Fig. 1; Movie S1). Under the visible condition, trials were identical to the eye condition in Experiment 1. Under the non-visible condition, the eye condition trials were conducted with two barriers placed next to Nikola to prevent it from seeing the target LEDs. The order of visibility conditions was counterbalanced across participants.

Data analysis. Using the same software and preprocessing steps as in Experiment 1, RTs were subjected to 2 (cue type) × 2 (visibility) repeated-measures ANOVA. The mean ± SE error percentage was low in all conditions (99.0% ± 1.8%, 98.0% ± 2.6%, 99.3% ± 1.6%, and 97.5% ± 2.6% for the visible–valid, visible–invalid, non-visible–valid, and non-visible–invalid conditions, respectively), and there was no evidence of a speed–accuracy trade-off.

Experiment 3

Participants. Experiment 3 tested 16 volunteers (12 females and 4 males; mean ± SD age, 33.3 ± 8.7 years). The conditions of participants were the same as in Experiment 1.

Design. The experimental design was the same as in Experiment 2.

Apparatus and stimuli. Experiment 3 used identical apparatus and stimuli to Experiment 2, except that the targets were changed from LEDs to buzzer sounds (Movie S1). Two buzzers (Grove Buzzer [SKU 107020000]; Seeed Technology, Shenzhen, China) were placed in the same locations as the LEDs in the above-described experiments to provide the sound targets.

Procedure. The procedure was identical to that in Experiment 2 except that the participants were asked to localize the sound signals.

Data analysis. The RT data were analyzed using the same software, preprocessing steps, and ANOVA as in Experiment 2. The mean ± SE error percentage was low for all conditions (98.4% ± 2.6%, 97.7% ± 2.1%, 97.9% ± 4.3%, and 97.1% ± 2.9% for the visible–valid, visible–invalid, non-visible–valid, and non-visible–invalid conditions, respectively), and there was no evidence of a speed–accuracy trade-off.
