Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of the appendix are relatively rare and often discovered incidentally during appendectomies or surgeries for other conditions. Appendiceal carcinoid lesion is a rare neoplasm found only in 0.3–0.9 % appendiceal specimens [1]. These tumors typically present a benign course, but their detection is crucial for appropriate staging and management. This case report aims to emphasize the importance of meticulous pathological examination in colorectal surgeries and to review the clinical features, diagnostic criteria, and management strategies for incidental appendiceal NETs. Synchronous primary tumors are characterized by the presence of two or more distinct histological tumors identified in a single patient either simultaneously or within a six-month timeframe [2]. We present a case of incidental detection of appendicular carcinoid in a patient who underwent right hemicolectomy for adenocarcinoma right colon, following the SCARE guidelines [3].

Case presentation

A 48-year-old male presented with a 3-month history of intermittent abdominal pain, weight loss, and altered bowel habits. Physical examination was unremarkable except for mild tenderness in the right lower quadrant. Laboratory tests revealed anemia (hemoglobin: 10.2 g/dL) and elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels (15 ng/mL). Colonoscopy identified a mass in the right colon, and biopsy confirmed adenocarcinoma. The patient underwent an elective right open hemicolectomy with D3 lymphadenectomy with mechanical ileocolic anastomosis. The surgical specimen included a segment of the ileum, cecum, ascending colon, and the appendix. Cut surface showed a growth in the ascending colon measuring 5.5 × 4.5x4cm which was 9.5 cm away from the distal resected end. The appendix appeared grossly unremarkable.

Microscopic examination of the colonic mass exhibited an invasive tumor arranged in the form of glands, nests and solid sheets. The tumor cells exhibit moderate pleomorphism with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Focal signet ring cell differentiation was also noted. Thus, a diagnosis of a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma invading through the muscularis propria into the pericolic fat (pT3) was made. Lymphovascular invasion was present, and twenty out of twenty one lymph nodes were positive for metastatic carcinoma (pN2b). The surgical margins were clear of tumor. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells were positive for CK20 and MUC5AC and negative for CK7 [Fig. 2]. Incidentally, a 0.8 cm well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor (NET) was found at the tip of the appendix. The tumor cells were monomorphic with round to oval nuclei and scant cytoplasm with stippled chromatin. On IHC, they were positive for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, consistent with neuroendocrine differentiation. The mitotic rate was low, and the Ki-67 index was <2 %, indicating a low-grade (G1) tumor [Fig. 3]. The appendix was processed entirely, however, no lymphovascular invasion or metastasis was observed in the periappendiceal tissue or lymph nodes. Thus, a final diagnosis of moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma, ascending colon, pT3N2b with well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor of the appendix was made. The post-operative period was uneventful. The patient was given eight cycles of oxaliplatin and capecitabine chemotherapy. He tolerated the treatment well and is currently under follow-up with no evidence of any recurrence or metastasis. Discussion

Incidental appendiceal neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are relatively rare, discovered in approximately 0.3–0.9 % of appendectomy specimens [4]. These tumors are often asymptomatic and typically present a benign course [5]. The detection of these tumors during surgeries for other conditions, such as in this case of colonic adenocarcinoma, underscores the importance of thorough pathological examination of resected specimens. There are very few cases reported in the literature of incidental detection of appendiceal neoplasms incidentally discovered in cases of resections done for colorectal adenocarcinomas [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. The management and clinical implications of appendiceal NETs vary based on the size, location, and histological features of the tumor. Tumors <1 cm, like the 0.8 cm NET found in this case, are generally considered low-risk. They are often confined to the appendix, and if they do not exhibit high-risk features such as lymphovascular invasion, high mitotic rate, or mesoappendiceal invasion, appendectomy alone is typically sufficient. This approach minimizes the need for additional surgical interventions, reducing patient morbidity associated with more extensive surgeries [4].

For tumors between 1 and 2 cm, additional imaging and follow-up are recommended to monitor for any signs of progression or metastasis. Tumors larger than 2 cm or those with high-risk features usually necessitate a right hemicolectomy to ensure complete removal and to address potential metastatic spread [4].

In the case presented, the incidental NET was found during a right hemicolectomy performed for a primary diagnosis of adenocarcinoma of the right colon. The primary tumor was moderately differentiated, with significant lymphovascular invasion and metastasis to 20 out of 21 lymph nodes, indicating an advanced stage of colonic adenocarcinoma (pN2b). The detection of the appendiceal NET, although incidental, was crucial for comprehensive staging and appropriate patient management.

The patient's treatment plan included adjuvant chemotherapy with oxaliplatin and capecitabine, targeting the primary colonic adenocarcinoma. The presence of the NET did not alter the chemotherapy regimen, given its small size and low-grade nature. However, the discovery of this incidental tumor highlights the need for diligent follow-up, as patients with synchronous tumors may have an increased risk of recurrence or development of new malignancies.

Regular follow-up, including periodic imaging and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level assessments, is essential for early detection of any recurrence or metastasis. This comprehensive approach ensures that incidental findings are not overlooked and that patients receive optimal care based on the entirety of their pathological findings.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of thorough pathological examination of resected specimens in colorectal surgeries, as incidental findings can significantly impact patient management. Although incidental appendiceal NETs are typically indolent and often do not require extensive surgical intervention, their detection is crucial for accurate staging and appropriate follow-up. Clinicians should be aware of the potential for synchronous tumors and manage each case based on individual risk factors and tumor characteristics.
