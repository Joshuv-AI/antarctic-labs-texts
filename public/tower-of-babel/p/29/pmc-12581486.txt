Radiology knowledge and career interest among medical students in Egypt, Libya, and Yemen: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Muataz Kashbour, Omar Al Dabssi, Mahmoud Ahmed Yahia Abdelaziz, Sarah Amro, Ruaa Mustafa Qafesha, Radfan Yahya Al-Abdi, Mohamed Abdelaal Nasser, Menna Elbadry, Mariam Balti, Ahmed H Elmasry, Moad Ali Gassim Shetwan, Aiesha Imbarek Hassan Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiology plays a crucial role in diagnosing and managing numerous diseases, yet it remains underrepresented in many medical school curricula. This is the first multicenter study to evaluate the knowledge and attitudes of Arab medical students towards radiology, aiming to identify educational disparities and their potential causes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted across three Arab countries: Egypt, Libya, and Yemen. It targeted medical students in their 4th, 5th, or 6th year, as well as postgraduate internship year. Data was collected using an online questionnaire comprising 38 questions organized into seven sections. The questions were formatted using a multiple-choice Likert scale and yes/no responses. Statistical analysis was carried out using Jamovi software version 2.3.ResultsA total of 802 students (51.4% male, 48.6% female), ranging from 4th-year students to interns, completed the questionnaire. The overall level of radiology knowledge was modest (9.0 ± 2.4), with mean scores slightly above half of the maximum possible (17 points). Yemen exhibited the highest mean score (9.3 ± 2.4), followed by Egypt (8.9 ± 2.5) and Libya (8.6 ± 2.2). Students who received their radiology education through individual modules achieved significantly higher knowledge scores than those instructed through integrated modules (p = 0.0001). The participants also demonstrated a high level of knowledge about radiology screening tests. However, understanding radiation exposure levels for CT, MRI, and ultrasound was limited. Of the total, 491 students (61.2%) expressed interest in pursuing a career in radiology, while 311 (38.7%) cited lack of interest and concerns about radiation exposure as deterrents.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that medical students in Egypt, Libya, and Yemen have awareness of some core radiology concepts; particularly those related to fetal safety and established screening practices. However, their overall knowledge of radiology is modest, with substantial gaps in areas such as radiation exposure levels and contraindications. It highlights the need for enhanced radiology education early on in clinical years to address these gaps in the field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08110-w.Keywords: Radiology, Education, Medical, Students, Medical, Career choice, Radiation effects, Cross-Sectional studies

Introduction

Radiology is crucial in all aspects of patient care and is a vital diagnostic and therapeutic tool in clinical practice [1]. However, there is a lack of radiology training in preclinical and clinical courses in medical schools. Radiology education in most institutions is often limited to anatomical courses. Studies have indicated that medical students lack the skills to interpret radiological images [2, 3]. Consequently, healthcare professionals advocate for the augmentation of radiology training within medical education curricula [4, 5]. In the first three years of the medical school program, less than 40% of medical schools incorporate lessons on imaging algorithms, as indicated by Straus et al. who conducted a study by the American College of Radiology Task Force on Medical Student Education [6].

Several gaps were highlighted in the literature in radiology education. The ability to accurately select appropriate imaging tests is a fundamental skill of physicians in medical practice. However, previous studies showed a concerning number of recent medical graduates lack the proficiency to select proper clinical investigations and understand the risks involved with standard diagnostic tests [7, 8]. This situation elicits concerns regarding the insufficiency of targeted radiological training, potentially resulting in redundant diagnostic tests. Therefore, it is essential to provide enhanced radiological education for medical students, as it broadens their understanding of medical imaging and encourages their interest in pursuing a career in radiology [8].

Another important gap of radiology education is ultrasound education. Historically, medical students have had limited or no exposure to hands-on ultrasound training, and many curricula still lack structured ultrasound integration. Studies have shown that students would benefit from ultrasound education and that it enhances their understanding of anatomy, physical examination, and clinical decision-making. Lack of early ultrasound training may hinder students’ imaging interpretation skills and awareness of diagnostic options [9, 10].

Another factor that created a gap in radiology perception is the lack of understanding of the role of Artificial Intelligence (AI). While some students view AI as a tool that enhances diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, others express concern that it may diminish the role of radiologists or even replace certain tasks altogether. A study by Dahmash et al. revealed that a significant proportion of medical students felt uncertain about pursuing radiology due to fears that AI could reduce job security in the field [11]. However, Li et al. concluded that combining AI technology with radiology education will enhance competency levels and optimize learning outcomes [12].

Other factors contributing to the limited awareness of radiology is radiation risk and protective measures. Both medical doctors and undergraduate students have shown a lack of awareness of radiation risks and protective measures [13, 14]. To overcome those problems, the key is to understand the indications of radiological exams and the risks and benefits of each exam for the patient. This goes through offering a good radiological education for undergraduate medical students.

Such gaps in knowledge may also partly explain the limited interest in radiology as a future career. In a study conducted in Syria to evaluate radiology knowledge among medical students, only 24.5% expressed an interest in pursuing radiology as a future career [15]. This shows a significant need for more interest in radiology. Another study discovered that better quantity and quality of radiology training increases the likelihood that medical students will pursue a career in radiology [16].

Until now, no comprehensive, multi-center study has been conducted in Arab countries to assess radiology knowledge and perceptions among medical students. This study aims to fill this gap by assessing radiological knowledge among medical students through a questionnaire, identifying any disparities and the reasons behind them. The study findings may bring attention to notable shortcomings within the educational system, lead to a better educational curriculum, and contribute towards closing the gaps in radiology knowledge among medical students in the Arab region.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between January and February 2024 across universities in Egypt, Yemen, and Libya. These countries were selected due to the availability of active collaborators, institutional support for multicenter data collection and the feasibility of obtaining ethical approvals across these settings.

We included 4th, 5th, 6th year students, along with interns, as they represent the clinical phase of medical training when students are expected to interact with diagnostic imaging in practice. They were included provided they had completed primary radiology training either as a main subject or as a supplementary course during their medical studies at public and private universities. Preclinical students were excluded due to minimal or no exposure to clinical radiology at that stage.

Rather than selecting specific universities (e.g., public or private), we distributed an online questionnaire through social media platforms (Facebook and WhatsApp) to ensure broad participation. Direct email distribution was not feasible due to lack of access to institutional student mailing lists. Social media groups are one of the primary communication channels among medical students in participating countries and therefore provide an effective and inclusive means of survey dissemination. To reduce bias, the survey form included a drop-down list of all universities in each country, enabling participants to self-report their institutional affiliation.

The study included medical students in their 4th, 5th, or 6th year, as well as postgraduate interns. Students in the first three years of medical school, corresponding to the preclinical phase in participating curricula, were excluded.

Ethical approval

We obtained ethical approval from one of the collaborating medical schools in each participating country, ensuring compliance with national and institutional guidelines. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the following Research Ethics Committees: Zagazig University (Egypt, Approval No: 101038-20-8-2023), Alasmarya Islamic University (Libya), and Althawra Hospital (Yemen, Approval No: 4-2023). A formal approval number was not issued by the Libyan committee, but written confirmation of ethical approval is available upon request.

Questionnaire and measurements

The questionnaire used in this study was previously utilized by Alchallah et al. [15], structured and validated by other studies [14, 17, 18]. The questionnaire was originally in English. All participating institutions use English-based medical education, and thus, no translation was required. The survey introduction clearly stated the study’s objectives and assured participants of the confidentiality of their responses. Participants needed to provide informed consent, and the design of the survey guaranteed anonymity, as no personal information was collected from the respondents. To avoid the risk of duplicate responses, we limited submissions to one per IP device via Google Forms’ settings.

The questionnaire consisted of 38 questions that were distributed among six sections. The first section consisted of six socio-demographic questions (age, marital status, gender, current residence, educational year, GPA). The remaining sections are as follows: background and experience (eight questions), basic knowledge (nine questions), levels of radiation exposure (five questions), radiology as a screening test (four questions), and radiology as a future career (six questions). The questionnaire is provided in (supplementary file).

For the Radiology Knowledge score, only three sections were included in the scoring: basic radiology knowledge (9 questions), radiation exposure (5 questions), and radiology as a screening test (4 questions). One question in the latter section (“What is your source of information?”) was excluded from scoring, as it related to information sources rather than knowledge. Each correct answer was awarded one point, yielding a maximum total knowledge score of 17. Before questionnaire distribution, radiology experts reviewed the questionnaire for content validity. To clarify the terminology regarding knowledge levels, the terms “poor,” “adequate,” and “good” knowledge refer to students’ self-assessed radiology knowledge, based on their subjective evaluation in the questionnaire. Separately, we calculated the objective Radiology Knowledge score by summing the correct answers to 17 knowledge-based questions (maximum possible score = 17). This score was used for statistical analysis but was not categorized into “poor,” “adequate,” or “good” tiers, as no standardized cut-off points exist for this instrument. Thus, the study combined subjective self-assessment with objective knowledge testing, which is consistent with approaches used in prior studies assessing medical students’ radiology knowledge [15, 17].

Pilot study

To ensure the integrity of our study, we conducted a pilot study in Egypt (n = 70 students). Egypt was selected because ethical approval was obtained earlier there, and our collaborator was available to facilitate rapid data collection. This allowed us to detect any issues related to language comprehension or technical errors, thereby enhancing the reliability of the research instrument. Although the pilot was limited to Egypt, its purpose was to test feasibility and clarity, which are not expected to vary substantially between countries. After refinement, the final version of the questionnaire was distributed uniformly in Egypt, Libya, and Yemen.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed data using Jamovi computer software version 2.3. We reported categorical data as frequencies and percentages and continuous data as means and standard deviations (SD). Individual radiology knowledge scores were calculated by adding up the total points obtained by each student. Mean knowledge score percentages were calculated as the average of individual scores. Comparison of knowledge scores between the two categories was carried out using the unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Comparison of knowledge scores between countries was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test and pairwise comparisons. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 802 medical students, ranging from their fourth year to their internship year, completed the questionnaire. The distribution across the countries was as follows: 327 (40.8%) from Egypt, 158 (19.7%) from Libya, and 317 (39.5%) from Yemen. The gender breakdown showed a slight male predominance with 412 (51.4%) males compared to 390 (48.6%) females. The ages of participants ranged from 19 to 27 years, with an average age of 24.1(1.9) years.

Regarding the living situation, a majority of 644 (80.3%) resided in urban areas, while 158 (19.7%) lived in rural settings. The mean Radiology Knowledge score was 9.0 (± 2.4). Yemen ranked highest with a score of 9.3 (± 2.4), followed by Egypt at 8.9 (± 2.5) and Libya at 8.6 (± 2.2) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Radiology background and experience

Most participants, 598 (74.6%), reported that they had personally undergone a radiographic examination, and 705 (87.9%) reported that their relatives had also undergone one. A large number of students showed interest in learning more about radiology, which was evident among 707 students (88.2%). However, only 334 (41.6%) of the participants had completed clinical rotations in radiology. Additionally, a significant portion, 205 (25.6%), were unfamiliar with interventional radiology (Table 2) When assessing their understanding of radiology, participants’ self-evaluations varied: 199 (24.8%) rated their comprehension as poor, 392 (48.9%) as adequate, 180 (22.4%) as good, and only 31 (3.9%) as excellent. Similarly, their grasp of radiation doses was considered poor by 370 (46.1%), adequate by 272 (33.9%), good by 136 (17%), and excellent by a mere 24 (3%) (Table 2).

Knowledge of basic radiology concepts

Our results showed a mix of understanding and gaps. Table 4 summarizes the distribution of correct responses to knowledge-based questions. Students demonstrated good knowledge when asked about the most radiation-sensitive age group, with 579 students (72.2%) providing the correct response. Awareness was higher regarding the impact of radiation on the fetus, as 783 students (97.6%) answered correctly and affirmed its influence. However, slightly over half of the students were uncertain about which radiological exam impacted the fetus most. The safest radiological investigation was quite well-known among students with 674 students (84.0%) identifying the correct answer. In contrast, knowledge of organ sensitivity to radiation was weaker, as only 389 students (48.5%) answered correctly, while 413 (51.5%) did not. Furthermore, students showed a lack of knowledge regarding CT and MRI contraindications with 90.1% and 50.4%, respectively, providing incorrect answers to these questions (Table 4).

Knowledge of radiation exposure levels

Medical students were tested on their knowledge of radiation exposure levels, and the results were concerning. About 83.2% of students gave incorrect responses for chest CT, 64.7% for pelvic MRI, 76.8% full-body CT, and 49.3% abdominal ultrasound (Table4).

Knowledge of radiology in screening

Medical students showed a high level of knowledge about screening tests in radiology, accurately recognizing mammography for breast cancer (91.5%), ultrasound for abdominal aortic aneurysm (70.4%), CT for lung cancer (70.2%) and DEXA for osteoporosis (78.2%) (Table 4).

Radiology as a future career choice

In our study, 311 out of 802 students chose not to pursue a career in radiology. Approximately two-thirds of these individuals 195 (62.7 %) reported a lack of interest in radiology as their primary reason. The second most prevalent concern was radiation exposure, which affected 183 students (58.8%). Lifestyle considerations influenced the decisions of 70 students (22.5%), while 71 (22.8%) cited miscellaneous reasons. Community influence played a role for 54 students (17.3%), and lack of knowledge of the field impacted 53 students (17%). (Table 5 & 6).

Comparative analysis of knowledge and career preferences

A comparative analysis revealed that total scores significantly differed between countries (p= 0.0003), with Yemen having the highest mean score, while Libya had the lowest. Participants who received radiology instruction through individual modules achieved significantly higher knowledge scores compared to those taught via integrated curricula (P = 0.001). Similarly, students who had completed a dedicated radiology rotation demonstrated significantly greater knowledge levels than those without such exposure (P = 0.001). In terms of career interest, a notable geographic disparity was observed: 49.5% of students from urban areas expressed an interest in pursuing radiology as a specialty, compared to only 11.7% of students from rural regions. Conversely, 30.8% of urban students and 8.0% of rural students indicated disinterest in a radiology career. These findings suggest that both educational structure and geographical background significantly influence knowledge acquisition and career inclination in radiology.

Discussion

This multicenter cross-sectional study assessed the radiology knowledge and career perceptions of 802 clinical-year medical students (4th, 5th, or 6th year, as well as interns) from Egypt, Libya, and Yemen. The findings underscore critical educational deficiencies, particularly in radiation safety and contraindications to imaging modalities, while also offering insight into the factors shaping students’ interest in radiology as a career.

Although participants generally demonstrated a foundational understanding of radiology and radiation exposure, 73.7% rated their radiology knowledge as poor or adequate, with only 26.3% considering it good or excellent. Similarly, 46.1% rated their knowledge of radiation exposure as poor, and 33.9% as adequate. These findings align with previous studies: Alchallah et al. [15] found that 62.5% of students rated their radiology knowledge as poor or adequate, while Leong et al. [19] reported that 66% of final-year European medical students described their knowledge as poor or nonexistent. These consistent patterns underscore a widespread perception of insufficient radiology training during undergraduate medical education. The effectiveness of self-rating as a measure of knowledge is limited when used alone, as it may not always reflect actual competency. However, when combined with objective assessment, as in our study, it provides a more comprehensive and reliable evaluation of students’ knowledge and confidence. This dual approach has been adopted in previous studies [15, 17], supporting its value in exploring medical students’ radiology knowledge.

Identifying vulnerable populations to radiation exposure is a fundamental aspect of radiological safety education. In our study, 72.2% of students correctly identified children as the most radiosensitive age group, consistent with Alchallah et al. [15], O’Sullivan et al. [20], and Kada et al. [14], the latter of whom reported similar levels of awareness among Norwegian students. Furthermore, nearly all participants (97.6%) acknowledged the risks of radiation exposure to the fetus, but only 45.9% correctly identified CT as the most harmful modality during pregnancy. This indicates a gap in applying theoretical knowledge to clinical decision-making.

This gap in applied understanding among students mirrors patterns observed in clinical practice. Studies have shown that many practicing physicians and healthcare professionals also demonstrate insufficient awareness of radiation safety. For example, Hobbs et al. [21] reported that 26% of physicians could not identify imaging modalities involving ionizing radiation, while Ditkofsky et al. [22] found that 44.6% of emergency physicians failed to recognize which studies emit radiation. Similarly, Qutbi et al. [23] noted that only 16.4% of healthcare staff at teaching hospitals had good radiation knowledge. These results indicate that misconceptions begin early in training and may persist into clinical practice if not effectively addressed.

Encouragingly, structured education has demonstrated efficacy in bridging these gaps. Hobbs et al. [21] observed significant post-training improvements in radiation knowledge among trainees. Likewise, Shafiq et al. [24] showed that students attending dedicated radiology courses had substantially better awareness of radiation protection. These findings underscore the importance of early, targeted instruction. Given that most medical students in our study received radiology instruction through lecture-based formats, incorporating case-based learning and clinical exposure could bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and its clinical application—particularly in high-risk populations such as pregnant women and children.

In 2007, the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) highlighted the ovaries, testes, bone marrow, and lens of the eye as the most radiosensitive tissues [25], underscoring the importance of recognizing these high-risk organs when selecting imaging modalities. In our study, 51.5% correctly identified the gonads as the most radiosensitive organ, consistent with O’Sullivan et al. [20], who reported a 51% correct response rate. Scali et al. [26] found significantly lower awareness among senior students, with only 24% identifying the gonads correctly. This discrepancy points to variability in how radiation protection principles are conveyed across medical schools.

Despite notable gaps in understanding organ radiosensitivity, students in our cohort demonstrated relatively strong awareness of imaging modality safety: 84% correctly identified ultrasonography as the safest, non-ionizing imaging modality, a significantly higher proportion than the 42% reported by Alchallah et al. [15]. This indicates that students were generally able to distinguish between ionizing and non-ionizing modalities. However, their quantitative understanding of radiation exposure and associated cancer risks was markedly limited.

For instance, in a dedicated section of the survey, students were asked to estimate radiation dose equivalents in chest X-ray units and the cancer risk associated with abdominal CT imaging. Alarmingly, 83.2% of students incorrectly estimated the radiation dose from a chest CT, and only 32% correctly estimated the lifetime cancer risk from a CT abdomen/pelvis in a 30-year-old woman. though this was still better than the 8.6% reported by Prezzia et al. [18]. This pattern suggests that while students can identify safe modalities in a qualitative sense, they often lack the numerical or clinical context needed to understand dose magnitude or biological risk.

These gaps mirror previously reported findings. Scali et al. [26] found that senior medical students frequently underestimated radiation doses, while Hobbs et al. [21] and Ditkofsky et al. [22] noted similar deficiencies among practicing physicians, where 26–45% failed to recognize which imaging techniques involved ionizing radiation. Qutbi et al. [23] also observed that only 16.4% of healthcare professionals demonstrated strong knowledge of radiation safety Together, these findings reinforce the need for educational interventions that go beyond identifying safe modalities and emphasize quantitative comprehension of radiation exposure and patient risk.

Appropriate utilization of MRI and CT imaging requires a solid understanding of their respective contraindications. CT imaging involves ionizing radiation and carries a cumulative cancer risk, particularly in children and pregnant patients [26, 27]. MRI poses different risks, such as device malfunction or projectile injuries due to strong magnetic fields [28, 29]. In our study, participants demonstrated limited knowledge in this area: only 9.9% of students correctly identified all CT contraindications, a finding that aligns closely with Alchallah et al. [15], who reported a correct response rate of 8.2% among Syrian medical students. While 46% recognized key MRI contraindications, such as metallic implants and claustrophobia, indicating relatively better awareness. However, this figure still underscores a knowledge gap, as shown in comparative studies. Alchallah et al. [15] found that only 21.6% of their participants answered all MRI-related safety questions correctly, and Prezzia et al. [18] reported that just 26.4% of fourth-year American medical students demonstrated comprehensive understanding of MRI contraindications. Moseley [30] and Tsai et al. [30] emphasized the importance of MRI safety zone knowledge and compatibility screening, while Cetin et al. [27] highlighted the need for improved CT risk education among students. Our findings reinforce the urgent need for curriculum-integrated safety training focused on contraindications and risk stratification.

Awareness of radiation exposure levels was limited among participants. Over two-thirds (68.5%) were unaware that medically necessary X-rays have no strict annual limit, echoing findings by Kada et al. [14], who reported similar misconceptions in 89% of Norwegian students. Although 32% of our cohort correctly estimated the cancer risk from abdominal CT—higher than the 8.6% reported by Prezzia et al. [18]—this still reflects a substantial gap. These results underscore the urgent need for structured radiation safety education in medical training to support safer imaging decisions.

In our investigation, two major reasons contributed to the misconceptions regarding radiation dosages. The first reason is the lack of information offered by lecturers, with only 21.3% of students viewing lectures as a source of information. The second factor is the shortage of preclinical radiology rotations. This information gap must be addressed in medical training programs. Otherwise, clinicians will continue to mistakenly expose patients to radiation through unnecessary imaging, increasing their chances of developing cancer [14].

Regarding Ultrasound education, ultrasound was assessed in two domains: knowledge of its role as a screening tool (abdominal aortic aneurysm) and awareness that it does not involve ionizing radiation. While a majority of students correctly recognized ultrasound for aneurysm screening, almost half were uncertain about its radiation safety profile. Similar to our cohort, Alchallah et al. reported that only about half of students (51.3%) correctly recognized that ultrasound does not involve ionizing radiation, highlighting persistent misconceptions. Likewise, 69.1% of students in their study identified ultrasound as a screening tool [15]. Our questionnaire was designed to assess basic theoretical knowledge of ultrasound, but it did not evaluate practical scanning skills or operator competency. Other studies have demonstrated that hands-on ultrasound education is both feasible and highly effective and can improve medical students’ understanding and their clinical practice [9, 10].

Medical students should begin training in radiology early in their education to explore their interest in this field. Our study found that 41.6% of students completed a radiology rotation, compared to only 27% of students in a Syrian study by Alchallah et al. [15]. We acknowledge that the relatively low proportion of students who had completed radiology rotations may partly reflect inclusion of earlier clinical-year students (4th and 5th years). This could bias overall findings toward lower knowledge scores. However, including these groups allowed us to identify gaps at different stages of training and compare knowledge progression across year levels.Additionally, Alnajjar et al. [31] reported that 25% of Saudi medical students either completed or planned an elective in Interventional Radiology (IR). These results emphasize the need for clinical radiology rotations for junior students in the Arab world due to its importance in diagnosing and treating diseases. Notably, 78.55% of students reported learning radiology primarily through lectures, which offer mostly theoretical knowledge rather than practical experience.

In this study, approximately 61.2% of participants preferred to pursue a career in radiology, which is significantly higher than previous studies conducted in Syria, where only 24.5% of students considered this field a potential career [15]. The primary reasons for not pursuing radiology were a lack of interest (24.31%) and a fear of radiation exposure (22.82%). However, these concerns can be mitigated by providing better education about radiology during the early years of medical school and encouraging students to engage more with the practical aspects of this field to help alleviate their fears.

These concerns are consistent with literature. Misconceptions about radiation safety and limited clinical exposure have been shown to deter students from radiology. Addressing these issues through early, integrated training may help foster more accurate perceptions of the field and improve specialty recruitment.

Although our questionnaire did not include items on artificial intelligence (AI), it is increasingly recognized as a factor influencing medical students’ perceptions of radiology. AI was excluded because the tool we used (validated in previous studies) did not originally assess this domain. Nonetheless, prior studies show mixed attitudes: some students fear AI may reduce radiologists’ roles, while others see it as a tool to enhance education and diagnostic accuracy. Future research should incorporate AI to better capture its influence on career interest and curriculum development [11, 12].

Recommendations

To address knowledge gaps and improve perceptions of radiology, we recommend early integration of structured radiology education into the undergraduate curriculum, with a focus on practical training in radiation safety, modality selection, and contraindications. Incorporating e-learning tools and simulation-based modules may enhance engagement and reinforce theoretical concepts. These interventions can help ensure students are well-equipped for informed diagnostic decision-making.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the use of a self-reported questionnaire may lead to bias, as participants might overestimate or underestimate their knowledge and experiences. Secondly, the cross-sectional nature of our study limits our ability to establish a cause-effect relationship between radiology education and knowledge levels. Thirdly, although our sample included 802 students, the size was relatively small in Libya, and the total number of medical students in each country could only be approximated rather than precisely determined. As a result, the representativeness of our sample cannot be fully established, and the findings must be interpreted with caution and may not be generalizable to the entire student population. Fourthly, although the sample contains multiple countries (three Arab countries: Egypt, Libya, and Yemen), the findings may not be generalizable to the entire Arab world due to variability in educational programs and exposure to radiology. Fifthly, our analysis focused on the clinical stage of education as a whole (4th year through internship) and did not stratify knowledge scores or radiology exposure by academic year. Due to dataset limitations, we were unable to conduct a robust year-by-year analysis. Future studies with larger, more balanced samples should investigate progression by academic year to provide more granular insights into radiology education effectiveness. In addition, our study did not evaluate the potential impact of artificial intelligence on students’ knowledge, attitudes, or interest in radiology, which represents another important area for future research. Finally, the study did not cover the potential impact of external sources of information, such as personal interest, extracurricular or elective experiences, or emerging technologies like artificial intelligence, all of which could influence students’ knowledge, attitudes, and interest in radiology.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that medical students in Egypt, Libya, and Yemen demonstrated awareness of some core radiology concepts; particularly those related to fetal safety and established screening practices. However, their overall radiology knowledge is modest, with substantial gaps in areas such as radiation exposure levels and contraindications. Early integration of radiology education and increased clinical exposure are critical to ensuring future physicians are well-equipped to make informed diagnostic decisions.
