Visible light accelerates skin wound healing and alleviates scar formation in mice by adjusting STAT3 signaling
Authors: Fangqing Deng, Rong Yang, Yingchun Yang, Xu Li, Jing Hou, Yanyan Liu, Jueru Lu, Shuaiqi Huangfu, Yuqi Meng, Si Wu, Lianbing Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the wound healing process, the activation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is considered crucial for the migration and proliferation of epithelial cells, as well as for establishing the inflammatory environment. However, an excessive STAT3 activation aggravates scar formation. Here we show that 450 nm blue light and 630 nm red light can differentially regulate the phosphorylation of STAT3 (p-STAT3) and its downstream cytokines in keratinocytes. Further mechanistic studies reveal that red light promotes wound healing by activating the PI3 kinase p110 beta (PI3Kβ)/STAT3 signaling axis, while blue light inhibits p-STAT3 at the wound site by modulating cytochrome c-P450 (CYT-P450) activity and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. In a mouse scar model, skin wound healing can be significantly accelerated with red light followed by blue light to reduce scar formation. In summary, our study presents a potential strategy for regulating epithelial cell p-STAT3 through visible light to address skin scarring issues and elucidates the underlying mechanisms. Subject terms: Regenerative medicine, Molecular medicine

Introduction

As a physiological boundary of the human body to the external environment, the skin provides an essential protective barrier that isolates external stimuli (microbes, radiation, toxic chemicals, and other potential threats) from the internal environment1,2. The integrity of the skin is crucial for maintaining the stability of the homeostasis, which requires the body to have efficient mechanisms to initiate a series of wound repair processes in the shortest possible time after skin damage occurs3.

Previous studies have demonstrated the crucial role of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) activation in wound healing process4,5. Immediately after skin damage occurs, STAT3 around the wound is activated and initiates multiple processes to collectively promote wound healing6,7. STAT3 promotes the production of various growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) by direct or indirect action8,9, which enhances the survival and proliferation of epithelial cells, promotes vascular formation at wound site, and accelerates the migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts to fill the gap4,10. STAT3 can also coordinate the accumulation of collagen through transforming growth factor-β signaling, which provides critical support for subsequent healing processes11. In addition, it has been shown that STAT3 promotes the infiltration of various inflammatory cells such as monocytes and macrophages at skin wound site by upregulating the downstream inflammatory factors such as Mcp-1 and IL-6, and exacerbates the local inflammatory response of the wound12,13. This is essential for disinfection and wound cleaning in the initial stage of wound healing. Meanwhile, Mcp-1 can recruit epithelial cells such as fibroblasts to aggregate around the wound and promote their proliferation and secretion, which accelerates wound healing14,15. However, the sustained effects of Mcp-1 lead to the local excessive aggregation and proliferation of epithelial cells and immune cells, exacerbating the formation of scar tissue16,17. The conflicting roles of STAT3 in skin wound healing and scar formation make the activation state of STAT3 an unrevealed target to solve skin trauma problems.

Currently, the process of wound healing until scar formation can be roughly described as three overlapping stages: the inflammatory phase, the proliferative phase, and the remodeling phase18,19. Inflammation and proliferation are the primary events in the wound healing process, while the tissue remodeling processes after the wound completely closed are associated with scar formation. In fact, almost all skin wounds in adults result in noticeable scars after recovery15,20. These scars often bring negative experiences to person daily lives, such as affecting the appearance of the skin, causing physical movement disorders, and especially facial scars causing social shame and psychological trauma21. More and more people are seeking scar repair through local treatment, medication injection, or even plastic surgery21. However, these methods often cannot meet the needs of patients due to the high costs, long treatment cycles, and limited treatment effects22,23. Although it is believed that effective control of STAT3 activation during the inflammatory phase is an effective strategy to alleviate scar problems24, a proper interventional method is missing.

As a noninvasive and mild therapeutic method, visible-light phototherapy has been developed to help patients relieve skin inflammation and promote tissue repair and regeneration25–27. Although it has been reported that red light irradiation can accelerate wound healing by promoting angiogenesis, cell proliferation and migration, and collagen synthesis in the process of wound healing, its molecular mechanism and effects on scar formation are still unclear10,28. In our work, we found a natural difference between visible red and blue light in regulating the STAT3 activation. When skin injury occurred, the irradiation of the wound site with red light activated STAT3 in the skin by PI3Kβ and accelerated the wound healing. Following the red light treatment by early inflammation stage for 3 days, blue light was switched to irradiate the wound site, which activated CYT-P450 in the skin and induced massive ROS production. The phosphorylation of STAT3 was inhibited by ROS and scar formation was alleviated. Red and blue light act as the “switch” for STAT3 activity regulation to accelerate skin wound healing and alleviate scar formation, respectively. These results reveal the promising potential of STAT3 as a photo-target for an inexpensive, convenient, gentle, and noninvasive phototherapy solution for skin scar problem.

Results

Blue light and red light differentially regulated the migration and proliferation of keratinocytes and fibroblasts

As important parts of the skin epidermis and dermis, the behavior of keratinocytes and fibroblasts at the wound site is directly related to the healing of the wound. Short-wavelength blue light and long-wavelength red light are preferred for visible-light phototherapy research because they either carry higher energy or have greater penetration depth, respectively. Therefore, we first validated the effects of 450 nm blue light and 630 nm red light LED (Fig. 1a, b) on the behavior of two types of skin epithelial cells. In the light box shown in Fig. 1b, mouse keratinocytes (PAM212) and fibroblasts (L929) were irradiated with doses of 0 J cm−2, 40 J cm−2, 80 J cm−2, and 160 J cm−2 of blue or red light. By measuring the cell viabilities after irradiation, we found that the increase in PAM212 and L929 cell viability was positively correlated with the red light irradiation dose, while decreased with increasing blue light irradiation dose (Fig. 1c, d). Under the red light irradiation, the viability of keratinocytes increased and reached a maximum at a dose of 80 J cm−2 (Fig. 1c). To confirm the safe dose of biological irradiation, we evaluated the cell vitality after blue light exposure at a condition of 80 J cm−2 and found no significant cell death (Supplementary Fig. 1). To better evaluate the effects of red and blue light on the proliferation of the two cells, we measured the cell growth curves under red and blue light irradiation doses of 80 J cm−2. The cell proliferation of PAM212 accelerated significantly after red light treatment within 24 h, while an opposite effect was observed with blue light (Fig. 1e). Within 32 h, the proliferation of L929 was similarly regulated by red and blue light as PAM212 (Fig. 1f). Finally, we evaluated the effect of red and blue light irradiation on the migration ability by scratch assay and found that red light significantly promoted the migration of PAM212 and L929 after 24 h of irradiation, while blue light inhibited the migration of both cells (Fig. 1g, h). The contrary effects of red and blue light on the behavior of the skin cells have aroused our great interest. To test the impact of red and blue light on wound healing and scar formation, we further validated the effects of red and blue light with a mouse wound model.

Blue light inhibited skin wound healing and relieved scar, red light promoted skin wound healing and increased scar formation

To investigate the effects of visible red and blue light on wound healing and scar formation, we conducted a mouse model experiment as shown in Fig. 2a. The Balb/c mice of the same age with skin wounds of 5 mm diameter on the back were subjected to 9-day red and blue light irradiation (80 J cm−2 per day). The wound healing was recorded on days 0, 3, 5, 7, and 9. The penetration test proved that visible red and blue light can penetrate the skin (Supplementary Fig. 2). After the wounds were completely healed and scarred, H&E staining, Masson staining, and immunofluorescence analysis were performed on the scarred area. From the wound healing point of view, red light irradiation effectively promoted the healing of skin wounds (Fig. 2b). The wounds healed completely on the seventh day and the size of the scars left on the 9th day was significantly smaller than that of the dark group (Fig. 2b). On the contrary, blue light irradiation significantly inhibited the normal healing process and the skin wounds had not healed completely until the 9th day (Fig. 2b). The wound area analysis also showed that the effects of red and blue light treatments on the wound healing process were completely opposite (Fig. 2c). Interestingly, although the scars formed by the red light-treated group were smaller (Fig. 2d, e), the results of H&E staining and Masson staining showed that the stratum corneum of the scars in the red light treated group was thicker than that in the blue light-treated group (Fig. 2f, g). The thickness of the stratum corneum in the blue light group was close to that of the normal group mice. In addition, the results of Masson staining showed that more collagen was synthesized by red light irradiation compared with blue light treatment which was closer to normal tissue (Fig. 2f) and the weight of mice under different light treatments did not fluctuate significantly during the experiment (Fig. 2h).

Blue light promoted an anti-inflammatory environment, and red light prolonged the inflammatory cycle of wound

Inflammation and its subsequent responses accelerate wound healing, while the removal of inflammatory infiltration alleviates scar formation29,30. To investigate the possible regulatory effects of red and blue light on the immune microenvironment by wound healing and scar formation, we further studied the effects of red and blue light on the secretion of inflammatory factors in mouse keratinocytes, skin tissue, and serum. We found that red light increased the secretion of TNF-α and decreased the content of IL-10 in keratinocytes, while blue light had the opposite effects on the secretion of these two cytokines in keratinocytes (Fig. 3a). Interestingly, when we cultured macrophages in the culture medium of keratinocytes irradiated previously with red or blue light, possible polarization of macrophages was observed. The mRNA of M1 type marker cxcl10 and M2 type marker cxcl13 increased slightly when cultured in keratinocyte culture medium irradiated with red and blue light (Fig. 3b). However, direct irradiation of macrophages did not affect the secretion of inflammation-related cytokine TNF-α and IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggested that red and blue light irradiation may affect the immune microenvironment through skin cell produced cytokines.

Furthermore, we tested the effects of visible red and blue light on the immune microenvironment in healthy mouse skin. We found that red and blue light do regulate the immune microenvironment of skin tissue by affecting the phenotype of macrophages and the level of inflammation-related cytokines in skin tissue. The number of CD163+ macrophages (M2 Type) in the stratum corneum increased by 40.98% after blue light irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in skin tissue also increased slightly (Supplementary Fig. 4c), while the level of inflammatory TNF-α decreased significantly after blue light irradiation. In contrast, the number of iNOS+ (M1 Type) macrophages increased by 13.93% and decreased the anti-inflammatory IL-10 in the stratum corneum of the skin after red light irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 4). Although the level of TNF-α also decreased, the degree of decrease was significantly lower than that of blue light (Supplementary Fig. 4c). We also observed significant cytokine changes in mouse serum with a continuous irradiation with red and blue light (7 days, 80 J cm−2, 1 h/day) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Especially, a significant increase in the levels of IFN-γ, IL-1α, TNF-α and IL-13 after exposure to red light suggest a possible pro-inflammatory effect of red light, while blue light decrease in the levels of IFN-γ and IL-13 show a possible anti-inflammatory effects. We speculate that the distinct immune regulatory effects of red and blue light on skin may affect differently the process of wound healing and scar formation (Supplementary Fig. 5).

In next step, we evaluated the effects of red and blue light on the immune microenvironment of skin at the wound site. From the anatomical photos of the internal side of wounds, the inflammation of the red light-treated group was more severe than that of the darkness-treated group, while the inflammation in the blue light-treated group almost disappeared (Fig. 3c). The expression levels of IL-1b and INF-γ in the wound sites were significantly higher in the red light-treated group than in the blue light-treated group (Fig. 3d). The expression of INF-γ in the wound site after red light treatment was significantly higher than that in the dark-treated group (Fig. 3d). In addition, the results of macrophage polarization at the wound sites also showed that blue light treatment accelerated the disappearance of the inflammatory infiltration state in the skin wound. The level of both CD163+ and iNOS+ macrophages was lower by blue light treatment than that of the red light, and dark-treated groups (Fig. 3e). The level of IL-10 in the wound site was also highest in the blue light group (Fig. 3f). All these results indicate the differential regulatory effects of red and blue light on the skin inflammatory environment.

Existing studies have shown that early inflammation at the wound site can promote wound healing by promoting epithelial cell proliferation29,31–33. Combined with our experimental results, we suggest that red light prolongs the inflammatory cycle at the wound site which accelerates skin wound healing and increases scar formation. In contrast, blue light alleviates the inflammatory response and affects cell migration and proliferation at the wound site, thereby slowing down wound healing and alleviating scar formation.

450 nm blue light and 630 nm red light differentially regulated the activation of p-STAT3-VEGF-A/FGF-2/Mcp-1 axis

The opposite regulatory abilities of red and blue light for cell migration, proliferation, and inflammation aroused our great interest. Since the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis plays an important role in promoting cell proliferation, migration, and early inflammatory responses at wound site during skin wound healing (Fig. 4a), we subsequently verified the effects of red and blue light treatment on this axis in normal skin and wounded skin. We were surprised to find that red and blue light irradiation indeed differentially regulated the activation of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis in skin tissue (Supplementary Fig. 6a). In the mouse skin without red and blue light irradiation, we only detected basal levels of p-STAT3 and VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2. Ly6g+ monocytes were almost undetectable in the skin. After blue light irradiation, the p-STAT3, Mcp-1 and Ly6g signals in the stratum corneum disappeared and only weak VEGF-A/FGF-2 signals were detected. In contrast, a clear activation of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis was observed in mouse skin after red light treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6a). By detecting the expression of Mcp-1 cytokines in normal skin, we found that red and blue light differentially regulated the levels of Mcp-1 (Supplementary Fig. S6b, c). Similarly, the expression of VEGF-A and FGF-2 in normal skin was promoted by red light and inhibited by blue light (Supplementary Fig. 6a, e). As an inflammatory recruiting cytokine14,17,34, we believe that the differently regulated Mcp-1 by red and blue light is the reason for the differential regulation of the skin immune microenvironment.

a Schematic diagram of the role of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/FGF-2/Mcp-1 axis in keratinocytes during the wound healing and scar formation processes. b Immunolocalization of p-STAT3, Mcp-1, Ly6g, VEGF-A, and FGF-2 in scar-skin treatment as shown in Fig. 2a. c, d The protein content (c) and transcription (d) of Mcp-1 in scar skin. e, f The transcription of VEGF-A (e) and FGF-2 (f) in scar skin.

We repeated the above tests in the wounded skin and found that compared with the naturally healing wounds, red light irradiation significantly promoted the activation of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis and Ly6g in the wound area. Especially in the scar sections of the red light-treated group, we observed obvious VEGF-A/FGF-2 signals around newly formed blood vessels (Fig. 4b). These growth factors increased after red light irradiation, accelerated the vascularization, and promoted wound healing. Blue light significantly inhibited the activation of this signaling pathway, and the Ly6g signal of monocytes almost disappeared in the skin tissue of the blue light-treated group (Fig. 4b). The trends of VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 detected in the scarred area were consistent with those detected in normal skin after red and blue light treatment (Fig. 4c–f). Besides in skin, we also observed that red light upregulated the expression of VEGF/EGF and FGF-2 while blue light downregulated the level of FGF-2 and FGF21 in the serum of light-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Overall, these results demonstrate that the natural differential regulation of p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis is the basis for the differential regulation of skin wound healing and scar formation by red and blue light. To further explore the molecular mechanisms of the regulation, we conducted transcriptomic analysis of keratinocytes after visible red and blue light irradiation. By RNA-seq analysis, 1335 and 806 genes were differentially expressed in red light group and blue light group, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Cluster analysis of transcriptome data also indicated that red and blue light differentially activate keratinocyte transcription (Supplementary Fig. 7b). We propose that these differentially expressed genes are the fundamental reasons for the differential regulation of wound healing process by red and blue light.

Blue light-regulated CYT-P450 expression and ROS production to inhibit p-STAT3-VEGF-A/FGF-2/Mcp-1 axis

By analyzing and comparing the transcriptomic data of keratinocytes treated with blue light irradiation and dark, we compiled the upregulated and downregulated signaling pathways after light irradiation (Fig. 5a). The most significant difference was observed in two signaling pathways related to CYT-P450 metabolism (Fig. 5a), where Cyp1b1 and Cyp1a1 were the genes with the most significant fold change in expression (Supplementary Fig. 8). Existing literature has reported that Cyp1b1 and Cyp1a1 in mitochondria respond to 450 nm blue light and are activated, leading to the generation of a large number of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through related metabolic pathways35–37. Therefore, we used DCFH-DA probe to detect the intracellular ROS in keratinocytes after irradiation with 80 J cm−2 red and blue light. We found that compared to the control, blue light irradiation significantly induced an increase of intracellular ROS in keratinocytes, while red light caused a slight decrease of intracellular ROS (Fig. 5b, c). To further confirm the association between blue light and intracellular ROS production, we checked the generation of ROS in keratinocytes under blue light of different energy densities. The results showed that both overall cellular ROS levels (DCFH-DA) and mitochondrial ROS levels (Mito-sox) showed significant dose-dependency (Fig. 5d). We also found a significant ROS increase in mouse skin of the blue light treatment group, while red light treatment did not cause ROS accumulation in skin tissue (Fig. 5e). When we used Galangin as the inhibitor of Cyp1b1 and Cyp1a1 to treat keratinocytes, the intracellular ROS level decreased (Fig. 5f). Upon further blue light irradiation, we found that ROS in the Light-Galangin group was higher than that in the Dark-Galangin group (Fig. 5f). These results fully demonstrated the ability of blue light to induce cellular and tissue ROS production. The elevation of intracellular ROS can lead to decreased cell viability, and weaken cell proliferation and migration abilities, which is consistent with our previous experimental results of blue light-treated cells (Fig. 1c–h).

ROS, as an important second messenger, participates in the regulation of multiple members of the STAT family. Many studies have shown that an increase in intracellular ROS levels can inhibit p-STAT338–41. Therefore, we further verified the effect of blue light-induced ROS production on p-STAT3 at the cellular level. After blue light irradiation, the level of p-STAT3 in the cells decreased significantly, while the content of STAT3 did not change significantly. However, by adding ROS scavenger NAC, p-STAT3 levels could be restored to the same level as the control group (Fig. 5g). In addition, we also observed a decrease in the secretion of VEGF-A, MCP-1, and FGF-2 in keratinocytes after blue light treatment (Fig. 5g), which is consistent with the decrease in fluorescent signal of VEGF-A, MCP-1, and FGF-2 observed in skin after blue light irradiation (Fig. 4b). These results demonstrate that blue light can inhibit p-STAT3 in keratinocytes by inducing ROS production. Combining the previous experimental results, the mechanism for blue light is that it induces the generation of intracellular ROS by activating Cyp1b1 and Cyp1a1, which inhibits p-STAT3 and prolongs wound healing process, but relieves scar formation.

Red light activated the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/FGF-2/Mcp-1 axis through PI3 Kinase p110 beta

Like by blue light, we also analyzed and compared the transcriptome data of keratinocytes with red light irradiation. The upregulated and downregulated signal pathways after the irradiation are summarized in Fig. 6a. Unlike blue light, red light upregulated a wide range of signal pathways related to protein synthesis and energy metabolism, such as the chemokine signaling pathway and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 6a). To explore the possible mechanism for red light regulating p-STAT3, we analyzed the signal pathways related to skin wound healing and scar formation. The key proteins involved in these signal pathways are associated with the regulation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6b). Phosphoinositide 3-kinase regulates the phosphorylation of many proteins by responding to the stimulation of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), including p-STAT3. The latest research shows that regulation of p-STAT3 through PI3Kβ can control the escape of breast cancer34,42,43. In the transcriptome analysis of red light, the transcription level of the PI3Kβ (Pik3cb) gene increased after red light irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 9). Therefore, it is possible that red light promotes the p-STAT3 process by promoting the expression of PI3Kβ in cells and tissues. In further experiments, we found that red light indeed promoted the expression of PI3Kβ in normal and scar skin, while blue light had no significant effect on the expression of PI3Kβ and p-STAT3 (Fig. 6c–e). To further validate the promoting effect of PI3Kβ on p-STAT3 under red light exposure, we treated keratinocytes with red light in the presence of a PI3Kβ inhibitor and found the protein level of p-STAT3 in keratinocytes decreased, while red light treatment can alleviate this trend (Fig. 6f). These results suggest that red light can promote the p-STAT3 by upregulation PI3Kβ. Further, we validated the regulatory ability of red light on the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis in the presence of STAT3 inhibitor. Red light irradiation increased the protein expression levels of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis and could partially reverse the downregulation of VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 caused by p-STAT3 inhibition (Fig. 6g). These results fully reveal that red light can activate the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis in keratinocytes by upregulating PI3Kβ.

Sequential red and blue light irradiation effectively promoted wound healing and alleviated scar formation

It is interesting to test whether the differential regulatory effects on p-STAT3 by red and blue light could be used in skin trauma to solve the contradiction between wound healing and scar formation. Therefore, we designed a visible-light therapy strategy with red light to promote wound healing in the early stage and blue light subsequently to alleviate scar formation (Fig. 7a). According to existing literature, 2–3 days after the formation of trauma is the occurrence period of inflammation18. To ensure efficacy, we treated the wound sites with red light in the first 3 days after wound formation, and then with blue light (R + B). Correspondingly, blue light irradiation followed by red light irradiation (B + R) was used for comparison (Fig. 7a). In 3 days, the wound area of the R + B group of mice was significantly smaller than that of the Dark group, while the wound healing rate of the B + R group was lower than that of the Dark group (Supplementary Fig. 10). The wounds of the R + B group of mice were completely healed after about 7 days (Fig. 7b), which was similar with the results of the group treated with only red light (Fig. 2b). However, the wounds of the B + R group still had not completely healed on the 9th day of treatment (Fig. 7b, c). These results suggested that the type of light exposure in the first 3 days of the wound might be a determining factor for the speed of wound healing, which was related to the effects of red and blue light on early inflammatory environments of the wound. On the 12th day of treatment, the scar area of the R + B group was the smallest, with an average of only 3 mm2 (Fig. 7d, e). Masson and HE staining analysis showed the R + B group had significantly thinner stratum corneum and higher collagen content than that in the B + R group (Fig. 7f, g). Surprisingly, the thickness of the stratum corneum in the R + B group almost returned to the level of normal skin (Fig. 7e, g). During the treatment period, there was no significant difference in body weight among the groups (Fig. 7h). Our results demonstrated a combined effect of promoting wound healing with red light and alleviating scarring with blue light in precedence.

To confirm the role of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis during this process, we again detected the activation of the p-STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis in this wound model. As expected, p-STAT3 signal in the B + R group was higher than the Dark group, with both Mcp-1 and Ly6g continuously activated in the skin scar tissue (Fig. 8a). In contrast, the lowest levels of p-STAT3, Mcp-1, and almost disappeared Ly6g signals were observed in the R + B group (Fig. 8a). In the R + B group, the gene expression level and cytokine content of Mcp-1 were significantly lower than those in the B + R and Dark groups (Fig. 8b, c). Although VEGF-A/FGF-2 signals did not show a significant difference in skin fluorescence immunostaining, we still detected the lowest expression levels of VEGF-A/FGF-2 in the scar tissue of the R + B group (Fig. 8a, d, e). In addition, we observed evident subcutaneous inflammation in the anatomical photos of the scar tissue in the Dark and B + R groups of this animal model, while almost no visible inflammation state in the R + B group was observed (Fig. 8f). This was supported by the analysis of the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and IL-1b (Fig. 8g). The results of the immunofluorescence staining of macrophages (CD163+ and iNOS+) showed the significantly alleviated infiltration of the immune cells in the scar tissue of the R + B treatment group, indicating the completed wound healing process. In contrast, the scar tissue of the B + R group was still in the proliferation phase of cell infiltration and continued developing scar tissue (Fig. 8h).

By integrating and analyzing the results, we have discovered and verified the regulatory ability of red and blue light to p-STAT3-VEGF-A/FGF-2/Mcp-1 axis during mouse skin wound healing and scar formation processes. On this basis, we have developed a simple visible-light therapy strategy to solve the contradiction between wound healing and scar formation. Our work explored the potential of visible red and blue light for skin trauma treatment, revealed the mechanism of visible light in wound healing and scar formation, and provided a convenient and inexpensive phototherapy for skin scarring problems.

Discussion

Since Fisher first reported in the 1980s the tumor-promoting effect of immunosuppression induced by ultraviolet radiation in mice44, scientists have begun to focus extensively on the photobiomodulation (PBM) of light on cells or biological individuals25,45–48. It has now been elucidated that ultraviolet radiation changes the conformation of chromophores (such as pyrimidine, vitamin D, nucleotides, etc.), promotes the generation of ROS, and causes immunosuppression in the organism. Clinically, the immunosuppressive properties of ultraviolet radiation have been used to treat immune hyper-function induced autoimmune diseases, such as psoriasis and specific dermatitis27,49,50. In addition, extensive research has shown that infrared light can regulate immune response in the body, for example, by enhancing the activation of skin Langerhans51,52. However, due to the cell-killing effect of ultraviolet radiation and the thermal effect of infrared radiation, the biological applications of both have been greatly limited53,54. In comparison, many studies have shown that the application of visible light is safe, promising, and effective in practice. For example, blue light is often used to address the inflammation of skin lesions in patients, while the promoting effects on cell proliferation and migration is considered a significant PBM feature of red light55–57. Among many applications of PBM, wound healing implies the unique advantages of visible light in clinical treatment of skin trauma problems52,58. However, current research has not elucidated the clear mechanism of red and blue light in promoting wound healing10, nor has it revealed whether visible light has the potential to regulate scar formation.

Current research has elucidated the specific process of wound healing until the formation of scars18,20,59. When skin damage occurs, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGE) secreted by platelets and inflammatory cytokines released by resident skin mast cells cause local increase in capillary permeability, promoting neutrophil migration to the site of injury18. Within 24–48 h after injury, the number of neutrophils as the main inflammatory cell at the wound site peaks. These neutrophils differentiate into macrophages by the action of monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (Mcp-1) secreted by keratinocytes at the wound site, which can engulf bacteria and excess cell debris. They also secrete cytokines, such as VEGF-A, to promote granulation tissue formation, endothelial cell proliferation, and wound site vasculogenesis60. Epithelial cells at the wound site, such as keratinocytes and fibroblasts, continue to produce cytokines such as VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 to further promote the recruitment of inflammatory cells and various endothelial cell proliferation processes15,19,31,59. About 72 h after the injury, cell debris at the wound site is basically completely cleared and the wound enters the proliferation phase. Under the stimulation of various growth factors and inflammatory cells, endothelial cells fill the vacancy at the injury site by proliferation and migration processes18. In this process, the body has evolved redundant cascading inflammatory responses to avoid possible infection and quickly construct an inflammatory environment at the wound site through overlapping inflammatory cytokine secretion and inflammatory cell recruitment. This inflammatory environment leads to the generation of excessive proliferation signals and ultimately irregular arrangement of skin tissue cells (remodeling stage), which will form of an abnormal skin tissue state, namely scar tissue, in the damaged area21.

STAT3, as a key transcription factor in coordinating proliferation, migration, and inflammation in tissues61,62, has been shown to play an important role in wound healing and scar formation processes by directly or indirectly coordinating downstream VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 signals to promote inflammation and cell migration at the wound site4–6,9. A continuous activation of p-STAT3 in skin wounds promotes scar formation. Skin scars can be relieved by inhibiting the expression of downstream cytokine MCP-116,17,24. Therefore, STAT3 is a potential and critical target for addressing skin trauma. Current studies show that many factors, including G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), Toll-like receptors (TLRs), and microRNAs, can be involved in the regulation of p-STAT342. Among them, the regulation of p-STAT3 by PI3Kβ activated by GPCRs has aroused our strong interest34,63.

We believe that the reason for visible light to have a wide range of PBM in skin tissue is due to the expression of Opsins in skin cells64. As photoreceptor proteins, Opsins have been widely studied in the light signal transduction and image formation processes mediated in the retina65,66. Although existing studies on PBM targets have revealed that some photoreceptor molecules such as porphyrins, nucleic acids, and xanthophyll proteins can respond to light stimuli50,67, their roles in light signal perception and transduction are not as systematic and coherent as Opsins65,68–70. As the main participant in signaling transduction in cell membrane, GPCR are responsible for most physiological responses caused by environmental stimuli in the body71,72. Opsin, as a member of the GPCR family, has been shown to be expressed in skin cells and to play various regulatory functions64,73–75. Since the only signal messenger downstream of GPCRs is the PI3K family member, and red light upregulated PI3Kβ in cells and tissues in our study (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 9), we speculate that this is the regulatory effect of some Opsin in the skin. Of course, this speculation needs further experimental verification to reveal the role of opsins in wound healing mediated by red light.

In addition, our results in this study showed that visible light has good regulatory ability on the skin’s immune microenvironment (Fig. 3). However, no significant changes in inflammation-related cytokines such as TNF-α/IL-10 were detected after direct irradiation of macrophage RAW264.7 with red and blue light (Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests that the effects of red and blue light on the skin immune environment are mainly through epithelial cells, rather than immune cells. We believe that the regulatory effect of visible light on tissue immune microenvironment has certain potential for anti-tumor treatment, as shown by some studies76–78. The penetration depth, precise irradiation dosage control, and in conjunction with drug treatment are the main challenges for effective tumor phototherapies with visible light.

In summary, we demonstrated the opposite effects of red and blue light on wound healing and scar formation, which were caused by the differences of red and blue light in regulating the STAT3-VEGF-A/Mcp-1/FGF-2 axis. Based on this, phototherapy was developed with red and blue light as a “switch” to p-STAT3 and achieved wound healing and scar relief at the same time. This work demonstrates STAT3 as a therapeutic target for a “one-stop” solution to skin trauma and provides an inexpensive, convenient, and noninvasive phototherapy solution for treating skin scars.

Methods

Cell culture

PAM212 (cat. No. MZ-2610) cell lines were purchased from Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China), and L929 (cat. No. CL0339), Raw264.7 (cat. No. CL0266) cell lines were purchased from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). The cells were cultured in phenol red-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) with 10% (v/v) of fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100U/ml penicillin, 100 mg/ml streptomycin. The CO2 incubator (CLM-170B-8-CN, ESCO, Singapore) was kept at 37 °C, 80% humidity, 5% CO2, and placed in the sterile intercellular chamber.

Animal model

SPF-grade female Balb/c mice (4–5 weeks) were purchased from Xi’an Keao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were transferred to EVC mouse mobile microbarrier (HH-MMB-1, monkey animal experimental equipment Technology Co., Ltd, Suzhou, PRC) and raised at 26 °C. Mice were adapted to the rearing environment for 7 days, and then used depilated cream to remove the hair on their back skin. Mice that did not regrow hair on the second day after depilation were selected for subsequent animal radiation experiments. After the mice were anesthetized, a 5 mm diameter hole punch was used to create a skin wound on the back of the mice to establish a skin wound model. Sufficient water and food were provided to the mice. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Anesthetize the mice by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a concentration of 0.3% (w/v) dissolved in sterile PBS (inject 0.1 mL per 10 g of mouse body weight). All animal experiments involved in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwestern Polytechnical University (Ethics Number: 202201045).

Antibodies and reagents

Antibodies of VEGF-A (A12303), FGF-2 (A22448), p-STAT3 (AP0715), STAT3 (A11216), Mcp-1 (A7277), PI3Kβ (A22467), and β-actin (AC026) were supplied by ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd (PRC). Antibodies of CD163 (GB11340-1), iNOS (GB11119), Ly6g (GB11229) were supplied by Service Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Antibodies of p-AKT(AF2212), MK2206 (PI3K inhibitor) were supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd (PRC). Cryptotanshinone (STAT3 inhibitor) was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA). Mcp-1, IL-10, TNF-α Elisa kit were supplied by ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd (PRC). Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was provided by Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd (PRC). Cell Counting kit 8 (CCK-8) was supplied by Gen-view (USA), Reactive oxygen species (ROS) probes 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) and N-Acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd (PRC). A mitochondrial reactive oxygen species probe (Mito-sox) was obtained from Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd (PRC).

LED devices

In this study, ZWYC-290A illumination incubator (ZHICHENG®, Shanghai, PRC) was applied to irradiate cells and mice. The illumination incubator can provide an adjustable 450 nm blue light and 630 nm red light source (Fig. 1a), and the maximum power of illumination incubator is 3500 W, the irradiation area is 850 cm × 450 cm. By adjusting the power and irradiation time, we can irradiate mice and cells with visible red and blue light at different gradient doses (40 J cm−2, 80 J cm−2, 160 J cm−2), and the control group was kept in darkness. Particularly, the irradiation dose of cell samples for RNA-seq analysis was 80 J cm−2.

RNA extraction

For cell samples, an appropriate number of cells were seeded in 100-mm cell culture dishes and were cultured overnight. The cells were irradiated when they adhered to the bottom of the dish and the confluency of cells reached about 80%. Twelve hours after the end of irradiation, cells were digested with trypsin and collected for RNA extraction by adding Trizol (TRleasyTM Total RNA Extraction Reagent, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, PRC). For mouse skin tissue samples, sterile scissors were used to cut the tissue sample into small pieces (less than 2 mm), which were then frozen in liquid nitrogen and carefully ground into tissue fragments in a sterile quartz grinding bowl. Collect these skin tissue fragments, add Trizol and homogenize with a homogenizer to extract RNA from the skin tissue.

Real-time PCR quantification

The HIfair® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (11121ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, PRC) was used to reverse transcribe RNA into the first strand cDNA. The gene expression differences in these samples were detected by real-time PCR quantification. QPCR instrument was purchased from Bioer Technology Co., LTD (FQD-96A, Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, PRC). Primers used for this analysis are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

In total, 105 PAM212 cells were seeded into 100-mm cell culture dish and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then divided into three identical treatment groups by subculture. The red group and blue group were irradiated with 80 J cm−2 red and blue light, respectively, the control group was covered with aluminum foil to make sure that a total dark environment was provided. All treatments were performed in the ZWYC-290A illumination incubator. After light treatment, cells were cultured for 12 h. Then, Trizol reagent was used to extract the total RNA of those sample. Finally, sequencing was performed on the Illumina Hiseq™ platform (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, PRC). The RNA-seq analysis data used in this study were presented in Supplementary Data 1.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting kit 8 was used to test cell viability. PAM212 and L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. After irradiation treatments, DMEM containing CCK-8 solution (5 mg/mL) was added to all wells immediately and incubation for 40 min. Multiscan GO spectrophotometer (1510, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to detect the optical density of different treatment groups at 450 nm.

Wound‑healing migration analysis

The effect of red and blue light irradiation on the migration abilities of PAM212 and L929 were evaluated using wound healing analysis. In total, 2 × 105 cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultured until the cell confluence reached 80–90%. Then, a straight scratch area was formed in the center of the well using a sterile 200 μl pipette tip. The culture dish was washed with PBS to remove scraped cells, and fresh phenol red-free serum-free medium was added. Subsequently, the cells were irradiated with visible red and blue light at a dose of 80 J cm−2. After 24 h of irradiation, the migration of cells was observed and photographed under the inverted microscope.

Western blot analysis

Before light exposure, PI3Kβ inhibitor (MK2206, 1uM) and STAT3 inhibitor (Cryptotanshinone, 2 μM) or NAC (5 mM) were added to the culture medium of keratinocytes. After receiving irradiation of 80 J cm−2 red or blue light, the keratinocytes were lysed with RIPA buffer (Solarbio) containing 1% PMSF to extract proteins from the cells. The protein concentration was then determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated using FuturePAGE™ 4–12% 12-well precast gels (ET12412Gel, ACE Biotechnology, PRC), and then transferred into PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membranes. The membranes were rapidly blocked using NcmBlot Blocking Buffer (P30500, NCM Biotech, PRC) at room temperature, and then incubated with diluted primary antibodies: PI3Kβ, STAT3, p-STAT3, p-AKT, Mcp-1, VEGF-A, FGF-2, β-actin. Appropriate secondary antibodies (1:10000) were then chosen for incubation for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein blotting was observed on the chemiluminescence imaging system (JS-M6, Peiqing Science and Technology, PRC).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To detect the effects of red and blue light irradiation treatment on the levels of relevant cytokines in cells and skin, we used MCP-1 (RK00381), IL-10 (RK00016), and TNF-α (RK00027) Elisa kit purchased from ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd (PRC) to measure the levels of relevant cytokines in PAM212, Raw264.7, and skin tissue. In brief, samples/standards (100 μl) were added to the antibody-coated wells and incubated for 2 h. After washing the unbound samples/standards, biotinylated secondary antibodies (100 μl) were added and incubated for 1 h. Then, excessive secondary antibody was washed off, 100 μl of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was added to each well, followed by the addition of 100 μl of substrate solution TMB (3, 3′, 5, 5′-Tetramethylbenzidine) and incubated in the dark for 15 min. The optical density at 450 nm was measured using the Multiscan GO spectrophotometer (1510, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Intracellular ROS detection

For intracellular reactive oxygen species, after visible red and blue light irradiated, the cells were stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 0.5 h. Then, the fluorescence status of DCFH-DA fluorescence probe in the cells was identified using flow cytometry (EasyCell103A0, Wellgrow Tech, PRC), fluorescence microscope (CKX53SF, Olympus, Japan), and Muti-Mode Microplate Reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA). For mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, cells were incubated with 5 μM Mito-sox for 15 min, and the fluorescence status of Mito-sox fluorescence probe in the cells was identified using fluorescence confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany) and Muti-Mode Microplate Reader.

Skin ROS fluorescence staining

Dorsal skins of mice, treated with red and blue light or darkness, were taken and frozen with liquid nitrogen, then the skins paraffin sections and ROS fluorescence staining (Dihydroethidium, DHE) were commissioned by Service Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Peripheral blood cytokine detection

To amplify the PBM induced by red and blue light in organisms, we increased the times of irradiation treatments (once a day for 1 week) in mice with shaved backs, and then the Mouse XL Cytokine Array Kit (ARY028, R&D Systems, USA) was used to analyze the cytokine changes in peripheral blood serum of irradiated mice.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The normal skin and scar skin of mice were treated with 4% PFA fixation and paraffin embedment. The treated skin sample slices (5 µm) were subjected to H&E dyeing procedures after deparaffinized and subjected to rehydration. Imaging was performed via tissue slice scanner (Science, Winmedic Tech Co., Ltd., PRC).

Immunohistochemical staining

The mice skin samples were fixed under room temperature with 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin. The thickness of the treated tissue slices was 5 μm. The corresponding skin slices were subjected to immunohistochemical staining using diluted antibodies Mcp-1, CD163, iNOS, Ly6g, and PI3Kβ. Imaging was performed via tissue slice scanner.

Immunofluorescence staining

The mice skin samples were fixed under room temperature with 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin. The thickness of the treated tissue slices was 5μm. The corresponding skin slices were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using diluted antibodies VEGF-A, FGF-2, p-STAT3. Imaging was performed by commissioned Service Biotechnology Co., Ltd (PRC).

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism 9 software was used for data statistics, and the repetition number of all experimental settings was ≥3. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA were performed to determine the statistical significance. P value < 0.05 was considered a significant difference (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).
