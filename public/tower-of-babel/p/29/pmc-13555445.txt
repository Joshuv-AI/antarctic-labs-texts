Transcervical Enucleation of a Giant Cervicothoracic Intramural Esophageal Spindle Cell Tumor
Authors: Jacob Beiriger, Hilary C. McCrary, Kajsa Affolter, Nicolas Contreras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mammary-type myofibroblastoma and spindle cell lipoma are rare benign spindle cell tumors commonly present in soft tissue sites. We report a novel case of a giant intramural retinoblastoma susceptibility gene (RB1)–deleted spindle cell neoplasm of the esophagus in a 61-year-old man with dysphagia. The 9-cm cervicothoracic tumor extended from the upper esophageal sphincter to the level of the aortic arch. The tumor was enucleated from the esophageal wall through a transcervical incision. This type of giant cervicothoracic tumor provided an anatomic and surgical challenge and was able to be successfully resected through the neck.

Benign intramural esophageal tumors in adults account for <1% of all esophageal neoplasms, with leiomyomas representing 60% to 70% of benign esophageal tumors and 40% to 66% of subepithelial or intramural esophageal lesions.1 Benign intramural esophageal lesions include leiomyoma, granular cell tumor, schwannoma, fibrovascular polyp, and gastrointestinal stromal tumors. These are typically manifested as submucosal masses in the middle or lower esophagus, with sizes ranging from <1 cm to 18 cm, depending on cause. Giant lesions (>5 cm) are nearly always symptomatic with dysphagia and require surgical enucleation.Retinoblastoma susceptibility gene (RB1)–deleted benign mesenchymal tumors like spindle cell lipoma (SCL) and mammary-type myofibroblastoma (MF) are characterized by chromosome 13q14 deletion, loss of RB1 gene, and absence of retinoblastoma protein (Rb) expression. SCLs and MFs typically occur in subcutaneous tissues of the trunk and extremities as slow-growing masses, composed of bland spindle cells, adipocytes, and CD34-positive stroma with Rb loss. No cases of SCL, MF, or any RB1-deleted mesenchymal tumor have been reported in the esophagus or upper aerodigestive tract.2, 3, 4

We report a case of a 61-year-old man with prior lymphoma and mediastinal irradiation who presented with 1.5 years of progressive dysphagia, dyspnea, and chronic aspiration. Cross-sectional imaging and endoscopic ultrasound identified a 9-cm, well-circumscribed intramural esophageal mass arising from the muscularis propria and extending from the cricopharyngeus to the level of the aortic arch, and positron emission tomography showed no hypermetabolic activity (Figures 1, 2). Initial biopsy at an outside institution demonstrated a low-grade myxoid neoplasm without a definitive diagnosis established after immunohistochemical analysis, including an absence of MDM2 amplification by fluorescence in situ hybridization. After optimization, surgery proceeded through a low transverse cervical approach with enucleation of the intramural mass. Approaching the esophagus from the left, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve was identified, and the muscular fibers of the esophagus were divided to expose a well-defined tumor capsule. The mass was able to be separated from the muscular wall attachments cephalad and caudally, delivering the mass from the mediastinum into the operative field (Supplemental Figure). The mucosa was densely adherent to the mass, requiring en bloc excision. An approximately 5-cm defect resulted and was repaired primarily in 2 layers, reinforced with a tunneled left pectoralis major rotational flap. A mediastinal drain and a nasojejunal tube were placed with the patient kept intubated postoperatively because of poor lung reserve. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 2 and recovered uneventfully. He was discharged on postoperative day 9. On follow-up, he was noted to have left partial vocal cord paresis and underwent injection laryngoplasty. Two independent gastrointestinal pathologists at a tertiary care center confirmed the diagnosis. The final resection pathologic examination demonstrated an 8.3 × 5.9 × 3.0-cm well-circumscribed mass lesion, within the esophageal wall, composed of bland spindle cells, mature adipocytes, and prominent ropy collagen bundles. The background contained a fibromyxoid stroma with mast cells. In addition, cartilaginous metaplasia was present. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated CD34 positivity and Rb loss. These features were consistent with a tumor from the RB1-deleted family with a differential diagnosis including SCL and MF. The lesional cells were positive for desmin and androgen receptor, findings more suggestive of MF. However, the overall histologic appearance on routine hematoxylin and eosin staining, along with the absence of estrogen and progesterone receptor expression, was more consistent with SCL (Figure 3). Comment

SCL and mammary-type MF are benign mesenchymal neoplasms with RB1 gene deletion and loss of Rb protein expression.2, 3, 4 They share overlapping morphologic features and appear as well-circumscribed lesions composed of bland spindle cells with a mast cell–rich background. Whereas mature adipocytes and ropy collagen are typical of SCL, MF exhibits a range of variants, including lipomatous forms, that can mimic SCL. Their immunoprofiles also overlap as both tumors express CD34 and show loss of Rb1. Desmin is more commonly expressed in MF, although occasional expression in SCL has been reported.5

The available clinicopathologic and molecular studies of SCL, mammary-type MF, and related RB1-deleted neoplasms focus on classic anatomic sites and do not include the gastrointestinal tract or the esophagus as a primary site.2,4 The combination of esophageal location, giant size, and rare cartilaginous metaplasia distinguishes this case from well-described esophageal leiomyomas and represents an unprecedented presentation of RB1-deleted tumors.3,4

Enucleation is often the treatment of choice for benign intramural esophageal tumors, with a transcervical or transthoracic approach, depending on tumor location, size, and extent.1 Thoracoscopic enucleation has high success rates for thoracic esophageal tumors with low conversion rates and low rates of mucosal perforation.1 Transcervical approaches are preferred for cervical esophageal tumors whenever possible as this spares morbidity of chest violation with the added risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Transtracheal resections of difficult-to-access cervical tumors have also been described.6 Given the anatomic challenge of tumors crossing the thoracic inlet, hybrid transcervical-thoracoscopic techniques can be used for tumors at such a location.7 For giant cervicothoracic lesions that extend into the upper mediastinum, an initial transcervical approach provides benefits of easy identification of the critical structures of the neck at the beginning of dissection. These masses can be mobilized to such an extent that even if the caudal dissection cannot be completed through the neck, a right-sided chest approach can be used to complete resection. We had anticipated this need, but the mass was able to be delivered into the neck to finalize resection. A well-vascularized muscle flap is common practice to reinforce full-thickness esophageal repairs and was employed successfully in this patient with a tunneled pectoralis major flap, given the previous neck irradiation.
