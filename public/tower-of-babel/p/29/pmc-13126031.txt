A qualitative investigation of stakeholders’ perceptions of social prescribing for families with infants through the lens of a creative play on prescription programme for 0-2-year-olds and their carers
Authors: Paige E Davis, Jenny Retzler, Susanna Kola-Palmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClassically social prescription interventions focus on loneliness in adulthood, however another transitional stage of the lifespan associated with loneliness is new parenthood. A first-of-its-kind, socially prescribed, creative play programme is being trialed for families with 0-2-year-olds in a deprived ward in the North of England. Yet little is known about the perspectives of key stakeholders on this type of social prescription. This study examined the experience, attitudes and perspectives stakeholders hold around social prescription for families with infants.Study designIndividual in depth interview study.MethodThis study recruited 12 different stakeholders with practice or policy roles relating to family health and wellbeing, from general practitioners to members of parliament, with different power dynamics and roles to take part in a seven-question semi-structured interview. The interview addressed relationships between stakeholders, how they feel families might value or stigmatize socially prescribed creative play interventions and their thoughts and experiences with social prescription. The interview transcripts were analysed using an inductive descriptive thematic analysis.ResultsThree main themes were identified 1) Normalizing a New Offer: Getting it Right the First Time, 2) Barriers to Socially Prescribed Family Intervention and 3) Social Prescription for Complicated Families with Varying Needs.ConclusionsFindings suggest social prescription for families with infants would be welcome, and may relieve strain on our health systems. However, questions remain around how best to deliver such interventions, demonstrating the importance of evaluation to provide evidence to inform wider commissioning and promote sustainability and scaling.Keywords: Social prescription, Psychological well-being, Play intervention, Stakeholder participation, Family support

Introduction

Social prescription (SP) is a way to link vulnerable individuals to non-medical community-based support [1]. Interest in SP as a public health intervention is widespread in countries around the world [[2], [3], [4]]. There are initiatives forming in the UK to look at SP from a lifespan approach targeting key life transition times [5]. Transition to parenthood is a key period associated with social isolation, chronic loneliness and mental health decline [[6], [7], [8]]. Parental mental health has a profound impact upon their infant's behavioural development and future mental health [9,10]. In response, the first Creative Play on Prescription (CPoP) programme for new parents/carers and their 0-30-month-old children was created by an arts charity in one of the most deprived wards in the UK [11]. It first ran in 2021 for families coming out of Covid-19.

In the UK, SP programmes can be accessed through local agencies (charities, social care, and health service) or self-referral according to National Health Service (NHS) guidelines [12]. Although referral pathways vary, for CPoP healthcare professionals and charities were the main prescribers. Initial evaluation of CPoP found much lower referral rates than expected. Many different healthcare professionals are involved in SP, especially when including families with infants. Such diversity of healthcare professionals may result in inconsistencies in SP referral patterns, due to their differences in interests, power and knowledge of the practice [13]. Research on varied stakeholders’ reflections around SP for families is needed to help these professionals understand each other better and promote reflexive thinking about the service improvements that would increase efficacy and efficiency. This could ultimately lead to increased support and continuity of care, important factors in family SP delivery [14].

Past research on stakeholders' and patients' views of SP, have found healthcare professionals perceive SP positively, but this was not reflected in referral rates [2]. A literature review found patients were also mainly positive about the programmes [1], but identified a need for more joined up approaches to these services. Although studies exist on views of SP for families (e.g. Ref. [15]), to date no studies have examined stakeholders perspectives on SP for families with infants. A scoping review around social prescribing for children and youth found that studies report positive outcomes, but included only studies targeting families of children 5 years old and above [16]. One of the core elements in evaluation and development of a complex intervention for children under 5, like CPoP, is to consider how diverse stakeholder perspectives can be included in research [17]. Understanding perspectives from an array of stakeholders in the family SP for under 5's arena may inform specific modifications or policies that researchers and service providers could implement to increase effectiveness and impact of this type of under researched intervention [16].

Thus, the aim of this study is threefold:

1)To understand how a variety of prescribers and stakeholders view SP family interventions for children under 5 years and their parents.

To understand how a variety of prescribers and stakeholders view SP family interventions for children under 5 years and their parents.

2)To increase understanding of relationships between prescribers and stakeholders around these interventions.

To increase understanding of relationships between prescribers and stakeholders around these interventions.

3)To explore how practitioners may either value or stigmatize SP intervention for children under 5 years and their parents.

The CPoP intervention itself was run for 6-or-12-weeks by [redacted for anonymous review]. The programme featured a professional musician trained in infant and child development, along with a specialist host. Sessions were semi-structured with music in a group setting for the first half hour. The next 15-20 min consisted of a playtime theme relating to the music, and parents were served drinks and snacks by the host. The final 10 min were used as a transitional time to wind down, cuddle, sing lullabies and calm babies. For more in depth descriptions of the intervention please see Refs. [11,18].

Participants were identified two ways; 1) CPoP providing organizations, and 2) through a call for participants distributed via the researcher's networks. The sample included both contacts who had referred into CPoP and professionals who had influence over policy at local and national levels. Interested participants were given a link to an informed consent form and a pre-interview survey. Interviews occurred virtually (4 interviews), or in a quiet area in the charity organisation's café (8 interviews) between March-September 2023 lasting between 11 and 43-min. Interviews were recorded through a Dictaphone and later transcribed and anonymised.

The pre-interview survey consisted of 4 demographic questions and 4 questions about the individual's employment; their title, the setting where they work, the groups of people they interact with, and the time they have been working in the field. Finally, participants were asked for a pseudonym. All questions were asked in order and can be found in Fig. 1. After answering all questions, participants were thanked and given information on how they could receive their transcripts.

Interview questions and prompts.

Data were analysed using thematic analysis. Because of the investigatory and semantic nature of the research, the first author used an inductive, descriptive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's [19,20] six step approach; 1) data familiarisation; 2) creating a coding scheme (initial codes found in Table 2); 3) developing themes from codes to look at aggregated meaning across the data set and cross checking with the two other authors. Sense checking with the CPoP practitioner [18]; 4) reviewing themes in discussion with authors (the initial 9 themes were consolidated into three main themes, two of which had 1 sub-theme); 5) Themes were named (thematic list found in Fig. 2); and 6) the final report was written. Saturation was reached around 12 participants, when themes were no longer being developed.

We considered reflexivity and how it informs researchers position throughout the life of the project. The first and third authors are mothers of two children and have attended programmes catering to children and carers, thus having “insider” knowledge on how the setting feels to carers. Neither has attended a SP creative play programme they were not researching. The second author is a developmental psychologist, and all three take the stance that SP creative play programmes are beneficial to their participants. Results

Although the interviews came from a variety of stakeholders with different roles, unequal power and diverse skill sets, the 12 participants had similar thoughts on SP for families with infants, and SP practice in general. Three main themes were identified, all of which pertained to families and stakeholders themselves; 1) Normalizing a New Offer: Getting it Right the First Time, 2) Barriers to Socially Prescribed Family Intervention and 3) Social Prescription for Complicated Families with Varying Needs.

1)Normalizing a new offer: Getting it right the first time

Normalizing a new offer: Getting it right the first time

This theme encompassed the presentation, normalization and the meaning of new SP programmes for families with infants. All participants unanimously viewed SP initiatives as valuable and needed. SP programmes for families with infants were considered to fill a service gap providing family support and early years development.

Several participants highlighted the importance of positive framing when presenting SP to families. Examples included emphasizing cost-free access or linking SP to holistic wellbeing. Some raised concerns around the medicalized language, “prescription,” suggesting it could be perceived as stigmatising or lead to unrealistic expectations. Others felt that play-based interventions were already culturally normalized and less likely to be misinterpreted.

A recurring theme was the lack of public awareness—SP was described as a “well-kept secret”. Stakeholders stressed the need for clear, accessible communication to foster public awareness, enhance uptake and avoid associations with statutory interventions like social services.

Finally, participants agreed that first impressions matter. The quality of delivery, from promotion to execution, was seen as integral to success and uptake. Extracts in Table 3.

Theme 1: Normalizing a New Offer: Getting it Right the First Time.

This theme encompassed a range of structural issues and sustainability concerns. Participants identified several structural and systemic barriers to implementing SP for families with infants. A leading concern was intervention sustainability, with fears that short-term initiatives could raise expectations only to disappear. It was agreed that financial and organizational infrastructure needed to be in place and specialized for enhanced uptake and acceptability of SP from families with infants. Integration into existing systems was seen as essential to continuity and uptake, yet many professionals lacked familiarity with infant health programmes, often reverting to school-aged children when discussing SP.

Workload and excessive demands – especially in primary care – were considered barriers, limiting capacity to engage with new SP initiatives. Some respondents also highlighted a disconnect in professional roles, with early years care perceived as outside of their remit.

A significant barrier was perceived family mistrust of statutory services, particularly in geographical areas of deprivation. However, SP initiatives like CPoP were seen as opportunities to rebuild trust through positive, non-threatening engagement.

Finally, participants highlighted the need for, and challenge of, demonstrating impact of SP initiatives. While qualitative benefits can be observed, through personal narratives and anecdotes, the lack of quantifiable outcomes was seen to pose a risk to long-term funding. Nonetheless, many agreed that even unmeasurable benefits were still meaningful. See Table 4.

Theme 2: Barriers to socially prescribed family intervention.

The final theme encompassed the complex profile of needs in many families, and respondents emphasised that SP for families must address the multidimensional, intersectional needs of parents/carers and infants. There were no single reasons stakeholders discussed to cause families to be prescribed SP interventions. Context and background thus need to be taken into consideration and thus programmes likely require bespoke, flexible approaches. CPoP, specifically, was seen as a valuable therapeutic tool, especially for families unfamiliar with the positive impacts of play. SP through creative play can support parent-child bonding, model healthy interactions, and provide a non-medicalized solution to support mental health. Many stakeholders spoke of a gap in services for those with low-to-moderate mental health needs, which SP could help address, especially post Covid-19. The Covid-19 pandemic's impact on social isolation was a major concern. Participants viewed SP as a way to build peer social connections, providing much-needed social support for new parents. Covid-19 lockdown impacts may be an exceptional example of vulnerability, however it was something evident in the extracts and social isolation itself has ongoing relevance outside of Covid-19. Discussion

This study explored stakeholder perspectives on SP interventions targeted towards carers and their infants, including CPoP, an innovative creative play intervention. Three interrelated themes were identified: the importance of normalization, structural barriers, and the complexity of family needs.

The first theme brought up the stakeholders’ view that there is value and need for SP programmes in infancy and new parenthood. The promise for interventions like CPoP has been documented in the literature (e.g. Ref. [3]). One potential issue with future promotion and roll out of such programmes, as identified in early evaluations of CPoP, is uptake. Given issues of low uptake are likely to originate from a combination of the lack of family trust in services, as well as the stigma associated with needing help [3,21], normalization of family-oriented SP is needed. Future research into how best to embed family-oriented SP within broader, trusted systems of care should explore which recruitment strategies and contexts work for the families that SP creative play programmes are targeting [17]. Using such understanding to inform service delivery is likely to positively impact uptake and sustainability.

The second theme, barriers to SP intervention, was around funding infrastructure, professionals’ knowledge about infancy, GP workloads, and patient trust in the system. Participants raised concerns around the ability to quantify the benefits of SP, with the current lack of evidence of clinically measurable improvements being viewed as a barrier to continued funding for adoption and scaling. For example, the funding for CPoP comes via different organizations funding single cohorts of participants, resulting in funding instability. To continue to grow and scale SP of CPoP it would need to have coordinated funding through the NHS or local government [22]. Research that offers quantitative analysis of the benefits and costs would provide a much-needed evidence base in favour of such programmes.

The NHS as a healthcare infrastructure was addressed with some participants reporting a disjointed approach to care pathways. This contributes to lack of coordinated care, and disconnect between stakeholders. The observations around lack of integrated care pathways aligns with research findings in studies of politicians, local government and primary care in the NHS as well as a study investigating primary care and other areas of health and social care [23,24]. The disjointedness revealed in this study could have been because of the newness of the offer itself, or the nature of SP being non-medical. Participants also spoke about taking pressure off of GPs who had increasing workloads believing that SP could take pressure of the NHS strained by austerity. This is seen in the increasing demand for policy around brief, scalable intervention. It would be prudent for those interested in setting up SP for families with infants to understand that there may be barriers around finances, time, and lack of coordinated care influencing SP offers.

The final theme focused on complicated families and their needs highlighting Covid-19 as a catalyst for the complexity. Indeed, post Covid-19 isolation has seen an increase in perinatal depression and anxiety worldwide [25]. Stakeholders spoke of how SP creative play programmes may improve mental health through access to peer support networks. This social opportunity has already been reported in research on CPoP18. Connecting parents could be a key element to combatting the loneliness seen in new parenthood [11,18]. It has been found that peer support for parents provides reassurance, validation and normalization [7], Furthermore, past studies have found that social networks are associated with greater support and higher parental self-efficacy [26]. Family-oriented SP programmes should consider including proactive efforts to connect families with children who are similar ages or parents/carers dealing with the same issues (e.g. sleep).

In relation to CPoP specifically, stakeholders considered its bespoke nature to be an important factor in why it is effective. Personalization for each parent and infant would be important for family-oriented SP interventions to consider, and this could potentially improve skills, provide peer support and friendship or encourage confidence depending on the individual's needs [7,26]. Not only are the needs of families complex coming out of Covid-19, but with technology, less free play and leisure time, parents/carers lack understanding of their roles in their children's play behaviours [27]. Stakeholders had a strong belief that parents/carers need to re-learn how to creatively play with their children. Doing so in a safe and comfortable SP environment can help them to improve their own dyadic relationship with their infant as well as be open to other health signposting [28].

This study does not come without limitations. Firstly, several of the stakeholders were unfamiliar with infant health and development and may therefore underestimate the importance of early intervention. Nevertheless, they offered valuable system-level insights, and their perspectives reflect those of stakeholders in such roles, rather than early years specialists. Secondly, our findings may also reflect that some of the participants knew about CPoP, having referred families to it, while others had not. Along this vein, stakeholders had diverse power dynamics both with the researcher and those using SP. For example, although the MPs had power to advocate for these kinds of SP programmes in government, their interviews may have been influenced by their political party. Furthermore, the way that they relate to the researcher as opposed to their constituents needing intervention may be different. This structure could be viewed as a limitation or a strength. Future studies might further investigate power dynamics around SP creative play for participants, stakeholders and researchers.

Overall, the stakeholders emphasised the potential impact of SP for families of infants as a preventative intervention, providing non-medicalized support to families and reducing strain on other healthcare services and providers. Findings inform public health practice and policy in several ways, providing insight into features of programme design that may give them the best chance of succeeding. First, where family-oriented SP programmes are being trialled, healthcare professionals and social prescribers should be supported to ensure the offer is accessible, non-stigmatising and culturally sensitive, thereby promoting uptake, engagement and trust among those being targeted. Future training and communication tools could be developed to facilitate clear communication of SP, especially for families wary of statutory services or unfamiliar with SP. Second, embedding SP of creative play into existing early years and health systems (e.g. family hubs) may be an effective way to facilitate integration, continuity, and scalability of programmes that are being trialed. Finally, we stress the importance of integrating evaluation practices into the introduction of such programmes to develop a stronger evidence base on which future commissioning decisions can be made. Given the complexity of the intervention context and family needs, realist evaluation approaches that can pinpoint what works, for which families, and under what circumstances, may be especially valuable [16,29].
