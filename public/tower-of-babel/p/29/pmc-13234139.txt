Towards a physics informed digital twin to predict cerebral blood flow and cerebral vascular regulation
Authors: Jennifer K. Briggs, J. N. Stroh, Soojin Park, Brandon Foreman, Michael M. Tymko, Jay Carr, Melike Sirlanci, Philip N. Ainslie, Richard K. P. Benninger, Tellen D. Bennett, David J. Albers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebral blood flow is essential for brain function and is governed by cerebral perfusion pressure and physiological mechanisms collectively referred to as cerebral vascular regulation (CVR). Direct measurement of cerebral blood flow and individual CVR mechanisms is challenging and often unavailable clinically, limiting personalization of blood flow and CVR targets, particularly in neurological injury. Physics-informed digital twins enable estimation, tracking, and forecasting of unmeasured physiological states from limited data. Therefore, a digital twin of cerebral hemodynamics that includes CVR mechanisms could help overcome measurement barriers. Here, we introduce CereBRLSIM (Cerebral Blood Regulation Latent State Inference and Modeling), a digital twin that assimilates physiological knowledge and patient data to infer CVR function and predict intracranial hemodynamics. Using in vivo experiments and simulated data, CereBRLSIM predicted cerebral blood flow and estimated myogenic, endothelial, and metabolic CVR mechanism dynamics. When personalized to data from six neurocritical care patients, CereBRLSIM differentiated cerebral hemodynamic pressure-flow phenotypes, predicted outcomes, and forecasted blood flow with higher accuracy than machine learning models. This work provides an interpretable and clinically compatible approach for quantifying CVR function and forecasting cerebral blood flow, potentially enabling precision diagnostics and understanding cerebral hemodynamics.

Introduction

Cerebral blood flow (CBF) is fundamental for supplying nutrients and clearing waste throughout the brain. Despite comprising a small percentage of total body mass, the brain consumes approximately 20% of the body’s oxygen supply. Further, while other organs tolerate 20–40 min without blood flow, the brain sustains irreversible damage in under 5 min of inadequate CBF1,2. CBF-related injuries, such as stroke or traumatic brain injury, occur at epidemic levels3,4. Yet, the complex physiological dynamics governing CBF and strategies for its effective management in neurocritical care remain poorly understood. Deeper understanding is hindered by challenges in measuring CBF and the physiological mechanisms governing it in humans. A promising pathway for overcoming these limitations is the development of mathematical models and digital twins. Mathematical models enable prediction of unmeasured physiological dynamics. Standing alone, mathematical models typically represent average population behavior. Alternatively, these models can be synchronized with real patient data to represent a unique patient’s physiological function, making the model a “digital twin”5,6. Here, we refer to this synchronization process as “model personalization”. Digital twins offer the same benefits as stand-alone mathematical models, while also enabling patient-specific predictions and tracking changes in patient state. Additionally, when the model is personalized by estimating parameters that have physiological meaning, the digital twin may provide diagnostic capabilities7–9. For these reasons, a digital twin of the cerebral vascular system could be transformative for precision medicine in neurocritical care and enable new insights into cerebrovascular physiology10,11.

The cerebral vascular system is highly complex, consisting of a fluid driven by pulsatile pressure gradients through a branching network of compliant tubes whose geometry is heterogeneous across people and actively changes on multiple time scales. Even with continuous measurements of the entire cerebral vasculature, the computational requirements of solving standard fluid dynamics models, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, across the full cerebral vasculature are prohibitive. Alternatively, the problem can be simplified by making assumptions concerning which physical properties dominate fluid flow. The most common simplification is to represent CBF by Poiseuille’s law12–14. This can be justified by assuming flow is primarily driven by cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), created between arterial blood pressure (ABP) and intracranial pressure (ICP), and approximating the vasculature as a single tube with a bulk length and radius (Supplementary Note 2).

However, predicting CBF with Poiseuille’s law is not straightforward because radius and length are bulk approximations of averaged geometry and cannot be directly measured. Given CBF data, a lumped parameter called “cerebral vascular resistance”15, could be estimated to represent the effects of vessel geometry. However, if CBF data are not available, vascular resistance cannot be estimated. Using a constant vascular resistance to predict CBF typically fails16, likely because radius actively changes via cerebral vascular regulation (CVR). Therefore, in the absence of continuous CBF measurements, additional information about radius dynamics is likely essential to model CBF.

CVR is the collective behavior of myogenic, endothelial, and metabolic mechanisms (Fig. 1a)17–19. While the term “cerebral autoregulation” is often used to describe the specific response of these mechanisms to changes in pressure18,20. However, here we use the term “CVR” to avoid ambiguity and explicitly refer to all dynamics associated with these mechanisms. Function of CVR mechanisms is heterogeneous across injury and patient demographics17,18,21 and is correlated with neurocritical care patient outcomes22,23. We hypothesize that a digital twin (called the Cerebral Blood Regulation Latent State Inference and Modeling, CereBRLSIM) that includes the dynamics of CVR mechanisms with variable functionality on bulk radius in Poiseuille’s equation will enable prediction of heterogeneous CBF and CVR dynamics. To test this hypothesis, we first develop and verify the model underlying CereBRLSIM, demonstrating that it reproduces CBF dynamics across a range of controlled in vivo hemodynamic experiments. Second, we embed this model into an inference pipeline that personalizes parameters representing the function of the CVR mechanisms on the basis of observable data. Using simulated data, we show that these CVR-related parameters can be estimated with reasonable accuracy. Third, we personalize the CereBRLSIM to neurocritical care patient data and show that it outperforms other machine learning and deep learning approaches in predicting CBF.

CereBRLSIM enables novel estimation of CBF dynamics and CVR mechanism function, offering new possibilities for cerebral hemodynamics research and the treatment of CBF-related pathologies.

Results

Cerebral blood regulation latent state inference and modeling (CereBRLSIM)

To develop the mathematical model component of Cerebral Blood Regulation Latent State Inference and Modeling (CereBRLSIM), we approximated global CBF (Q) using Poiseuille’s law (eq. (1)). This was done approximating the vasculature as a single tube with a bulk length (L) and radius (r) and assuming CBF if primarily driven by CPP (ΔP) and effects of viscosity (μ) (Supplementary Note 2).1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q\approx \frac{\pi \Delta P{r}^{4}}{8\mu L}.$$\end{document}Q≈πΔPr48μL.

We included effects of myogenic, endothelial, and metabolic mechanisms on bulk radius (r) in eq. (1). The myogenic mechanism is triggered by changes in circumferential tension, the endothelial mechanism is triggered by changes in vascular wall shear stress, and the metabolic mechanism is triggered by changes in arteriole blood gases and metabolic activity (Fig. 1a,b). The full model description and equations are given in the methods and mathematical justification is given in Supplementary Note 2. Briefly, each CVR mechanism exerts a force (ϕmech) on r. This force is modulated by a “CVR parameter” (Cmech), which represents the ‘functionality’ of each mechanism. These CVR parameters will later be estimated with patient data to predict mechanism functionality. The change in radius per unit force by that mechanism is modulated by a length tension function (K(r)), which represents the length tension relationship of smooth muscles13,25.

During a dynamic cerebral autoregulation test, ABP was perturbed by instructing subjects to transition from squatting to standing every 10 s (Fig. 2a, pink). In the flow-mediated dilation tests shear stress was increased by inhaling CO2 to dilate upstream vessels (Fig. 2b, pink) (ref. 31). During the neurovascular coupling test, cerebral activity was increased through an eye-opening and reading task (Fig. 2c, pink).

To verify CereBRLSIM’s ability to reproduce vascular dynamics from these in vivo experiments, we simulated eq. (1) with different combinations of CVR mechanisms being “on” or “off” (by setting CVR parameters = 0 or 1, respectively). Unchanging radius was simulated by setting all CVR parameters = 0 (Fig. 2 “Pressure-Passive”). We adopt the clinical term “pressure-passive,” which typically denotes impaired vascular reactivity32–35, though its clinical use extends beyond the context described here. We then isolated the primary mechanism for each in vivo test by turning off all non-primary mechanisms (CVR parameters set to 0) and turning on the primary mechanism (CVR parameter set to 1) (Fig. 2 “mechanism only”). For example, in the dynamic cerebral autoregulation test, the myogenic mechanism is the primary mechanism. Therefore, Cmyo=1 while Cendo=Cmeta=0. Finally, to test whether CereBRLSIM could reproduce vascular dynamics with all mechanisms active, we turned all mechanisms on by setting Cmyo = Cendo = Cmeta = 1 (Fig. 2 “All Mechanisms”).

For the dynamic cerebral autoregulation test, simulated cerebral blood velocity (CBvsim), was compared to CBv, measured by transcranial doppler. Simulations using only the myogenic mechanism and all mechanisms had lower MSE and higher correlation compared to the pressure passive model (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). In the flow-mediated dilation test, simulated diameter was compared to diameter measured by duplex ultrasound. By definition, pressure passive simulations did not predict a change in vessel diameter (Fig. 2b, left). When simulating only the endothelial mechanism, the predicted change in diameter was much larger than experimentally measured (Fig. 2b, Endothelial Mechanism Only). Simulations using all mechanisms had lower MSE and higher correlation than both the pressure-passive and the endothelial-only model (Fig. 2b, right, Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1). Feasibility of CVR parameter estimation

CereBRLSIM contains three CVR parameters: Cmyo, Cendo, and Cmeta, which correspond to myogenic, endothelial, and metabolic function, respectively. For example, after an increase in CPP and a corresponding rise in CBF, a functional myogenic response (Cmyo=10) leads to a rapid decrease in CBF, while an absent myogenic response (Cmyo=0) shows no restoration of CBF (Fig. 4a). CereBRLSIM was personalized by simulating the model using CPP and ETCO2 data and then using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to estimate the most likely CVR parameter values given CBF data. We conducted simulation experiments to assess estimation certainty and accuracy. The results were similar when variables used to drive the model were held fixed (Supplementary Fig. 8a,b) or a single parameter was estimated while the other two were held fixed at their true values (Supplementary Fig. 8c). These results indicate that when data changes over time and all other parameters are held fixed, the CVR parameters can be estimated with reasonable certainty and accuracy compared to the parameter search space.

CereBRLSIM predicts CBF and CVR function for traumatic brain injury patients

We validated CereBRLSIM on neurocritical care data by simulating the model forward using CPP, PbTO2, and ETCO2 measurements and then estimating the most likely CVR parameter values given CBF measurements (Fig. 5a) from six patient datasets. We assessed if: (1) the personalized CereBRLSIM can predict CBF and (2) CVR parameters can differentiate pressure-flow phenotypes and patient outcomes. These phenotypes, as defined by ref. 16, are: positive global correlation between CBF and CPP; near-zero correlation; and negative correlation (Fig. 5b). Visual inspection shows high concordance between CereBRLSIM-predicted CBF and patient data (Fig. 5c). CereBRLSIM also had lower MSE than the pressure-passive model for all patients (Fig. 5d, CereBRLSIM MSE: 0.31 ± 0.59 vs Pressure Passive MSE: 2.72 ± 4.04), indicating that personalized CVR is necessary to predict CBF in neurocritical care patients. Further, CVR parameters distinguished positive pressure-flow phenotypes from negative or zero pressure-flow phenotypes (Fig. 5e). Patients with positive pressure flow relationships had much lower CVR parameter values (corresponding to dysfunctional CVR) than those with negative or zero.

To assess if the CVR parameter estimates were informative of known outcome metrics, we used the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE), which quantifies patient neurological state six months after discharge. GOSE ranges from 0–8 with 0 indicating deceased and 8 indicating complete neurological recovery. Cmyo and Cendo were not linearly related to GOSE, but Cmeta was linearly correlated with GOSE (Fig. 5f). Therefore, Cmeta was able to predict patient outcomes, particularly very poor outcomes (GOSE=0) (with the caveat of small sample size). Similar results were found when correlating CVR vascular metrics with the Pressure-Reactivity Index (PRx) and Mean Flow Index (Mx), which are time-series based metrics of cerebral autoregulation (Supplementary Fig. 9).

CereBRLSIM forecasts CBF

Finally, we assessed the forecasting ability of CereBRLSIM. CereBRLSIM took 1.1 ± 0.42 s to forecast 1 h of patient data, indicating it can be solved much faster than real time. By construction, CereBRLSIM hypothesizes the change in the bulk radius (Fig. 6c), and the action of each CVR mechanism over time (Fig. 6d). To assess how CereBRLSIM performed compared to traditional machine learning methods, we compared to a neuralODE (a continuously differential neural network that can be simulated forward in time), and a multivariate linear regression model, trained with the same 1 h of data given to CereBRLSIM. Compared to the pressure-passive assumption, neural ODE, and multivariate regression, CereBRLSIM predicted CBF with the lowest MSE at 5, 10, 30, and 60 min into the future (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Table 3). These results indicate that CereBRLSIM can be personalized to neurocritical care patient data to predict CVR function and CBF dynamics with strong fidelity.

Discussion

Adequate CBF is essential for brain function and is tightly regulated by CVR mechanisms. CBF-related injuries, like stroke and traumatic brain injury, impact CVR and CBF, thereby impacting patient outcomes. However, measurements of CVR function and dynamics are not feasible in clinical settings and measurement of CBF is rare38. A digital twin that can quantify CVR function and forecast CBF could be transformative for the study and treatment of CBF-related injuries by allowing for personalized diagnostics, virtual experiments to identify optimal treatments, and real-time monitoring of physiological function that is otherwise difficult to measure. In this study, we developed a digital twin of the cerebral vascular system, Cerebral Blood Regulation Latent State Inference and Modeling (CereBRLSIM), that can be personalized to individual neurocritical care patient data, opening new opportunities in CBF-related research and clinical decision support. In this study, we evaluated two primary components of a digital-twin: the mathematical model underlying it (Figs. 2, 3) and model personalization using retrospective data (Figs. 4–6). Real-time, bidirectional deployment, which can be a defining feature of operational digital twins, remains a goal for future work.

We conducted three evaluation steps to test CereBRLSIM. First, we showed CereBRLSIM closely reproduced dynamics of five in vivo cerebral hemodynamic experiments (Figs. 2 and 3). Next, using simulated data, we showed that CVR parameters, which correspond to mechanism function, could be estimated (Fig. 4). Finally, we personalized CereBRLSIM to six neurocritical care patients and showed that it was able to forecast heterogeneous CBF dynamics with lower MSE than machine learning or deep learning models and that CVR parameters could differentiate patients based on their subsequent outcomes and pressure flow phenotypes (Figs. 5 and 6).

The definition and grouping of mechanisms underlying CVR is variable17,18. Here, we group mechanisms based on time scales and physiological triggers to mathematically resolve their dynamics. Terms “cerebral vascular reactivity”, and “cerebral autoregulation” are also used to describe these mechanisms or their subsets. To avoid ambiguity, we use the term cerebral vascular regulation (CVR) to refer to the collective effects of all three mechanisms. Although the acronym CVR is sometimes used to refer to cerebral vascular resistance, a downstream consequence of vascular reactivity, that meaning is not intended here.

Our results show that modeling individual mechanisms is not usually sufficient to predict dynamics of in vivo experiments (Fig. 2). This aligns with prior studies indicating such experiments may not isolate mechanism dynamics18,19,24,39. CereBRLSIM may help address this gap because it can estimate individual CVR mechanism function (as in Fig 5) and model separable dynamics of each CVR mechanism (Fig 2, Fig 6c,d).

It is well-established that CBF demonstrates non-linear relationships with CO236,40 and CPP41. Our results indicate these non-linear relationships can be explained through the length-tension relationship of smooth muscles and do not require non-linearities or ranges of regulation to be directly encoded in the CVR mechanisms themselves. Future research could test this hypothesis experimentally and assess whether asymmetries in the K(r) curve could explain asymmetric responses to CPP37,42.

Currently, there is no established method to estimate CVR function mechanistically in neurocritical care patients. This makes CereBRLSIM potentially impactful, but also poses challenges validating if estimated CVR parameters correspond to their intended physiological meaning. While we showed that CVR parameters can be estimated, which is important from a mathematical and optimization perspective (Fig. 4), that does not confirm their physiological interpretation. Proper validation of CVR parameters would involve human or animal experiments in which each CVR mechanism is blocked or genetically knocked-out43. This remains a topic of future work. However, three of our findings support the predictive value of CVR parameters. First, by estimating CVR parameters, CereBRLSIM could reproduce CBF dynamics (Fig. 5c) better than the pressure passive model. This suggests that our mathematical representations of CVR reasonably capture CBF dynamics and supports the claim that CVR parameters may reflect underlying mechanism function. Second, CVR parameters were predictive of impaired CVR, as suggested by positive pressure-flow relationships (Fig. 5e). Third, CVR parameters correlate with patient outcome (Fig. 5f). Together, these findings support the interpretation of CVR parameters and suggest their potential use as computational biomarkers for clinical decision support.

Digital twins fit into a broader field of physics-informed machine learning, where models are informed by data but constrained by fundamental principles of physics and physiology. CereBRLSIM highlights the potential benefits of physics-informed machine learning because it offers interpretability and also improved prediction accuracy compared to a more traditional deep learning approach (e.g., neural ODE, Fig. 6). Specifically, healthy cerebral blood flow is ≈ 10 ml/s (50 ml/100 g/min)44, with ischemia occurring around 4 ml/s. Relative to pressure-passive and machine learning approaches, CereBRLSIM achieves lower mean squared error, with prediction differences that are smaller than the separation between healthy and ischemic CBF. This indicates that the improvement in accuracy is physiologically meaningful.

CVR mechanisms are thought to operate on different spatial scales17. Representing the vasculature using a single bulk vessel (Supplementary Note 2), limits our ability to resolve these scales. This choice was necessary to resolve CVR parameters given data sparsity. Future work could incorporate spatially resolved data (e.g., MRI) into a spatially extended version of CereBRLSIM to examine regional CVR and CBF dynamics.

CVR function likely changes over time, especially in neurocritical care settings. In our experiments, we assume stationary CVR parameters to estimate and forecast CereBRLSIM. This assumption appears justified because of the strong predictive performance. However, future work could use filtering methods to update CVR parameters dynamically, enabling real-time tracking of patient state, which may offer valuable clinical insight. Similarly, the initial baseline values of inputs such as radius and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{ETCO}_{{2}\,\mathrm{opt}}$$\end{document}ETCO2opt were fixed because the patient’s vascular state at model initiation is unknown. Future work could incorporate approaches to estimate or personalize these baseline values dynamically.

In non-pathologic conditions, smooth muscles are typically able to maintain vessel radius on timescales longer than tens of seconds, making the impact of vascular compliance negligible on longer timescales13. Therefore, in this study, we addressed the impact of vascular compliance on short time scales by applying a smoothing average (Supplementary Note 2). However, if isometric contraction is impaired, which may occur with CVR impairment, the radius will passively change with pressure over all time scales. Although CereBRLSIM does not explicitly model this passive relationship, we account for its effects by estimating α, the parameter controlling pressure-flow scaling (Supplementary Note 2). Patients with impaired isometric contraction should have larger α values. Indeed, patients with positive pressure-flow relationships, indicating no CVR and potentially impaired isometric contraction, exhibited higher α values than those with negative or zero pressure-flow relationships (Supplementary Table 2). Thus, while the simulated radius loses physiological interpretability when isometric contraction is impaired, the model remains useful for predicting CBF because α implicitly, albeit crudely, captures the impact of vessel compliance on CBF. Future work should incorporate heterogeneous effects of compliance on r.

CVR mechanisms rely on physiological triggers that are not directly measured (e.g., brain metabolic activity). CereBRLSIM uses available multimodal data to approximate these triggers (see methods). Future work should explore whether these triggers can be approximated using alternative or improved data sources, or estimated through machine learning approaches.

We estimated CVR parameters with uncertainty quantification using Markov Chain Monte Carlo estimation (MCMC). Given that the model was run in finite time, the estimated posterior distributions of the CVR computational biomarkers are not exact. To minimize this problem, we simulated multiple chains and methodically chose chains that converged to similar posterior distributions. Still, for some patients, some simulations run from the same posterior displayed different dynamics than the optimal solution. We do not believe this impacts the findings except that the uncertainty in posterior distributions may be over-estimated. Future work should explore other MCMC methods to improve convergence and reduce uncertainty in posterior distributions.

To increase the interpretability of the CVR parameters, we restrict MCMC estimation to the CVR-related parameters and keep all remaining parameters fixed (e.g., τ, Qopt, ropt). Simulated data experiments (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 8), showed CVR parameter estimates were reasonably accurate when patient input data changed over time. However, this approach does not address identifiability of the full parameter set, nor does it quantify the uncertainty introduced by fixing the non-estimated parameters, or further uncertainty introduced throughout an operational pipeline. A full assessment of structural and practical identifiability across a broader range of patient data and parameters (e.g., ref. 45), as well as assessments of operational constraints, decision needs, and propagated uncertainty represents important areas for future work.

As this work represents an initial study, our dataset was limited to six neurocritical care patients. Statements regarding predictive accuracy should be interpreted in the context of this cohort and available data, rather than as population-level performance guarantees, which should be a topic for future investigations. Similar caution applies to observed correlations between CVR parameters and GOSE, PRx, and Mx: the small sample size and noise in these measures preclude definitive conclusions, and these results should be considered exploratory.

We compare CereBRLSIM with multivariate linear regression and a neural ODE, with all approaches trained on the same 1-h segment of patient data. To ensure consistency, each method was applied using standard configurations without extensive tuning. The goal of these comparisons is to provide baseline reference points that highlight the inherent difficulty of cerebral blood flow estimation and enable relative performance assessment under comparable conditions. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as comparative baselines rather than definitive assessments alternative approaches.

In this study, we developed and validated a physics-informed digital twin of cerebral hemodynamics that incorporates three mechanisms underlying cerebral vascular regulation. CereBRLSIM can be personalized to patient data by estimating parameters governing CVR mechanism function and was shown to predict cerebral blood flow in in vivo controlled experiments, infer CVR mechanism function and forecast blood flow in neurocritical care patients. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of creating a digital twin that integrates physiological knowledge with patient data to infer latent cerebrovascular states and supports individualized characterization of cerebral hemodynamics under measurement constraints.

Metabolic mechanism response

The metabolic mechanism ensures that blood flow is adequate to meet metabolic demand and clear waste. Physiological processes underlying the metabolic mechanism are abundant and incompletely understood. Neurovascular coupling (NVC) and reactivity to blood gases, including partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) and partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), and are thought to be the primary metabolic related-contributors to CVR17. NVC is often considered an independent mechanism. However, because NVC acts on a similar timescale and has similar emergent dynamics as CO2 reactivity (e.g., increases in metabolism cause vasodilation), it is difficult to distinguish the effects without direct experimentation and is therefore modeled in the same mechanism to avoid model estimability issues. Therefore, the metabolic mechanism is modeled as the sum of three components: vasoreactivity to partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), vasoreactivity to partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), and neurovascular coupling (NVC)17.

Vasoreactivity to carbon dioxide

The emergent relationships between CBF and carbon dioxide are nonlinear36. We hypothesize (Fig. 3) that this nonlinearity can be explained by the length-tension function (K(r)) rather than a non-linear metabolic response to CO2 levels. Therefore, we assume fCO2 is linearly related to partial pressure of carbon dioxide17.

Vasoreactivity to oxygen

The cerebral vasculature is only reactive to oxygen cases of severe hypoxemia17,48–50. Therefore, we model fO2 as sigmoid dependent on PO2 with some ischemic threshold γ.

Dynamic cerebral autoregulation (dCA) squat-to-stand test

Squat to stand protocol was adapted from ref. 56. After 10 min of standing to ensure baseline cardiovascular state, subjects were instructed to alternate between a squatting and a standing position, each held for 10 s. Beat-to-beat blood pressure was measured by finger photoplethysmography (Finometer Pro, Finapres Medical Systems, Amsterdam, Netherlands) and CBv was measured at the right MCA using 2-MHz transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD, Spencer Technologies, Seattle, WA).

Flow-mediated dilation test

Flow-mediation dilation experiments were originally collected in Carr et al.31, with methodology adopted from ref. 28. Baseline end-tidal gas concentrations for each subject were established. Participants breathed through an end-tidal forcing system, which provided O2 and CO2 in proportions appropriate to maintain baseline concentrations. ETCO2 was then elevated 9 mmHg above baseline for 30 seconds to invoke a rapid increase in CBv and shear stress. Neurovascular coupling test

Neurovascular coupling test protocol was adapted from refs. 30,57. After 2-min of resting with eyes closed to ensure baseline vascular and neurologic state, subjects were instructed to open their eyes and read for 30 s. ABP was measured with finger photoplethysmography and CBv was measured using 2-MHz TCD at the left posterior cerebral artery (PCA), which has the greatest response in CBv to visual stimulation30,58. Nonlinear relationships between CBF, ETCO2, and CPP

To validate that CereBRLSIM could describe the non-linear relationships between change in CBF, ETCO2, and CPP, in silico experiments were simulated to mimic in vivo experiments from Claasen et al.36 and Klein et al.37. Traumatic brain injury patient data

Six two hour patient time series were extracted from the multimodal data set of Traumatic Brain Injury neurocritical care patients at the University of Cincinnati (Supplementary Table 2). The data utilized in this manuscript include ABP, ICP, PbTO2, End Tidal CO2, and regional CBF observed by a Bowman probe (https://hemedex.com/products/bowman-perfusion-monitoring-system/).

Time-series metrics

Mean Flow Index (Mx) and Pressure Reactivity Index (PRx) were calculated according to standard protocol59. To calculate Mx, CPP (ABP-ICP) and CBF were averaged over ten second non-overlapping windows. Correlation coefficients between 5 min of averaged samples were calculated using a moving window with 4/5 overlap. These correlation coefficients were then averaged over the one hour dataset. PRx was calculated with the same methodology but correlated ABP and ICP. Pressure Flow Relationships were assessed similar to16. First, the linear regression between smoothed CPP and CBF was calculated over one hour of patient data. Time series were labeled positive PFR if the slope was > 0.2, zero PFR if slope < 0.2 and ≥ -0.1. Negative PFR patients had a slope < 0.1.

CVR parameter estimation

CBF forecasting

After CereBRLSIM was estimated for 1 h of patient data, 100 samples were randomly selected from the joint posterior distribution of the CVR parameters. These samples were used to initialize CereBRLSIM, which was then simulated forward for 1 h.

NeuralODE

To compare CereBRLSIM performance against a deep learning approach, neuralODE models were used. NeuralODEs68 are the most comparable deep-learning approach to CereBRLSIM because they are continuously differentiable and can be simulated forward in time. A feedforward neural network with two fully connected layers with 20 neurons each was trained using the Adam optimizer with 200 mini-batches, each consisting of 200 seconds worth of data. Increased mini-batch size did not impact results. The model was trained for 1200 iterations. Analogous to CereBRLSIM, an individual neuralODE model was trained using the first hour of patient CBF measurements, approximately 4000 data points/patient and then simulated forward for the next hour.

Multivariate linear regression

To assess how CereBRLSIM compared to a mutlivariate linear regression that leveraged the same datasets, a patient specific multivariate linear regression was trained on the first hour of patient data using inputs of ETCO2, CPP, and PbTO2 with predictor CBF as the predictor variable. The patient specific multivariate linear regression model was then used to predict CBF for the next hour.
