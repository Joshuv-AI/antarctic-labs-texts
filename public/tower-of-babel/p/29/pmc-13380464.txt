Effects of early-life Enterococcus faecium spray application and dietary Bacillus-based probiotic supplementation on gut integrity and immune responses in broilers challenged with BCO lameness
Authors: Khawla Alharbi, Andi Asnayanti, Woro Wulandari, Antoine Meuter, Adnan Alrubaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotics have gained attention as alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters in broiler production due to their ability to support gut health, immune function, and disease resistance. Bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO) is a major cause of lameness in broiler chickens and is commonly associated with bacterial translocation from the gastrointestinal tract. Strategies that improve intestinal integrity and host responses may help mitigate this condition. This study evaluated the efficacy of a probiotic program comprising an Enterococcus faecium-based spray (2 × 10⁹ CFU/bird at hatch) and a triple-strain Bacillus-based feed additive (B. subtilis 597, B. subtilis 600, and B. amyloliquefaciens 516 at 500 g/t feed from day 1 to 56), applied alone or in combination. A wire-flooring challenge model was used to simulate BCO transmission. A total of 1,560 Cobb 500 broilers were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: NC (negative control, 6 replicate pens), GalliPro® Hatch (E. faecium spray only, 6 replicate pens), GalliPro® Fit (Bacillus-based feed supplement only, 6 replicate pens), and Hatch/Fit (combined treatment, 6 replicate pens). Two additional wire-floor seeder pens served as the infection source for the BCO challenge. Tight junction gene expression (CLDN1, CLDN5, OCLN, ZO-1, and ZO-2) was assessed in the ileum and trachea at d 7, 28, and 56, along with serum IgM, IgA, and serotonin concentrations. Probiotic supplementation significantly influenced ileal gene expression, with significant effects of treatment, age, and treatment × age interaction for selected tight junction genes (P < 0.05). OCLN and ZO-1 were upregulated at d 7 (P< 0.001), followed by sustained increases at d 56, particularly in the Hatch/Fit group (p < 0.05). Tracheal responses were variable. Plasma IgA concentrations increased with age (P < 0.001), reflecting normal immune maturation. Notably, serum serotonin concentrations were markedly elevated in the Hatch/Fit group, indicating microbiota-driven modulation of the gut–brain axis. Bacterial isolation identified a polymicrobial profile dominated by Staphylococcus spp. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that combined probiotic supplementation modulated tight junction gene expression and serum serotonin concentrations in BCO-challenged broilers. These results support evaluation of combined probiotic strategies in commercial production systems.Keywords: Broiler chickens, Chondronecrosis, Lameness, Gut integrity, Probiotics

Introduction

The poultry industry plays a critical role in meeting the growing global demand for affordable, high-quality animal protein and is one of the most efficient and rapidly expanding sectors contributing to global food security (Kleyn and Ciacciariello, 2021; Birhanu et al., 2023). However, intensive production systems have also been associated with increased susceptibility to metabolic, infectious, and skeletal disorders (Julian, 2005; Wideman, 2016). In parallel, restrictions on antibiotic growth promoters have accelerated interest in alternative strategies that sustain productivity while improving bird health and welfare (Dibner and Richards, 2005; Gadde et al., 2017; Alagawany et al., 2018). Among these alternatives, probiotics have received substantial attention due to their ability to support intestinal health, host defense, and production performance (Gadde et al., 2017; Alagawany et al., 2018; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020).

Gut health is recognized as a central determinant of broiler performance, welfare, and disease resistance (Markowiak and Śliżewska, 2018; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Beyond digestion and nutrient absorption, the intestinal tract serves as an essential physical and immunological barrier against pathogens (Awad et al., 2017). When barrier integrity is compromised, intestinal permeability increases, allowing bacteria and their products to translocate into systemic circulation and contribute to extraintestinal diseases (Awad et al., 2017; Yosi and Metzler-Zebeli, 2023).One important consequence of disrupted gut integrity in broilers is bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO), a major cause of lameness and economic loss in commercial poultry production (Julian, 2005; Wideman and Prisby, 2013; Wideman, 2016). This condition is generally associated with bacterial entry into the bloodstream, followed by colonization of structurally vulnerable sites in rapidly growing bones (Wideman and Prisby, 2013; Al-Rubaye et al., 2015). Mechanical stress, microfractures, vascular limitations, and microbial challenge all contribute to lesion development (Thorp, 1994; Wideman, 2016). Accordingly, BCO is both a serious welfare issue and a useful model for understanding how gut-associated bacterial translocation may contribute to systemic skeletal disease (Wideman and Prisby, 2013; Wideman, 2016).

In addition to barrier function, the gastrointestinal tract plays a key role in regulating immune activity (Kabir, 2009; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). The gut-associated lymphoid tissue coordinates interactions between luminal microbes and host immune cells, shaping cytokine production and immune homeostasis (Kabir, 2009; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Probiotics have been reported to influence both innate and adaptive immune responses, including modulation of inflammatory mediators and enhancement of mucosal defense (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Yosi and Metzler-Zebeli, 2023). The intestine is also a major site of serotonin production, and microbial signals can influence host serotonergic pathways (Yano et al., 2015). Because serotonin is involved in intestinal physiology, immune signaling, and bone biology, the gut–immune–bone axis represents an important framework for investigating how probiotic interventions may affect both local and systemic outcomes in broilers (Yano et al., 2015).

Among the probiotic organisms used in poultry, Enterococcus faecium and Bacillus spp. are of particular interest (Alagawany et al., 2018; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). E. faecium supplementation has been associated with improved intestinal morphology, better microbial balance, and enhanced mucosal immune function in broiler chickens (Cao et al., 2013; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). More broadly, probiotic supplementation has been linked to favorable changes in gut microbiota composition and host physiology in poultry (Kabir, 2009; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Bacillus-based products are also attractive because their spores are stable during feed processing and storage, and these organisms can contribute digestive enzymes, antimicrobial metabolites, and competitive effects within the intestinal environment (Alagawany et al., 2018; Grant et al., 2018). Meta-analytic evidence further indicates that dietary probiotics can modulate gut barrier-related and immune-related gene expression, as well as intestinal histomorphology, in broilers (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Yosi and Metzler-Zebeli, 2023).

Early-life microbial colonization is especially important because the post-hatch period represents a critical window for intestinal and immune development (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Probiotic delivery at hatch therefore offers a practical strategy to influence initial microbial establishment (Grant et al., 2018; Do et al., 2024). In a recent study, spraying day-old chicks with an E. faecium-based probiotic at hatch reduced the incidence of BCO lameness under challenge conditions, supporting the idea that early microbial intervention can influence downstream skeletal health (Grant et al., 2018; Do et al., 2024). Continuous dietary supplementation may complement this early effect by maintaining microbial stability and supporting intestinal homeostasis throughout the production cycle (Alagawany et al., 2018; Grant et al., 2018; Markowiak and Śliżewska, 2018).

Recent evidence suggests that combining early-life and dietary probiotic interventions may yield stronger benefits than using either approach alone (Alagawany et al., 2018; Alharbi et al., 2025). Specifically, a probiotic program combining an E. faecium hatch spray with a triple-strain Bacillus-based feed additive significantly reduced BCO-associated lameness, with the combined treatment outperforming the individual interventions (Alharbi et al., 2025). These findings support the possibility of combined effects mediated through improved gut integrity, altered host–microbe interactions, and reduced bacterial translocation (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Yosi and Metzler-Zebeli, 2023; Alharbi et al., 2025). However, despite this promising evidence, the broader effects of such a combined strategy on gut barrier function, immune response, and serotonergic activity remain insufficiently defined.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the combined effects of early-life Enterococcus faecium application and continuous dietary Bacillus-based supplementation on intestinal barrier integrity, immune responses, and BCO development in broiler chickens. We hypothesized that this combined probiotic strategy would enhance gut barrier function, modulate host immune pathways, and consequently reduce bacterial translocation, leading to a lower incidence of BCO-associated lameness compared.

Materials and methods

Lameness incidence data from this experimental trial have been previously reported (Alharbi et al., 2025) and are included here to provide biological context for the interpretation of gut integrity, immune responses, and related physiological outcomes.

Ethical approval

All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the guidelines of the University of Arkansas Agricultural Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Ag-IACUC; protocol #23067).

Housing conditions and bird allocation

The study was conducted over a 56-day period using an established aerosol transmission model for bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO) induction (Asnayanti et al., 2024). A total of 1,560 day-old Cobb 500 broiler chicks (Cobb-Vantress Inc., Siloam Springs, AR, USA) were randomly distributed at placement into 26 floor pens (1.5 m × 3.0 m per pen), corresponding to an initial stocking density of approximately 750 cm² per bird.

At d 14, flock size per pen was standardized to 50 birds to maintain a final stocking density of approximately 900 cm² per bird. At d 14, flock size per pen was standardized to 50 birds to maintain a final stocking density of approximately 900 cm² per bird. Birds selected for removal were those that were visibly smaller, unhealthy, or exhibited signs of poor condition to improve flock uniformity across pens. Pens were arranged in two rows of 13 pens and assigned to treatments using a randomized block design. Two wire-floor seeder pens were included to initiate and maintain the BCO challenge and served solely as the infection source. The remaining 24 floor pens were assigned to the experimental treatment groups, with six replicate pens per treatment, resulting in approximately 360 birds per treatment. Data from the wire-floor seeder pens were not included in the statistical analyses. Environmental parameters, including temperature, lighting, and ventilation, were controlled using automated systems. Birds were maintained under a 23 h light:1 h dark photoperiod (20 lux) throughout the experiment. Ambient temperature was gradually reduced from 32.2°C during the first three days to 23°C by d 15 and maintained thereafter. Each pen was equipped with two feeders and an independent nipple drinker line, which was disinfected prior to chick placement.

Probiotic intervention strategy

The probiotic treatments applied in this study have been described previously (Alharbi et al., 2025). Briefly, birds were exposed to either early-life microbial intervention, continuous dietary supplementation, or a combination of both approaches. The early-life treatment consisted of an Enterococcus faecium-based probiotic (GalliPro® Hatch; Novonesis, Hørsholm, Denmark), administered as a single spray at hatch. The solution was prepared following manufacturer recommendations to deliver approximately 2 × 10⁹ CFU per bird. Application was performed using a manual spraying system, with a food-grade dye included to verify uniform coverage. Continuous supplementation involved a multi-strain Bacillus-based probiotic (GalliPro® Fit; Novonesis, Hørsholm, Denmark), incorporated into feed at 500 g/t and provided throughout the entire 56-day production cycle. Diets were formulated and manufactured at the University of Arkansas Poultry Feed Mill and supplied ad libitum during starter (d 0–18), grower (d 18–42), and finisher (d 42–56) phases. The experimental diets were formulated as previously described (Alharbi et al., 2025). Detailed ingredient composition and nutrient profiles are presented in that study. Clinical evaluation of lameness

From d 22 onward, birds were evaluated daily for lameness by gently encouraging movement within each pen. Individuals exhibiting impaired mobility, abnormal gait, or reluctance to stand were classified as clinically lame. These birds were humanely euthanized using CO₂ inhalation and subjected to post-mortem examination. Femoral and tibial lesions were assessed and scored according to previously established criteria (Alharbi et al., 2025).

Tight junction gene expression in the ileum and trachea

Ileal and tracheal tissues were collected from six birds per treatment group at days 7, 28, and 56. At each sampling time point, one clinically healthy bird was selected from each of the six replicate pens, resulting in six biological replicates per treatment. Immediately after euthanasia, approximately 2–3 cm segments of the distal ileum and trachea were excised, rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove luminal contents, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Each biological replicate represented an individual bird (n = 6 per treatment per time point).

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Cat. No. 74134 or 74136; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. No. 1708891; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green One-Step Kit (Cat. No. 1725150 or 1725151; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene expression levels of tight junction markers, including claudin-1 (CLDN1), claudin-5 (CLDN5), occludin (OCLN), zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), and zona occludens-2 (ZO-2), were evaluated. The housekeeping gene β-actin was used as an internal control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in duplicate, and results were expressed as fold change relative to the control group at the corresponding time point. Plasma immunoglobulin concentrations

Blood samples were collected from ten birds per treatment group for the determination of circulating immunoglobulin levels. Samples were obtained via cardiac puncture immediately postmortem using a 3 mL syringe and transferred into pre-heparinized tubes. Blood plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min and stored at −20°C until analysis.

Plasma samples were diluted according to the manufacturer’s recommendations prior to ELISA analysis. Concentrations of immunoglobulins (IgA, IgM, and IgY) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Chicken IgA ELISA Kit, Cat. No. ab157691; Chicken IgM ELISA Kit, Cat. No. ab157692; Chicken IgY ELISA Kit, Cat. No. ab157693; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured using a Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA), and concentrations were calculated based on standard curves. All samples were analyzed in duplicate.

Serotonin analysis

On d 56, blood samples were collected from two birds per pen across the treatment groups (Control, Hatch, Fit, and Hatch/Fit) for the determination of serum serotonin levels. Samples were obtained via cardiac puncture immediately post mortem using sterile syringes and transferred into serum collection tubes. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min to separate serum. The collected serum was aliquoted and stored at −20°C until analysis. Each biological replicate represented an individual bird. Serotonin concentrations were quantified by a certified commercial laboratory (Whitebeck Labs, Springdale, AR, USA) using validated analytical procedures according to their standard protocols.

Bacterial sequencing

At d 56, six clinically lame birds per treatment group were selected from the replicate pens for post-mortem examination under aseptic conditions. Blood, tibial lesion, and femoral lesion samples were collected from clinically lame birds to isolate bacterial populations associated with BCO. The procedures used were adapted from previously established methodologies (Alharbi et al., 2024a, 2024b; Anthney et al., 2025). Tissue samples were cultured on three selective and non-selective media, including Tryptic Soy Agar (Difco Laboratories, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), CHROMagar™ Orientation, and CHROMagar™ Staphylococcus (CHROMagar, Paris, France). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24–48 h. Following incubation, colonies were differentiated based on morphology and color, enumerated, and subsequently subcultured to obtain pure isolates. Genomic DNA was extracted from purified colonies using the DNeasy PowerLyzer Microbial Kit (Cat. No. 12855-100, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was stored at 4°C until further molecular analysis. The V1–V5 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using conventional PCR. Reaction mixtures and cycling conditions are detailed in Table 3, Table 4. Amplification products were confirmed by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels using 0.5 × TBE buffer at 60–70 V. Purified PCR products were submitted to Eurofins Genomics (Louisville, KY, USA) for sequencing. Sequence data were analyzed using ApE Plasmid Editor software (version 3.0.6; M. Wayne Davis, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) and compared against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using the BLAST algorithm. Bacterial species were assigned based on sequence similarity thresholds of ≥98%.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using “GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Tight junction gene expression and plasma immunoglobulin concentrations were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of treatment, age, and their interaction. When significant effects were detected, Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare treatment groups with the control. Serum serotonin concentrations were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Data distribution and variance were assessed prior to analysis. Where minor deviations from assumptions were observed, ANOVA was considered robust due to the balanced experimental design and comparable sample sizes across treatment groups.

Overall, significant treatment-related differences in tracheal tight junction gene expression were observed primarily for OCLN, ZO-1, and ZO-2, whereas fewer differences were detected for CLDN1 and CLDN5.

For IgM, concentrations remained relatively stable among treatment groups at both d 28 and d 56 (Fig. 3A). Although numerical differences were observed among treatments, none of the comparisons were statistically significant. Two-way ANOVA confirmed the absence of significant effects of treatment, age, or treatment × age interaction (P > 0.05), indicating that probiotic supplementation did not alter plasma IgM concentrations during the study period.

In contrast, plasma IgA concentrations increased with age across all treatment groups (Fig. 3B). However, no significant differences among treatments were observed at either sampling time, and no treatment or treatment × age interaction effects were detected (P > 0.05).

Serum serotonin concentrations at d 56 are presented in Fig. 4 and Table 13. The negative control group exhibited a mean serotonin level of 1029.00 ± 40.75 ng/mL. Bacterial identification

The distribution of bacterial isolates recovered from blood, femoral, and tibial samples of lame birds is presented in Fig. 5. A total of 37 isolates were identified, with Staphylococcus aureus representing the most predominant species (24%), followed by Staphylococcus cohnii (21%). Other frequently detected species included Bacillus cereus (8%) and Escherichia coli (11%), while Staphylococcus haemolyticus, Micrococcus luteus, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus each accounted for approximately 3–5% of the total isolates. Less prevalent species included Staphylococcus lentus, Staphylococcus simulans, Staphylococcus kloosii, Brevibacterium sp., Enterococcus faecalis, and Enterococcus gallinarum, each contributing a small proportion (≤5%). Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the combined effects of early-life Enterococcus faecium application (GalliPro® Hatch) and continuous dietary Bacillus-based supplementation (GalliPro® Fit) on epithelial integrity, immune responses, and serotonergic activity in broilers under a BCO challenge model. The integration of intestinal, respiratory, immunological, and neuroendocrine responses demonstrates a coordinated host response with important implications for mitigating BCO-associated lameness. Previous work from the same experimental trial demonstrated that probiotic-treated groups exhibited reduced cumulative lameness incidence compared with the control group, with the combined Hatch/Fit treatment showing the greatest reduction (Alharbi et al., 2025). Although lameness outcomes were reported previously, these findings provide important biological context for the present study and suggest that improvements in gut barrier function and host physiology may contribute to the enhanced resilience observed in probiotic-treated birds.

The most consistent responses were observed in the ileum, where tight junction genes (CLDN1, CLDN5, OCLN, ZO-1, and ZO-2) were significantly upregulated. This finding reinforces that probiotics primarily exert their effects at the gastrointestinal level, which is widely recognized as a central site in BCO pathogenesis (Wideman and Prisby, 2013; Wideman, 2016). The observed upregulation of tight junction genes indicates improved epithelial barrier integrity, which likely limits the translocation of opportunistic pathogens into systemic circulation, a critical step in the development of BCO.

These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that probiotics enhance tight junction integrity through increased expression of claudins, occludin, and ZO proteins (Awad et al., 2017; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020). Mechanistically, these effects are linked to microbial metabolite production, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which regulate epithelial signaling pathways such as AMPK activation and histone deacetylase inhibition (Peng et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2012). Probiotics also modulate inflammatory signaling, helping to reduce epithelial damage and maintain barrier function (Kogut, 2019). A key outcome of this study is the enhanced and sustained effect observed in the combined Hatch/Fit treatment. This supports the concept of early-life microbial programming, in which initial colonization influences long-term gut function and immune competence (Oakley et al., 2014; Jha et al., 2019). Early probiotic exposure likely establishes a beneficial microbial community. When reinforced through continuous dietary supplementation, this leads to improved resilience against pathogenic challenges.

Importantly, these results are consistent with recent challenge-model studies demonstrating that early-life interventions can reduce BCO incidence and improve skeletal health outcomes (Do et al., 2024; Alharbi et al., 2025). The superior performance of the combined Hatch/Fit strategy suggests a combined interaction between early microbial exposure and sustained dietary supplementation. Together, these findings highlight the importance of early microbial colonization and maintaining gut integrity as key factors in controlling BCO.

In contrast to the ileum, tracheal gene expression responses were more variable and, in some cases, showed reductions in specific tight junction markers. This divergence highlights the tissue-specific nature of probiotic action. Unlike the gastrointestinal tract, the respiratory epithelium is not directly exposed to dietary microbes, and probiotic effects in the trachea are likely mediated indirectly through systemic signaling pathways, immune modulation, or circulating microbial metabolites (Macpherson et al., 2008; Jha et al., 2019; Abd El-Hack et al., 2020; Anthney et al., 2025). Although these responses provide insight into systemic regulation, they are unlikely to represent the primary mechanism underlying BCO mitigation.

Despite the pronounced modulation of epithelial gene expression, systemic immune responses (IgM and IgA) were not significantly influenced by treatment, although IgA increased with age. This suggests that probiotic supplementation primarily enhances local mucosal defenses rather than systemic humoral immunity, particularly under non-acute challenge conditions. This observation is consistent with previous reports indicating that probiotics exert immunomodulatory effects mainly at the mucosal level without markedly altering circulating immunoglobulin levels unless birds are exposed to strong pathogenic stressors (Bar-Shira and Friedman, 2006; Mountzouris et al., 2007; Al-Khalaifah, 2018). The age-related increase in IgA likely reflects normal maturation of the mucosal immune system, which plays a critical role in maintaining microbial homeostasis and preventing pathogen adhesion (Gershon and Tack, 2007; Al-Khalaifah, 2018).

A particularly novel finding of this study is the significant elevation of serum serotonin levels in the combined Hatch/Fit group. This provides strong evidence for the involvement of the gut–brain axis in probiotic-mediated effects in poultry. Serotonin (5-HT), primarily synthesized in the gastrointestinal tract, plays an essential role in regulating gut motility, immune responses, and stress physiology (Gershon and Tack, 2007; Grondin and Khan, 2024). Increasing evidence indicates that gut microbiota can influence serotonin production through tryptophan metabolism and SCFA-mediated pathways (Bar-Shira and Friedman, 2006; Gershon and Tack, 2007; Yano et al., 2015). The elevated serotonin levels observed in the combined treatment group suggest that early microbial exposure, combined with continuous supplementation, enhances microbiota-driven neuroendocrine signaling. This finding has important implications for BCO, as serotonin has been associated with behavioral regulation, stress reduction, and locomotor activity in poultry (Koopman et al., 2021; Grondin and Khan, 2024). Reduced stress levels may indirectly decrease susceptibility to infection, as stress is known to impair immune function and compromise host defenses (Gershon and Tack, 2007; Koopman et al., 2021; Grondin and Khan, 2024). In addition, serotonin has been implicated in bone metabolism, influencing osteoblast and osteoclast activity (Lavoie et al., 2017; Franco et al., 2020), suggesting a potential role in maintaining skeletal integrity.

The bacterial isolation results further support the mechanistic link between gut integrity and BCO pathogenesis. The diverse range of bacterial species recovered from blood and bone tissues confirms the polymicrobial nature of BCO and highlights the role of systemic bacterial dissemination. The predominance of Staphylococcus spp., particularly S. aureus and S. cohnii, is consistent with previous studies identifying these organisms as key contributors to osteomyelitis lesions in poultry (Alharbi et al., 2024a,b; Anthney et al., 2025). The detection of additional bacterial genera, including Escherichia coli and Enterococcus spp., further supports the involvement of opportunistic pathogens originating from the gastrointestinal tract. Importantly, the identification of BCO-associated bacteria in birds without clinical signs of lameness provides additional evidence for the subclinical progression of the disease. This observation suggests that bacterial colonization of skeletal tissues may occur prior to the onset of visible symptoms, highlighting the early and insidious nature of BCO development (Alharbi et al., 2024a,b).

Collectively, these findings support a multi-level mechanism in which probiotics primarily act through enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity, thereby limiting bacterial translocation, while also modulating systemic physiological and neuroendocrine responses. This integrated response highlights a functional link between gut health and skeletal outcomes in broilers and underscores the importance of early microbial programming combined with sustained dietary supplementation as an effective strategy to reduce BCO-associated lameness.
