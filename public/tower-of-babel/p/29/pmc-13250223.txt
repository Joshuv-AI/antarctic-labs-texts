Complementary role of humanitarian civil society organisations in bridging gaps in immunisation programming in conflict and crisis-affected settings: the REACH Project experience
Authors: Comfort Z Olorunsaiye, Lara S Ho, Paul K Malalu, Gautam Chatterjee, Peter Mutanda, Shiferaw Dechasa Demissie, Brooke Amara Farrenkopf, Laura Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring immunisation coverage in Fragile and Conflict-Affected Settings (FCAS) remains a critical global health challenge. National Immunisation Programmes (NIPs) are unable to provide programming in some areas because of conflict and insecurity or the destruction of healthcare infrastructure. Humanitarian civil society organisations (CSOs) can fill those programming gaps, but they need to be able to do so promptly and effectively. This paper presents an analysis of gaps experienced by NIPs across the REACH Project, funded by Gavi to immunise ‘zero-dose’ children (children who did not receive any diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis-containing vaccine) in FCAS in Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan and Sudan. We offer insights that can be used by humanitarian and immunisation CSOs that aim to overcome the challenges that NIPs face in these settings in ways that are truly complementary and not substituting for what NIPs could achieve.The NIP contributions were categorised as ‘No NIP Contribution’, ‘Limited NIP: Geographic Limitations’, ‘Limited NIP: Functional Limitations’ and ‘Limited NIP: Transitioning Contexts’ reflecting the nature and extent of NIP contributions. Based on NIP contribution context, humanitarian CSOs filled immunisation programming gaps by working with NIPs in complementary ways to re-establish or extend immunisation services and by using conflict-sensitive approaches to support immunisation programming. Different conflict-sensitive programmatic adaptations were critical based on the needs in each context.Despite variable NIP contribution levels, collaboration between NIPs and humanitarian CSOs was vital to the success of REACH. The REACH Project demonstrates how to effectively adapt immunisation programming within a context-specific hybrid NIP-humanitarian CSO model in FCAS.Keywords: Immunisation, Coverage, Humanitarian settings, Conflict-affected regions, Zero-dose children, Expanded Programme on Immunisation

Introduction

Equitable access to life-saving vaccines is a persistent global health challenge in conflict-affected, fragile and humanitarian settings (Fragile and Conflict-Affected Settings, FCAS). Despite decades of progress, millions of children, especially those in humanitarian settings, are missed by routine immunisation programmes, perpetuating health inequities.1 2 In 2022, WHO and UNICEF estimated 14.3 million ‘zero-dose’ children, that is, children who did not receive any diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP)-containing vaccine; the majority live in low- and middle-income countries in Africa and South-East Asia.1 3 Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan and Sudan rank among the highest, with Ethiopia alone accounting for over one million zero-dose children.4 5 Similar to other post-conflict settings, prolonged instability, repeated population displacement, attacks on health services and weakened cold-chain and governance systems in FCAS repeatedly disrupt routine immunisation efforts, producing persistent ‘zero-dose’ cohorts and undermining earlier interventions.6,8 For these reasons, Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan and Sudan were prioritised for introducing the Reaching Every Child in Humanitarian Settings (REACH) Project: each has experienced prolonged conflict and repeated disruptions to immunisation delivery that have produced persistent zero-dose cohorts, and each hosts active humanitarian organisations with which NGO-led, conflict-sensitive approaches can be evaluated for complementarity with National Immunisation Programmes (NIPs).9,12

NIPs are expected to be responsible for immunisation programming; however, they often face complex barriers in humanitarian settings. Armed conflict destroys infrastructure and displaces communities and health workers, disrupting supply chains and routine service delivery.13 14 Geographic isolation and weak health systems further impede outreach to remote, nomadic, cross-border and displaced populations.5 14 Socioeconomic determinants—poverty, low maternal education and gender inequality—further reduce uptake, with children from the poorest households and those of young or unmarried mothers at greatest risk.2 13 15 FCAS account for half of zero-dose children worldwide, underscoring the urgent need for adaptive, context-specific humanitarian-centred immunisation programming.1 13 14

Recent evidence highlights innovative approaches to reach missed communities in FCAS. Mobile and outreach services, community engagement, geospatial mapping and portable cold chain devices have improved vaccine delivery in FCAS.13 14 16 Nevertheless, inconsistent implementation, narrow age-focused and antigen-focused campaigns and persistent access barriers limit impact. Effective partnerships among NIPs, humanitarian civil society organisation (CSOs) and local communities are vital for tailoring services and building trust.8 13 14 Sustainability remains a concern, as many gains are fragile without robust support from NIPs.2 17

In light of persistent sustainability challenges, this paper addresses an important gap in the literature on delivering full routine immunisation in FCAS. There is limited evidence on how humanitarian NGO-led initiatives can complement NIPs to close persistent vaccination gaps in zero-dose communities.18 19 While prior research documents who is missed and reports outreach outcomes, it rarely examines the operational design and coordination mechanisms that make NGO efforts additive to NIPs.20 21 The REACH Project addresses these gaps by applying conflict-sensitive programming to sustainably enhance immunisation coverage in FCAS. Using lessons from the REACH Project work in Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan, and Sudan, this study examines (1) NGO roles in identifying and reaching children in zero-dose communities, (2) coordination practices that prevent parallel and duplicative systems, and (3) Gavi and NIP operational models needed to sustain complementary approaches to routine immunisation services in FCAS.18 19 22 23 This practice paper seeks to inform global efforts to achieve the Immunisation Agenda 2030 goal of leaving no one behind in humanitarian and conflict settings.17 24 25

The Gavi-REACH Project

The Gavi-funded REACH Project, led by the International Rescue Committee (IRC) since July 2022, operates in 160 districts across Ethiopia, Somalia, South Sudan, and Sudan with 13 local and two global partners, FlowMinder and ThinkPlace (see online supplemental figure 1).18 REACH is funded through Gavi’s ZIP (ie, Zero-dose Immunisation Programme) initiative,26 and aims to bridge conflict-related immunisation access gaps in FCAS by working with NIPs and humanitarian CSOs to deliver full immunisation in communities left out of routine services.

A qualitative, retrospective learning exercise was conducted to document the successes, challenges, implementation methods and lessons from the REACH Project. Specifically, the learning exercise sought to understand how traditional NIP approaches were adapted to identify and reach missed communities in FCAS. The exercise involved document reviews, key informant and group interviews, and participatory workshops, and was conducted by two consultants with oversight from the IRC Health Unit evaluation team and REACH Project Management Unit (PMU) in February–March 2025. The learning exercise documented (1) NIP limitations in FCAS where conflict and crisis have complicated or cut-off routine immunisation and (2) conflict-sensitive programming approaches critical to REACH’s success despite NIP accessibility constraints. Districts were purposively and systematically selected: first stratified by REACH country, thereafter by community type (nomadic, host population, internally displaced persons (IDPs) and refugees), reflecting typical ZIP communities. Within each stratum, districts were randomly sampled across accessibility levels (fully accessible, partially accessible, and inaccessible (figure 1). The final sample, refined with input from the REACH PMU, included 20 districts across the four countries.

NIP contributions were derived from contextual data from workshops, key informant interviews with REACH Consortium, ministry of health officials and partners (eg, UNICEF). We coded each district to reflect NIP contributions, across expected NIP roles in immunisation programming, including vaccine supply, last-mile delivery, cold chain equipment, cold chain assessment and maintenance, NIP oversight for staff (ie, salaries, supervision, training), consumable supplies, transport for staff, data collection and reporting, policies and potential waivers (for catch-up doses), disease surveillance, and communication/community engagement. We developed a colour-coded matrix to analyse the data. The unit of analysis was the sampled district. For each EPI service mapped we recorded the level of contribution by the NIP and/or humanitarian CSO in the matrix using a three-level code (red=No NIP contribution, yellow=shared responsibility between NIP and humanitarian CSO, and green=full NIP contribution/ownership) (online supplemental table 1). One of the consultants coded each focus area using the matrix and a codebook that defined the criteria for each colour and discussed the codes with the second consultant. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, referring to the field notes, and adjudication with PMU staff when needed, to ensure contextual accuracy. In consultation with the PMU, we reviewed country-level patterns and used the consolidated codes with contextual data to assign the final category for each district and country (see online supplemental table 2) for an illustrated example of district coding assignment).

We describe the contributions of NIPs across REACH districts and the corresponding strategies used by humanitarian CSOs to bridge gaps in NIP presence and capacity, to support knowledge translation in the immunisation and humanitarian community.

Understanding NIP contribution typology and gaps in immunisation programming norms

We classified NIP contributions in FCAS into four categories, reflecting context-specific constraints and actual roles. Each typology reveals predictable gaps in NIP role and guides tailored humanitarian CSO contributions to bridge identified gaps. The categories are No NIP Contribution; Limited NIP: Geographic Limitations; Limited NIP: Functional Limitations and Limited NIP: Transitioning Contexts. Although we distinguish geographic and functional limitations for analytic clarity, these constraints often coexist in practice. We therefore classified each sampled district according to the predominant barrier observed: geographic limitation where lack of access prevented NIP personnel and supplies from entering the area, and functional limitation where the NIP retained access but lacked adequate resources, logistics, workforce, or infrastructure to deliver services reliably without humanitarian CSO support.

No NIP Contribution. This occurred when the officially recognised government lacked physical access or where armed non-state actors maintained physical control of contested areas. Such areas are controlled by non-state actors or outside of government reach. While areas with No NIP contribution lacked formal government presence and contributions, coordination still occurred with local health authorities and governing bodies, typically the administrative structures established by non-state actors. However, these local authorities faced significant resource constraints and were unable to provide immunisation services for several years because they were unable to access immunisation resources, such as vaccines, cold chain equipment, and other infrastructural investments, through the Gavi Alliance since they were not a formally recognised government. Although REACH re-established immunisation services in these areas, existing structures relied primarily on volunteer personnel whose contributions were limited to coordination and participatory decision-making rather than financial or material support for immunisation programmes.

This describes contexts where the NIP is unable to physically reach communities within its borders because of the threat of non-state armed groups or outbreaks of active fighting. Limited NIP: Functional Limitations. In functional limitation contexts the primary barrier is operational capacity rather than lack of access: the NIP has formal authority and physical access to communities but lacks the funding, transport, cold-chain maintenance, workforce or other logistical capacity required to deliver immunisation services reliably. In these settings last-mile delivery and supervision are feasible in theory but not sustainable without external support. Humanitarian CSOs therefore supplement NIP functions by funding transport, procuring consumables, repairing cold-chain equipment and deploying context-specific logistics (eg, tractors, canoes, quad bikes, head porters) to enable routine delivery to local payams/communities and vaccination sites. Most of the sampled districts in South Sudan and Sudan were in this category, although there were slight variations. For example, the South Sudan context was that of logistical limitations due to protracted conflict and destruction of infrastructure or displaced health workers, whereas in Sudan, the limitations were due to active conflict and fighting. Despite these nuances, the NIPs in both countries were functionally constrained in their capacity to fulfil expected immunisation roles due to logistical and resource challenges, worsened by conflict. It is noteworthy that most of the FCAS in both countries have historically experienced disinvestment and marginalisation, which contributed significantly to the eruption of conflict.27

Across the limited categories, the NIP was unable to fully contribute to all expected immunisation roles as they would under normal circumstances. Although there were areas that were exceptions in each category, most of the districts in each country consistently aligned with a single NIP typology, underscoring the broader systemic constraints affecting NIP engagement in FCAS. Where both access denial and resource shortfalls were observed within a district, we assigned the district to the category that best reflected the predominant barrier (see online supplemental table 2).

Conflict-sensitive immunisation programming to bridge the gaps in NIP contributions

The REACH Consortium played a critical role in addressing the gaps due to No NIP Contribution in contested districts or Limited NIP contexts in others. Humanitarian CSO contributions were tailored to the specific context and level of NIP access and engagement. Due to varying levels of NIP contributions in different contexts, humanitarian CSOs collaborated with NIPs to reach ZIP communities. The following examples provide a descriptive analysis of adaptations to immunisation programming norms that were implemented by humanitarian CSOs to bridge the gaps in NIP roles.

No NIP Contribution

In contested areas where the NIP lacked physical access due to armed non-state actor control, REACH delivered all immunisation programming. Programming adaptations were explicitly required to overcome both NIP absence and the restrictive conflict setting, which limited traditional channels of vaccine supply and deployment. Although coordination with local non-state administrative authorities occurred, these entities lacked the resources to contribute meaningfully to immunisation programming, leaving humanitarian CSOs to take on both operational and logistical responsibilities. Contrary to Gavi’s standard one-country model, vaccine procurement and cold chain equipment for these contested areas were negotiated directly by REACH with UNICEF and Gavi. This shift demonstrates how humanitarian programming adapted not just to the absence of NIP infrastructure, but also to the challenging governance structures, common in FCAS. To maintain immunisation programming in insecure communities, REACH deployed Indigo backpacks, portable rechargeable cold chain devices that maintain vaccine viability for 5–7 days, enabling vaccination teams to safely deliver high-quality immunisation through extended outreach and mobile vaccination services.

Limited NIP: Geographic Limitations

In Limited NIP: Geographic Limitation contexts, the NIP has limited physical access to communities due to security concerns, often related to ongoing conflict or the presence of non-state armed groups. In Baidoa in Southwest State, Somalia, for instance, while the government controls towns with the support of international forces, surrounding areas over 5 km from the towns fall into ‘grey areas’, ie, neither under government nor armed non-state actor control (Al-Shabaab, an insurgent group), or completely under the control of non-state actors. Such fragmented governance is a direct consequence of crisis and amplifies the NIP’s geographic constraints.

In these grey areas, the REACH Consortium filled coverage gaps by leveraging humanitarian principles and IRC’s field experience to train and support locally hired vaccinators. These vaccinators, employed by humanitarian CSOs, were instrumental in negotiating humanitarian access through engagement with community leaders. While NIP trainers contributed to capacity building, the overall immunisation programming relied heavily on REACH’s flexible model, underscoring how humanitarian programming becomes indispensable in conflict areas where traditional public-sector approaches face physical constraints.

Limited NIP: Functional Limitations

Resource and logistical constraints that exist in stable settings intensify under active or protracted conflict conditions, further impeding NIP contributions to immunisation programming. Due to the protracted conflict in South Sudan and active conflict in Sudan, the NIPs in both countries face varying dimensions of logistic and resource constraints, which hampered their ability to contribute adequately to immunisation programming. In Sudan’s active conflict context, RSF (ie, Rapid Support Forces) control over transportation routes within REACH districts obstructed safe vaccine movement from centralised facilities to regional health centres, necessitating collaboration between the NIP and humanitarian CSO partners to support last-mile vaccine delivery. In South Sudan’s protracted crisis, geographic and climatic factors, such as inadequate road infrastructure and seasonal flooding, further challenge vaccine transportation. Conflict-aggravated financial and resource constraints in the health system mean NIPs can deliver vaccines only as far as the County Health Departments, in most cases. To bridge these functional gaps, REACH funded and implemented context-specific logistical innovations (eg, tractors, canoes, quad bikes, head porters) to enable last-mile delivery in areas where the NIP had access but lacked the resources or capacity to reach local payams/communities and vaccination sites. While these innovations may apply beyond ZIP areas, their adoption in REACH districts underscores the acute and chronic challenges of crises in FCAS and the indispensable role of ZIP support in bolstering NIPs functionally limited by conflict and fragility.

Limited NIP: Transitioning Contexts

REACH districts in Ethiopia are in the Limited NIP: Transitioning Context, recovering from conflict and re-establishing lost immunisation programming. In Axum, Tigray Region, Ethiopia, in re-establishing immunisation services, post-conflict, REACH partnered with government structures to vaccinate children in missed communities. Additionally, because many health extension workers (HEWs) were displaced, the NIP’s recruitment and training mandate was complemented by REACH, which trained new HEWs to deliver vaccinations. Furthermore, post-conflict resource shortages also hindered NIP oversight. To address vehicle and personnel gaps, REACH coordinated joint supportive supervision visits with the Tigray Regional Health Bureau’s staff and conducted additional mentorship visits. REACH also supported last-mile delivery of vaccines and supplies to Woreda (district) cold chain stores and vaccination sites. To restore cold chain capacity, REACH repaired broken refrigerators and installed solar panels.

Conflict-sensitive approaches to immunisation programming norms in the REACH Project

Across REACH districts, NIPs faced recurrent shortfalls in six core EPI functions that form the operational backbone of routine immunisation systems. These functions (target population estimation, microplanning and service delivery, vaccinator training, policy frameworks, data management and community engagement) are the EPI areas most consistently disrupted in FCAS due to insecurity, displacement, governance fragmentation and access constraints. In these settings, NIPs often retain responsibility for policy and oversight but lack the operational reach to implement services in contested or inaccessible areas. REACH complemented, rather than replaced, NIP roles by introducing conflict-sensitive adaptations tailored to local realities. These adaptations were selected based on documented service gaps, access limitations and NIP capacity assessments, and are vital to sustaining immunisation services in FCAS (table 2).

Target population estimation: Routine immunisation systems rely on census-based population estimates and national-level zero-dose children modelling, which assume stable populations and do not disaggregate to insecure or inaccessible areas. In FCAS, displacement, outdated census data and the accumulation of children up to 59 months in zero-dose communities render these norms inadequate. REACH triangulated rapid facility assessments, community surveys, household headcounts and satellite-derived geospatial layers to estimate population denominators. In Ethiopia’s Sheraro and Sedal districts, for example, household headcounts were used to identify missed communities that government estimates had not captured.

Microplanning and service delivery: Standard microplans are static and assume predictable population distribution and safe access for outreach teams. In FCAS, mobility, displacement and insecurity undermine these assumptions, leaving zero-dose children and ZIP communities unidentified. REACH introduced dynamic spatial mapping to locate concentrations of zero-dose children and guide session strategies. In Baidoa (Somalia), geospatial mapping revealed that a dense cluster of zero-dose children was located in an IDP camp, prompting a shift to fixed-site sessions. In contrast, in pastoralist, mining and returnee communities in Ethiopia and South Sudan, REACH used extended outreach, cross-border vaccination, mini-campaigns and ‘quick-in, quick-out’ (the quick-in, quick-out service delivery model is a targeted approach designed to rapidly deliver vaccines, within a safe window of opportunity, usually following access negotiation, in insecure areas while minimising risks to vaccinators and community members) operations negotiated with local leaders to operate safely in contested and insecure areas.

Vaccinator training and capacity development: Standard refresher training assumes stable staffing and does not address humanitarian access negotiation or the need to vaccinate older children. In FCAS, displacement of health workers and skill gaps require hiring locally and training new vaccinators, and safety risks complicate supervision. REACH conducted baseline assessments to identify capacity gaps, co-delivered, with NIPs, refresher courses on vaccinating zero-dose and older children, and introduced specialised modules on humanitarian access negotiation. These modules enabled vaccinators to secure safe passage through ‘grey areas’ controlled by non-state armed groups. Incentives and ongoing supportive supervision improved vaccinator retention in unstable environments.

Policy framework and waivers for vaccination age eligibility: NIP policies in stable settings govern age eligibility and rarely allow vaccination beyond 12–23 months. In FCAS, however, large cohorts of zero-dose children require flexible age-eligibility policies. REACH partners, with Gavi support, negotiated national and subnational waivers across all countries. In Ethiopia, decentralised governance enabled regional health bureaus to approve age eligibility waivers before the national policy changed, illustrating how REACH navigated multi-layered policy environments.

Data management: Traditional immunisation data tools track infants and children aged 12–23 months and are not designed for older missed cohorts. In FCAS, delayed reporting and incomplete subnational data further hinder identification of zero-dose children. REACH expanded tally sheets, registers, and monitoring charts to capture vaccinations up to 59 months and record sex-disaggregated vaccination data. A CommCare-based digital dashboard enabled real-time monitoring across insecure districts, improving visibility of performance and enabling rapid course correction where needed.

Communication and community engagement: Standard immunisation communication strategies target caregivers of infants and assume stable community norms. In FCAS, misinformation, mistrust, and non-standard age groups require tailored communication approaches. REACH applied human-centred design principles to co-create behaviour change prototypes facilitated by ThinkPlace, addressing provider hesitancy and community norms. Demand-creation campaigns clarified vaccination age eligibility through 59 months, while community dialogues and engagement with local leaders reinforced message uptake and reduced hesitancy among families and health workers.

Impact of the REACH Project

As of December 2025, REACH delivered a total of nearly 28.6 million (28 560 521) vaccine doses globally. Approximately 2.34 million (2 343 610) doses of DTP, first dose (DTP1) vaccine were administered, of which 964 143 doses were to children aged 12–59 months as a proxy for zero-dose children vaccinated. Nearly 2.2 million (2 210 227) children were fully vaccinated based on the completion of the third dose of DTP (DTP3), a traditional measure of full basic vaccination status. In the four countries highlighted in this practice paper (Somalia, Ethiopia, Sudan and South Sudan), REACH administered a total of 17.8 million (17 802 390) vaccine doses by December 2025. Approximately 1.54 million (1 537 669) DTP1 doses were administered, of which 548 695 doses were to zero-dose children aged 12–59 months. Across the four countries, 1.48 million (1 484 519) children were fully vaccinated based on DTP3 coverage.

Implementation and adaptation challenges

REACH teams operated under considerable constraints, including restricted humanitarian access due to insecurity, contested governance, and climate-related disruptions; limited or inaccurate zero-dose data; policy restrictions that excluded older children from vaccine eligibility; and shortages of vaccines and cold-chain equipment. Other challenges include inadequate data systems unable to document zero-dose children; unpredictable population movements and displacement; demand-side and supply-side barriers; and broader health system breakdowns from prolonged conflict and underinvestment. These conditions required continuous adaptation, including tailored humanitarian access negotiation, geospatial zero-dose children identification, immunisation policy advocacy, alternative vaccine supply models and routes, innovative data monitoring efforts, flexible service delivery, and integration of immunisation services with broader primary healthcare services.

Lessons and recommendations for immunisation and humanitarian programme managers and decision makers

The REACH Project offers practical lessons and implications for immunisation and humanitarian programme managers and decision makers in FCAS. Conflict can disrupt NIP operations in diverse and unpredictable ways, for instance, destroying healthcare infrastructure, displacing populations and health workers, restricting access to communities. In such volatile environments, standard immunisation programming norms often fail or become unsafe, requiring flexible, context-specific responses. Humanitarian CSOs are uniquely positioned to bridge these gaps, bringing deep community ties, rapid operational capacity, and the ability to negotiate access through fragmented governance authorities. Their embedded relationships and locally grounded infrastructure allow them to quickly identify and respond to programming gaps and sustain vaccine delivery where NIPs are constrained. Rather than replacing government systems, humanitarian CSOs serve as indispensable partners, filling critical voids, strengthening community trust, and enabling immunisation continuity in conflict-affected settings where conventional government structures cannot operate. The main recommendations, based on lessons from REACH, include:

Flexible service delivery models: In FCAS, government-led vaccine delivery may be disrupted or entirely blocked due to insecurity, displacement, or contested jurisdiction, requiring service models to be nimble enough to respond to the specific nature of disruption, whether it’s geographic inaccessibility, the collapse of health infrastructure, or loss of cold chain functionality. Mobile outreach, anticipatory vaccine positioning, and conflict-resilient cold chain innovations are essential lifelines in environments where static service delivery systems cannot function.28 29

Implementing these lessons in similar FCAS requires concerted coordination and dedicated financing. Gavi, UNICEF, and immunisation partners should develop flexible funding mechanisms to embed conflict-sensitive immunisation programming adaptations and ensure no child is left behind.

Limitations

Although the REACH Project outcomes highlight key operational considerations for delivering full immunisation services in FCAS, certain limitations should be considered. First, the qualitative evidence underpinning this practice paper is based on self-reported accounts from NIP and humanitarian CSO staff, which may be affected by recall bias and organisational sensitivities. To mitigate this, data were triangulated with existing reports, published literature, and input from Ministry of Health and international organisation staff. Second, although we distinguish geographic and functional limitations for analytic clarity, these constraints often coexist in practice. We therefore classified each sampled focus area according to the predominant barrier observed. Thus, overlaps across NIP contribution typologies are possible. Third, while the four-country focus provides comparative insights, the importance of oversight from Gavi PMU and dedicated ZIP funding cannot be overstated. Thus, the transferability of specific strategies to other humanitarian contexts with different governance structures may be limited.

Conclusions

REACH demonstrates that varied NIP involvement—from absence to geographic or functional limitations or transitions—creates immunisation service gaps in humanitarian contexts. Understanding the levels of NIP contributions can inform what approaches and delivery models REACH and CSO partners had to employ in each unique context. Furthermore, REACH demonstrated the ability to adapt and introduce innovative approaches, centred on humanitarian principles, to deliver full immunisation services to communities that have been left out in FCAS. Institutionalising conflict-sensitive strategies within NIP frameworks is vital to sustain gains beyond projects and funding. REACH demonstrates the need for flexible, context-specific approaches aligned with NIP capacity. Future investments in NIP-humanitarian CSO capacity building will translate these insights into resilient, context-specific health systems approaches that not only advance the Immunisation Agenda 2030 to leave no child behind,25 but also strengthen humanitarian response and sustain health equity amid conflict, displacement, and climate-related disruptions.
