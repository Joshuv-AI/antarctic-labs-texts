Decellularized extracellular matrix scaffolds from Pleurotus ferulae mushrooms for sustainable production of steak-like cultured meat with authentic texture
Authors: Doyeon Kim, Manho Kim, Chanho Lee, Hyejun Jang, Wijin Kim, Ju Hyun Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReplicating the fibrous texture of steak-like meat remains a significant hurdle to the commercialization of cultured meat as a sustainable alternative to conventional livestock farming. Many existing scaffolds often lack the biocompatibility, scalability, or structural cues necessary to fabricate steak-like cultured meat. Here, we developed a cost-effective and sustainable scaffold derived from the decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) of the edible mushroom, Pleurotus ferulae. This biomaterial was selected because of its rapid growth, low environmental impact, and inherent fibrous microarchitecture, which exhibits structural similarities to skeletal muscle. An optimized decellularization protocol was established to fabricate porous scaffolds from P. ferulae, and comparative analysis revealed its superior anisotropic microstructure compared to other fungal species. The P. ferulae-derived scaffold supported robust cell adhesion, proliferation, and crucially, guided the aligned differentiation of bovine muscle satellite cells into organized, parallel myotubes that structurally recapitulate native muscle tissue. Texture profile analysis demonstrated that the engineered cultured meat construct exhibited hardness, chewiness, and gumminess comparable to those of real beef, outperforming acellular controls. Furthermore, a thermal processing assay confirmed Maillard reaction-induced browning and the formation of a meat-like macroscopic appearance. Collectively, these results validate P. ferulae dECM scaffolds as a scalable, sustainable, and consumer-acceptable platform for producing steak-like cultured meat with biomimetic textural and sensory properties.Keywords: Cultured meat, Pleurotus ferulae, Decellularization, Biomimetic scaffold, Bovine muscle satellite cells

Introduction

The 21st century has witnessed a continuous increase in global population, which is expected to reach approximately 9.7 billion by 2050 [1]. This demographic expansion has been accompanied by a significant surge in food demand, particularly for meat, which is a major source of animal protein. Rising global affluence and improved living standards have further driven meat consumption, intensifying the pressure on traditional livestock production systems. However, conventional animal agriculture is associated with considerable environmental burdens, including high consumption of energy, water, and land resources [2,3], and a substantial contribution to greenhouse gas emissions, particularly CO2 [4]. Given limited global resources and worsening environmental problems, the sustainability of these systems has been increasingly questioned. Concurrently, ethical concerns regarding animal welfare provide further impetus to explore alternative meat production strategies [5]. Therefore, the development of sustainable and environmentally sound food production methods is a critical global challenge.

Cultured meat, produced via in vitro cultivation of animal cells, has emerged as a promising alternative with the potential to reduce the environmental impact associated with traditional livestock farming [6]. With respect to cell sourcing, current approaches primarily rely on primary muscle stem or progenitor cells isolated from livestock species such as cattle and pigs, while immortalized myogenic cell lines [7] and induced pluripotent stem cells directed toward the myogenic lineage are being actively explored as alternative strategies to enable scalable production [8]. However, replicating the complex tissue structure, flavor, and textural properties of conventional meat remains a major challenge. Real meat is primarily composed of muscle tissue, characterized by highly organized bundles of aligned myofibers that play a crucial role in determining texture, chewiness, and overall sensory perception. Therefore, the development of effective three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds that facilitate cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation while mimicking the native tissue architecture is important for advancing cultured meat technology [9]. Various techniques, including polymer electrospinning, 3D printing, and decellularization, have been investigated for scaffold fabrication, each presenting distinct advantages and limitations [[10], [11], [12]]. While electrospinning provides a straightforward route to ECM-like nanofibers with tuable morphology, it is sensitive to temperature and humidity. 3D printing allows for precise shape control, but the inherent resolution–throughput trade-off, along with shear stresses during extrusion, can damage cells, increasing process complexity and cost [13]. Compared with other technically complex and costly approaches, decellularization has emerged as a relatively simple and cost-effective method that obviates the need for specialized equipment [14]. This approach involves the selective removal of cellular components from biological tissues while preserving the structural and compositional integrity of the extracellular matrix, thus retaining the native microenvironment and biological cues necessary for promoting cellular function.

In the context of cultured meat production, minimizing the reliance on animal-derived materials for scaffolds is highly desirable. Therefore, plant-based and fungal decellularization has attracted considerable interest [[15], [16], [17]]. Plants and fungi offer advantages, such as ease of cultivation, rapid growth rates, cost-effectiveness, and scalability [18]. Plant-derived scaffolds preserve the native cellulosic cell wall and vascular microarchitecture, which limits the tunability of pore size and mechanical compliance within a given tissue. In addition, some plant protein–based biopolymers, such as zein, have been reported to elicit immune responses, necessitating caution in material selection and process design [19,20]. By contrast, fungi, particularly edible mushroom, are attractive sources for scaffold fabrication because of their biomass accumulation, facile cultivation, and minimal resource requirements [21]. The inherently porous structure of mushrooms facilitates intracellular communication and supports cell attachment and proliferation. Additionally, the fibrous nature of mushroom tissues, which often exhibit anisotropic tearing properties similar to those of muscle grains, imparts a meat-like texture, supporting their use as a meat substitute [22]. Structurally, the hyphal walls of the mushroom consist predominantly of a β-glucan matrix embedded with chitin microfibrils and other polysaccharides, with cellulose serving as the principal component of the cell wall [[23], [24], [25]]. Decellularization effectively removes cellular constituents, such as lipids, nucleic acids, amino acids, and monosaccharides, yielding a scaffold primarily composed of residual proteins and polysaccharides [26,27]. The intrinsic mechanical properties of structural polysaccharides like chitin and β-glucan, particularly their stiffness and elasticity, contribute to a cooked texture that closely replicates that of real meat [28]. The successful generation of muscle tissue constructs has been demonstrated through the culture and subsequent differentiation of myoblasts on decellularized mushroom scaffolds [29,30]. However, the high cost of certain mushroom species such as Tricholoma matsutake limits their practical application in scalable cultured meat production.

To address these considerations, the present study focused on the decellularization of two readily cultivable mushroom species, Pleurotus eryngii and its improved strain Pleurotus ferulae, for use as decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) scaffolds in cultured meat applications. These king oyster-type Pleurotus species were selected for their intrinsic fibrous architecture, which closely mimics skeletal muscle, as well as their established local supply chains, year-round availability, and low unit costs support process optimization and cost-effective scale-up. Our investigation involved a comparative analysis of the decellularization efficiency of P. ferulae and P. eryngii with surfactants used to remove cellular components while preserving structural integrity. Subsequently, bovine muscle satellite cells (bMuSCs) isolated from Korean cattle were seeded onto the dECM scaffolds, followed by differentiation induction to evaluate the formation of organized muscle tissue constructs. The overarching goal of this study is to demonstrate the feasibility of using mushroom-derived decellularized scaffolds, a non-animal biomaterial with inherent meat-like textural properties, to produce consumer-preferred cultured meat. This approach is expected to contribute significantly to the development of sustainable and ethically acceptable meat production methods in response to the global challenge of increasing meat consumption, thereby paving the way for broader application of environmentally friendly non-animal biomaterials in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Bovine muscle satellite cells (bMuSCs) were isolated from Korean cattle following a previously established protocol [31]. Briefly, bovine rump muscle (Korean cattle breed) was obtained from a local slaughterhouse. Approximately 50 g of muscle tissue was sterilized, minced finely, and enzymatically dissociated using 0.25 % TE at 37 °C for 1 h. Fibroblasts were depleted through three rounds of pre-plating. Isolated bMuSCs were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. The bMuSCs were cultured in growth medium (GM) consisting of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10 % (v/v) FBS, 1 % (v/v) P/S, 5 ng/mL bFGF and 10 μM p38 inhibitor. Cells were maintained on Matrigel-coated dishes at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 days. Cells were subcultured in TE until they reached approximately 60 % confluence.

C2C12 mouse myoblasts (ATCC® CRL-1772™, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % (v/v) FBS and 1 % (v/v) P/S. Medium was changed every 2 days, and cells were subcultured using TE upon reaching 60–70 % confluence.

For myogenic differentiation, bMuSCs were cultured in GM until reaching approximately 90 % confluence. GM was then replaced with differentiation medium (DM) composed of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 2 % (v/v) horse serum, 1 % (v/v) P/S, and 1 % (v/v) GlutaMAX™. DM was refreshed every 2 days, and cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5 % CO2).

Mushroom decellularization and scaffold preparation

Fresh Pleurotus eryngii and Pleurotus ferulae were cleaned and cut into 10 mm × 2 mm × 5 mm sections (length × width × height). Decellularization was performed based on preliminary optimization studies (Fig. 1A and B). The optimized protocol (Protocol #2) involved sequential immersion: first in 1 % (w/v) SDS solution for 5 days at room temperature with gentle agitation, followed by immersion in a solution containing 0.1 % (v/v) Triton X-100 and 10 % (v/v) commercial bleach for 2 days. After the chemical treatment, the decellularized mushroom sections were washed extensively with distilled water to remove residual chemicals. The washed scaffolds were frozen overnight at −20 °C and subsequently lyophilized using a freeze-dryer (FD8508, Ilshin BioBase, Dongducheon, Republic of Korea) for 48 h. Lyophilized scaffolds were stored in a desiccator until further use. For texture profile analysis, larger scaffolds (10 × 10 × 10 mm) were prepared using identical decellularization and lyophilization procedures.

Lyophilized dECM scaffolds derived from P. eryngii and P. ferulae were characterized using SEM. Gelatin coating on dECM scaffolds

The lyophilized dECM scaffolds were coated with gelatin according to the methods outlined in Fig. S2A and S2B. Briefly, scaffolds were immersed in a sterile 1 % (v/v) porcine gelatin solution in PBS. After coating, excess gelatin was carefully removed from the scaffold surface using sterile wipes. The gelatin-coated scaffolds were immediately used for cell seeding.

Cell seeding on dECM scaffolds

The lyophilized dECM scaffolds were cut into smaller pieces (10 × 2 × 5 mm), soaked in PBS, and sterilized under ultraviolet (UV-C) light. After sterilization, the residual PBS was aseptically removed, and the scaffolds were placed in SPL3D™ Cell Floater 6-well or 24-well plates (SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, Republic of Korea). C2C12 cells and bMuSCs were trypsinized, counted, and resuspended. A cell suspension volume of 40 μL containing 1 × 106 cells was carefully pipetted onto each scaffold, distributing the suspension across the top surface at three distinct points. Scaffolds were incubated for 90 min at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 to allow initial cell attachment. Subsequently, prewarmed medium was gently added to each well to submerge the scaffolds. The cell-laden scaffolds were maintained under standard culture conditions, with the medium replaced every 2 days.

For larger scaffolds (10 × 10 × 10 mm) intended for textural analysis, a total 5 × 106 cells resuspended in 80 μL medium were seeded. To promote cell distribution, the cell suspension was injected into the scaffold interior at five different locations using a sterile syringe with a fine-gauge needle (27G). After a 90 min attachment period, the medium was added.

Cell viability and proliferation assays on dECM scaffolds

Cell viability on the dECM scaffolds was assessed after 4 days of culture using a Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit. Briefly, cell-laden scaffolds were washed three times with PBS and incubated in a staining solution containing 2 μM calcein-AM and 4.5 μM propidium iodide (PI) for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. The scaffolds were washed and immediately visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Bensheim, Germany).

Cell proliferation was quantified by measuring metabolic activity using water-soluble tetrazolium salt-8 (WST-8) assays. At each designated time point, cell-laden scaffolds were transferred to 48-well plates and incubated in culture medium containing 10 % (v/v) WST-8 reagent for 2 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, 100 μL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan GO, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Analysis of cell alignment

Cell alignment on the dECM scaffolds was assessed by analyzing cell nuclear orientation. After 4 days of culture, the scaffolds were fixed with paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were then stained by incubating scaffolds in 1 μg/mL DAPI solution for 5 min. After washing the scaffolds thrice with PBS, they were mounted and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The images were analyzed using ImageJ software to determine the angle of the major axis of each DAPI-stained nucleus relative to the reference axis. Nuclear orientations were normalized by subtracting the mean orientation angle for each image field to represent the distribution around the local average alignment.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cell-laden dECM scaffolds using RiboEx™ LS according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using TOPScript™ RT DryMIX with oligo (dT) primers. The qPCR was performed on a qTOWER3 system (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) using TOPreal qPCR 2 × PreMIX (SYBR Green with low ROX). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. All experiments were conducted in three independent biological replicates, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method comparing differentiated and undifferentiated controls.

Histological analysis and immunohistostaining

After induction of myogenic differentiation, cell-laden dECM scaffolds were fixed in 4 % PFA for 30 min at room temperature. For histological analysis, the fixed scaffolds were embedded in OCT compound and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen blocks were sectioned at 20 μm thickness using a cryostat (HM525 NX, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mounted on glass slides. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard protocols to assess tissue morphology.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), fixed scaffolds were washed with PBS and permeabilized in 0.25 % (v/v) Triton X-100 for 30 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 3 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted 1:250 in 1 % BSA-containing PBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (PBS-T). After three washes with PBS-T, the scaffolds were incubated with the secondary antibodies (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 1 μg/mL DAPI for 5 min. After the final washes with PBS-T, the specimens were mounted and imaged using a CLSM (LSM880, Carl Zeiss).

Quantification of myogenic differentiation

Myogenic differentiation was quantified from immunofluorescence images by calculating the percentage of Desmin- and myogenic factor 6 (MYF6)-positive cells as well as the fusion index using ImageJ software. Marker positivity was determined by dividing the number of nuclei co-localizing with the respective immunostaining by the total number of DAPI-stained nuclei. The fusion index was defined as the proportion of nuclei stained within Desmin-positive myotubes, characterized as elongated, multinucleated cells containing more than two nuclei, relative to the total nuclear count.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

TPA was performed on cultured meat constructs (bMuSC-laden large dECM scaffolds after myogenic differentiation) and control samples of fresh bovine rump muscle, both cut into uniform cubes (10 × 10 × 10 mm). Analysis was conducted using a texture analyzer (TEX-100N, Japan Instrumentation Systems, Nara, Japan) equipped with a cylindrical probe. A two-cycle compression test (double bite) was performed, compressing samples to 50 % of their original height at a constant crosshead speed of 3 mm/s. Parameters such as hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, chewiness, and resilience were calculated from the resulting force-time curves using the instrument's software.

Browning index and protein content analysis

To determine the browning index, the cultured meat constructs and control beef samples were fried in a preheated pan containing small amounts of olive oil. Protein content analysis was performed by extracting proteins from beef and cultured meat samples. First, the samples were washed with PBS and dried completely in an oven at 55 °C. Dried samples were weighed and minced with fine sterile scissors in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with periodic vortexing and then centrifuged at 14,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants containing soluble proteins were collected. Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3), unless otherwise stated. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Pairwise comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Student's t-test. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.005; values of p ≥ 0.05 were considered not significant (ns).

Results and discussion

Optimization of decellularization methods for mushroom dECM scaffold production

Decellularized scaffolds are commonly fabricated through physical, chemical, and enzymatic treatments, or combinations thereof, to remove cellular components while preserving the inherent structure of the ECM [32]. Each decellularization strategy has distinct advantages and limitations. Specifically, both detergent-based and detergent-free methods have been demonstrated to be effective for plant-derived scaffolds [33]. Detergent-based protocols typically involve the use of ionic or nonionic detergents. SDS, an ionic detergent, exerts a potent lytic effect by disrupting lipid-lipid, lipid-protein, DNA-protein, and protein-protein interactions, thereby compromising the integrity of nuclear and cytoplasmic membranes. In contrast, non-ionic detergents, such as Triton X-100, preserve protein-protein interactions and thus minimize ECM disruption [34]. Therefore, meticulous optimization of the detergent concentration and treatment duration is essential to ensure sufficient removal of cellular components without adversely affecting the ECM structure.

To establish optimal parameters for producing mushroom-derived dECM scaffolds, three distinct decellularization protocols were systematically evaluated using mushroom specimens sectioned into appropriate dimensions (Fig. 1A). Although all protocols utilized SDS and Triton X-100, they varied in combinations and concentrations of these agents and bleach, as well as in their exposure times (Fig. 1B). The efficacy of each protocol was assessed by quantifying residual DNA. Native fungal tissue and the resulting dECM scaffolds were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and visualized via fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 1C). Cell nuclei were visible in native P. eryngii mushroom tissues, whereas the scaffolds processed via the first and second protocols exhibited a complete absence of detectable nuclei and DNA. However, scaffolds prepared using the third protocol, which involved 1 % SDS in combination with 0.25 % (v/v) bleach for 5 days, displayed residual DNA, indicating inadequate decellularization.

Although the first protocol achieved effective decellularization, which was attributed to the high SDS concentration, the resulting severe disruption of protein-protein interactions led to the collapse of the ECM structure, compromising scaffold integrity. Conversely, the second protocol successfully removed cellular components while preserving the ECM structure, likely due to the reduced SDS concentration and sequential detergent application that minimized protein denaturation. Based on these observations, the second protocol was determined to be the most suitable for effective decellularization without compromising the structural integrity of the ECM. Therefore, for subsequent experiments, mushroom samples were processed using this optimized protocol, treated with 1 % sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for 5 days, followed by a 2-day treatment with 0.1 % Triton X-100 and 1 % bleach, to ensure both comprehensive cellular removal and preservation of scaffold architecture.

Characterization of dECM scaffolds from P. eryngii and P. ferulae

To fabricate scaffolds that are structurally analogous to real meat, which is a crucial requirement for cultured meat production, the optimized decellularization protocol was applied to two mushroom species, P. eryngii and P. ferulae, followed by a comparative structural analysis to evaluate their suitability as engineering scaffolds. Residual cellular nuclei were visualized by DAPI staining to evaluate the efficacy of the decellularization process. As shown in Fig. 2A, no nuclear material was detected in the dECM scaffolds derived from either mushroom species, indicating the effectiveness of the decellularization method. Preservation of the ECM architecture was verified through histological analysis of both native and decellularized samples using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2B). These results demonstrated the complete removal of cellular components while confirming that the overall ECM structure remained intact in both species. Notably, the characteristic aligned, fibrous morphology is more pronounced in the P. ferulae scaffold compared to the P. eryngii scaffold.

The swelling ratio, a critical parameter for evaluating scaffold performance, was measured to determine the capacity of the scaffold to absorb and retain sufficient culture medium, which is essential for nutrient delivery during cell cultivation. The dECM scaffolds obtained from both P. eryngii and P. ferulae exhibited high swelling ratios exceeding 2000 %, with no statistically significant difference between them (Fig. 2C). The inherently high moisture content and porous nature of mushrooms contribute to their high absorption capacity. Complementary porosity measurements revealed that both scaffolds exhibited a porosity of approximately 80 % (Fig. 2D), supporting their potential to ensure sufficient oxygen and nutrient delivery for sustaining cellular proliferation. To further examine the microstructural properties, specifically the anisotropic and porous features of the dECM scaffolds, SEM analysis was conducted on longitudinally sectioned specimens, including cross-sectional and mid-height sections parallel to the fiber orientation (Fig. 2E). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed discernible porous structures in both the mushroom dECMs. However, a distinct anisotropic alignment of fibers was predominantly observed in the P. ferulae dECM, whereas this organized pattern was less evident in the P. eryngii counterpart.

The anisotropic organization of muscle fibers in native tissues is a key determinant of meat texture, chewiness, and overall physical properties [35]. Therefore, recapitulating such an aligned myofiber orientation is essential for developing cultured meat that authentically mimics the sensory characteristics of conventional meat. The unidirectional fibrous architecture inherent to mushroom tissues provides a topographical microenvironment conductive to promoting muscle cell attachment, proliferation, and aligned differentiation. In particular, the superior anisotropic features observed in the Pleurotus ferulae dECM suggest its potential advantage in guiding the formation of organized muscle tissues, thereby enhancing the similarity in physical properties between conventional and cultured meat.

Optimization of cell seeding and comparative biocompatibility of P. eryngii and P. ferulae dECM scaffolds

Effective and uniform cell distribution is a prerequisite for successful 3D tissue culture. Initial cell seeding density and early adhesion can influence key attributes of the final construct, including texture and overall protein content [36]. Accordingly, we optimized the seeding protocol to maximize cell capture while maintaining uniform distribution throughout the 3D architecture. To establish an optimal seeding protocol, C2C12 myoblasts were introduced into P. eryngii dECM scaffolds using varying volumes of the cell suspension while maintaining a constant cell number (Fig. S1A). A seeding volume of 40 μL resulted in even cell dispersion and uniform adherence throughout the scaffold. In contrast, a larger volume of 60 μL led to incomplete absorption of the cell suspension, causing cell deposition predominantly at the overflow interface (Fig. S1B). Therefore, a 40 μL seeding volume was adopted as the optimal condition for all subsequent experiments. Comparable results were observed with the P. ferulae dECM scaffold, which also supported uniform cell distribution using the optimized seeding protocol (Fig. S1C).

Decellularized scaffolds derived from other mushroom species, such as Agaricus bisporus (button mushrooms), effectively support the adhesion of various cell types, including myoblasts [24,30]. However, plant-derived decellularized scaffolds often require chemical modification to enhance cell attachment due to the absence of canonical cell adhesion motifs, including the Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequence [29,37]. Given that the chitin/β-glucan complex, a major structural component of mushrooms, lacks the RGD motif, we examined whether surface coating of the mushroom dECM scaffolds via immersion in a gelatin solution could enhance cell adhesion (Fig. S2A). A comparative analysis between gelatin-coated and uncoated scaffolds revealed no significant difference in cell adhesion, as determined by cell nuclei staining (Fig. S2B). This outcome suggests that the passive absorption of serum proteins from the culture medium, such as fibronectin and vitronectin, onto the mushroom scaffolds provides sufficient attachment cues for the cells, rendering additional gelatin coating unnecessary [38,39]. These observations confirmed that the mushroom dECM scaffolds possess an inherently cytocompatible surface, permitting the continuation of experiments without additional surface modifications.

Using the optimized seeding procedure, the biocompatibility of both P. eryngii and P. ferulae dECM scaffolds was evaluated. C2C12 cells were cultured for up to 7 days, and viability was assessed using a Live/Dead assay. After 4 days of culture, fluorescence microscopy revealed a predominance of viable, calcein-AM-positive cells (green) and minimal propidium iodide-positive dead cells (red) on both the P. eryngii (Fig. 3A) and P. ferulae scaffolds (Fig. 3C), confirming the high biocompatibility of both materials. Furthermore, cellular proliferation, quantified via a WST-8 assay, demonstrated an exponential growth profile on both scaffolds over the 7-day culture period (Fig. 3B and D). These results confirmed that both decellularized scaffolds exhibited favorable biocompatibility with minimal cytotoxic effects and effectively supported cellular adhesion and proliferation.

Notably, Live/Dead assay images quantitatively indicated that the cells cultured on the P. ferulae scaffold adopted a more aligned orientation than those cultured on the P. eryngii scaffold. To quantitatively validate this observation, cell alignment was analyzed by measuring the orientation of the major nuclear axis from DAPI-stained images (Fig. 3E). This analysis revealed that the cells on the P. eryngii scaffold exhibited a broad, almost random distribution of nuclear orientation angles, which is consistent with less-aligned microstructure of the scaffold itself (Fig. 3F). In contrast, cells cultured on the P. ferulae scaffold were predominantly oriented within a 0–15° range relative to the mean alignment direction, indicating a significantly higher degree of alignment (Fig. 3G).

These results demonstrate that dECM scaffolds derived from Pleurotus species were biocompatible and capable of supporting cell adhesion and proliferation without notable cytotoxicity. However, the inherent anisotropic and porous architecture of the P. ferulae scaffolds more effectively induced aligned growth of muscle cells. As recapitulating the parallel organization of myofibers is critical for mimicking the texture of native meat, the superior ability of the P. ferulae dECM to guide cell alignment makes it the more promising candidate. Accordingly, we selected the P. ferulae dECM scaffold for the development of steak-like cultured meat.

Biocompatibility and proliferation of bMuSCs on P. ferulae dECM scaffolds

To validate the suitability of the P. ferulae dECM scaffold for meat production, we transitioned from the C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line to primary bMuSCs isolated directly from Korean cattle. The isolated bMuSCs were first differentiated under 2D culture to verify their intrinsic myogenic capacity. Immunofluorescence showed clear expression of Desmin and MYF6, accompanied by multinucleated myotube formation (Fig. S3A). Quantification further demonstrated a substantial population of Desmin- and MYF6-positive cells along with a notable fusion index, indicating functional maturation (Fig. S3B and C). We next sought to determine if the topographical cues of the scaffold could induce contact guidance in these primary cells, a critical step for forming organized muscle tissue (Fig. 4A). Cell orientation within the scaffold microarchitecture was quantified using nuclear staining to assess contact guidance (Fig. 4B). Consistent with prior observations for C2C12 cells, bMuSCs aligned predominantly along the longitudinal axis of the scaffold, with approximately 70 % of cells oriented within 0–15° of the mean alignment angle. A Live/Dead assay revealed negligible cytotoxicity, with a vast majority of cells remaining viable, indicating that the decellularized scaffold supported bMuSC adhesion and survival without adverse effects (Fig. 4C).

Given that the myogenic differentiation of muscle progenitors is promoted by high cell density and low serum conditions [40,41], we examined the bMuSC proliferation profiles within the scaffold to identify the optimal temporal window for initiating differentiation. The metabolic activity, which is proportional to the number of viable cells, was measured every two days using a WST-8 assay. The results showed that bMuSCs underwent an exponential growth phase until day 7, reaching approximately 300 % of their initial seeding density (Fig. 4D), followed by a plateau between days 7 and 9, indicative of scaffold-imposed limits. Based on this growth kinetic, day 7 post-seeding was designated as the optimal time point for initiating myogenic differentiation.

Myogenic differentiation of bMuSCs and formation of cultured meat construct

Based on the WST-8 assay results, bMuSCs were seeded onto P. ferulae dECM scaffolds and allowed to proliferate for 6 days before initiating differentiation for an additional 6 days (Fig. 5A). The process of myogenic differentiation is primarily orchestrated by a cascade of muscle regulatory factors (MRFs), including myogenin (MYOG) and myoblast determination protein 1 (MYOD1), which are key transcription factors that activate muscle-specific gene expression programs [42]. The activation of these early markers leads to terminal differentiation and the expression of structural proteins such as myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7), a major contractile protein in mature muscle filaments. To quantify differentiation, we performed qRT-PCR for MYOG, MYOD1, and MYH7 mRNA transcripts (Fig. 5B). Compared to the undifferentiated controls, MYOG and MYOD1 mRNA expression levels in the differentiated construct increased by approximately 600-fold and 9-fold, respectively, demonstrating the effective activation of early myogenesis. Concurrently, MYH7 expression increased more than 120-fold, indicating the substantial formation of myotubes within the P. ferulae scaffold.

Histological analysis of cryosections by H&E staining revealed dense tissue formation in the bMuSC-laden scaffold, in contrast to the acellular scaffold control (Fig. 5C). Notably, the aligned microarchitecture of the scaffold was preserved throughout the entire culture period, with no evidence of degradation. To specifically assess muscle fiber maturation, immunohistochemistry was performed for Desmin and MYF6 (Fig. 5D). Desmin, a muscle-specific type III intermediate filament critical for myofiber integrity and MYF6, a regulator of late-stage myogenic differentiation [43], were detected in the cell-laden scaffolds. Quantitative image analysis across multiple fields of view further showed high fractions of Desmin- and MYF6-positive cells among total nuclei (Fig. 5E). Co-expression of these markers confirmed that bMuSCs underwent comprehensive myogenic maturation to form well-developed muscle-like tissue within the P. ferulae scaffold. In addition, the fusion index, defined as the percentage of total nuclei located within multinucleated myotubes, indicated robust myoblast fusion in the cell-laden scaffolds (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the aligned microarchitecture of the mushroom-derived scaffold facilitated the attachment and proliferation of primary bMuSCs and, more importantly, promoted their efficient differentiation and organization into aligned myofibers, culminating in the formation of a structured, steak-like cultured meat construct.

Comparison of TPA between cultured meat and real meat

Replicating the complex textural profile of conventional meat remains a primary challenge for consumer acceptance of cultured meat products. TPA is a widely used instrumental method to objectively quantify key textural attributes. By subjecting a sample to a controlled double-compression cycle, TPA simulates the forces experienced during mastication to measure primary parameters such as hardness. These primary values are then used to calculate secondary parameters, including chewiness and gumminess, which are critical for mimicking the sensory experience of eating meat [44].

Cultured meat constructs, generated by differentiating bMuSCs on decellularized P. ferulae dECM scaffolds, were texturally compared with size-matched portions of real beef (Fig. 6A). The analysis first revealed that the presence of differentiated myotubes within the cultured constructs increased the hardness, chewiness, and gumminess by approximately 2- to 3-fold compared to acellular scaffolds (Fig. 6B). This result demonstrates that the process of myogenesis, including cellular contributions and associated ECM remodeling, imparts substantial textural integrity to the scaffold. Importantly, when compared to real beef, the cultured meat constructs exhibited no statistically significant differences in hardness, chewiness, or gumminess. Similarly, the secondary parameters of springiness and cohesiveness were also statistically indistinguishable between the cultured and conventional meat samples (Fig. 6C). In addition, to evaluate the effect of thermal process, we compared the textural properties of the cultured meat constructs before and after grilling (Table S2). In cell-laden cultured meat constructs, grilling increased hardness, chewiness, and gumminess by approximately 52 %, 46 %, and 51 %, respectively, whereas changes in scaffold-only controls were substantially smaller, supporting that cultured muscle tissue is responsible for the cooking-induced texture development. These data collectively indicate that P. ferulae-derived dECM scaffolds provided a biomimetic microenvironment capable of supporting the formation of muscle tissue with textural properties that closely replicate those of real beef, thereby addressing a significant barrier to consumer acceptance.

Maillard reaction and protein content analysis of cultured meat

In addition to texture, the flavor and color developed during cooking are central to the sensory appeal of meat. These attributes are largely governed by the Maillard reaction, a non-enzymatic chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that occurs upon heating [45,46]. To assess this property, the visual and chemical browning of the cultured meat was evaluated.

Visual comparison before and after pan-frying revealed minimal color changes in the acellular P. ferulae scaffold, whereas the cell-laden cultured meat construct developed a characteristic reddish-brown hue similar to that of pan-fried beef (Fig. 7A). Notably, the morphology of the scaffold remained intact throughout the process, with no collapse or distortion observed, thereby confirming structural stability during cooking. This color transition is attributed to the Maillard reaction occurring between the amino groups of proteins within the cultured muscle fibers and reducing sugars, which may include chitin-derived oligomers from the fungal scaffold (Fig. 7B) [[47], [48], [49], [50]].

Quantitative analysis of the browning index corroborated these visual findings. The cultured meat exhibited a browning index that was slightly higher than, though not statistically different from, that of real beef. Conversely, the browning index for the acellular scaffold remained near baseline levels (Fig. 7C). The limited browning in the acellular scaffold is attributable to its lower protein content relative to cultured meat, which consequently hinders a sufficient Maillard reaction. Total protein analysis confirmed this hypothesis, showing that the cultured meat construct retained approximately 1.8-fold more protein than the acellular scaffolds (Fig. 7D). However, the protein content of the cultured meat was still lower than that of real beef (Table S3). This discrepancy may be partly due to the loss of endogenous mushroom proteins during decellularization. Future efforts should therefore focus on refining the decellularization process and increasing total protein content through strategies such as high-density culture to expand biomass, application of maturation cues to promote myogenesis, and nutritional fortification with food-grade proteins. Nevertheless, the protein content achieved in the current cultured meat construct was sufficient to induce a discernible Maillard reaction, yielding both the visual appearance and the precursor chemistry required for desirable flavor development. These findings highlight P. ferulae-derived dECM as a promising scaffold for producing steak-like cultured meat that fulfills key textural and sensory parameters crucial for consumer adoption.

However, several considerations remain. The overall protein content was still below that of conventional beef, and we did not directly evaluate indicators, such as flavor and nutrition. Moreover, potential toxicity from residual decellularization reagents also remains a significant concern. Further investigations should apply a range of strategies to increase protein content, establish explicit acceptance criteria through validated residue testing, and assess storage and processing stability by monitoring compositional changes, including water retention and nutritional values. In addition, non-chemical processing alternatives, such as autoclaving, should be explored to ensure biocompatibility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study addresses a critical challenge in the production of cultured meat by developing a novel, biomimetic scaffold from the edible mushroom Pleurotus ferulae. While the broader commercialization of cultured meat hinges on advancements in areas such as serum-free media and scalable bioreactor technology, creating a structured, steak-like product with realistic texture remains a formidable obstacle. Our work demonstrated that decellularized P. ferulae provides an effective solution by recapitulating the anisotropic microarchitecture of native muscle tissue.

Importantly, our optimized mushroom decellularization protocol yielded an edible dECM that, without additional surface treatment such as gelatin coating, supported bMuSC adhesion, proliferation, and alignment, culminating in organized myogenic differentiation and steak-like construct. Notably, the engineered construct exhibited a texture profile and post-cooking browning index comparable to those of conventional beef, addressing two key parameters for consumer acceptance. Among the mushroom species evaluated, P. ferulae-derived dECM possessed particularly pronounced anisotropic features, providing topographical cue that guided myoblast alignment and subsequent differentiation into elongated parallel myotubes. Furthermore, the use of mushroom-derived dECM presents several strategic advantages. The intrinsic porous network and meat-like textural properties of the raw material, combined with the minimal environmental impact and year-round availability of cultivated mushrooms, highlight their suitability as scaffold biomaterials for cultured meat applications. Moreover, our decellularization protocol required neither costly specialized equipment nor harsh chemical crosslinking agents such as glutaraldehyde. This enhances economic feasibility and facilitates regulatory approval for food applications.

While the decellularization process inherently leads to some protein loss from the initial material, the substantial protein enrichment observed in the final bMuSC-laden constructs relative to the acellular scaffolds demonstrated their nutritional and sensory value. Collectively, these findings establish that decellularized P. ferulae scaffolds are a promising, scalable, and ethically sound platform for the production of steak-like cultured meat, representing a significant step toward the realization of sustainable and appealing alternatives to conventional animal agriculture.
