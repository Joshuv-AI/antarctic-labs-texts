Dual-Modality SPECT and Viscosity-Responsive AIE Fluorescence Probe for Pancreatic Cancer Diagnosis and Intraoperative Guidance
Authors: Kuangshan Zhang, Qian Chen, Qian Huang, Xunhao Qi, Yu Qin, Dong Wang, Ran Zhu, Guanglin Wang, Xiaoming Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The accurate early diagnosis and achievement of R0 resection in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remain formidable clinical challenges. Plectin-1, a protein that is overexpressed in PDAC, has been identified as a promising target for diagnostic applications. In this study, a novel dual-modality probe, designated PTP-QM-DOTA, was developed to specifically target plectin-1. The probe integrates the radionuclide 177Lu with a viscosity-responsive aggregation-induced emission (AIE) fluorophore, QM, thereby enabling both preoperative single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging and intraoperative fluorescence navigation. In comparison with a nontargeted control probe (HSP-QM-DOTA), PTP-QM-DOTA demonstrated a 23.95 ± 0.54-fold increase in fluorescence intensity in high-viscosity media (96% glycerol) relative to pure water, thus exhibiting pronounced viscosity-responsive behavior. The probe demonstrated high radiolabeling efficiency, excellent radiostability, and favorable biocompatibility, with no significant evidence of toxicity or organ damage observed. In vivo SPECT imaging clearly delineated PDAC tumors within 1 h postinjection, with tumor uptake reaching 6.44 ± 1.88% IA/g at 30 min postinjection. The AIE properties of the fluorophore substantially enhanced fluorescence intensity and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in tumor regions, enabling precise tumor localization. The collective findings of the present study establish the first evidence for 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA as a safe, reliable, and efficient dual-modality peptide probe with possible potential for integrated diagnosis and surgical guidance of PDAC.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is the fourth most prevalent cause of cancerous afflictions on a global scale. The dismal survival rate is largely attributable to the lack of discernible early symptoms and the aggressive biological behavior of the disease. Despite the fact that surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment for early stage pancreatic cancer, only 15–20% of patients are eligible for curative surgery at diagnosis. This is due to the fact that the majority of patients present with nonspecific symptoms only at advanced stages. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) constitutes over 90% of all pancreatic cancers, underscoring the urgent need for improved early diagnostic strategies.

Beyond the challenges posed by the diagnosis, surgical resection of PDAC is hampered by positive margins and lymph node metastasis. In order to address the aforementioned issues, there is a compelling demand for dual-modality targeted probes that enable precise preoperative tumor diagnosis, intraoperative localization, and margin assessment. Substantial research efforts have been directed toward the development of probes that combine radionuclide imaging with fluorescence capabilities. − Radionuclide imaging is a highly valued technique in medical imaging due to its high sensitivity and specificity. It is a valuable tool in the medical field, with broad clinical applications in the detection of tumors, the differentiation of lesions, and the assessment of functional in cardiovascular and neurological systems. , Fluorescence navigation has also been demonstrated to be effective in surgical guidance, for example, indocyanine green (ICG) has been shown to aid in margins delineation and perfusion assessment in liver surgery, while 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) has been shown to assist neurosurgeons in localizing glioblastoma.

Plectin-1 is expressed at a high level in PDAC. , This large scaffold protein (500 kDa) is aberrantly overexpressed on the membranes of PDAC tumor cells, thereby promoting tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion. , Since its identification as a marker in pancreatic malignancies, the targeting peptide PTP (KTLLPTP) has been isolated via phage display and shown to bind specifically to Plectin-1. Conjugates of PTP with radionuclides and fluorescent labels have subsequently been employed in targeted imaging studies of pancreatic tumors. −

Conventional fluorophores such as cyanine dyes are commonly used for tumor imaging. but often suffer from poor photostability, high background signals, and aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). In contrast, aggregation-induced emission (AIE) materials have attracted considerable interest for biological imaging due to their advantageous properties, including low background, high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and excellent photostability. , For instance, Zhu et al. recently employed an enzyme-responsive AIE probe for fluorescence imaging of PDAC.

In order to surmount the challenges posed by inaccurate diagnosis and surgical localization in PDAC, a dual-modality peptide probe was designed, PTP-QM-DOTA, which targets the plectin-1. This probe facilitates both radionuclide imaging and viscosity-responsive AIE fluorescence, thereby supporting effective surgical navigation. PTP-QM-DOTA was efficiently radiolabeled with 177Lu (γ-rays: 55, 112, and 208 keV) and exhibits AIE in the high-viscosity tumor microenvironment. Using 177Lu as a radiolabel, we hope to explore the feasibility of using a single probe for both diagnosis and therapy in future studies. To demonstrate the specific binding of PTP-QM-DOTA to PDAC cell lines and tumor models, we selected a nontargeting peptide fragment, HSP­(AGFSLPAGC), to synthesize HSP-QM-DOTA, as a control, and ensured consistent process and quality. Cellular studies confirmed selective accumulation in Panc02 cells with high plectin-1 expression. Following intravenous administration, 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA enabled clear visualization via both SPECT and fluorescence imaging, with rapid blood clearance (Scheme ). These results validate PTP-QM-DOTA as a promising and safe dual-modality probe for precise diagnostic localization and intraoperative guidance in PDAC.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Radiolabeled Probes

The dual-modality probes were synthesized via solid-phase synthesis (Figure a and b). The target compounds, PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA, were obtained with high chemical purity (98.8% and 99.9%, respectively) after HPLC purification (Figure c, Figure S5, and S6). MALDI-MS analysis gave m/z [M + H] + values of 1791.92 and 1968.89, consistent with the theoretical masses of PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA (Figure S1 and S2). 1H NMR spectra in D2O further confirmed the expected structures (Figure S3 and S4). The results presented above demonstrate the successful synthesis of PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA. Both probes displayed excellent chemical stability when incubated in PBS at 90 °C for 1 h or stored under ambient light for 1 week (Figure S7).

It is noteworthy that both probes are equipped with DOTA, which facilitates the coupling process with metallic radionuclides, such as 177Lu. The radiolabeling efficiency of the reaction was found to exceed 98% (PTP-QM-DOTA: 98.50 ± 0.076%, HSP-QM-DOTA: 98.70 ± 0.16%) under optimized conditions of pH 4.5, 90 °C for a duration of 0.5 h (Figure d). In order to further evaluate the radiochemical purity of radiolabeled probes, they were subjected to incubation in PBS buffer at 37 °C, pH 7.4 for varying lengths of time. Despite the passage of 8 days during which the radio stability of the sample remained at a high level (PTP-QM-DOTA: 96.01 ± 0.88%, HSP-QM-DOTA: 94.78 ± 0.31%) (Figure e). It is evident that the exceptional radiolabeling efficiency and radiochemical purity of the probes ensure efficient utilization of radioisotopes and safety in vitro and in vivo in subsequent biological studies.

Fluorescence Characterization

In order to ascertain the optical properties of the probes, the optimal excitation wavelengths for a 10 μM probe solution were determined via UV–vis spectroscopy. This investigation thus revealed the optimal excitation wavelengths of 362 and 435 nm of two probes (Figure a). The probes contain the AIE group QM, enabling fluorescence emission in an aggregated state. The AIE and viscosity-responsive fluorescence properties of the probes were investigated by measuring the fluorescence intensity of the probe solutions at different concentrations and ratios in a glycerol–water mixture. Utilizing the IVIS imaging system, it was evident that the fluorescence intensity of both probes in a pure aqueous solution exhibited a marked enhancement with an increase in probe concentration and glycerol ratio (Figure b). As illustrated in Figure c and d, the fluorescence intensity of both probes exhibited a gradual increase with increasing concentration in pure water. As the concentration increased from 2.5 μM to 40 μM, the increase in fluorescence intensity of PTP-QM-DOTA was significantly higher than that of HSP-QM-DOTA under the same concentration change (9.59 ± 0.33 vs 2.25 ± 0.03) (Figure e). This finding suggests that QM fluorescence enhancement is attributable to the restricted intramolecular rotation (RIR) mechanism, and that increase in system viscosity exerts a substantial influence on probe fluorescence behavior. Furthermore, under concentrations where probe concentration was held constant (10 μM), gradually increasing the glycerol proportion demonstrated that viscosity enhancement effectively restricted QM fluorophore rotation, thereby amplifying fluorescence intensity. When pure water was replaced with a 96% glycerol solution, the fluorescence intensity of PTP-QM-DOTA increased by 23.95 ± 0.54-fold, while that of HSP-QM-DOTA increased by 24.12 ± 1.0-fold (Figure f, g and h). The fluorescence intensity of both probes at specific wavelengths increased significantly with rising probe concentration and environmental viscosity, thus confirming their viscosity-responsive properties. These results indicate that the fluorescence “turn on” characteristics of the probes are strictly dependent on their aggregation state and the viscosity of the microenvironment, thereby providing a theoretical basis for their specifically activatable imaging in tumor sites. Furthermore, the observed differences in the AIE properties between the two probes may be attributed to subtle variations in their aggregation behavior or the degree of restriction of intramolecular rotation resulting from their distinct hydrophilic peptide segments (PTP and HSP).

In Vitro Assays of Probes

In order to assess the expression of the plectin-1 protein in cells, immunofluorescence imaging was performed on the Panc02 and HepG1–6 cells (Figure a). Additionally, we performed quantitative analysis of the plectin-1 immunofluorescence intensity in the captured images. The immunofluorescence intensity results demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity in Panc02 cells was significantly higher than in HepG1–6 cells (0.89 ± 0.03 vs 0.06 ± 0.01) (Figure c), suggesting that the expression of plectin-1 protein in Panc02 is much higher than in HepG1–6. Furthermore, the expression of plectin-1 protein in tumors was assessed by sacrificing the Panc02 cells tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice at 7 and 14 days. The tumors were collected, sliced and stained (Figure b). The results demonstrated that the immunofluorescence intensity of plectin-1 in Panc02 cells tumors at 14 days was higher than that at 7 days (Figure d), further indicating a positive correlation between plectin-1 expression and progression in Panc02 cells tumor.

The assessment of the probes’ cellular toxicity was conducted by employing the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that there was no significant change in cell viability with increasing concentrations and radioactivity of the probe, indicating that both probes exhibited no toxicity (Figure e and f). This is because 177Lu is a low-energy beta emitter with a relatively short maximum range and dispersed energy deposition, resulting in no significant cell killing. Indeed, there is increasing cytotoxicity (from 100% to 80%) with increasing radioactivity. But no difference was visible between substances, which may suggest unspecific effects. In order to assess the uptake of the probes, 20 μM of probes were cocultured with Panc02 and HepG1–6 cells for a period of 2 h. Subsequent to this, the cells were imaged by means of a fluorescence imaging system (Figure g). The results of the fluorescence analysis demonstrated that the normalized fluorescence intensity in the PTP-QM-DOTA group was significantly higher than that in the HSP-QM-DOTA group in both cell types (Figure h). Furthermore, the level of fluorescence intensity in HepG1–6 cells was found to be considerably lower in comparison to that observed in Panc02 cells. Based on these findings, the fluorescence signal of PTP-QM-DOTA positively correlates with the expression level of the target protein, preliminarily demonstrating that PTP-QM-DOTA possesses specific recognition ability toward the Panc02 cell line. Compared with HepG1–6 cells, the higher fluorescence intensity inside Panc02 cells highlights the critical role of the plectin-1-targeting PTP moiety. This specific binding, together with the AIE property of the probe, enables high-fidelity fluorescence imaging.

Dual-Modal Imaging Study

In order to investigate the in vivo nuclear imaging capability of the probes, 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA were administered via tail vein injection in subcutaneous Panc02 cells subcutaneous tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice, followed by imaging with microSPECT/CT. The SPECT images of 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA demonstrated that uptake of the tumor reached a high level within 0.5 h, after which it gradually decreased. It was observed that significant tumor signals remained detectable within the tumor region for a duration of 1 h before rapidly decaying (Figure a). In the control group, no significant tumor signals were observed after 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA injection (Figure b), thereby eliminating the possibility of a false positive effect due to high tumor tissue blood flow. Quantitative analysis of SPECT images revealed that 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA uptake of 3.52 ± 0.07%IA/cc at 0.5 h, compared to the only 1.42 ± 0.23% IA/cc for 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (Figure c). The tumor to muscle (T/M) ratio for 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA reached 2.34 ± 1.10, which is at least twice that of 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (Figure d). The findings thus obtained serve to confirm the probe’s capacity for tumor-specific targeting and nuclear imaging. The results obtained demonstrate the imaging effect of PTP-QM-DOTA achieved better results than previous studies.

Furthermore, the fluorescence imaging potential of the probes was evaluated by means of the intravenous injection of PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA via tail vein into the subcutaneous Panc02 cells tumor-bearing mice. Fluorescence imaging results revealed that the fluorescent signal from the subcutaneous tumor gradually intensified after PTP-QM-DOTA injection, reaching a maximum at 0.5 h and then gradually diminishing thereafter. In contrast, the control group (HSP-QM-DOTA) exhibited no significant enhancement (Figure a). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity of PTP-QM-DOTA reached 1.40 ± 0.18 times the preinjection baseline (Figure b). This outcome is consistent with the SPECT imaging results. Subsequently, 0.5 h following probe injection, the tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and the major organs were collected. Furthermore, the implementation of fluorescence imaging was undertaken. The results demonstrated that the intensity of tumor fluorescence persisted at a significantly higher level compared to the control group even 0.5 h after the injection of PTP-QM-DOTA (Figure c and d). Despite the lower probe concentration within the tumor in comparison to liver and kidney tissues, the high-viscosity environment inside the tumor triggered the viscosity-responsive fluorescence enhancement effect of QM. ,, Under the existing control conditions, the targeting peptide exhibits stronger fluorescence signal than the nontargeting peptide (PTP-QM-DOTA: 0.96 ± 0.16 vs HSP-QM-DOTA: 0.32 ± 0.01) (Figure e and f). Fluorescence intensity in excised tumor tissue at various postinjection time points was found to align with in vivo observations. Due to the lack of systematic matching of charge, hydrophobicity, size, and aggregation propensity for the nontargeting control peptide, the possibility that the observed signal difference arises from the peptides’ own physicochemical properties cannot be excluded. Thus, the conclusion on targeting specificity should be considered preliminary.

The consistent findings between SPECT and fluorescence imaging underscore the dual modality’s reliability: while 177Lu-labeling provided dynamic quantification of probe biodistribution, the viscosity-activated fluorescence offered real-time visual confirmation of tumor-specific retention. This synergy not only validates PTP-QM-DOTA’s targeting specificity but also highlights its potential for both preoperative whole-body screening and intraoperative surgical navigation.

Biodistribution, Pharmacokinetics, and Safety Study

In order to investigate the biodistribution of the probes, 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA were administered via tail vein injection into tumor-bearing mice. The mice were euthanized at 0.5 and 1 h postinjection. Subsequently, the weight and radioactivity of the major organs were measured. The results demonstrated that 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA was predominantly eliminated via the kidneys (0.5 h: 21.21 ± 8.39% IA/g; 1 h: 11.75 ± 4.31% IA/g), with minimal hepatic uptake (0.5 h: 7.97 ± 0.70% IA/g; 1 h: 5.60 ± 0.21% IA/g) (Figure a, Table S1). In contrast, 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA demonstrated augmented hepatic and renal uptake at 0.5 h (18.90 ± 4.10% IA/g and 16.34 ± 1.20% IA/g, respectively), with subsequent escalations observed at 1 h (Figure b, Table S2). In comparison with 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA, 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA exhibited superior biodistribution and pharmacokinetic performance, as evidenced by reduced retention times in both the liver and kidneys. This reduction in retention times suggests a potential reduction in the incidence of radiation-induced damage. In order to assess probe metabolism in blood, tail vein injection experiments were conducted, revealing a plasma half-life of only 0.14 h of 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 0.12 h of 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (Figure c). In addition, the safety of the probe was investigated by intravenously injecting 60 nmol of the probes into mice. The major organs were harvested and sliced, then paraffin embedding and staining was conducted for a period of 7 days. The application of H&E staining to major organs in C57BL/6 mice revealed no significant pathological damage (Figure d). In the complete blood count, liver and kidney function indices report, compared to the saline injection group, neither probe demonstrated significant abnormalities in blood cells or indications of impaired liver and kidney function, and all results fell within the normal range (Figure e–I). The results of the study are a first indication of a possible clinical application and safety of the probe. This study defines a lower safety threshold for follow-up investigations, achieving biosafety alongside imaging in the model, thus offering a safe starting point for subsequent dose-escalation studies.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The radionuclide 177Lu was procured from Chengdu Syncor Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The two probes utilized in this study were synthesized by Suzhou Kangming Yongrui Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The experiment utilized a range of other chemicals, all of which were commercial reagents.

Cell and Animal Models

The cell lines used in this study, namely the pancreatic cancer cell line Panc02 and the hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG1–6, were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in accordance with the instructions stipulated by the supplier. The experimental animals were procured from Changzhou Cavins Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China). Female C57BL/6 mice aged between 4 and 6 weeks were used to create subcutaneous tumor models. The injection of Panc02 cells into the axillary region of mice was performed, with in vivo experimentation initiated upon the tumor volume reaching approximately 100 mm3.

Probe Synthesis

The synthesis of both probes was accomplished through the utilization of the Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis method. The purification of both probes was accomplished through the utilization of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using methanol as mobile phase (1 μg/mL). In the HPLC analysis, we used an Agela C18 column (10 μm, 50 × 250 mm). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: mobile phase A: water containing 0.1% TFA; mobile phase B: methanol containing 0.1% TFA; flow rate: 16 mL/min; gradient: 0–3 min (A:95%, B:5%), 4–57 min (A: 95%→0%, B: 5%→100%), 58–60 min (A: 0%, B: 100%); UV detection wavelength: 254 nm. The structures of these samples were characterized using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF, Bruker Daltonics, USA) and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR, AVANCE NEO, Bruker, Switzerland).

Radiolabeling and Radiostability Testing

Radiolabeling of the probe was performed using a molar ratio of 177Lu/PTP-QM-DOTA= 1/50. A solution of 0.5 nmol of the precursor was prepared and mixed with 7.4 MBq of 177LuCl3. This solution was then diluted to 30 μL with 0.4 M sodium acetate buffer, and the pH was adjusted to 4.5. The reaction tube was placed in a thermostatic stirrer (Model YY10, Yunyan, China) and incubated at 90 °C with 800 rpm for 30 min. The radiolabeling efficiency and radiostability in PBS at room temperature were then determined using a radio-thin-layer chromatography system (Scan-RAM, LabLogic, UK).

Fluorescence Performance Testing

In order to investigate the performance of the probe in terms of fluorescence, it was dissolved in 400 μL of pure water, thus preparing a solution with a concentration of 10 μM. The excitation and maximum emission wavelengths were determined by means of a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Biophotometer, Santa Cruz, USA). Furthermore, to investigate the probe was dissolved at a concentration of 10 μM in 400 μL of glycerol–water mixture at varying ratios (glycerol volume ratios: 0%, 10%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 96%), and the fluorescence intensity was measured by a fluorescence spectrometer. Subsequently, the probe was dissolved in 400 μL of pure water at concentrations of 2.5 μM, 5 μM, 20 μM, and 40 μM to detect the fluorescence intensity. The measurement of all samples was conducted using a steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh, UK).

Cell Cytotoxicity Study

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was utilized to evaluate the biosafety of the probe at the cellular level. Panc02 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL in a 96-well plate (100 μL per well) and incubated for 12 h until the cells adhered. The original culture medium was then removed, and fresh medium containing different concentrations of PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA was added for additional 24-h incubation. Subsequent to the removal of the medium, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (MCE, USA) and 90 μL of fresh medium were added to each well. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and then left to incubate for a period of 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Synergy NEO, BioTek, USA). Similarly, using the same way, we tested the survival rate of cells after incubation with 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA of different radioactivity levels (0.037 MBq to 3.7 MBq).

Plectin-1 Expression Testing

In order to confirm the high expression of plectin-1 in the cells, 1 × 105 Panc02 and HepG1–6 cells were seeded in 15 mm confocal culture dishes and incubated for 12 h to allow cell attachment. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 30 min. Following three washes with PBS, the cells were subjected to incubation with an antiplectin-1 primary antibody (1:200 dilution, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for a period of 2 h. Subsequent to the washing step, Alexa Fluor 647-labeled goat secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, Zhenke Bio, Shanghai, China) was applied and incubated for a period of 1 h. Following the completion of the final PBS washes, DAPI staining was performed for nucleic imaging was conducted using a confocal microscope (FV1200, Olympus, Japan), and the images were collected with FV10-ASW (version 4.02) and processed with ImageJ software (version 2.14.0).

In order to validate the expression of plectin-1 in tumor tissues of the animal model, tumors from Panc02 cells subcutaneous tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice (n = 3) were harvested on days 7 and 14 postimplantation, fixed, and sectioned. The same staining and imaging protocol that was used for cellular protein expression validation was applied in this research, images were collected with KFSlideOS (version 1.0.5) and processed with ImageJ software (version 2.14.0).

Cellular Uptake

The cellular targeting and uptake efficiency of the probe were evaluated using cell fluorescence imaging and the average cellular uptake per single cell. Panc02 and HepG1–6 cells (1 × 105 cells) were seeded in 15 mm confocal culture dishes and incubated for 24 h, followed by the addition of 200 μL DMEM containing 20 μM of the probe for a further 2 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min and costained with DAPI for 5 min. Imaging was performed using a confocal microscopy system (FV1200, Olympus, Japan), and all images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ software.

MicroSPECT/CT Imaging Study

In order to evaluate the in vivo performance of the radiolabeled probes, 150 μL of NS (normal saline) containing 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (7.4 MBq, 0.5 nmol) was intravenously injected into Panc02 cells subcutaneous tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice (n = 3). Tumor-bearing mice were scanned using a microSPECT/CT (Milabs, Netherlands) under 1% isoflurane anesthesia at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 4 h postinjection of the radiolabeled probes. The scanned data were then reconstructed using U-SPECT-Rec 2.51g software, after which the distribution of radiolabeled probes was subsequently analyzed quantitatively using PMOD software (version 3.602).

Fluorescence Imaging

In order to investigate the performance of fluorescence imaging, subcutaneous Panc02 cell tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice (n = 3) were intravenously injected with NS containing 100 μL (60 nmol) of PTP-QM-DOTA and HSP-QM-DOTA. The mice were anesthetized using 1% (v/v) isoflurane, and in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 h postinjection by means of an imaging system (IVIS Lumina III, PerkinElmer, USA). Furthermore, at 0.5 h postinjection, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors along with major organs were collected for ex vivo imaging to analyze the fluorescence intensity in various organs. Moreover, in order to analyze the temporal dynamics of the fluorescence signal within tumors, subcutaneous tumors were excised at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 h following probe administration and subjected to fluorescence imaging. For all fluorescence imaging, the excitation and emission filters were set at 440 and 620 nm, respectively. The processing and analysis of all acquired images was conducted utilizing the Living Image software (version 4.2).

Biodistribution Study

In the subcutaneous Panc02 cells subcutaneous tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice (n = 3), NS containing 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA or 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (7.4 MBq, 0.5 nmol) were intravenously injected via tail vein. The mice were euthanized at 0.5 and 1 h postinjection, and the major organs were collected and weighed (n = 3). The γ-counter (LB2111, BERTHOLD, Germany) was utilized to ascertain the magnitude of radioactive counts in diverse tissues and tumors.

Biocompatibility and Pharmacokinetics

In order to investigate the hematological metabolism of the radiolabeled probes in mice, C57BL/6 mice were injected with NS containing either 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA or 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA (3.7 MBq, 0.25 nmol). Blood samples were collected at various time points postinjection via the retro-orbital hemorrhage method, and the radioactivity of the blood samples was measured using the γ-counter (LB2111, BERTHOLD, Germany). The investigation was conducted with the objective of ascertaining the safety of the radiolabeled probes. Twenty-four h after the injection of NS with radioactive probes (14.8 MBq, 120 mol), the mice were anesthetized and subjected to orbital blood sampling. Routine blood tests and hepatic/renal function indicators were measured to assess the acute toxicity of 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA and 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA. Mice were euthanized 1 week after intravenous injection of 100 μL NS containing 60 nmol and 14.8 MBq of either 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA or 177Lu-HSP-QM-DOTA. The major organs were collected, paraffin-embedded, and examined for pathology using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Statistics

The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and assessed for significance using t tests and one-way ANOVA. A p-value of 0.05 was set as the significance threshold, with significance levels marked as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that plectin-1 is highly overexpressed in PDAC and serves as a promising diagnostic target. The dual-modality probe PTP-QM-DOTA was successfully synthesized, exhibiting efficient 177Lu labeling, high radiostability and pronounced AIE properties. In vivo, 177Lu-PTP-QM-DOTA facilitated clear tumor visualization by SPECT within 0.5 h, while its fluorescence signature provided precise intraoperative guidance with minimal background. The probe demonstrated rapid blood clearance, minimal retention in nontarget organs, and no significant appreciable toxicity, underscoring its safety and translational potential. Nevertheless, there is potential for further enhancement in terms of tumor uptake efficiency. In subsequent research, we will modify the probe to extend its short tumor retention time while simultaneously attempting diagnosis and therapy. Future research should concentrate on probe optimization to enhance targeting, evaluation in other plectin-1-overexpressing malignancies, and comprehensive preclinical validation to advance clinical translation.
