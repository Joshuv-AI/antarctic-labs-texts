Screening and biochemical profiling of ‘Mac-07’ crosses for resistance to CLCuD under field and glasshouse conditions
Authors: Sahar Nadeem, Muhammad Kashif Riaz Khan, Amjad Hameed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton is a valuable crop for the textile industry yet, its production is significantly affected by Cotton Leaf Curl Disease (CLCuD), a major cotton constraint. The present study was conducted under field trials and glasshouse conditions to analyze the effect of CLCuD in cotton. Single plant progeny rows (SPPRs) of different cotton accessions were grown in the field. In the glasshouse, two sets of cotton plants were maintained in a controlled environment. One set was kept healthy, while the other was graft-inoculated with a Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCuV) infected plant. After 90 days post-inoculation, SPPRs and grafted plants were screened for symptom development using a disease rating scale from 0 to 6. Estimation of antioxidants and metabolites revealed significant differences in CLCuD-resistant and susceptible varieties. Elevated levels of total phenolic content (TPC), tannins, total oxidant status (TOS), total soluble proteins (TSP), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were observed by CLCuD-susceptible genotypes in the field and glasshouse. In contrast, increased antioxidants for example, peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and, catalase (CAT) were observed in CLCuD- resistant varieties. Under field conditions, CLCuD-resistant varieties showed elevated antioxidant enzymes, with CAT, POD, and APX activities increasing by 32%, 3%, and 8% respectively, while superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity decreased by 25% compared to susceptible lines. Under glasshouse conditions, resistant genotypes showed stronger antioxidant responses than susceptible ones; for instance, POD and APX activities were ~ 62% and ~ 6% higher, respectively, while CAT and SOD increased by 15% and 3%. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the field experiment indicated that five key factors contributed to 80.26% of the variation observed among genotypes. Analysis of the glasshouse experiment explained 74.24% of the total cumulative variability. These factors were identified as the most influential in explaining differences in morphological and biochemical traits. In our study, genotypes Mac-07, T7-1-2, and T7-2-5, showed high chlorophyll a, lycopene, TPC, tannins, MDA, and antioxidant enzymes in the field. Under glasshouse conditions, their un-inoculated plants exhibited elevated level of chlorophyll a and b, total chlorophyll, lycopene, APX, SOD, CAT, and POD. Overall, Mac-07, T7-1-2, and T7-2-5 demonstrated superior performance against CLCuD across both conditions and can be considered strong candidates for future CLCuD-resistant cotton breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07250-x.Keywords: Gossypium hirsutum, Mac-07, Cotton leaf curl disease (CLCuD), Field and glasshouse studies, SPPRs, Biochemical profiling, Antioxidant metabolites, Plant-virus interaction, Resistance breeding

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is a prominent oilseed and fiber crop, substantially grown for its crucial role in the oil and textile industries, making its importance undeniable. Pakistan is ranked 5th in cotton production [1]. A sum of 65% of raw cotton material contributes to the textile industry [2]. In many countries, it is referred to as “white gold” because of its ability to generate substantial profits, by-products, and edible oil [3]. However, despite its economic importance, cotton production faces several challenges that threaten its profitability and sustainability.

Cotton Leaf Curl Disease (CLCuD) is the most dominant concern of cotton in Pakistan and India [4, 5]. It has become a major threat to cotton, causing up to 80–87% crop loss in the sub-continent [6–11]. CLCuD is caused by Cotton Leaf Curl Virus (CLCuV). CLCuV (genus: Begomovirus; family: Geminiviridae), is a single-stranded DNA plant virus transmitted by the whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), which is widespread in the Indian subcontinent [12–15]. The whitefly is an indiscriminate feeder, facilitating the rapid and proficient spread of CLCuV from infected plant hosts to neighboring crops [16, 17]. CLCuV is transmitted in a circulative, tenacious manner by the whiteflies [18]. The complex interaction between cotton plants, whiteflies, and begomoviruses emphasizes the importance of comprehending and effectively managing these factors to mitigate the impact of CLCuD on cotton crops.

CLCuD-affected plants exhibit symptoms such as leaf curling, vein thickening, enation formation, vein yellowing, and stunted growth, along with reduced leaf size and increased brittleness [19–22]. At the seedling stage, CLCuD can reduce the cotton yield by 15–70% [23–27]. Moreover, the presence of βC1 protein in the host plant also impedes the ubiquitination process by interacting with the SKP1/CUL1/F- box complex, which disrupts the signaling pathways of plant hormones [28, 29]. This disruption leads to the accumulation of virus and appearance of symptoms in the infected cotton plants [30].

In order to cope with the stress, developing CLCuD-resistant varieties is essential; however, the emergence of resistance-breaking CLCuV strains has rendered existing resistant germplasm ineffective, underscoring the urgent need to identify new genetic sources for sustainable disease management. In this context, resistance (R) genes represent a promising avenue for enhancing CLCuD resistance. These genes provide targeted resistance against specific viruses eliciting cell death surrounding the infectious plants cells, thereby hampering the viral spread. R genes, viz., R1CLCuDhir and R2CLCuDhir, play a crucial role in CLCuD resistance [31, 32]. A significant example of CLCuD-resistant G. hirsutum accession is Mac-07 which was recognized to hold resistance against CLCuD. A. Hussain et al. (2023) identified a total of 1.2 million InDels and 4.7 million SNPs to be associated with CLCuD resistance by gene ontology and metabolic pathway enrichment. Moreover, RNA-sequenced data obtained from various tissues at varied growth stages revealed genes linked with CLCuD resistance in Mac-07 [33]. Among the resistance mechanisms being explored, recent studies have shown that the plant retinoblastoma-related proteins (RBR) interact with the viral Rep protein to facilitate replication; disrupting this interaction hampers viral replication and may confer resistance against CLCuD [34].

Cotton cultivation is challenged by various stresses, requiring comprehensive screening approaches to identify resilient genotypes. Recent studies on biotic and abiotic stress resistance (salinity, drought, heat stress etc.), using morphological and biochemical markers have emphasized the importance of multi-trait evaluations in stress tolerance breeding [34–38]. Similarly, in the present study, morphological and biochemical traits were assessed to identify genotypes with potential resistance to CLCuD.

For protecting the most important natural fiber producing crop, a mega project aiming at the improvement of genetics of the cotton plant for combating CLCuD, was initiated in 2011 under Pak-US (managed through ICARDA, Pakistan) joint venture program. In these studies, a resistant accession namely Mac-07 was identified along with other resistant cotton lines. A latest study at NIAB (Nuclear Institute for Agriculture and Biology), Faisalabad, Pakistan, also proved the resistance of Mac-07 through grafting against prevailing CLCuD complex. Recently at NIAB, a breeding programme was also initiated to develop high yielding CLCuD resistant/tolerant germplasm using Mac-07. The current study focuses on evaluating Mac-07 derived cotton cross combinations for their response to CLCuD under diverse screening methods. Combined evaluation of cotton genotypes using SPPRs, grafting under glasshouse conditions, disease scale assessments, PCR-based viral detection, and biochemical profiling enables reliable identification of CLCuD-resistant and susceptible cotton varieties.

The objectives of our current study are: (1) to compare the level of CLCuD resistance among Mac-07 based cotton cross combinations using disease severity rating under field and glasshouse conditions. (2) To validate the presence of CLCuV PCR-based viral detection. (3) To evaluate the relative effectiveness of SPPRs and grafting techniques in differentiating CLCuD-resistant and susceptible genotypes. (4) To quantify and compare changes in key biochemical markers (e.g., total phenols, antioxidant enzymes, chlorophyll content) in response to CLCuD infection across field and glasshouse environments. (5) To identify statistically significant differences in resistance or susceptibility among genotypes, providing potential candidates for resistant cotton breeding.

Materials and methods

The current study was carried out at the Cotton Genomics and Marker Assisted Breeding Laboratories in the Plant Breeding and Genetics Division (PBGD), and Phytopathology Lab at the Plant Protection Division, NIAB, Faisalabad. The research was performed under field conditions of NIAB (Latitude: 31.3989° N, Longitude: 73.0331° E) and glasshouse conditions.

Experimental material

Seeds of five cotton parent genotypes and 31 hybrids used in this study were harvested and provided by the Phytopathology Lab and Cotton Genomics Lab, NIAB, Faisalabad, Pakistan.

Field evaluation (SPPRs method)

The field experiment was conducted over two consecutive years (2021 and 2022) using a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with three replications. Thirty-six cotton genotypes (including Mac-07 crosses) were evaluated through SPPRs, with 10 plants per genotype in each replication. Genotypes were randomly arranged using a non-repetitive layout to minimize positional bias. To allow natural disease pressure, whitefly populations were not controlled throughout the season. Grafting procedure in the glasshouse

Seeds from SPPRs tested in the field were also grown in a whitefly-free glasshouse to observe the response of different cotton genotypes against CLCuD. Plants were grown at a temperature range of 30 ± 2 °C during the day and 20 ± 2 °C at night, with a relative humidity of 60–70%.

For the glasshouse experiment, two sets were established—grafted and healthy—each consisting of 10 plants per genotype. One set of plants was maintained in whitefly-free conditions, designated as a control or healthy set. The other set was graft-inoculated with CLCuV. With the aid of a scalpel, a sliced cut was produced at the growing tip of the plant. A fresh branch from the CLCuV infected plant was detached, and a similar cut was also produced on this branch. A parafilm was used to join together the cut surfaces. For the water source, a test tube filled with distilled water was used for the CLCuV-affected symptomatic grafted cut branch. Distilled water was maintained in the test tubes for seven consecutive days. After a week, the tubes were removed. Grafting success, CLCuV transmission, and symptom development were observed regularly. The experiment was continued for 90 days post grafting [40, 41]. Genotype V-5 was designated as CLCuD- susceptibility check based on its previously documented high susceptibility to CLCuD in our earlier studies conducted under similar trials. Its consistent disease response makes it a reliable standard for evaluating resistance in tested genotypes.

PCR confirmation of CLCuV

Leaf tissue samples of cotton genotypes were collected from the field and glasshouse.

Total DNA was isolated from cotton genotypes using the CTAB (Cetyltrimethylammonium Bromide) protocol. The isolated DNA was used as template in PCR using CLCuV isolate coat protein primer, which are useful of CLCuV detection and identification [32, 42]. Universal primers based on conserved sequences as follows were used for PCR: forward primer (5′-CCGTGCTGCTGCCCCCATTGTCCGCGTCAC-3′) and reverse primer (5′- CTGCCACAACCATGGATTCACGCACAGGG-3′) [43–45].

Each 20 µl of PCR reaction mixture consisted of 10 µl PCR Master Mix (Thermoscientific Dreamtaq™ Green, LT-02241, Lithuania), 1.0 µl forward and 1.0 µl reverse primer (Molecular Biology Products), 2 µl DNA template and 6 µl nuclease free water. Thermal profile was set for the initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 33 cycles (denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 45 s) and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were separated on 1.5% agarose gel and stained with EtBr. Bands were observed using UVI-pro gel documentation system.

PCR confirmation of CLCuV- β satellite

After CLCuV confirmation, cotton genotypes from the field and glasshouse were subjected to another PCR reaction for the confirmation of β-satellite _ a vital disease causing component [46]. Cotton Leaf Curl Multan Virus (CLCuMuV) universal β-satellite forward (5′-GGTACCACTACGCTACGCAGCAGCC-3′) and reverse primers (5′-CACATGGGGACCCTCCCATCCATGG-3′) based on conserved sequence were used [47, 48].

For every sample, 20 µl PCR reaction mixture consisting of 10 µl Master Mix, 1.0 µl each of forward and reverse primer (Molecular Biology Products), 2 µl DNA template and 6 µl nuclease free water was prepared. For the amplification, β-satellite also requires the annealing temperature of 52 °C. Thermal profile was exactly the same as used for CLCuV detection.

Biochemical assays

For biochemical analysis, leaf samples were collected at the flowering stage from the third fully expanded leaf (from the apex) between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM to minimize diurnal fluctuations in metabolite levels. For each genotype, three biological replicates were sampled, and were immediately stored in polyethylene bags, kept on ice, and preserved at − 20 °C. Based on their varying levels of CLCuD susceptibility and resistance, a subset of 17 genotypes was selected for evaluation under field conditions, while 13 genotypes were tested under glasshouse conditions. The selection was made to represent a broad spectrum of disease responses, ensuring a comprehensive assessment under both environmental conditions.

Sample extraction

Leaf samples were extracted in potassium phosphate buffer, then brought to centrifugation at ~ 22,000×g for 10 min. Supernatant was carefully removed and the extract was stored at -20°C for the estimation of biochemical parameters [49, 50]. All enzymatic and non-enzymatic assays described here are based on the UV spectrophotometry method.

Non-enzymatic assays

Measurement of pigments

Plant pigments (chlorophyll a and b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids, and lycopene) were measured using the method of Arnon [51]. For this purpose, leaf extract was prepared by homogenizing 0.075 g of cotton leaves in 80% acetone and incubating at room temperature in the dark. After 24 h of incubation, samples were centrifuged at ~ 11,000×g for 10 min. The absorbance of pigments was taken at 663 nm, 645 nm, 505 nm, 470 nm, and 453 nm.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

TPC was estimated by the method described by Ainsworth et al. (2007) [52]. 150 µl of prepared leaf extract was taken in the Eppendorf tubes, and in it 1.2 ml of Na2CO3 was added. Then, 150 µl of Folin Ciocalteu reagent was added and vortexed. A standard curve was made by measuring the absorbance of TPC by double beam spectrophotometer at 765 nm, and concentration of TPC was calculated in terms of uM/g fresh weight of cotton leaves.

Measurement of tannins

0.05 g of fresh leaf sample was extracted in 500 µl of 95% methanol and incubated at room temperature. After 48 h of incubation, 0.1 g PVP was added in the tubes, which were then centrifuged at ~ 22,000×g for 10 min. The estimation of tannins was done using the method of Burns [53]. Absorbance of tannins was measured at 765 nm by considering tannic acid as standard. Tannin concentration was calculated as µuM/g fresh weight of leaves [54].

Malondialdehyde content (MDA)

MDA was measured by thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent. A 0.125 g leaf extract was homogenized in 5 ml of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The mixture was then centrifuged at ~ 22,000×g for 10 min. 1 ml of supernatant was separated upon centrifugation, and 4 ml of 20% TCA containing 0.5% TBA was added. The homogenate was heated at 95 °C for 30 min and cooled rapidly in an ice bath, then centrifuged at ~ 22,000×g for 10 min. Absorbance of supernatant was estimated at 532 nm, and the value of nonspecific assimilation measured at 600 nm was subtracted. MDA was calculated in terms of µM/g fresh weight of leaves [55].

Total soluble proteins (TSP)

TSP was estimated using Bradford method [56]. The assay procedure contained 5 µl of leaf sample, 95 µl of 0.1N NaCl, and 1 ml Bradford dye. For 5 min, the mixture was kept until the protein–dye complex was formed. The absorbance of proteins was measured at 595 nm. TSP was recorded in mg/g fresh weight.

Total oxidant status (TOS)

TOS was determined by the method suggested by Erel (2005) [57]. For TOS, two reagents were prepared. Reagent I contained 75 µl xylenol orange, 0.4 g NaCl, 5 ml glycerol, and 25 mM H2SO4. The volume was brought up to 50 ml. On the other hand, reagent II was prepared by adding 0.0317 g o-Dianisidine and 0.0196 g ferrous ammonium sulphate. For TOS analysis, the assay contained 900 µl reagent I, 44 µl reagent II and 140 µl sample extract. After five minutes of adding reagent II, optical density was taken at 560 nm. Oxidant status was measured in µM/g fresh weight.

Enzymatic assays

Peroxidase (POD) activity

POD activity was analyzed by the method suggested by Maehly and Chance with certain modifications [58]. Cotton leaf extract was prepared in 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.0). An assay solution for the measurement of POD activity was prepared by adding 535 µl dH2O, 200 mM of 250 µl phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 200 mM of 100 µl guaiacol, 400 mM of 100 µl H2O2, and 15 µl leaf sample extract. The reaction started after adding the enzyme extract. Absorbance was measured at 470 nm, every 20 s (i.e. 0 s, 20 s, 40 s and 60 s, respectively).

One unit of POD activity refers to the change in absorbance per minute and specific activity as enzyme units per gram soluble protein with an extinction coefficient 6.39 mM−1 cm−1. The enzyme activity is expressed as unit per gram of protein [55, 59].

Catalase (CAT) activity

For the measurement of CAT activity, enzyme extract was prepared by emulsifying leaf tissues in 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). CAT estimation was done following the method of Beers and Sizer [60]. A homogenate mixture was prepared by adding 2 ml KH2PO4 buffer, 100 µl of 59 mM H2O2, and 100 µl of leaf sample. The absorbance was measured by UV- spectrophotometer at 240 nm at different time intervals viz., 0 s, 30 s, and 60 s.

H2O2 decomposition is calculated by using an extinction coefficient of 39.4 mM−1 cm−1. One unit of CAT activity is equivalent to 1 mM of H2O2 degraded per minute and is estimated in units per gram of protein [55, 59].

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) assay

APX estimation was done using the method of Dixit et al. (2001) in which 0.5 g of cotton leaves were extracted in 100 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.0), 0.5 M EDTA, 10 mM ascorbic acid, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol [61]. For enzymatic assay, rate of H2O2-dependent oxidation of ascorbate was measured in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.0), 0.6 mM ascorbic acid, and 50 µl of sample extract [55, 62]. The reaction initiated after the addition of 1 ml H2O2 and decrease in ascorbic acid oxidation was determined by measuring absorbance at 290 nm at 0 secs, 30 secs and 60 s.

The specific APX activity is measured as a unit per gram of protein. The extinction coefficient 2.8 mM−1 cm−1 is used to estimate the reaction [59, 63].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay

For SOD determination, the enzyme extract was prepared by homogenizing cotton leaves in 50 mM KH2PO4 buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.1 mM EDTA and 1 mM DTT [61]. The reaction mixture was prepared by adding 250 µl of 0.2 M KH2PO4 buffer, 100 µl L-methane, 100 µl triton-X, 50 µl nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), 50 µl riboflavin, and 50 µl sample extract. Then, the reaction mixture was placed under white light for 10 min. SOD activity was examined by calculating its ability to prevent the photochemical reduction of NBT following the method of Giannopolitis and Ries [64]. SOD was measured at 560 nm in units per gram fresh weight.

One unit of SOD activity is defined as amount of enzyme that led to 50% inhibition of NBT reduction [55, 59].

Statistical analysis

Data collected from all enzymatic and non-enzymatic assays was subjected to two- way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using XL-STAT version 2014.5.03 (Company: Addinsoft Sas, Paris, France). Comparison of means was done by Tukey’s HSD test. ANOVA revealed significant differences among the tested genotypes for biochemical traits. A 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05) was set for the statistical analysis. Values shown here are mean of their replicates ± standard error (SE).

Non-enzymatic assays

Under field conditions, CLCuD- resistant varieties showed significantly higher chl a as compared to susceptible varieties. For instance, Mac-07 showed higher chl a and V5 showed lesser chl a, as shown in Fig. 2a.

Discussion

CLCuD is a major threat to cotton. The major issue in coping with CLCuD is its ability to adapt to its hosts through eluding host defense mechanisms. Changes at the cellular level along with differential gene expression (DEG) as host antiviral response are contributory parameters to disease virulence [32, 69]. The only efficient way so far to handle this stress is the production of CLCuD-resistant varieties [70–72]. The purpose of our current study also focuses on screening and identifying CLCuD-resistant varieties, both by molecular and biochemical means. In both experiments, parent variety Mac-07 appeared CLCuD-resistant whereas rest of the three parent genotypes IR-NIBGE-3701, FH-118 and Tarzan were observed as CLCuD- susceptible. The disease severity level can serve as an indicator for resistance in a plant against a specific pathogen [52, 61, 73, 74].

Lycopene has antioxidant characteristics by which it scavenges free radicals [75]. Lycopene content showed minimal variation among resistant and susceptible genotypes in our study. This trend is comparable to observations by Nadeem et al. (2022), who reported a lack of significant difference in lycopene levels among tomato hybrids affected by Tomato Mosaic Virus (ToMV), suggesting that lycopene may not be a consistent indicator of resistance in these cases [50].

An elevated level of chl a was observed in CLCuD-resistant genotypes, whereas chl b and total chl levels remained statistically insignificant under field conditions. Similarly, a field study by Chauhan et al. (2023) also reported that chl b levels showed no significant variation among cotton genotypes [10].

In the glasshouse experiment, grafted plants of CLCuD-resistant/ tolerant genotypes showed comparatively more chl b and total chl than grafted plants of CLCuD-susceptible genotypes. This is because of the reason that higher levels of chl b help in coping with the virus tolerance mechanism. In an experiment conducted by Siddique et al. (2015), it was found that chl b level was higher in Cotton Leaf Curl Burewala Virus (CLCuBuV) inoculated plants of tolerant genotypes, which also increased total chlorophyll. A possible interpretation is that CLCuBuV tolerant genotypes were able to up-regulate chl b synthesis upon virus infestation, yet CLCuBuV susceptible genotypes were unable to do so. Enhanced chl b levels leading to increased total chl play a key role in CLCuV tolerance mechanism [76].

Carotenoids are primary pigments associated with light harvesting systems [77]. Carotenoids showed no significant differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes in our study. This may indicate that carotenoid levels are more tightly linked to physiological functions rather than defense-related responses [78]. Similar observations were made by Nadeem et al. (2022) in tomato hybrids under ToMV stress, where carotenoid content did not correlate with ToMV resistance [50].

Phenols play a key role in conferring defense to plants. An increased level of polyphenols and phenolic compounds helps plants cope with biotic and abiotic stresses like disease progression, temperature, salinity, etc. [79]. Yet, under glasshouse conditions, virus inoculated plants of resistant genotypes showed more TPC as compared to the viral-inoculated plants of susceptible genotypes. The idea suggests a positive correlation between host resistance and the amount of TPC, which may prove to be a significant reason for the screening of CLCuD-resistant populations [80]. Siddique et al. (2015) recorded a similar behavior between decreased TPC in the CLCuV inoculated plants of susceptible genotypes and increased TPC in the inoculated plants of resistant genotypes [76]. A possible elucidation for lesser TPC in CLCuV-inoculated plants of susceptible genotypes is that several plant pathogens interfere with the plant defense response during successful infection [81]. Our study coincides with the results stated on other host–pathogen interactions, which depicted that certain phenols are lethal for the plant pathogens, because of which the resistant genotypes are more likely to produce higher TPC which accumulates in the plants to fight pathogenic stress [82, 83].

Tannins are polyphenols found abundantly in higher plants. Like phenols, they are also involved in defense responses against pathogens, heat, and drought stresses [84]. The response of tannins was found to be similar to TPC. Under glasshouse conditions, graft-inoculated plants of CLCuD-resistant genotypes showed more tannins than un-grafted plants. Perveen et al. (2001) investigated the biochemical host-plant resistance in cotton and found that resistant varieties showed more phenols and tannins than disease susceptible varieties [85]. Our results are in contrast to the findings of Chauhan et al. (2022) who found that healthy plants show more tannins in contrast to the diseased plants [78].

MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation, is an indicator of cellular damage caused by pathogenic infection [86–89]. MDA upregulates several genes in plants at the time of stress [90]. MDA levels tend to increase in CLCuV-grafted plants as compared to the un-inoculated plants. Karthikyan et al. (2022), in their research conducted on black gram cultivars infected with the urdbean leaf crinkle virus, found MDA level to be higher in infected cultivars [91]. The research suggests that there is a correlation between MDA and plant resistance that could be possibly used as a biochemical tool for studying host–pathogen interactions.

The role of soluble proteins has been reported in many host–pathogen interactions [92, 93]. Generally, infected plants show more soluble proteins, which may be because of pathogen attack and activation of defensive mechanisms in the host [94]. Agrios (1997) suggested that disease-affected plants accumulate more TSP, because of the pathogen attack and activation of plant defense responses [95]. However, the results of our study contrast with these previous findings, as no significant differences in TSP were observed between the genotypes under both field and glasshouse conditions.

TOS is a measure of the whole oxidation status of the living body [96]. Congruently, whole antioxidant capacity is described in terms of total antioxidant status (TAS) [97]. CLCuD- affected plants showed elevated TOS levels under both field and glasshouse experiments. A study conducted by Kaloki et al. (2019) indicated that under stress, plants of sensitive genotypes tend to increase in TOS, which ultimately affects plant yield [98].

Increased Production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) results in cellular damage by lipid peroxidation and ruining the macromolecular structures of proteins, nucleic acids, pigments and lipids [99, 100]. To save the plant from the extent of cellular damage, a series of defense events are activated to neutralize the harmful effects of ROS [101, 102]. Plants can protect themselves from cellular disintegration by the help of antioxidant enzymes like CAT, APX, POD, and SOD [103, 104].

In our field experiment, susceptible genotypes showed increased CAT, POD, APX, and SOD. Zafar et al. (2023) conducted a field-based study on cotton genotypes and reported that susceptible genotypes exhibit elevated level of these antioxidants under stress [105].

However, the response of genotypes to CLCuD in the glasshouse contrasted with the field, where healthy plants of resistant varieties exhibited increased enzyme levels. Many glasshouse studies have revealed increased production of antioxidants in disease resistant varieties. Siddique et al. (2014) and Tariq et al. (2020) quoted similar behaviour of resistant genotypes of cotton displaying more antioxidants than susceptible genotypes under glasshouse conditions [106, 107]. Shamshad et al. (2024) also found the elevated levels of these enzymes in rice cultivars that were found resistant to brown spot disease. Increased production of these antioxidant enzymes can improve a plant’s resistance to systemic viral infection [108].

Antioxidant enzymes, along with MDA—a key indicator of lipid peroxidation—are widely recognized biochemical markers for assessing oxidative stress and plant defense responses. Their differential expression under stress provides critical insight into host resistance mechanisms, as supported by numerous studies in plant-pathogen interaction research [100, 109].

High levels of antioxidant enzymes, along with its demonstrated resistance to CLCuD serves as a valuable donor genotype in breeding programs. These antioxidant traits can be introgressed into susceptible cultivars through conventional or molecular-assisted breeding approaches. Marker-assisted selection (MAS), targeting genes or Quantitative Trait Loci (QTLs) associated with these enzymatic activities can accelerate the development of new CLCuD-resistant varieties. Rahman et al. (2022), in their recent study, highlighted the significance of CRISPR/Cas9 (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats) genome editing techniques in enhancing cotton’s resistance to both abiotic and biotic stresses [110]. The establishment of such modern techniques in cotton has been demonstrated in several studies, primarily using both endogenous and exogenous gene markers such as GFP, DsRed2, GhCLA1, and GhMYB25-like genes [111–113]. The development of transgenic cotton, particularly resistant to insects, diseases, and pesticides, has generated substantial economic benefits over the past two decades [114]. For instance, the Chinese Bt cotton has raised an estimated annual income by 1.5 billion USD [115].

Enhancing CLCuD in cotton remains a significant challenge that requires sustained and extensive efforts. Although considerable progress has been made through various breeding and biotechnological approaches to induce resistance against CLCuD, the development of consistently resistant cultivars continues to demand focused and ongoing research. Through classical breeding methods, resistance traits have been purposefully introduced into cultivated cotton via crosses between diverse allopolyploid and diploid genotypes. These genetic enhancements have provided valuable resources for resistance breeding pipelines, strengthening efforts to develop cotton varieties with improved resilience to viral pathogens and ensuring sustainable cotton production [116, 117]. Systemic acquired resistance (SAR) in cotton was induced through exogenous application of elicitors, including methyl jasmonate, salicylic acid, and benzothiadiazole [118]. Many biotic and abiotic stress-related gene families in cotton, such as R1CLCuDhir and R2CLCuDhir genes in conferring CLCuD resistance [31] and BPC gene family (Basic PentaCysteine Genes) involved in abiotic stress resistance, have been identified as potential targets for developing cotton varieties with enhanced resistance against different stresses. These findings provide a strong foundation for future functional studies and hold significant potential for application in resistance breeding programs aimed at improving cotton’s resilience against CLCuD and other pathogens [119].

Present study provides valuable insights for CLCuD resistance under field and glasshouse conditions, yet there are certain limitations that must be considered. First, glasshouse conditions do not fully mimic the complex biotic and abiotic stresses present in the field. Secondly, the biochemical markers were estimated, yet molecular validation of resistance pathways was not conducted. To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying disease resistance in cotton, additional molecular-level investigations are required.

Conclusion

The present study identified Mac-07, T7-1-2, and T7-2-5 as promising CLCuD-resistant genotypes based on consistent performance under both field and glasshouse conditions. These genotypes maintained higher levels of chlorophyll, lycopene, TPC, tannins and antioxidant enzymes when exposed to CLCuD. While these biochemical and antioxidant traits may reflect an active defense response and hold potential as indicators of CLCuD resistance, their utility as selection markers warrants further validation through multi-location trials and genetic studies.
