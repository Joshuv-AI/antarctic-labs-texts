A study on prolonged use of resuscitative endovascular balloon of the aorta (REBOA) with shunt flow in a porcine hemorrhagic shock model
Authors: Nobuaki Kiriu, Daizoh Saitoh, Koji Yamamura, Ruka Sasa, Tetsuro Kiyozumi, Satoshi Tomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the future efficacy and prolonged availability of double-balloon resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (DB-REBOA) to determine whether bypassing upstream blood at the occlusion site improves survival in a porcine hemorrhagic shock model.MethodsTwenty-five pigs (approximately 40 kg, estimated circulating blood volume 25%) were subjected to hemorrhagic shock by controlled phlebotomy. Animals were treated with standard REBOA (control, n = 12), internal shunt DB-REBOA (internal shunt, n = 4; an intraluminal conduit was set parallel to the DBREBOA in the aorta), or external shunt DB-REBOA (external shunt, n = 9; upstream blood from the occlusion site was shunted by connecting the internal carotid and femoral arteries with a pressure-resistant tube). Zone 1 aortic occlusion was maintained for 90 min, followed by balloon deflation. Survival, systolic blood pressure, and serum lactate levels were analyzed.ResultsTen of 12 control animals developed cardiac arrest shortly after balloon deflation. All animals in the internal shunt group survived, whereas 5 of 9 animals in the external shunt group experienced cardiac arrest. Internal shunt DB-REBOA maintained stable systolic BP during prolonged occlusion, resulting in significantly lower serum lactate levels at 90 min compared to controls. External shunt DB-REBOA resulted in unstable hemodynamics and no significant metabolic benefit.ConclusionPreservation of distal perfusion using an internal shunt markedly improved survival and physiological stability during prolonged Zone 1 aortic occlusion. This strategy may extend the safe duration of REBOA when definitive hemorrhage control is delayed. Introduction

Hemorrhage remains the leading cause of death following traumatic injury. Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) has been introduced as a less invasive alternative to resuscitative thoracotomy for temporary control of non-compressible torso hemorrhage, with the aim of augmenting proximal arterial pressure and cerebral and coronary perfusion [1].

Despite these benefits, concerns have been raised regarding the safety of REBOA, particularly during prolonged Zone 1 occlusion. Observational data from trauma registries suggest an association between REBOA and increased mortality in patients with severe torso trauma [2]. Furthermore, the UK-REBOA randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a strategy incorporating REBOA did not reduce mortality and may even worsen outcomes compared with standard care alone [3]. One proposed explanation is that transient hemodynamic stabilization achieved with REBOA may delay definitive hemorrhage control [4].

Experimental and clinical studies have consistently shown that prolonged complete aortic occlusion results in severe distal ischemia, metabolic acidosis, and cardiovascular collapse after balloon deflation [5, 6]. Consequently, clinical guidelines recommend minimizing the Zone 1 occlusion time, generally to less than 30 min and not exceeding 60 min, as prolonged complete occlusion increases the risk of distal ischemia and metabolic injury according to contemporary clinical practice guidelines [7].

To address this limitation, alternative strategies such as partial REBOA, intermittent REBOA, and modified balloon designs have been proposed, such as bilobed or grooved balloons [8–10]. Experimental and early clinical studies have suggested that partial REBOA reduces distal ischemia and mitigates hemodynamic instability. However, such approaches often require frequent and precise adjustments of balloon inflation to balance proximal pressure support and distal perfusion, which may be difficult to maintain in austere or resource-limited environments such as battlefields.

In the present study, we sought to verify whether irreversible changes occur during a 60-minute observation period after balloon deflation following 90 min of REBOA use. As a potential mitigation strategy, we hypothesized that reducing the afterload on the heart and alleviating ischemia in peripheral areas distal to the occlusion site could be effective. Diverting a portion of the blood flow that is upstream of the balloon occlusion within the aorta downstream—that is, by creating a shunt flow—was anticipated to improve cardiac afterload and organ ischemia. We evaluated a conceptually distinct approach: a double-balloon REBOA (DB-REBOA) system designed to isolate the bleeding segment while preserving distal perfusion via an internal or external shunt, and maintenance of distal blood flow during prolonged Zone 1 occlusion to improve survival and physiological stability in a porcine hemorrhagic shock model. The internal shunt DB-REBOA system differs conceptually from partial REBOA. Rather than relying on continuous manual titration of the balloon volume, it establishes a fixed conduit for distal flow once the balloons are inflated. This passive mechanism may be advantageous in time-intensive or austere settings where continuous expert adjustment of balloon inflation is not feasible.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing

Twenty-five male hybrid pigs (10–12 weeks old, body weight approximately 40 kg) were housed individually under controlled temperature (24 °C) and a 12:12-hour light–dark cycle with free access to food and water.

Anesthesia and perioperative management

The premedication consisted of intramuscular medetomidine (0.04 mg/kg), midazolam (0.3 mg/kg), and butorphanol (0.4 mg/kg). After sedation, intravenous access was established and atropine sulfate (0.5 mg) was administered intravenously. General anesthesia was induced with intravenous alfaxalone (1–2 mg/kg), followed by endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Additional alfaxalone was administered as needed, and neuromuscular blockade was achieved using intravenous rocuronium.

Surgical preparation and vascular access

An arterial catheter was placed in the internal carotid artery for the phlebotomy and proximal arterial pressure monitoring. A central venous catheter was inserted through the external jugular vein. Bilateral femoral artery access was established for REBOA catheter insertion and distal arterial pressure monitoring. Splenectomy and cystostomy were performed to standardize the hemorrhagic response and prevent bladder distension. Correct balloon positioning in Zone 1 was confirmed using fluoroscopy. The structure of the internal shunt DB-REBOA catheter (7 Fr) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. However, preliminary experiments revealed that the catheter lumen of the DB-REBOA was too narrow, preventing sufficient blood flow to achieve an adequate shunt flow after balloon occlusion. Anticipating that enlarging the catheter lumen would eventually achieve adequate blood flow, this experiment explored two methods for generating a shunt flow.

Hemorrhagic shock model

The estimated circulating blood volume was 66 mL/kg. Hemorrhagic shock was induced by controlled phlebotomy at 25% of the circulating blood volume for over 20 min. Maintenance infusion of normal saline was continued throughout the experiment.

REBOA and shunt configurations

The animals were allocated to control (standard REBOA), internal shunt DB-REBOA, or external shunt DB-REBOA groups. In the internal shunt configuration, a parallel intraluminal conduit (a 10 Fr-long sheath with two handmade side ports) allowed blood to flow from the proximal to the distal aorta during occlusion. The 10 Fr-long sheath (Fast-Cath™, Abbot Medical Japan Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with side holes (3 mm×2) positioned distal to the distal balloon was inserted into the femoral artery contralateral to the DB-REBOA catheter insertion site. In the external shunt configuration, the proximal (carotid) and distal (contralateral femoral) arterial sheaths were connected using pressure-resistant tubing, which induces blood flow upstream of the occlusion site (above the upper balloon) to be shunted downstream (below the lower balloon). The schematic diagrams of the internal and external shunt models are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Experimental protocol

Immediately after phlebotomy, the REBOA balloon(s) were inflated, and Zone 1 occlusion was maintained for 90 min. Normal saline infusion was increased to 300 mL/h during inflation. Balloon deflation was performed for 2 min, followed by observation for 60 min (Table 1). Survival was defined as spontaneous circulation with systolic blood pressure ≥ 30 mmHg at 60 min after deflation (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Data collection and statistical analysis

The systolic arterial pressure was continuously recorded. Serum lactate levels were measured at baseline, after phlebotomy, 90 min after inflation, and after deflation.

Survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Systolic blood pressure and serum lactate levels were compared among groups at predefined time points using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When a significant difference was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey method.

A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0.1.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Survival

The survival outcomes are shown in Fig. 1. Ten of the twelve animals in the control group experienced cardiac arrest shortly after balloon deflation. All animals in the internal shunt group survived the observation period, whereas five of the nine animals in the external shunt group developed cardiac arrest.

Discussion

In this porcine hemorrhagic shock model, prolonged Zone 1 aortic occlusion using internal shunt DB-REBOA significantly improved survival, hemodynamic stability, and metabolic status compared with standard REBOA. In contrast, the external shunt strategy failed to confer similar benefits and was associated with marked hemodynamic instability and frequent shunt thrombosis. The following reasons may be considered for these differences: first, the internal shunt likely provided a more stable and continuous distal flow pathway driven by the proximal–distal pressure gradient, whereas the external shunt required an extracorporeal circuit that may have introduced additional resistance and compliance effects. Second, the external shunt circuit was more prone to thrombus formation and intermittent obstruction, which potentially led to unstable or reduced distal perfusion. Third, the internal configuration minimized blood–surface contact outside the vascular lumen, potentially reducing thrombogenicity. These findings suggest that maintaining controlled distal perfusion during prolonged aortic occlusion, rather than complete occlusion alone, is critical for mitigating the detrimental physiological consequences of extended REBOA.

Prolonged REBOA and survival

Standard REBOA is widely used as a bridge to definitive hemorrhage control; however, its safe duration remains limited. Prolonged complete Zone 1 occlusion has been associated with severe ischemia–reperfusion injury, cardiovascular collapse after balloon deflation, and increased mortality [5, 6]. Therefore, clinical guidelines recommend minimizing the occlusion time, often to less than 30 min. The present control group findings are consistent with these observations, as the majority of animals developed cardiac arrest shortly after balloon deflation after 90 min of complete occlusion.

In contrast, all animals treated with internal shunt DB-REBOA survived the observation period, despite prolonged occlusion. This finding suggests that the deleterious effects traditionally attributed to prolonged REBOA are not solely a function of occlusion time itself but rather the physiological consequences of sustained distal ischemia and subsequent reperfusion. The preservation of distal perfusion appears to fundamentally alter the tolerance of the organism to prolonged aortic occlusion.

Hemodynamic and metabolic implications of distal perfusion

The internal shunt DB-REBOA strategy maintained stable systolic blood pressure throughout the occlusion period while simultaneously reducing metabolic derangement, as evidenced by lower serum lactate levels. This dual benefit highlights the important distinction between proximal pressure augmentation and true physiological resuscitation. While standard REBOA increases afterload and proximal perfusion pressure, it does so at the cost of complete distal ischemia, progressive metabolic acidosis, and abrupt cardiovascular collapse after deflation [6].

In contrast, preservation of distal flow through an internal shunt likely attenuates ischemia of the abdominal organs and lower extremities, thereby reducing lactate accumulation and blunting the systemic inflammatory and metabolic responses to reperfusion. These effects may explain the improved hemodynamic stability observed after balloon deflation in the internal shunt group. Consequently, the Compass® device—which can be attached to the side port of the REBOA insertion sheath and enables continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring for distal flow—is considered effective in clinical practice.

Implications in the context of recent clinical trials

Recent clinical studies, including the UK-REBOA randomized controlled trial, have raised concerns that REBOA may not improve or may even worsen outcomes in some trauma populations [3]. One proposed mechanism is that transient improvements in vital signs may delay definitive hemorrhage control [4]. Our findings do not contradict these observations; rather, they address various clinical problems, such as abrupt hemodynamic collapse after balloon deflation, severe proximal hypertension during prolonged aortic occlusion, and metabolic deterioration associated with complete distal ischemia. When definitive hemorrhage control is delayed, strategies to mitigate the physiological effects of prolonged REBOA are essential.

The internal shunt DB-REBOA concept should therefore be viewed not as an alternative to definitive hemorrhage control, but as a potential adjunct to extend the safe window of temporary stabilization when immediate surgical or endovascular intervention is not available.

Clinical and operational relevance

Clinicians with delayed evacuation and limited access to definitive care increasingly face situations such as prolonged field care, disaster response, and battlefield medicine [11–13]. In such cases, complete aortic occlusion over an extended period may be unavoidable. While partial REBOA relies on the manual titration of balloon inflation to allow limited distal flow, DB-REBOA may provide a more stable and reproducible distal perfusion pathway without requiring continuous operator adjustment. Thus, DB-REBOA may reduce operator workload and potentially improve reproducibility in prolonged or resource-limited settings. Our findings suggest that preserving distal perfusion during aortic occlusion may substantially improve physiological tolerance and survival, thus providing a potential strategy for bridging patients to definitive care under challenging conditions.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, this study has unequal sample sizes across experimental groups. The experiments were performed sequentially as part of the development process for the DB-REBOA device. Because this device was a prototype with substantial manufacturing costs, the number of experiments in the internal shunt group was limited. Consequently, the findings from the internal shunt group should be interpreted as preliminary proof-of-concept observations rather than definitive comparative evidence.

Secondly, this experimental animal model may not fully replicate the complexity of human traumatic hemorrhage. Third, the prototype catheter requires frequent saline flushing to maintain shunt patency, indicating that further device refinement is necessary before clinical application. Fourth, delayed mortality and organ failure, especially in relation to intestinal ischemia, may occur beyond the 60-minute observation period. Autopsies were performed in all cases, including fatal ones, and none of them showed grossly evident intestinal necrosis. Therefore, we did not perform a microscopic examination. Future studies that incorporate longer observation periods and histopathological evaluation will be necessary to characterize long-term outcomes better.

Conclusions

In this study, the double-balloon configuration allowed partial redistribution of blood flow while maintaining proximal perfusion pressure, which may have contributed to improved systemic stability. Importantly, DB-REBOA is not intended to replace partial REBOA but rather to provide an alternative strategy in situations where partial balloon control is technically difficult or where prolonged occlusion may be unavoidable.

Taken together, these findings suggest that preserving even limited distal perfusion may be beneficial during prolonged REBOA, and that device modifications enabling controlled distal flow may represent a promising direction for future investigation.
