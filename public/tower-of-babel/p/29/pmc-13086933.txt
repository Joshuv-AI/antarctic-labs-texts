Evaluation of static oxidation characteristics and analysis of displacement efficiency during air injection in light oil reservoirs
Authors: Zhezhi Liu, Bowen Yang, Suhan Zhang, Bin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Air injection oil displacement is a highly promising enhanced oil recovery technology due to its wide gas source availability, low cost and strong reservoir adaptability. To enhance the application effectiveness of air injection for low-temperature oxidation (LTO) flooding in a specific light oil reservoir in China’s Xinjiang oilfield, this study systematically investigates the static oxidation characteristics of crude oil and their influence on oil displacement performance. High-pressure static oxidation experiments and long-core physical simulation tests were conducted to examine the effects of oxidation time, water saturation, oxygen concentration, medium oil components (C7–C17), and reservoir minerals on the LTO behavior of crude oil. The oil displacement efficiency under different permeability conditions during air flooding was also analyzed. The results indicate that the LTO process exhibits stage-wise characteristics: in the initial stage, oxygen consumption is rapid, light and medium components decrease while heavy components increase, leading to higher viscosity and density of the crude oil. Reservoir conditions significantly influence oxidation pathways—high water saturation, low oxygen concentration, and high medium components inhibit oxidation, whereas reservoir cuttings promote cracking reactions due to their catalytic effect, resulting in oil lightening. Long-core flooding experiments further reveal that air flooding is more suitable for formations with relatively high permeability, which helps delay gas channeling, improve sweep efficiency, and thus achieve higher oil recovery. This research clarifies the intrinsic relationship between oxidation reactions and oil displacement performance during air injection, providing a theoretical basis for the application of air injection technology in light oil reservoirs.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of continuously growing global energy demand and increasing challenges in developing conventional oil and gas resources, enhanced oil recovery (EOR) technologies have become a critical pathway for sustaining the sustainable development of oil fields. Statistics indicate that over 50% of the original oil in place remains underground due to the difficulties associated with extraction and cannot be recovered through conventional methods1,2. Particularly in China, newly proven reserves predominantly come from low-permeability, ultra-low-permeability, and tight oil reservoirs, where water flooding often faces challenges such as poor injectability and producibility3–5. Notably, the coupled effects of key reservoir parameters (e.g., pore pressure, permeability, and water saturation) further influence fluid flow and development efficiency6,7. Therefore, exploring efficient displacement media and technologies suitable for low-permeability reservoirs is of significant importance.

Among various EOR methods, gas injection is regarded as an effective approach for developing light oil reservoirs due to its favorable flow control characteristics and sweep efficiency. Compared to water flooding, gas molecules are smaller and can more easily penetrate nanoscale pores, effectively replenishing formation energy2. Additionally, gases exhibit higher compressibility, and the elastic energy released during depressurization can further enhance oil displacement efficiency. Numerical simulation studies have shown that single development strategies have limited adaptability, while composite displacement strategies are often superior to single displacement, enabling dual improvements in displacement efficiency and sweep efficiency8. However, commonly used gases such as natural gas and CO2 face limitations in large-scale application due to constraints in gas source availability, cost, and technical requirements9. CO2 capture and storage involve high costs, natural gas resources are limited, and nitrogen is relatively expensive. In contrast, air offers the distinct advantages of wide availability, on-site accessibility, and low cost, making it particularly suitable for water-scarce regions and water-sensitive reservoirs10,11. Estimates indicate that the gas cost per ton of oil produced is nearly zero for air, approximately 400–600 yuan for oxygen-reduced air, while exceeding 1200 yuan for CO2, 4000 yuan for natural gas, and 2000 yuan for nitrogen, demonstrating the significant economic competitiveness of air12.

Light oil reservoirs typically exhibit characteristics such as low viscosity and favorable fluidity. However, issues including strong heterogeneity and relatively low water flooding efficiency constrain the ultimate recovery factor13. During air injection, low-temperature oxidation (LTO) reactions occur between crude oil and oxygen. This process not only generates drive gases such as N2 and CO2 but also enhances oil mobility through oxidation-induced modification, thereby improving oil recovery14. Nevertheless, the LTO reaction pathways involved in air injection are complex and influenced by various factors such as crude oil composition, reservoir minerals, water saturation, and oxygen concentration. The underlying mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, and the applicability of the dynamic balance law between capillary and viscous forces on gas channeling and sweep efficiency15 in the coupled system of air flooding and low-temperature oxidation still needs verification, which limits the further application of this technology.

Currently, numerous scholars have conducted a series of studies on the LTO behavior of crude oil. Liao et al. first divided the crude oil oxidation process during air injection into four temperature intervals, providing a theoretical basis for the technical applicability in different reservoir types12. Gao et al. confirmed through LTO and long-core displacement experiments that pressurization promotes oxygen consumption and CO/CO2 generation in crude oil. Their air flooding and subsequent composite flooding achieved oil displacement efficiencies of 40.17% and 51.44%, respectively, providing experimental support for transitioning high-water-cut reservoirs to air flooding16. Luo et al. revealed the order of influence of pressure, temperature, and water saturation on crude oil oxidation behavior, pointing out that air flooding exhibits good potential for enhanced oil recovery in low-permeability reservoirs but also carries risks of gas channeling17. Qi et al. studied the thermal-kinetic behavior of light crude oil during oxygen-reduced air flooding, finding a negative correlation between oxygen concentration and activation energy, thereby confirming the advantage of air flooding in recovery under high-temperature conditions18. Pan et al. focused on the LTO characteristics of shale oil, indicating that reservoir cuttings significantly catalyze oxidation reactions19. Zhang analyzed the oxidation mechanism of heavy oil from a fractionation perspective, emphasizing the crucial role of heavy components during the high-temperature oxidation stage20. Qian et al. clarified through physical simulation experiments the existence of a “gas channeling turning point” during air flooding and its impact on recovery, suggesting that transitioning high-water-cut reservoirs to air flooding still holds potential for enhancing oil recovery21. Li et al.22 systematically investigated the effects of temperature and pressure on crude oil viscosity and SARA composition through high-pressure static oxidation experiments, finding that as temperature increases, crude oil viscosity rises, and aromatic hydrocarbons participate in reactions converting into asphaltenes, leading to crude oil heavy-end upgrading. Qi et al.23 analyzed changes in the molecular structure of crude oil before and after oxidation using FT-ICR MS and GC-MS, indicating that LTO reactions generate a large number of oxygen-containing compounds (such as carboxylic acids, ketones, and aldehydes), accompanied by the generation of CO, CO2, and CH4, forming a flue gas-like oil displacement effect. Huang et al.24 revealed through core flooding experiments the dual effects of thermal impact and crude oil viscosity increase during LTO, pointing out that the feasibility of air injection technology depends on the balance between these two effects on recovery.

While existing studies have advanced the understanding of LTO behavior of crude oil in an air atmosphere25–28, systematic research on oxidation behavior under low-temperature, static conditions and its intrinsic relationship with displacement efficiency remains insufficient, particularly within the specific context of air injection in light oil reservoirs. Especially under the coupled effects of multiple factors in actual reservoirs, the pathways of oxidation reactions, product distribution, and the mechanisms governing the evolution of crude oil physical properties are still not well elucidated. Furthermore, existing research has largely focused on the dynamics of gas flooding and ultimate recovery performance, whereas the underlying mechanisms of how oxidation reactions influence crude oil composition, fluidity, and final displacement efficiency are not adequately understood. Additionally, the intrinsic relationship among frontal advancement, gas channeling behavior, and oxidation-induced oil modification during air flooding in reservoirs with different permeability characteristics requires further in-depth investigation.

Based on this, using light crude oil from the Xinjiang Oilfield in China as the research subject, this study comprehensively conducted high-pressure static oxidation experiments and long-core physical simulation experiments. It focused on investigating the effects of oxidation time, water saturation, oxygen concentration, medium oil components (C7–C17), and reservoir minerals on LTO behavior, and analyzed the oil displacement efficiency and recovery performance of air flooding under different permeability conditions. The aim is to reveal the key mechanisms of oxidation reactions during air injection in light oil reservoirs and their impact on displacement performance, in order to provide theoretical basis and technical support for field application.

Experimental section

Experimental materials and apparatus

This study utilized dehydrated crude oil from the Xinjiang Oilfield as the experimental oil sample, with a viscosity of 6.92 mPa·s (at 102 °C) and a density of 0.859 g/cm³ (at 25 °C). To investigate the influence of C7–C17 components on crude oil oxidation, a comparative oil sample was prepared. First, the original crude oil (with a C7–C17 mass fraction of 54.03%) was distilled to remove a portion of its native C7–C17 fraction, obtaining a base oil. Then, a C7–C17 alkane mixture was added to this base oil at a mass fraction of 9.5%, increasing the total mass fraction of medium components to approximately 58% to create an effective contrast. Experimental gases included dry air and three types of oxygen-reduced air with oxygen concentrations of 3%, 8%, and 13%, respectively. The experimental water was produced formation water, classified as a NaHCO₃ water type, with a total salinity of 21,831 mg/L. Rock cuttings were obtained from core samples of the oil-producing intervals in the field, crushed to 160–200 mesh, and dried for later use. Their mineral composition, as shown in Table 1, primarily includes quartz, plagioclase, and minor clay minerals. For the long-core displacement experiments, a set of artificial cores with gas-measured permeabilities of 5, 15, 30, and 50 mD, respectively, were employed.

The core experimental equipment included a high-temperature and high-pressure static oxidation device and a DGM-III multi-functional core displacement apparatus (Jiangsu Hai’an Petroleum Instrument Company, China). The oxidation device featured a stainless steel oxidation reactor (specifications: 0.8 cm × 1.4 cm × 100 cm) capable of withstanding temperatures up to 600 °C and pressures up to 80 MPa. The displacement system was equipped with a constant-temperature oven, an oil-free air compressor, a constant-rate/constant-pressure pump, and a long-core holder (core assembly length: 0.8 m; maximum working pressure: 70 MPa; temperature: 200 °C), allowing real-time pressure monitoring. Additionally, the experiments utilized Agilent 6890B and 7890B series gas chromatographs, a DMA48 density meter, a DV-III viscometer, along with auxiliary components such as six-port valves, back-pressure regulators, gas mass flow controllers, and volume metering devices. Results and discussion

Study on the characteristics of LTO reactions in crude oil

Effect of time on crude oil oxidation

The analysis results of the produced gas indicate that the crude oil reacted rapidly with oxygen during the initial stage of LTO. The oxygen concentration decreased significantly from an initial 21% to 13.73% within the first 11 days (as shown in Fig. 4), suggesting that this period represents the primary oxygen consumption phase of the oxidation reaction. Subsequently, the oxygen consumption rate gradually slowed, with the oxygen content further declining to 9.46% by day 60. Concurrently, the CO2 concentration increased rapidly in the early stage and then stabilized, indicating a transition of the oxidation reaction from intense to moderate. This change is primarily attributed to free radical chain reactions and oxygenation reactions between the light components in the crude oil and oxygen, generating oxygenated intermediates (such as carboxylic acids, aldehydes, and ketones), which subsequently release CO2 through decarboxylation or further oxidation29–31. As the reaction progressed, the reactive components were gradually consumed, leading to a corresponding decrease in both the oxygen consumption and gas generation rates.

Fig. 4Variation of crude oil oxidation product gases with time.

Variation of crude oil oxidation product gases with time.

Further analysis of hydrocarbon components (Fig. 5) indicates that as oxidation time increased, the proportions of light (C5–C6) and medium (C7–C17) components gradually decreased, while the proportion of heavy (C18–C35+) components continuously increased. By day 11, the heavy components had increased by approximately 9.37%, indicating that low-temperature oxidation promotes condensation and aromatization reactions among crude oil molecules, leading to the formation of higher molecular weight aromatic structures and heterocyclic compounds, thereby driving the crude oil toward heavier composition.

Fig. 5Distribution characteristics of hydrocarbon components in crude oil under different oxidation times.

Distribution characteristics of hydrocarbon components in crude oil under different oxidation times.

To further investigate the changes in molecular structure during the oxidation process, infrared spectroscopy analysis was performed on samples from different oxidation stages (as depicted in Fig. 6; Table 3). The spectrum of the pre-oxidation sample already exhibited weak absorption peaks in the 4000–3500 cm−1 range, attributable to minor oxygen or nitrogen-containing functional groups. As the oxidation reaction progressed, absorption peaks in regions such as 3800–3200 cm−1 and 1735–1520 cm−1 were significantly enhanced, indicating the continuous generation of oxygenated compounds including alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids32. Furthermore, signals for olefins and aromatic structures intensified in the 3200–3000 cm−1 region, and the out-of-plane deformation vibration of aromatic rings at 723 cm−1 became more pronounced, further confirming the formation of aromatized and condensed ring structures33,34. These structural changes lead to stronger intermolecular forces, which macroscopically manifest as an increase in crude oil density and viscosity (Fig. 7), indicating that the oxidation reaction significantly alters the physical properties of the crude oil.

Effect of water saturation on crude oil oxidation

Experimental results under different water saturation conditions (as shown in Table 4) indicate that the presence of water significantly inhibits the oxidation reaction of crude oil. As the water saturation increased from 0% to 90%, both the oxygen consumption and CO2 production gradually decreased. This reflects that the aqueous phase impedes effective contact between oxygen and crude oil and partially dissolves oxygen, thereby reducing the oxidation intensity35. As shown in Fig. 8, at a water saturation of 30%, the proportion of heavy components in the oxidation products increased, and the density and viscosity of the crude oil rose significantly, indicating that condensation polymerization remained the dominant pathway. However, when the water saturation reached 60% or higher, the degree of oxidation reaction markedly weakened, with negligible changes observed in hydrocarbon composition and crude oil physical properties. This suggests that the crude oil largely retains its initial characteristics under high water saturation conditions.

Effect of oxygen concentration on crude oil oxidation

To investigate the influence of oxygen concentration on the LTO behavior of crude oil, a series of oxidation experiments were conducted under varying oxygen concentrations. The results (Table 5) show that as the oxygen concentration in the gas decreased, both the oxygen consumption capacity and CO2 generation of the crude oil significantly declined. This phenomenon can be attributed to the reduced dissolution of oxygen into the crude oil under low-oxygen conditions, which limits effective contact with reactive oil components. Consequently, oxygenation reactions become insufficient, the formation of intermediate oxygenated compounds is suppressed, and the overall oxidation intensity is weakened36,37.

Further analysis of the hydrocarbon component distribution (Fig. 9) revealed that after LTO, the crude oil generally exhibited a decrease in light and medium components and an increase in heavy components. However, under low-oxygen conditions, the extent of these compositional changes was markedly reduced, indicating that oxygen concentration plays a critical regulatory role in the degree of LTO, directly influencing the oxidation pathway and final product distribution. In summary, oxygen concentration is a decisive factor governing the extent of the low-temperature oxidation reaction, exerting a significant impact on both reaction intensity and product composition.

Effect of medium components on crude oil oxidation

By comparing the oxidation behavior of the crude oil and a prepared oil sample enriched with medium components (C7–C17), it was found that the oxygen consumption in the prepared oil system was significantly lower. The oxygen concentration in the produced gas increased to 16.35%, with oxygen consumption reduced by approximately 2.04% compared to the crude oil. Concurrently, CO2 generation decreased from 0.85% to 0.37%, indicating that both oxygenation reactions and carbon-chain cleavage processes were suppressed. The C7–C17 components consist primarily of structurally stable alkanes, naphthenes, and aromatics. Their higher content reduces the overall reactivity of the system, thereby inhibiting oxidation efficiency38. Hydrocarbon component distribution results (Fig. 10) further show that although both oil samples exhibited a trend of decreasing light and medium components and increasing heavy components after oxidation, the magnitude of change across carbon number ranges was significantly smaller in the prepared oil. This reflects that the “inerting effect” of medium components substantially restricts the molecular structure heavy-end upgrading and property alterations during the oxidation process39.

Effect of reservoir rock on crude oil oxidation

To investigate the role of reservoir rock in the LTO process of crude oil, this study compared the oxidation characteristics of crude oil alone and a crude oil–reservoir rock system under identical conditions. The results indicate that after an oxidation period of 10 days, the addition of reservoir rock increased the oxygen consumption of the crude oil from 6.69% to 8.18%, while the CO2 concentration in the produced gas rose from 0.85% to 1.12%. These changes suggest that both oxygenation reactions and carbon-chain cleavage processes were enhanced, leading to a deeper degree of LTO and an accelerated oxidation reaction pathway, thereby shortening the auto-ignition delay time. Analysis of the mineral composition of the reservoir cuttings reveals the presence of 3.41% clay minerals. Their high specific surface area and surface acidic sites (including both Brønsted and Lewis acid sites) significantly promote contact between crude oil and oxygen molecules and, through adsorption, reduce the bond dissociation energy of C–C bonds in crude oil molecules40, particularly enabling selective catalytic cracking of heavy components such as C20+ into lighter and medium hydrocarbons in the C5–C17 range41,42. Meanwhile, the high-pressure environment of 40 MPa in the experiment further compresses the contact distance between crude oil molecules and the mineral surface, compensating for the insufficient reaction energy at low temperature and allowing the cracking reaction rate to exceed that of condensation polymerization during LTO43, which macroscopically manifests as lightening and viscosity reduction of the crude oil. In contrast, in the baseline experiment without rock cuttings, the crude oil underwent only oxidative condensation polymerization due to the absence of acid-site-mediated cracking pathways, resulting in a trend toward heavier components. With the addition of reservoir cuttings, cracking became dominant, shifting the hydrocarbon distribution toward lighter and medium components (as seen in Fig. 11) and markedly reducing the heavy fractions. Additionally, the increase in total gas consumption lowered the system’s pressure-maintenance capacity, leading to a significant pressure drop in the reactor by the end of the oxidation experiment, which has important implications for the feasibility and reaction efficiency of air flooding. In summary, although condensation polymerization continues in the presence of cuttings, cracking reactions dominate the oxidation process, ultimately resulting in decreased density and viscosity of the oxidized crude oil.

Experimental study on air flooding in long cores through physical modeling

To clarify the mechanism by which permeability influences the effectiveness of air flooding, this study selected four sets of cores with different permeabilities (labeled 1# to 4#), whose basic model parameters are listed in Table 8. Long-core displacement experiments were conducted under simulated reservoir conditions (102 °C, 40 MPa), and the flooding performance before and after gas injection is shown in Fig. 12.

By monitoring the injection pressure differential and the gas-liquid production ratio (as shown in Fig. 13), the dynamic behavior of gas flooding was systematically analyzed. Prior to gas breakthrough, the injection pressure continuously increased while the gas-liquid production ratio remained relatively stable. After breakthrough, the injection pressure dropped rapidly, and the pressure differential gradually decreased before stabilizing. This phenomenon can be attributed to the viscous resistance of the rock to gas and the hindering effect of gas-liquid interfacial tension on gas movement during the early stage of gas flooding, causing gas to accumulate at the core inlet and pressure to rise continuously44. As preferential flow channels for gas developed within the reservoir, the flow paths became increasingly concentrated in larger pores. The gas flow velocity significantly exceeded that of the liquid, leading to a decrease in gas density at the inlet, followed by a reduction and eventual stabilization of the pressure differential. Concurrently, the gas-liquid production ratio increased rapidly after breakthrough, indicating enhanced gas mobility and improved displacement efficiency.

Further analysis of the displacement pressure differential under different permeability conditions reveals that the low-permeability core (1#) exhibited a significantly higher pressure differential compared to the high-permeability core (4#). In core 1#, the maximum displacement pressure differential reached 8.18 MPa, markedly exceeding that of the other cores. The primary reason is that the low-permeability core possesses a more compact pore structure with smaller pore throats, leading to enhanced capillary forces and increased resistance to gas migration, which results in a higher initiation pressure differential45,46. Furthermore, under the same injection linear velocity, lower permeability correlates with greater flow resistance for gas within the core, consequently elevating the displacement pressure differential. Therefore, core permeability is a key factor influencing the air displacement pressure differential as well as gas and liquid production behavior. Low-permeability conditions contribute to a higher initiation pressure differential and stronger gas retention effects, significantly impacting oil displacement efficiency.

The relationships between displacement efficiency and oil recovery factor versus injected pore volume (PV) are shown in Figs. 14 and 15, respectively. Figure 14 indicates that a distinct “oil bank” phenomenon occurred during the gas flooding process, characterized by a rapid increase in displacement efficiency after gas breakthrough, followed by a sharp decline and eventual stabilization after gas channeling. The oil within this “oil bank” primarily consists of two components: first, oil reactivated due to the expansion effect of air on the reservoir oil, making some residual oil mobile again; and second, remaining oil located in micropores that is difficult to access by water flooding. Compared to conventional water injection, air, with its smaller molecular size, can more easily penetrate microfractures and micropores in low-permeability reservoirs, thereby effectively displacing the remaining oil that is hard to reach by water flooding. This significantly improves both sweep efficiency and displacement efficiency, ultimately enhancing the oil recovery factor.

As shown in Fig. 15, within the low-permeability range covered by the experiments, higher core permeability corresponds to a higher final oil recovery factor achieved by air flooding. Specifically, the oil recovery factors for cores 1# to 4# during air flooding were 14.06%, 15.32%, 17.31%, and 17.43%, respectively. Core 4# (permeability: 14.16 mD) achieved the highest recovery, indicating that under certain low-permeability conditions, higher permeability facilitates more uniform gas front advancement, delays gas channeling, and thus improves displacement efficiency. It should be specially noted that the conclusion of relatively high-permeability cores delaying gas channeling obtained in this study is only applicable to the low-permeability reservoir interval of 1.09–14.16 mD, which is essentially different from the gas channeling law of medium-high permeability reservoirs (permeability > 100 mD). For tight cores with permeability around 5 mD, their pore throats are extremely small, and capillary force becomes the dominant factor restricting gas flow. Injected gas tends to accumulate at the inlet and form local dominant flow channels, thus triggering rapid gas channeling. In contrast, cores with permeability around 14 mD have relatively developed pore throats, and the constraint of capillary force is weakened, allowing gas to advance uniformly and avoiding the premature formation of dominant channels. On the contrary, medium-high permeability reservoirs have large pore throats where the influence of capillary force is negligible, and gas flow is controlled by viscous force. High permeability will accelerate gas breakthrough along large pores, and this law is not applicable to the low-permeability reservoir range in this study. However, it should be noted that as permeability increases further, the risk of gas channeling may also intensify. Therefore, in practical applications, gas injection strategies need to be optimized considering reservoir heterogeneity47.
