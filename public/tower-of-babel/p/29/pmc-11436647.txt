Microbe-drug association prediction model based on graph convolution and attention networks
Authors: Bo Wang, Tongxuan Wang, Xiaoxin Du, Jingwei Li, Junqi Wang, Peilong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The human microbiome plays a key role in drug development and precision medicine, but understanding its complex interactions with drugs remains a challenge. Identifying microbe-drug associations not only enhances our understanding of their mechanisms but also aids in drug discovery and repurposing. Traditional experiments are expensive and time-consuming, making computational methods for predicting microbe-drug associations a new trend. Currently, computational methods specifically designed for this task are still scarce. Therefore, to address the shortcomings of traditional experimental methods in predicting potential microbe-drug associations, this paper proposes a new prediction model named GCNATMDA. The model combines two deep learning models, Graph Convolutional Network and Graph Attention Network, and aims to reveal potential relationships between microbes and drugs by learning related features. Thus improve the efficiency and accuracy of prediction. We first integrated the microbe-drug association matrix from the existing dataset, and then combined the calculated microbe-drug characteristic matrix as the model input. The GCN module is used to dig deeper into the potential characterization of microbes and drugs, while the GAT module further learns the more complex interactions between them and generates the corresponding score matrix. The experimental results show that the GCNATMDA model achieves 96.59% and 93.01% in AUC and AUPR evaluation indexes, respectively, which is significantly better than the existing prediction models. In addition, the reliability of the prediction results is verified by a series of experiments.

Introduction

The human body harbors a vast array of microbes, primarily consisting of bacteria, but also including archaea, fungi, viruses, and protozoa. These microorganisms inhabit various organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, oral cavity, stomach, skin, and genitourinary tract. Normally, the human microbiome colonizes these organs, forming healthy symbiotic or parasitic relationships with the host1. Research has shown that microbes play an indispensable role in human activities, significantly impacting human health regulation. For example, they promote the body's metabolism2, produce essential vitamins and genetic products3, and prevent the invasion of pathogens4. Thus, dysbiosis or imbalance of the body's microbiome could lead to the occurrence of human diseases, such as obesity5, diabetes6, vaginitis7, and even cancer8. Therefore, using drugs to regulate the state of the microbiome within the human body represents a new trend in medical treatment.

Microbes and drugs complement each other in the treatment of human diseases. On the one hand, multiple studies have shown that microbes are involved in the absorption and metabolism of drugs, thereby regulating the efficacy and properties of drugs9. For example, enzymes from colonic bacteria catalyze the reduction of the drug sulfasalazine to 5-aminosalicylic acid, thereby inducing an anti-inflammatory effect10. On the other hand, drugs also alter the diversity and functionality of microbial communities in the human body. For example, the diabetes medication metformin induces microbial metabolism, which may contribute to its therapeutic effects11. However, everything has two sides. The interaction between microbes and drugs can also lead to the spread of superbugs, posing a new threat to human health12. Therefore, it is urgent to identify new potential associations between microbes and drugs to more effectively address this challenge.

Related work

Modern medical researchers aim to further investigate whether certain drugs have resistance against various microbes. However, traditional laboratory analyses are time-consuming and costly, making deep learning methods a complementary approach for predicting potential microbe-drug associations. For instance, Dehghan et al. developed a deep learning model called CCL-DTI13, which mainly explores improved methods for drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction. DTI prediction uses drug molecules and protein sequences as inputs to predict binding affinity values and improves the performance of the task prediction module by using a contrastive loss function to learn a more discriminative feature space. Rafiei et al. proposed the CFSSynergy14 model, which improves the accuracy of drug synergy prediction by combining features and similarity methods. This model first extracts features of drugs and cell lines, then calculates the similarity between these features, and finally uses XGBoost for prediction. Zhu and others incorporated the KATZ metric to detect possible associations between microbes and drugs, designing a predictive model named HMDAKATZ15. Long and colleagues proposed a method named GCN-MDA16, which infers potential associations between microbes and drugs by adding Conditional Random Fields (CRF) to Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN). Furthermore, in 2021, Zhu and others introduced a new predictive method based on Laplacian Regularized Least Squares, named LRLSMDA17, which can effectively discover potential associations between microbe-drug pairs. Later, Long and others constructed a Graph Attention Network framework named EGATMDA18 for potential microbe-drug association prediction. Utilizing novel heterogeneous graph neural networks, they proposed a new framework for heterogeneous network embedding representation HENRMDA19, integrating metapath-2vec and bipartite network recommendations for predicting microbe-drug associations by incorporating biomedical data from various sources to construct heterogeneous networks of microbes and drugs.

Inspired by the aforementioned methods, this paper proposes a model named GCNATMDA, which leverages a combination of Graph Convolutional Networks and Graph Attention Networks to predict potential microbe-drug associations. The framework structure diagram of the GCNATMDA model is shown in Fig. 1. In the first part, we calculate and construct multiple similarity matrices, including Gaussian kernel spectral similarity based on microbes and drugs, microbial protein sequence similarity, and drug chemical structure similarity. These similarities are fused separately and input into the graph convolutional network (GCN) together with the binary association matrix of microbes and drugs. The second part is based on GCN (Graph Convolutional Network) to learn the high-dimensional features of microorganisms and drugs, as GCN can effectively process graph-structured data, it extracts rich and complex feature representations by utilizing the feature matrices of microorganisms and drugs, and captures for the microorganism and drug complex interaction associations between microbes and drugs and transformed such associations into low-dimensional potential association matrices; And in the third part is based on the Graph Attention Network (GAT). The model effectively integrates information between nodes through a multi-head attention mechanism. Each head captures different relational features, which are then concatenated to form a comprehensive node representation. Specifically, the new feature vector for each node is obtained by learning the weights between adjacent nodes, which are calculated through the attention mechanism, indicating the importance of information transmission between nodes. Finally, the model uses these enriched node representations to reconstruct the microbial drug score matrix. This step involves interacting the latent feature vectors of each microbe and drug pair to predict their association strength. The reconstructed matrix displays the potential association scores between each microbe and each drug, where cells with higher scores indicate strong potential associations, and those with lower scores indicate the opposite.Fig. 1GCNATMDA framework diagram.

GCNATMDA framework diagram.

Overall, our model has the following advantages:

Multi-source Information Fusion: GCNATMDA is capable of integrating a variety of biological and chemical information, including microbial protein sequence similarity, drug chemical structure similarity, and Gaussian kernel spectral similarity, providing the model with a rich biological context and chemical features.

Application of Graph Convolutional Networks: The model employs Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) to effectively process graph-structured data, mining complex feature representations and potential interaction relationships between microbes and drugs through iterative propagation and updating of node features.

Graph Attention Mechanism: Through the Graph Attention Network (GAT) and multi-head attention mechanisms, the model dynamically allocates weights during the aggregation of node features. Each "head" captures different relational features, which are then concatenated to form a comprehensive node representation. This enables global-level information integration, further enhancing the model's capability to capture features.

Reconstruction of Potential Association Matrix: Lastly, the model utilizes these enriched node representations to reconstruct the microbe-drug score matrix, predicting the association strength between each pair of microbes and drugs. Higher scores in the matrix indicate strong potential associations.

Drug chemical structure similarity

Based on the existing relevant information, we first calculate the structural similarity of drugs through their chemical structures. Initially, the molecular formula of a drug is converted into its corresponding SMILES23,24 representation, where SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) is a textual string notation for representing chemical structures. This notation facilitates computer processing and possesses uniqueness, avoiding the occurrence of isomers, thereby making the calculation results more accurate. Subsequently, we utilize the Chemical Development Kit (CDK)25 to calculate the features of compounds. Finally, the similarity is calculated using the Tanimoto coefficient by comparing the fingerprints of two compounds. A fingerprint is a binary representation of a compound, where each bit represents the presence or absence of a specific chemical feature. The Tanimoto coefficient26 measures the proportion of shared features between two fingerprint keys to quantify similarity. The similarity metrics are calculated using the formula shown in Eq. Graph convolutional networks

Construction of graph convolutional networks

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)28 are a multi-layer neural network architecture specifically designed for learning the structure of graph data. GCNs effectively capture and learn the low-dimensional feature representation of nodes in a graph through a multilayer approach. In each layer, GCN combines the adjacency matrix with the nodes' features, performing a series of operations to update the nodes' features. These operations include linear transformations (such as the product of a weight matrix and node features) and nonlinear transformations (such as activation functions), allowing each layer to aggregate information from neighboring nodes. Through this layered structure, GCNs can not only represent the relationships between each node and its direct neighbors but also capture higher-level topological structures between nodes layer by layer, thereby generating a rich feature representation for each node.

Results

Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis

In the GCNATMDA model, there are several hyperparameters. To explore the impact of these hyperparameters, we performed fivefold cross-validation on the MDAD dataset to observe the trend of performance changes, which allowed us to adjust their values and compare various evaluation metrics. The results are illustrated in Fig. 2. We compared four relatively important parameters from the GCNATMDA model: the number of hidden layers in the GCN, the number of nodes in the hidden layer units, the number of attention heads in the GAT, and the value of the L2 regularization coefficient. Generally, increasing the number of layers and units in the GCN can aggregate more node information. However, excessive increments in hidden layers and units per layer can lead to overfitting and meaningless latent representations, affecting the model's expressive power and computational complexity. We tested the number of hidden layers in the GCN module and set the size of the hidden layers to 16, 32, 64, and 128. As shown in Fig. 2a, b, the model's evaluation metrics peaked when the number of hidden layers was 3 and the unit number was 64. Beyond these values, the evaluation metrics declined due to increased model complexity, which caused the model to learn noise in the data instead of its latent distribution, leading to overfitting. Moreover, we assessed the model's metrics with different numbers of attention heads in the GAT module and varying l2 regularization coefficients. In Fig. 2c, the metrics improved with the growth of l2_coef, peaking at 0.003, then sharply declined. In Fig. 2d, the number of attention heads affected the frequency of weighted aggregation by the attention mechanism. Each attention head could learn different attention weights, highlighting the relationships between different nodes. However, too many or too few attention heads can affect the model's expressive ability, stability, and generalizability. Therefore, we set the number of attention heads in GAT to 3.Fig. 2Analysis of the influence of hyperparameters on the performance of GCNATMDA.

Analysis of the influence of hyperparameters on the performance of GCNATMDA.

Comparison with other methods

We benchmarked the GCNATMDA model against five methods: HMDAKATZ, GCNMDA, EGATMDA, HNERMDA, and NIRBMMDA. A brief summary of these methods is as follows:

HMDAKATZ is specifically designed to predict microbe-drug associations based on the KATZ metric.

GCNMDA is a method for predicting associations between human microbes and drugs, based on GCN and Conditional Random Fields.

EGATMDA predicts human microbe-drug associations by constructing a graph attention network.

HNERMDA introduces a heterogeneous network embedding representation for predicting associations between human microbes and drugs.

NIRBMMDA proposes a computational model based on Neighborhood Inference (NI) and Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) to predict potential microbe-drug interactions.

We ran these methods on the MDAD dataset using the default model-specific parameters for these methods and adjusted their hyperparameters. We performed fivefold cross-validation for all methods. Known microbe-drug associations were used as positive samples, while negative samples in the training and test sets were randomly generated. To reduce the error of random sampling, each comparative method was repeated 5 times, and the final AUC and AUPR scores were calculated based on the average of these 5 iterations. The results of multiple sets of comparative experiments are shown in Fig. 3. Our GCNATMDA model achieved the highest AUC value of 0.9659 and an AUPR value of 0.9301. EGATMDA followed with an AUC value of 0.9586 and an AUPR of 0.926. The lowest scores were observed for HMDAKATZ, which had an AUC of 0.901 and an AUPR of 0.886, indicating a comparative underperformance in the predictive task. Statistical tests confirmed that the differences in AUC and AUPR scores between the models were statistically significant, with p-values less than 0.01, validating the superior performance of GCNATMDA over the other methods.Fig. 3Performance metrics of GCNATMDA and five other methods on the MDAD dataset.

Performance metrics of GCNATMDA and five other methods on the MDAD dataset.

Performance evaluation on two independent datasets

To validate the performance and robustness of our model, GCNATMDA, on other independent datasets, we tested it on two datasets: aBiofilm and DrugVirus. We compared it with several other relevant methods. The experimental results, as shown in the figure, reveal that GCNATMDA achieved significant performance improvements on both datasets. Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the differences in performance metrics, and the results indicated that the improvements by GCNATMDA were statistically significant, with p-values less than 0.01, demonstrating robust enhancements over the competing methods.

In the aBiofilm dataset, the performance of GCNATMDA was particularly notable, with AUC and AUPR values reaching 0.957 and 0.922, respectively, in fivefold cross-validation. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 4. These results not only outperformed traditional methods such as KATZHMDA, HNERMDA, and NIRBMMDA but also surpassed the more recently proposed methods GCNMDA and EGATMDA. The experimental comparisons across different databases show that while other advanced comparative methods also have good predictive results, they still have some limitations. First, these methods tend to be simplistic when mining the potential complex relationships and intensities between nodes, lacking advanced techniques for detailed feature extraction and learning of association strengths. Secondly, methods such as EGATMDA and HNERMDA attempt to construct heterogeneous networks, but there is still room for improvement in processing and learning the features of heterogeneous networks. On the other hand, GCNATMDA not only considers the association matrix between microbes and drugs, integrating drug chemical structure similarity, microbial protein sequence similarity, and Gaussian kernel interaction similarity, these multidimensional data integrations help to more comprehensively understand the complex interactions between microbes and drugs. Moreover, through the combined use of graph convolutional neural networks and graph attention networks, the model can effectively learn deeper and more complex feature representations from the graph structure. Finally, using the graph attention network, the model can dynamically learn and adjust weights between the nodes of microbes and drugs, helping the model to focus on the most important node relationships, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of predictions.

Ablation study

To verify the importance of each module, we developed two variant models for comparison: GCNATMDA-LatentFeature and GCNATMDA-Attention. Specifically, GCNATMDA-LatentFeature removes the latent features of microbes and drugs computed by the GCN module; GCNATMDA-Attention omits the attention mechanism from GCNATMDA to observe its impact on performance. The ablation studies were conducted on the MDAD dataset.

Figure 6 compares GCNATMDA-LatentFeature, GCNATMDA-Attention, and the original model, highlighting the highest values. It is evident from the results that omitting either of these two modules significantly reduces the overall performance of the model, especially in terms of AUC, AUPR, and accuracy. Specifically, the removal of GCNATMDA-LatentFeature led to a decline in model performance because the GCN module is capable of learning valuable latent feature representations of nodes from the entire graph. Without it, these features are no longer provided to the attention module, causing the model to lose a critical source of information and thus diminishing its ability to recognize node relationships. On the other hand, the removal of GCNATMDA-Attention also resulted in a significant performance drop. The graph attention module, by dynamically allocating weights, focuses on specific information in different parts of the graph, enhancing the model's discriminative ability at the node level. It helps the model to concentrate on the most valuable node connections within the graph, thereby improving the model's prediction accuracy. Without this module, the model lacks the capability to perform such fine-grained feature focus and selection, leading to an overall decrease in performance.Fig. 6GCNATMDA compared to its variant models.

Discussion

Due to the rising antimicrobial drug resistance and the lengthy, expensive process of new drug development, an increasing number of researchers are focusing on the prediction of potential microbe-drug associations using computational models. In this paper, we propose the GCNATMDA model that identifies potential microbe-drug associations by combining graph convolutional networks with graph attention networks.

The excellent performance of GCNATMDA is attributed to several key factors. First, GCNATMDA fuses multi-source information, providing the model with a rich biological context and chemical features, enabling it to accurately identify and predict potential associations between microbes and drugs. Second, by applying graph convolutional networks, the model effectively learns the nodal features of microbes and drugs from the graph structure. Through iterative propagation and updating of nodal features, it captures the complex topological structures and implicit connections between nodes. Lastly, the graph attention network allows the model to dynamically allocate attention weights during the aggregation of node features, enabling global-level information integration.

From a biological perspective, GCNATMDA not only enhances the model's prediction accuracy but also increases its biological interpretability by providing detailed information about the interactions between drugs and microbes. This is highly valuable for researchers when designing experiments and validating model predictions. In the real world, it could also help guide experimental biologists in exploring new antibiotics or adjusting existing treatment plans, especially in the face of increasing antibiotic resistance. However, we must acknowledge the challenges in translating computational predictions into experimental validations and clinical applications. The predictions made by the current model need to be verified through rigorous biological experiments. Future research will focus on improving the model's predictive efficiency and reducing its reliance on large-scale validation data, while also seeking more collaborative opportunities to integrate these computational tools with experimental methods.

Conclusion

In this study, we present a model named GCNATMDA, designed to accurately predict potential associations between microbes and drugs. The motivation for this research stems from the high cost and time-consuming nature of traditional experimental methods, and the inefficacy of existing computational methods in effectively capturing and utilizing complex structural information in large heterogeneous networks. By integrating graph convolutional and graph attention mechanisms, our model aims to address these issues, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and efficiency. Compared to traditional computational approaches, the innovation of our model lies in its ability to effectively extract and utilize latent features of microbes and drugs through graph structural algorithms, incorporating crucial topological information to boost the model's ability to capture complex interaction networks. The application of graph convolutional networks allows our model to delve deeply into the complex interplay between microbes and drugs, while the introduction of graph attention networks enables the model to dynamically allocate weights to the importance of interactions between nodes, leading to adaptive learning and more precise predictions.

This work primarily targets the limitations of existing methods in feature extraction. Specifically, current models often overlook potential relationships within complex network structures, whereas our GCNATMDA model leverages the power of deep learning to fully mine these latent microbe-drug interactions, providing a richer biological information feature representation. Moreover, our method fine-tunes the weight of each association through GAT, significantly enhancing the accuracy of drug target predictions. Experimental results on three datasets demonstrate that our model surpasses the performance of both traditional and other advanced methods, validating its superiority in predicting microbe-drug associations. These improvements not only propel the research progress in the field of microbe-drug association prediction but also provide a powerful tool for precision medicine and new drug development.

Despite the excellent computational performance of our model, we recognize that it has certain limitations. While constructing the similarity framework for microbes and drugs, although we have considered relevant biological and chemical prior information, we see the potential to further enhance predictive capability by expanding other data integrations. Additionally, we note that incomplete information for certain microbes and drugs may also impact the model's predictive accuracy.

In the future, we plan to include associations between microbes-diseases and diseases-drugs in our prediction framework. We aim to further enrich the input features by fusing multi-source information from different domains and constructing more refined biological network models, thereby significantly enhancing the predictive capacity of our model. We also hope that researchers will supplement the currently missing data for microbes and drugs to complete the database and improve the overall performance of the model.
