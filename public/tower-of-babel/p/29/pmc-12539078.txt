Enhancing fruit quality of cold-stressed physalis peruviana during late-season through spraying with phenylalanine, cysteine, and selenium
Authors: Arezoo Akbari, Taher Barzegar, Vali Rabiei, Silvana Nicola, Siamak Shirani Bidabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow temperatures are a significant environmental factor that can adversely impact the growth and productivity of tropical plants, such as Cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.). In the current experiment, the foliar application of amino acids L-phenylalanine (Phe), L-cysteine (Cys), or sodium selenite (Se) was investigated on qualitative and biochemical properties of Cape gooseberry fruits subjected to low temperature condition in 2-year field experiment (2021 and 2022).ResultsOur findings indicated that low temperature stress reduced membrane stability index and vitamin C content of fruits in comparison to the control plants in both years. Nonetheless, MDA, H2O2 and antioxidant enzymes activity meaningfully enhanced as a consequence of low temperature conditions. Application of Phe, Cys and Se alleviated low temperature stress by reducing MDA accumulation and significantly increasing total soluble solids, proline, total phenols, and flavonoid content. Amino acid Phe, Cys, and Se positively influenced H2O2 accumulation by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities. Furthermore, these treatments stimulated the production of proline, and activities of CAT, SOD, POD, and PAL enzymes, which helped mitigate the damaging effects of cold stress and improved fruit quality. Overall, foliar application of Phe, Cys, and Se enhanced antioxidant components, enzyme activities, and proline accumulation, leading to improved fruit quality in Cape gooseberry cultivated under cold stress conditions.ConclusionOverall, the foliar application of Phe, Cys, and Se enhanced antioxidant compounds, enzyme activities, and proline accumulation, contributing to improved membrane stability and fruit quality in Cape gooseberry plants grown under cold stress conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07467-w.

Background

Cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.) is a species in the Solanaceae family, notable for its distinctive fruit development, as the fruit remains enclosed within the calyx throughout its growth and ripening stages [17]. Physalis originated from South America and is now commercially cultivated in various tropical and subtropical countries [28]. Fischer et al. [33] noted that Cape gooseberry thrives in temperatures ranging from 16 to 23 °C, with humidity levels of 70 to 80% and annual rainfall between 1000 and 1800 mm.

Cold stress is a critical abiotic factor that adversely affects plant growth, crop yield, fruit quality, and geographic distribution [77]. Crops cultivated in open environments frequently encounter various abiotic stress throughout their growth stages. Under such conditions, plants undergo a range of morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular alterations to confront these challenges [45]. Yan et al. [81] reported that exposure to low temperatures can slow the uptake of essential nutrients, thereby restricting plant growth. The adverse effects of chilling stress on plants are likely due to the initiation of oxidative stress. Cold exposure markedly increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in plant cells and promotes lipid peroxidation of membranes [44]. Gill and Tuteja [35] noted that excessive production of ROS is highly toxic to plants, causing damage to proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and DNA, ultimately leading to cell death. The plant antioxidative defense system plays a crucial role in mitigating the adverse impacts of ROS generated by chilling stress [44, 76].

Various strategies such as breeding for enhanced chilling tolerance, genetic engineering, adjusting crop management techniques, and applying chemical treatments such as chemical fertilizers (soil- and foliar-applied), biostimulants (amino acids, symbiotic bacteria and algae, etc.) and other compounds have been employed to enhance chilling tolerance and prevent chilling injury [47]. However, these strategies are not always effective in preventing chilling injury during both preharvest and postharvest stages. The application of amino acids via foliar spraying is a modern method utilized to enhance plant growth and productivity [23]. This approach is effective because amino acids directly contribute to increasing protein levels, which are the building blocks of essential enzymes, including various antioxidant enzymes and non-enzymatic compounds involved in regulating metabolic processes and activating antioxidants [10, 68]. The importance of nitrogen derived from amino acids, provided in substantial amounts, as an effective means to enhance growth and performance in agricultural crops is well recognized. Moreover, both nitrogen and amino acids are essential components for protein synthesis [32]. The importance of amino acids also arise from their widespread utilization in the biosynthesis of a wide range of non-protein nitrogenous substances, such as pigments, vitamins, coenzymes, and purine and pyrimidine bases [32].

Amino acids are fundamental building blocks in protein structures, which essential for plant growth and development. These proteins significantly influence the production of various plant metabolites, such as enzymes, vitamins, alkaloids, and terpenoids [62]. Previous studies have shown that spraying basil plants with various amino acids can significantly enhance growth and yield [73]. Research by Sarojnee et al. [67] demonstrated that applying amino acids to pepper (Capsicum annum L.) plants led to notable improvements in plant height, canopy diameter, number of branches, shoot dry matter, vitamin C content and marketable yield compared to the control. Additionally, using the amino acid phenylalanine (Phe) as a foliar spray before harvest can increase phenolic compounds, including anthocyanins and flavonoids, in grape fruits [60]. Application of Phe at a 5 mM concentration significantly enhanced cold injury resistance in tomato fruits by preserving membrane integrity. This preservation was associated with a reduction in electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde accumulation [2]. The elevation in phenols and flavonoids accumulation is associated with increased activity of the phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) enzyme. Simultaneously, increased vitamin C accumulation boosts the antioxidant capacity, crucial for maintaining membrane integrity in cold-stressed tomatoes [2]. Phe serves as a precursor for synthesis of anthocyanidins (flavonoid structure) and enhances anthocyanin and antioxidant activities [4]. Phe is an interesting amino acid, serving not only as a nitrogen molecule but also as a precursor in the synthesis of phenolic compounds through phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway [36]. Aghdam et al. [2] reported that Phe treatment under cold stress in tomato is associated with enhanced activities of ROS-scavenging enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX, and GR), resulting in reduced H₂O₂ levels and increased ascorbic acid accumulation. Activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway, indicated by higher PAL activity, also led to increased phenolic and flavonoid content and improved DPPH scavenging capacity, which may contribute to cold stress tolerance. The phenylpropanoid pathway originates from phenylalanine and produces various compounds including: flavonols, dihydrochalcones, flavones, flavanones, and anthocyanins, that many of them exhibit antioxidant properties [50]. Several studies have demonstrated the vital role of antioxidant systems in safeguarding plants from oxidative damage induced by cold stress. Modulating these antioxidants with an external agent could increase plant antioxidant enzyme activity enhancing cold tolerance [19].

Cysteine (Cys) is a thiol-containing amino acid with an amino group (NH2), carboxylic acid (COOH), and sulfhydryl group (SH) as reactive centers. This unique structure makes Cys a powerful antioxidant and an effective ROS scavenger. The thiol side chain easily oxidizes and provides protection against oxidative damage caused by biotic and abiotic stresses [29]. Due to the presence of thiol, Cys acts as a potent antioxidant against reactive oxygen species that prevents damage induced by stress [34]. Cys treatment-maintained levels of soluble solids, titratable acidity, vitamin C, as well as activity of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzymes in litchi fruit [9]. Plum fruits treated with Cys exhibited higher levels of vitamin C, anthocyanin, phenol, flavonoid, and antioxidant activity compared to control during storage at low temperatures [71]. However, limited research exists on the impact of Phe and Cys on chilling injury of fruits and vegetables before harvesting.

Selenium (Se) is a vital component of seleno-amino acids and selenoproteins, playing crucial roles in cell growth and function with significant biological importance for animals and humans [61]. Se shares properties closely resembling those of sulfur and fulfill the same biochemical functions. The uptake, translocation, and metabolism of Se mimic those of sulfur, and substituting sulfur with Se results in Se analog compounds that enhance Se content in plants [61]. Selenium’s ability to enhance the antioxidant defense mechanisms in plants has been well documented, showing beneficial effects on various quality parameters of tomato fruits, including increased soluble solid content, titratable acidity, and total sugar content [52, 87]. Se application in tomato fruits has also been shown to enhance the presence of pigments and antioxidant compounds [59, 88]. Additionally, Se application has led to increase in fruit weight and number, firmness, selenium content, and lycopene content of tomatoes [52].

Spring and autumn chilling are common in the temperate regions of Iran. As a warm-season vegetable, Cape gooseberry is sensitive to cold stress at all stages of growth and development. The occurrence of low temperatures in spring, coinciding with transplanting, and in autumn, during the maturation and ripening of Cape gooseberry fruits, causes chilling injury and crop damage. Although the beneficial effects of biostimulants on plants are well recognized, there is currently no definitive data clarifying the roles of Phe, Cys, and Se in Cape gooseberry fruits subjected to low temperature stress. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the physiological effects of different levels of Phe, Cys, and Se in alleviating the detrimental impacts of low temperature on the qualitative and biochemical traits of Cape gooseberry fruits.

Carotenoids content

The interaction effect of year and treatments had a significant impact (P ≤ 1%) on fruit carotenoid content (Table 1). In both years, foliar application of Phe, Cys and Se significantly increased carotenoid content in fruit of plants exposed to low temperature conditions (Tables 1 and 2). The highest carotenoid content (0.0378 and 0.0369 mg g−1FW) was observed in fruit of plants treated with 0.5 mM Cys in 2021, and with 0.75 and 2.5 mM Phe in 2022. However, there was no significant difference between plants subjected to low temperature (CLT) and control plants (Table 2).

Membrane stability index

The data presented in Table 1; Fig. 1 show the membrane stability index (MSI) of fruit from plants treated with different concentrations of Phe, Cys, and Se over two years under low-temperature conditions. The statistical analysis revealed that the interaction between treatments and year had a significant effect (P ≤ 0.01) on the MSI of Cape gooseberry fruit (Table 1). The results showed that low temperature stress decreased the fruit MSI, while foliar spraying with Phe, Cys and Se improved MSI under low temperature conditions. The highest MSI values (38.46 and 36.01) were observed in fruits from plants treated with 0.5 and 0.25 mg L−1 Se in 2022 (Fig. 1).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) contents

The data in Table 1; Fig. 2 display the MDA and H2O2 contents of fruits treated with different concentration of Phe, Cys and Se over two years under low temperature conditions. Generally, in both years, low temperature stress significantly (P ≤ 0.01) increased MDA and H2O2 accumulation in fruits compared to control plants (Fig. 2A and B). However, the effects of Phe, Cys and Se on MDA and H2O2 contents depended on the treatments concentration. The minimum values of MDA content (12.48 and 12.58 µmol g−1FW) were obtained in fruit of control plants in 2022 and 2021, respectively (Fig. 2A). Additionally, H₂O₂ content of fruits in plants grown in 2022 was significantly higher than in those grown in 2021. The lowest H2O2 contents (1.64 and 1.79 µmol g−1FW) were observed in fruits of plans treated with 0.5 mg L−1 Se and control plants, respectively (Fig. 2B).

Total phenol and flavonoid content

As shown by the statistical analysis in Table 1, the interaction effect of foliar treatments and year on total phenol and flavonoid contents was significant (P ≤ 0.01). Based on the results, low-temperature stress significantly increased the total phenol and flavonoid contents of fruits compared to control plant fruits in both years (Table 3). Moreover, total phenol and flavonoid contents in the first year were significantly higher than those in the second year. Foliar application of all Phe, Cys and Se treatments significantly enhanced the total phenol and flavonoid contents of fruits compared to both CLT and control plants in 2021 (Table 3). The highest total phenols (18.4 mg g−1FW) and flavonoid (3.86 mg g−1FW) contents were obtained in fruit of plants treated with 2.5 and 0.75 mM Phe, respectively, in 2021 (Table 3).

Vitamin C content

As shown in Table 1; Fig. 3, the vitamin C content of fruits in the first year was significantly higher than in the second year. In both years, low temperature stress significantly (P ≤ 0.01) decreased vitamin C content of fruits compared to control and treated plants. In addition, foliar application of Phe, Cys and Se increased the vitamin C content of Cape gooseberry fruits under low temperature conditions in both years. The highest vitamin C content (91.56 mg 100 g−1 FW) was found in fruits of plants treated with 0.5 mM Cys under low temperature stress in 2021 (Fig. 3).

Antioxidant capacity

Statistical analysis revealed a significant interaction between foliar treatments and year on antioxidant capacity (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 1). The results showed a notable increase in the antioxidant capacity of Cape gooseberry fruit subjected to cold stress and treated with amino acids Phe, Cys, and selenium in both 2021 and 2022. The fruit of plants treated with 0.5 mM Cys exhibited the highest antioxidant capacity (74.11%) in 2021 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Cape gooseberry is valued for its unique flavor and nutritional benefits, including high levels of vitamins and antioxidants. However, Cape gooseberry plants are sensitive to low-temperature stress, which can significantly affect fruit quality. Exposure to cold conditions during fruit development often results in poor coloration, reduced sweetness, and decreased firmness of the fruits [3, 28]. Our results indicated that foliar application of Phe, Cys and Se under low temperature conditions enhanced fruit TSS and TA contents in Cape gooseberry (Table 2). Tissue dehydration occurs when there is an imbalance between root water uptake and leaf transpiration, resulting in water deficiency in plant tissues during drought and low-temperature stress conditions [14]. The reduction in water availability caused by low temperatures can lead to an increase in the level of soluble solids in plants. Numerous studies have shown that soluble sugar content in Arabidopsis leaves significantly increases during cold acclimation [49, 66]. In agreement with our results, previous studies have shown that the application of amino acids in strawberries [56] and tomatoes [38] led to increased levels of TA and TSS contents compared to the control group. Similarly, foliar application of selenium in apples enhanced both TSS and TA contents in the fruits [15].

Carotenoids are bioactive compounds belonging to the isoprenoid family that play important roles in the coloration, nutritional quality, and antioxidant defense of fruits [72]. However, low-temperature stress can disrupt carotenoid biosynthetic pathways and reduce their accumulation in fruits. Cold temperatures decrease the activity of key enzymes such as phytoene synthase and lycopene β-cyclase, which are essential for carotenoid biosynthesis, leading to lower levels of these compounds [78]. Therefore, the application of nutritional treatments or plant growth regulators to maintain or increase carotenoid levels under cold stress conditions can be an effective strategy to improve fruit quality and stability [40]. As indicated by our results, the foliar application of amino acids and Se increased the carotenoid content in Cape gooseberry fruits under low-temperature conditions (Table 2). Consistent with our findings, Pakkish and Mohammadrezakhani [58] demonstrated that applying 35 and 70 ppm arginine to mango trees improved fruit weight, anthocyanin, carotenoids, phenols, and TSS, with the 70 ppm treatment having a greater effect. Similar to our findings, Hasanuzzaman et al. [39] reported that Se enhanced carotenoid levels in two types of lettuce, particularly in the red variety. The amino acids, particularly when applied as a foliar spray, can enhance carotenoid synthesis, along with other compounds like phenolics and flavonoids, leading to improved fruit quality and antioxidant capacity [21]. Se can directly or indirectly influence carotenoid pigment production in tomato fruits [59]. Additionally, increased carotenoid content has been observed in basil plants treated with Se at concentrations ranging from 30 to 120 mg L⁻¹ [12].

Abiotic stresses such as cold stress can lead to the accumulation of ROS, which in turn cause oxidative damage to plant cells, particularly affecting cellular components such as membrane lipids [42]. Malondialdehyde is widely recognized and studied as a cytotoxic byproduct of lipid peroxidation and serves as a key oxidative stress marker in many plant-related studies [80]. In our experiment, cold stress significantly increased the accumulation of MDA and H2O2, leading to a reduction in the MSI of Cape gooseberry, along with enhanced lipid peroxidation and membrane damage (Fig. 2). Consistent with our findings, MDA levels and electrolyte leakage were found to increase in sensitive wild tomato varieties under low-temperature stress, indicating that cold conditions induce oxidative stress [41]. Similarly, Al-Khayri and Khedr [6, 7] reported that cold stress in apricot and peach fruits led to elevated MDA and H2O2 levels in untreated plants compared to treated ones, demonstrating that cold exposure compromises membrane integrity and increases electrolyte leakage. The accumulation of ROS under low-temperature stress triggers lipid peroxidation in plant cell membranes, disrupting membrane structure and leading to the leakage of cellular contents, thereby exacerbating cold-induced damage [53]. In agreement with our observations, pretreatment with Phe in plum fruits reduced ion leakage and MDA accumulation while increasing proline levels [70]. Barand et al. [19] also reported that cold stress increased H2O2, MDA, and proline contents in pistachio plants (Pistacia vera L.); however, amino acid pretreatment reduced lipid peroxidation and H2O2 levels, while enhancing proline content and the activity of antioxidant enzymes under cold conditions. These results are consistent with previous findings by Aghdam et al. [2], who showed that exogenous application of Phe during cold storage improved the oxidative status of tomato fruits. The application of Se reduced H2O2 and MDA levels and minimized membrane damage compared to control plants (Fig. 2). This is consistent with the results reported by Djanaguiraman et al. [27] in soybean and sorghum, as well as by Habibi [37] in sunflower.

Fruits and vegetables are the primary sources of vitamin C in the human diet, and its content in these products is strongly influenced by environmental factors and growth conditions. Low-temperature stress can affect the amount of vitamin C in fruits and vegetables; in many species, cold reduces the synthesis and accumulation of ascorbic acid due to disruptions in the activity of enzymes involved in its biosynthetic pathway [51]. In the present study, low temperature stress significantly reduced the vitamin C content in Cape gooseberry fruits; however, foliar application of Cys, Phe, and Se increased vitamin C levels under these low temperature conditions (Fig. 3). Such a finding was reported by Hasanein et al. [38], who demonstrated that applying amino acids at a rate of 200 ppm significantly increased vitamin C levels compared to other treatments in tomato plants.

Phenols and flavonoids are key components of the non-enzymatic antioxidant system, playing a crucial role in neutralizing free radicals and thereby mitigating the effects of oxidative stress [82]. According to the results (Table 3), low temperatures significantly increased the total phenol and flavonoid content in both years. Low temperatures promote the synthesis of phenolics and flavonoids by enhancing the activity of the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is dependent on higher PAL activity [79]. The study results indicated that the application of amino acids led to an increase in phenol levels in thyme plants [64], which is consistent with our findings. PAL is the main enzyme that initiates the phenylpropanoid pathway, leading to the production of various plant metabolites such as cinnamic, coumaric, ferulic, and caffeic acids, as well as flavonoids, tannins, and lignin. Plants accumulate phenolic compounds as a passive defense mechanism in response to PAL activity, with the extent of accumulation primarily influenced by the availability of the primary precursor, L-phenylalanine. Therefore, using Phe to enhance PAL activity results in an increase in phenolic compounds in plants under cold stress. Foliar spraying with Phe increased flavonoid levels in grapes [60] and enhanced phenol and flavonoid content, as well as PAL enzyme activity, in tomatoes [2]. Zhang et al. [85] demonstrated that the use of the amino acid cysteine enhanced the overall phenolic and flavonoid content in Loquat (Eriobotrya japonica L.) fruit. Plants may systematically accumulate phenolic compounds to cope with stress or in response to pathogen attacks, and this accumulation may partly derive from the precursor L-phenylalanine [16].

Proline accumulation under cold stress is a well-documented phenomenon [18]. Acting as an osmoprotectant, proline accumulates at high concentrations in plant cells during stress without disrupting cell structure or metabolism. For example, in Arabidopsis, proline levels notably increase after just 4 h of exposure to cold stress at 4–8 °C, and this elevated proline content persists even after new leaves develop under cold conditions [43]. Consistent with our findings, the application of Phe and Cys increased proline content in garlic bulbs [75]. Similarly, Se application at various stages in wheat seedlings exposed to low temperatures elevated proline levels and enzyme activities, reducing membrane damage by enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms [5]. Additionally, low concentrations of Se (as sodium selenate, 3 and 6 mg L⁻¹) increased proline levels in sorghum seedlings under cold stress [1].

Plants generally improve their cold tolerance by activating both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems, which assist in scavenging internally produced ROS and maintaining cellular redox balance [54, 55]. An increase in the levels of carotenoids, total phenols, and flavonoids, key antioxidant compounds, along with enhanced activities of CAT, POD, PAL, and SOD was observed following foliar application of the amino acids Phe, Cys, and Se. This suggests that the increased antioxidant capacity can be attributed to the accumulation of these compounds and the elevated activity of antioxidant enzymes. Previous studies have reported a strong correlation between total phenolics, flavonoids, vitamin C, and antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, POD, and SOD with antioxidant capacity in various plant species [22, 31]. The beneficial antioxidant effects of Se in plants are well documented, as it enhances antioxidant enzyme activities [63, 65]. Se is essential as a component of the glutathione peroxidase enzyme (GSH-Px) in plants [24]. Additionally, Se plays a crucial role in antioxidant functions [25], protecting plants against biotic and abiotic stresses. It helps combat oxidative stress by inhibiting lipid peroxidation and boosting GSH-Px activity [61]. Research shows that Se improves the antioxidant capacity of fruits by enhancing both enzyme activities and antioxidant compound levels [15].

POD plays a vital role in reducing H₂O₂ accumulation and inhibiting MDA formation, thereby preventing lipid peroxidation of cell membranes and maintaining membrane integrity. In a study comparing two banana cultivars, one cold-resistant and the other cold-sensitive, the cold-resistant cultivar exhibited a significant increase in POD activity under stress, whereas the cold-sensitive cultivar showed a notable decrease [83]. In our study, low-temperature stress led to an increase in CAT activity, a potent antioxidant enzyme that protects the plant, influenced by amino acid treatment. The positive effect of Cys on antioxidant enzyme activity can be linked to its metabolic products, such as glutathione, which possess antioxidant properties. In agreement with our data, a previous experiment on Physalis showed increased activities of antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, POD, and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), in response to foliar application of Cys under water deficit conditions [48]. Similarly, Turfan [75] reported that supply of Cys and Phe positively affected APX, CAT, POD, and SOD activities in garlic bulbs. Moreover, in agreement with our results, pretreatment of Japanese parsnip fruits with Cys increased SOD, CAT, and POD activities compared to control fruits [85]. Ali et al. [9] also demonstrated that Cys application in litchi plants-maintained CAT activity.

Conclusions

Over two years of field cultivation under late-season cold stress, the effects of phenylalanine and cysteine treatments were more pronounced than those of selenium. Fruit quality traits, including TSS, TA and carotenoid content, were most influenced by the amino acids, with phenylalanine at both tested concentrations (2.5 and 0.75 mM) significantly increasing TSS and TA. Additionally, cysteine at 0.5 mM enhanced fruit carotenoid content and vitamin C levels. By modulating antioxidant enzyme activities, phenylalanine and cysteine increased phenolic and flavonoid contents as well as the overall antioxidant capacity of the fruit, contributing to improved plant resistance to cold stress and reducing ion leakage and MDA levels. Although selenium is not essential for plants, its foliar application on Cape gooseberry under cold stress had notable effects. Specifically, 0.5 mg L⁻¹ selenium significantly supported membrane integrity and enhanced antioxidant capacity and enzyme activities to mitigate end-of-season cold stress. Based on these findings, the application of amino acids and selenium can be recommended as an effective strategy to mitigate low-temperature stress and enhance fruit quality in Cape gooseberry plants.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and treatments, plant material, and growth conditions

The field experiment was conducted at the University of Zanjan, Zanjan, Iran (36◦40′ N Long, 48◦24′ E Lat, 1596.3 m altitude) during 2021 and 2022. The Cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.) seeds, sourced from Pakan Seed Company, Isfahan, were sown in peat moss-filled trays. Seedlings were grown in a climate-controlled greenhouse at the University of Zanjan (25 ± 2 °C day, 20 ± 2 °C night; 60–65% relative humidity; natural light) and irrigated with Hoagland nutrient solution. The experiment was conducted in a randomized complete block design with three replicates. The experimental treatments included different concentrations of amino acid phenylalanine (0.75 and 2.5 mM, Phe), cysteine (0.5 and 1.0 mM, Cys), and selenium selenate (0.5 and 0.75 mg L−1, Se), and distilled water was used as the control treatment. For each treatment, 30 seedlings were used (with 10 seedlings per replicate). Different concentration of treatments was sprayed on the seedlings at the 4–5th true leaf stage. After foliar spraying of treatments, 210 plants with uniform size were subjected to low-temperature stress (climate chamber with 4 ◦C, 16 h light/8 h dark with a relative humidity of 50–60% for 48 h.). In addition, a control group (30 plants), which was not treated with amino acid or Se and did not undergo chilling stress, was grown under optimal conditions. After applying chilling stress, the plants were returned to optimal growth conditions. Subsequently, the seedlings were transplanted to the field with a spacing of 150 cm between plants within rows and 200 cm between rows. Two weeks after seedling establishment, the seedlings treated with Phe, Cys, and Se were re-sprayed with the same treatment concentrations at three developmental stages: the 8th–10th true leaf stage (300 mL per plant, until runoff, using a mechanical mist sprayer), anthesis (500 mL per plant), and fruit set (800 mL per plant). The irrigation schedule was set for every two days, and weed control was performed manually throughout the growth period. After plants were exposed to autumn chilling, for six days in October, 2021 (Fig. S1) and in November, 2022 (Fig. S2), fruits were harvested at the calix and fruit changed color from green to orange stage. Table S1 presents the physical and chemical properties of farm soil, and Tables S2 and S3, and figures S1 and S2 display the monthly average meteorological parameters during the plant growth period and the minimum daily temperature for October 2021 and November 2022, respectively.

Five fruits from three plants per replicate in each treatment were randomly collected to evaluate TSS and TA contents. TA was determined by adding 3 to 4 drops of phenolphthalein and titrating with 0.1 N NaOH until a permanent light pink color appeared. The TSS content of fruits was measured using a portable digital refractometer (Atago Co., Ltd., Japan) and expressed in ◦Brix.

Carotenoids content

Six fruits from three plants per replicate in each treatment were randomly collected to evaluate carotenoid contents. Carotenoids were extracted from 0.5 g of fresh fruit using 80% (v/v) acetone. The absorbance of the extract was measured at 480 and 510 nm using a SAFAS Monaco (RS 232) spectrophotometer. Membrane stability index (MSI)

To assess the MSI of fruits, six mature fruits from three plants per replicate were randomly selected and cut into 1 cm slices. Fruit samples were rinsed three times with distilled water to remove any external impurities, and then incubated in vials at 25 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the electrical conductivity of the soaking solution (EC1) was measured. The same samples were then autoclaved at 120 °C for 20 min, and a second electrical conductivity measurement (EC2) was recorded after the solution had cooled to room temperature. MSI was determined using the formula detailed by Ezhilmathi et al. Malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) contents

MDA content was determined following the method outlined by Zhao et al. [86] with some modifications. Fruit samples (0.5 g) from three fruits of five plants per treatment, in triplicate, were extracted in 1.5 mL of 5% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm, with correction for nonspecific absorbance at 600 nm. MDA levels were expressed as µmol g-¹, with 1 unit equivalent to 1 µmol of MDA per gram of plant material. Simultaneously, fruits from the same five plants were collected from the same five plants for hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) quantification. H₂O₂ levels were assessed spectrophotometrically following the method described by Alexieva et al. [8], based on its reaction with potassium iodide (KI). The reaction mixture consisted of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) containing the fruit extract supernatant, potassium phosphate buffer, and 2 mL of KI reagent (1 M w/v in double-distilled water). A blank containing 0.1% TCA without fruit extract was used for calibration. The reaction was allowed to proceed in the dark for one hour, after which the absorbance was measured at 390 nm. H₂O₂ concentrations were quantified using a standard curve prepared with known H₂O₂ concentrations.

Total phenols and flavonoids contents

To measure total phenols and flavonoids, three fruits from each of five plants per treatment were randomly collected in triplicate. Total phenol content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method [69], with methanol/water (80:20, v/v) as the extraction solvent. The extract was combined with an equal volume of Folin–Ciocalteu (50%, v/v) and allowed to react at room temperature. Subsequently, a 2% Na2CO3 solution was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 720 nm, and the total phenol content was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE) per 100 g fresh weight (FW). The total flavonoid concentration in the fruit extracts was determined using a colorimetric assay [46]. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm immediately after the reaction against a blank. The results were reported as millimoles (mM) of quercetin equivalents per 100 g FW.

Vitamin C content

Vitamin C content of fruits was determined using the titration method with the 2, 6-dichlorophenol-indophenol dye solution according to the procedure described by Terada et al. [74]. Vitamin C content was expressed as mg 100 g−1 FW.

Antioxidant capacity

To determine antioxidant capacity, three fruits per plant were collected from five plants per treatment in triplicate. The scavenging activity of the free radical DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) was evaluated following the method described by Dehghan and Khoshkam [26]. Briefly, 0.1 mL of the sample was mixed with a DPPH solution (0.1 mM in methanol) and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The reduction in absorbance was meausered at 517 nm. The percentage reduction of DPPH was calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%\;\mathrm{Inhibition}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{DPPH}\%=\;\left(\mathrm{Abs}\;\mathrm{control}\;-\;\mathrm{Abs}\;\mathrm{sample}\right)/\mathrm{Abs}\;\mathrm{control}\;\times\;100$$\end{document}

Proline content

Proline content in fruits was assessed across all treatments by extracting proline from three fruits of each plant, sampled from five representative plants in every plot. The extraction involved using 10 ml of 3% aqueous sulfosalicylic acid with 0.5 g of fruit material, followed by filtration. The resulting solution was mixed with an equal volume of ninhydrin reagent and glacial acetic acid in a test tube, heated at 100 °C for one hour, and then cooled in an ice bath to halt the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm, and proline concentration was calculated using a standard curve. The results were reported as mg proline per gram fresh weight (FW) [20].

Antioxidant enzymes activity

To assess the antioxidant enzyme activities, including POD, CAT, and SOD, 2 g of frozen fruits were homogenized in a potassium phosphate buffer. Following centrifugation (12,000 g, 4 °C, 15 min), the resulting supernatants were utilized for enzymatic assessments. POD activity was determined using the guaiacol assay method, where, a change of 0.01 units per minute in absorbance at 470 nm was considered equivalent to one unit of POD activity. CAT activity was determined by monitoring the breakdown of H2O2 at 240 nm using a UV spectrophotometer [84]. SOD activity was assessed according to Zhang et al. [84], with one unit of SOD activity defined as the amount of enzyme required to induce 50% inhibition of the nitro blue tetrazolium chloride reduction measured at 560 nm. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity was evaluated based on Nguyen et al. [57], with slight adjustments using 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and 50 mM borate buffer (pH 8), respectively. All enzyme activities were expressed as U mg−1 protein.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were performed using a completely randomized block design (8 treatments including: 2 Phe, 2 Cys, 2 Se, 1 CLT (control seedling exposed to low-temperature stress.) and 1 control plants (not chilling treated seedlings) × 2 years with 3 replications = 48data. Data were analyzed using the SAS statistical program (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and means were compared with Duncan’s multiple range tests at P = 0.05.
