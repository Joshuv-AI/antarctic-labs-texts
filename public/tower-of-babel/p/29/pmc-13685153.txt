Changes in the microbial spectrum and antimicrobial resistance of bloodstream infections in intensive care units before and during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Sümeyye Bayram, Funda Şimşek, Çiğdem Arabacı, Zekeriya Ervatan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBloodstream infections (BSIs) are major infectious conditions associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs). In critically ill patients, early and appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy plays an important role in preventing adverse clinical outcomes. However, the increasing burden of antimicrobial resistance necessitates that empirical treatment strategies be continuously reassessed based on up-to-date local resistance data. This study aimed to compare the distribution of bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance profiles of BSIs identified in ICUs before and during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.MethodsA total of 388 patients diagnosed with BSIs while hospitalized in the ICUs of Health Sciences University Prof. Dr. Cemil Taşçıoğlu City Hospital between March 2019 and April 2021 were retrospectively evaluated. During the pandemic period, patients with and without COVID-19 were analyzed together without stratification. Bacterial isolates recovered from blood cultures and their antimicrobial susceptibility results were compared between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods. Multivariable analysis identified independent risk factors for multidrug resistance (MDR) in Klebsiella pneumoniae, adjusting for clinical confounders.ResultsPre-pandemic, the predominant pathogens were K. pneumoniae (30.1%), A. baumannii complex (21.9%), and S. aureus (9.7%); during the pandemic, K. pneumoniae (34.4%), A. baumannii complex (21.2%), and E. faecium (10.4%) led the distribution. During the pandemic, non-fermentative Gram-negative bacilli showed a significant increase in ceftazidime resistance, while resistance to amikacin, gentamicin, and meropenem declined. In Enterobacterales, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production and carbapenem resistance increased during the pandemic. In K. pneumoniae, while carbapenem resistance remained statistically stable, colistin resistance increased significantly, raising a new concern. Prior antimicrobial therapy was the sole independent predictor of MDR in K. pneumoniae (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR] = 8.296, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] = 1.471–46.794, p = 0.017).ConclusionsDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, Gram-negative pathogens continued to predominate among BSIs identified in ICUs, with an observed trend toward increased antimicrobial resistance, particularly among Enterobacterales species. Notably, prior antibiotic exposure was identified as the key independent determinant of MDR status in K. pneumoniae. Given the dynamic nature of pathogen distribution and resistance profiles in BSIs, consideration of local and period-specific resistance patterns in ICU settings may help inform empirical antimicrobial treatment strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are serious and potentially life-threatening conditions that rank among the leading causes of mortality worldwide and are associated with prolonged hospital stays [1]. Despite significant advances in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, BSIs continue to represent a major clinical challenge [2]. In addition to the direct mortality caused by bacterial infections, limited access to effective treatment options due to increasing antimicrobial resistance further contributes to adverse outcomes [3]. In this context, an estimated 4.95 million deaths worldwide were reported to be associated with bacterial antimicrobial resistance in 2019 [4].

In intensive care units (ICUs), invasive procedures, advanced age, high comorbidity burden, severe clinical conditions, immunosuppression, and extensive use of antibiotics markedly increase the incidence of bloodstream infections and infections caused by resistant pathogens [5–7]. During the COVID-19 pandemic—as well as during past coronavirus crises such as the 2003 SARS-CoV-1 outbreak—substantial changes occurred in infection control practices, patterns of antibiotic use, and patient characteristics in ICUs, which are thought to have influenced the epidemiology of BSIs [8–11]. Moreover, several studies have reported an increased frequency of secondary infections among patients with COVID-19, along with a higher incidence of BSIs in this population [12–14].

The causative microorganisms of bloodstream infections and their antimicrobial resistance profiles may vary over time and across different regions and countries [15–17]. However, the impact of changes associated with the COVID-19 pandemic on the distribution of BSI pathogens and resistance patterns, particularly in ICU settings, has not yet been fully elucidated. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the bacterial pathogens causing ICU-acquired bacteremia and their antimicrobial resistance profiles during the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods in our hospital and to evaluate potential changes over time.

Methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, single-center observational study including patients diagnosed with bloodstream infections (BSIs) while being followed for any indication in the intensive care units (ICUs) of Prof. Dr. Cemil Taşçıoğlu City Hospital.

To evaluate the potential impact of the pandemic period, patients were divided into two time periods. The pre-pandemic period included patients hospitalized between March 1, 2019 and March 31, 2020, whereas the pandemic period comprised patients hospitalized between April 1, 2020, and April 30, 2021. March 2020 was considered a transition period during which pandemic-related clinical practices had not yet been standardized and was therefore included in the pre-pandemic period. Given that the marked effects of the pandemic on healthcare delivery, hospitalization profiles, and empirical antibiotic use were anticipated to begin as of April 2020, the pandemic period was defined accordingly.

As the primary objective of the study was to compare the microbiological characteristics of BSIs, patients with COVID-19 and those without COVID-19 were analyzed together without stratification. This decision reflects the uniform pandemic-era operational strains such as staff shortages, altered empirical antibiotic protocols, and modified infection control workflows that affected all ICU patients equally. Therefore, this non-stratified approach evaluates the overall ecosystem of healthcare stress rather than isolating the direct biological impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

A total of 3,482 blood cultures were evaluated, of which 1,094 were obtained during the pre-pandemic period and 2,388 during the pandemic period. Fungal isolates were excluded from the analysis. Repeated isolation of the same microorganism within a single episode was considered a single infection. Isolation of a different microorganism from the same patient, or growth detected at least 14 days after a previous positive culture and clinically compatible with a new infection, was defined as a new episode.

When common skin contaminants (coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium spp., Bacillus spp., Micrococcus spp., and Propionibacterium spp.) were isolated, they were considered contaminants unless the criteria defined by the CDC/NHSN were met, including compatible clinical findings and growth in at least two sets of blood cultures obtained at different times [18].

In total, 388 patients were included in the study. During the pre-pandemic period, 196 BSI episodes occurring in 188 patients were analyzed, whereas during the pandemic period, 212 BSI episodes in 200 patients were evaluated.

The resistance analyses included all bloodstream infections diagnosed during the patients’ ICU stay, encompassing both intensive care unit–acquired infections and those that developed prior to ICU admission.

Blood cultures were processed using a fully automated blood culture system (BACT/ALERT®; bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). Bottles flagged as positive were subcultured onto MacConkey agar, chocolate agar, and 5% sheep blood agar. Colonies considered to be causative pathogens were identified at the species level using the VITEK MS system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK® 2 automated system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France), and results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) criteria.

For antimicrobial resistance analysis, Gram-negative isolates were grouped as Enterobacterales and non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli, while Gram-positive isolates were categorized as staphylococci, enterococci, and streptococci, to reflect clinically relevant empirical treatment strategies and to ensure adequate statistical power.

Carbapenem resistance was defined as isolates showing resistance (R) or decreased susceptibility (I) to meropenem and/or imipenem according to EUCAST criteria in both Enterobacterales and non-fermentative Gram-negative bacteria. To ensure adequate statistical power and prevent data masking across heterogeneous species, Klebsiella pneumoniae, the most predominant pathogen in our ICU, was specifically selected for further multivariable risk factor analysis. The primary outcome for this sub-analysis was multidrug resistance (MDR), strictly defined according to the joint European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) consensus criteria as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [19]. Other phenotypic extremes (e.g., pan-drug resistance or isolated ESBL) were excluded due to sample size limitations.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows (version 15.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were compared between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods using the chi-square test. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For variables not normally distributed, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Additionally, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 32.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to identify independent risk factors for MDR status among K. pneumoniae isolates. The model simultaneously controlled for potential confounding variables, including patient age, gender, hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, prior antimicrobial use (> 3 days), mechanical ventilation (as a surrogate marker for clinical severity), presence of other invasive procedures, and the study period (pre-pandemic vs. pandemic). To avoid model overfitting and potential multicollinearity, proximal clinical drivers of resistance (such as prior antibiotic use and ICU stay) were prioritized in the multivariable model over underlying comorbidities whose effects are heavily mediated through these clinical parameters. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The proportion of polymicrobial bloodstream infections was 4.2% (n = 8) in the pre-pandemic period and 6.0% (n = 12) during the pandemic period, with no statistically significant difference between periods. Prior exposure to empirical or targeted antimicrobial therapy for more than three days before the onset of BSI was common in both periods, observed in 77.5% (n = 144) of patients in the pre-pandemic period and 83.0% (n = 166) during the pandemic period (p = 0.210).

Among the patients admitted to the intensive care units during the pandemic period, 94 (47.0%) were confirmed to have COVID-19 via Real Time PCR, while 106 (53.0%) were COVID-19 negative.

Distribution of causative microorganisms

The distribution of microorganisms causing bloodstream infections before and during the COVID-19 pandemic is summarized in Table 2. In the pre-pandemic period, the three most frequently isolated pathogens were Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 59, 30.1%), Acinetobacter baumannii complex (n = 43, 21.9%), and Staphylococcus aureus (n = 19, 9.7%). During the pandemic period, Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 73, 34.4%) and Acinetobacter baumannii complex (n = 45, 21.2%) remained the most common pathogens, followed by Enterococcus faecium (n = 22, 10.4%). The isolation rate of Pseudomonas aeruginosa increased from 1.5% (n = 3) in the pre-pandemic period to 5.2% (n = 11) during the pandemic period; however, this increase remained as a borderline statistical trend rather than a significant difference (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.054).The relative proportions of the most frequently isolated microorganisms are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Overall, 311 BSI episodes were classified as ICU-acquired. The proportion of ICU-acquired BSIs was similar between periods, accounting for 74.4% of episodes in the pre-pandemic period and 77.8% during the pandemic period. In both periods, the most common causative microorganisms of ICU-acquired BSIs were Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii complex, and Enterococcus faecium.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Gram-negative bacteria

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Gram-negative bacteria are presented in Table 3. Among Enterobacterales, the prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) positivity was significantly higher during the pandemic period compared to the pre-pandemic period (80.9% vs. 62.8%, p = 0.006). Resistance rates to amikacin, ertapenem, colistin, and piperacillin–tazobactam were also significantly higher during the pandemic period.

In contrast, resistance to several agents among non-fermentative Gram-negative bacilli, including amikacin, gentamicin, meropenem, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, was significantly higher in the pre-pandemic period. Notably, resistance to ceftazidime among non-fermentative Gram-negative bacilli increased significantly during the pandemic period (p < 0.001).

Crucially, alarmingly high rates of carbapenem resistance were observed among the leading Gram-negative pathogens in both study periods. For A. baumannii, carbapenem resistance reached absolute or near-absolute levels, standing at 100% (n = 43/43) in the pre-pandemic period and 91.1% (n = 41/45) during the pandemic period (p = 0.117). Similarly, K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited persistently high and unchanged carbapenem resistance rates across both cohorts, at 77.9% (n = 46/59) and 76.7% (n = 56/73), respectively (p = 1.000). While these carbapenem resistance rates did not differ significantly between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, their baseline magnitude represents a critical clinical challenge in our ICU. In contrast, colistin resistance among K. pneumoniae isolates increased significantly during the pandemic period (32.2% vs. 53.4%, p = 0.022), whereas no significant change was observed for A. baumannii (p = 1.000) (Table 4).

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Gram-positive bacteria

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Gram-positive bacteria are summarized in Table 6. Among staphylococci, no resistance to linezolid or glycopeptides was observed in either period. Methicillin resistance rates were identical in the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (63.6%), and no statistically significant differences were observed for other antimicrobial agents.

Among enterococci, resistance to glycopeptides was significantly lower during the pandemic period compared to the pre-pandemic period (2.9% vs. 26.7%, p = 0.010). Similarly, high-level gentamicin resistance decreased significantly during the pandemic period (54.5% vs. 80.0%, p = 0.032). Resistance to ampicillin did not differ significantly between periods.

All streptococcal isolates remained susceptible to ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and meropenem in both periods. Penicillin resistance was not observed in the pre-pandemic period and was detected in one isolate during the pandemic period, without statistical significance.

Although minor variations in individual pathogen frequencies were observed, the overall distribution of bloodstream infection pathogens remained largely similar between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods. Antimicrobial resistance patterns differed between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, particularly among Enterobacterales and non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli, while variations among Gram-positive groups were less pronounced.

Discussion

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) remain among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality, and the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance continues to complicate their management. In the present study, we evaluated the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the distribution of causative pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns of BSIs in intensive care units.

Our findings demonstrated that Gram-negative bacteria remained the predominant causative agents of BSIs in both the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, with Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii complex consistently ranking among the most frequently isolated pathogens. Gram-positive organisms, mainly staphylococci and enterococci, constituted the third most common group. These findings are in line with previous studies comparing the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, which similarly reported that the overall spectrum of predominant pathogens did not change substantially despite the profound impact of the pandemic on healthcare systems [20–22].

In contrast, other studies have reported shifts in pathogen distribution during the pandemic. Segala et al. observed an increased frequency of Staphylococcus aureus and Acinetobacter spp., accompanied by a decrease in Escherichia coli isolates [23]. In parallel with these observed changes, a remarkable temporal shift was also recorded in the non-fermenting bacteria group in our study; the proportion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates increased from 1.5% (n = 3) pre-pandemic to 5.2% (n = 11) during the pandemic. Although the absolute numbers remain low, this upward trend is noteworthy. Such increases in opportunistic pathogens like P. aeruginosa during pandemic surges may be associated with broader institutional challenges, including prolonged stays in intensive care units and selection pressure potentially resulting from widespread empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic use. Likewise, a study conducted in London highlighted an increase in Gram-negative BSIs during the pandemic period, which was attributed to disruptions in infection prevention and control measures, as well as modifications in clinical workflows implemented to manage the outbreak [24]. Differences between these findings and our results may be explained by variations in local epidemiology, the baseline prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms, the intensity of infection control measures, and patterns of antimicrobial use across institutions and regions.

While carbapenem resistance rates remained consistently high throughout both periods, the lack of a statistically significant increase suggests that pre-existing resistance patterns largely persisted during the pandemic. Crucially, the baseline magnitude of carbapenem resistance among our leading Gram-negative pathogens represents a critical clinical challenge, reaching near-absolute levels in A. baumannii (91.1%–100%) and remaining exceptionally high in K. pneumoniae (76.7%–77.9%). A multicenter European study similarly reported a marked increase in carbapenem resistance among Gram-negative bacilli during the COVID-19 pandemic, including in countries that previously had low resistance rates [17]. The absence of a significant increase in carbapenem resistance among non-fermentative Gram-negative bacilli in our study may be attributable to the already high baseline resistance rates observed prior to the pandemic, which may have limited the detectability of further increases. Additionally, long-standing infection control measures and established antimicrobial stewardship practices targeting these pathogens in intensive care settings may have helped mitigate additional selective pressure during the pandemic period. Regarding the observed decrease in resistance rates for certain other antimicrobial agents among non-fermenters during the pandemic, this trend might be paradoxically associated with the intensified contact isolation precautions and enhanced hand hygiene protocols implemented during the pandemic within our intensive care units, which may have inadvertently contributed to limiting the cross-transmission of specific resistant clones.

In contrast to carbapenem resistance, a significant increase in colistin resistance was observed among Enterobacterales during the pandemic period, most notably in Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates. Given that colistin is frequently used as a last-resort agent for the treatment of infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, this finding is particularly concerning. Similarly, a study conducted in Greece reported a decreasing trend in colistin resistance among Acinetobacter isolates recovered from bloodstream infections in intensive care unit patients, while colistin resistance among Klebsiella isolates increased; notably, no significant changes were observed in the already high carbapenem resistance rates in either group [25]. These findings are consistent with our results and further support the notion that increased selective pressure on last-line agents during the pandemic may have preferentially contributed to driven the emergence of colistin resistance.

Methicillin resistance among staphylococci did not differ between the two study periods. However, several studies have reported an increase in methicillin resistance during the pandemic period [25, 26]. The inclusion of non–intensive care unit–acquired bloodstream infections in our analysis may have led to the inclusion of community- or ward-acquired staphylococcal bacteremias, which could partly explain the comparable resistance rates observed between the two periods.

In our study, while ampicillin resistance rates remained statistically comparable, glycopeptide (vancomycin and teicoplanin) resistance among enterococci was observed to be significantly lower during the pandemic period. Although some contemporary literature suggests that SARS-CoV-2–associated intestinal barrier dysfunction could hypothetically promote the translocation of less resistant endogenous gut flora into the bloodstream [14], this mechanism remains strictly speculative in our cohort since we did not evaluate intestinal biomarkers or stratify these specific outcomes by COVID-19 status. Alternatively, this significant decline in glycopeptide resistance is more likely explained by shifts in empirical antibiotic selection pressure in our ICU or changes in the clinical characteristics of admitted patients during the pandemic peaks. Supporting the high variability of enterococcal resistance patterns, a study conducted in Romania similarly reported distinct fluctuations in Enterococcus susceptibility across different pandemic phases, primarily driven by changing patterns of antimicrobial use rather than viral mechanisms [27].

Several factors may have been associated with the observed changes in the distribution of bloodstream infection pathogens and their antimicrobial resistance patterns during the pandemic period. Worldwide, particularly in critically ill patients with COVID-19, the widespread empirical use of broad-spectrum antibiotics due to concerns about secondary bacterial infections may have increased selective pressure and corresponded with a higher occurrence of infections caused by multidrug-resistant microorganisms. Crucially, this intense antimicrobial consumption pressure acts as a major driver of resistance by eradicating susceptible flora and selecting for highly resistant nosocomial lineages. The escalated utilization of reserve antibiotics, especially carbapenems and broad-spectrum beta-lactams, during peak pandemic waves created an unprecedented selective environment within intensive care settings. This sustained exposure potentially accelerated the clinical emergence and horizontal transmission of multidrug-resistant strains [11, 28, 29]. Our multivariable binary logistic regression analysis provided robust mathematical validation for the underlying mechanisms of this resistance surge, yielding what is arguably the most critical insight of our study. When rigorously adjusted for demographic and clinical confounders, the pandemic period itself was not identified as an independent risk factor for K. pneumoniae MDR status (aOR = 0.944, p = 0.927). Most notably, prior exposure to antimicrobial therapy for more than 3 days emerged as the single strongest independent predictor, multiplying the risk of developing an MDR infection by more than eightfold (aOR = 8.296, p = 0.017). This pivotal finding underscores a fundamental clinical reality: the escalation in severe resistance patterns was not an abstract, inevitable consequence of the pandemic timeline or the virus itself. Instead, it was directly driven by the intense, broad-spectrum antimicrobial selective pressure that characterized the pandemic era. This indicates that antimicrobial stewardship, rather than chronological infection control alone, remains the paramount defense against the containment of multidrug-resistant outbreaks in intensive care settings.

In addition, the surge in patient numbers during the COVID-19 pandemic coincided with the reorganization of many hospital wards—and in some cases entire hospitals—into COVID-19 cohort units and intensive care units. To meet the increased workload, healthcare workers from non-ICU settings were frequently redeployed to intensive care units, where they occasionally faced challenges regarding comprehensive training or experience in infection prevention and control practices. Moreover, the substantial workload and physically demanding working conditions during the pandemic may have adversely affected adherence to hand hygiene and infection control measures. Collectively, these factors potentially contributed to an increased risk of healthcare-associated infections and cross-transmission [17, 30, 31].

In addition, the redeployment of infectious diseases specialists and infection control committees to frontline pandemic duties may have contributed to disruptions in routine infection prevention activities and antimicrobial stewardship efforts [30]. Potential challenges in maintaining strict compliance with screening and isolation measures for patients colonized with resistant pathogens prior to hospitalization, together with increased antimicrobial exposure, may have facilitated the selection and in-hospital dissemination of resistant phenotypes [23].

Although patients without COVID-19 were also included in our study, the potential impact of the disease itself and COVID-19–related treatments on modifying the risk dynamics of secondary bacterial infections and the distribution of causative pathogens should not be overlooked [14, 27, 31].

Another potential contributing factor is the widespread use of biocidal agents for personal and environmental disinfection during the pandemic. Prolonged exposure to low concentrations of these agents has been reported to potentially facilitate the development of cross-resistance to antibiotics, particularly among Gram-negative bacteria [26].

Overall, our findings suggest that antibiotic prescribing practices under pandemic conditions, the increased workload in intensive care units, and disruptions in infection prevention and control measures may have directly or indirectly influenced the development of antimicrobial resistance. The present findings once again highlight the urgent need for the implementation of effective infection control policies and robust antimicrobial stewardship programs [17].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Due to its retrospective and single-center design, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Importantly, patients hospitalized during the pandemic period were evaluated together without statistical stratification according to their COVID-19 status. Patients with active COVID-19 infection often required distinct clinical management pathways, higher rates of empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial usage, and different infection control protocols, which may have independently influenced the selection pressure for resistant pathogens. In our cohort, 94 patients (47.0%) were RT-PCR positive, while 106 (53.0%) were negative. It should be noted that these patient proportions were not static and fluctuated significantly across different waves and phases of the pandemic, driven by changing viral virulence, evolving clinical admission criteria, and institutional workload transitions. Because these cohorts were not analyzed separately, the observed resistance trends cannot be directly or exclusively attributed to SARS-CoV-2 infection or management, which represents a major limitation in interpreting period-specific dynamics. In addition, intensive care unit–acquired infections were not analyzed as a separate subgroup, which may have limited a more precise assessment of unit-specific transmission dynamics and resistance patterns. The evaluation of pathogens in broad groups may have led to the underrecognition of true species-specific and pathogen-level antimicrobial resistance differences, thereby potentially limiting the interpretability and generalizability of the findings.

Additionally, some subgroup analyses were constrained by small sample sizes, which may limit the clinical robustness of those specific resistance comparisons.

Due to its retrospective design, this study lacks molecular typing and genotypic analysis of resistance mechanisms. Consequently, we could not identify specific carbapenemase genes or evaluate clonal relatedness, leaving the potential impact of localized outbreaks or horizontal transmission unexplored.

Additionally, we did not account for baseline disease severity scores (such as APACHE II or SOFA) or specific indications for ICU admission. Another limitation of our study is the lack of multivariate regression or survival analyses that directly link specific resistance trends to mortality rate determinants.

Furthermore, as the study primarily covers the early phases of the pandemic, it does not allow for an assessment of the long-term impact of evolving infection control practices, antimicrobial prescribing patterns, and clinical management strategies on antimicrobial resistance. Consequently, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to more clearly elucidate the effects of pandemic-related conditions on antimicrobial resistance dynamics.

Conclusion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, while the overall distribution of pathogens causing bloodstream infections in patients in intensive care units remained largely unchanged, an increasing trend in antimicrobial resistance rates was observed, particularly among Gram-negative bacilli. Crucially, the significant surge in colistin resistance among Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates stands out as one of the most alarming clinical findings of our study, directly threatening patient outcomes due to colistin’s critical role as a last-resort therapeutic option. Our multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that this resistance surge was independently and powerfully driven by prior broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure for more than 3 days, rather than the chronological pandemic timeline alone. Although the COVID-19 pandemic has ended, the long-term impact of the extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics during this period on the epidemiology of multidrug-resistant microorganisms remains uncertain.

Given the dynamic nature of antimicrobial resistance patterns, continuous surveillance of local resistance data and strict adherence to individual antimicrobial stewardship programs remain essential to support appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy and optimize clinical outcomes in ICU patients with bloodstream infections.
