Safety, tolerability and immunogenicity of a quadrivalent recombinant norovirus vaccine (Pichia pastoris) in participants six weeks of age or older: Phase I/IIa trial
Authors: Gang Chen, Liwei Shi, Qingchuan Yu, Qingwei Liu, Xuanyi Wang, Yan Liu, Fan Ding, Lingfeng Yuan, Ping Wang, Xiang Zhou, Yong Zhang, Zhong Huang, Jingjing Li, Zhongyu Hu, Teng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNoroviruses are the leading cause of epidemic acute gastroenteritis and foodborne diarrheal disease in humans. Here, a randomized, double-blind, multi-cohort, placebo-controlled clinical trial was performed to determine the candidate vaccine’s safety and immunogenicity. The experimental framework incorporated an initial Phase I age/dose-escalation stage followed by a Phase IIa dose-expansion component. A total of 580 participants (five groups: 6 weeks to 23 months, 2–5 years, 6–17 years, 18–59 years, and ≥60 years) were randomized into 13 groups to receive intramuscular injections of either a placebo (aluminium-containing or aluminium-free) or vaccine formulation (low-dose or high-dose) on days 0, 30 and 60. The safety outcomes included the incidence of all adverse events within 0–30 days post-vaccination and all serious adverse events within 6 months. Immunogenicity analyses focused on seroconversion rates against four norovirus genotypes (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, GII.17) at 30 days post-third vaccination, including IgA, IgG and histoblood group antigen (HBGA)-blocking antibodies (BT50). The results of the Phase I and Phase IIa clinical trials indicated that the IgA seroconversion rates against the four norovirus genotypes at 30 days post-third vaccination were 49.45-86.81% in the low-dose group versus 66.12-85.25% in the high-dose group. IgG seroconversion ranged from 63.74% to 93.41% (low-dose) and 72.13% to 92.35% (high-dose), while HBGA-blocking antibody responses ranged from 64.84-86.26% (low-dose) and 78.14-89.62% (high-dose). Adverse reactions predominantly occurred within 0–7 days post-vaccination, with Grade 1–2 events predominating. The most frequent reactions included injection-site pain and fever. Introduction

Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) poses a significant global disease burden, causing 1.45 million deaths worldwide annually, including 800,000 deaths among children under five years of age [1]. The primary symptoms of AGE include diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and fever [2]. Epidemiological data demonstrate that low-income countries have substantially higher incidence rates of diarrhoeal diseases and mortality rates from these diseases in comparison to middle – and high-income countries [3-6]. Viral infections constitute the predominant etiology of AGE, with rotavirus, norovirus, astrovirus and enteric adenovirus accounting for over 90% of viral AGE cases. Following the widespread implementation of rotavirus vaccination in recent years, Human Norovirus (HuNoV) infection has gradually emerged as the leading cause of viral AGE [7-9]. It is estimated that norovirus-induced AGE affects over 600 million people globally each year, resulting in approximately 210,000 annual fatalities. Among children under five years of age, norovirus-associated AGE poses an annual economic burden of approximately $39.8 billion worldwide [10-12].

Vaccination is considered to be the most cost-effective preventive intervention against infectious diseases within public health frameworks. Globally, there is no marketed vaccine against norovirus infection. The difficulties in developing such a vaccine primarily relate to the genetic diversity of norovirus strains, poor knowledge of the characteristics of immunity to norovirus infection, and a lack of immune correlates of protection [13-16]. The recombinant expression of the norovirus VP1 capsid protein induces self-assembly into non-infectious virus-like particles (VLPs), which have demonstrated significant potential vaccine platforms against norovirus infections [15,17].

To date, no definitive or universally accepted correlate of protection has been established for norovirus. Consequently, multiple candidate indicators have been explored across studies, including serum HBGA-blocking antibodies, hemagglutination inhibition antibodies, salivary/serum/faecal IgA antibodies, and virus-specific IgG memory B cells [17-20]. Notably, most clinical studies have predominantly focused on serum HBGA-blocking antibodies [17,19,21-23]. The interplay between host genetic factors and immune responses may influence norovirus susceptibility, viral replication and protective outcomes. A key genetic determinant of susceptibility is secretor status, which correlates with HBGA expression on epithelial surfaces[11]. Although a definitive cellular receptor for human noroviruses remains unidentified, HBGAs have been demonstrated to bind to VP1 capsid proteins, facilitating viral attachment and/or cellular entry [24,25]. Accordingly, HBGA-blocking antibodies prevent infection by inhibiting viral-HBGA interactions [26-28].

Therefore, we used Pichia pastoris as a heterologous protein expression system by intracellularly expressing the capsid protein VP1 of four types of noroviruses, including ReNoV GI.1, ReNoV GII.3, ReNoV GII.4 and ReNoV GII.17, respectively, and obtained the VLP monovalent stock solutions of the four types after high-density fermentation, harvesting, antigen release, purification, filtration, etc. The candidate vaccine, prepared by mixing the four monovalent stock solutions and adding aluminium hydroxide adjuvant, was intramuscularly injected to prevent AGE caused by infection with the four genotypes of noroviruses (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4 and GII.17).

Here, a randomized, blinded, multi-cohort, placebo-parallel-controlled Phase I/IIa clinical trial was conducted in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region to evaluate the safety and tolerability of different doses of tetravalent recombinant norovirus vaccine in different populations. Moreover, the vaccine's immunogenicity was investigated, and the appropriate dose of the experimental vaccine for a subsequent clinical trial was determined.

Methods

Participants

Participants were eligible for the study if they were six weeks of age or older. Participants and/or their legal guardians were required to be capable of understanding the study procedures and had to sign an informed consent form (ICF) before participation. Furthermore, the participant’s axillary temperature had to be below 37.3°C. Participants aged six weeks to one year old were also required to have been born at full-term gestation (gestational weeks 37-42) and to have had a birth weight of 2.5-4.0 kg. The exclusion criteria included: abnormal laboratory tests with clinical significance; history of severe allergy to any component of the trial vaccine; previous serious side effects of vaccines or drugs; severe developmental disorders; severe genetic defects; severe nutritional disorders; severe developmental disorders; severe genetic defects; severe malnutrition; any coagulation disorders; contraindications to intramuscular injection; congenital or acquired immunodeficiency; convulsions; epilepsy, encephalopathies and psychiatric illnesses; absence of the spleen; functional absence of the spleen; severe cardiovascular diseases; severe hepatic or renal diseases; severe liver or kidney diseases; and diabetes with complications.

Study design

This trial was designed as a Phase I/IIa trial and was conducted in two stages. Phase I was the age/dose-escalation phase and was conducted at the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention of Liuzhou City. Phase IIa was the dose expansion phase and was conducted at the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention of Rongan City. The trial complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Code for Quality Management of Pharmaceutical Clinical Trials. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (Approval No. GXIRB2020-0030). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. A total of 340 subjects were enrolled in Phase I. Enrolment proceeded sequentially, stratified by age group and dose level. Age cohorts were enrolled in the following order: adults [18-59 years], followed by children [6-17 years] and elderly [≥60 years], then young children [2-5 years], and finally infants [6 weeks-23 months]. Within each age stratum, subjects were enrolled from low dose to high dose. Cohorts were structured as follows: adults, children, and young children randomized 1:1:1 to the low-dose group (12.5 μg/type/0.5 ml/vial), high-dose group (25 μg/type/0.5 ml/vial) or placebo (alum-containing) group; elderly and infants randomized 1:1:1:1 to the low-dose group, high-dose group, placebo (alum-containing) group or placebo (alum-free) group. A total of 240 participants (80 elderly, 80 young children, and 80 infants) were enrolled in Phase IIa, randomized 1:1 to the low-dose group or high-dose group. Within each age cohort, high-dose/placebo group enrolment was initiated after investigator-confirmed safety (seven-day post-first-dose safety data for low-dose/placebo subgroups). Cross-cohort escalation to the children and elderly cohorts required Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) approval based on safety data from adults (first dose to 30 days post-full immunization). Progression to young children cohorts required DSMB approval after safety review of the children and elderly cohorts.

Vaccines

The trial vaccine was produced by Anhui Zhifei Longcom Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd. It contained recombinant HuNoV GI.1-VP1 protein, HuNoV GII.3-VP1 protein, HuNoV GII.4-VP1 protein and HuNoV GII.17-VP1 protein. Each dose contained 12.5 μg/type/0.5 ml/vial (low dose) or 25 μg/type/0.5 ml/vial (high dose). All participants received three doses of the vaccine, 30 days apart. For individuals under one year of age, the vaccine was administered via intramuscular injection in the anterolateral thigh. For individuals one year of age and older, the vaccine was administered via intramuscular injection in the deltoid muscle of the upper arm.

Safety and reactogenicity

Participants were required to remain under observation at the site for 30 minutes following the administration of each vaccine dose to monitor and record any immediate adverse events. A diary card was provided to document body temperature, local reactions at the vaccination site, systemic reactions, and unsolicited adverse events occurring within 0–7 days post-vaccination. A contact card was also provided to record any unsolicited adverse events occurring between 8–30 days post-vaccination. All serious adverse events were followed up and recorded from the first dose until six months after the third dose of the vaccination. For participants aged ≥24 months in the age/dose-escalation phase, clinically significant changes in laboratory parameters were observed before the first dose and after each subsequent dose. The severity of adverse events was assessed according to the 2019 NMPA “Guidelines for Grading Standards of Adverse Events in Clinical Trials of Preventive Vaccines” and the 2014 NIH “Division of AIDS (DAIDS) Table for Grading the Severity of Adult and Paediatric Adverse Events” (Supplementary Material Table 1). The investigator determined the causality of adverse events, and all participants who had received at least one dose of the vaccine were included in the safety analysis set.

Immunogenicity

For adults, the elderly, young children and infants, 2.5 ml blood samples were collected before vaccination and 30 days after the completion of the three-dose vaccination series. Blood samples were not collected from children aged 6–17 years, as this age group is not considered at high risk for norovirus infection and was therefore excluded from the target population for late-phase clinical trials [29,30]. The serum was separated and stored at−20°C or below. The samples were sent to the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control for the detection of IgA, IgG using Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay (ELISA) and HBGA-blocking antibodies using HBGA-blocking antibodies assay [26-28]. The seroconversion rates (defined as a 4-fold or greater increase in antibody titres) of specific IgA, IgG and HBGA-blocking antibodies were analysed 30 days after the completion of the vaccination. The immunogenicity analysis set included elderly, young children and infants who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria, were randomized, completed the full vaccination series according to the protocol, and had valid antibody titre results from blood samples collected before and after vaccination for immunogenicity evaluation. For IgA/IgG detection, recombinant VLPs were diluted to optimal concentrations and coated onto 96-well microplates. Serially diluted serum calibrators were used to generate standard curves. The calibrators for IgA and IgG are sourced from human serum. All sera undergo standardization and rigorous quality control to ensure their qualification as calibrators. Test samples were diluted to fall within the quantification range of the standard curve and added to the coated plates for incubation. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG/IgA secondary antibodies were diluted to working concentrations, added to the plates, and incubated. Following incubation, chromogenic substrate was added for colour development under light-protected conditions. The reaction was terminated with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm. For HBGAs Blocking Assay, Porcine gastric mucin type III (PGM) solution containing HBGAs was diluted and coated onto 96-well microplates. VLPs were diluted in sample dilution buffer and pre-incubated with serum samples. Diluted VLPs served as the positive control, while sample dilution buffer alone served as the blank control. The pre-incubated mixtures were then transferred to the coated microplates and incubated. Subsequently, diluted rabbit polyclonal antibody (primary antibody) was added to the wells and incubated, followed by incubation with diluted HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. Colour development was initiated by adding chromogenic substrate under light-protected conditions, and the reaction was terminated with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The titer of blocking antibodies was calculated using GraphPad Prism via dose–response inhibition analysis (log (inhibitor) vs. normalized response – Variable slope). The primary antibody used in the assay is a rabbit polyclonal antibody prepared in-house. The preparation process is summarized as follows: New Zealand rabbits were immunized with Norovirus VLP protein. Rabbit serum was collected and purified using a Protein A/G column to obtain the polyclonal antibody. After quality control testing, it was validated and used as the primary antibody for the HBGA blocking assay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 software. The incidence of adverse events 30 days after the completion of the vaccination series was compared between groups using Fisher's exact test. The seroconversion rates of specific IgA, IgG and HBGA-blocking antibodies for the four genotypes (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, GII.17) were calculated, and the 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the Clopper–Pearson method. Intergroup differences were assessed using the chi-square test. The geometric mean concentration (GMC) of specific IgA and IgG antibodies and the geometric mean titre (GMT) of HBGA-blocking antibodies for the four genotypes were calculated for each group. Intergroup differences were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) on log-transformed data. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographic characteristics

In the dose-escalation and dose-expansion stages of the clinical trial, a total of 794 volunteers aged over six weeks of age were screened, with 580 randomized into four groups: low-dose group (220 participants), high-dose group (220 participants), aluminium-containing placebo group (100 participants) and aluminium-free placebo group (40 participants). The completion rates were 208 (94.55%) in the low-dose group, 207 (94.09%) in the high-dose group, 90 (90.00%) in the aluminium-containing placebo group and 37 (92.50%) in the aluminium-free placebo group (Table 1). All randomized participants were stratified into four cohorts, with comparable baseline demographic characteristics among the groups (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Grading of adverse reactions among all participants

The severity of adverse reactions in the low-dose group, high-dose group, aluminium-containing placebo group and aluminium-free placebo group was primarily classified as Grade 1 or Grade 2. The incidence rates of Grade 1 adverse reactions were 17.81%, 22.48%, 22.22% and 15.00% in the low-dose, high-dose, aluminium-containing placebo and aluminium-free placebo groups, respectively. Grade 2 adverse reactions occurred in 9.13%, 12.39%, 8.08% and 10.00% of participants in these groups, respectively. The incidence rates of adverse reactions of Grade ≥3 were 3.65%, 2.29%, 3.03% and 0.00% in the low-dose, high-dose, aluminium-containing placebo and aluminium-free placebo groups, respectively. Notably, the incidence rates of adverse reactions did not increase with the number of doses administered across all groups. No fatalities were reported during the study. The rates of serious adverse events (SAEs) were 6.85%, 7.34%, 9.09% and 7.50% in the low-dose, high-dose, aluminium-containing placebo and aluminium-free placebo groups, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences in the SAE rates between the groups. Of note, the low-dose group (children) experienced one SAE related to vaccination, which was proteinuria detected during the assessment. During the follow-up period, the participant did not report any discomfort or medication use and fully recovered.

Distribution of adverse reaction symptoms among all participants

Systemic reactions were predominant in all groups, with fever being the most common adverse reaction, followed by diarrhoea. Other systemic symptoms included vomiting, anorexia, fatigue and headache. The local reactions primarily consisted of pain, along with other symptoms such as induration, swelling, erythema and itching. No statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of adverse reactions between the groups (Table 2).

Immunogenicity

Seroconversion rates among all participants

Thirty days after full vaccination, the seroconversion rates for GI.1 IgA antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 66.48%, 67.21% and 16.35%, respectively. For GII.3 IgA antibodies, the rates were 86.81%, 85.25% and 13.46%, respectively. For GII.4 IgA antibodies, the rates were 49.45%, 66.12% and 18.27%, respectively. For GII.17 IgA antibodies, the rates were 70.33%, 77.05% and 12.50%, respectively. Both the low-dose and high-dose groups demonstrated significantly higher seroconversion rates of IgA antibodies compared to the placebo group, with statistically significant differences observed between the vaccine candidates and placebo (P < 0.0001) (Figure 1).

Post-vaccination seroconversion rates (defined as a ≥ 4-fold increase in antibody titres) of IgA, IgG and HBGA-blocking antibodies against four norovirus genotypes (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, GII.17) across all study groups.

Thirty days after full vaccination, the seroconversion rates for GI.1 IgG antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 78.02%, 85.25% and 25.00%, respectively. For GII.3 IgG antibodies, the rates were 93.41%, 92.35% and 18.27%, respectively. For GII.4 IgG antibodies, the rates were 63.74%, 72.13% and 16.35%, respectively. For GII.17 IgG antibodies, the rates were 86.81%, 84.70% and 8.65%, respectively. Both the low-dose and high-dose groups demonstrated significantly higher seroconversion rates of IgG antibodies compared to the placebo group, with statistically significant differences observed between the vaccine candidates and placebo (P < 0.0001) (Figure 1).

Thirty days after full vaccination, the seroconversion rates for GI.1 HBGA-blocking antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 76.92%, 89.62% and 7.69%, respectively. For GII.3 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the rates were 86.26%, 89.07% and 4.81%, respectively. For GII.4 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the rates were 64.84%, 78.14% and 11.54%, respectively. For GII.17 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the rates were 76.92%, 83.61% and 8.65%, respectively. Both the low-dose and high-dose groups demonstrated significantly higher seroconversion rates of HBGA-blocking antibodies compared to the placebo group, with statistically significant differences observed between the vaccine candidates and placebo (P < 0.0001) (Figure 1).

Geometric mean concentration (GMC) or geometric mean titre (GMT) of antibodies among all participants post-vaccination

No statistically significant differences in the GMCs of all IgA antibody types were observed among the high-dose, low-dose and placebo groups before immunization (Supplementary Material Table 3). Thirty days after full vaccination, the GMCs of GI.1 IgA antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 720.53, 1154.83 and 73.54 AU/ml, respectively. For GII.3 IgA antibodies, the GMC values were 2657.79, 2353.53 and 156.88 AU/ml, respectively. For GII.4 IgA antibodies, the GMC values were 2967.48, 2992.37 and 416.99 AU/ml, respectively. For GII.17 IgA antibodies, the GMC values were 1316.30, 1658.55 and 208.79 AU/ml, respectively. The GMCs of IgA antibodies for all types in both the low-dose and high-dose groups were significantly higher than those in the placebo group, with statistically significant differences between the vaccine groups and the placebo group (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2).

Post-vaccination GMCs or GMTs of IgA, IgG and HBGA-blocking antibodies against four norovirus genotypes (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, GII.17) across all study groups.

No statistically significant differences in the GMCs of IgG antibodies were observed between the high-dose and low-dose groups across all genotypes before immunization (Supplementary Material Table 4). For the GII.4 genotype, pre-immunization IgG antibody GMCs were significantly different among the high-dose, low-dose and placebo groups (P = 0.0371), while no significant intergroup differences were detected for the other genotypes. Thirty days after full vaccination, the GMCs of GI.1 IgG antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 91,176.50 AU/ml, 114,555.85 AU/ml and 5,996.85 AU/ml, respectively. For GII.4 IgG antibodies, the GMC values were 117,364.06 AU/ml, 120,682.51 AU/ml and 12,396.15 AU/ml, respectively. For GII.17 IgG antibodies, the GMC values were 58,990.77 AU/ml, 64,664.57 AU/ml and 3,270.41 AU/ml, respectively. The GMCs of IgG antibodies for all types in both the low-dose and high-dose groups were significantly higher than those in the placebo group, with statistically significant differences between the vaccine groups and the placebo group (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2).

No statistically significant differences in the GMTs of all HBGA-blocking antibody types were observed among the high-dose, low-dose and placebo groups before immunization (Supplementary Material Table 5). Thirty days after full vaccination, the GMTs of GI.1 HBGA-blocking antibodies in the low-dose group, high-dose group and placebo group were 148.92, 205.74 and 24.40, respectively. For GII.3 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the GMT values were 429.49, 451.30 and 36.23, respectively. For GII.4 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the GMT values were 187.84, 201.50 and 50.48, respectively. For GII.17 HBGA-blocking antibodies, the GMT values were 108.85, 127.81 and 21.05, respectively. The GMTs of HBGA-blocking antibodies for all types in both the low-dose and high-dose groups were significantly higher than those in the placebo group, with statistically significant differences between the vaccine groups and the placebo group (P < 0.0001) (Figure 2).

Discussion

In the current study, the vaccine demonstrated favourable safety and tolerability profiles. AEs primarily consisted of transient vaccination-site pain and self-limiting fever, typically resolving with routine outpatient management or self-administered medication. Most AEs (predominantly Grade 1-2) occurred within 0–7 days post-vaccination, with no dose-dependent increase in the incidence of AEs across administrations. No statistically significant safety differences emerged between the dose groups. The incidence rates of adverse reactions in the low-dose group, high-dose group, aluminium-containing placebo group and aluminium-free placebo group were 27.85%, 30.28%, 28.28% and 22.50%, respectively, with no significant intergroup differences observed. This indicates that the trial vaccine did not demonstrate substantial safety risks compared to placebo controls. Systemic reactions across all groups predominantly manifested as fever (predominant presentation), followed by diarrhoea, with additional symptoms including vomiting, anorexia, fatigue and headache. Local reactions were exclusively observed in the low-dose group (5.02%), high-dose group (8.72%) and aluminium-containing placebo group (4.04%), primarily characterized by pain at the injection site, accompanied by induration, swelling, erythema and pruritus. The overall adverse reaction profile and symptom spectrum were consistent with data from comparable products [17-19,31,32]. Notably, the higher incidence of local reactions in the high-dose group may be attributed to its elevated antigen content and the localized irritant effects of the aluminium adjuvant. No significant differences were detected in laboratory parameter abnormalities among the study groups.

In the trial, both the seroconversion rates and GMTs of HBGA-blocking antibodies were significantly higher in the vaccine group than in the placebo group. The candidate vaccine demonstrated favourable immunogenicity in participants aged six weeks and older following a three-dose injection series. The high-dose group exhibited higher HBGA-blocking antibody seroconversion rates against GI.1 and GII.4 genotypes, as well as higher GMTs for the GI.1 and GII.17 genotypes compared to the low-dose group, with statistically significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05). Additionally, the high-dose group showed higher HBGA-blocking antibody seroconversion rates for the GII.3 and GII.17 genotypes and higher GMTs for the GII.3 and GII.4 genotypes compared to the low-dose group, though these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the IgA and IgG antibody seroconversion rates and GMTs were predominantly higher in the high-dose group than in the low-dose group, aligning with the overall trend observed for HBGA-blocking antibodies. These results collectively indicate that the high-dose group achieved superior immunogenicity compared to the low-dose group. Furthermore, compared to GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, and GII.17, we observed the low seroconvertion rates, as well as lower GMC and HBGA blocking titer for GII.4. Analysis of pre-vaccination GMC and HBGA blocking titers revealed that participants possessed higher pre-existing GMC and HBGA blocking antibodies against GII.4 compared to GI.1, GII.3, and GII.17 (Supplementary Material Table 3 and Table 4 and Table 5). Consequently, the seroconversion rates calculated based on pre – and post-vaccination GMC and HBGA titers were slightly lower for GII.4 than for GI.1, GII.3, and GII.17, a finding consistent with results reported for TAK-214[18,19]. Additionally, surveillance data on norovirus epidemiology in China in recent years indicate that GII.4 has persistently been the predominant circulating strain [33,34]. The higher frequency of GII.4 infections has resulted in elevated baseline antibody levels against GII.4 in the majority of the adult population, which aligns with our serological data.

Meanwhile, age-stratified analyses revealed that the seroconversion rates and the GMTs of HBGA-blocking antibodies were significantly higher in the vaccine group than in the placebo group. This finding is consistent with trends observed among all participants. Notably, the post-vaccination seroconversion rates and GMTs of HBGA-blocking antibodies against the four norovirus strains (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4 and GII.17) were lower in the elderly and adult groups than in the infant and young children groups (Supplementary Material Table 7 and Table 8). We suspect that the main reason for the lower seroconversion rates in the elderly and young compared to infants and young children may be that we define a 4-fold increase in GMT as a positive conversion rate, and that the elderly and young may have higher antibodies than infants and young children due to prior infection exposure (Supplementary Material Table 6). The reason why the GMTs of HBGA antibody in the elderly is lower than that in infants may be due to immunosenescence, both the innate and adaptive immune systems experience age-related impairments that contribute to insufficient protection following vaccination [35,36]. These differential immunogenicity patterns across age groups provide important empirical evidence to inform participant selection strategies for subsequent clinical trial phases. Furthermore, investigating the interrelationships among IgA, IgG, and HBGA-blocking antibodies represents a key focus of our research. Our experiments involved measuring serum levels of IgA, IgG, and blocking antibodies in individuals across different age groups, followed by analysis to determine whether correlated trends exist in the variations of these three antibodies across age strata. We did not assess the differential capabilities of IgG and IgA antibodies in blocking the binding between norovirus and HBGAs in this study; this aspect warrants further investigation in future work.

This candidate vaccine is designed to prevent acute gastroenteritis caused by infection with the four norovirus genotypes (GI.1, GII.3, GII.4, and GII.17). Accordingly, these advantages are mainly reflected in the following two aspects: Firstly, while most reported vaccines cover only two norovirus genotypes, our candidate vaccine includes four, potentially offering broader protection during norovirus outbreaks. The development of a multivalent VLP vaccine, covering a broader range of variants, has the potential to enhance population protection against norovirus. Secondly, data from the Phase I/IIa trial indicate satisfactory immunogenicity across all age groups, particularly in infants and young children (Supplementary Materials Tables 7 and 8), which we find encouraging. Establishing an optimal dosing strategy and target population is of practical importance for informing subsequent clinical trials and supporting further vaccine development. We recognize the challenges inherent in norovirus vaccine development, including the high genetic diversity of circulating strains, limited understanding of the immune correlates of protection, and the absence of well-defined protective immune markers [13,14,20]. The current study aims to assess the safety and preliminary immunogenicity of the candidate vaccine; protective efficacy has not been evaluated herein. It is important to note that immunogenicity and HBGA-blocking antibody responses may not reliably predict vaccine efficacy. Evaluation of protective efficacy will be addressed in subsequent clinical trials (NCT06524947).

In conclusion, this vaccine displayed acceptable safety and immunogenicity characteristics. While the safety profiles were comparable between the dose groups, the high-dose formulation demonstrated superior immunogenic performance compared to the low-dose regimen. These findings provide a theoretical basis and empirical support for subsequent clinical investigations.
