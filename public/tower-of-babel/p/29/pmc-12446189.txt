The role of moist wound dressings in the management of sensitivity to leukoplast adhesive: A case report
Authors: Mohammad Raza Faramarzi, Hossein Alikhah, Esmaiel Maghsoodi, Erfan Hosseinian
Published: 2025

Introduction

The skin is the human body's largest organ, serving multiple functions, including acting as a barrier, providing sensory input, and offering defense mechanisms. It acts as an intermediary that facilitates the connection of vital monitoring devices and serves as a barrier to access other organs, such as veins and pleura [1]. In elderly individuals, the bond between the adhesive and epidermis is often stronger than the bond between the epidermis and dermis, leading to the removal of the epidermal layer when adhesives are removed, thus compromising the skin's barrier function. Additionally, adhesives on the skin can result in allergic contact dermatitis, a known side effect of medical adhesive bandages and applied medical devices [2].

Wound healing is a complex and continuous process that requires an appropriate environment. Wound dressings, serving as temporary substitutes for damaged skin at the wound site, can temporarily restore the skin's function at the wound location. Consequently, wound dressings have become one of the most critical therapeutic methods in clinical wound treatment [3]. Hydrogels are extensively utilized in wound healing applications because they create a moist environment at the wound site, absorb tissue exudates, allow oxygen permeability, and act as a barrier against microbial invasion [4]. Specifically, multifunctional hydrogel dressings have garnered significant attention [5]. Hydrogel dressings with wet adhesion can adhere to the wound tissue in the presence of exudate, blood, water, etc., to stop bleeding, prevent infection, and enhance wound healing [6,7].

In this context, we utilized hydrocolloid dressings, which provide a moist and insulating environment for healing, protecting non-infected wounds while allowing the body's enzymes to aid healing. These dressings are unique because they are easy to apply and require less frequent replacement than other wound dressings. Moreover, they are flexible to form around the skin and body movements and provide a moist environment that allows the body's enzymes to assist in healing. In this case, we highlight not only the standard pharmacological management but also the specific contribution of hydrocolloid dressings in preventing secondary complications such as maceration, infection, and delayed epithelialization, which are common in elderly patients with fragile skin. This study aims to clarify the unique benefits of moist wound dressings as an adjunct to systemic therapy in adhesive-induced bullous reactions.

Case presentation

Patient information

The patient was a 70-year-old Persian woman weighing 68 kg and standing 1.60 m tall. She was a widow without health insurance, living with her son's family. Of Kurdish descent and a Sunni Muslim, she originated from Iran and belonged to a family of moderate socio-economic status. Her medical history included hyperthyroidism and hypertension. She was hospitalized due to seizures and loss of consciousness. The patient had previously experienced an allergic reaction to adhesive tape (leukoplast), which caused hives and itching. However, blood blisters developed on her skin 4 h after using the adhesive tape. A neurologist subsequently referred her to a wound care specialist for evaluation and intervention.

Clinical findings

The assessment confirmed the presence of blood blisters on the skin. Upon examination using the TIME framework (Tissue Management, Inflammation, Moisture, and Edge of the Wound), it was found that the tissue was clean, with the dermal layer involved in three sections and exhibiting coagulation. Differential diagnosis

Given the patient's age and clinical presentation of large blood-filled bullae, several differential diagnoses were systematically considered. Bullous pemphigoid, an autoimmune blistering disease common in elderly patients, was evaluated as a primary differential diagnosis. ELISA testing for bullous pemphigoid antibodies (BP180 and BP230) was performed and returned negative results, effectively ruling out this autoimmune condition. The clinical presentation strongly favored adhesive-induced injury based on the acute temporal relationship (4 h post-application) and localized distribution matching the adhesive placement. Comprehensive medication history review excluded furosemide and anticoagulants as contributing factors, confirming that the patient's current regimen did not include medications known to predispose to bullous formation or bleeding complications.

Severity comparison with the patient's previous allergic reactions revealed significant escalation from mild hives and pruritus to blood-filled bullae formation, suggesting progression from Type I to a more severe hypersensitivity reaction. Other differentials including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (excluded due to lack of mucosal involvement), drug-induced bullous eruptions (ruled out after medication review), and friction blisters (eliminated due to rapid onset pattern) were systematically considered. The final diagnosis of severe adhesive sensitivity reaction with secondary bullous formation was established based on clinical presentation, temporal relationship, patient history, and negative autoimmune serology.

Therapeutic intervention

According to the 2021 Wound Management guidelines, the blisters were aspirated, and blood blisters were fully debrided, followed by a moisture-retentive dressing. The attending physician prescribed topical and intravenous hydrocortisone (50 mg twice daily) and intravenous cefazolin (1 g every 8 h). Additionally, the patient was educated on maintaining adequate fluid intake and adhering to a high-protein diet enriched with vitamins, including Vitamin C.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was instructed to return to the center after four days for follow-up. Initially, there was no moisture or maceration around the wound; however, moisture accumulation was observed following the application of the dressing. Discussion

The novelty of this case lies not in the use of corticosteroids, which is standard, but in the deliberate integration of a hydrocolloid moist dressing to address the unique challenges posed by adhesive-induced bullous lesions in an elderly patient. Unlike previous reports that focus solely on allergen identification and avoidance, our approach demonstrates how personalized wound care (specifically, the use of advanced moist dressings) can prevent common complications such as wound desiccation, secondary infection, and delayed healing. This is particularly relevant in populations at higher risk for poor wound outcomes, such as the elderly or those with comorbidities.

The presented case report underscores the pivotal role of moist wound dressings in managing sensitivity to Leukoplast adhesive. The results of this study are consistent with previous research highlighting the benefits of moist wound healing. A study by Winter first introduced the concept of moist wound healing, indicating that wounds heal faster in a moist environment compared to a dry one [9]. More recent research by Gounden and Singh (2024) and She et al. (2020), further supports this, demonstrating that moisture-retentive dressings can reduce healing time and minimize pain [10,11]. This aligns with the current study, where applying hydrocolloid dressing facilitated wound healing and reduced discomfort. The absence of initial moisture or maceration may have contributed to effective control of inflammation; as Nuutila et al. (2021) noted, managing the wound's moisture level is crucial in preventing secondary infections and promoting healing [9].

The literature supports using corticosteroids and antibiotics to manage inflammatory responses [12], and prevent infections, respectively [13]. In this case, the administration of hydrocortisone and cefazolin aligns with established protocols, reflecting their role in reducing inflammation and infection risk. Additionally, integrating dietary recommendations, including fluid intake and a high-protein diet rich in vitamins, particularly Vitamin C, is consistent with evidence suggesting enhanced wound healing through nutritional support [14].

The scientific rationale for the interventions is grounded in the pathophysiology of adhesive sensitivity, which can manifest as contact dermatitis or, in severe cases, blistering [15].The blister formation in this patient, following the application of Leukoplast adhesive, indicates an acute inflammatory response, warranting immediate action to prevent further tissue damage. The use of hydrocolloid dressings is justified by their ability to absorb exudate while maintaining a moist wound environment, which is crucial for healing.

A comparative review of similar cases in the literature reveals varying approaches to adhesive-related skin injuries. Atwater et al. (2022) reported multiple cases of medical adhesive allergens managed with topical corticosteroids and barrier creams [2], while Hamnerius et al. (2023) described colophonium-related allergic contact dermatitis treated with systemic corticosteroids and avoidance strategies [3]. However, few studies have documented the comprehensive multimodal approach employed in our case. The rationale for selecting hydrocolloid dressings over alternative options was multifaceted: (1) superior moisture retention compared to traditional gauze dressings, (2) reduced frequency of dressing changes minimizing trauma to healing tissue, (3) conformability to body contours ensuring optimal contact, (4) transparent or translucent properties allowing wound monitoring without removal, and (5) proven efficacy in managing exudate while maintaining optimal moisture balance. Alternative options such as foam dressings, alginate dressings, or hydrogel sheets were considered but deemed less suitable due to either inadequate adhesion properties or suboptimal moisture management for this specific presentation.

Beyond conventional moist wound dressings, alternative therapeutic approaches have shown promise in wound management. According to Kushmakov et al., local ozone therapy may shorten the course of treatment and reduce the risk of infection. The mechanism of ozone therapy involves the generation of reactive oxygen species that exhibit antimicrobial properties, enhance oxygen delivery to tissues, and stimulate cellular metabolism, thereby promoting wound healing. Ozone's bactericidal, virucidal, and fungicidal effects are attributed to its ability to disrupt microbial cell walls and interfere with enzymatic systems. Additionally, ozone therapy can improve local circulation and enhance the immune response at the wound site. However, regarding the disadvantages of ozone therapy, such as ozone gas toxicity, its therapeutic utility depends on the concentration, the site of administration, and the treatment type [16]. Ozone treatment was used to prevent a 67-year-old lady with a diabetic foot from getting her foot amputated in a case study by Aytacoglu et al. While ozone therapy presents an interesting alternative, the choice of treatment modality should be individualized based on wound characteristics, patient factors, and available resources [17].

The limitation of this study is the potential variability in patient response to adhesive products, which may not be generalizable to all patients with similar reactions. Additionally, we recognize that the improvement observed in this patient may be largely attributable to systemic corticosteroid therapy, which is the established first-line treatment for severe allergic contact dermatitis. The lack of a control group or comparative arm limits our ability to definitively attribute the observed benefits to the moist wound dressing alone. However, the rapid resolution of peri-wound pain, absence of maceration, and accelerated re-epithelialization suggest that the hydrocolloid dressing played a supportive role in optimizing the local wound environment and patient comfort.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case report highlights the importance of individualized wound care management in patients with adhesive sensitivity. The primary takeaway is the effectiveness of combining moist wound dressings with pharmacological and nutritional interventions to enhance healing outcomes. This case emphasizes the need for heightened vigilance in identifying and addressing sensitivity reactions to medical adhesives, ensuring patient safety and comfort.
