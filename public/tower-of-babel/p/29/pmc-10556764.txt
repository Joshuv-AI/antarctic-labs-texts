Introduction

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) refers to the focused assessment of patients with ultrasound at the bedside. It has been integral to the initial evaluation of critically ill and trauma patients in the Emergency Department (ED) [1,2]. Low-resource countries have been using POCUS increasingly, even are more challenges [3,4]. The results of POCUS help to rapidly evaluate patients with undifferentiated complaints and guide early appropriate intervention, particularly for critically ill patients and patients with significant trauma [5].

Shock is a life-threatening condition which requires prompt recognition, diagnosis, and intervention [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Even though a diagnosis of shock is made by hypotension, defined by systolic blood pressure (SBP) <90 mmHg, or mean arterial pressure (MAP) <65 mmHg and evidence of hypoperfusion evidenced by a lactic acid level >1.5 mmol/L along with the clinical picture and physical examination, its accurate diagnosis can be difficult in certain cases [11]. It is also challenging to obtain serum lactate in resource-limited countries. The cause of shock is multifactorial, which can lead to very low blood pressure. Any lack of improvement with intervention should alert the treating provider to search for other causes, such as acute occlusion of the artery. Acute occlusion could be the result of emboli and blunt trauma. Gradual occlusion can occur from infiltrative diseases and fibromuscular dysplasia [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Bilateral occlusions are very rare.

In this article, we report the role of point-of-care ultrasound in the absence of radial pulse and unrecordable blood pressure during the assessment of shock in a rare bilateral upper extremity occlusion. The work has been reported per the SCARE criteria [17].

Case presentation

A 60-year-old male patient presented to the ED with a chief complaint of left-side flank pain of 1-week duration. The pain was not clearly described or characterized. He reported urinary frequency, urgency, dribbling, and a feeling of incomplete bladder emptying. Furthermore, the patients endorsed vomiting of ingested matter two to three times daily for the same duration. The patient had a past medical history of hypertension and was taking Amlodipine 5 mg daily with good drug adherence. Social history revealed a smoking history of 40 pack years.

On clinical examination, the blood pressure and radial pulse were undetectable, respiratory rate were 24–32 breaths per minute, the temperature was 36.8 degrees Celsius, and oxygen saturation was 96 % on room air. The patient was triaged as having possible septic shock secondary to a urinary tract infection (UTI). On physical examination, the chest was clear; there was no abdominal tenderness, no murmurs, and the gross neurological exam was unremarkable. There were no cyanosis, paresis, sensory or motor deficits in his extremities. There was a palpable femoral pulse, but his pedal pulse was undetectable. He was oriented to person, place, and time, and there was no other pertinent positive finding in systemic examinations.

The patient was resuscitated with 30 mL/kg of Ringer's lactate. Broad-spectrum antibiotics and vasopressors were promptly initiated.

On subsequent patient reassessment, despite unrecordable blood pressure, the patient was making adequate urine with heart rate on the monitor showing 120–150 beats per minute. Unfortunately, due to resource limitations, the hospital does not have the capacity to record 12 lead ECG and we rely on the patients' s single lead monitor tracings. The patient became agitated after the beginning of vasopressors, and the clinical picture of septic shock was questioned. A thorough physical examination demonstrated absent radial and brachial pulse bilaterally. A point-of-care ultrasound performed by the emergency physician showed a non-compressible visible clot and an absence of color Doppler flow, showing bilateral brachial artery occlusion (Fig. 1). A radiologist confirmed the diagnosis. There was a normal IVC with a 40 % collapsibility index and hyperactive precordium. The POCUS of the lungs was also unremarkable. The vasopressors were discontinued. Laboratory testing was pertinent for leukocytosis (24,000/uL), elevated creatinine, elevated alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase (>2 times the upper limit of normal). The urinalysis did not show a urinary tract infection. The hospital does not have a CT scan, and patients oftentimes need to go to another facility to receive advanced imaging studies, which is difficult to facilitate in critically-ill patients. Anti-coagulation was started, and intravenous antibiotics were continued. Discussion

Shock is a commonly encountered condition in critically-ill patients. Systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure <65 mmHg, tachycardia, impaired mental status, oliguria, and biochemical indicators such as hyperlactemia are used to make the diagnosis of shock [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Very low blood pressure or heart rate may lead to unreadable blood pressure/undetectable pulses in certain limbs. These findings should improve with medical intervention. Another cause for similar events is acute occlusion of the arteries. The acute occlusion could result from emboli, blunt trauma, infiltrative diseases, and fibromuscular dysplasia [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16],[18], [19], [20]]. Bilateral occlusions are very rare. If the low blood pressure was from septic shock or pump failure, the blood pressure should have improved with fluid resuscitation and vasopressors. Still, the high heart rate on the monitor and the lack of blood pressure recording in our case pointed to an alternative diagnosis. Point-of-care ultrasound will be beneficial in situations where there is no arterial line for hemodynamic monitoring or serum lactate level to assess tissue perfusion [5].

Assessment of the peripheral pulses (usually radial pulse) is an important clue to estimate the systolic blood pressure with the presence of a pulse correlating to a systolic SBP of ≥80 mmHg [21]. In contrast to this study, this case report showed an absent peripheral radial and brachial pulse despite the patient appearing hemodynamically stable and no other signs of shock [6]. According to the study done by Antonelli et al. [6], clinical bedside examination using capillary refill times, skin temperature, and pulse pressure have not been sufficiently precise and reproducible to estimate cardiac output [6]. Based on the peripheral pulse and blood pressure measurement, our patient was diagnosed as shock and started on double vasopressor, where he developed an adverse effect of tachyarrhythmia and altered mental status. By utilizing vascular POCUS, the patient's diagnosis completely changed. Due to the scarcity of information and diagnostic tools, this is frequently misdiagnosed as a shock, resulting in exposure to unnecessary vasopressors resulting in poor patient outcomes. The use of POCUS led to an instant diagnosis and early therapeutic intervention.

Bilateral brachial artery occlusion is a rare event that can occur in the setting of atrial fibrillation, sepsis, or fibromuscular dysplasia. Emboli, local arteriosclerosis disease process, blunt trauma, and local infiltrative processes lead to occlusion [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16],[18], [19], [20]]. Prompt diagnosis is of paramount importance for early intervention to save a limb. The diagnosis requires diagnostic tests such as angiography, Duplex sonogram, or CT scan. Due to a lack of recorded ECG it was not clear if the patient had atrial fibrillation, and there was no previous history of atrial fibrillation. There was no diagnostic test that indicated if the patient had fibromuscular dysplasia. There was no obvious trauma or sign of visible or palpable tumor of the upper extremities. Given the normal urinalysis and bilateral brachial artery thrombosis, the flank pain could also have been secondary to renal artery thrombosis. The patient may have had intermittent atrial fibrillation that showered emboli.

Similarly, according to a case report by Alaka K (2016), a 24-year-old Hindu female patient was referred with asymptomatic low blood pressure. She was diagnosed as Takayasu's arteritis, also known as pulseless disease or occlusive thromboaortopathy [13]. A systematic head-to-toe ABCDs Resuscitation Ultrasound approach (ResUS) will be helpful for the workup of critically ill patients [5].

Physicians facing diagnosis dilemmas approach patients' conditions with algorithms and guidelines based on chief complaints, vital signs, and physical examination results. In resource-limited setting, the diagnostic tests are limited or not available at all. Some examples of shortcomings are lack of ECG papers, lack of laboratory reagents, and malfunctioning devices with no supply of replacement parts. Furthermore, the lack of specialty services to give expert advice and valuable guidance leads to failure to diagnose or treat. Point-of-care ultrasound is now widely available even in resource-limited settings, partially thanks to donations. Basic knowledge can be easily taught in a short period of time, and it is non-invasive and will not interfere with patient evaluation. Emergency medicine programs train their physician to perform and interpret basic ultrasounds such as gallbladder, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), limited cardiac, limited obstetrics, optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD), lung and focused assessment with sonography in trauma (FAST). The advantage of the POCUS is that it is available for patients who cannot leave the emergency department and can be performed at the bedside.

Conclusion

Point-of-care ultrasound is a valuable diagnostic tool that can help narrow down differential diagnostics and guide early proper management and intervention. When available, POCUS should be used systematically in all critically ill patients to identify a source for a shock or rule out disease entity that can lead to a shock.
