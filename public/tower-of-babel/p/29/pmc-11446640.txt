Induction to Methadone 80 mg in the First Week of Treatment of Patients Who Use Fentanyl: A Case Series From an Outpatient Opioid Treatment Program
Authors: Scott Steiger, Caravella McCuistian, Leslie W. Suen, Brad Shapiro, D. Andrew Tompkins, Alexander R. Bazazi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCurrent guidelines for methadone titration may unnecessarily delay reaching effective doses for patients using fentanyl, resulting in an increased risk of ongoing fentanyl use, dissatisfaction with treatment, and early dropout. Development and evaluation of rapid methadone induction protocols may improve treatment for patients using fentanyl.MethodsRetrospective chart review was conducted for patients admitted in 2022 to a single licensed opioid treatment program (OTP) where a rapid induction protocol provides methadone 40 mg on day 1, 60 mg on day 2, and 80 mg on day 3 to patients using fentanyl <65 years old without significant medical comorbidities. The primary feasibility outcome was completion of the protocol, defined by receipt of methadone dose 80 mg or more on treatment day 7. The primary safety outcomes were oversedation, nonfatal overdose, and death. A secondary outcome was retention in treatment at 30 days.ResultsRapid induction was ordered for 93 patients and completed by 65 (70%). Average dose on day 7 for patients who completed was 89 mg (SD 9.5 mg) versus 49 mg (SD 14.0 mg) for those who did not. No episodes of oversedation, nonfatal overdose, or death were observed. At 30 days, 85% of the patients who had the rapid protocol ordered (79/93) were retained, with 88% (57/65) who completed the protocol retained versus 79% (22/28) who did not complete (OR 1.9, 95% CI 0.6–6.2).ConclusionsRapid induction to methadone 80 mg by day 7 was feasible for outpatients using fentanyl in this study at a single OTP. No significant safety events were identified.

Illicitly manufactured fentanyl and related analogues continue to drive the overdose crisis in the United States, where over 100,000 drug overdose deaths occurred in 2022.1 Medication treatment for opioid use disorder (OUD) using opioid agonist therapies like methadone and buprenorphine were developed to address OUD from heroin, and they are mainstay treatment options proven to reduce overdose mortality.2,3

Methadone and buprenorphine remain effective in reducing mortality and morbidity for individuals using fentanyl, but new challenges have emerged and may continue to evolve as other high-potency synthetic opioids become more prevalent in the drug supply.4,5 The unique pharmacodynamics of fentanyl often lead dependent individuals to use fentanyl several times per day to stave off withdrawal, resulting in development of a much higher opioid tolerance compared to heroin or prescription opioids.6 Buprenorphine appears to be more likely to precipitate opioid withdrawal in patients using fentanyl (vs heroin and other short-acting opioids), complicating the induction process and leading to a proliferation of different strategies for helping patients achieve induction.7 Methadone doses that were effective for patients using heroin seem to be less effective at eliminating fentanyl use, suggesting that higher doses may be required.6,8,9

Methadone induction guidelines are conservative, focused on minimizing overdose risk from the methadone itself rather than rapidly reaching a dose sufficient to suppress withdrawal. One reason for this caution is that studies of individuals conducted before the introduction of fentanyl to illicit drug markets found that risk of overdose is highest in the first 4 weeks after starting methadone.3 The specific reasons for this are not known, and guideline titration schedules are based on patient response to a dose at steady state, which is reached after 4–5 days given an average half-life of 24 hours.10 A 2013 expert panel convened by the American Society of Addiction Medicine recommended a maximum initial dose of 30 mg, followed by 5-mg increases no sooner than every 5 days.11 Other expert panels are marginally more aggressive, recommending an initial dose of 10–30 mg, followed by increases of 5–10 mg no sooner than every 3 to 4 days of daily dosing.12 Following either approach may still require several weeks, if not months, to reach an effective dose—generally estimated to be between 80 and 120 mg for people using heroin—that reduces nonmedical opioid use by suppressing withdrawal and opioid cravings.10 Achieving successful induction may become even more challenging in patients with co-occurring substance use disorders, such as methamphetamine use disorder, which has been linked to decreased retention rates.13,14 Another possibility for the increased mortality early in treatment with methadone, then, is ongoing nonmedical opioid use.

Addiction experts, including frontline clinicians, researchers, and people who use fentanyl, have argued that shortening the time frame to reaching an effective methadone dose is not only feasible without compromising safety but also urgently needed given the high mortality associated with ongoing fentanyl use.12,15,16 The United States Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration released updated regulations in February 2024 that allow an initial dose of up to 50 mg, without new guidance on dose titration.17 Clinics are thus free to innovate in response to patient need, but published outcomes using faster methadone induction protocols are minimal. Alternative strategies to achieve higher methadone levels more quickly have previously been reported, but only in the inpatient setting.18-21 We present a case series describing patient characteristics and early outcomes of all individuals who were offered a rapid induction protocol at a single, hospital-based outpatient Opioid Treatment Program (OTP).

METHODS

The Local Context

The Opiate Treatment Outpatient Program (OTOP) at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital is a publicly funded OTP licensed to serve 810 patients, seeing all individuals regardless of ability to pay. OTOP developed the rapid induction methadone protocol in response to changes in local conditions. The proportion of new patients reporting fentanyl use increased dramatically, and this subgroup appeared to be less likely to be retained in treatment per manual chart review. Many new patients reported to clinicians that they were spending much less money on fentanyl than in the past with heroin. In San Francisco, fentanyl is sold as a powder or in pressed pills and is most commonly smoked, which is markedly different from black tar heroin that is generally injected or snorted.22

Before the development of the rapid induction protocol, the OTOP medical team, composed of nurse practitioners and physicians, tended to order initial dose titration plans on the upper end of guideline-recommended dosing schedules. Standard practice for patients who reported intravenous heroin use was to start methadone at 30 mg and increase to 40 mg on day 2, 50 mg on day 3, with the possibility of going to 60 mg on days 5–6 after reevaluation with the medical team. After that, clinicians evaluated patients every fifth day of attendance to consider dose increase in 10-mg increments. Patients with perfect attendance who were still experiencing withdrawal or drug cravings would thus be able to reach 80 mg after approximately 2 weeks and could be receiving 100 mg before the end of the first month. Even if these doses were inadequate to eliminate drug cravings and ongoing use, patients generally reported relief of withdrawal and reduced nonmedical opioid use. With fentanyl, however, clinicians observed that patients were reporting minimal relief of withdrawal symptoms until they reached a daily dose of 80–100 mg and reported ongoing use of fentanyl for several weeks after intake. Clinicians wondered if a more rapid methadone induction would improve the patient experience, decrease their use of fentanyl, and keep them in treatment longer.

Intervention Description

The medical team prioritized safety as they developed a rapid induction clinical protocol with a goal of helping patients with ongoing withdrawal and cravings to reach 80–100 mg by the end of the first week of treatment. The OTOP medical team developed the new dosing schedule for the protocol by consensus. Patients who gave verbal consent to rapid induction were eligible to receive a total of 40 mg on the first day, 60 mg on the second day, and 80 mg on the third (Table 1). Each of these was an observed dose provided to patients by nurses trained in assessment for signs of intoxication. Thereafter, if patients received their full dose for at least 3 consecutive days, they met with a medical provider to consider an increase of up to 20 mg. Patients who missed 1–2 doses remained at the last dose they received before again proceeding with the titration. If a patient missed 3 or more days, clinic staff telephoned the patient and hospitals in the city to try to locate them. When patients who missed at least 3 days returned to clinic, their dose was reviewed by a medical provider before receiving a dose, as required by federal regulation.23 When a nurse was concerned for intoxication, they referred the patient to a medical provider for evaluation.

Rapid Methadone Protocol

The medical team elected to restrict the rapid induction protocol to patients requesting methadone maintenance who reported using fentanyl as their primary opioid at least daily to ensure that only patients likely to have a high tolerance would receive it. They also excluded groups identified in guidelines to be at higher risk of overdose, including patients who were older than 65 years, as well as those with known heart failure, end-stage renal disease, cirrhosis, and advanced pulmonary disease.10-12 Concomitant substance use, including benzodiazepine and alcohol use, was not an exclusion criterion. All new patients received education about the elevated risk of overdose in the first 2 weeks of methadone treatment, and patients using fentanyl were advised that the rapid protocol could potentially increase that risk. Eligible patients were not mandated to receive the rapid induction protocol, and clinicians were expected to use a process of shared decision-making with patients, who could opt for a lower initial dose or less frequent dose increases. All patients were advised that they would be able to meet regularly with medical providers to adjust their dose as needed. Clinicians were expected to use clinical judgment, and they could choose to order a lower initial dose or slower pace of titration if they were concerned about the validity of a patient’s self-report of fentanyl use. If an eligible patient was assessed by a clinician to be sedated, they were not offered rapid induction.

Pilot Period

The clinic first informally piloted the intervention beginning in June 2021. During this period, the rapid induction was offered only to returning OTOP patients who met the criteria above and had tolerated doses of methadone over 100 mg in a prior OTOP treatment episode. Clinicians met with each patient briefly on the first 3 days to assess dose response and safety of another dose increase. Initial feedback from patients during this pilot period was encouraging, with no reports of oversedation or overdose. One patient was lost to follow-up but returned later reporting no adverse events and requesting another rapid induction. Given this, the team decided the rapid induction protocol could be expanded to others starting in January 2022.

Evaluation

To assess the feasibility of the rapid induction protocol, we reviewed medical records of all patients admitted to OTOP in 2022 (Fig. 1). We excluded patients from our analysis who presented to intake already on methadone, including transfers from other OTPs and hospital discharge referrals (who had already begun the induction process while hospitalized), as well as individuals requesting buprenorphine (resulting in a cohort of 202 patients starting methadone). We then excluded patients who were ineligible for rapid induction, for reasons including (1) age >65 years, (2) medical contraindication, and (3) no self-reported daily fentanyl use. For patients deemed eligible who did not have rapid induction ordered, we reviewed records to ascertain the reasons, which were aggregated to categories of (1) sedated at intake, (2) patient preference, (3) clinician concern for lower opioid tolerance, and (4) unknown. Our a priori primary outcome was completion (yes/no) of the protocol defined as reaching a methadone dose of at least 80 mg on day 7 given evidence of superiority of doses of at least 80 mg.10 For patients who had orders placed for the rapid induction protocol but did not reach a dose of 80 mg by day 7, we aggregated reasons to (1) missed doses (patient did not come to clinic on a dosing day) and (2) patient preference. To compare those who completed and did not complete the rapid induction protocol, we estimated unadjusted odds ratios of completion for age, housing status, baseline urine drug screen results, and 30-day retention; due to data sparsity, we present only Fishers exact test results for race/ethnicity and gender.

To assess for significant safety events, OTP and San Francisco County Department of Public Health electronic health records (EHRs) were reviewed by 2 OTOP physicians (ARB, SS) for documentation of patient-reported or provider-observed sedation during rapid induction, patient-reported nonfatal overdose, and medical examiner–reported fatalities. We elected not to use a standardized measure of sedation24 because reports of a patient presentation less severe than the lowest level on the scale would still have represented a safety concern and routinely been recorded as sedation in clinical notes in the OTOP EHR. As part of routine clinical monitoring, the county medical examiner’s office was contacted for all patients who are not seen in clinic for 14 or more days, and fatalities were recorded in the medical record. Although fatalities occurring out of county were not captured, all fatalities attributable to methadone within the county were likely to be captured with our methods. In preparing this manuscript, on October 1, 2023, OTOP and county EHR were reviewed for patients who had been lost to follow-up before completing 30 days of treatment to ascertain whether further contact had been made after their last dose at OTOP. This evaluation protocol was submitted and deemed exempt from review as a quality improvement project by the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Board (22-37811).

RESULTS

Of 202 patients presenting for induction to methadone treatment at OTOP in 2022, 126 were eligible for the rapid induction protocol (Fig. 1). The most common reason patients were ineligible for the protocol was not using fentanyl daily (82%, 59/76 daily heroin; 15%, 11/76 daily prescription opioids; 3%, 2/76 less than daily fentanyl). About a quarter of eligible patients (26%, 33/126) did not have the protocol ordered, with the most common reasons being provider concerns about patient opioid tolerance (39%, 13/33) or observed sedation at intake (18%, 6/33). Approximately 10% of eligible patients (13/126) opted for a slower pace of induction. Of the 93 patients who had the rapid protocol ordered, 70% (65/93) met the criteria for completion.

Among patients who initiated the rapid induction protocol, the demographics of those who completed were similar to those who did not, though odds of completion increased slightly with each year of age (Table 2). All patients who had the rapid protocol ordered had toxicology positive for fentanyl. Nearly 95% (17/18) of patients with toxicology negative for methamphetamine at intake completed the protocol, whereas only 64% (48/75) of those positive for methamphetamine completed (odds ratio [OR] 0.1, 95% CI 0.01–0.8). Patients who completed the protocol achieved an average dose of 40 mg higher than those who did not complete (89 vs 49 mg) on day 7. At 30 days, of those who had the rapid protocol ordered, 85% (79/93) were retained, with 88% (57/65) of patients who completed the protocol retained versus 79% (22/28) of patients who did not (OR 2.1, 95% CI 0.6–6.2). A large majority of the patients (86%, 25/28) who did not complete the protocol had multiple absences during the first week of treatment. Three patients opted to slow the pace of induction, with none reporting sedation.

Review of OTOP EHR revealed no provider observations or patient reports of sedation at the time of anticipated peak methadone concentration or nonfatal overdoses during the rapid induction period. Review of the county EHR showed no evidence of nonfatal overdoses or medical encounters in which a patient was sedated. Per contact with the County Medical Examiner, no patient fatalities occurred within San Francisco during or within 14 days after a rapid induction. Of patients who started the rapid protocol, 14 were lost to follow-up within the first month of treatment. Review of both EHRs for this evaluation identified encounters with either OTOP or an outside provider at some point after discharge for all but one patient, who was lost to follow-up after receiving an initial methadone dose of 30 mg.

DISCUSSION

This case series adds to emerging evidence of the feasibility of a more rapid methadone induction protocol for patients with OUD attributed to fentanyl use. Previous case reports and case series highlighted the feasibility of a rapid induction in the hospital, facilitated by the closer monitoring that is available in the inpatient setting.19-21 Ours is the first report of a method that allows most patients reporting daily fentanyl use to receive 80 mg as early as day 3 of outpatient treatment, with most achieving 80 mg by end of their first week. We found no evidence of oversedation, overdose or deaths among patients who underwent the rapid induction protocol, and a suggestion of improved early retention among those who completed. We found support for relying on patient self-report of daily fentanyl use to determine eligibility, with 100% of the initial urine drug tests returning positive for fentanyl among patients who started the rapid protocol. Rapid induction seems to have been acceptable to most new patients using fentanyl, with almost no eligible patients opting for a traditional induction at intake and only a few choosing to slow the pace of induction once they had started. This is an important finding given that previous work has identified an association between fentanyl use and dissatisfaction with medication treatment of OUD.25

The case series suggests some opportunities for research, refinement of the protocol, and dissemination to other OTPs. We restricted this protocol to patients using fentanyl, but it may also be suitable for other subpopulations. Future research could examine the effects of a first-day methadone dose greater than 40 mg, especially as newly published US federal guidelines allow for up to 50 mg to be given on day 1 if deemed clinically appropriate.17 Additional research could explore adoption of the strategies previously reported by providers in the inpatient setting, including the feasibility and acceptability of having patients observed when their serum methadone levels are nearing peak to consider the safety of additional doses each day. Given that absence from clinic was the most common reason that patients were unable to reach 80 mg quickly, there may be benefit to applying an evidence-based behavioral intervention like contingency management to the protocol to support early adherence to treatment.26 We were unable to ascertain the specific concerns about opioid tolerance that led providers not to order the protocol in 10% (13/126) of eligible patients. Because individual biases may affect these assessments, research to help clinicians to estimate opioid tolerance among individuals using fentanyl may lead to better care. All but one of the patients who failed to complete rapid induction had positive methamphetamine toxicology at intake, and special interventions may be needed for patients with OUD and co-occurring stimulant use.13,14 Finally, our analysis focused on the doses patients received in the first week of treatment, not on the doses or serum levels required for patients to refrain from fentanyl use. Because current practices are still largely based on data from people who use heroin, further research is needed to determine these methadone levels for people who use fentanyl and help create up-to-date guidance for OTP providers.

Interpretation of our findings is subject to some limitations. Although this study failed to identify any substantial safety events, it was not powered to detect rare events. A large majority of our patients presented with co-occurring stimulant use, which may limit generalizability. As a hospital-based OTP with the equivalent of 2.3 full-time nurse practitioners and 1.8 full-time physicians, this program may have more medical staff than most OTPs, allowing patients to have frequent visits with the medical team. The level of medical staffing needed for safe adoption of a more rapid induction protocol may merit further study.

CONCLUSION

Our protocol for rapid methadone induction for patients with OUD attributable to fentanyl use was feasible to implement. Improving OTP induction procedures in response to the fentanyl epidemic is one key piece of the effort to transform the nation’s OTP system and has the potential to lower barriers and increase access. Further research is needed to optimize methadone induction for people who use fentanyl and to clarify the relationship of induction dosing to critical outcomes including retention in treatment, reduction in or abstinence from fentanyl use, and patient perceptions and preferences.
