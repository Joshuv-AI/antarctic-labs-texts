Effect of Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer on immobilization of heavy metals and wheat growth: a field study in calcareous fluvo-aquic soil
Authors: Di Wu, Anqi Ren, Zijie Shang, Renqi Ding, Lei Gu, Zhifan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveHeavy metal contamination in calcareous fluvo-aquic soils poses a significant threat to agricultural safety and human health. While modified biochar shows promise for remediation, field studies on its mechanisms and ecological effects under such conditions are still lacking.This study aimed to synthesize a novel silicon-phosphorus biochar-based compound fertilizer (BCF) and evaluate its efficacy in immobilizing heavy metals, influencing soil microbial communities, and promoting wheat growth in a calcareous fluvo-aquic farmland soils contaminated with heavy metals.MethodsA Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer (BCF) was synthesized from maize straw biomass, diatomite, triple superphosphate and urea at different pyrolysis temperatures (300℃, 450℃, 600℃) and mixture ratios (5:1:1:x (B5PNx) and 10:1:1:x (B10PNx)). A field experiment with 11 treatments was conducted on contaminated calcareous fluvo-aquic soil to assess the effects of BCF types and application rates (450, 900, and 1800 kg ha⁻1) on soil properties, the availability of Cd, Zn, and Pb, heavy metal accumulation in wheat grains, wheat yield, and soil microbial community structure. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to analyze the pathways of Cd immobilization.ResultsThe application of BCF significantly increased soil cation exchange capacity (CEC) and soil organic matter (SOM). It also markedly reduced the bioavailability of Cd, Zn, and Pb in soil, primarily due to the increased surface area, pore volume, and functional groups (e.g., N–H, PO₄3⁻, Si–O-Si) of BCF. BCF application, particularly the B5PNx, significantly decreased the concentrations of Cd, Zn, and Pb in wheat grains and enhanced wheat growth. This was linked to the enrichment of beneficial soil microorganisms such as Proteobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, and Actinobacteria. SEM analysis supported that B5PN effectively immobilized soil available Cd, thereby reducing grain Cd accumulation and increasing yield.ConclusionThe Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer, especially the B5PNx formulation, is an effective, low-cost, and environmentally friendly amendment for remediating heavy metal-contaminated calcareous fluvo-aquic soil. It works by immobilizing heavy metals, improving soil health, and enriching a beneficial microbial community, offering a promising strategy for mitigating heavy metal bioavailability and reducing metal transfer in calcareous soil-wheat system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09423-8.

Introduction

With the rapid development of global industrialization and urbanization, soil heavy metal pollution has become an increasingly widespread concerned environmental issue and challenge due to its non-degradability and easy migration [1], as well as adverse effects on crop growth and human health [2]. The National Soil Pollution Status Survey Report released by China in 2014 indicated that 19.4% of heavy metals (HMs) and other pollutants in China's arable soil exceeded regulatory limits. In China, it is estimated that approximately 10 million hectares of contaminated farmland and 3.33 million hectares of farmland are unsuitable for cultivation due to pollution, most of which are contaminated by HMs [3]. As the second largest crop in China, wheat is increasingly polluted by heavy metals year by year [4, 5]. An increasing number of studies have found that wheat is prone to accumulate HMs [6], which poses a high health risk to humans [7, 8]. When metal contents in wheat grains exceed the threshold (0.2 mg/kg), it may cause serious damage to human organs [9]. The northern winter wheat region contributes 61.80% of the national wheat [10], and most soil in this region belongs to slightly alkaline soil [11]. Therefore, developing and implementing effective remediation strategies on slightly alkaline farmland soil contaminated with heavy metals is imperative to maintain agricultural productivity and safeguard human health.

To address these challenges, various modified biochar, as an environmentally friendly passivation material, has received increasing attention in improving soil quality and managing heavy metal migration [12], and can overcome the shortcomings of single biochar such as fewer adsorption sites and weak affinity [13]. To enhance remediation efficiency, several modification strategies are actively being explored. The loading of clay minerals onto biochar is considered an efficient method due to its ability to enhance heavy metals adsorption by adjusting the specific surface area and functional groups of biochar, coupled with counteracting the high cost of production [14]. As a miraculous porous material containing clay minerals, diatomite is becoming a promising candidate for creating a composite with biochar due to its strong ion-exchange capacity and adsorption on heavy metals resulted from high porosity and a large specific surface area [15]. In addition, when used with exogenous P or N fertilizer, biochar can act as a slow-release carrier, reducing N loss and boosting crop productivity [16, 17], as well as immobilizing Cd by forming metal-phosphate precipitates [18]. However, most of these studies were conducted as pot experiments in acidic or neutral soil conditions [14, 19, 20], while lack of field experimental research on slightly alkaline farmland soils in northern China.

This study hypothesized that modified biochar material may exhibit enhanced capacity for immobilizing HMs and stimulating wheat growth as an alternative fertilizer. The objective of this research was to synthesize a Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer (BCF) utilizing maize straw biomass, diatomite, triple superphosphate and urea, and exploring its efficacy in immobilizing heavy metals and promoting wheat growth in calcareous fluvo-aquic soil through analyzing soil available heavy metal contents, heavy metal accumulation in wheat grains, wheat yield and microbial community. Structural equation model (SEM) analysis was applied to further reveal the pathways and effects of BCF on wheat growth and heavy metals availability. This study will provide an innovative and low-cost remediation strategy that are oriented toward slightly alkaline field soils contaminated with heavy metals.

Materials and methods

Study area

The experimental site is located in the farmland near Bian Village, Kaifeng City, Henan Province (114°24′E, 34°47′N), which belongs to the warm temperate continental monsoon climate, with an average annual temperature (14.52℃) and precipitation (627.5 mm). The tillage pattern in this area is dominated by maize-wheat rotation, and the studies soil is Calcareous fluvo-aquic soil. The Huafei River, which flows from north to south through the study area, receives a large amount of wastewater from industrial sources (smelters, carbon plants, pharmaceutical companies, thermal power plants, fur processing plants, etc.). Since 1962, the Huafei River has been used for agricultural irrigation in this area [21]. Some enterprises, such as fertilizer plants and zinc smelting plants, release some pollutants including heavy metals into the soil environment in the form of dry and wet sedimentation. In addition, coal combustion is the main source of pollution in Kaifeng City. The dust deposition produced by the combustion of raw coal may also be an important pollution source that cannot be ignored [22]. Therefore, the study area presents a coexistence of sewage irrigation and industrial and agricultural pollution, which leads to the accumulation of heavy metals in the soil-crop system. Especially, the contents of Cd and Zn in the soil in this study area are much higher than the risk control standard of soil pollution in agricultural land (GB 15618–2018).

Synthesis of single biochar/biochar-based compound fertilizer

Synthesis of single biochar

Corn stalk (collected from the eastern suburb of Kaifeng City, Henan Province, the contents of Cd, Zn and Pb in the corn stalk being 0.33 ± 0.05 mg kg−1, 160.91 ± 21.11 mg kg−1 and 0.24 ± 0.08 mg kg−1, respectively) was used as the raw material of biochar, which was washed with deionized water and oven-dried at 70 ℃ for 24 h to remove moisture [23]. After being crushing crushed and sieved, the corn stalk was transformed into a powdered material with particle size of less than 2 mm. The powder was tightly packed into a stainless-steel canister. The pyrolysis process was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere at three target temperatures (300℃, 450℃, and 600℃) for 1 h. Based on the pyrolysis temperature, the single biochar materials (Bx series) were named as B300, B450, and B600, respectively. (see supplementary materials for details).

Synthesis of Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer

For the synthesis of Si-P biochar-based compound fertilizer, corn stalk, diatomaceous earth, and calcium superphosphate were mixed in a mass ratio of 5:1:1or 10:1:1. The selected mass ratios (5:1:1 and 10:1:1) were based on our previous optimization studies and literature reports, aiming to achieve a balance among pore structure development, nutrient loading efficiency, heavy metal immobilization performance, and material stability [24, 25]. The canister was then placed in a tube furnace operating at three target temperatures (300℃, 450℃and 600℃) for 1 h under nitrogen gas, and then the biochar was cooled under room temperature (25℃). The loaded BCF was mixed with urea (total N ≥ 46.5%) at a mass ratio of 4:1(solid urea to biochar), and then the mixture was moistened with distilled water and agitated until the mixture was completely mixed and stabilized. After baking at 50℃ for 48 h, the dried products were then ground through a 100-mesh screen and stored in self-sealing bags for later use. Biochar materials prepared by mixing corn stalk, triple superphosphate and diatomite at a mass ratio of either 5:1:1 or 10:1:1 were named as B5PNx and B10PNx series, respectively [24, 25]. Then, according to the different pyrolysis temperature (300℃, 450℃ and 600℃), the B5PNx and B10PNx series were named as B5PN300, B5PN450, B5PN600 and B10PN300, B10PN450, B10PN600, respectively.

Field experiment and sample collection

The field experiment was conducted in the suburban farmland of Kaifeng city (114°24′E, 34°47′N) using a randomized factorial design while wheat (Jimai 22, a common local variety) was grown as a test crop. The basic physical and chemical properties of the studied soil in the experimental field were as follows: The pH was 7.76 ± 0.14, organic matter content was 2.77 ± 0.086%, cation exchange capacity was (CEC) 8.83 ± 0.21 cmol kg−1, Cd, Zn and Pb contents were 7.87 ± 0.44, 1458.58 ± 36.67 and 108.29 ± 2.42 mg kg−1, respectively [25]. Field experiment comprised of eleven fertilizer treatments including control (CK), commercial N-P-K chemical compound fertilizer (FK), B300, B450, B600, B10PN300, B10PN450, B10PN600, B5PN300, B5PN450 and B5PN600, each in triplicates. Three levels of fertilizer dosages, 450 kg ha−1, 900 kg ha−1 and 1800 kg ha−1 (representing 0.5, 1 and 2 times of normal fertilizer dose, their mass ratios corresponded to only about 0.017%, 0.034% and 0.068%, respectively), were tested. The highest application rate (1800 kg ha⁻1) remained below 0.1% (w/w), substantially lower than the 1–5% (w/w) [26–28] rates commonly reported in biochar remediation studies. In total, 99 microplots with an area of 0.64 m2 (0.8 m*0.8 m) were designed by random block (Fig. 1). Each plot was separated with surrounding protection rows of 0.2 m in width and contained separated irrigation inlets and drainage outlets. When applying biochar materials and fertilizers, they should be evenly spread on the soil surface, then manually turned over to mix them, and applied to a depth of 0–20 cm. The field management measures (such as water and pest control) were consistent with local practices. To eliminate the influence caused by the use of fertilizers, no base fertilizer was applied to the farmland soil before planting wheat, and no additional fertilization was carried out during the wheat growth period.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the experimental treatment distribution

Schematic diagram of the experimental treatment distribution

The roots, stems, and grains were separated and thoroughly washed with deionized water after harvesting all wheat from each plot. All samples were dried at 80℃ and stored at 65℃ for later analysis. Furthermore, the soil was sampled from each plot by collecting 5 randomly selected subsites (0–20 cm deep).

Experimental analysis

Analysis of biochar materials properties and structure

The specific surface area (SBET), pore volume (PV), and pore size (PD) of Bx and BCF were measured using N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K on a surface area and porosity analyzer (Quantachrome Quadrasorb SI-4, USA). The surface functional groups were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Bruker VERTEX 70, Germany), and the scanning range was 400 cm−1 to 4000 cm−1. The structure and morphology of the materials were observed by field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, JSM-7001 F, Japan). Elemental distribution of biochar materials was analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with FE-SEM. Their element contents were measured using an organic element analyzer (Elemantar Vario El III Unicube, Germany) and an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) (Thermo Fisher ICAP 6200, USA). The SBET was calculated with the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. The PV and PD were calculated with the Barret-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. The contents of the acidic functional group in biochar materials were measured with Boehm titration [29]. The pH value was measured with a pH meter in deionized water extracts (1:20, w/v) (The analysis results can be seen in supplementary information).

Analysis of soil physiochemical properties

Soil pH value was measured with a pH meter (Mettler Toledo FE28-Standard, Switzerland). CEC was measured by leaching the soil with ammonium acetate at pH 7 (following LYT 1243–1999 determination of forest soil CEC). The content of soil organic matter (SOM) was measured by the Qiulin method (National standards of the people’s Republic of China, GB 9834–88). For the total concentration of heavy metals and the available heavy metals, soil samples were digested with HNO3-HF-HClO4 and extracted by DTPA (Diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) respectively, and then measured by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Thermo Fisher X SERIES 2, USA).

Analysis of wheat plants

Wheat samples were digested with HNO3-HClO4, and the element content was determined by ICP-MS. Three blank samples and three standard samples (GBW07401 for soil and GBW10011 for wheat) were set for each group of digestion samples. The recovery rate of heavy metals ranged from 90 to 110%. Microbial community composition analysis in soil

The soil microbial community structure was analyzed by 16S high-throughput sequencing. Next, the microbial diversity index (ACE, Shannon and Simpson) was calculated to reflect the species richness and diversity of environmental communities.

Statistical analysis

The Pearson correlation analysis (for indicators such as microorganisms and environmental factors) and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, the statistical analysis of the results of different biochar material treatments was performed at a significance level of 0.05) were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Statistical Software Package). and the statistical analysis of the average results of different biochar material treatments was performed at a significance level of 0.05. All graphs were produced using Origin Pro 2021(Origin Lab. USA). Structure equation model (SEM) was performed to analyze the hypothetical pathways of BCF addition effects on wheat growth and heavy metals in wheat.

Immobilization effect of biochar materials on available heavy metals in soil

The chelating agent DTPA is commonly used to assess the bioavailable heavy metals in soils, as it can effectively simulate the extraction of heavy metals from soil by plant root exudates and its subsequent uptake by roots [31]. Figure 3 displayed the ratios of the available Cd, Zn and Pb to total amount in soil under various treatments. Totally, the B5PNx series exhibited the most significant passivation effect among all treatments. The proportions of available Cd, Zn and Pb in soil treated with B5PNx were lower than those in the CK and FK groups (13.17% and 13.25%, 7.81% and 8.16%, and 9.88% and 10.30%, respectively). Besides, the B5PNx prepared at different pyrolysis temperatures significantly affected the proportion of available heavy metals in soil (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Especially at 1800 kg ha−1, the proportion of available Cd, Zn, and Pb in soil treated with B5PN600 biochar prepared at 600℃ was the lowest among all groups, which was obviously lower than that of the CK and FK (16.16% and 16.24%, 9.13% and 9.47%, 11.24% and 11.66%, respectively). Additionally, the passivation effect of biochar on soil heavy metals increased significantly with the increment of application rates, specifically in the sequence of 1800 kg ha⁻1 > 900 kg ha⁻1 > 450 kg ha⁻1. By contrast, at an application rate of 1800 kg ha⁻1, the proportions of available Cd, Zn, and Pb in soil after Bx treatment were only reduced by 3.77%, 2.31%, and 2.02% compared to the CK group, respectively. The proportions of available Cd, Zn, and Pb in soil treated with B10PNx decreased by 5.62%, 2.84%, and 4.64% compared to the CK group, respectively, which was superior to Bx but inferior to B5PNx series.Fig. 3The ratios of available Cd (A), Zn (B) and Pb (C) to total amount in soil under different treatments. Wheat growth and heavy metal bioaccumulation under BCF application

Wheat growth conditions

The total grain weight, 1000-grain weight and biomass of wheat under different fertilization treatments are shown in Fig. 8. The addition of single biochar and BCF could promote the growth of wheat, and the effect was increased significantly with the rising application. At the application rates of 1800 kg ha−1 (less than 0.1%), the B10PNx application presented significant promoting effects on grain weight, 1000-grain weight and biomass of wheat, which were increased by 22.14%, 5.61% and 13.11% compared with CK group, respectively. The promoting effect of Bx and B10PNx on wheat biomass was significantly higher than that of FK group by 6.28% and 6.33%. Notably, compared with CK and FK groups, B5PNx significantly improved the biomass and yield of wheat, and the total grain weight of wheat was increased by 29.33% and 8.83%, the 1000-grain weight of wheat was increased by 6.42% and 2.78%, and wheat biomass increased by 21.31% and 14.04%, respectively. Besides, the promoting effect of B5PNx on the biomass and yield of wheat increased significantly with the rise of pyrolysis temperature. Heavy metal accumulation in wheat plant

In general, the inhibitory effect of different treatment groups on Cd, Zn and Pb contents in wheat roots and grains was in the sequence of B5PNx > B10PNx > Bx, the inhibitory effect of B5PNx was the most significant (Fig. 9). Discussion

The mechanisms of immobilizing heavy metals in soil by BCF

Totally, the passivation effect of BCF on heavy metals is superior to Bx. This is usually explained by the larger specific surface area and pore volume, which provide more adsorption sites. However, our results indicated that B5PNx exhibited better immobilization despite a lower surface area than B10PNx (Table S1). This suggests that complexation between oxygen‑containing functional groups and heavy metals plays a more critical role [34]. The modified BCF contained more acidic functional groups (especially lactone groups in B5PN600), providing abundant sites for Cd, Pb, and Zn complexation. New functional groups (N–H, Si–O–Si, P–O) introduced by modification also formed stable complexes with heavy metals. FTIR analysis supported stronger peak intensities in B5PNx, supporting its superior passivation. Additionally, coprecipitation contributed greatly: SiO₃2⁻ and PO₄3⁻ from diatomite and phosphate fertilizer reacted with Cd2⁺, Zn2⁺, and Pb2⁺ to form insoluble silicates and phosphates [35–37]. Although BCF did not significantly increase soil pH, it maintained stable alkaline conditions in the calcareous soil. Such conditions may favor the persistence of insoluble phosphate and silicate precipitates and support the long-term stability of heavy metal immobilization [35, 37]. In addition, pyrolysis temperature also plays a non-negligible role in immobilizing available heavy metals in soils by altering biochar properties, such as pH value, functional groups, and pore structure [38, 39]. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, BCF showed a stronger passivation effect on soil heavy metals. This may be related to the larger specific surface area and rougher surface structure formed at higher temperatures. These properties can provide more active adsorption sites for heavy metal ions [40]. Some studies manifested that an increase in pyrolysis temperature leads to the formation of more dense aromatic carbon structures and available polar functional groups in biochar, thereby enhancing its adsorption and detoxification effect on heavy metals [41].

BCF promotes wheat growth by enhancing nutrient supply and inhibiting heavy metal transport

Our field experiments showed that the promoting effect of BCF on wheat growth was significantly higher than that of single biochar and common chemical fertilizer, and the effect was obviously enhanced with the increment of application rate. The primary reason could be that the modification of triple superphosphate promotes the formation of exchangeable monatomic complexes between P ions and bicentate complexes on the surface of biochar [42]. This monatomic complex enables P to be effectively fixed on the surface of biochar, which increases its stability and also improves the bioavailability of P in soil, thus solving a series of problems such as low P content in single biochar and low utilization efficiency of P in conventional chemical fertilizers [43]. The surface of biochar is rich in P element, which is closely related to metabolic components such as nucleic acid, sugar phosphate, coenzyme, nucleotide and phospholipid [44]. Meanwhile, phosphorus participates in enzymatic reactions and physiological processes of plants [45], and plays a key role in crop growth and development by promoting nitrogen uptake by crops in agroecosystems. Another noteworthy factor in promoting wheat growth is that more Si can be effectively loaded onto the biochar surface due to the loading modification of diatomite. Available silicon in soil is an important nutrient for the growth of rice, maize, wheat or barley [46, 47]. Badu et al. [48] and Ma et al. [49] found that silicon in plants can precipitate (siliconization) in the cell wall to form specific cells (siliceous cells, etc.), thereby improving plant disease and stress resistance and promoting nutrient uptake.

The BCF not only promotes crop growth as a nutrient, but also inhibits wheat uptake of heavy metals by immobilizing them in soil. Just as the SEM results indicate, B5PNx exerts an indirect negative effect on Cd content in wheat grain through decreasing available Cd content in soil (Soil CEC also has a directly negative effect on it), and it is far greater than its own directly positive effect. One possible explanation is that silicon application may reduce Cd absorption and translocation in plants, potentially through mechanisms involving transporter gene regulation reported in previous studies [50]. Previous studies have suggested that silicon can immobilize heavy metals through the formation of insoluble metal-silicate complexes and by increasing soil pH [51]. The Si–O-Si and Si–H groups detected in our BCF are known to participate in these reactions. Silicon has been shown to reduce the translocation of heavy metals from roots to shoots by depositing in the endodermis and cell walls, physically blocking apoplastic transport pathways, and down-regulating the expression of metal transporter genes [52]. This interpretation is consistent with our observation that BCF treatment significantly reduced heavy metal content in grains, and this reduction was lower than that in the roots. Also, silicon can promote plant growth under heavy metal stress by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities, maintaining photosynthetic efficiency, and improving nutrient balance [53]. The improved wheat biomass and yield under BCF treatment may be related to multiple physicochemical and biological processes, including potential silicon-mediated physiological effects. In addition, the porous structure of biochar may enhance the dispersion and availability of silicon, while silicon may modify biochar surface properties to increase its adsorption capacity [46, 54]. This synergy explains why BCF is more effective than the other treatments.

Although Cd content in grain has a direct positive effect on grain weight (i.e., a toxic effect), the final grain weight still increased. The reason is that the indirect positive effect of B5PNx (by decreasing available Cd content in soil) is greater than that of Cd content in grain. It implied that the immobilization of available Cd in the soil by B5PNx indirectly reduce the Cd accumulation in grains and ultimately promote the increase in grain weight. Anyway, the reduction of heavy metal content in edible parts of plants is a key factor for food safety production [28]. Totally, our findings indicate that BCF effectively reduced Cd, Zn, and Pb accumulation in wheat edible tissues at low application rates (< 0.1%), while potentially reducing remediation costs due to the relatively low application rate and multifunctional properties of BCF.

Soil microorganisms are essential for regulating biogeochemical cycles, sustaining ecosystem function, and promoting plant growth. Their responses to agricultural inputs therefore serve as a key indicator of soil health and sustainability. The higher ACE index under B600 indicates that increased carbon input promotes soil bacterial richness [55]. Both B5PN600 and B600 enhanced microbial diversity, as reflected by increased Shannon indices, likely due to the introduction of novel carbon sources and ash-derived nutrients [56]. However, the decline in ACE and Shannon indices at relatively high BCF application rates may be partly associated with the increase in the Simpson index, indicating enhanced dominance of certain microbial groups and reduced community evenness. This suggests that BCF may selectively favor adapted taxa while reducing less competitive microbial populations. Previous studies have shown that biochar effects on microbial diversity are highly dependent on soil properties, nutrient status, and C/N ratio [57]. In addition, biochar-induced changes in nutrient availability and CEC, as well as pyrolysis-derived compounds, may selectively influence sensitive microbial taxa [58, 59]. However, these mechanisms remain inferential because microbial functional genes, and nutrient dynamics were not directly measured in this study.

BCF application differentially affected the dominant bacterial phyla, reflecting their different ecological strategies. The increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria under B5PN600 is consistent with its copiotrophic lifestyle and its function in carbon and nitrogen cycling [60]. The carbon-rich BCF probably promoted organic matter decomposition, and its phosphorus-solubilizing ability further improved nutrient availability [61]. Similarly, the increased relative abundance of Gemmatimonadetes under B5PN600 can be explained by its preference for well‑aerated, neutral to slightly alkaline soil environments [62–64]. Biochar improved these conditions by enhancing soil porosity. Its positive correlation with available phosphorus also indicates a role in phosphorus cycling [65]. The higher abundance of Actinobacteria under B600 and B5PN600 was associated with increased soil organic matter [66, 67], which supports their decomposing activity [68, 69]. Their phosphorus-solubilizing capacity may also help improve plant nutrient status. In contrast, Acidobacteria abundance decreased under B5PN600, in line with its preference for acidic and nutrient-poor environments [70, 71]. The alkaline and nutrient-rich conditions induced by BCF likely inhibited its growth, while promoting Actinobacteria through higher nitrate availability [72, 73]. The obvious reduction in Bacteroidetes abundance suggests high sensitivity to BCF-induced changes, possibly due to inhibitory compounds such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at high application rates [74]. This highlights the importance of optimizing BCF application rates to balance agronomic benefits and microbial community stability.

By combining microbial community data (Sect. " Relative abundance of soil microbial communities") with correlation analysis (Fig. 7), Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Gemmatimonadetes were identified as potentially important microbial taxa associated with BCF application. Proteobacteria are widely reported to participate in nutrient cycling, organic matter transformation, and environmental adaptation under heavy metal stress [75, 76]. The SEM suggested that BCF directly influenced the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, while also indirectly regulating Proteobacteria through decreased soil available Cd and increased soil CEC. Additionally, the enrichment of Proteobacteria may be associated with reduced Cd transfer from wheat roots to grains, which could further contribute to improved wheat growth. Therefore, the enrichment of Proteobacteria may be associated with improved rhizosphere conditions and may indirectly participate in BCF-mediated remediation processes. Overall, these results provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential involvement of microbial community shifts in BCF-mediated remediation processes.

For agricultural applications, remediation materials need to have long-term stability and economic benefits. The existing literature indicates that biochar possesses long-term stability, which is attributed to its inherent physical and chemical properties as well as the transformation of its active components. On the one hand, the aromatic carbon skeleton of biochar exhibits high recalcitrance to microbial mineralization (mean residence times: hundreds to thousands of years in soil; [77], ensuring the persistence of its soil ameliorative functions over years. On the other hand, the silicon and phosphorus components in BCF exhibit slow‑release characteristics. Phosphorus is partially transformed into less soluble mineral forms (e.g., Ca‑P and Mg‑P complexes) during pyrolysis or soil application. This reduces phosphorus leaching and fixation, and prolongs the agronomic effectiveness compared with soluble chemical fertilizers [78]. Silicon is gradually released, regulated by soil pH and moisture, providing long‑term stress relief for plants [79]. Furthermore, silicon modification may further improve this stability by forming protective coatings or complexing with phosphorus. Additionally, the BCF reduces the cost and bring more benefits compared with traditional remediation approaches. First of all, its dual functions of soil remediation and nutrient supply result in superior long-term economic and environmental benefits. Secondly, employing agricultural waste as feedstock helps reduce production costs and is consistent with the principles of the circular economy. Furthermore, BCF can decrease overall treatment costs by reducing the demand for additional fertilizers and amendments.

Conclusion

In our study, a Si-P BCF was synthesized and applied at low doses (< 0.1%) in field, and its immobilization effects on soil heavy metals, wheat growth and soil bacteria community were investigated. The BCF addition can greatly improve soil quality and promote wheat growth, and their improvement effect increases with the increment of application rates and pyrolysis temperature. Especially, the application of BCF reduced the available Cd, Zn and Pb contents in farmland soil, inhibited heavy metals transport from roots to grains, and reduced the risk of heavy metal accumulation in wheat grains. Besides, adding BCF was associated with the enrichment of potentially beneficial microorganisms such as Proteobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes and Actinobacteria, thereby potentially improving nutrient utilization and rhizosphere microenvironment conditions, which promotes wheat growth. SEM results further evidenced that applying B5PNx may effectively reduce Cd accumulation in wheat grains by regulating the available Cd content in the soil, thereby potentially contributing to improvement of wheat growth. Totally, our study provided an effective, low-cost and environmental-friendly method for the remediation on heavy metal pollution in farmland soil. Although our results are promising, long-term field trials over multiple years are still needed. They will help us determine whether repeated BCF applications can reduce soil Cd content below regulatory limits and maintain long-term stability.
