Genomic sequence evolution underlying human neocortical interareal diversification
Authors: Wei He, Weizhen Hou, Chengyong Jiang, Xiuxiu Zhang, Yaoyi Wang, Yuqiu Zhou, Kevin T. Gobeske, Shaojie Ma, Jiacheng Fan, Haiyang Wang, Peilong Li, Wenbin Xie, Biao Yan, Nenad Sestan, Liang Chen, Jiayi Zhang, Ying Zhu

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeocortical expansion and diversification in primates, especially humans, underpin advanced cognitive abilities, yet the molecular and cellular bases of neocortical area specification remain incompletely understood.ResultsHere, we perform an integrative multimodal analysis combining single-nucleus multiomic and spatial transcriptomic sequencing, morphological and electrophysiological profiling, and secondary comparisons across humans, macaques, and mice.We uncovered enhanced area-specific cellular diversification in primates, including distinct upper-layer projection neuron and interneuron subtypes, increased electrophysiological and morphological complexity, and enriched cell connectivity and crosstalk. Notably, this increased interareal heterogeneity coincides with the expansion of evolutionarily young DNA sequences in primates, especially Hominoidea, which are enriched for transposable elements (TEs). We deduce that TEs are likely to contribute to areal specification by acting as cis-regulatory elements and by diversifying the transcriptome. Specifically, TEs harbor evolutionarily novel area-specific transcription factor binding sites for species- and/or area-specific transcription factors in human genome, correlating with the expression of diverse gene biotypes across neocortical areas. Furthermore, our findings reveal that the expression of TE transcripts distinguishes the upper-layer neurons in the frontal cortex, likely enhancing cellular diversification through mechanisms beyond protein-coding gene expression.ConclusionsThese findings illuminate genomic and cellular mechanisms contributing to human neocortical reorganization during evolution, providing insights into the molecular underpinnings of primate brain specialization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04177-w.

Background

The human neocortex has undergone remarkable expansion in size and reorganization of cellular and circuit arrangements during evolution that is exceptional even compared with other primates or other human brain regions [1–5]. Evolutionarily earlier and simpler cortical maps become reorganized in the human neocortex to yield dramatically new areal and functional specializations [6, 7]. This expansion and diversification serve a foundation of the astounding cognitive skills and sophisticated behaviors of humans. However, the molecular and cellular underpinnings orchestrating the neocortical area specification during evolution remain inadequately explored.

Spatial and single-cell genomic technologies, coupled with histologic and electrophysiologic methods, provide powerful tools to understand cellular taxonomy, spatial organization, and functional roles in neocortical areas across species. Despite the acknowledged elaboration of an enlarged prefrontal cortex [8], intricate synaptic connections [9], and the expansion of cortical layers with their inherent cellular complexity in primates compared to other mammals [8, 10], the gene expression patterns across the human neocortical areas have been observed to display surprising uniformity, with exceptions emerging in the form of distinct gene expression patterns observed in the primary visual cortex [11, 12]. This observation raises a broader question: how do we reconcile the relatively modest cross-area transcriptional diversity with the profound differences in the cytoarchitecture and functions exhibited by distinct cortical areas? Prior work in the developing mouse cortex has shown that sharp spatial gradients of enhancer activity can generate area-specific regulatory states even when overall gene expression is relatively similar [13–15]. These findings provide an important framework for understanding how regulatory variation, rather than large shifts in transcript abundance, can shape areal identity.

Building upon this framework, an important challenge is to identify additional sources of regulatory sequence variation that may contribute to areal diversification in primates. Part of this challenge arises from the complexity of explaining DNA sequence alterations, particularly in noncoding regions of the human genome [16–18]. Within this genomic complexity, transposable elements (TEs) have emerged as prominent players [19, 20], comprising primarily retroelements such as endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), including primate-specific Alu repeats [21], and SINE-VNTR-Alu (SVAs), composites of ERVs and Alu restricted to hominids [22]. TEs have been increasingly recognized as major drivers of genome evolution owing to their recombinogenic and regulatory potential [23], even though most are unable to spread further due to inactivating mutations. Despite their prevalence, the regulatory role of TEs in cellular diversification and neocortical area specification remains enigmatic.

Here, we employed a multimodal approach to compare cell types across neocortical areas and species, and uncovered increased neocortical area heterogeneity in primates compared to mice, manifesting as increased molecular, morphological, and electrophysiological cellular diversity. Further comparative sequence analysis revealed a coincidence of DNA insertions and the evolutionary trajectory of neocortical areas. We identified distinct TE families emerging as contributors to the specification of different areas through diverse mechanisms, with their emergence coinciding with key evolutionary milestones for each area.

Results

Cellular diversity, molecular specialization, and functional variation across human neocortical areas

To characterize cellular diversity and spatial organization of the adult human neocortex, we performed single-nucleus multiome (snATAC-seq and snRNA-seq from the same cell) and spatial transcriptome profiling on postmortem tissue obtained from two healthy adult donors, sampling four prototypical cortical areas: the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC, a homotypical association area), the primary visual cortex (V1C, a hypergranular sensory area), the primary motor cortex (M1C, an agranular primary area), and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC, an agranular prosiocortex) (Tables S1-S3; Fig. 1A). Cortical layer boundaries were defined in the spatial transcriptomic sections and validated by corresponding H&E staining, providing a laminar framework for downstream analyses (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Stringent quality control metrics confirmed high-quality snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data across donors and cortical areas (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). By integrating our spatial transcriptomic information and single-nucleus multiome clusters with published human neocortex single-cell RNA-seq data that include cortical layer annotations (Figures S3-S4) [24], we established a comprehensive three-level hierarchical transcriptomic taxonomy of cell types, including 3 Classes, 22 Subclasses, and 93 Subtypes (Fig. 1B), identifying 40 glutamatergic and 35 GABAergic neuron subtypes, and 18 non-neuronal cell subtypes with high concordance between snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq clustering (Fig. 1B). Subtypes were further grouped into nine excitatory neuron subclasses based on laminar and projection properties, five inhibitory neuron subclasses based on canonical marker genes, and four glial subclasses (oligodendrocyte precursors, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, microglia), and four other subclasses (endothelial, PVM, T-cell and VLMC) (Fig. 1D, E).Fig. 1Taxonomy and spatial distribution of cells in the adult human neocortex. While all subclasses appeared across cortical areas, UMAP projections colored by area and subclass (Fig. 1C, D) showed clear area-associated shifts across multiple subclasses, suggesting substantial interareal transcriptomic divergence. This divergence was most pronounced among intratelencephalic (IT) neurons, followed by inhibitory neurons, and non-neuronal cells. Consistent with these initial observations, hierarchical clustering of subtype transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 1B) confirmed this divergence and further showed that it was largely driven by area-specific cell subtypes, defined as those with > 90% of cells originating from their respective area (Additional file 2: Table S4). To further investigate the molecular basis of this divergence, we performed subclass-level differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analyses, identified and calculated area-specific upregulated/downregulated genes (AUGs/ADGs) in each subclass, using both our data and published multi-area human cortical data. These independent analyses yielded highly consistent results: glutamatergic subclasses showed the strongest interareal transcriptional divergence, with IT and L6 CT neurons exhibiting the highest numbers of DEGs, whereas inhibitory DEGs were concentrated in PVALB and SST subclasses and glial DEGs were largely restricted to oligodendrocytes and astrocytes (Additional file 1: Fig. S5 and Additional file 1: Fig. S6; Additional file 2: Table S6).

Amongst all areas, V1C showed the most distinct transcriptomic signatures, including five area-specific L2–3 IT subtypes accounting for 98.4% of all V1C L2–3 IT neurons and six L4 IT subtypes representing 99.9% of V1C L4 IT neurons, and a single V1C-specific L6 corticothalamic (CT) subtype comprising all L6 CT neurons in this region (Fig. 1B), implying that the V1C identity among these excitatory subtypes is primarily driven by a set of highly dominant area-specific subtypes. V1C also displayed V1C-specific inhibitory and glial subtypes, including PVALB 6, SST 4, and Astro 3. Spatial mapping further showed that these inhibitory and glial subtypes were strongly enriched in V1C layers 2–4 (Fig. 2A), aligning with the laminar distribution of V1C-specific excitatory populations, suggesting coordinated area-specific organization across both excitatory and non-excitatory cell classes. In contrast, ACC and M1C each harbored fewer area-specific populations, including two ACC-enriched L2–3 IT subtypes (L2–3 IT 7 and L2–3 IT 8) and one M1C-enriched L2–3 IT subtype (L2–3 IT 10) (Fig. 1B and Additional file 1: Fig. S7). To further characterize these area-specific subtypes, we examined subtype-level DEGs and identified marker genes for all subtypes (Additional file 1: Fig. S8 and Additional file 2: Table S5). We next prioritized two V1C-specific interneuron subtypes—PVALB 6, marked by HOMER3, and SST 4, marked by DPP4, for independent experimental validation of their subtype identities and area-predicted laminar distributions. Immunohistochemistry of human specimens further confirmed the enrichment of PVALB 6 GABAergic neurons in upper cortical layers, especially layer 4 of V1C (Fig. 2B, C). Complementarily, chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) analysis validated the V1C specificity of SST 4, revealing its predominant localization across layers 2–3 of V1C (Fig. 2D). Given the extensive co-localization of V1C-specific astrocytic, glutamatergic and GABAergic subtypes, we next asked whether these molecular and laminar differences translate into distinct patterns of local cellular interactions [26, 27]. To address this, we applied CellChat to infer area-dependent ligand–receptor signaling landscapes across each areas. In this framework, preferential ligand and receptor expression across interacting cell types was used to predict putative differences in intercellular communication. This revealed that areal-specific subtypes contributed to local signaling pathways by selective expression of receptors and ligands. For example, CXCL12 was highly expressed in the PVALB 6 subtype in V1C, coinciding with the enrichment of its receptor, DPP4, in multiple V1C-specific subtypes (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A), predicting enhanced CXCL12–DPP4 signaling that may support specialized synapse formation, as previously reported [28]. Likewise, the PVALB 6 subtype was inferred to contribute to EFNA5-EPHA3 mediated Ephrin signaling (Additional file 1: Fig. S10B), a pathway implicated in axon guidance, synaptic plasticity and synaptic action [29]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that interareal transcriptomic differences—especially within areal-specific cell subtypes-provide additional sources of ligands and receptors that may shape area-specific circuit organization.

To elucidate whether these predicted differences in intercellular signaling are reflected in corresponding divergence in intrinsic neuronal physiology, we focused on layer 4, where area-specific subtypes and unique gene expression profiles were most prominent, and performed patch-clamp recordings that intentionally enriched inhibitory neurons by targeting cells with canonical interneuron morphology (Fig. 3A) [30]. We performed whole-cell recordings on 117 biotin-filled layer 4 neurons in neocortical slices from 16 human neurosurgical resections (Fig. 3B, C; Additional file 1: Fig. S11 and Additional file 2: Table S8). Action potential waveforms and corresponding phase plots revealed pronounced differences for excitatory and inhibitory neurons across cortical areas (Fig. 3C). By integrating the 21 recorded electrophysiological features, we created a UMAP space to visualize these neurons based upon their electrophysiological similarities (Additional file 1: Fig. S12A). This revealed one excitatory neuron cluster and two inhibitory neuron clusters having distinct features of action potential upstroke/downstroke ratio, spike half-width, and afterhyperpolarization (AHP) latency (Figures S12A-S12D). Moreover, we identified additional area-specific features that were either shared or distinct between excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Shared features included larger action potential peak values in the temporal cortex and smaller membrane time constant (Tau) values in the occipital cortex for both excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Fig. 3D, E). Distinct features included larger amplitude of repolarizing maximal rates (RepoR) and a larger interval between the second and third spike (IS2) in frontal cortex excitatory neurons alone (Additional file 1: Fig. S12E). Other cell type-distinct features included larger membrane capacitance (Cm) in occipital inhibitory neurons and larger peak amplitude in temporal inhibitory neuron clusters (Additional file 1: Fig. S12F). These results mirror patterns of celltype- and area-specific transcriptomic divergence organized into functional gene programs related to ion transport, membrane excitability, and synaptic signaling, as revealed by GO enrichment analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S13 and Additional file 2: Table S7). For instance, we observed V1C-specific upregulation of ATP2B2, which enables calcium-dependent ATPase activity, and GRIN2A, a glutamate ionotropic receptor; both are known for their high levels that reduce Tau [31]. Similarly, we identified upregulation of the potassium voltage-gated channel KCNH1 in dlPFC, which has been linked to RepoR increase (Fig. 3F) [32]. In human cortical slices, the lack of genetic fluorescent markers prevented prospective targeting of PVALB 6 neurons, and subtype identity was therefore determined post hoc by immunostaining; as a result, only one recorded neuron out of 90 recorded inhibitory neurons was confirmed as HOMER3⁺/PVALB⁺ (Figures S12G–S12H). To assess whether functional and electrophysiological divergence is accompanied by cell structural differences, we next examined the neuronal morphology across neocortical areas. We reconstructed inhibitory neuron morphology from biotin-filled cells following whole-cell recordings and found that occipital inhibitory neurons had significantly more dendritic endings compared to other analyzed areas (Additional file 1: Fig. S14A), indicating increased dendritic arbor complexity. Sholl analysis showed that, within proximal segments, occipital inhibitory neurons possessed significantly longer dendritic length and more intersections (20–40 and 60–70 μm; 20–70 μm from the soma), yet their distal segments had shorter dendritic length and fewer intersections (420–450 μm; 430–440 μm from the soma), compared to those in the temporal cortex (Additional file 1: Fig. S14B), revealing an area-specific redistribution of dendritic branching across distance from the soma. Such morphological changes in inhibitory neurons across neocortical areas also match the observed enrichment of DEGs in neuron projection pathways (Additional file 1: Fig. S13 and Additional file 2: Table S7), especially the identification of V1C AUGs that has been reported to regulate neuron projection, such as EPHB1 and MAP1A (Additional file 1: Fig. S14C) [33, 34], indicating alterations in local circuits within each area. Utilizing the robust labeling of postsynaptic densities by HOMER3 immunofluorescence, we likewise reconstructed morphologies of V1C-specific PVALB 6 interneurons (Fig. 3G, H). HOMER3-positive PVALB interneurons in the occipital cortex exhibited distinct dendritic features and soma size compared to those in other areas. Occipital cortex HOMER3-positive PVALB cells also had significantly larger dendritic field area, more dendritic endings and more branch nodes (Fig. 3I). Likewise, occipital HOMER3-positive occipital interneurons had more dendritic intersections and longer dendritic length per radius through most dendritic segment partitions (30–120 and 220–440 μm from soma) (Fig. 3J). Overall, this revealed increased dendritic complexity and soma surface area originating in occipital HOMER3-positive PVALB interneurons. Transcriptome-derived indicators, morphological studies, and electrophysiologic multimodal analyses collectively outline consistent profiles of cell phenotypic diversity across neocortical areas, summarized in Additional file 2: Table S18.

Greater heterogeneity of primate neocortex

Since cellular and structural complexity has been expanded remarkably in the primate neocortex, to delineate the molecular and cellular events governing the evolution of human and non-human primate neocortical organization, we conducted cross-species transcriptomic integration between our data and published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from mice [35] and crab-eating macaques [36]. Published human snRNA-seq data were also incorporated for further validation of the cross-species integration analyses for glutamatergic and GABAergic neuronal subtypes (Fig. 4A, B) [37]. For optimal compatibility and data quality, homologous areas or the most evolutionarily concordant areas were matched across species (Additional file 2: Table S9). To compare inter-areal transcriptomic heterogeneity across species, we assessed areal transcriptomic similarity across major excitatory and inhibitory neuronal subclasses, including L2–6 IT, CT, and NP excitatory subclasses and Pvalb, Sst, Vip, and Lamp5 inhibitory subclasses, as well as non-neuronal cells, using inter-areal Pearson correlations based on highly variable genes. Overall, when considering multiple neuronal subclasses together, areal transcriptomic similarity amongst neuronal subclasses was decreased in humans and macaques than in mice (Fig. 4C), supporting increased inter-areal transcriptomic heterogeneity in primates. Thus, we investigated whether the observed elevated areal heterogeneity of the primate neocortex could be attributed to the presence and distribution of area-specific subtypes. Hierarchical clustering of excitatory neurons revealed predominant clustering by subclass rather than by species (Fig. 4D and Additional file 1: Fig. S15). Interestingly, human and macaque V1C-specific L2-4 IT neuron subtypes formed a distinct branch, while no transcriptionally corresponding V1C-specific L2-3 IT neuron subtypes were observed in mice (Fig. 4D, light blue rectangle) except for L2-3 IT 2, which showed a mouse counterpart (mm|L4 IT 6) with V1C enrichment (Fig. 4D, dark blue rectangle). Similarly, the human M1C-specific subtype (L2-3 IT 10) showed close clustering with its macaque M1C-specific homolog (mq|L2-3 IT 2) (Fig. 4D, light yellow rectangle), and was also not found in mice (Fig. 4D). Likewise, the human ACC-specific neuron subtypes (L2-3 IT 8 and L5 IT 3) did not exhibit homologous subtypes in mice, while clustering closely with macaque IT neuron subtypes present across multiple cortical areas (Fig. 4D and Additional file 1: Fig. S15, green rectangle, respectively). In contrast, L5 ET neurons were represented by fewer transcriptionally defined subtypes in primates compared to mouse homologous pyramidal tract (PT) cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S15, blue rectangle), potentially reflecting allometric cortical expansion. Together, these findings outline increased areal heterogeneity of primate upper-layer excitatory neurons, likely conferring more diverse cellular functions and cortico-cortical connectivity patterns.

Unlike glutamatergic neurons, most primate GABAergic neuronal subtypes showed strong correspondence to mouse homologs, as indicated by the intermingling of human, macaque, and mouse subtypes within fine-scale hierarchical clustering branches (Fig. 4E). However, we did not observe mouse subtypes that fully capture the primate-specific areal specializations of certain GABAergic neuronal subtypes. For instance, the human V1C-specific PVALB 6 subtype co-clustered with the macaque mq|Pvalb 6 subtype and the mouse mm|Pvalb 6 subtype (Fig. 4E and F), indicating conservation at the subtype level across species. Notably, while this PVALB subtype is area-specific in primates, its mouse homolog lacks areal specificity. In addition, the primate V1C-specific PVALB subtype exhibited consistent expression of genes such as HOMER3 and SEMA6D (Additional file 1: Fig. S16A), but these genes were neither enriched in the hierarchically related mouse PVALB populations nor in other mouse PVALB subtypes (Additional file 1: Fig. S16A). Together, these findings suggest divergence in areal specialization between primates and mice, rather than the absence of a homologous PVALB subtype in mice. In contrast, the human V1C-specific SST 4 subtype formed a branch shared between human and macaque, whereas no transcriptionally corresponding SST subtype was detected in mice (Fig. 4E, F), indicating the absence of a homologous SST 4 subtype in mouse within the limits of the current integration. This primate-specific branch exhibited consistent expression of genes such as DPP4 and EFNA5 in human and macaque, a pattern not observed in mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S16B).

Due to the neuron-enriched protocol of the mouse dataset, cross-species glial diversity comparisons were not feasible. Nevertheless, the observed increase in areal heterogeneity of both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the primate neocortex supports the hypothesis of enhanced cell subtype diversification in primate brains, which could potentially facilitate the intricate patterns of local and long-range connectivity that exemplify the primate brain.

Generation of interareal heterogeneity by DNA sequence evolution

As mutations serve as a major source behind adaptive evolution, our focus turns towards unraveling the genetic underpinnings responsible for the increased heterogeneity associated with areal specification during evolution, by identifying area-specific up- (AUPs) and down-regulated ATAC-seq peaks (ADPs), using similar approaches as for AUGs and ADGs (Methods). To assess the robustness of our peak calling, we compared the ATAC-seq peaks identified in our study with independent public resources. The identified peaks showed an 85% overlap with candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCRE) identified from a published human neocortex snATAC-seq data of BICCN consortium (Additional file 1: Fig. S17A) [38], and 68% overlapped with cCRE annotated in the SCREEN database of ENCODE encyclopedia (Additional file 1: Fig. S17B) [39], predominantly corresponding to distal and promoter-like regulatory elements (Additional file 1: Fig. S17C), supporting the high quality of our data. The union metric of interareal AUPs and ADPs, called differentially accessible peaks (DAPs), mirrored patterns seen for DEGs, with the strongest enrichment in glutamatergic IT and L6 CT neurons, followed by PVALB and SST interneurons and glial subclasses, and were most prominent in evolutionarily diversified subclasses (Additional file 1: Fig. S18A). Notably, compared with AUGs in corresponding cell types, larger proportions of AUPs were observed in dlPFC L2-3 IT neurons, ACC glutamatergic subclasses, V1C inhibitory subclasses (including PVALB and SST subclasses), as well as in glial subclasses, as indicated by elevated AUP/AUG odds ratios (FDR of chi-squared test < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S18B), suggesting that chromatin-level differences may be more prominent than gene-level expression differences in these populations.

Given the enrichment of AUPs in evolutionarily active subclasses, we postulated that AUPs in the human neocortex might have undergone DNA sequence evolution, which have a role in regulating cellular diversifications and thus could be a mechanism for larger areal specialization across the neocortex. To investigate this hypothesis, we assessed evolutionary ages of all ATAC-seq peaks identified above based on when their DNA sequence first appeared in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5A and Additional file 2: Table S10), with younger peaks indicating more recent sequence emergence during evolution. We then determined the evolutionary ages of AUPs significantly enriched in each cell subclass to mark their association with recently diversified cell types in primates and humans (Fig. 5B and Additional file 2: Table S11; see Methods). We found that evolutionarily active peaks were more likely to be enriched within cell types known to have undergone significant changes during evolution and to display increased interareal diversity. AUPs had the youngest evolutionary age in the dlPFC, which is a primate-specific area especially expanded in humans (Fig. 5B), supporting a link between recent regulatory innovations and dlPFC specialization. Specifically, dlPFC AUPs in L4 IT neurons were enriched in peaks recently appearing in the human genome, and dlPFC AUPs in L2-3 IT, L4 IT, L6 IT, L6b neurons, and OPC were enriched in peaks recently emerging in Hominoidea genomes (Fig. 5B). While AUPs in most subclasses of ACC were enriched with peaks conserved in Euarchontoglires, in deep-layer neuron subclasses of ACC, where significant diversification occurred during primate evolution [40, 41], AUPs are enriched in Hominoidea- or primate-specific peaks. To further illustrate the potential regulatory relevance of primate- and human-specific AUPs, we examined representative loci in which these young AUPs coincide with regional accessibility differences and predicted TF binding, together with coherent expression patterns of the implicated regulators across species. For example, in human V1C, we identified a human-specific AUP upstream of the TF gene JDP2 as well as a primate-specific AUP within the JDP2 locus (Additional file 1: Fig. S19, grey shaded bars). These highlighted AUPs were predicted to be the binding sites of MEF2C and FOS, respectively, and were associated with subclass-resolved expression patterns of JDP2 across L4 IT excitatory neurons and PVALB/SST interneurons (Additional file 1: Fig. S19, heatmap). In addition, we observed a primate-specific AUPs near the V1C-specific TF gene BHLHE22 that was predicted to harbor JDP2 binding (Additional file 1: Fig. S19, grey shaded bars), together with concordant subclass-resolved expression patterns of JDP2 and BHLHE22 in human V1C L4 IT neurons (Additional file 1: Fig. S19, heatmap).

Overall, these locus-level examples support our global observations that evolutionarily young AUPs show non-random enrichment in evolutionarily dynamic subclasses and primate cortical areas, supporting an association between recent regulatory sequence emergence and areal transcriptomic diversification.

Transposable elements as key contributors to AUPs

TEs represent a major source for DNA sequence insertions, prompting our investigation into the relationship between TE evolution and the emergence of AUPs in the human genome. By intersecting AUPs with TE intervals, we found that a large percentage of evolutionarily young AUPs peaks overlap with TEs in the human genome, with 48.7% of primate AUPs, 80.8% of Hominoidea AUPs, and 58.7% of human AUPs overlapping with TEs. In contrast, only 13.2% of Euarchontoglires AUPs overlap with TEs (Fig. 5C), indicating that TE insertions contribute disproportionately to recently emerged, evolutionarily young regulatory elements.

To explore these relationships in greater detail, we conducted additional enrichment analysis and noted that AUPs from distinct neocortical areas show enrichment in various TE families. For instance, the most active autonomous TE family, LINE-1 (L1) [42], was most prominently enriched in ACC glutamatergic neurons, while Mammalian-wide Interspersed Repeats (MIR) and Endogenous Retroviruses (ERVs) were enriched within AUPs of multiple V1C subclasses. Intriguingly, our investigation unveiled that evolutionarily active cell subclasses and neocortical areas have a preponderance of younger and more active TEs in their AUPs (Fig. 5D, Additional file 1: Fig. S20 and Additional file 2: Table S11; see Methods). Notably, the primate-specific TE family, Alu [21], showed significant enrichment in AUPs of upper-layer (L2-3 and L4) IT neurons of the dlPFC (Fig. 5D and Additional file 1: Fig. S21), and AUPs of OPCs were enriched in the hominid-specific TE family, SINE-VNTR-Alu (SVA) [22] (FDR = 0.04; Fig. 5D and Additional file 1: Fig. S21), suggesting a potential involvement of evolutionarily young TE families in evolutionarily active cell types, such as upper layer neurons undergoing primate-specific upper layer expansion and OPCs which have been shown to have accelerated evolution in the human lineage when compared to neurons [43]. Furthermore our findings revealed enrichments of L1 and Alu families in AUPs of deep layer neurons (L5 IT, L5 ET, L5-6 NP, L6 CT, L6 IT, and L6b neurons) in ACC, as well as in AUPs of PVALB and SST GABAergic subclasses in V1C (Fig. 5D). Most of these subclasses had a large number of interareal DEGs, with subclasses such as L5 IT, PVALB and SST neurons notably containing area- and species-specific subtypes, highlighting the fundamental role of evolutionarily active TE families in promoting primate neocortical heterogeneity.

To further investigate the potential influence of TE insertions on neocortical area-specific gene expression and cell-type specification, we identified peak-gene pairs on the same chromosome with a statistically significant Pearson correlation (Methods), considering the pairs as potential cis-regulatory elements (CREs) and their associated target genes. Notably, our analysis revealed that TE-overlapped CREs, specifically the TE-overlapped area-specific upregulated CREs (AU-CREs), exhibited a diverse range of associations with genes of distinct biotypes across different cortical areas (Fig. 5E and Additional file 2: Table S11). Specifically, V1C TE-overlapped AU-CREs within gene bodies or distally (beyond the gene body and 2 kb from the transcription start site) were predominantly linked to protein-coding genes in excitatory neurons (Fig. 5E). In contrast, TE-overlapped AU-CREs in excitatory neurons and PVALB interneurons of ACC displayed a prevalent enrichment in long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) (Fig. 5E). Both dlPFC and M1C exhibited comparatively little enrichment (Fig. 5E). Notably, in dlPFC, L1-, Alu-, and MIR-overlapped AU-CREs in L2-3 IT neurons displayed enrichment primarily associated with small noncoding RNAs, while L4 IT neurons exhibited L2-overlapped AU-CREs enriched within gene bodies of lncRNAs, and L1-overlapped AU-CREs enriched to function as distal CREs of pseudogenes (Fig. 5E).

Collectively, these findings highlight the potential significance of TE insertions in establishing cell type-, area- and species-specific genomic variations that facilitate the molecular and cellular diversification of primate and human neocortex.

Furthermore, AUPs embedded with distinct TE families in different areas displayed enrichment in H3K4me3-marked regions (Fig. 6A and Additional file 2: Table S12), signifying active promoters [46]. To investigate the role of TE-embedded AUPs as enhancers or promoters, we predicted transcription factor binding on them (Additional file 2: Table S13). Principal component analysis of transcription factors recruited by TE-embedded AUPs revealed major separation by cortical area rather than TE families (Fig. 6B). This implies that different subsets of elements in the same TE families recruit area-distinct sets of transcription factors. Specifically, in L2-3 IT neurons, Alu-embedded AUPs in dlPFC and L1-embedded AUPs in ACC were identified as outliers, while TE-embedded AUPs of ACC and V1C in L4 IT neurons showed distinct patterns compared to the other two areas, particularly L1-embedded AUPs in ACC and MIR-embedded AUPs in V1C (Fig. 6B). Among interneurons, PVALB interneurons exhibited ACC and V1C-specific separation, whereas SST interneurons showed distinction in TE-embedded AUPs of V1C, especially those embedded with Alu, L1, L2, MIR, and ERVL-MaLR (Fig. 6B).

The above findings imply that TE-embedded AUPs are likely to contain transcription factor binding sites exhibiting area-specific patterns. To illustrate, focusing on L2-3 IT neurons as an example, our investigation revealed that TE-embedded AUPs in V1C displayed a notable enrichment in binding sites for retinoic acid-related orphan receptors, specifically RORA and RORG (Fig. 6C and Additional file 2: Table S13). These receptors are documented to play a pivotal role in neuronal differentiation during development [48, 49]. This correlation suggests potential contributions of the retinoic acid-related orphan receptors and the evolution of their binding sites due to TE insertions in the specification of primate V1C.

Moreover, our analysis pinpointed TWIST2 as a compelling illustration distinguished by its human-specific expression and evolutionarily novel transcription factor binding sites in distinct areas embedded with different transposable element families. TWIST2 has been characterized in our previous study showing a human-specific expression in the frontal and temporal cortices compared with chimpanzees and macaques (Fig. 6D, barplot) [50]. Our current data confirmed higher expression of TWIST2 in the frontal areas and further uncovered the primary expression of TWIST2 in L2-3 IT and L4 IT neurons (Fig. 6D, dot plot). Concordant with this expression profile, TWIST2 binding motifs were highly enriched in the frontal L1-embedded AUPs (Fig. 6C), localizing preferentially within L1-derived segments rather than flanking sequences to support their direct provisioning by TE integration (Fig. 6E). Of particular interest is the discovery that the binding motif of TWIST2 exhibits a striking resemblance to the de novo motif identified in L1 elements within the human genome (Fig. 6F and Additional file 2: Table S13). Subsequent analysis suggests that the consensus sequence for this de novo motif harbors base mutations distinctive to three nascent L1 subfamilies, L1PA1-3, which emerged specifically in hominidae (Fig. 6F) [51–53]. These newly evolved sequences are likely to contribute additional weaker potential TWIST2 binding sites compared to their ancient counterparts in great apes (Fig. 6G). The evolution of these binding sites coupled with human-specific area-distinct expression of TWIST2, represents genetic mechanisms that could potentially contribute to human neocortical evolution, such as the upper layer diversification in the human frontal cortex.

TE expression distinguishes the frontal areas

Upon detection of H3K36me3 signals indicative of active transcription [47], we therefore explored the diversity of TE transcription across areas. We quantified TE expression at the single-cell level using RNA-seq data from our sn-multiomics dataset, and identified a total of 2,322,951 TE intervals expressing in our dataset. Noteworthy distinctions emerged when clustering cells based on TE expression, revealing clear separations corresponding to subclasses defined by gene expression (Fig. 7A, B), indicating that TE transcription captures cell-class structure in a manner consistent with canonical gene-based identity. Higher TE divergence across neocortical areas was also observed using bulk RNA-seq data (Additional file 1: Fig. S22) [50], reinforcing our conclusion. Particularly intriguing was the observation that the TE expression profile exhibited heightened area specificity, surpassing the gene expression profile, notably in the dlPFC and M1C areas, where minimal gene expression diversity was discernible between them (Fig. 7C), suggesting that TE transcription provides an additional axis of inter-areal variation not fully captured by gene expression alone. Subsequently, we identified area-specific up-regulated TE intervals (AU-TEs) using a similar approach employed for identifying AUGs (Additional file 2: Table S14). The distribution of AU-TEs across subclasses highlight the specificity of V1C, except for L2-3 IT neurons, exhibiting elevated AU-TEs in the dlPFC (Fig. 7D and Additional file 1: Fig. S23A). Across neuronal subclasses, L1 AU-TEs and Alu AU-TEs were broadly utilized to distinguish neocortical areal differences (Additional file 1: Fig. S23A), indicating that these TE families represent major contributors to areal TE-expression signatures. Notably, AU-TEs in L2-3 IT neurons were predominantly composed of L1 and Alu families, with a pronounced preference for the L1 family in dlPFC (Fig. 7D). While Alu AU-TEs in all areas demonstrated over 97% belonging to primate-derived subfamilies [54], the proportion of L1 AU-TEs from primate-derived subfamilies was selectively higher in prefrontal areas, reaching 78.2% in dlPFC and 73.8% in ACC, compared to 53.4% in M1C and 65.7% in V1C. This observation highlighted the potential roles played by evolutionarily dynamic TE expression in the specification of dlPFC L2-3 IT neurons.

Further analysis of AU-TE genomic localization in L2-3 IT neurons, showed that both all AU-TEs and primate-derived AU-TEs exhibited similar distribution patterns across genomic categories, indicating that species specificity does not substantially alter their positional preference (Additional file 1: Fig. S23B). Most L1 and Alu AU-TEs were situated near genes, particularly in intronic regions, with some constituting parts of exons and UTRs (Fig. 7E, primate-derived AU-TEs; Additional file 1: Fig. S23C, all AU-TEs), suggesting potential coupling between TE transcription and host gene transcriptional activity. Notably, both L1 and Alu AU-TEs also exhibited high enrichment in intergenic regions, with the exception of V1C, likely due to the co-expression of TEs with their host genes. In dlPFC, a higher percentage of Alu AU-TEs were located in intergenic regions and exons (Fig. 7E, primate-derived AU-TEs; Additional file 1: Fig. S23C, all AU-TEs).

Since the regulatory mechanisms by which transposable elements (TEs) act as trans-acting factors remain elusive, as a preliminary investigation, we calculated Pearson correlation between the expression levels of TEs and their nearest genes and estimated the significance of the correlation based on randomly selected background genes. Then, we quantified the percentage of TEs that displayed a significant correlation with their nearest genes across various genomic distances. We found a high percentage (same strand: 96%; opposite strand: 89%) of TEs showing a significant correlation with the genes where they resided (Fig. 7F). Conversely, TEs located in intergenic regions exhibited a lower percentage of significant correlation with their nearest genes. For intergenic TEs, no higher correlations were observed with closer genes compared to distal genes (Fig. 7F), suggesting that the relationship between TE-derived RNAs and gene expression may extend beyond local genomic proximity.

These findings collectively demonstrate that dynamic TE expression constitutes an important and previously underappreciated dimension of transcriptional organization that may contribute to neocortical area specialization and cell type–specific transcriptional landscapes.

Discussion

Our findings from diverse, multimodal data revealed integrated and overlapping patterns of genetic, epigenetic, transcriptional, morphological and electrophysiological diversity of cells in the human neocortex. This diversity extends across the evolutionary spectrum from mammals to primates and humans. Comparative genomics further illuminated genomic innovations in the human genome, suggesting their potential role in the emergence of novel cis- and trans-regulatory elements responsible for diversifying the human neocortex.

Our findings delineate disparate modes of cortical specification between frontal areas and V1C in the occipital lobe. In the V1C, the area housing the most area-specific cell types, the specification of cells appears to involve positive selection of genomic innovations, particularly transposable element (TE) insertions, which regulate protein-coding genes associated with synaptic transmission, neuronal projection, and ion channels, thereby influencing the morphological and electrophysiological properties of V1C neurons. Concurrently, the co-localization of excitatory neurons, interneurons, and astrocytes, along with inferred signaling from cell–cell communications facilitated by area-specific cell types involving axon guidance and neuron projection, indicates mutual shaping during evolution through cell–cell interactions, resulting in the co-specification of cell types within this area. Previous studies have underscored the impact of the placement of excitatory neurons in determining the positioning of interneurons, as well as the roles of astrocytes in regulating synaptic formation, pruning, and transmission, emphasizing the critical role of cell–cell interactions during development [55, 56]. In summary, our findings suggest that area-specific cell–cell communications in V1C may select genomic variants leading to the local adaptation of cell types within this area.

In contrast, few area-specific cell types were identified across frontal areas, exhibiting minimal differences in gene expression. Expanded epigenetic specification and TE expression, compared with the transcriptome, were observed in L2-3 IT neurons of dlPFC, shedding light on additional mechanisms of cellular diversification beyond gene expression. One such mechanism we found involves areal-specific changes in chromatin accessibility associated with dlPFC-specific activation of enhancers, likely regulating noncoding genes that have been largely overlooked and understudied. Another potential contributing factor is the area-specific expression of evolutionarily active TE families, notably L1 and Alu, which distinguishes L2-3 IT neurons across different areas. These findings underscore the critical role of these evolutionarily dynamic TE families in the specification of areas within the human neocortex.

TEs, comprising approximately half of the human genome, have been reported as a crucial source of novel transcription factor binding sites during primate evolution [57, 58]. Our study demonstrates area-specific chromatin accessibility of TE-embedded ATAC-seq peaks, giving rise to area-specific transcription factor binding sites. This, coupled with species- and area-specific transcription factor expression, promotes human neocortical heterogeneity. Beyond their roles as cis-regulatory substrates, accumulating evidence suggests that TEs may also influence gene regulation through trans-acting mechanisms, including modulation of mRNAs [59–61], protein recruitment [62, 63] and chromatin structures [64, 65]. In this context, our study underscores the potential role of TE transcripts in the areal diversification of upper-layer neurons in the human neocortex. Despite a decline in the overall activity of transposons over the past 35–50 million years in the hominid lineage, exceptions such as L1 and Alu elements remain active [42], coinciding with prefrontal cortical expansion in the primate lineage. Expressions of L1 transcripts have been reported to recruit Nucleolin, inducing chromatin decondensation by binding to histone H1 [66]. This, in turn, leads to significant changes in chromatin accessibility without concurrent alterations in gene expression, offering a potential mechanism to elucidate the function of area-specific TEs that do not substantially impact gene expression directly. The observed enrichment of L1 and Alu elements in the prefrontal cortex implies a potential unknown role of TEs in driving human prefrontal cortex expansion and specification. The precise mechanism through which TE transcripts enhance human neocortical areal cellular diversification warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, we conducted a cross-species, multimodal analysis of neocortical areas by combining single-nucleus multiomics and spatial transcriptomics with morphological and electrophysiological profiling. This work demonstrates that the primate neocortex exhibits increased interareal heterogeneity driven by distinct evolutionary mechanisms across functional areas, with molecular divergence matching area-specific electrophysiological and morphological phenotypes. While the primary visual cortex displays prominent transcriptomic divergence and specialized cell types that alter local circuitry, frontal areas exhibit expanded epigenetic specification where chromatin accessibility variations exceed gene expression differences. Evolutionarily young DNA insertions, particularly TEs emerging within primate, Hominoidea, and human lineages, introduce novel binding sites for area-specific transcription factors. Among these, Hominoidea specific L1 subfamilies provide additional binding motifs for transcription factors enriched in frontal areas. Furthermore, TE expression profiles differentiate frontal areas more effectively than canonical gene expression in upper-layer neurons. These insights elucidate how the coordination of genomic sequence evolution, epigenetic regulation, and transposable element activity underlies the cellular and functional reorganization of the primate brain.

Methods

Tissue procurement

For spatial transcriptome and single-nucleus multiome sequencing, the primary motor cortex (M1C; BA4), primary visual cortex (V1C; BA17), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; BA24) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC; BA9, BA46) were dissected from the human brain specimens and snap frozen in isopentane on dry ice and stored at −80ºC.

Spatial-transcriptome analysis

Tissue RNA integrity (RIN) was tested using Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), and tissue morphology was detected by hematoxylin (51275, Sigma-Aldrich) and eosin (109844, Sigma-Aldrich) (H&E) staining. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the instructions outlined in the Methanol Fixation, H&E Staining & Imaging for Visium Spatial Protocols provided by 10x Genomics. Tissues with RIN > 7.0 and compact morphology were used for further procedures (see Additional file 2: Table S1).

Tissue embedding was performed using Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. Compound (4583, SAKURA) on dry ice and then stored at − 80 °C until sectioning. Brains were taken from storage at − 80 °C and balanced at − 20 °C in a cryostat (CM1950, Leica) for 30 min before sectioning. Then sections with a thickness of 10 μm were collected.

Tissue Optimization and Spatial Gene Expression Kits (10x Genomics, Visium Spatial Gene Expression Reagent Kit, Spatial 3’ v1) were used for optimizing the time of tissue permeabilization and library construction. All procedures were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue section was first processed with H&E staining and imaging. Then tissue permeabilization was done to capture the mRNA. Following by reverse transcription, second strand synthesis, cDNA amplification, fragmentation and amplified as a final library. The resulting libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq6000 to a depth of ~ 400 M reads tissue-targeted spots with paired-end, dual indexed sequencing and run parameters read read1 150 bp, i7 index 10 bp, i5 index 10 bp and read2 150 bp. Space Ranger (v1.2.2) was used to process the sequencing data.

Neocortical layers were manually annotated by integration of marker gene expression and cytoarchitecture in the H&E slides with Loupe Browser v6.4.0 (10x Genomics).

Single-nucleus multiome sequencing

Nuclei were isolated to gain single-nucleus suspension. Briefly, dissected sample (~ 100 mg) was used to isolate nuclei through Dounce homogenization (DWK life science, 357542) with loose and tight pestles, 20 times, respectively in 5 mL lysis buffer (320 mM sucrose, 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM Mg(Ace)2, 10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0]), 0.1 M EDTA, 1,000 U/mL RNAse Protector, 1 mM DTT, 0.05% IGEPAL® CA-630). DTT, RNAse Protector and IGEPAL® CA-630 were added immediately before use. After filtered by 70 μm falcon cellular sieve, tissue homogenate was centrifugated at 2,500 rcf for 5 min at 4ºC. The pellet was resuspended in 3 mL lysis buffer and then isometric sucrose solution (1,800 mM sucrose, 3 mM Mg(Ace)2, 10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0]) was added. After gently mixed upside down, centrifugation was done at 2,500 rcf for 5 min at 4ºC. The pellet was resuspended in 100 μL 1 × lysis buffer (10x Genomics, multiome protocol) and then incubated on ice for 2 min. 1 mL wash buffer (10x Genomics, multiome protocol) was added to dilution background information. After filter the nuclei solution using 20 μm pluriStrainer, centrifugation was done at 500 rcf for 5 min at 4ºC. 100 μL 1 × Nuclei buffer (10x Genomics, multiome kit) was added to resuspend nuclei pellets.

The nuclei were then counted, resuspended at 8 k per μL, treated with transposition enzymes and then encapsulated on a 10x Genomics Chromium machine, using the Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression kit, v1(10x Genomics). After encapsulation, the resulting libraries were done pre-amplification PCR first, and then divided into two parts for single-nucleus RNA-seq library construction and single-nucleus ATAC-seq library construction, respectively. Both libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq6000 to a target depth of ~ 100 k reads per nucleus, respectively. Cell Ranger ARC (v2.0.0) was used to process sequencing data to generate gene expression data and chromatin accessibility data.

Preprocessing of single-nucleus multiome data

The output of 10x Cell Ranger ARC software was imported into Signac (R package, v.1.5.0) [67]. For quality control, Seurat (R package, v4.0.3) [68] was used to calculate the percentage of mitochondria mRNA reads of each cell. Transcription start site (TSS) enrichment score was calculated by Signac and ATAC reads located in the ENCODE blacklist (hg38, https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist) [69] were also counted. Cells matched the following criterion were retained for downstream analysis: (1) the number of detected genes was more than 500; (2) the percentage of mitochondria reads was less than 5%; (3) the total number of ATAC reads in peaks (called by Cell Ranger) was more than 1,000; (4) TSS enrichment score more than 1.0; (5) the percentage of ATAC reads in the ENCODE hg38 blacklist is less than 5%.

Finally, DoubletFinder (R package, v2.0.3) [70] was used to identify doublet cells. And we only used the single-nucleus transcriptome (snRNA-seq) data. DoubletFinder was run for 100 times with various random seeds to eliminate randomness, and the final doublet score for cells is the average of all runs. Then those cells with doublet score > 0.3 were removed.

Construction of hierarchical cell taxonomy based on transcriptome

Seurat standard clustering workflow was applied to each sample to identify transcriptomic clusters using snRNA-seq data. All clusters were then classified into three classes – glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons and non-neuronal cells based on known markers. We then merged cells of the same class from all samples. The merged non-neuronal cells were further classified into astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs), microglia, endothelial cells, vascular leptomeningeal cells (VLMCs) and T-cells based on known markers (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D).

Further clustering was performed to obtain subtypes in glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPC and microglia, respectively, as follows: (1) cells were divided into subsets of two donors; (2) mitochondrial genes, XIST, TSIX and genes on chromosome Y were removed; (3) a total of 3,000 anchor features between donors were identified, and Seurat CCA integration was applied with 50 dimensions to generate the integrated data; (4) data scaling, PCA (50 dimensions) and UMAP visualization were performed on the integrated data, and Louvain clustering algorithm was used to identify subtypes (for glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons, the resolution is 1.6 respectively; for astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPC and microglia, the resolution is 0.2 respectively). In predefined microglia, there was a perivascular macrophages (PVM) subtype according to the expression of LYVE1 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D), which was then isolated as an independent subclass. We did not perform a further clustering on PVM, endothelial, VLMC, and T-cells. These subclasses were determined to contain a single subtype. Finally, we excluded 1 glutamatergic subtype and 2 oligodendrocyte subtypes from downstream analysis, as they were donor-specific (the proportion of cells from one donor is greater than 90%).

Presto (R package, v1.0.0) [71] was used to identify marker genes of all subtypes in the single-nucleus transcriptome data. The union set of top 100 positive markers and top 100 negative markers of all subtypes were used as the highly variable genes in the Seurat object. Data scaling, PCA (50 dimensions) and UMAP reduction based on the top 50 PCs were calculated by Seurat.

To construct the dendrogram of subtypes, the average of PCA scores was used to calculate the pairwise distances between subtypes, and the dendrogram was generated from the distance matrix using the R “hclust” function with the agglomeration method “ward.D2”.

We also calculated the proportion of each neocortical area in subtypes. To correct the sampling bias across samples, we calculated a normalization factor for each sample by dividing the average total nucleus number across samples by the total nucleus number of each sample. For each subtype, the number of nuclei from each sample was multiplied by its normalization factor. The normalized number of nuclei in a subtype was then calculated by adding normalized number of nuclei from different donors. Finally, for each subtype, the inter-area proportion was calculated using the normalized nuclei number of four neocortical areas.

We determined the subclass identity of glutamatergic and GABAergic subtypes by mapping our single-nucleus data to the publicly available single-cell data (Allen Cell Types Database-Human Multiple Cortical Areas Smart-Seq, https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq/human-multiple-cortical-areas-smart-seq) [24] and our spatial transcriptome data.

For glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons, the transcriptome data was integrated with the glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons of the Allen Brain reference data respectively. First, we identified 3,000 anchor features using Seurat “SelectIntegrationFeatures” function, and calculated anchor object using Seurat “FindIntegrationAnchors” function with 50 dimensions. Then we passed the anchor object to “TransferData” to calculate each cell’s prediction score for subtypes of the reference data. Each cell was assigned to the reference subtype with highest prediction score. And for each of our subtypes, the proportion of cells assigned to each reference subtype and the average prediction score for those assigned cells were calculated. Each of our subtypes was assigned to the subclass containing the reference subtype with the highest average prediction score (Figures S3A-S3B).

To fit the diversity of layer structure among neocortical areas, we further assigned the layer identity of each IT neuron subtype according to its cortical layer abundance (see “Mapping cells to spatial transcriptome data” section) in the area with the most cells of that subtype, and the assignment strategy was as follows: subtypes with the highest abundance in layer 2 or with top two abundances in layer 3 and layer 2 were assigned to L2-3 IT; subtypes with the highest abundance in layer 4 or with top two abundances in layer 3 and layer 4 were assigned to L4 IT; subtypes with the highest abundance in layer 5 were assigned to L5 IT; subtypes with the highest abundance in layer 6 were assigned to L6 IT. As the only exception, the subtype “L6 IT 3” showed high abundance in both upper layer (L2-3) and deep layer (L5-6), and we assigned it to L6 IT due to the expression of THEMIS, the lack of CUX2 and FEZF2, and the proximity in the dendrogram to other deep layer IT neuron subtypes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D).

NS-Forest v3.9 (Python script, https://github.com/JCVenterInstitute/NSForest) [72] was used to determine the minimum marker gene set for each subtype, whose combined expression pattern was sufficient to distinguish the corresponding subtype. The original algorithm used the median to represent the gene expression in subtypes, while we used the 20% trim-mean instead. The marker gene sets for subtypes were listed in Additional file 2: Table S5. We selected at most two markers of each subtype for visualization (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

Mapping cells to spatial transcriptome data

The H&E stained image of each sample was imported into squidpy (Python package, v1.2.0) [73], and nucleus segmentation on the image was performed using stardist (Python package, v0.8.2) [74, 75] with the pretrained model “2D_versatile_he”. We captured the stardist output by squidpy “segment” function and counted the number of nuclei on each spot by squidpy “calculate_image_features” function.

Each sample of snRNA-seq data was mapped to the corresponding sample (the same donor and area) of spatial transcriptome data using cell2location (Python package, v0.1) [76]. For each sample, the training genes required by cell2location were the union set of subtypes’ top 200 positive markers and top 200 negative markers, which were identified by Presto using that sample of data. Since the accurate cell2location result requires an expression profile of subtypes with high discrimination, so we evaluated the subtype quality by f-beta score in each sample respectively, which was calculated in a support vector machine (SVM) classifier implemented by scikit-learn (Python package, v1.1.2) [77]. For each sample, only subtypes with f-beta score > 0.5 or cell number > = 30 in that sample were kept. This metric made all subtypes available in at least one area and kept most area-specific subtypes only available in that area.

Cell2location also required a hyperparameter “N_cells_per_location” to estimate the global cell density in spatial spots. For each area of donor No.61, we calculated the average nuclei number of spatial spots as the value of “N_cells_per_location”. For each area of donor No.53, the parameter “N_cells_per_location” is the same as that used in the same area of donor No.61, because in donor No.53 we did not get good results for nuclei segmentation due to the low resolution of H&E stained images. Cell2location calculated the “abundance” of subtypes for each spot, which represents how likely a subtype is to be located at that spot. We calculated the average subtype abundance for each spatial layer and generated an abundance matrix (layer as row, subtype as column). To combine the independent results of two donors, first we normalized the abundance matrix via dividing each column by the sum of that column, then we calculated the average of the two donors’ matrix. Finally, the average matrix was normalized by column again.

After assigning the subclass identity of all subtypes (see “Construction of hierarchical cell taxonomy based on transcriptome” section), we calculated the subclass abundance at the same way used for calculating the subtype abundance, and both the training genes and the parameter “N_cells_per_location” were the same for the same sample.

We did not average the subclass abundance by layer, instead we visualized the subclass distribution on the spatial transcriptome spots. For each spot of the spatial transcriptome, first we ranked the original subclass abundances and only kept the top two subclasses to define the subclass composition of that spot. Then we randomly generated two points within the spot to represent the two subclasses respectively, and the transparency of both points was the percentile ranking of the abundance of that spot compared with the abundances of all spots.

Mapping subtypes to cortical layers of the published human snRNA-seq data

To further validate the layer assignment of subtypes, we aligned our cells to the Allen Brain reference dataset used in “Construction of hierarchical cell taxonomy based on transcriptome” section, employing the meta data of the dissected cortical layers. First, we split our snRNA-seq data by neocortical area and integrated our cells in each area with the reference cells from the same area. Our cells from dlPFC were aligned to the reference cells from middle temporal gyrus (MTG), as no cells were sampled from dlPFC in the reference data. For each neocortical area, a total of 3,000 anchor features were calculated by Seurat “SelectIntegrationFeatures” function, then Seurat “FindIntegrationAnchors” and “TransferData” function with 50 dimensions were performed to calculate each cell’s prediction score for different cortical layers of the reference data. We calculated the average prediction score in each subtype and generated a score matrix (layer as row, subtype as column). Finally, we normalized the score matrix via dividing each column by the sum of that column.

Integration with the published primate snRNA-seq data

In order to confirm about the areal specificity of the interneuron subtypes, we used Seurat to integrate interneurons from our dataset and the published primate dlPFC single-nucleus RNA-seq data [25] deposited in the NEMO Archive (RRID:SCR_002001) under identifier nemo:dat-zkgd0it accessible at Nemo Archive (https://assets.nemoarchive.org/dat-zkgd0it). The integration workflow of Seurat was conducted with 3,000 anchor features and 50 dimensions, followed by UMAP reduction on the integrated data.

Differential gene expression analysis between neocortical areas

The “FindMarkers” function (test.use = “MAST”) of Seurat was used to perform differential expression analysis between neocortical areas on the normalized snRNA-seq data, with the donor as a latent variable for “FindMarkers”. And the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were filtered with the absolute log2-transformed foldchange > 0.25, the adjusted P-value < 0.05 and the expression proportion > 0.2. For each subclass, DEGs in all pairwise comparisons between neocortical areas were identified. DEGs with positive foldchanges in all pairwise comparisons between a specific area and others, were considered as area-specific upregulated genes (AUGs) for that area. DEGs with negative foldchanges between a specific area and others, were regarded as area-specific downregulated genes (ADGs) for that area.

Constructing networks of pathways enriched with AUGs

The “compareCluster” function (ont = “ALL”, qvalueCutoff = 0.05) of enrichplot (R package, v1.19.1) was used to analyze the enrichment of AUGs in biological process, cellular component and molecular GO terms. The default parameter “cutoff = 0.05” of clusterProfiler (R package, v4.6.0) [78, 79] was used to reduce index > 0.2 (min_edge = 0.2).

Analysis of cell–cell communication

CellChat (R package, v1.0.0) [80] was used to infer cell–cell communication (CCC) for each neocortical area. To include more confident ligand-receptor (LR) pairs, we replaced the original LR resource (546 ligands, 546 receptors) from CellChat with the LR resource (1036 ligands, 1036 receptors) in the liana (R package, v0.1.7) [81], which is a curated resource integrated multiple CCC tools. And we used the liana “cellchat_formatDB” function to convert its LR resource to CellChat format. We ran CellChat at the subtype level using default parameters. Endothelial cells, VLMC and T-cells were excluded due to their small cell number and unbalanced sampling across samples.

Cross-species comparison of inter-areal heterogeneity

Single-cell RNA-seq data of mouse (Mus musculus) whole brain was downloaded from the Allen Brain Cell Atlas, following the guideline in https://AllenInstitute.github.io/abc_atlas_access [35]. We first selected cells dissected from “MOp” (primary motor area), “ACA” (anterior cingulate area), “VIS” (visual areas), “VISp” (primary visual area), “MOs_FRP” (secondary motor area, front pole) and “PL-ILA-ORB” (prelimbic area, infralimbic area, orbital area) as the matching areas to our areas (Additional file 2: Table S9). Then the following classes were kept for downstream analysis: “01 IT-ET Glut”, “02 NP-CT-L6b Glut”, “06 CTX-CGE GABA”, “07 CTX-MGE GABA”, “30 Astro-Epen”, “31 OPC-Oligo”, “33 Vascular”, “34 Immune”.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data from human neocortex were downloaded from the Neuroscience Multi-omics Archive (Identifier: nemo:dat-tfy47s9, https://data.nemoarchive.org/publication_release/Human_Cross_Areal_Analysis) [37]. We kept cells dissected from the same neocortical areas (dlPFC, M1C, ACC, V1C) as our study for downstream analysis.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data from the crab-eating macaque (Macaca fascicularis) neocortex were downloaded from Macaque Brain Atlas (https://macaque.digital-brain.cn/spatial-omics) [36]. To match the areas used in this study, we first selected cells dissected from the following areas: Brodmann area 46, Brodmann area 9, area M1 (F1), Brodmann area 24, Brodmann area 32 and primary visual cortex (see Additional file 2: Table S9). Then we took the published human neocortex snRNA-seq data as reference, using Seurat “TransferData” function to assign the subclass identity of cells in the macaque snRNA-seq dataset.

To perform a comparative analysis of transcriptional variation across the neocortical areas in datasets of our study, human (published), macaque and mouse, we first aligned the areas and the subclasses between the datasets of three species (Additional file 2: Table S9). Non-glial non-neuronal cells in all species were excluded from analysis, and L5 ET/PT cells were also excluded owing to their scarcity in V1C of the human and macaque data. Next, we used biomaRt (R package, v2.50) to convert macaque and mouse gene symbols to their respective human homologs. Subsequently, for each area of each species, we utilized Seurat “FindVariableFeatures” function to identify 3,000 highly variable genes from the cross-species homologous genes. Finally, within each subclass, we calculated the pairwise Pearson correlations between areas of each species, using only the union set of the highly variable genes from all areas of all species.

To balance the resolution of clusters in different public datasets, new subtypes were generated for each public dataset used in cross-species comparison, following the same clustering pipeline: (1) First, we down-sampled 1000 cells in each area of each subclass with Seurat “SketchData” function; and if any original subtype was not sampled or if the number of sampled cells was less than 30, we then sampled 30 cells for that subtype from the remaining cells. (2) We used Seurat to perform initial clustering, generate a series of clusters at different resolution parameters (0.1 ~ 4, step = 0.1). (3) For each resolution parameter, we utilized MetaNeighbor (R package, v1.18.0) [82] to predict the similarities (AUROC scores) between subtypes of public dataset and those of our dataset (reference), and only homologous genes between species were used in this step. (4) The core idea for finding the optimal resolution is to find the step where the number of top hits (AUROC to reference > 0.97) for all subtypes remains stable as the resolution increases; when the number of top hits remains constant from one resolution to its next 5 resolutions, then that resolution will be considered as the optimal one; and if there is no resolution that keeps the top hit number stable, we take the first resolution where the number of top hits is equal to the number of subtypes as the optimal one. (5) Finally, if one subtype’s maximum AUROC is more than 0.01 higher than all other AUROCs, then the reference with the maximum AUROC is considered as the best hit for that subtype; to avoid over-splitting, we merged those subtypes with the same best hit.

To establish a consensus subtype taxonomy across species, we employed the AUCROC scores from MetaNeighbor. The pairwise AUC scores of subtypes between different species were calculated by the “run_MetaNeighbor_US” function and were then subtracted from 1, producing the pairwise subtype distances. Subsequently, the cross-species subtype dendrogram was generated from the pairwise subtype distances using the “hclust” function (method = “ward.D2”) of the R language.

We utilized Seurat to integrate interneurons of human, macaque and mouse. For macaque and mouse, the down-sampled datasets were used. First, a total of 3,000 anchor features were calculated by Seurat “SelectIntegrationFeatures” function from the cross-species homologous genes. Next, SCTransform [83] and integration (“FindIntegrationAnchors” and “IntegrateData” function) with 50 dimensions was performed. Finally, UMAP reduction on the integrated data was calculated to visualize the extent of correspondence across subtypes of the three species.

To assess the potential presence of the human V1C-specific interneurons (PVALB 6 and SST 4) in other species, we used AUCell (R package, v1.16.0) [84] to calculate the AUC score for the marker genes of PVALB 6 and SST 4, which summarized their expression patterns. The marker genes for each subtype were selected by intersecting three gene sets: positive markers of the subtype compared to all other cells, and positive markers of the subtype compared to the two nearest subtypes in the dendrogram respectively. All positive markers were identified using Seurat “FindMarkers” function. We identified 20 markers for PVALB 6 and 59 markers for SST 4 (Additional file 2: Table S5) used in AUCell analysis.

Identification of reproducible ATAC peaks

We followed the pipeline described in the previous research [85], using the script provided in it (https://github.com/yal054/snATACutils) for peak calling. The position-sorted ATAC bam of Cell Ranger ARC was first deduplicated and name-sorted, and then was convert to the paired reads (bedpe format). The peak calling was performed on the paired reads of each subclass of each neocortical area respectively: (1) a pooled data was generated from the merged paired reads of two donor-replicates; (2) two pseudo-replicates comprising the random half of the pooled data were generated; (3) the four replicates (two donor- and two pseudo-) and the pooled data were converted to Tn5-corrected bed format, and peak calling was performed on each of them using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) [86] with the following parameters: –format BED –gsize hs –qvalue 0.01 –nomodel –shift −75 –extsize 150 –SPMR –keep-dup all –call-summits; (4) we kept reproducible peaks which either were detected in the pooled data and overlapped ≥ 50% of the peak length with a peak in both donor-replicates, or were detected in the pooled data and overlapped ≥ 50% of the peak length with a peak in both pseudo-replicates; (5) we extended peak summits by 250 bp on either side to a final width of 501 bp; (6) only peaks on chromosomes 1–22 and X were kept, and those in the ENCODE blacklist (hg38, https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist) [51] were also removed.

Peaks called by MACS2 from all subclasses of all neocortical areas were collected together and merged using an iterative overlap strategy implemented in the script: (1) all peaks were first ranked by their MACS2 scores decreasingly; (2) the top one peak was kept and any peak that directly overlapped with it was removed; (3) for the next in the remained peaks, this process was performed again; (4) the process was repeated iteratively until no more peaks overlap with others. This strategy avoided daisy-chaining and still allowed for using fixed-width peaks. Finally, the MACS2 scores of peaks were normalized per million (SPM), and those peaks with SPM < 5 were filtered. We identified a total of 273,103 reproducible ATAC peaks.

Denoising of single-nucleus ATAC-seq data

We generated a new single-nucleus chromatin accessibility (snATAC-seq) count matrix from the reproducible ATAC peaks using Signac “FeatureMatrix” function. For each snATAC-seq count matrix of glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPCs and microglia, we used pycisTopic (Python package, v1.0.2) [87] to identify co-accessible peak topics respectively, and the denoised snATAC count matrix was imputed from the cell-topic and topic-peak probabilities. As a hyperparameter for pycisTopic, the optimal number of peak topics was automatically determined by the function “evaluate_models” (glutamatergic = 95, GABAergic = 85, astrocyte = 35, oligodendrocyte = 50, OPC = 30, microglia = 20).

Cell clustering using denoised snATAC-seq data

Clustering was performed utilizing the pycisTopic results (see “Denoising of single-nucleus ATAC-seq data” section) of glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPC and microglia respectively: (1) the peak topics were regarded as the dimension reduction of the snATAC-seq data, and we corrected the batch effect of the peak topics using harmonypy (Python package, v0.0.6) [88]; (2) Nearest neighbor analysis, Leiden clustering and UMAP reduction were conducted by scanpy using the corrected peak topics. The optimal resolutions for each cell types were determined according to UMAP visualization (glutamatergic neurons = 2.0, GABAergic neurons = 2.0, astrocytes = 0.3, oligodendrocyte = 0.4, OPC = 0.2, microglia = 0.2). Finally, we measured the alignment of snRNA-seq clusters and snATAC-seq clusters by Jaccard indexes.

Differential chromatin accessibility analysis between neocortical areas

We normalized the denoised snATAC-seq count matrix by scanpy (Python package, v1.8.2) [89] “normalize_total” function and perform the differential accessible analysis by scanpy “rank_genes_groups” function with the Wilcoxon method. And the differential accessible peaks (DAPs) were filtered with the absolute foldchange > 1.5, the adjusted P-value < 0.01 and the accessibility proportion > 0.2. For each subclass, DAPs in all pairwise comparisons between neocortical areas were identified. DAPs which exhibited positive foldchanges in all pairwise comparisons between a specific area and others, were considered as area-specific upregulated peaks (AUPs) for that area. Similarly, area-specific downregulated peaks (ADPs) were identified as DAPs with negative foldchanges in all pairwise comparisons between a specific area and others. For each subclass, the union set of accessible peaks (accessibility proportion > 0.2) of all areas were regarded as the background peaks for it, which was used for downstream enrichment analysis (see “Enrichment analysis for AUPs” section).

We performed a comparative analysis between the relative number of AUPs and AUGs. Within each subclass, we calculated the proportion of AUPs/AUGs of specific area in relation to the whole AUPs/AUGs across all areas in that subclass. To assess whether the proportions of AUPs were significantly higher than those of AUGs, we employed the “prop.test” function in the R language, specifying the “alternative” parameter as “greater”. Finally, for each subclass, the P-values of all areas were adjusted using the R “p.adjust” function, with the “method” parameter set to “BH”.

Synteny analysis and peak evolutionary age determination

Batch coordinate conversion between human (hg38) and 12 different species (PanTro6, PanPan3, PapAnu4, RheMac10, CalJac4, Mm39, NomLeu3, OryCun2, Rn7, OtoGar3, GorGor6 and OchPri3) (see Additional file 2: Table S15 for details) was obtained through UCSC liftOver tool (option -minMatch = 0.95), which relies on whole-genome alignments with BLASTZ. The evolutionary age of the total 273,103 ATAC peak sequences was assumed to be the divergence time between human and the farthest species to it with at least 95% homology: (1) peak sequences of Euarchontoglires age were defined as those sequences in human genome which are derived before Euarchontoglires age and are shared mapped sequence across all the 12 different species when mapping; (2) peak sequences of primate age were defined as those sequences in human genome which are derived in the primate age and do not exist in other clades; these sequences are the shared unmapped sequences across glires (rodents and rabbits), but do not belong to any unmapped sequences for primate clade species; (3) peak sequences of Hominoidea age were defined as those sequences in human genome which are specifically derived in the Hominoidea age and do not exist in other primate species; these sequences are the shared unmapped sequences across other primates, but do not belong to any unmapped sequences for Hominoidea clade species: (4) finally, peak sequences of human age were defined as those sequences which are human specific and do not exist in other Hominoidea clade species; therefore, these sequences are the shared unmapped sequences across other Hominoidea clade species. Bedtools suite [90] Subtract and Intersect tools were used to extract the union and intersection intervals across the unmapped files in the above steps.

Enrichment analysis for AUPs

We performed Fisher’s exact test to analyze the evolutionary age enrichment for AUPs. For each type of AUPs (specific area and specific subclass), the accessible peaks of that subclass were used as the background (see “Differential chromatin accessibility analysis between neocortical areas” section), in which peaks excluded from AUPs were referred to as “non-AUPs” below. We constructed a contingency table \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[\begin{array}{cc}a& c\\ b& d\end{array}\right]$$\end{document}acbd for the enrichment test of specific evolutionary age: a, the number of AUPs with the given evolutionary age; b, the number of non-AUPs with the given evolutionary age; c, the number of AUPs without the given evolutionary age; d, the number of non-AUPs without the given evolutionary age. Then we applied the R “fisher.test” function to this contingency table, setting the “alternative” parameter to “greater”. Finally, within the given type of AUPs, we adjusted the P-values across different evolutionary ages by R function “p.adjust” function, using Benjamini–Hochberg method (“BH”, also known as “FDR”).

We also estimated the enrichment of AUPs in peaks overlapping with different transposable element (TE) families, using the same method described above. TE intervals in the human genome (hg38) were downloaded from UCSC Table Browser (group = Repeats, track = RepeatMasker). And overlap between peaks and TE intervals was determined by “findOverlapsOfPeaks” function of ChIPpeakAnno (R package, v3.28.1) [91].

Prediction of peak-gene linkage

We linked ATAC peaks to target genes utilizing the sn-multiome data in glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, OPC and microglia respectively. The original gene annotation of Cell Ranger contained limited biotypes of genes. In order to fully characterize gene expression regulation, we downloaded the comprehensive gene annotation on the primary assembly from GENCODE [92] (Human Release 43: https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/gencode/Gencode_human/release_43/gencode.v43.primary_assembly.annotation.gtf.gz). We then applied the Cell Ranger “mkref” and “count” functions to re-quantify the RNA assay of sn-multiome sequencing data, generating a new snRNA-seq data for inference of peak-gene linkages.

For each peak-gene pair where the peak is located within 500 kb from the gene TSS, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient r between the expression of the gene and the denoised accessibility of the peak. Next, we randomly selected 1000 peaks located on the different chromosomes to the gene as the background peaks. Subsequently, the Pearson correlation coefficients between the gene and the background peaks were calculated. Then a z-score was computed as z = (r − μ)/σ, where μ and σ were the mean and standard deviation of the background Pearson correlation coefficients respectively. Finally, the z-score was converted to a two-tailed P-value using the “stats.norm” function of scipy (Python package, v1.9.0) [93]. Only peak-gene pairs with a P-value < 0.05 were retained as significantly correlated linkages, and the corresponding peaks were regarded as potential cis-regulatory elements (CREs).

Within each family of TE-overlapped CREs, we tested whether AU-CREs were enriched in CREs regulating specific gene biotype, using the same method described in the “Enrichment analysis for AUPs” section. For conciseness, we categorized the gene biotypes of the gene annotation into four types: protein-coding, lncRNA, pseudogene and small non-coding RNA (small ncRNA). See Additional file 2: Table S16 for details.

Histone modification enrichment

AUPs with at least 125 bp overlap with transposable elements were kept as TE-embedded AUPs, which was identified using Bedtools “intersect” function (-f = 0.25). A union set of TE-embedded AUPs from all subclasses was also generated for each area. Subsequently, we employed GIGGLE (v0.6.3) [94] to perform histone modification enrichment for TE-embedded AUPs and the union set of each area, respectively. Datasets of histone modification were downloaded from the ENCODE portal [95–97] with their accessions listed in Additional file 2: Table S17. For each dataset, a GIGGLE index was built, and TE-embedded AUPs were used as query against the index. The P-values of right tailed test of GIGGLE results were kept for this analysis. Then, for each histone modification, we utilized scipy’s “stats.combine_pvalues” function (method = “mudholkar_george”) to combine P-values of different datasets. Finally, the combined P-values across histone modifications were adjusted using R “p.adjust” function (method = “BH”).

Motif enrichment

We conducted both known and de novo motif enrichment using findMotifsGenome.pl tool of Homer (v4.11) [98]. For each TE-embedded AUPs, known motif enrichment was implemented using a “-size” parameter of 200 and randomly selected background regions. Additionally, for each TE family, de novo motif finding was performed using the corresponding TE intervals in the human genome with “-size” set to “given” and random genomic regions as background. And annotatePeaks.pl tool of Homer was used to find the consensus sequences in the analyzed TE intervals.

To summarize the pattern of know motif enrichment results, we constructed a matrix filled by -log(P-values) of enrichment, with types of TE-embedded AUPs as rows and TF motifs as columns. Then we applied PCA dimension reduction on this matrix using R “prcomp” function, with centering and scaling (center = TRUE, scale = TRUE).

Analysis of the transcriptome of transposable elements

To generate the transcriptome of TEs in the human genome, first we downloaded the annotation file of TE locus in human genome from the website of TEtranscripts [99] (https://labshare.cshl.edu/shares/mhammelllab/www-data/TElocal/prebuilt_indices/hg38_rmsk_TE.gtf.locInd.gz). The expression level of TE locus at single cell level were quantified using Stellarscope (https://github.com/nixonlab/stellarscope) [100], taking the sequencing fastq files as input and generating TE count matrix. The output of Stellarscope was imported into Seurat, preserving only the barcodes shared with our sn-multiomic dataset. Those TEs expressed in less than three cells were also filtered. This resulted in a final TE count matrix containing 70,757 cells and 2,882,700 TEs. Additionally, the genomic annotations of TEs were identified by “annotatePeak” function of ChIPseeker (R package, v1.32.0) [101], using the comprehensive gene annotation on the primary assembly from GENCODE v43.

The TE count matrix mirrored characteristics of scATAC-seq data, being a highly sparse matrix with numerous features. Therefore, we utilized Signac pipeline to analyze the TE count matrix. Normalization was conducted using Signac’s “RunTFIDF” function, and variable features were identified via Signac’s “FindTopFeatures” function with a minimum cutoff set at “q90”. Dimension reduction was performed by Signac’s “RunSVD” function with 50 components. After dropping the first component which mainly captured variations in sequencing depth, we conducted UMAP visualization using the remained components.

We estimated the neocortical area heterogeneity in the UMAP embeddings generated from TE transcriptome. First, we calculated 50 nearest neighbors for each cell. Then, for any cell from specific area, the proportion of nearest neighbors originating from the same area was measured as the area-specificity score. Finally, the average area-specificity score for each subclass of each area was computed. For comparison between the transcriptome of TE and gene, we also estimated the area-specific score of subclasses based on the UMAP embeddings from the transcriptome of our sn-multiomic data.

Identification of differentially expressed TEs (DE-TEs) between neocortical areas was conducted using Seurat’s “FindMarkers” function. In this analysis, the “LR” test was used, the donor and total count were taken as latent variables, and the criteria for filtering were set as the log2-transformed fold change greater than 0.1, the adjusted P-value less than 0.05, and the expression proportion exceeding 0.1. DE-TEs which exhibited positive fold changes in all pairwise comparisons between specific area and other areas were identified as area-specific upregulated TEs (AU-TEs) for that area. Conversely, DE-TEs which exhibited negative fold changes in all pairwise comparisons between specific area and other areas were identified as area-specific downregulated TEs (AD-TEs) for that area.

TEs were associated with putative target genes using the similar method described in the “Prediction of peak-gene linkage” section. We quantified the Pearson correlation coefficient between the expression levels of each TE and its nearest gene. Subsequently, we randomly selected 1000 genes located on the different chromosomes to the TE as the background genes, to compute the z-score and P-value of the Pearson correlation coefficient. A significance threshold of P-value < 0.05 was applied.

For published bulk RNA-seq dataset in adult human brain which is downloaded from PsychENCODE (https://evolution.psychencode.org/) [50], TEtranscripts was utilized to quantify and normalize the expression levels for both genes and TE locus. Only features expressed in all donors were remained for downstream analysis. Seurat “FindVariableFeatures” function (selection.method = “mvp”) was employed to determine top 5,000 variable genes and top 5,000 variable TEs. Then we calculated the Pearson correlation between different neocortical areas based on the expression of variable genes and variable TEs.

Electrophysiological recordings and analysis

Surgical specimens were obtained from Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. Data included in this study were obtained from neurosurgical tissue resections for the treatment of refractory temporal lobe epilepsy or deep brain tumor (Additional file 2: Table S8). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants to use their tissue as well as to share the acquired electro-physiological data and anonymized demographic information, including age, sex, and diagnosis. All procedures were approved by Huashan Hospital’s institutional review board. Tissue block was immediately submerged in 4 °C slicing solution following surgery. The slicing solution is comprised of (in mM): 213 sucrose, 10 D-glucose, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2·2H2O and 2 MgSO4·7H2O. Osmolality was 290—300 mOsm kg−1. Prior to use, the slicing solution was kept at an icing status and continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. The human tissue block was transported from the hospital to the laboratory for further procedures.

Tissue blocks were carefully sectioned in order to preserve intact cortical laminar boundaries. 300 μm transverse brain slices were obtained using a vibratome (Leica 1200 V) in icing slicing solution. After sectioning, the brain slices were immediately transferred to a carbogenated 95% O2/5% CO2 and warmed (34 °C) Artificial Cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) to recover for at least 30 min in room temperature. The slicing ACSF is comprised of (in mM): 126 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 10 D-glucose, 3.4 CaCl2·4H2O and 2 MgCl2 and pH was adjusted to 7.3 using NaOH. Osmolality was 290—300 mOsm kg−1. Following this incubation, the brain slices were maintained in ACSF at room temperature for patch recording.

During electrophysiology recording, the slices were transferred to a custom chamber and were stabilized with a fine nylon net attached to a custom designed platinum ring. This recording chamber was continuously perfused with oxygenated ACSF solution throughout the recording. Under the IR-DIC microscope, layer 4 was identified as a narrow band of densely packed, small granular cells, whereas the adjacent layer which was dorsal to layer 4 with sparse neuronal distribution was identified as layer 3. Layer 5 was identified with smaller neuronal density and ventral to layer 4. Owing to the devoid of genetic approaches to effectively label these spatially distributed and in some cases exceedingly rare cell types, the localization of pyramidal neurons and interneurons in layer 4 were preliminarily determined by soma morphology under IR-DIC microscope. Thick-walled borosilicate glass (Sutter Instruments, BF150-86-10) electrodes were pulled (Sutter Instruments, P97) to a resistance of 5–7 MΩ. Before recording, the electrodes were filled with ~ 1.0—1.5 μL of internal solution with biotin (123 mM potassium gluconate, 10.0 mM HEPES, 12 mM KCl, 0.2 mM EGTA, 4 mM Mg-ATP, 0.3 mM Na-ATP, 10 mM Na-phosphocreatine, 20 μg/mL glycogen, and 0.5% biotin (Sigma B4261), pH 7.3). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were obtained using a Multiclamp 700 A amplifier, Digidata 1440 digitizer, pClamp 10.5 data acquisition software (Axon instruments, Molecular Devices, USA). The electrical signals were digitized at 20 kHz, holding the cell in current-clamp mode for 20 min. The access resistance was monitored throughout the recording (between 8 and 30 MΩ), and neurons were discarded if the access resistance was > 30 MΩ. To enrich for inhibitory neurons, during recordings we targeted layer 4 and preferentially selected neurons with morphological features characteristic of inhibitory interneurons, including typically smaller somata, simpler dendritic arbors, and a lack of dendritic spines [30].

Data were analyzed offline using Clampfit 10.5 (Molecular Devices, USA) and custom-written scripts in Python 3.8. The resting membrane potential (RMP) was measured after breaking into the cell (IC = 0). Other 20 electrophysiological features were measured from spontaneous firing or responses to hyperpolarizing, near-threshold, and suprathreshold current injections. Analysis of inhibitory neurons yielded 21 total components, excitatory neurons yielded 19 components, and all neurons combined yielded 19 components. The majority of the intrinsic electrophysiological features reported here were calculated using the IPFX toolbox in Python 3.8 (https://github.com/AllenInstitute/ipfx/) with default settings, except for RMP (Rest membrane potential), afterhyperpolarization amplitude (AHP Amp, membrane potential difference between spike threshold and the lowest potential in the hyperpolarization phase), AHP latency (AHPLat, the time from spike threshold to the lowest potential of hyperpolarization phase). The passive membrane properties, including the input resistance (Rin), the membrane capacitance (Cm), and the membrane time constant (Tau = Rin × Cm), were extracted from mono-exponential fits of the voltage responses to −100 pA hyperpolarizing current pulses applied around resting membrane potential lasting for 250 ms. The sag ratio was calculated as the difference between the minimum value and the steady state divided by peak deflection during −100 pA hyperpolarization current injection. Single action potential (AP) features, including the AP threshold, AP amplitude, AP peak value, trough value, half-width, the upstroke–downstroke ratio and latency were calculated using the first spike at rheobase. The interval1 (IS1, time between first and second spike), interval2 (IS2, time between second and third spike), ISI-cv (coefficient of variation of interspike interval), average firing rate and adaptation were calculated according to neuronal firing pattern at rheobase. The depolarizing rate max (DepoR), and repolarizing rate max (RepoR), the AHP Amp, AHP latency was calculated from the spontaneous spike by custom-written Python scripts.

Immunohistochemical analysis

After electrophysiology recordings, the brain slices (300 µm-thick) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) overnight. A solvent-based tissue clearing technique-PEGASOS [102], was adopted to clear the tissue for following imaging. Firstly, the samples were decolorized with 25% Quadrol for 12 h. Next, samples were washed with the PBS solution for 15 min. Samples were then permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1.5 h and blocked by 10% donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature, followed by primary antibody (rabbit anti-HOMER3, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#HPA040999, 1:500; guinea pig anti-PVALB, Synaptic Systems, Cat#195004, 1:500) hybridization for 72 h at 4 °C. The slices were washed for another five times (15 min for each time) in Tris-buffered saline. Secondary antibody (donkey anti-rabbit 647, Cat# 711-605−152, 1:500; donkey anti- guinea pig 594, Cat# 706-585−148, 1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch, US. Alexa Fluor 488, Invitrogen) was added and the slices were incubated at room temperature for 24 h in dark. Fluorescently stained samples were then washed three times (15 min for each time) in PBST buffer. Following the immunostaining, the autofluorescence was quenched with Autofluorescence Eliminator Reagent (Millipore #2160) according to manufacture instructions. Samples were then moved to the dehydration (25% EDTP) and delipidation (30%, 50% and 70% tB) solutions to go through the passive immersion procedure. After final clearing with the BB-PEG medium (75% benzyl benzoate, 22% PEGMMA, and 3% Quadrol), samples were maintained in the clearing solution until imaging.

The clearing slice was mounted on the microscope slide (Thermo Scientific, USA) and imaging was conducted using Olympus FV3000 Multiphoton confocal laser scanning microscope. To characterize the position of biotin-labeled cells in the samples, a 20 × fluorescent image of DAPI stained tissue was captured and analyzed according to similar standards as those in electrophysiology recordings. Tiled image stacks of individual cells were acquired at 1024 × 1024 resolution in the transmission channel automated reconstruction. Tiled images were exported as single-plane TIFF files and neuronal processes and soma were manually reconstructed using Neurolucida (MBF Bioscience) [103, 104]. Reconstructions of the morphology of putative interneurons were generated for neurons with good electrophysiology responses and biotin filled. The morphology of HOMER3 neurons were generated from HOMER3 immunostained neurons.

Prior to morphological feature analysis, reconstructed neuronal morphologies were subjected to correct for shrinkage. The shrinkage ratio was around 50% according to compare the distance of the soma to the pia before and after immunohistochemistry. The relative depth to pia of upper and lower boundaries of layer 4 in each slice were calculated using ImageJ (Fiji, National Institutes of Health). For features extraction, we first rotated the pia-to-white matter axis to vertical orientation. The origin of axonal (dendritic) tree and the center of each cell body (same cell type) were assigned spatial coordinate (0, 0) by Python script. We then projected aligned morphologies onto a cortical layer template. The well-aligned neurons were skeletonized in a two-dimensional planar using the OpenCV toolbox in Python. For each cell reconstructed, 5 morphological parameters were measured namely soma surface area (the area of cell body), dendrite exiting soma (the number of dendrites originating from the cell body), dendritic node number, dendritic ending number, and dendritic field area (the area of convex hull of dendrites after z projection). To summarize the changes in dendritic complexity, we performed Sholl analysis to measure the intersections between a series of soma center circles with 10 mm radius increments and neuronal dendrites and dendritic density within the concentric rings of neighboring radii.

Electrophysiological and morphological classification

Given that the excitatory neurons displayed similar electrophysiological and morphological properties to each other, with wide APs and typically long, spiny apical dendrite, while interneurons were equipped with shorter action AP half-width concomitant with a large and fast AHP [105]. We identified putative interneurons based on the spike half-widths < 2.5 ms and AHP amp > 8 mV following by well-established characteristic features of their dendritic arbors. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm in Python with default parameter settings were used to visualize similarities in putative interneurons based on the principal morpho-electric properties. We plotted the feature vectors constructed from a set of 21 normalized electrophysiological features including 5 subthreshold features (RMP, Rin, Cm, Tau and sag ratio), 12 suprathreshold features at rheobase (AP threshold, AP amplitude, AP peak value, trough value, half-width, upstroke–downstroke ratio, latency, IS1, IS2, ISI-cv, average firing rate, and adaptation) and 4 features from spontaneous spikes (DepoR, RepoR, AHP Amp and AHP latency). The classifier of morphology of interneurons or HOMER3-positive PVALB neurons were based on 5 normalized morphological features (dendritic ending number, dendritic node number, dendritic exiting soma, dendritic field area, and soma surface area) that were calculated in manually reconstructed neurons in two-dimensional coordinates.
