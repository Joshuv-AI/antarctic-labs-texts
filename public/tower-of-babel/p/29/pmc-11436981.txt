Case study of the environmental impacts and benefits of a PBF-LB additively manufactured optimized filtrate nozzle
Authors: Pasi Puukko, Lotta Hepo-oja, Saija Vatanen, Janne Kaipainen, Marja-Leena Mäkinen, Markku Lindqvist
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this paper, we present a comprehensive case study of a laser-based powder bed fusion-manufactured optimized filtrate nozzle, focusing on environmental impacts and benefits. The scope of the study covers the raw material production, part manufacturing and use phases. We compare the results for similar components manufactured by conventional, mainly material-removing manufacturing technologies. Primary data, measured from processes, are utilized for life cycle assessment calculations, which are completed with database data when necessary. The results show that by exploiting the design freedom of additive manufacturing, it is possible to achieve positive environmental benefits, namely, a 40% reduction in the carbon footprint during the use phase, although the manufacturing phase per se requires a similar amount or even more resources than the conventional manufacturing route. In addition to environmental benefits, we present here some other potential benefits for using optimized, additively manufactured parts.

Introduction

Due to the material-adding nature of additive manufacturing (AM), it is often considered an environmentally friendly manufacturing technology, especially compared to subtractive, material-removing technologies such as machining1,2. This assumption is related especially to the material efficiency. Other potential environmental benefits might arise from reduced logistic needs or greater design freedom, making it possible to optimize the performance of the product from the point of view of environmental sustainability. Toolless manufacturing could also be less of a burden environmental. Additionally, concepts such as digital spare parts (DSP) could be environmentally friendly, but currently, there are still several obstacles that hinder the exploitation of this concept3.

On the other hand, there are opposite indications, suggesting that AM is a less environmentally friendly technology. Kellens et al.4 reviewed several articles related to various AM technologies and their life cycle inventory (LCI) data, including feedstock materials and postprocessing, when data were available. They concluded that the specific energy values for AM unit processes were typically one to two orders of magnitude greater than those for conventional machining; therefore, the greater environmental impact caused during AM manufacturing should be compensated for by functional improvements during the use stage. The authors also noted an absence of LCI data related to the production of AM feedstock materials, such as metal AM powders, which made holistic comparison difficult.

Reijonen et al. compared PBF-LB manufactured components to their similar counterparts manufactured by CNC machining based on primary LCI data from PBF-LB and CNC machining5. The manufacturing of three different components was analyzed including a manifold, gear and an impeller. They found that both the energy consumption and use of feedstock material were highly geometry dependent in both AM and machining processes. Overall, CNC machining consumed less energy than did the PBF-LB process. However, the energy consumption of the PBF-LB process was greatly reduced by increasing the layer thickness, increasing the speed of the manufacturing process. On the other hand, feedstock material consumption was lower in the PBF-LB process than in CNC machining. A review article from 2018 reached the same conclusion: in general, AM processes consume more energy per unit of output than conventional manufacturing6.

One of the clear benefits of AM processes is a part consolidation, i.e., making readymade assemblies without the need for joints, typically combining several functionalities into one part during the same build. With the broad design freedom of some AM technologies, this potentially provides a path to environmentally friendly products. In addition to possible benefits in manufacturing and assembly processes, it could also create advantages during the service life of that component or part. The effect of part consolidation on the environment was studied by Yang et al.7. They presented a case study of a floor attachment component on an underground train and used that study for sustainability evaluation. Due to the scarcity of end-of-life data and rather moderate mass reduction of the component, they focused only on the impact on the manufacturing and assembly stage. They concluded that although they could show some benefits related to the production stage, future work needs to investigate the service life and end-of-life aspects more deeply.

Burkhart and Aurich8 emphasized a lifecycle-oriented approach when examining the sustainability potential of AM, assuming that the performance of a product (e.g., efficiency) can be improved with AM. They argued that the potential to decrease the environmental impact of AM is clearly greater during the use phase than during the material acquisition, manufacturing or disposal phases. However, no clear quantified case studies to support this assumption have been presented.

As having light weight is one of the clear benefits of AM parts, it is obvious that aerospace components should also be good candidates for manufacturing from the point of view of environmental impact. Huang et al.9 showed how the light weight of various aircraft components reflects energy savings potential and emissions. These authors reported remarkable positive environmental impacts, summarizing that if AM components could be used to their full potential, airplane fuel consumption could be reduced by as much as 6.4%. However, there is also a certain scarcity of LCI data on AM applications, which increases the uncertainty of the results, especially in applications where the potential is less obvious.

Several authors have suggested that the use phase most likely plays a remarkable role in the environmental impact of additively manufactured components. However, there are very few such studies that include the use phase, and those that exist usually address this topic very generally, as accurate data related to the use phase are scarce. The literature clearly shows a research gap related to studies where the use phase is taken into account. Thus, the purpose of this article is to present a comprehensive case study of a PBF-LB-manufactured optimized filtrate nozzle, focusing on environmental impacts and benefits, covering the production of feedstock material, manufacturing and use phase of that specific component, and compare the results to those of conventional manufacturing routes. In addition, this study provides insight into the environmental impacts of component manufacturing, where part consolidation is utilized in additive manufacturing. The design phase and end-of-life evaluation are beyond the scope of this study.

The objective is also to present a concept of positive climate impacts, also known as the carbon handprint, in the context of AM. The VTT Technical Research Centre of Finland Ltd. and LUT University have developed an approach for quantifying carbon and environmental handprints based on standardized methods10. A carbon handprint refers to the beneficial climate impacts that organizations can achieve and communicate by offering products and services that reduce the carbon footprints of others. The handprint of a product or service is achieved by comparing the footprint of the baseline with that of the offered solution when used.

Materials, methods, and key assumptions

Materials and manufacturing methods

The case component, a filtrate nozzle, is an essential part of the machine used in the mining industry that increases the dry content of a slurry or similar material. The machine consists of several filter cloths and several filter plates with filtrate nozzles, which separate dry material from the liquid phase. The purpose of the case component is to collect liquid from the plate and deliver it to the output channels. The dry phase of the removable material causes some abrasive forces on the case component, which might need to be replaced during maintenance, but this varies from one application to another. Otherwise, the mechanical requirements for the component are rather modest. This component is not subjected to any significant forces or variations in the load. As a design specification, one bar overpressure inside the component was used. The manufacturing material for the component is stainless steel AISI316L, which is a very common material used both in conventional and additive manufacturing.

The conventionally manufactured (CM) assembly of the filtrate nozzle consists of four separate pieces, which are joined by welding (Fig. 1). The pieces are manufactured from metal blanks (metal pipe, round bar, and sheet) by cutting and flattening the pipe and machining the round bar with a lathe, by laser cutting and bending steel sheets and then assembling the separate pieces together with MIG/MAG welding. Manufacturing is subcontracted from two different subcontractors, and separated pieces are transported to one location for assembly. The manufacturing batch included 420 pieces of the final component.

A conventionally manufactured filtrate nozzle was attached to the filtrate plate. From left to right: (a) machined flat on laser cut sheet, (b) bent steel sheet, (c) cut and flattened pipe, (d) machined round bar: all parts were attached by welding.

An additively manufactured version of the same component (Fig. 2) is manufactured by a laser-based powder bed fusion (PBF-LB) process using an EOS M290 machine and AISI316L stainless steel as the feedstock material (i.e., the same material used for conventional manufacturing). The manufacturing batch was 12 pieces, and no assembly was needed for actual component manufacturing. For this study, manufacturing was carried out with two different standard parameter sets provided by the machine manufacturer, namely, parameters for 40 μm (precision) layer thickness and parameters for 80 μm (production) layer thickness. While an operator with normal access to machine operation system cannot modify these parameters of this specific EOS machine independently, these two thicknesses (i.e., 40 μm and 80 μm), provided by machine manufacturer, were natural and only possible choice. The use of a greater layer thickness increases the productivity but can induce some pores in the metal structure11, possibly causing some deterioration of elongation and fatigue life. The purpose was to study the effect of a process parameter, namely, layer thickness, on the carbon footprint of manufacturing. The components were heat treated at 900 °C for 30 min, after which they were detached from the building plate by a band saw. After this, shot peening is carried out as a posttreatment process. Filtrate plates (large metallic sheets) are not considered within the scope of this study.

Optimization of the AM component

Prior to manufacturing, the design of the AM component was optimized and modified to improve the flow characteristics compared to those of the original nozzle. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling was utilized to reach the minimum back pressure in the nozzle. CFD is a computer-aided modeling process that produces quantitative predictions of fluid-flow phenomena based on conservation laws. The minimum back pressure in the nozzle would enable a higher initial process feed velocity, resulting in some time and energy savings. Laboratory tests (i.e., measurements of the pressure drop in the component) validated the results obtained via CFD modeling. The resulting final geometry of the nozzle became rather difficult to manufacture by using conventional manufacturing methods, i.e., including internal curved channels, which cannot be realized conventionally. The design was finalized by engineers to ensure its manufacturability with PBF-LB, which allows the use of such features.

To calculate the use phase electricity consumption for pumping, pressure difference values from the outlet nozzle with a 1.0 kg/s flow rate were used. Other input values, namely, the filtrate stream volume (l/cycle) and total area of the filters (m²), were measured from real cases but are not presented here in detail, as those values are from a third-party customer. However, the same values were used for comparison for both the CM and AM cases. From these values, we calculated the difference in liquid volume per cycle. Finally, the energy consumption of the pump per hour was used together with the utility rate and period to calculate the use phase electricity consumption needed for pumping. One should be aware that the electricity consumption of machines using additively manufactured nozzles relies on calculations, i.e., it has not been experimentally validated with AM nozzles.

Methods for LCA

Life cycle assessment (LCA) to calculate the carbon footprint was carried out according to standard ISO 14,067, which relies on the rules and conditions for product carbon footprint calculations. The methodology is further based on the ISO 14,040 and 14,044 standards, which define the guidelines for life cycle assessment in general. In this study, LCA was used to define the carbon footprint of additive and subtractive manufacturing of filtrate nozzles. The calculations were based on primary data when available, and the Ecoinvent database v3.8 was used to fill in the gaps in the data and to provide LCI data for raw materials and energy production. The analysis was carried out using the LCA calculation software SULCA (version 5.1.17)12 and the recommended PEF 3.0 impact assessment method13.

Data collection for LCA calculations was carried out by the OEM company and AM service supplier. The OEM provided data from conventional manufacturing and service supplier provided data from additive manufacturing. With respect to conventional manufacturing, the mass of each used material blank and welding wire and the corresponding transportation distance and mode of transportation from the submanufacturer to the place of assembly were collected from subcontractors. The electricity consumption for each manufacturing process, namely, pipe cutting, tube flattening, round bar machining, laser cutting and bending of the sheet metal and the welding process, was taken from Ecoinvent and used as an input parameter. The data for feedstock material in the conventional manufacturing case were taken from the Worldstainless database for each material type, i.e., tube, round bar and sheet metal. As outputs, the mass of the components and mass of waste (i.e., pipe, bar and sheet metal scrap and cutting chips from relevant processes) were gathered. No other outputs were identified (e.g., emissions to air). The effects of the cutting fluids and machining tools were neglected. Table 2 presents the main input and output parameters.

As the target was to focus on case component manufacturing, the effect of idle time related to both subtractive and additive processes was ignored. However, if the purpose is to compare different processes in a more general way, it is relevant to keep idle time in the scope14.

For AM powder, we used values from Worldstainless for steel material production, which represent the current combined European/American/Japanese/South Korean/South African averages for greenhouse gases. For gas atomization, the energy consumption was measured directly from a pilot-scale gas atomization unit (Hermiga), corresponding to 3.5 kWh/kg of powder. Additionally, gas consumption was measured via the same process, corresponding to 1.72 kg/kg of powder. These values refer to the manufacturing of whole batches (i.e., including waste). We also conducted a literature survey to find reference values to verify the measured values, but the values we found had many uncertainties; thus, we decided to use the measured values. One could assume that the measured value could somewhat overestimate the impact of the gas atomization process compared to that at the industrial scale since upscaling from pilot to production tends to make the process more efficient.

The functional unit of this study is 420 pieces of filtrate nozzles. Although one machine requires fewer nozzles than 420 pieces, the conventional manufacturing batch was 420 pieces. If we would have divided this so that the functional unit would have been, for instance, one piece of filter, we would have falsified the results related to conventional manufacturing, which utilized the economics of scale. Again, multiplying PBF-LB batches to achieve the same functional unit as conventional manufacturing (i.e., 420 pieces) would not affect the results, as the input and output values for one batch (12 pieces) of production take into account all necessary pre- and postprocessing steps.

Key assumptions

With respect to the manufacturing of the feedstock and auxiliary materials and their environmental impacts, data from the Ecoinvent 3.8 database were used. To calculate the carbon footprint of the argon gas used in the PBF-LB process, a conversion from gaseous argon to liquid argon was needed, as the carbon footprint data were available only for liquid gas. This was done using the assumption that 1 L of liquid argon equals 825 L of gaseous argon when released from a compressed container15. For the calculations, the density of gaseous and liquid argon was also used.

With respect to the atomization yield, we assumed that 5% is an average waste of the atomization process16. With respect to the rest of the powder batch, we studied two different scenarios: (1) we assumed that 60% atomization is suitable for the PBF-LB process and that the remaining 40% is scrap material (due to the wrong particle size); (2) similarly, 60% of the powder can be used for the PBF-LB process, but the rest of the atomized powder is used in other processes (e.g., directed energy deposition, DED). In the first scenario, all impacts are allocated for 60% of usable powder, while in the second scenario, all impacts are allocated to the whole batch, i.e., 100% of usable powder, and the impacts are divided using mass shares.

To compare two different manufacturing routes, i.e., conventional and additive manufacturing, we assumed that the same number of components are manufactured by the PBF-LB process as in conventional manufacturing, i.e., repeating batches of 12 pieces until all 420 pieces are manufactured, resulting in 35 builds in total. The input values for AM production were measured from one batch. In real life, AM nozzles would likely have been manufactured more on an on-demand basis (e.g., nozzles for one machine).

As there might be some failures in AM process, which would increase the waste, we studied, what would be the outcome, if we assume that 10% of printed components cannot be used because of some issues with them.

Related to the use phase, we assumed a 20-year utilization period and 90% utilization rate for both conventionally and additively manufactured filtrate nozzles and corresponding machines based on experiences related to conventionally manufactured nozzles (average value). This has not been experimentally verified with additively manufactured nozzles. However, we do not believe that there are differences between conventionally and additively manufactured nozzles in this respect. The main stress for the component is abrasive, and while the material properties of additively manufactured 316 L stainless steel are equal to or greater than those of bulk 316 L stainless steel, this assumption is justified.

For the use phase evaluation, we studied the environmental impacts in three different, industrially relevant geographical locations: Finland (Sodankylä), Chile (Santiago), and Australia (Sydney). For each of these locations, we utilized the location-specific electricity production profile taken from the Ecoinvent database.

The results related to the variation in the AM layer thickness (40 μm vs. 80 μm), the utilization rate of the powder batch (60% vs. 100%), and the effect of use location (Finland, Chile, Australia) are presented in the sensitivity analysis.

Figure 4 shows that steel production and gas atomization are jointly responsible for more than half of the carbon footprint of PBF-LB manufacturing. Notably, the shielding gas (argon) plays a remarkable role, while many other unit processes, such as heat treatment, are almost negligible. Compared to that of the conventional manufacturing route (i.e., machining and welding), the carbon footprint of PBF-LB is greater. In the case of conventional manufacturing, the carbon footprint of steel production is quite close to that of PBF-LB, but while the powder manufacturing process is not needed, the effect of the feedstock material is generally smaller.

Figure 5 presents the carbon footprint related to the use phase of the studied components for the whole lifetime of the machine, thus including both the materials and manufacturing and use phases. First, the use phase has a more dominant effect than the manufacturing and materials phase. However, although manufacturing and material play smaller roles, their effects are not negligible, as they are responsible for approximately 10–20% of the total carbon footprint of the product. Therefore, efforts to reduce the environmental load caused by manufacturing and materials are relevant. Second, there is a clear difference in the use phase carbon footprint when comparing conventional manufacturing to additive (PBF-LB) manufacturing. During the use phase, machines based on conventionally manufactured nozzles clearly have a larger carbon footprint than their additively manufactured counterparts. This is due to a pressure drop in the optimized additively manufactured nozzle, causing lower energy consumption related to pumping.

The use phase information includes the factors directly related to the filter nozzle, the energy needed for pumping and the effect of the transportation of nozzles from manufacturing locations to use locations. However, it is not the same as the carbon footprint of the whole machine but focuses purely on the effect caused by filter nozzles. On the other hand, changing from a conventionally manufactured nozzle to an additively manufactured nozzle should not induce any additional changes in the machine.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis is presented to highlight the effects of AM process parameter layer thickness (measured data), powder manufacturing scenario (data assumption), and use location (database data). In addition, we consider how possible failure during AM process and therefore increased waste would affect on carbon footprint.

Figure 6 includes data from another AM build batch, where a greater layer thickness (80 μm) was used. The use of the 80 μm process parameter markedly reduced the carbon footprint compared to that of the 40 μm process parameter. As shown in an earlier study5, this is mainly due to the shorter production time. Thus, although the laser power might be greater for the 80 μm processing parameters, the shorter production time dominates the energy and gas consumption and carbon footprint. One could argue that the use of a thicker layer might weaken the structure of the nozzle and therefore shorten the utilization period. Although a higher layer thickness induces some additional pores in the metallic structure, we have no reason to believe that this would have a significant effect on utilization due to the modest mechanical requirements of the part. However, as there might be also some other possible failures in AM process, and therefore increased waste, lines above the bars in Fig. 6 shows what would be the effect of 10% failure in AM process (i.e., 10% of components are such that they cannot be used) for the carbon footprint. As one could assume, this clearly increases the carbon footprint of the products.

In Finland, the average electricity profile consists of mainly fossil-free energy compared to that in Chile or Australia. As explained earlier, the carbon handprint is a concept that helps organizations communicate the positive environmental impacts of their products or services. Here, the baseline solution is a conventionally manufactured nozzle which has not been optimized, and the new solution is an optimized nozzle, which has a positive environmental impact when used. This concept fits well for additive manufacturing, where positive impacts can be achieved in the use phase.

Discussion and conclusions

We have presented here a case study in which we show the environmental impacts and benefits of an additively manufactured optimized filtrate nozzle throughout its lifetime, from feedstock material processing to the use phase. The results show that by utilizing the design freedom of additive manufacturing, it is possible to achieve positive environmental benefits during the use phase through improved internal structures and therefore optimized material flow through the component. We have also expressed a novel way to highlight the positive environmental impacts by using the carbon handprint concept. One should also notice, that when examining the of the use phase, the location of use might have remarkable effect on results, especially if the use phase is electricity intensive process.

In addition to environmental benefits, one could also see other advantages for using optimized PBF-LB manufactured nozzles. The consumption of pumping electricity has a direct link to the costs shouldered by the end user, and during the lifecycle of the machine, the savings could be remarkable. The manufacturing supply chain is simplified, which could bring some lead time benefits. Additionally, the novel geometry allows automated laser welding for assembling nozzles to filter plates, which has several advantages.

Twelve pieces of nozzle can be manufactured in one batch by using PBF-LB manufacturing. This could be beneficial considering, for instance, situations where a few nozzles should be replaced during the maintenance of the machine. Nozzles can be manufactured on demand, and the need for warehousing nozzles can be reduced.

The machine operator can also have some impact on the carbon footprint of AM process, since the layer thickness in manufacturing has quite significant effect on the carbon footprint. More productive process would be beneficial also from the economical point of view. However, one should be careful when considering process parameters like the layer thickness. As stated earlier, the higher layer thickness might induce some porosity in the component structure, possibly causing some deterioration of elongation and fatigue life, and it should be clear, that this would be harmful when manufacturing components for more critical applications.

This study also reveals some remaining gaps that future studies should clarify in the field of additive manufacturing. One of the most important steps is to create accurate data on the effect of powder manufacturing (i.e., atomization including the effect of atomizing gas) and its environmental impacts. Powder manufacturers should be active in publishing these data.
