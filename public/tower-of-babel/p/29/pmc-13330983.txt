Enhanced therapeutic effect of chemotherapy with magnetic mesoporous silica nanoparticle-mediated synergistic hyperthermia therapy
Authors: Cheng-Chang Lee, Yuan-Ting Lin, Kai-Qun Huang, Jih-Shang Hwang, Hsiu-Mei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research combined chemotherapy and magnetic hyperthermia therapy in a nano-drug delivery system based on mesoporous silica. The material exhibited dual imaging ability by adding Eu3+ ions to the silica framework with an iron oxide core, including IVIS and T2 MRI. By further grafting temperature-sensitive polymers acting as gatekeepers, the chemo drug camptothecin (CPT) was caged inside the pore until the temperature increased to 42 °C due to the magnetic hyperthermia effect. Moreover, tumor cells overexpress the folic acid (FA) receptor due to the need for FA molecules during their growth. It is promising that by grafting FA onto nanoparticles, tumor cells take up more material, which increases the specificity of the nanoparticles. MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT was successfully taken up by A549 cells, as observed with confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), and exhibited great inhibition of cancer cell A549 growth. At a concentration of 25 µg mL−1 under AMF, the cell viability of A549 cells was 56.2%. This is due to the synergistic effect of chemotherapy and hyperthermia therapy.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO), nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths were reported globally in 2020.1 Despite advances in clinical oncology, chemotherapy is still one of the most widely used treatment modalities. However, conventional chemotherapeutic agents suffer from poor specificity, resulting in severe systemic side effects such as fatigue, vomiting, and gastrointestinal toxicity due to indiscriminate damage to healthy tissues.2 Therefore, improving drug delivery efficiency and tumor selectivity remains a critical challenge in cancer therapy.

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have emerged as promising approaches to address these limitations, particularly by exploiting the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for passive tumor accumulation.3 Among these various nanocarriers, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have attracted extensive attention owing to their highly ordered mesoporous structure, large specific surface area (∼1000 m2 g−1), pore volume (∼1 cm3 g−1), and high loading capacity.4 In addition, abundant surface silanol groups enable versatile chemical functionalization, making MSNs a flexible platform for multifunctional therapeutic design. Numerous studies have demonstrated stimulus-responsive drug release from MSNs, including pH-responsive systems that reduce premature drug leakage during circulation.5

More recently, combination therapy has emerged as an effective strategy to overcome the limited efficacy of monotherapies. By integrating multiple therapeutic mechanisms into a single platform, synergistic effects can be achieved, resulting in enhanced antitumor efficacy.6 Among these approaches, hyperthermia therapy has gained increasing interest due to its noninvasive nature and relatively low systemic toxicity. Localized hyperthermia (42–46 °C) can sensitize tumor cells to chemotherapy and induce apoptosis when triggered by external stimuli such as near-infrared irradiation or alternating magnetic fields (AMF).7,8 Superparamagnetic iron oxide (Fe3O4) nanoparticles are particularly attractive for AMF-induced hyperthermia because they simultaneously enable magnetic heating and imaging functions.9

However, the concurrent integration of these functionalities within a single MSN platform is intrinsically challenging rather than additive. Although PEGMA-gated MSNs, folic acid (FA)-mediated targeting, Eu-doped silica imaging, and Fe3O4-based magnetic hyperthermia have each been reported individually or in partial combinations, their integration into a single, temperature-gated, actively targeted, and quantitatively validated theranostic system remains insufficiently explored. In particular, previous studies have often focused on either passive targeting or single therapeutic enhancement, while the combined roles of active targeting, thermally regulated drug release, and AMF-enhanced chemotherapy have rarely been systematically evaluated within one platform under identical experimental conditions.

In this work, we report a multifunctional mesoporous silica nanoplatform that integrates chemotherapy, magnetic hyperthermia, fluorescence imaging, and active tumor targeting. Camptothecin (CPT) and Fe3O4 nanoparticles were co-incorporated into MSNs to enable chemo-hyperthermia synergy, while Eu3+ ions were doped into the silica framework to provide intrinsic fluorescence imaging capability. A thermoresponsive poly(ethylene glycol) methacrylate (PEGMA) gatekeeper with a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) was introduced to regulate temperature-dependent drug release, and folic acid was grafted to enhance selective uptake by folate receptor-overexpressing cancer cells. By systematically comparing cellular uptake, cytotoxicity, and AMF-triggered therapeutic effects in cancerous and normal cells, this study aims to clarify how FA-mediated targeting and thermally gated release collectively contribute to synergistic cancer therapy, thereby advancing the rational design of multifunctional MSN-based theranostic systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), sodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), sodium chloride (NaCl), and potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4) were purchased from J.T. Baker.

Hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2·4H2O), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), toluene, (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES), triethylamine, 2-bromoisobutyryl bromide, poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate, (Mn = 300), di(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate, (Mn = 180), 2,2′-bipyridyl, copper(i) bromide (CuBr), folic acid, 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), camptothecin (CPT), and thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Europium trichloride (EuCl3·6H2O) was purchased from SHOWA. Aluminum oxide (Al2O3) was purchased from Fisher Chemical. Potassium chloride (KCl) was purchased from Mallinckrodt Chemical. Trypsin, antibiotic-antimycotic (AA), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and non-essential amino acids were purchased from Gibco. Minimum essential medium (MEM) was purchased from Biowest. The mouse fibroblast cell line L929 and human alveolar adenocarcinoma cell line A549 were purchased from ATCC.

Characterization

X-ray powder diffraction was performed using a Bruker D2 Phase instrument. Particle size and zeta potential analyses were performed using dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS system). Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were analyzed using Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analysis (ASAP 2020 (Micromeritics)). The surface area and pore size distribution curves of the undoped or various-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles were determined by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the functionalized MSNs were recorded using a BRUKER TENSOR Series II Spectrometer. The luminescence excitation spectra were recorded using a Jasco FP-6300 photoluminescence spectrophotometer with an excitation wavelength of 394 nm. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) curves were obtained using a Netzsch TG 209 F3 apparatus to determine the conjugation efficiency of PEGMA and FA when the temperature was increased to 750 °C. The drug release curve of camptothecin was analyzed using an ELISA reader (BioTek Synergy Mx) at 430 nm. T2-weighted MR imaging was performed using conventional spin-echo acquisition (TR/TE = 300 ms/10.6 ms, slice thickness = 2.00 mm) using a 7 T scanner (BRUKER S300 BIOSPEC/MEDSPEC MRI, Karlsruhe, Germany). The concentrations of Eu3+ ions and the iron oxide core of the MMSN-Eu were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), using a Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP TQ, and reported as mass percentages. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the elemental composition and chemical states of MMSN-Eu by using a PHI VersaProbe 4 (Al Kα Monochromator). The AMF was generated by an induction heating machine (HD-15 kW).

Preparation of MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT

Firstly, the magnetic core-Fe3O4 nanoparticles were synthesized according to the procedure reported by Ting (2010) with slight modifications.10 Secondly, the mesoporous silica shell and Eu ion were fabricated via a modified Stöber method, following the protocol of Asefa et al. (2012).11 Briefly, 0.06 g Fe3O4 nanoparticles were dispersed in 93 mL of distilled water with 1.4 mL of 1 M NaOH and stirred at 80 °C for 2 hours. Subsequently, 0.5 g CTAB was added to the solution to form micelles. Then, 2.5 mL of TEOS and 7.5 mL of 0.05 M EuCl3·6H2O were gradually dropped into the solution and reacted for 1 hour. The mixture was isolated and washed 3 times with distilled water and ethanol by centrifugation, and the CTAB template was removed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 hours to obtain MMSN-Eu powder.12

For further surface modification, APTES was carried out as described by Song et al. (2020), introducing amino groups,13 yielding MMSN-Eu-NH2. Then, bromine-containing initiators were introduced onto the surface following Lo et al. (2021) using a reaction with 2-bromoisobutyryl bromide under nitrogen protection,14 yielding MMSN-Eu-Br. The thermosensitive gatekeeper PEGMA was grafted onto the surface via ATRP, as reported in Lo et al. (2021),14 yielding MMSN-Eu-PEGMA. Folic acid was conjugated using carbodiimide chemistry according to the method of Chen et al. (2019),15 yielding MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA.

For drug loading, camptothecin was loaded via solvent diffusion by incubating the nanoparticles in a DMSO solution of CPT, based on the protocol by Lo et al. (2021),14 yielding MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT.

Magnetic field-induced heating experiment and heat-controlled drug release experiments

To investigate the magnetic field-induced heating behavior of MMSN-Eu-PEGMA, aqueous dispersions with concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL−1 were prepared in distilled water. All experiments were conducted using a fixed sample volume of 1 mL. The samples were placed at the center of the induction coil of a commercial high-frequency induction heating system (HD series, maximum output power 15 kW) and exposed to an alternating magnetic field (AMF) operating at a frequency of 100 kHz. The temperature evolution was monitored every 120 s for a total exposure time of 40 min using a fiber-optic temperature probe.16

The heating efficiency was evaluated by estimating the specific absorption rate (SAR) using a standard calorimetric method based on the initial temperature–time slope. Due to the closed and factory-calibrated design of the commercial induction heating system, parameters such as the magnetic field amplitude and coil geometry were not directly accessible. Therefore, SAR was used as a system-dependent calorimetric parameter, and the hyperthermia condition in this study was defined based on achieving a therapeutically relevant temperature (∼42 °C).

For the heat-triggered drug release experiment, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA@CPT was compressed into tablet form and immersed in aqueous medium with an initial temperature of 28 °C. Upon AMF exposure, thermally induced gatekeeper dissociation led to drug release. Supernatant samples were collected every 5 min, and the absorbance at 430 nm was measured using an ELISA reader to determine the cumulative drug release profile.14

In vitro cell experiment

Cell culture

L929 cells were cultured in MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (AA) at 37–39 °C in an environment containing 5% CO2. A549 cells were cultured in F-12K supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% AA at 37–39 °C in an environment containing 5% CO2.

Cell viability test and treatment test

To examine the effect of the material on the viability of normal and cancer cells, L929 and A549 cells were co-incubated with different concentrations of the materials. First, cells were seeded in a 96-well culture dish at a concentration of 1 × 104 cells per well and then incubated for 24 hours. Second, the medium was exchanged with 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL−1 MMSN-Eu, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA@CPT, and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT solutions in each well, respectively, and incubated for 20 hours. Third, the medium was replaced with a new medium, and the cells were incubated for 24 hours. After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 80 µL of MTT reagent was added to each well in darkness and incubated for 4 hours. Finally, after the reaction, 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well, and the dish was shaken for 15 min to induce color. The OD570 value in the ELISA reader was then calculated into cell viability by the following formula:143

To examine the treatment effect of the materials under AMF, cells were co-incubated with the materials. First, cells were seeded in a 96-well culture dish at a concentration of 1 × 104 cells per well and then incubated for 24 hours. Second, the medium was exchanged with 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL−1 MMSN-Eu, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA@CPT, and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT solutions in each well, respectively, and incubated for 20 hours. Then, the samples were treated with a 15 kW, 100 kHz AMF until they reached 42 °C. Next, the medium was replaced with a new medium, and the cells were incubated for 24 hours. After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Then, 80 µL of MTT reagent was added to each well in darkness and incubated for 4 hours. Finally, after the reaction, 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well, and the dish was shaken for 15 min to induce color. The OD570 value in the ELISA reader was then calculated into cell viability by formula (3).

Confocal test piece preparation

We took a piece of 15 mm circular coverslip that was sterilized by a UV lamp and placed it into a 24-well dish at a concentration of 2 × 104 cells per well for 24 hours. Next, the sample was co-incubated with 500 µL of the culture medium (100 µL mL−1) for 4 hours to confirm whether the material was phagocytized by the cells. Then, the material was washed with PBS, and 300 µL formaldehyde/PBS solution was added for 10 minutes to fix the cells. After washing with PBS, Triton X100 was added to loosen the plasma membrane structure. We used DAPI to stain the sample nucleus for 5 min. We placed the cover slip on the glass slide, applied nail polish to fix it, and sealed the slide. After confirmation with a fluorescence microscope, a laser confocal microscope was used to observe the material uptake by cells.17

Combination therapy on cancer cell survival rate test

The synergistic effect of chemotherapy and magnetic hyperthermia therapy was mainly quantified by comparing the observed drug combination response with the expected combination response. The reference model used in this study was the highest single drug, and the pointed combination effect is the maximum of the single drug response at the corresponding concentration (SynergyFinder ver. 2). After determining that the combination inhibited cancer cell survival ability, values were calculated along with dose–response curves and matrices. A total of 4 drug combinations were used: MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT + AMF at doses of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg mL−1 and CPT at doses of 3.125, 6.3, 12.6, and 25.2 µg mL−1.18 A score of <−10 indicates that the interaction between the two drugs was antagonistic, a score of −10–10 indicates that it was additive, and a score of >10 indicates that it was synergistic.19

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by using Microsoft Excel 2019. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were the average value of three independent experiments. Measurements were presented as means ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set as *p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

MMSN and MMSN-Eu were synthesized by coating the Fe3O4 nanoparticle core with a mesoporous silica layer; thus, the Fe3O4 core was first synthesized. The powder XRD pattern was used to confirm the construction of MMSN and MMSN-Eu, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). The peaks of Fe3O4 (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440), which were observed at 2θ = 30°, 35.4°, 43.7°, 53.5°, 57.0°, and 62.5°, respectively, were assigned to JCPDS Card No. 11-0614.19 This indicated that Fe3O4 nanoparticles were in MMSN and MMSN-Eu, and the broad peak at 2θ = 22.5° suggests the amorphous silica layer of these materials.18 In the low-angle X-ray differential analysis diagram of MMSN and MMSN-Eu in Fig. 1(b), there is a strong (100) peak at 2θ = 2.24° and 1.96°, respectively, indicating that they both have an ordered hexagonal porous structure.20 The d100-spacing of these materials was calculated by Bragg's law to further study their porous properties, which are 3.95 and 4.5 nm, respectively.

For magnetic imaging, the material should be paramagnetic at room temperature; thus, a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) was used to examine the magnetic properties of MMSN and MMSN-Eu. The corresponding magnetic moment was determined in Fig. 4(c). The hysteresis curve passed through the origin without coercivity and residual magnetism, indicating that MMSN and MMSN-Eu were superparamagnetic with saturation magnetizations of 0.01 and 0.025 emu g−1, respectively. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, under a 7 T magnetic field (TR/TE = 300 ms, FOV = 7 cm, NEX = 1, slice thickness = 2.00 mm, and matrix = 256 × 256), MMSN-Eu with a concentration of 0–4 mg mL−1, results in darker images as the concentration increases.30 The image in Fig. 4(d) suggests that the MMSN-Eu-based material is a capable developer for T2 MRI imaging.

For hyperthermia therapy and thermal control drug release, the superparamagnetic properties of MMSN and MMSN-Eu were examined at room temperature and above, and zero-field-cooled (ZFC)/field-cooled (FC) were measured up to 350 K, as shown in Fig. 5(a). These curves exhibit a blocking process with increasing temperature. The ZFC curve presents a maximum at a temperature related to the average blocking temperature. Above this temperature, the nanoparticles have superparamagnetic behavior. The temperature at which the ZFC and FC curves merge is known as the irreversibility temperature, above which even the largest particles in the sample are superparamagnetic.31 Thus, the results showed that the blocking temperature (TB) was 50 K for both MMSN and MMSN-Eu, indicating that MMSN and MMSN-Eu are superparamagnetic at room temperature.16

The temperature must reach over 42 °C to achieve hyperthermia therapy.8,32,33 The heating ability was examined by recording the temperature variation of different concentrations of MMSN-PEGMA (0–200 µg mL−1) under AMF (15 kW, 100 kHz). As shown in Fig. 5(b), MMSN-PEGMA solutions can heat up to 42 °C after 20 minutes. SAR of Fe3O4 is approximately 6.75 × 103 (W g−1) calculated with formula (2).

The thermal control drug release ability of MMSN-PEGMA was determined, and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA@CPT with a concentration of 200 µg mL−1 was treated with AMF (15 kW, 100 kHz) for 60 minutes. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the cumulative release of CPT remained below 5% until burst release at 42 °C. This is because the gatekeeper polymer-PEGMA changes its structure at the design temperature.14

Schematic illustration of the functional design and working mechanism of MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT. The nanoplatform consists of an iron oxide (Fe3O4) magnetic core coated with a mesoporous silica shell, in which camptothecin (CPT) is loaded inside the mesopores and gated by a thermoresponsive PEGMA polymer layer. Cell experiments

MMSN-Eu-PEGMA was grafted with the cancer cell-targeting polymer FA. To examine its targeting effect, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and two-cell models were used. L929 mouse fibroblast cells represent normal cells, while A549 human alveolar adenocarcinoma cells represent FA receptor-overexpressing cancer cells. Fig. 6 shows that MMSN-Eu-PEGMA and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA were incubated with two different cell lines separately for 4 hours, and the picture shows the uptake of material (red signal). As a result, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA did not enter L929 cells, as shown in Fig. 6(a and c); on the other hand, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA was observed to overlap with the cell nucleus of A549 cells (blue signal), while MMSN-Eu-PEGMA did not, which proved the targeting ability of the FA molecule,12 as shown in Fig. 6(b and d).

To investigate the synergistic therapeutic effect of chemotherapy and magnetic hyperthermia, A549 cells were further treated with different concentrations (0–200 µg mL−1) of nanomaterials under alternating magnetic field (AMF) exposure (15 kW, 100 kHz). The temperature was elevated to 42 °C through magnetic heating, followed by MTT analysis, as shown in Fig. 7(c). Notably, CPT-free nanomaterials (MMSN-Eu, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA, and MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA) under AMF represent the “nanomaterial-only + AMF” control groups, enabling direct evaluation of the hyperthermia effect and intrinsic material biocompatibility. These groups already induced substantial cell death, confirming the effectiveness of magnetic hyperthermia alone.

Compared with CPT-free systems, CPT-loaded nanocomposites exhibited significantly lower cell viability under AMF stimulation, which is attributed to thermally triggered CPT release at 42 °C. Among all groups, MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT consistently resulted in the lowest cell viability across all tested concentrations, owing to enhanced FA-mediated cellular uptake combined with localized drug release and hyperthermia. It should be noted that free CPT is non-magnetic and is not directly affected by AMF; therefore, CPT-only treatment under AMF is physically equivalent to CPT exposure at the same bulk temperature. Accordingly, CPT-only groups without nanoparticles serve as the single-agent chemotherapy controls, while comparison between Fig. 7(b) (without AMF) and Fig. 7(c) (with AMF) isolates the contribution of magnetic hyperthermia and enables assessment of chemo-hyperthermia synergy.

Moreover, as shown in Table 4, the IC50 of CPT itself against A549 is 22.3 µg mL−1,34 but CPT combined with the targeted nano drug delivery system and hyperthermia effect (MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT + AMF) can reduce the IC50 value to 9.9 µg mL−1 (CPT concentration MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA@CPT was calculated according to TGA data). Thus, the synergistic effect of CPT and magnetic hyperthermia was calculated, and the summary synergy score was 12.87, which is above 10, proving that CPT and hyperthermia therapy have synergistic effects.35

Conclusion

In this study, Eu-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles functionalized with PEGMA and folic acid (MMSN-Eu-PEGMA-FA) were successfully developed as a multifunctional nanoplatform for targeted cancer therapy and imaging. The nanoparticles exhibited well-defined mesoporous structures, good colloidal stability, and effective surface functionalization, enabling drug loading and folate receptor-mediated targeting. Cellular studies and quantitative CLSM analysis demonstrated significantly enhanced uptake in folate receptor-overexpressing A549 cancer cells, while minimal internalization was observed in normal L929 cells. Moreover, under alternating magnetic field (AMF) stimulation, the nanocomposites showed enhanced therapeutic efficacy, indicating a synergistic effect between chemotherapeutic delivery and magnetic hyperthermia.

The novelty of this work lies in the integration of Eu-based fluorescence imaging, FA-targeted delivery, and AMF-responsive therapeutic enhancement within a single mesoporous silica nanoplatform. However, this study is limited to in vitro evaluations, and in vivo biodistribution, targeting efficiency, and long-term biosafety were not addressed. Future work will focus on in vivo validation, optimization of drug loading and release behavior under physiological conditions, and extension of this platform to other therapeutic agents and targeting ligands. Overall, this study establishes a versatile foundation for mesoporous silica-based cancer theranostic nanoplatforms.
