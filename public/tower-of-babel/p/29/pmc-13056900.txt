Explainable LSTM-AdamW based fault diagnosis of aircraft rotating components using airborne acoustic signals under dynamic operating conditions
Authors: Yıldırım Özüpak, Emrah Aslan, Ievgen Zaitsev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acoustic signal (AS) has emerged as a powerful non-contact technique for early detection of incipient faults in aircraft rotating components due to its high sensitivity to transient damage mechanisms. However, the strong non-stationarity and noise susceptibility of acoustic signals under dynamically varying operating conditions present significant challenges for reliable fault diagnosis. In this study, an explainable deep learning framework based on a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network optimized with the AdamW algorithm is proposed for fault diagnosis of aircraft-related rotating components using acoustic signals. The framework leverages sequential learning to capture the temporal evolution of acoustic signals and is systematically compared with conventional recurrent architectures, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs). Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed LSTM–AdamW model achieves superior diagnostic performance, reaching a test accuracy and macro-F1 score of 99.26% under dynamic operating conditions. The enhanced performance is attributed to the LSTM’s ability to model long-term temporal dependencies and the regularization benefits of the AdamW optimizer through decoupled weight decay. To improve transparency and physical interpretability, explainable artificial intelligence techniques based on Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) are employed. The explainability analysis reveals that classification decisions are driven by localized, physically meaningful transient acoustic patterns associated with fault-induced events. In addition, a Taylor diagram–based statistical assessment confirms strong agreement between model predictions and reference signals, indicating robust preservation of temporal signal characteristics. The results suggest that the proposed explainable LSTM–AdamW framework provides a reliable, computationally efficient, and interpretable solution for acoustic signal-based fault diagnosis in aerospace applications, with strong potential for real-time condition monitoring and predictive maintenance.

Introduction

Condition monitoring and fault diagnosis play a critical role in ensuring the safety, reliability, and efficiency of mechanical systems operating under harsh and dynamically varying conditions1. Unexpected failures in rotating machinery, engines, and energy conversion systems may lead to costly downtime, performance degradation, and severe safety risks. As a result, there has been increasing interest in advanced sensing technologies and intelligent data-driven methods capable of detecting incipient faults before catastrophic failure occurs. Among various sensing modalities, acoustic sensing using microphone-captured sound signals has emerged as a particularly effective non-contact technique for early fault detection due to its high sensitivity to transient phenomena such as crack initiation, frictional impacts, and material degradation2. Acoustic signals capture sound emissions generated by localized energy releases within a structure, enabling the identification of fault-related events at an early stage. However, the non-stationary, high-dimensional, and noise-sensitive nature of these signals poses significant challenges for traditional signal processing and threshold-based diagnostic approaches, especially under dynamic operating conditions.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques have been widely adopted to overcome these limitations3. Conventional ML methods rely on handcrafted features and shallow classifiers, which often struggle to generalize across operating conditions and fault types. Deep learning models, on the other hand, offer powerful feature learning capabilities and have demonstrated promising performance in AS-based fault diagnosis tasks. Nevertheless, several key challenges remain unresolved, including robustness to operating condition variations, computational efficiency, and lack of interpretability in deep models. A growing body of literature has explored deep learning–based approaches for fault diagnosis using AS and related sensory data4. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are commonly employed to process time–frequency representations such as spectrograms or continuous wavelet transform (CWT) scalograms, achieving high classification accuracy in controlled experimental settings5. Hybrid architectures combining CNNs with recurrent layers, such as CNN–LSTM models, have further improved performance by capturing both spatial and temporal features6. Despite these advances, several limitations persist7. First, many existing studies report performance metrics based primarily on accuracy, while neglecting class imbalance and generalization issues, which are better captured by macro-F1 scores8. Second, a large proportion of published models are evaluated under fixed-speed or laboratory conditions, with limited attention to robustness under dynamic operating regimes9. Third, high-performing models often involve deep and complex architectures, leading to increased computational cost and reduced suitability for real-time industrial deployment10. Finally, the majority of deep learning–based AS diagnostic systems operate as black boxes, providing limited insight into the physical relevance of learned features and decision mechanisms11.

RNNs, LSTM networks, offer a promising alternative due to their ability to model temporal dependencies in sequential data12. AS inherently exhibit time-dependent fault evolution patterns, making recurrent architectures well suited for this task13. However, relatively few studies have systematically compared different recurrent models for AS-based fault diagnosis while simultaneously addressing optimization strategy, interpretability, and computational efficiency. This gap motivates the development of a robust, explainable, and efficient recurrent learning framework tailored to AS characteristics.

The overall architecture of the proposed explainable fault diagnosis framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. The framework begins with airborne acoustic signal acquisition from aircraft-related rotating components operating under dynamic speed conditions. The raw signals are segmented into fixed-length temporal sequences and normalized before being fed into recurrent neural network models. A comparative evaluation is conducted among RNN, GRU, and the proposed LSTM architecture optimized with AdamW. Finally, model predictions are analyzed using SHAP and LIME to provide both global and local interpretability, ensuring that classification decisions are physically meaningful and transparent for aerospace condition monitoring applications.

Despite the rapid development of deep learning–based acoustic signal analysis for fault diagnosis, several critical limitations remain unresolved. Existing studies predominantly focus on stationary or laboratory-controlled operating conditions, where signal characteristics remain relatively stable. However, real-world aircraft rotating components operate under highly dynamic regimes involving continuous speed variations, load transitions, and transient operating phases. Under such conditions, acoustic signals become strongly non-stationary, highly noise-sensitive, and temporally complex, significantly degrading the reliability of conventional feature-based and convolution-based diagnostic models.

Furthermore, most high-performing deep learning approaches rely on time–frequency image representations and deep convolutional architectures, which introduce high computational cost and limited interpretability, thereby restricting their applicability in safety-critical and real-time aerospace environments. In addition, the majority of existing studies evaluate performance primarily using accuracy metrics, without adequately addressing class imbalance, temporal generalization, or physical interpretability of learned representations.

To address these limitations, this study proposes an explainable and lightweight recurrent deep learning framework based on an LSTM network optimized with AdamW for acoustic signal–based fault diagnosis of aircraft rotating components under dynamic operating conditions. Unlike CNN-based or hybrid image-driven models, the proposed approach directly operates on raw sequential acoustic signals, enabling effective modeling of temporal dependencies while significantly reducing architectural complexity. The integration of SHAP and LIME further enhances model transparency by linking diagnostic decisions to physically meaningful transient acoustic patterns.

Literature review

AS–based fault diagnosis has gained increasing attention due to its ability to capture early-stage damage and transient fault-related phenomena in mechanical and thermal systems. With the growing availability of high-frequency AS data, machine learning and deep learning techniques have been widely adopted to improve diagnostic accuracy and automation. Early studies mainly employed handcrafted features with conventional classifiers, which often showed limited robustness under varying operating conditions14. More recent works have focused on deep learning models, particularly convolutional and recurrent architectures, to extract discriminative spatial and temporal features from AS. Despite notable performance improvements, many existing studies place limited emphasis on robustness, computational efficiency, and interpretability, highlighting the need for explainable and reliable AS-based diagnostic frameworks.

Zheng et al., reviewed acoustic emission–based techniques for detecting internal valve leakage. They explained AS generation using acoustic analogy theory, compared signal processing and AI approaches, summarized technical challenges, and outlined future trends such as sensor fusion, lightweight deep learning, and IoT integration15. Tai et al., surveyed the use of acoustic emission–based monitoring for multi-bolted flange systems. They evaluated evidence from 145 publications, emphasized improvements brought by modern sensors and analysis techniques, and pointed out limitations related to noise effects and operator dependency while suggesting directions for future experimental studies16. Mi et al. proposed DATLTM, a deep adversarial transfer learning method for rolling bearing fault diagnosis. It uses EWT for data division, ERWD to align domain distributions via a 1-D CNN, and LogME to measure transferability, achieving superior cross-domain accuracy on CWRU and self-conducted datasets17. Leaman, reviewed the application of acoustic emission analysis for gear condition monitoring, with emphasis on wind turbine gearboxes. He discussed signal analysis methods and AS modeling limits, highlighting the lack of standardized procedures and the need for physics-based predictive models18. Yang et al., developed a bearing fault diagnosis approach exploiting the rapid attenuation behavior of acoustic emission signals. They introduced the TFC-TFECR feature method and an SVM optimized via sparrow search, achieving high classification accuracy for cylindrical roller bearings under different speeds19. Chen et al. utilized Acoustic Emission (AE) technology and time–frequency analysis to monitor friction wear in sealing pairs. The study identified a specific friction signal frequency of $270 \pm 40$ kHz and pinpointed transition speeds for lubrication states, enabling precise assessment of wear and operational integrity20.

Han et al., proposed an AS-based initial engine damage diagnosis method using multi-sensor adaptive fusion and CNNs. By weighting sensor features via entropy and learning fused representations, the approach improved fault tolerance and achieved high accuracy in early damage identification21. Hu et al., investigated combustion chamber fault diagnosis in marine diesel engines by analyzing vibration signals from multiple locations. They applied entropy-based correlation analysis to select optimal measurement points and used a 1D CNN, achieving high accuracy in early fault identification and health assessment22. Abdelati et al., addressed engine misfire detection by integrating acoustic emission and vibration data within an ANN framework. After extracting statistical features and applying feature selection with Bayesian optimization, their multi-signal approach improved robustness and achieved higher diagnostic accuracy than single-sensor methods23. Lu et al. proposed a mapping mechanism between acoustic emission (AE) signals and the wear state of mechanical seal friction pairs. Their research identified specific static wear response frequencies for M106K-WC (70 kHz) and WC-WC (90 kHz), finding that RMS amplitudes of dynamic wear signals positively correlate with total wear amounts24. Uddin and Dindar, reviewed the application of artificial intelligence in acoustic emission–based railway track monitoring. They compared standalone and hybrid AI models, discussed benefits and constraints, and highlighted future trends such as explainable AI for reliable, real-time defect detection25. Havugimana et al., reviewed artificial intelligence applications in engine management over the last 15 years. They analyzed engine performance, control, and diagnosis, highlighting strengths and limits of neural networks and fuzzy logic, and emphasized the growing role of reinforcement learning and advanced ML methods26.

Hou et al. proposed a sparsity-oriented MOMEDA method for bearing fault diagnosis using acoustic signals. By leveraging Laplace distribution characteristics to suppress noise and enhance periodic impulses, the method effectively identifies weak faults, including cage and mixed faults, even in complex environments with strong interference27. Ciaburro and Iannace, reviewed acoustic emission as a passive nondestructive technique for early damage detection in materials and structures. They examined AS-based monitoring strategies and recent machine learning methods for damage detection, localization, fracture characterization, and failure prediction under noisy conditions28. Fu et al., evaluated the use of artificial neural networks to predict efficiency and emissions of a calibrated spark-ignition engine. Using speed and load as inputs, the ANN accurately estimated fuel consumption and exhaust emissions, demonstrating potential to reduce experiments and support engine development under steady and transient conditions29. Melchiorre et al., investigated accurate onset time detection in acoustic emission signals for crack localization. They compared an AIC-based approach with an AI-driven R-CNN trained on seismic and AS data, showing improved precision in P-wave onset identification for structural monitoring30. Firmino et al., compared vibration- and sound-based intelligent systems for misfire detection in a spark-ignition engine. Using FFT features and ANN classifiers, both approaches achieved high accuracy, while acoustic analysis offered a non-contact alternative with comparable diagnostic performance31. Lee et al., proposed an AI-based acoustic emission localization method for complex composite structures. By correlating real AS with AI-modulated laser-induced waves in time–frequency domains, they enabled fast and accurate source localization and effective noise discrimination in real applications32.

Hou et al. proposed a Deep Transferable Convolutional Neural Network (DTCNN) for unsupervised cross-domain bearing fault diagnosis. The model utilizes Cooperative Domain Alignment (CDA) to match statistical distributions and partial-contractive layers to capture robust, domain-invariant features. This approach achieves high precision and noise resistance across varying industrial working conditions33. Nashed et al., developed an acoustic emission–based condition monitoring approach for gas turbines. Using envelope extraction, CWT time–frequency features, and a deep CNN, they accurately classified normal and faulty states, demonstrating AS’s potential for early fault detection beyond vibration-based methods34. Hu et al., proposed a transfer learning–based acoustic emission fault diagnosis method for diesel engine valve leakage35. Recent research focuses on robust and explainable fault diagnosis. Siddique et al.36 combined signal representations with uncertainty-aware learning, while Zaman et al.37 highlighted hybrid architectures for real-time detection. Additionally, transformer-based models have improved decision reliability in dynamic systems38. These trends toward interpretability and adaptability motivate the LSTM–AdamW approach proposed in this study.

In summary, existing AS-based fault diagnosis studies achieve high accuracy under controlled conditions; however, limitations remain in robustness, interpretability, and deployment feasibility, motivating the present study.

Dataset description

The dataset used in this study consists of vibration and acoustic signals collected from rolling bearings operating under time-varying rotational speed conditions. The data were provided by the University of Ottawa and acquired using a SpectraQuest Machinery Fault Simulator (MFS-PK5M), which enables controlled reproduction of bearing faults under laboratory conditions. The dataset consists of airborne acoustic (microphone-captured audio) signals from engine and bearing health states, publicly available as ‘Engine Acoustic Emissions’39. Bearing fault detection is commonly performed by identifying Fault Characteristic Frequencies (FCFs) in the frequency domain. Each bearing defect type is associated with a specific FCF, which is proportional to the rotational speed and determined by the bearing’s geometric parameters. Under variable-speed operation, these frequencies evolve over time, making the dataset particularly suitable for evaluating advanced signal processing and machine learning techniques. It should be noted that the signals used in this study correspond to airborne acoustic measurements captured using a microphone, rather than true acoustic signals obtained via contact piezoelectric sensors. While both modalities are acoustic in nature, airborne acoustic signals represent pressure waves propagating through air, whereas AS correspond to elastic stress waves generated within the material and require direct sensor contact. This distinction is explicitly considered in the interpretation of experimental results.

Although the number of original recordings is limited, the segmentation strategy significantly increases the effective number of training samples while preserving temporal continuity within each sequence. Importantly, dataset splitting is performed at the recording level prior to segmentation to prevent information leakage between training and testing sets. Early stopping, AdamW regularization, and validation-based model selection further mitigate overfitting risks associated with limited data availability. Dataset splitting was performed at the recording level prior to segmentation to ensure strict independence between training and testing sets and to prevent temporal leakage. The acoustic signals were segmented into fixed-length sequences using a non-overlapping windowing strategy. No overlap was applied between consecutive segments in order to preserve sample independence and avoid temporal redundancy. Since dataset splitting was performed at the recording level prior to segmentation, all segments within the training, validation, and test sets originate from mutually exclusive recordings. This strategy ensures statistical independence between samples across different subsets and prevents information leakage that could artificially inflate classification performance.

The dataset contains four 12-second mono audio recordings sampled at 44.1 kHz, acquired using a microphone positioned near the SpectraQuest MFS-PK5M simulator with ER16K deep-groove ball bearings. Faults were artificially introduced: inner race, outer race, and rolling element defects. Each recording was segmented into non-overlapping sequences of 1000 samples (~ 0.023 s duration), yielding ~ 1500 segments per recording and a total balanced dataset of approximately 6000 segments (1500 samples per class: normal, inner, outer, roller). Training/validation used two full recordings (~ 3000 segments, 80/20 split), testing used the remaining two unseen recordings. The rolling bearing model used in the experiments is an ER16K deep-groove ball bearing. The acoustic signals were sampled at 44.1 kHz. All experiments were conducted under time-varying rotational speed profiles to emulate realistic dynamic operating conditions.

Figure 2 illustrates the experimental setup and acoustic emission sensor placement employed in this study. The rolling bearing is mounted on a laboratory-scale rotating machinery test rig and operated under time-varying speed conditions to emulate realistic dynamic operating scenarios. A contact-type acoustic emission sensor is directly attached to the bearing housing to ensure high sensitivity to localized elastic wave signals generated by friction, surface defects, and incipient fault mechanisms. The sensor output is routed through a preamplifier and a data acquisition system, where the signals are digitized at a high sampling rate. The recorded AS are subsequently segmented into fixed-length temporal sequences and normalized before being fed into the recurrent deep learning models. This configuration ensures that the captured signals preserve physically meaningful transient characteristics relevant to fault diagnosis.

GRU introduces two gates: the update gate and the reset gate, which jointly determine how much past information is retained and how new input is integrated. This structure enables more stable learning of long-term dependencies commonly present in degradation processes.

Optimization strategy

To achieve stable convergence and strong generalization performance, the LSTM model is trained using the AdamW optimizer combined with cosine annealing learning rate scheduling and early stopping43. AdamW decouples weight decay from gradient-based parameter updates, thereby reducing overfitting and improving generalization in deep recurrent networks44. Training protocol

All models are trained using mini-batch gradient descent with a batch size of 32 and a maximum of 50 epochs. The categorical cross-entropy loss function is employed for multi-class classification:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\sum\:_{k=1}^{C}{y}_{i,k}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}\left({\widehat{p}}_{i,k}\right)$$\end{document}

Early stopping is applied based on validation loss with a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting. Model weights corresponding to the minimum validation loss are retained for final evaluation. All hyperparameters of the LSTM model, including the number of hidden units, learning rate, batch size, sequence length, and optimizer settings, were fixed across all experiments and operating conditions. Hyperparameter values were selected through preliminary validation experiments using a single operating condition and subsequently kept constant to ensure a fair comparison and reproducibility across different dynamic regimes.

To ensure a fair comparison, all recurrent architectures (RNN, GRU, and LSTM) were trained under identical experimental conditions, including input sequence length, batch size, learning rate schedule, early stopping criteria, and number of epochs. Hyperparameters were selected through preliminary validation experiments using the same search space for all models. The rolling bearing model used in the experiments is an ER16K deep-groove ball bearing. The acoustic signals were sampled at 44.1 kHz. All experiments were conducted under time-varying rotational speed profiles to emulate realistic dynamic operating conditions.

The gradual accumulation of positive SHAP values suggests a stable and additive decision process rather than abrupt reliance on isolated features. This finding complements the global SHAP analysis and LIME-based interpretations by confirming that, at the instance level, the proposed model integrates information across a broad temporal context while still maintaining interpretability through localized contributions.

Discussion

This section critically analyzes the obtained results by examining the proposed model’s robustness, interpretability, computational efficiency, and comparative standing against state-of-the-art methods in AS-based fault diagnosis.

Robustness under dynamic operating conditions

Robustness under varying operating conditions remains one of the most critical challenges in AS–based fault diagnosis. Many models reported in the literature achieve high accuracy under fixed-speed or laboratory-controlled environments; however, their performance often degrades when exposed to dynamic loads, speed variations, or noise contamination. For instance, CNN-based approaches relying on time–frequency representations demonstrate excellent classification accuracy in steady conditions, yet their sensitivity to distribution shifts has been frequently reported50,51. Similarly, hybrid GAN-based methods improve noise tolerance but introduce additional training instability and complexity, which may limit their applicability in real-time industrial systems52.

The proposed LSTM + AdamW model demonstrates enhanced robustness by explicitly modeling temporal dependencies inherent in AS. Unlike static feature-based CNNs, the recurrent architecture captures sequential fault evolution patterns, allowing the model to adapt to transient signal variations caused by changing operating conditions. Moreover, the AdamW optimizer contributes to robustness through effective regularization, preventing overfitting to specific operating regimes. The consistently high test accuracy and macro-F1 score obtained across different fault classes indicate that the proposed approach maintains reliable performance even when signal characteristics vary, addressing a key limitation observed in earlier AS-based diagnostic studies.

The model effectively mitigates end-of-variable (EOV) effects and order variation common in variable-speed machinery through its long-term memory mechanism53,54.

Model explainability and physical validation

Explainability is increasingly recognized as a prerequisite for deploying intelligent fault diagnosis systems in safety-critical engineering applications. Despite achieving high accuracy, many state-of-the-art deep learning models remain black-box solutions, offering limited insight into their decision-making processes51,55. CNN-based approaches often rely on post-hoc visualization methods such as Grad-CAM, which provide only coarse spatial relevance and limited physical interpretability when applied to AS.

In contrast, the proposed LSTM-based framework inherently aligns with the physical nature of AS phenomena by learning temporal fault signatures rather than static spatial patterns. The integration of LIME and SHAP analyses enables both local and global interpretability, revealing that model decisions are driven by physically meaningful transient bursts and specific time blocks associated with fault-induced acoustic events. This correspondence between highlighted signal regions and known fault mechanisms supports physical validation of the model outputs. As a result, the proposed method not only delivers superior predictive performance but also enhances trustworthiness by providing interpretable explanations consistent with domain knowledge.

Computational efficiency and state-of-the-art performance

Computational efficiency is a decisive factor for real-world deployment, particularly in online monitoring and embedded diagnostic systems. Transformer-based and deep CNN architectures reported in the literature often involve millions of parameters and require extensive training resources, limiting their scalability and real-time applicability55. While some lightweight CNN models reduce computational burden, this often comes at the expense of reduced robustness or generalization capability.

The proposed LSTM + AdamW model achieves state-of-the-art performance with a comparatively moderate computational footprint. By leveraging sequential modeling rather than deep convolutional stacks, the model converges rapidly and requires fewer parameters than many CNN- or transformer-based alternatives. The AdamW optimizer further accelerates convergence and stabilizes training, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning. The combination of high test accuracy, near-perfect macro-F1 score, and short training time demonstrates that the proposed model offers an optimal balance between performance and efficiency, outperforming existing approaches without incurring excessive computational cost.

Physical interpretation of acoustic signatures in aircraft components

The temporally localized regions identified by LIME and SHAP correspond to transient AS bursts associated with frictional impacts, micro-slip events, and incipient spall formation in aircraft rotating components. These transient emissions are known to intensify during load transitions and speed variations, explaining their elevated importance in the explainability analysis.

The explainability analyses using LIME and SHAP highlighted specific temporal regions in the AS that contribute significantly to the model’s diagnostic decisions. These high-contribution time blocks often correspond to transient AS patterns, such as bursts and impulses, which can be physically mapped to common fault mechanisms in aircraft components. By correlating these patterns with domain knowledge from ASrospace engineering, the framework bridges data-driven insights with physical understanding, enhancing its applicability in safety-critical environments. Prediction confidence and diagnostic reliability

To further enhance diagnostic reliability in safety-critical aerospace applications, prediction confidence scores were derived from the softmax outputs of the LSTM–AdamW model. For each classification, the confidence is computed as the maximum softmax probability, with thresholds applied to flag uncertain predictions (e.g., below 0.85). Low-confidence cases can trigger maintenance warnings, prompting human inspection or additional sensor data collection, thereby integrating the model into a hybrid decision-making system that prioritizes safety.

Practical deployment considerations for aircraft health monitoring

The proposed LSTM–AdamW model has ~ 152,000 parameters and a memory footprint < 50 MB, enabling deployment on resource-constrained embedded platforms. On an NVIDIA Jetson Nano (4 GB), average inference time is 45 ms per 1000-sample sequence (batch size 32), supporting near real-time monitoring at > 20 sequences/second. This is sufficient for continuous airborne acoustic monitoring during flight, where sequences can be processed with minimal latency. Integration with existing aircraft health management systems could trigger alerts when confidence falls below 0.85, prompting further sensor fusion or human inspection. The non-contact nature of airborne acoustic sensing further simplifies retrofit installation compared to contact-based vibration or true AS sensors. These characteristics indicate that the proposed framework is well suited for deployment in onboard or edge-computing–based aircraft health monitoring systems, where low latency, interpretability, and robustness under operational variability are critical requirements.

The study limitations and future suggestions

Despite its strong performance, the proposed framework has limitations: (1) validation is currently based on a laboratory dataset with four classes and limited speed profiles; real airborne data from aircraft engines may introduce additional noise, multi-source interference, and more fault severity levels. (2) The model processes single-channel airborne acoustic signals; multi-sensor fusion (e.g., with vibration or temperature) could further enhance robustness. (3) Hyperparameter tuning was performed manually via validation; automated methods (e.g., Bayesian optimization) could improve efficiency.

Future work will: (1) validate the framework on real flight-recorded airborne acoustic data across full flight envelopes (take-off, cruise, landing). (2) Extend to multi-sensor fusion (acoustic + vibration). (3) Investigate few-shot/transfer learning for adaptation to new aircraft types or unseen faults. (4) Explore edge deployment optimizations for low-power aerospace hardware.

Conclusion

This study presented an acoustic signal–based fault diagnosis framework using recurrent neural networks, with particular emphasis on a proposed LSTM model optimized via the AdamW algorithm. Comparative experiments involving RNN, GRU, and the proposed LSTM architecture demonstrated that temporal modeling plays a critical role in accurately capturing fault-related patterns in AS. The proposed LSTM + AdamW model consistently achieved superior performance, yielding a test accuracy and macro-F1 score of 99.26%, outperforming existing approaches reported in the literature. Beyond classification performance, the proposed framework addressed key practical challenges in intelligent fault diagnosis, including robustness under dynamic operating conditions, computational efficiency, and model interpretability. Explainability analyses using LIME and SHAP revealed that the model’s predictions are driven by physically meaningful transient signal segments, providing valuable insight into the underlying fault mechanisms and increasing trust in the diagnostic decisions. The Taylor diagram analysis further confirmed the strong statistical agreement between model predictions and reference data, highlighting the model’s ability to preserve temporal dynamics. Compared with state-of-the-art AS-based methods, the proposed approach offers a balanced combination of high accuracy, reduced architectural complexity, and enhanced interpretability. These characteristics make it well suited for real-time and industrial condition monitoring applications. Future work will focus on extending the framework to multi-sensor data fusion and evaluating its generalization capability under more diverse operating scenarios.

Although the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance and robustness under dynamic operating conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experimental validation is conducted on a publicly available laboratory-scale dataset, which may not fully capture the complexity of real-flight airborne acoustic environments. Second, the current study focuses on single-sensor acoustic signals; potential performance gains from multi-sensor fusion, such as vibration–acoustic or AS–signal integration, are not explored.

Future work will focus on validating the proposed framework using real aircraft flight data and extending the model to multi-sensor fusion scenarios. In addition, adaptive domain generalization and uncertainty quantification techniques will be investigated to further enhance reliability and decision confidence in safety-critical aerospace applications.

Nevertheless, it should be noted that the experimental validation is based on laboratory-scale data; future work will focus on validating the proposed framework using real-flight airborne acoustic recordings and extending the approach toward operational aircraft health monitoring systems.
