Standardization of C6/36 cells for DENV-2 virological assays: isolation, TCID₅₀ and serum neutralization
Authors: Elmo José dos Santos, Amanda da Rocha Santos, Mayanna Moreira Costa Fogaça, Naiara Soares da Silva, Milena Silva Souza, Ruth Dálety da Silva Brito, Paloma Oliveira Vidal, Samuel Santos Pereira, Jéssica Pires Farias, Alexander Birbrair, Rúbens Prince dos Santos Alves, Wilson Barros Luiz, Luís Carlos de Souza Ferreira, Jaime Henrique Amorim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed the standardization of detection, isolation, titration, replication kinetics, and virus neutralization assay protocols for DENV-2 (New Guinea C strain) with the C6/36 mosquito-derived cell line.ResultsDENV-2 was isolated, titrated by TCID₅₀, and quantified via molecular assays using serially diluted viral RNA to generate a standard curve using the C6/36 cell line. The serum neutralization assay was optimized in 96-well plates based on visual scoring of cytopathic effect (CPE). Optimized serum dilutions effectively reduced nonspecific cytotoxicity caused by residual complement, preventing misinterpretation and enabling reliable detection of serum neutralizing activity in C6/36 cells. RT-qPCR assays calibrated using a TCID₅₀-titrated DENV-2 viral stock showed high amplification efficiency (90–100%) with R² ≥ 0.98. The results demonstrate that the standardized workflow enables proof-of-principle DENV-2 neutralization testing in C6/36 cells, yielding neutralization patterns consistent with those observed in Vero cells, and supports the use of this platform as an alternative for resource-limited laboratories.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Insect cells, Dengue virus, Virus titration, Neutralization titers, Standardization

Introduction

Dengue virus (DENV) isolation, titration, and neutralization assays still rely predominantly on mammalian cell, such as Vero cells, which require CO₂ incubators, specific culture conditions, and higher operational costs, limiting their implementation in laboratories with restricted infrastructure [1, 2]. Although Aedes albopictus C6/36 cells [3] are widely used due to their high permissiveness to arboviruses, validated and integrated workflows for DENV assays, particularly for serum neutralization assays, have not been previously demonstrated.

In this study, we standardized a simple, low-cost, and feasible workflow for DENV-2 using C6/36 cells that encompasses: (i) virus isolation, (ii) infectivity quantification by TCID₅₀, (iii) RT-qPCR quantification calibrated to infectious units (Fig. 1), and (iv) a CPE-based virus neutralization test (Fig. 2) [4]. Importantly, we identify and experimentally demonstrate an underrecognized methodological artifact in virus isolation workflows—complement-driven cytotoxicity in mosquito cells that can be misinterpreted as viral CPE—and provide a standardized condition to avoid false-positive readings. The workflow offers a methodological alternative for arbovirus research, particularly relevant for resource-limited laboratories.

Methods

Experimental design, replicates, and reproducibility criteria

All experiments were performed with a minimum of three technical replicates per condition, and each assay—including viral isolation, RT-qPCR, TCID₅₀ titration, replication kinetics, and neutralization tests—was independently repeated at least three times in independent experimental runs, yielding consistent outcomes across assays. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was evaluated using predefined morphological criteria observed in C6/36 cells infected with DENV-2, including syncytium formation, cell rounding, and loss or detachment of the monolayer; in stained plates, CPE was confirmed by absence of crystal-violet–retained monolayers. When applicable, CPE interpretation was performed by two independent observers, and discordant wells were re-evaluated. RT-qPCR confirmation was required for all virus-positive samples. Statistical analyses followed the procedures detailed in the Statistical analysis section, with significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. Together, these parameters supported technical reproducibility, minimized observer-dependent variability, and supported robust interpretation of all virological assays.

Cell culture, virus stocks, and infection procedures

C6/36 and Vero CCL-81 cells were kindly provided as a gift by Dr. Carla Torres Braconi (Federal University of São Paulo, Brazil). Both cell lines were used at low to moderate passage numbers and maintained under consistent culture conditions throughout the study. Cell cultures were routinely monitored by microscopy for morphological integrity and signs of contamination throughout the experiments. C6/36 cells were maintained in L-15 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and incubated at 27 °C without CO₂. Vero CCL-81 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Cell monolayers were routinely examined by inverted microscopy. The DENV-2 New Guinea C (NGC) strain (GenBank M29095) and two RT-qPCR–confirmed DENV-2–positive clinical serum samples (codes 1609 and 1698) were stored at − 80 °C and used as viral sources. For infection assays, confluent C6/36 monolayers were washed with 1× PBS before addition of either viral stock or clinical serum. Adsorption was performed for 1 h at 27 °C with gentle rocking every 15 min. After removal of the inoculum, cultures received L-15 medium containing 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics and were incubated for up to 10 days. Cultures were monitored daily for virus-induced cytopathic effect (CPE), and CPE-positive samples were confirmed by DENV-2–specific RT-qPCR.

Optimization of serum dilution for viral isolation

Serum samples were collected from three healthy volunteers. Each serum sample was divided into two aliquots: one was heat-treated at 56 °C for 35 min in a water bath to inactivate complement activity, while the other was left untreated. Both aliquots were then subjected to a twofold serial dilution (log₂) up to a final dilution of 1:4096. The serial dilutions were prepared in intermediate 96-well culture plates, each well containing 100 µL of Leibovitz’s L-15 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic solution. Subsequently, 100 µL of each diluted serum sample was added to the wells, maintaining serial dilution across plate rows. Diluted samples were then transferred to 96-well plates seeded with C6/36 cells. Cultures were examined every 24 h for a total period of 72 h using an inverted light microscope (Kasvi, Brazil). Each dilution condition, with or without heat inactivation of the complement system, was tested in quadruplicate for each volunteer.

Nucleic acid extraction and RT-qPCR

Following infection, viral RNA was extracted from culture supernatants using the Loccus Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit, processed on the Extracta32 automated platform (Loccus, Brazil), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification and confirmation of viral presence were performed by real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using the QIAquant thermal cycler (Qiagen, Germany). For DENV-2 detection, the following primers and probe were used: D2F (5’-CAGGTTATGGCACTGTCACGAT-3’), D2R (5’-CCATCTGCAGCAACACCATCTC-3’), and D2Probe (5’-HEX-CTCTCCGAGAACAGGCCTCGACTTCAA-BHQ1-MGB-3’), with the HEX fluorophore at the 5’ end and the BHQ1 quencher at the 3’ end. RT-qPCR reactions were carried out in a final volume of 10 µL, consisting of 7.5 µL of master mix and 2.5 µL of extracted RNA. The master mix included: 2.5 µL of TaqPath™ 1-Step RT-qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 0.5 µL each of primers D2F and D2R (final concentration: 400 nM), 0.4 µL of D2Probe (final concentration: 200 nM), and 3.6 µL of nuclease-free ultrapure water [5]. In addition, the previous detection of dengue virus in serum samples, as well as confirmation of viral serotype, were conducted using VIASURE Zika, Dengue & Chikungunya Real Time PCR Detection Kit or VIASURE Dengue Serotyping Real Time PCR Detection Kit, respectively (CerTest Biotec, Spain), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Viral titration by TCID₅₀ assay

The viral titration assay was adapted from the protocol described by Hsieh et al. [6]. Briefly, 96-well plates were seeded with 1 × 10⁵ cells per well (either C6/36 or Vero CCL-81) and incubated for 24 h under standard culture conditions to allow cell adhesion. On the following day, a DENV-2 NGC inoculum was prepared and subjected to tenfold serial dilutions (from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻¹²) in culture medium—Leibovitz’s L-15 for C6/36 cells or DMEM for Vero cells—supplemented with 1% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) antibiotics. One hundred microliters of each dilution were added to quadruplicate wells containing the respective cell monolayers. After a 1-hour incubation period to allow viral adsorption, the inoculum was removed and replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium (L-15 or DMEM) supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Plates were incubated for 5 to 7 days at 27 °C for C6/36 cells or at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for Vero cells. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was monitored daily using an inverted light microscope. At the end of the incubation period, wells were visually scored for the presence or absence of CPE. To fix and stain the cell monolayers, wells were first aspirated and washed with 100 µL of PBS. Then, 100 µL of 1% formaldehyde (v/v) were added to each well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, the wells were rewashed with PBS, and 100 µL of 5% crystal violet solution were added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Plates were then rinsed, and results were recorded. The 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID₅₀) was estimated using the Reed–Muench method, based on the number of wells exhibiting CPE at each dilution point.

Viral quantification by RT-qPCR

To generate the RT-qPCR standard curve, viral RNA was extracted from the DENV-2 NGC strain, which had been previously titrated using the TCID₅₀ assay in C6/36 cells. The resulting TCID₅₀ value was used as the reference for equivalent viral quantification by RT-qPCR. The purified RNA was serially diluted in tenfold steps (10− 1 to 10⁻⁷) using nuclease-free water. Each dilution point was prepared in duplicate to ensure reproducibility. RT-qPCR reactions were performed using primers and a TaqMan probe targeting a highly conserved region of the DENV-2 genome, as previously described in subsection 4.4. Reactions were carried out on a QIAquant real-time PCR system (Qiagen, Germany). Cycle threshold (Ct) values obtained for each dilution were used to construct the standard curve. Reaction efficiency was calculated from the slope of the curve, with acceptable parameters defined as a correlation coefficient (R²) ≥ 0.98 and amplification efficiency between 90% and 100%.

Viral replication kinetics

Four culture flasks containing confluent monolayers of C6/36 or Vero cells were infected with DENV-2 NGC at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01, following the infection protocol described previously. After viral adsorption and replacement with incubation medium (Leibovitz’s L-15 or DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics), 200 µL aliquots of culture supernatant were collected at 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168, 192, 216, and 240 h post-infection. Each aliquot was immediately stored at − 80 °C for later analysis. Stored samples were subsequently processed for quantification by RT-qPCR.

Serum samples used in neutralization assays

Serum samples underwent RT-qPCR for molecular detection of arboviruses (dengue virus, zika virus, and chikungunya virus). None of the donors of samples used in this study tested positive for DENV by RT-qPCR, and none were recruited as suspected acute or convalescent dengue cases. Antigenicity and cross-reactivity against other DENV serotypes and zika virus were assessed using our previously described in-house ELISA protocol [7].

Cytopathic effect-based virus neutralization test

C6/36 or Vero CCL-81 cells were seeded into sterile 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated to allow monolayer formation. Serum samples, previously heat-inactivated to eliminate complement activity, were serially diluted in twofold increments (log₂), ranging from 1:20 to 1:2560. Each diluted serum sample was incubated with an equal volume of DENV-2 viral suspension containing 1 × 10³ TCID₅₀ at 37 °C for 1 h. After this neutralization step, the serum-virus mixtures were transferred to the designated cell culture plates for infection. Viral adsorption was performed at proper culture conditions for each cell line for 1 h, using gentle rocking every 15 min, with a final multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. Following adsorption, the inoculum was removed and replaced with culture medium supplemented with 2% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) antibiotics. Cultures were incubated for 5 days, and cell monolayers were examined daily under an inverted light microscope to assess the presence or absence of virus-induced cytopathic effect (CPE) in each well. Neutralization titers were defined as the highest serum dilution showing complete inhibition of CPE in all replicate wells (100% protection).

Statistical analyses and figure design

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Nonparametric comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and linear regression was used for RT-qPCR standard curve analysis. The p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Schematic illustrations and graphical representations of experimental procedures and results were created using BioRender (BioRender.com).

Results

DENV-2 isolation

As shown in Fig. 3A, bright-field microscopy demonstrated that untreated C6/36 cells (negative control) maintained their normal morphology, while cells exposed to serum (complement) or DENV-2 NGC exhibited similar cytopathic changes. These morphological alterations were indistinguishable from virus-induced cytopathic effects (CPE). To address this problem, tests carried out with serial dilutions of serum samples, with or without prior heat inactivation, demonstrated that cytotoxicity in heat-inactivated samples was limited to the 1:16 dilution (Fig. 3B). Conversely, in non-inactivated sera, complement-mediated toxicity persisted up to the 1:128 dilution. A significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed between the two conditions regarding the highest dilution showing cytotoxicity in C6/36 cells.

These results indicate that heat inactivation effectively suppresses complement toxicity and that serum-induced cytotoxicity beyond a 1:128 dilution is no longer detectable. Based on these findings, a working dilution of 1:200 was established for viral isolation from human serum to prevent misattributing complement effects as viral CPE. Using this optimized condition, DENV-2 was successfully isolated from two clinical samples (1609 and 1698), both previously confirmed as DENV-2–positive by RT-qPCR (data not shown). Infected C6/36 cultures displayed characteristic syncytium formation—clusters of fused and degenerated cells—similar to those observed in NGC-infected control cultures (Fig. 3C). Viral identity was confirmed via serotype-specific RT-qPCR targeting DENV-2 (Fig. 3D), with Ct values comparable between clinical isolates and the NGC control, supporting the purity of the viral isolates and ruling out interference from residual serum components.

Quantification of DENV-2 NGC by TCID₅₀ and RT-qPCR

Viral titers in this study were determined using two complementary methods: TCID₅₀ and real-time RT-PCR (RT-qPCR). Figure 4A shows a schematic of 96-well plates seeded with either C6/36 or Vero CCL-81 cells, indicating the presence (white) or absence (purple) of CPE after incubation with serial dilutions of the DENV-2 NGC strain. Based on the CPE patterns observed, viral titers were calculated as TCID₅₀/mL for both cell lines. The values, summarized in Table 1, were comparable between C6/36 and Vero CCL-81 cells, supporting the technical feasibility of TCID₅₀-based titration in the C6/36 system. Using the TCID₅₀-standardized DENV-2 NGC stock, an RT-qPCR standard curve was generated with serotype-specific primers and a TaqMan probe. The curve showed excellent linearity, with an R² of 0.9985 (Fig. 4B), and was used to quantify viral RNA loads by converting Ct values into TCID₅₀/mL equivalents.

This method was applied to study viral replication kinetics in Vero and C6/36 cells. Supernatant samples were collected from infected cultures at various time points from day 1 to day 10 post-infection. As shown in Fig. 4C, Ct values decreased sharply between 24 and 72 h post-infection in C6/36 cells, indicating a rapid increase in viral load (measured as viral RNA). From 72 h onward, Ct values stabilized around 20, suggesting a plateau in viral RNA release. In contrast, replication in Vero cells progressed more slowly, with Ct values decreasing gradually over several days. When converted to TCID₅₀/mL equivalents (Fig. 4D), the viral titers increased progressively from 24 to 96 h in C6/36 cells, reaching levels between 10⁶ and 10⁷ TCID₅₀/mL that remained stable through day 10. Vero cells, however, showed delayed amplification of viral RNA, with a marked increase only after 120–144 h, ultimately surpassing C6/36 titers at the latest time points. Together, these findings highlight distinct replication dynamics in mosquito- versus mammalian-derived cells: C6/36 cultures support rapid viral amplification and early plateauing, whereas Vero cells exhibit slower kinetics but achieve higher peak titers. This suppports the combined use of TCID₅₀ and RT-qPCR for detailed assessment of viral growth curves in different cell lines.

Standardization of serum neutralization assay

To establish a reliable and accessible neutralization assay for DENV-2, we selected four reference serum samples for standardization. Two samples had previously been classified as DENV-2-specific and monotypic based on IgG ELISA, while the other two were confirmed to be non-reactive for all DENV serotypes. These samples were tested in parallel using C6/36 and Vero CCL-81 cell lines infected with the DENV-2 NGC strain. As presented in Table 2, both DENV-2-specific sera yielded neutralizing titers of 1:40, effectively inhibiting cytopathic effect (CPE) in infected cultures of both cell types. In contrast, the ELISA-negative control samples failed to neutralize viral replication and displayed visible CPE, consistent with the absence of specific antibodies. These results demonstrate that CPE-based virus neutralization testing (CPE-VNT) can be successfully performed with C6/36 cells.

Discussion

This study directly addressed methodological gaps in the use of C6/36 cells for DENV-2 isolation, quantification, and serum neutralization by focusing on sources of misinterpretation, assay reliability, and practical applicability. A central finding was that heat-labile serum factors, consistent with complement activity in human serum, induce cytotoxic effects in C6/36 cells that are morphologically indistinguishable from virus-induced CPE. This represents a major confounder for virus isolation and titration. By experimentally defining a conservative serum working dilution (1:200), we demonstrate a simple and practical strategy to eliminate complement-driven artifacts without impairing viral detection, thereby improving specificity and interpretability of C6/36-based assays. Further validation using larger and more heterogeneous serum panels will be required to refine this operational threshold.

In parallel, our results show that TCID₅₀ titers obtained in C6/36 cells are comparable to those generated in Vero cells, supporting the use of mosquito-derived cells as a feasible and accessible platform for infectivity quantification and viral stock preparation. Anchoring RT-qPCR quantification to TCID₅₀-calibrated standards further allowed integration of genome-based and infectivity-based measurements. This approach enabled internally consistent assessment of viral replication kinetics and allowed standardized comparisons across experiments.

Importantly, we demonstrate that CPE-based virus neutralization testing can be consistently performed in C6/36 cells when complement interference is controlled, yielding serum neutralization titers consistent with those obtained in Vero cells. Together, these findings establish a streamlined, low-cost workflow that integrates virus isolation, infectivity titration, molecular quantification, and neutralization assays within a single mosquito cell platform. This workflow is particularly suited for laboratories with limited infrastructure, while maintaining methodological rigor and internal consistency across virological readouts.

Limitations

The present study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the assays were performed using a single viral serotype (DENV-2, NGC strain), and while the findings are likely applicable to other flaviviruses, additional validation across multiple DENV serotypes and arboviruses would strengthen the generalizability of the protocol. Second, although CPE-based readouts offer a cost-effective and visually interpretable endpoint, they remain inherently subjective and dependent on operator expertise; automated image-based quantification or complementary molecular confirmation could further improve assay precision. Moreover, the absence of direct comparisons with high-throughput neutralization assays such as FRNT or PRNT limits cross-platform benchmarking, and could be explored in future work. Another consideration is that both C6/36 and Vero cells present innate immune defects (including impaired interferon responses), and these distinct host restrictions may influence viral replication dynamics, limiting extrapolation of infectivity data to physiological systems. In addition, this protocol was validated exclusively using DENV-2 (New Guinea C strain), and its applicability to other dengue serotypes or to different arboviruses will require independent validation, and although inter-observer agreement was used to mitigate subjectivity in microscopy-based CPE assessment, automated or quantitative image-based approaches could further improve reproducibility in future applications. Finally, this study focused on controlled laboratory conditions, and future work should assess the robustness of the workflow using larger and more heterogeneous serum panels, including field-derived clinical samples from diverse epidemiological settings. Collectively, these limitations do not detract from the practical utility of the protocol but underscore areas for refinement and expansion in subsequent research.
