Incidence and mortality of refeeding syndrome in critically ill patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Larissa Schneider, Wagner Luis Nedel, Amanda Vilaverde Perez, Leonardo Jun Cervera Sei, Rita C Silveira, Marina Verçoza Viana, Tatiana Helena Rech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo systematically review the literature and to synthesize evidence regarding the incidence and mortality of refeeding syndrome in critically ill patients. This study was registered on the PROSPERO database. We performed a systematic review on the Medline, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases. Randomized clinical trials (RCT) and observational studies including critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients that reported the incidence and/or mortality of refeeding syndrome were eligible. Two reviewers independently screened studies , extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for cohort studies and the Cochrane Collaboration tool for RCT. The pooled effect estimates for binary and continuous variables are shown using proportions, odds ratio (OR), or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach. Twenty-eight studies including 10,412 patients were analyzed. The reported incidence of refeeding syndrome ranged from 0% to 88%, with a pooled incidence of 23% (95% CI 15–33), showing substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 99%). In sensitivity analyses stratified by age, the pooled incidence was 29% (95% CI 19–41) in adults, 5% (95% CI 3–9) in pediatric patients, and 25% (95% CI 17–35) in neonates. No consistent association between refeeding syndrome and mortality was observed in the overall population (OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.79–2.88), nor in adult or pediatric subgroups. Evidence regarding neonatal mortality was limited to a single study. Refeeding syndrome was frequently observed among critically ill patients and showed substantial variability across age groups and study definitions. In this meta-analysis, refeeding syndrome was not statistically associated with mortality; however, the certainty of evidence was low and heterogeneity was high. These findings highlight the need for standardized definitions and higher-quality studies to better clarify the clinical implications of refeeding syndrome in critically ill populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41063-8.Keywords: Refeeding syndrome, Critical illness, Pediatric intensive care unit, Neonatal, Meta-analysisSubject terms: Endocrine system and metabolic diseases, Nutrition disorders

Introduction

Refeeding syndrome is a potentially life-threatening condition that suddenly increases insulin secretion, raises glucose intake, and shifts electrolytes (mainly phosphate) from extracellular fluid into the intracellular compartment, resulting in hypophosphatemia1–3. Due to a combination of prolonged fasting, malnourishment, reduced nutrient intake, and an increased catabolic state, critically ill patients fall especially vulnerable to refeeding syndrome during the reintroduction of food1,4,5.

The incidence and mortality rates of refeeding syndrome have shown considerable variability across studies4–6. This variability can largely be attributed to the absence of a universally accepted definition of refeeding syndrome, especially regarding plasma phosphate concentration1,4,6,7. Besides, a broad spectrum of populations experience refeeding, ranging from adult7–9, pediatric10–13, and neonatal14–16 patients, both critical5,8,17 and non critical18,19, all of them showing different prognosis5,20. Nutritional support guidelines for critically ill patients recommend the systematic assessment of electrolytes to monitor the risk of refeeding syndrome. However, there remains limited knowledge regarding the real impact of refeeding on the prognosis of this patient population21.

Moreover, the literature has no consensus on the optimal treatment strategy for refeeding syndrome4,5, including the need for thiamine supplementation and phosphate monitoring. Studies suggest that the incidence of refeeding syndrome may decrease by meticulous refeeding protocols3,22, which would involve gradually reintroducing a diet, starting with low-calorie enteral or parenteral feeding and progressively increasing caloric intake while closely monitoring electrolyte levels3,17,22. However, a recent systematic review showed insufficient evidence to robustly support the efficacy of these interventions to prevent and treat refeeding syndrome in critically ill patients1.

The severe complications associated with refeeding syndrome necessitate more accurate assessments of its incidence and mortality rates. Thus, this meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the evidence on the incidence and mortality rates of refeeding syndrome in critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients.

Material and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review protocol, following the PRISMA 2020 checklist, is provided in Supplementary material 1. This study was registered on the PROSPERO database under the registration number CRD42023422769.

Search strategy

We aimed to identify all studies reporting the incidence and/or mortality of refeeding syndrome in critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients. A systematic literature search was conducted in MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to June 2023, with an update in August 2024. Eligible studies were published in English, French, Portuguese, or Spanish. The complete search strategy is presented in Supplementary Material 2.

In addition, a manual search of reference lists from eligible articles was performed.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included randomized clinical trials (RCT) and prospective or retrospective cohort studies evaluating the incidence and/or mortality of refeeding syndrome in critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients. Refeeding syndrome was defined according to the criteria adopted by the original authors (Table 1).

Case reports and case series, review articles, letters to the editor, experimental studies, and studies with insufficient data were excluded. Studies evaluating hypophosphatemia from causes unrelated to refeeding syndrome were also excluded. When duplicate publications were identified, the most comprehensive report with the longest follow-up was included.

Study selection and data collection

Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers (LS and LJCS), who screened titles and abstracts and subsequently assessed full texts for eligibility. The Rayyan online platform (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar) was used to facilitate screening. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consultation with a third reviewer (THR).

Data extraction was conducted independently using a standardized form. The following information was collected: first author, year of publication, country, study design, patient age group (adult, pediatric, or neonatal), ICU type (medical, surgical, or mixed), diagnostic criteria for refeeding syndrome, sample size, number of patients with refeeding syndrome, and outcomes of interest in the refeeding and control groups (mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, and treatment strategies).

Outcome data were extracted according to the time points reported in each primary study, typically corresponding to in-hospital follow-up or ICU stay. When multiple time points were available, data from the longest reported follow-up were preferentially extracted to ensure consistency.

When available, type of nutritional support (enteral or parenteral) and daily energy intake were also recorded. Studies were included in each quantitative synthesis only if they met predefined eligibility criteria and reported complete and clearly described outcome data. Studies with insufficient or unclear reporting were excluded from the corresponding meta-analyses.

Study quality assessment

Two reviewers (LS and LJCS) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for cohort studies23 and the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized trials24. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome domains, with scores ranging from 0 to 9; studies scoring ≥7 were considered high quality.

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool assesses sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and other potential sources of bias. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.

Corresponding authors were not contacted regarding missing outcome data. When missing data were identified, their potential impact was considered in the risk of bias assessment. Studies in which incomplete data precluded reliable synthesis were excluded from pooled analyses.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

This meta-analysis followed Cochrane Collaboration guidelines25. The primary outcomes were the incidence of refeeding syndrome and/or mortality. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.1, with the meta package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The number of events in each group was reported as absolute numbers. The characteristics of the population in each study were tabulated in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet to determine eligibility for each synthesis. These data can be provided to interested parties by direct contact with the corresponding author (THR).

Only studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria and presenting complete outcome data were included in each meta-analysis. Studies lacking sufficient information for specific outcomes were excluded from the respective analyses.

Pooled effect estimates for binary and continuous variables were expressed as proportions, odds ratios (ORs), or mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). When necessary, medians were transformed into means, and interquartile ranges were converted into standard deviations according to Hozo et al.26.

Prevalence estimates were pooled using a random-effects meta-analysis model with the DerSimonian and Laird variance estimator, selected a priori due to the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among studies, including differences in patient populations, diagnostic criteria for refeeding syndrome, and study designs.

The Cochran Q and I2 test were used to evaluate heterogeneity between studies. Substantial heterogeneity was defined as I2 >50%25,27. Heterogeneity was explored using sensitivity analysis based on age (adult, pediatric, and neonatal).

Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, and no statistical imputation methods were applied due to the variability and limited reporting of missing values across studies.

Potential publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry25,28. Egger’s test was used to assess publication bias for the outcomes of incidence and mortality. For exploratory analyses, Egger’s test was not applied due to the low number of included studies.

Results

Search and selection of studies

The initial electronic search yielded 1,143 potentially relevant articles. This study found no additional studies by its manual search. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, this review chose 42 articles for full-text review, excluding 14 of them, as only 28 articles fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and quality

Table 1 shows the main characteristics of the studies this systematic review included. Its search found one RCT (434 patients)29 and 27 cohort (9,978 patients)7,11,12,14,17,30–51, 10 prospective (2,362 patients)12,14,17,30,33,39,41,43,45,50 and 17 retrospective studies (7,616 patients)4,7,11,31,32,34–38,42,44,46–49,51, including 10,412 patients, of which 6,214 were adults; 2,508, children; and 1,586, newborns. One study failed to inform the age of its participants35. The definitions of refeeding syndrome varied largely, but 100% of definitions included phosphate levels.

Of the total 10,412 patients, 3,755 developed refeeding syndrome whereas 6,657 did not. All studies informed incidence of refeeding syndrome, but 14 showed no mortality rates (Table 1). The time to assess the development of refeeding syndrome varied widely across studies (day 1 to day 8 after the starting of nutritional therapy). Additionally, three studies failed to report such information 31,32,35.

Following the Cochrane Collaboration Tool, a high risk of bias was deemed for the only RCT in this study29 due to data incompleteness. This study deemed the overall quality of the included prospective studies as acceptable according to the Newcastle-Ottawa scale. All included studies offered complete outcome and follow-up assessment for their primary outcome data. Table 2 shows the methodological quality assessment of this study for the chosen studies.

Incidence of refeeding syndrome

The incidence of refeeding syndrome ranged from 0 to 88% across studies. Figure 2 shows the forest plot of the pooled incidence in the overall population. Egger’s test (p=0.045) and funnel plot analysis suggested potential publication bias (Supplementary Figure 1), indicating possible uncertainty in the pooled estimate. Accordingly, the certainty of evidence was considered very low (Supplemental material 4).

Forest plot for the 28 studies evaluating the incidence of refeeding syndrome in critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients.

A sensitivity analysis stratified by age (Table 3) showed an estimated incidence of 29% in adult patients, 5% in pediatric patients, and 25% in neonatal patients. However, these subgroup estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the substantial heterogeneity and limited number of studies in some subgroups.

Refeeding syndrome and mortality

Overall, 14 studies reported mortality outcomes (Table 1). In the pooled analysis, refeeding syndrome was not significantly associated with increased odds of mortality (OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.79–2.88, I2 = 86%, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3). The wide confidence interval and high heterogeneity indicate considerable uncertainty regarding this estimate. No publication bias was detected by funnel plot analysis (Supplementary Figure 2) or Egger’s test (p = 0.651). The certainty of evidence was therefore considered very low (Supplemental material 4).

Forest plot for the 14 studies evaluating the mortality of refeeding syndrome in critically ill adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients.

A sensitivity analysis stratified by age was conducted (Table 3). However, mortality data in neonatal patients could not be synthesized, as only one study14 reported this outcome. This study showed an OR of 3.97 (95% CI 2.10–7.52), which should be interpreted cautiously due to the absence of corroborating evidence.

Exploratory analysis

Eight studies including 1,387 patients evaluated ICU length of stay and showed a modest increase among patients with refeeding syndrome (MD 1.62 days, 95% CI 0.09–3.16, I2 = 61%, p = 0.01). Five studies involving 820 patients assessed the duration of mechanical ventilation, indicating a small increase (MD 1.8 days, 95% CI 0.05–3.55, I2 = 84%, p < 0.01). In contrast, hospital length of stay, evaluated in six studies with 2,351 patients, was not significantly different between groups (MD 0.63 days, 95% CI –3.25 to 4.51, I2 = 87%, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Figure 3A–C). Given the substantial heterogeneity, small effect sizes, and borderline confidence intervals, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

Several studies have examined the impact of refeeding syndrome on ICU and hospital length of stay in pediatric and neonatal populations. Blanc et al.11 and Veldscholte et al.12 reported longer ICU stays and duration of mechanical ventilation in pediatric patients with refeeding syndrome, and Veldscholte et al.12 also reported longer hospital stays. In neonatal patients, Cormack et al.14 observed increased ICU length of stay. However, these findings are based on a limited number of studies and should be interpreted cautiously.

Treatment of refeeding syndrome

Nine studies11,17,34,37,38,41,44,47,51 addressed the treatment of refeeding syndrome (Supplementary Table 1), primarily focusing on phosphate supplementation. Three studies11,38,44 also included thiamine supplementation, and one study51 reported reduced nutritional support. No study formally assessed treatment efficacy using comparative or randomized designs. Consequently, due to methodological heterogeneity and limited outcome reporting, no pooled analysis could be performed, and conclusions regarding treatment effectiveness remain uncertain.

Discussion

Refeeding syndrome is a potentially life-threatening condition. This meta-analysis, which included 28 studies, suggests that its incidence may be high among critically ill patients, despite the absence of a consistent association with increased mortality. These findings are primarily derived from observational studies and are supported by evidence with very low certainty, which limits the strength of causal inferences.

The incidence of refeeding syndrome varied widely across the included studies, ranging from 0% to 88%, reflecting substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Differences in patient populations, nutritional practices, monitoring protocols, and clinical settings likely contributed to this variability. Moreover, the absence of a standardized definition of refeeding syndrome - particularly regarding phosphate thresholds and timing of assessment - represents a major source of inconsistency. Age-stratified analyses showed higher reported incidence in adult and neonatal populations compared with pediatric patients. However, these differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect variations in diagnostic criteria, surveillance intensity, and underlying disease severity rather than true differences in biological susceptibility.

Consistent with this heterogeneity, this review observed substantial variability in incidence rates across age groups (29% in adults, 5% in pediatric patients, and 25% in neonates). This variability may be attributed to differences in case mix, disease severity, and nutritional management, as well as to the absence of a universally accepted definition. Naik et al. evaluated eight definitions of refeeding syndrome and demonstrated incidences ranging from 1.5% to 88%, depending on the criteria applied7, highlighting the major impact of diagnostic variability on reported outcomes.

Across all included studies, hypophosphatemia was a core diagnostic component, although biochemical thresholds, timing, and additional criteria varied considerably. In critically ill patients, electrolyte disturbances (particularly hypophosphatemia) are common and may arise from mechanisms unrelated to refeeding, including inflammation, renal dysfunction, circulatory failure, and metabolic stress. Consequently, reliance on biochemical abnormalities alone may lead to misclassification and overestimation of refeeding syndrome incidence. These diagnostic limitations reduce specificity and complicate interpretation of associations with clinical outcomes, contributing to heterogeneity and limiting causal inference.

In many studies, phosphate levels alone were used to define refeeding syndrome, with thresholds ranging from <1.4 mmol/L to ≤0.77 mmol/L, or based on relative declines after nutritional initiation. Considerable variability was also observed in the timing of assessment, ranging from the first 72 hours to up to 8 days. Additional criteria, including potassium and magnesium disturbances, thiamine deficiency, glycemic alterations, and fluid imbalance, were variably applied. Some studies adopted ASPEN Consensus criteria, while others incorporated neonatal-specific markers such as hypercalcemia. These methodological inconsistencies pose challenges for reproducibility and may partially explain the observed funnel plot asymmetry and potential publication bias. They may also contribute to the lack of a consistent association between refeeding syndrome and mortality, as multiorgan dysfunction and critical illness severity likely play a more dominant role in prognosis.

Despite the high reported incidence, this meta-analysis did not identify a consistent association between refeeding syndrome and mortality in adult or pediatric patients. Although associations with longer ICU stay and duration of mechanical ventilation were observed, these findings should be interpreted as observational and exploratory. They may reflect greater baseline severity, metabolic vulnerability, or clinical complexity rather than an independent effect of refeeding syndrome. Limited adjustment for key confounders, particularly disease severity and nutritional strategies, further constrains interpretation. Overall, the available evidence suggests that refeeding syndrome may function more as a marker of vulnerability than as a direct determinant of adverse outcomes.

The neonatal group was the only population in which an increased mortality risk was observed. However, this result was derived from a single study and should be interpreted with caution. Studying refeeding syndrome in the neonatal period is challenging due to variability in definitions, patient characteristics, and nutritional practices. Moreover, neonates (especially those born preterm or with intrauterine growth restriction) have a high baseline risk of adverse outcomes independent of refeeding syndrome. Hypophosphatemia remains the most common manifestation in this group and has been associated with serious clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, specific neonatal guidelines are lacking49, and current evidence supports close biochemical monitoring and early supplementation in high-risk infants52.

The absence of a clear increase in mortality complicates the assessment of the need for specific interventions. Although one randomized study reported improved survival with protocolized caloric restriction8, it was excluded from this review due to methodological differences. Among the included studies, only one evaluated caloric restriction as a treatment strategy51. Other evidence suggests that caloric restriction may carry risks in critically ill adults, particularly regarding severe malnutrition4,53. A recent systematic review54 highlighted substantial heterogeneity in nutritional monitoring, diagnostic tools, and reporting, limiting reliable evaluation of therapeutic strategies.

This meta-analysis showed modest increases in ICU stay and mechanical ventilation duration among patients with refeeding syndrome, without impacting mortality or overall length of hospital. However, effects on ICU stay and mechanical ventilation duration were small, heterogeneous, and inconsistent across studies. Some studies reported longer stays, whereas others did not observe significant differences: while studies in pediatric patients11,12 showed an association between refeeding syndrome and longer stays in the ICU and hospital, one of the studies involving neonates did not observe differences in the length of ICU stay between babies who did or did not have refeeding syndrome 14. These discrepancies may reflect variations in sample size, patient profile, and coexisting critical illness17,34,37–39,44. While hypophosphatemia may affect ventilator weaning, it is generally treatable, and prolonged ventilation is influenced by multiple factors, including malnutrition and ICU-acquired weakness58. Taken together, these findings suggest that refeeding syndrome may act primarily as a marker of disease severity rather than an isolated causal factor55.

This review has strengths. Its search in multiple languages and databases minimized the risk of excluding potential eligible articles. Additionally, the use of broad search terms led to a comprehensive initial screening. To our knowledge, this is the first review to include all critically ill populations, ranging from neonates to adults. These results offer relevant insights into the impact of refeeding syndrome across age groups and support ongoing surveillance efforts56,57.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Most included studies were observational, predominantly retrospective, which precludes causal inference and leaves the results susceptible to residual confounding. Differences in baseline disease severity, nutritional practices, metabolic status, and monitoring intensity may have influenced both the development of refeeding syndrome and clinical outcomes. In addition, sample sizes varied widely and were often small, limiting statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses, and contributing to imprecision in effect estimates.

Potential selection bias cannot be excluded, as study populations were often defined by local screening practices or clinical suspicion, and important confounders such as illness severity, comorbidities, nutritional protocols, and baseline nutritional status were not consistently reported or adjusted for. Furthermore, the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria for refeeding syndrome likely led to misclassification and substantial between-study heterogeneity, which was not fully resolved by age-based subgroup analyses.

Finally, generalizability is limited because most studies were single-center or region-specific and conducted within particular healthcare systems, with variable nutritional support strategies. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Larger prospective studies using standardized diagnostic criteria and comprehensive confounder adjustment are needed to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

Refeeding syndrome was frequently observed among critically ill patients in this systematic review and meta-analysis, with substantial variability in reported incidence across studies and age groups. Although no consistent association with mortality was identified in adult and pediatric populations, the certainty of the evidence was very low and heterogeneity was considerable. These findings derive predominantly from observational studies and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship between refeeding syndrome and clinical outcomes. Rather, refeeding syndrome may function as a marker of disease severity or metabolic vulnerability in critically ill patients.

The lack of standardized diagnostic criteria, limited control for confounding factors, and heterogeneity in study designs restrict the generalizability and interpretability of the results. This meta-analysis highlights the need for a standardized definition and management for refeeding syndrome. Further well-designed prospective studies are needed to establish standardized definitions, clarify prognostic implications, and guide evidence-based management strategies for refeeding syndrome in critically ill populations.
