Evaluation of analytical characteristics of the Vcheck C10 point-of-care chemistry analyser for feline sera
Authors: Jae-Ha Jung, Eunseo Goh, Yeji Chun, Hyerin Lee, Jiwoo Baek, Yeongbae Seo, Yoonseo Choi, Yongbaek Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of the present study was to characterise the analytical performance of the Vcheck C10 dry chemistry analyser and evaluate its comparability with a reference wet chemistry system (AU480) in feline serum samples.MethodsAnalytical precision was determined by within-run and between-run testing using pooled serum and commercial quality control materials. Agreement between analysers for 16 biochemical analytes was examined using Passing–Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analysis using 57 feline serum samples. Total observed error (TEobs) was assessed against published allowable total error (TEa) criteria to evaluate analytical quality.ResultsAcceptable precision was observed for the majority of analytes, although total bilirubin (TBIL) exhibited increased variability at lower concentration ranges. Regression analysis demonstrated strong linear associations for most analytes (r = 0.82–0.99). Bland–Altman analysis indicated that 94.4–98.0% of paired results were within the limits of agreement. Comparison of TEobs with TEa limits identified acceptable analytical performance for 10 analytes, whereas blood urea nitrogen, calcium, creatinine, TBIL and total protein exceeded allowable limits.Conclusions and relevanceThe Vcheck C10 demonstrated reliable analytical precision and favourable correlation with the reference analyser for most analytes in feline serum, supporting its utility in point-of-care testing in primary practice. Nevertheless, analyte-specific limitations in agreement with the wet chemistry reference method were identified for certain analytes, emphasising method-consistent interpretation and the use of analyser-specific reference intervals in clinical decision-making and longitudinal monitoring.Keywords: Chemistry, point-of-care testing, serum, Vcheck C10

Introduction

As companion animal ownership increases, the pet population has shifted toward a growing proportion of cats. 1 This change has increased demand for feline-specific medical care and diagnostic testing. In parallel, the veterinary diagnostic market has expanded, with increasing reliance on point-of-care testing (POCT) devices for rapid clinical decision-making. 2 POCT analysers are widely used in primary veterinary practice owing to their rapid turnaround, minimal sample volume requirements, improved accessibility and quality control. 3 However, concerns persist regarding their analytical accuracy, quality assurance and species-specific performance compared with central-laboratory-based wet chemistry analysers.4–6

The increasing availability of POCT chemistry analysers based on diverse analytical principles highlights the need for species-specific analytical validation.7,8 Most analytical performance studies have focused primarily on dogs, whereas feline-specific data remain limited.9–11 Despite interspecies physiological differences, clinical interpretation in cats often relies on dog-centred data. Therefore, further validation of POCT analysers in feline serum is needed to improve reliability and clinical interpretation.

The Vcheck C10 (Bionote) is an in-clinic dry chemistry POCT analyser designed for rapid biochemical analysis. This study aimed to evaluate the analytical performance of the Vcheck C10 in feline serum compared with a reference wet chemistry analyser.

Materials and methods

Study design

Serum samples from 71 client-owned cats were prospectively collected at the Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital of Seoul National University between August 2025 and February 2026. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (SNU-250828-3). Cats with diverse health statuses were included to encompass clinically relevant variability in analyte concentrations.

Sample preparation and measured analytes

Residual samples from routine diagnostics were included. Ten samples with obvious haemolysis and four with insufficient volume were excluded. Icteric samples (total bilirubin ⩽8 mg/dl) were retained to ensure a broad range. In total, 57 feline serum samples were evaluated. Approximately 1 ml of blood per cat yielded at least 500 μl of serum, sufficient for both analysers. A total of 16 biochemistry analytes were evaluated: albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), amylase (AMY), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium (CA), cholesterol (CHOL), creatinine (CREA), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), glucose (GLU), lipase (LIPA), inorganic phosphorus (PHOS), total bilirubin (TBIL), total protein (TP) and triglyceride (TRIG).

Precision assessment

The precision of the Vcheck C10 analyser was determined using pooled feline serum and level 2 quality control material (QCM) at an intermediate concentration level (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Within-run precision was assessed by nine consecutive measurements of pooled serum from 18 cats within a single run following a single freeze–thaw cycle. Between-run precision was evaluated using pooled serum from 12 cats, aliquoted and stored at −20°C, with one aliquot analysed daily for 5 days. The same protocol was applied to the QCM. Between-run precision for the reference wet chemistry analyser (AU480; Beckman Coulter) was assessed using pooled feline serum under the same protocol (see Table S3 in the supplementary material), with manufacturer-provided data shown in Table S4 in the supplementary material.

Method comparison

Paired fresh serum samples from individual cats were analysed using both the Vcheck C10 and AU480 analysers. On the Vcheck C10 analyser, AST and TRIG were measured using dual-analyte cartridges, and the remaining analytes were measured using the ‘Comprehensive 17’ cartridge (see Table S1 in the supplementary material). All cartridges were refrigerated and equilibrated to room temperature for 30 mins before analysis. Correlation and agreement were evaluated. Interference-flagged individual analyte results (all haemolysis level 1) and below the limit of detection were excluded. Details of the analytical methods for each analyser are described in Table S2 in the supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

For precision analysis, mean, standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated. CV was calculated as follows: CV = (SD/mean) × 100. Total observed error (TEobs) was calculated as |%bias| + 2 × CV, using between-run CV values from QCM and percentage bias calculated as follows: %bias = (manufacturer mean − measured mean)/manufacturer mean × 100.12,13 Method comparison was performed using Passing–Bablok regression with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), correlation analysis and Bland–Altman plots to assess agreement, including mean bias and limits of agreement (LOA) with corresponding CI. Combined inherent imprecision (CVcombined) was calculated as the square root of the sum of squared between-run CVs from the AU480 (CVref) and Vcheck C10 (CVnew), obtained from pooled feline serum. 14 Acceptance limits were defined as: 0 ± 1.96 × CVcombined × mean of the two methods.

Results

Within-run and between-run precision of the Vcheck C10 analyser

Within-run and between-run CVs are summarised in Table 1. In pooled serum samples, CVs were below 10% for most analytes, except for TBIL (65.08% in within-run) and ALP (10.48% in between-run). Because CV is inflated at low mean concentrations, TBIL was additionally evaluated using icteric plasma (mean 5.7 mg/dl; CV 2.93%) (described in text only). For GGT, pooled serum concentrations were too low to be quantified, yielding zero values and making CV calculation not possible. In contrast, QCM CVs remained within 10% for all analytes in both within- and between-run assessments.

Correlation and regression analysis between Vcheck C10 and AU480

Passing–Bablok regression analysis demonstrated generally strong analytical concordance between analysers for most analytes (Table 2, Figure 1). GGT and most LIPA results were below the limit of detection, precluding regression analysis. For TBIL, most results were excluded because of haemolysis-related interference flags despite no gross haemolysis. Correlation coefficients (r) for 15 analytes were in the range of 0.82–0.99, showing strong linear associations. The 95% CI for the intercept included zero for BUN, CHOL, CREA, LIPA, TBIL and TP, indicating minimal constant error. Slopes were near unity, with 95% CI including 1.0 for ALB, ALT, AST, CA, CREA, LIPA, TBIL and TP, indicating no clear proportional bias. However, ALP, AMY, BUN, CREA, GLU, PHOS and TRIG showed proportional bias. In contrast, Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated significant proportional bias for most analytes, except for AMY and TP (Table 3).

Agreement analysis between Vcheck C10 and AU480

Bland–Altman analysis indicated that the Vcheck C10 was generally comparable to the AU480 across most analytes (Table 4, Figure 2). For seven analytes, including ALB, BUN, CA, CREA, PHOS, TBIL and TP, the average differences between methods were minimal, with mean bias values remaining close to zero, indicating minimal systematic deviation. Most measurements fell within the LOA (94.4–98.0%), consistent with the expected distribution. However, ALT, AMY and TRIG displayed larger mean differences, indicating increased variability between the two analytical systems for these analytes. Precision-based assessment showed that only a subset of analytes fell within the expected range (Table 5). GLU, CHOL, TBIL and TP had higher proportions (>30%), whereas ALB, AST, CA, LIPA and TRIG showed poor agreement (<30%).

Assessment of TEobs in relation to published TEa

Comparison of TEobs with published allowable total error (TEa) limits revealed acceptable performance for nine analytes, including ALB, ALP, ALT, AMY, AST, GGT, GLU and TRIG, under both the American Society for Veterinary Clinical Pathology (ASVCP) and the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) criteria, whereas CHOL met only the ASVCP requirement (Table 6). TEobs exceeded TEa for BUN, CREA and TP, and was marginally above the allowable limits for CA and TBIL.

Discussion

In this study, the analytical performance of the Vcheck C10 dry chemistry analyser was assessed in feline serum, with agreement assessed against a reference wet chemistry system (AU480). Overall, the Vcheck C10 demonstrated satisfactory repeatability, with within-run and between-run CVs generally remaining below 10%. Higher imprecision was observed for TBIL and ALP, consistent with previous reports.11,15 For TBIL, elevated CVs in pooled serum were likely influenced by very low mean concentrations. When SD is similar across concentrations, dividing by a smaller mean inflates the CV, so higher CVs are expected at low analyte concentrations. Within-run precision assessed using icteric plasma showed lower CV values, and QCM level 2 also demonstrated lower CV at higher concentrations, indicating that the apparent imprecision was concentration-dependent rather than reflective of true analytical instability. ALP exhibited slightly higher between-run imprecision. Although both analysers use a p-nitrophenylphosphate-based reaction, ALP reflects enzyme activity rather than concentration. Consequently, subtle differences in instrument-specific conditions, such as pH, temperature and reaction kinetics, may contribute to variability despite a similar assay principle.15,16 Although ALP is considered stable at −20°C, storage and assay conditions may still affect enzymatic activity. 17 Between-run CVs for QCM were below 10% for all analytes; however, imprecision was moderate, exceeding 5% for ALP, ALT, AMY, BUN and PHOS, and approaching or exceeding 9% for CREA and TBIL. This contributed to increased TEobs, which exceeded the ASVCP TEa for BUN and CREA, with marginal exceedance for TBIL and PHOS.

Regression and agreement analyses demonstrated generally good concordance between the Vcheck C10 and AU480 analysers; however, several analytes exhibited LOA with potential clinical implications. ALT and CREA may affect clinical classification, including liver enzyme interpretation and International Renal Interest Society staging of chronic kidney disease. For CREA, despite exceeding percentage-based TEa, absolute bias remained within the CLIA limit (±0.3 mg/dl), indicating minimal clinical impact. 18 AMY showed wide LOA but limited relevance, while TRIG and GLU showed moderate discrepancies; GLU differences may be smaller than stress-induced variability. AMY and TRIG showed notable inter-instrument differences, findings that have also been reported in previous studies, although one of the two studies used plasma samples.15,19 Evaluation based on combined inherent imprecision reflects analytical performance rather than clinical acceptability. 20 In this study, between-run CVs for the AU480 and Vcheck C10 were derived from different pooled feline serum samples, which may have contributed to differences in acceptance limits. Therefore, although precision-based limits provide insight into analytical variability, clinical interpretation should primarily rely on TEa-based criteria.

These results underscore the importance of analyser-specific reference intervals (RIs) for accurate clinical interpretation. TEobs further revealed that BUN, CREA and TP most notably exceeded TEa, consistent with a prior report. 15 For BUN, elevated TEobs reflected the combined influence of moderate imprecision and systematic bias rather than a single dominant error source. For CREA, imprecision appeared to be the predominant factor, likely influenced by its relatively low concentration range.21,22 Conversely, increased TEobs for TP was primarily attributable to systematic bias, which was also described in ASVCP guidelines. 13 The relatively stringent TEa for TP and consistent identification of bias as the main contributor in prior studies highlight the importance of calibration and method bias control. 23 These findings highlight cautious interpretation of these analytes and the use of analyser-specific RIs or consistent analyser use in clinical monitoring.

Differences in analytical methodologies may have contributed to inter-instrument variability. ALT and AST were measured using International Federation of Clinical Chemistry ultraviolet-based methods in the AU480, whereas the Vcheck C10 employs enzymatic colorimetric methods. 23 CHOL and GLU were measured using different reaction principles with distinct interference profiles, and CREA was measured by the Jaffe method in the AU480 vs an enzymatic method in the Vcheck C10, potentially affecting specificity.24–27 Differences in susceptibility to endogenous interference may also explain discrepancies for PHOS and TBIL. 26

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cases was limited, as simultaneous analysis on both analysers required larger sample volumes, restricting inclusion to serum samples, whereas plasma is more commonly used in feline clinical practice. Second, duplicate measurements were not feasible because of the limited sample volume in the method comparison study. 14 Third, the effective sample size was reduced for GGT, LIPA and TBIL, leading to low concentration clustering and non-determinable results. For TBIL, reanalysis including haemolysis-flagged samples (n = 43) yielded a % bias of 26.1%, within the ASVCP TEa, suggesting clinical acceptability of mild haemolysis. Fourth, limited replicates may also have resulted in wider CI for CV estimates. 12 Future studies should better reflect clinical practice by incorporating plasma samples, expanding the range of analyte concentrations, and increasing the number of replicates to improve the robustness of agreement and precision assessments.

Conclusions

The Vcheck C10 dry chemistry analyser demonstrated good analytical precision and correlation with a reference wet chemistry system in feline serum. Nevertheless, method-dependent differences were observed for selected analytes, highlighting the need for analyser-specific RIs and consistent analyser use in longitudinal monitoring. When these considerations are applied, the Vcheck C10 represents a useful in-clinic option for feline biochemical analysis.
