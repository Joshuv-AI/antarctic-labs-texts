Disappearance of Electro-Freezing in Salt Solutions Beyond a Critical Ion Concentration: Salt-Type Independence
Authors: Zhenghao Guo, Lintao Kuang, Junhao Li, Zhi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Static electric fields have been widely proposed to control freezing temperatures, but previous studies have been focused on pure water, leaving the underlying mechanisms incompletely understood. Here, we systematically investigate electrofreezing in salt solutions across varying concentrations and ion types. Our experiments demonstrate that above a critical ionic concentration, the field’s nucleation-promoting effect is significantly weakened or even eliminated. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that strongly hydrated ions disrupt local electric fields, interfering with water molecule alignment induced by the external field and preventing the formation of ordered interfacial structures necessary for ice nucleation. At high concentrations, a salt-enriched interfacial layer further suppresses ice crystal growth, ultimately nullifying the electrofreezing effect. These findings provide new insights into ice nucleation mechanisms under complex ionic conditions, with important implications for applications requiring precise control over freezing in saline environments.

Introduction

Effective control over nucleation behavior is crucial for a wide range of practical applications including cryopreservation of food, organs, and cells, − as well as deicing in aerospace and cold-chain transportation of pharmaceuticals. − Among the various strategies for regulating nucleation, electrofreezing has emerged as a highly efficient physical method. This approach involves applying an external electric field to orient water molecules, thereby enhancing the probability of nucleation and increasing the freezing point. − The concept of electrofreezing dates back to 1861, with Dufour’s early reports. Since then, researchers have consistently observed electric-field-induced increases in water’s freezing point or nucleation rate, attributing these phenomena to mechanisms such as liquid filament formation, the presence of an adsorbed layer on solid surfaces, and interactions within local dipole fields. , Despite significant progress, the fundamental mechanism of electrofreezing remains incompletely understood and highly debated, particularly in complex aqueous environments where many nucleation behaviors remain unresolved.

Recent studies have highlighted the significant and unexpected influence of ionic species on electrofreezing. These findings underscore potential synergistic interactions between ions and applied electric fields. For instance, Lubomirsky et al. demonstrated that multivalent cations (e.g., Al3+, Mg2+), with their high charge density and strong polarizing capabilities, can stabilize ice-like hexagonal arrangements at water interfaces, effectively lowering nucleation barriers. In contrast, Na+ ions exhibited a much weaker effect. Further evidence of this complexity comes from Wang et al., who showed that a vertical upward electric field (5000 V/cm) on a mica substrate could raise the freezing temperature of Na+-containing droplet from −21 °C to −15 °C. Notably, this enhancement was not observed with other monovalent cations (e.g., Li+, K+, Rb+, Cs+) or when the field orientation was reversed. While these studies provide valuable insights into field–ion–water interactions, a unified theoretical framework remains elusive. Meanwhile, high-salinity environments are commonly encountered in practical scenarios such as seawater freezing, antifreeze formulation, cryopreservation of biological tissues, and crystallization of saline wastewater. − Therefore, elucidating electrofreezing behavior in highly concentrated saline solutions is not only essential for refining our theoretical understanding of field-regulated nucleation but also offers new opportunities for precise control of phase transitions in complex aqueous systems.

In this study, we systematically investigated electrofreezing behavior in salt solutions with various concentrations. Our experimental results demonstrate that the electrofreezing takes effect in low-concentration electrolyte solutions, however, it gradually diminishes and eventually vanishes beyond a critical ionic concentration. Notably, this concentration-dependent neutralization of electrofreezing was consistently observed across all tested salt types. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that strongly hydrated ions induce local disturbances in the electric field, progressively undermining the field-driven alignment and ordering of interfacial water molecules that are essential for ice nucleation. These findings provide new insights into ice nucleation mechanisms under complex ionic conditions.

Results and Discussion

To systematically investigate the influence of externally applied electric fields on the ice nucleation behavior of water droplets, while minimizing interference from electrical currents and electrochemical side reactions, we developed an improved electrofreezing experimental platform. In this setup, droplets of fixed volume (∼8 μL) were precisely dispensed onto a copper substrate coated with a 50 μm polyimide (PI) insulating layer using a precision microsyringe. The copper substrate serves a dual purpose: it acts as both an efficient cooling surface to ensure a stable temperature decrease and as the bottom electrode. A tungsten microneedle was employed as the top electrode, making direct contact with the droplet apex and establishing a stable vertical electric field across the droplet (Figure a). The PI film, with a high resistivity of ∼1016 Ω·cm, provides excellent insulation, effectively suppressing charge flow within the droplet and ensuring that the electrofreezing process occurs under zero net current conditions. This design eliminates parasitic effects such as electrolysis and Joule heating, allowing the observed ice nucleation behavior to be attributed directly to the field-induced orientation and structural rearrangement of interfacial water molecules (Figure b). The applied voltage ranges from 0 to 1800 V, corresponding to a maximum electric field strength, E, of approximately 3.6 × 107 V/m (calculated as E = U/d, where U is the applied voltage, d = 50 μm is the thickness of the dielectric layer).

During each experiment, the sample was cooled at a constant rate of 6 °C/min from room temperature until ice nucleation was detected. The nucleation event was identified in real time using a high-speed camera, recorded as a sudden transition of the droplet from transparent to opaque (Figure c). Since crystal growth occurs much faster than nucleation, the time at which macroscopic ice crystals become visibly detectable can be reliably regarded as the nucleation time without introducing significant temporal error. , Given the stochastic nature of nucleation and the influence of multiple variables, each condition was tested in at least 30 independent replicates to obtain statistically meaningful distributions of nucleation temperature (Figure d). This experimental platform ensures high stability and spatial uniformity of the applied electric field, providing a robust foundation for further studies on the synergistic effects of ionic species and concentration on electrofreezing behavior. We performed a control experiment by applying and subsequently removing the electric field. The results revealed minimal variation in contact angle across salt concentrations, with a slight reduction observed under the electric field (Figure S1). This experiment ruled out the effects of contact angle and deformation on the results. Throughout all experiments, the sample was cooled at a linear rate of 6 °C/min until the onset of ice nucleation was observed. This cooling rate was selected to ensure experimental stability while minimizing potential errors from rapid thermal gradients or supercooling hysteresis (Figure S2). Ice nucleation was identified by the sudden optical transition of the droplet from transparent to opaque, indicating the macroscopic growth of an ice crystal (Figure c).

Figure a illustrates the relationship between nucleation temperature and applied electric field strength for pure water and NaCl solutions of varying concentrations. Below a critical electric field threshold of approximately 1.8 × 107 V/m, no electrofreezing is observed. However, when the field strength exceeds this threshold, a significant increase in nucleation temperature occurs for salt solutions with concentrations below 3.5 mol/L. The nucleation temperature can be elevated up to 10 °C when the electric field strength is 3.6 × 107 V/m, which is in good agreement with previous reports in literature. ,,, To eliminate potential artifacts from electrode polarization or material-specific effects, we performed electrode-swapping experiments. As shown in Figure b, the results demonstrate remarkable consistency between the two configurations, confirming that (1) Na+ and Cl– ions affect water freezing behavior similarly, and (2) the electric field’s polarity has negligible influence on the nucleation characteristics of NaCl solutions.

A striking concentration-dependent threshold behavior emerges as NaCl concentration increases. When the ionic concentration reaches approximately 3.5 mol/L, the field-induced modulation of nucleation temperature is completely suppressed. Beyond this critical concentration, the nucleation temperature remains unchanged across all tested electric field strengths. These findings provide compelling evidence for a critical ionic concentration threshold (∼3.5 mol/L) in NaCl aqueous systems, beyond which external electric fields lose their ability to influence ice nucleation behavior. This threshold likely originates from two competing effects at high salinities: (1) strong ionic hydration that limits water molecule mobility, and (2) significant electrostatic screening that reduces the electric field’s penetration depth. Together, these factors inhibit the field-driven alignment of water dipoles, thereby preventing the formation of structured nucleation sites. These findings were corroborated in our simulations, where higher salt concentrations reduced water molecule mobility (Figure S3), and ions accumulated at the surface under the electric field (Figure S4). To better understand the impact of salt ions on the electric field, we also computed the field distribution within the simulation system (Figure S5).

While our initial experiments identified a critical concentration threshold beyond which electric fields lose their ability to modulate ice nucleation, this phenomenon likely arises from multiple contributing factors, including ion species, ionic strength, and total ion concentration. To systematically investigate these effects, we conducted a comprehensive series of experiments using salts with varying valencies and molecular structures. Specifically, we examined three distinct categories of salts: AB-type (NaCl, LiCl, KCl, NaI), AB2-type (CaCl2, MgCl2), and AB3-type (FeCl3, AlCl3) (Figure S6).

All experiments were conducted under a standardized electric field strength of 3.6 × 107 V/m. As shown in Figure c, salts within the same structural category exhibit remarkably consistent concentration-dependent trends in nucleation temperature enhancement (ΔT). For example, all AB-type salts exhibit decreasing ΔT with increasing concentration. When concentrations reach approximately 3.5 mol/L, the electrofreezing effect ceases. Similarly, AB2-type salts (CaCl2, MgCl2) lose their electrofreezing effect at around 2 mol/L, while AB3-type salts (FeCl3, AlCl3) exhibit complete suppression at approximately 1.5 mol/L. The consistent patterns observed within each salt category provide compelling evidence that the molecular characteristics of the ionic species, rather than simply their concentration, dictate the effectiveness of electric field-induced nucleation enhancement.

To identify the specific physicochemical property controlling the disappearance of electrofreezing effects in concentrated salt solutions, we systematically analyzed the molecular concentration, ionic concentration, and ionic strength at which this suppression occurs across different salt types. The ionic strength was calculated using the established formula I=12∑icizi2 where c i is the molar concentration of ion i and z i is its valence. As shown in Figure d, despite variations in salt chemistry, the ionic concentrations at which electrofreezing ceases cluster tightly between 6 and 7 mol/L, suggesting a universal threshold. In contrast, both ionic strength and molecular concentration show significant variation across different salts. These findings indicate that ionic concentration serves as the primary determinant governing electric field effectiveness in high-salinity environments. This insight enables predictive assessment of electrofreezing behavior for various salt systems, though minor variations persist due to intrinsic ionic properties.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this concentration-dependent suppression, we conducted MD simulations under experimentally relevant conditions. We chose to use polyethylene (PE) in our simulations as a simplified model because it reduces the system’s complexity and allows us to focus on the fundamental interactions between water, ions, and the material surface. PE’s low polarity minimizes the formation of structured hydration layers, enabling a clearer observation of the basic behavior of water molecules and ions at the interface (Figure S7a–c). − Our simulation system consisted of a water slab with randomly distributed Na+/Cl– ion pairs above the PE surface, with ion numbers adjusted to achieve target concentrations (Table S1).

To systematically investigate how salt ions regulate the system’s microscopic structure, we classified water molecules into three distinct categories based on their interaction with ions: bound water, hydration water and free water. Specifically, water molecules located in the first solvation shell directly interacting with ions through strong electrostatic interactions are defined as bound water. Those in the second solvation shell hydrogen-bonded to bound water, indirectly affected by ions are referred to as hydration water. In contrast, water molecules located far from the ions and experiencing negligible electrostatic perturbation are categorized as free water. − Notably, bound water forms stable local structures primarily through ion–dipole interactions, while hydration water serves as a dynamic transition zone that maintains hydrogen-bond continuity while propagating ionic effects. Li et al.’s MD simulations demonstrated that salt ions’ local electric fields significantly distort the hydrogen-bond network in the first solvation shell, with perturbations extending into the second shell to create a spatially decaying ordered region. − For analytical consistency, we collectively refer to both bound and hydration water as bound water in subsequent analyses. The boundary between these categories was quantitatively determined based on the peak positions of the radial distribution function (RDF), as summarized in Table S2 and Figure S8. ,

As the salt concentration increases, the number of bound water molecules shows a significant rise. In the high-concentration 3.5 mol/L NaCl solution, although nonbound water still dominates the interfacial region (Figure f), its spatial distribution becomes markedly disordered. Moreover, within a certain distance from the interface, the number of free water molecules approaches zero, reflecting a severe depletion of unbound water. This, combined with our analysis of sodium ion distribution, further substantiates this observation (Figure S4). This observation is in good agreement with the Soria et al.’s findings that increasing salt concentrations lead to a substantial reduction in free water content. Consequently, the interfacial free energy at the ice–liquid boundary and nucleation barrier rise significantly, which severely impedes both the initial formation and subsequent growth of ice nuclei, thereby lowering the overall nucleation efficiency. ,

Further insight is gained by analyzing the water density distribution under an applied electric field. In the 3.5 mol/L NaCl system, the characteristic positive and negative density peaks typically observed in pure water or low-concentration saline systems fail to emerge (Figure c). This suggests that the ability of the electric field to induce dipolar orientation and polarization of water molecules is substantially weakened or even effectively screened under high-salinity conditions. Specifically, the intense solvation of salt ions and the complex hydration structures formed at high concentrations interfere with the water molecules’ response to the external electric field. − This disrupts the interfacial alignment and polarization of water dipoles, thereby hindering the formation of ordered molecular structures at the interface. Such findings reveal that high-concentration ionic environments impose significant limitations on the electric field’s capacity to manipulate interfacial water structure at the molecular level, ultimately suppressing field-induced polarization configurations and weakening the electric field’s role in facilitating ice nucleation.

We analyzed the distributions of the orientational tetrahedral order parameter (q) for different systems (Figure S9). The q distribution of ice exhibits a pronounced peak at 0.9–1.0, reflecting its highly ordered tetrahedral structure. In contrast, for free water in 1 M NaCl solution, the application of an external electric field induces a rightward shift of the q peak to 0.5–0.6, indicating a tendency of the water molecules to adopt ice-like ordering. By comparison, the q distribution of bound water in the same solution remains largely unchanged under the electric field. Its peak is located significantly lower than that of free water, further to the left, and far from that of ice, suggesting that bound water maintains a stable local structure and is less responsive to electric-field-induced ordering.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that high-salinity aqueous environments not only disrupt the interfacial structure of water through strong ion–water interactions but also significantly attenuate the ability of the applied electric field to drive molecular ordering and polarization (Figure S10). At the same time, we also referred to the ion exclusion effect of the electric field, − the simulation results observed that Na+ indeed accumulate at the surface (Figure S4). This dual inhibitory effect acts synergistically to raise the nucleation energy barrier, reduce the nucleation rate, and ultimately suppress the formation and growth of ice nuclei. Notably, these simulation-based findings are strongly corroborated by experimental observations, reinforcing the reliability of our model in capturing the coupling effects between ionic strength and electric field regulation in ice nucleation systems.

Conclusions

In summary, we investigated the electric-field-induced ice nucleation behavior in salt solutions of varying concentrations and revealed a distinct concentration-dependent suppression effect. While low-concentration electrolytes exhibit clear nucleation promotion under moderate electric fields, this effect gradually weakens and disappears beyond a critical ionic strength. Molecular dynamics simulations indicate that at high concentrations, hydrated ions accumulate at the interface (In this study, Na+ ions are assumed to migrate toward the PE interface under the applied electric field, based on Sergio’s investigation of the effects of PE coatings on activated carbon electrodes.), forming a salt-rich layer where water molecules are largely bound and display low polarizability. The addition of salt ions also inhibited the diffusion of water molecules. This interfacial structure disrupts the field-induced alignment of water molecules, thereby suppressing ice nucleation. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the failure of electrofreezing in saline environments and highlight the importance of interfacial ion–water interactions. Further exploration of methods to mitigate this shielding effect may offer new opportunities for regulating ice nucleation in complex aqueous systems, with potential applications in cryopreservation, atmospheric science, and energy systems.

Experimental Method

Freezing Measurements

In the freezing experiments, ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18 MΩ·cm was used as the freezing sample. The ultrapure water was prepared by a standard ultrapure water system (ULUP-I, ULUPURE, China), with real-time monitoring of water quality during preparation to ensure purity. Immediately after preparation, the ultrapure water was transferred to the cooling platform for freezing experiments. During the experiments, 8 μL droplets were deposited onto the cooled surface inside the refrigeration chamber. To prevent condensation and frost formation, dry nitrogen gas (N2) was continuously introduced into the chamber, and the relative humidity was stabilized at approximately 5% by controlling the gas flow rate. Ice nucleation was recorded in real-time using a high-speed camera to capture detailed information at the nucleation moment. The temperature of the cooling surface was precisely controlled by a Peltier cooling system integrated with a temperature controller and a water circulation system. Four Peltier modules, sealed within the cooling platform, were used. Thermal conductive silver grease was applied between the cooling stage and the copper plate (which served as both electrode and dielectric substrate) to improve thermal conduction efficiency. For high-precision temperature measurement, a temperature sensor with 0.1 °C resolution was embedded within the copper plate, coated with thermal grease to enhance thermal contact. Each experimental condition was repeated at least 15 times. The data points shown in Figure a represent the average of 15 measurements, with error bars calculated based on sample standard deviation.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

To investigate the effects of electric field and salt concentration on water molecules at the polyethylene (PE) interface, an amorphous cell containing 10 PE chains with a degree of polymerization of 50 (density 0.91 g/cm3, dimensions 32 × 32 × 25 Å3) was constructed using Materials Studio. Water molecules were placed above the PE surface with randomly distributed Na+/Cl– ion pairs, with specific quantities set according to the salt solution concentrations (Table S1). Water molecules were modeled using the TIP4P/ICE four-point water model, while PE chains and ions were described by the Amber force field to ensure accurate representation of interfacial and bulk behaviors. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied: fully periodic in the x and y directions along the cell plane, and also periodic along the z direction perpendicular to the PE interface. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions, with a vacuum layer extending the box to 12 nm along the z axis to probe the interfacial structure. The system was first energy-minimized using the conjugate gradient method, followed by 1 ns equilibration with a 2 fs time step. Electrostatic interactions were treated with the PME method, van der Waals and electrostatic cutoffs were set to 0.85 and 0.9 nm, respectively; temperature was controlled via a V-rescale thermostat with linear heating to 250 K over 30 ps, and pressure along x, y and z directions was regulated using Berendsen coupling at 1 atm. Subsequently, a uniform electric field of −3.6 × 107 V/m was applied along the z direction for 40 ns NVT simulations, with data collected every 2 ps; we also recorded the decay curve of the electric field (Figure S5). Additionally, our experimental duration was long enough to reach equilibrium. Water polarity was analyzed using GROMACS gmx density, with the 35–40 ns trajectory divided into 300 bins along the z axis.
