Usability testing of an upper-extremity rehabilitation robot for stroke rehabilitation
Authors: Yingnan Lin, Hewei Wang, Li Ding, Qingming Qu, Jianghong Fu, Yifang Lin, Jie Gu, Yanyan Huang, Jie Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the usability and user experience of the FLEXO-Arm1 upper limb rehabilitation robot among stroke survivors and rehabilitation therapists.MethodsA mixed-methods approach was employed, incorporating System Usability Scale (SUS) and User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) assessments alongside semi-structured interviews. Participants included stroke survivors with varying degrees of upper limb dysfunction and rehabilitation therapists who utilized the FLEXO-Arm1 for Passive Movement Training (PMT) and task-oriented training (TOT).ResultsOverall SUS scores indicated that stroke survivors rated the robot’s usability higher (A-, 87%) than rehabilitation therapists (B-, 67%). UEQ analysis revealed positive evaluations across all scales, particularly in attractiveness and clarity, with stroke survivors providing higher ratings than therapists. Semi-structured interviews highlighted operational challenges, particularly for those with severe upper limb dysfunction, while affirming the robot’s acceptance and potential as a supplementary rehabilitation tool.ConclusionThe FLEXO-Arm1 robot demonstrates favorable usability and user experience for both stroke survivors and therapists. While the device shows promise in rehabilitation settings, enhancements are necessary for users with severe impairments. Further studies should explore the device’s long-term impact on rehabilitation practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04689-9.Keywords: Stroke, Rehabilitation, Robot, Upper extremity, Usability testing

Introduction

Robot-Assisted Training (RAT) marks a substantial advancement in upper-extremity rehabilitation following stroke. This technology autonomously executes specific tasks, augmenting and enhancing human functions, and plays a pivotal role in the evolving landscape of rehabilitation science and technology [1]. Upper extremity impairment post-stroke presents a formidable challenge for clinical rehabilitation. In our country, approximately 3.94 million new stroke cases are reported annually [2], with about 36% of stroke survivors exhibiting persistent upper-extremity impairments even four years post-onset [3]. Addressing these challenges is a primary concern for clinical therapists. The concept of the “robot therapist” is emerging as a cornerstone of future rehabilitation efforts, emphasizing the need for continuous refinement of robotic control methods to replicate complex therapeutic techniques and ease the workload on therapists [4, 5].

Control methods for upper-extremity exoskeleton rehabilitation robots primarily fall into two categories: passive and active control [6]. Historically, Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) Control and impedance/admittance control have been the primary strategies used. However, PID position control often results in significant tracking errors, while earlier impedance/admittance strategies struggled to facilitate smooth movements due to insufficient dynamic compensation [7, 8]. To address these limitations, Shanghai Electric Intelligent Rehabilitation Medical Technology Co., Ltd. developed the FLEXO-Arm1 system robot, which integrates both a teaching mode and a human-machine collaborative interaction mode. This system optimizes traditional control methods, addressing the key shortcomings seen in clinical applications of upper-extremity rehabilitation robots.

The clinical efficacy of this robotic system, a prerequisite for meaningful usability testing, has been established in a prior, rigorous, multi-center randomized controlled trial [9]. That pivotal study demonstrated that robot-assisted training is non-inferior to enhanced upper extremity therapy in reducing motor impairment for stroke survivors, solidly supporting its role as an effective complement to therapist-administered rehabilitation. Furthermore, insights from a complementary resting-state fMRI study [10] elucidated a potential neural mechanism for these outcomes, indicating that the therapy promotes motor recovery by upregulating activity in the ipsilesional motor cortex and enhancing cross-hemispheric sensorimotor integration.

Building upon this foundation of proven clinical efficacy, usability becomes a critical subsequent factor when evaluating the device for clinical development. According to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 9241-11 standard [11], usability refers to how effectively users can achieve their objectives, the ease with which they can learn to use the device and their overall satisfaction. Usability testing allows participants to share genuine experiences of using the equipment. This study offers a comprehensive review of the active and passive control strategies employed by the FLEXO-Arm1 robot, engaging stroke survivors in evaluating the clinical usability and viability of the proposed methodology.

Methods

The research was conducted in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University and involved 40 stroke survivors and 10 therapists. This sample size was determined to be adequate for a usability study, as literature suggests that 80–95% of usability problems can be identified with 10–20 participants [12], and our total number of stroke survivors (N = 40) exceeds this common benchmark. This ensures a comprehensive exploration of the user experience across different impairment severity levels. Stroke survivors met the diagnostic criteria established at the 5th Academic Conference of the Chinese Stroke Society and the Tiantan International Cerebrovascular Disease Conference 2019.

Usability testing

The FLEXO-Arm1 system, developed by Shanghai Electric Intelligent Rehabilitation Medical Technology Co., Ltd., features a five-degree-of-freedom robotic exoskeleton with two degrees at the shoulder, two at the elbow, and one at the wrist (Fig. 2). The study focused on shoulder and elbow joints. Prescribed movements during testing involved vertical shoulder flexion-extension, horizontal abduction-adduction, and elbow flexion-extension, with the hand maintained in a neutral position. Throughout all training and assessment procedures, participants were consistently instructed to remain seated with their backs against the chair backrest. For patients with severe impairment, the robotic system itself provided substantial gravitational support and restricted the range of motion, thereby naturally limiting the opportunity for large trunk compensations. Furthermore, all sessions were directly supervised by a therapist who provided verbal cues and, if necessary, gentle physical restraint to minimize compensatory trunk movements, ensuring that the observed motor efforts originated as much as possible from the upper limb.

FLEXO-arm1 system

At the beginning of each test, the therapist entered essential patient information, including name, gender, age, stroke classification, affected side, and primary assessment outcomes. The robotic arm was aligned with the paralyzed side, and adjustments were made to the arm’s height and length to match the patient’s seated posture and arm length, ensuring comfort and optimal motion range.

The testing protocol consisted of two phases: 10-minute of Passive Movement Training (PMT) based on passive control strategy, followed by 20 min of Task-Oriented Training (TOT) utilizing an active control strategy, without rest between phases. The movement trajectory was continuously monitored for irregularities by therapists. If any were observed, the emergency stop button was manually activated immediately to address the comprehensive deviations.

The FLEXO-Arm1 system employs a passive control strategy based on Proportional-Derivative (PD) control, integrated with bio-force signals, to enable direct teaching. In this mode, the therapist manually guides the mechanical arm, which replicates the training by following the therapist-defined trajectory. To enhance movement stability and reduce errors, the system incorporates a trajectory smoothing technique, specifically the minimum joint acceleration method, ensuring a continuous, smooth, and personalized motion trajectory.

During the testing phase of the teaching mode, the therapist initiates the mechanical arm’s movement according to the patient’s upper extremity function. The robot then reproduces this trajectory, guiding the affected limb through repetitive PMT. This approach provides a systematic method to evaluate the efficacy of the FLEXO-Arm1 teaching mode [17].

In contrast, TOT utilizes an active control strategy combining impedance control with feedforward compensation to dynamically adjust for smooth motion [18]. During the TOT testing phase, stroke survivors selected gaming interfaces for robot-assisted exercises, with therapists adjusting the difficulty level based on each patient’s residual function. When patients encountered difficulties completing task due to functional constraints, therapists modified the game level. Figure 3 displays six game scenarios for task-oriented training. Each game contained five adjustable difficulty levels, dynamically modulated through task density, range of motion requirements, and robotic assistance levels [10].

Questionnaire assessment

Upon completing the PMT and TOT phases, stroke survivors and therapists provided feedback via the System Usability Scale (SUS), User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ), and semi-structured interviews.

The SUS [19, 20], developed by Brooke in 1986, is a concise yet reliable tool for evaluating product usability. It includes 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with odd-numbered items reflecting positive statements and even-numbered items representing negative statements. Items 4 and 10 assess learnability, while the remaining items evaluate usability. Scoring involves adjusting the raw scores, and the total SUS score is calculated by multiplying the adjusted total by 2.5, with a maximum score of 100. Scores over 50 suggest satisfactory usability, with those above 70 classified as good, and scores above 80 deemed excellent.

The UEQ [19, 20], first developed in German in 2005, assesses user experience by evaluating classical usability aspects (efficiency, clarity, dependability) and hedonic aspects (attractiveness, novelty, stimulation). The UEQ contains six scales and 26 items, rated on a 7-point scale from − 3 (very bad) to + 3 (very good). A score above + 0.8 indicates a positive evaluation, while a score below − 0.8 suggests a negative assessment.

A semi-structured interview was developed to explore participants’ experiences with the FLEXO-Arm1 device, focusing on its ease of use, ease of learning, and the operability of the donning process. The guide (provided as Supplementary Material 3) included open-ended questions and prompts to ensure consistent coverage of key topics while allowing for flexibility to probe emerging themes.

Statistical methods

All research data were double entered into EXCEL software. Categorical data were numerically represented, while quantitative data were expressed as ±s. SUS and UEQ results were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and UEQ data was processed using UEQ Data Analysis Software version 10. For the semi-structured interview analysis, we employed a combined methodology of thematic analysis and descriptive statistics. The data were analyzed by two independent coders through an iterative process of familiarization, initial coding, and theme development. The reliability of the coding process was substantiated by an almost perfect inter-coder agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.82). Data saturation was confirmed when two consecutive interviews yielded no new substantive codes.

Results

All stroke survivors and rehabilitation therapists successfully completed all stages of testing. The survivors fully understood the training tasks, and no interface errors, abnormal movement trajectories or device-related adverse events occurred during the trials.

SUS results

Rehabilitation therapists achieved an overall SUS score of 73.25 ± 8.37, while all stroke survivors scored 79.63 ± 9.94. Scores for the stroke survivor subgroup were as follows: M group 83 ± 9.05, M-M group 82.5 ± 8.06, M-S group 81.5 ± 9.43, and S group 71.5 ± 7.84. Table 2 displays the distribution of SUS scores across these groups. The SUS score for both therapists and survivors fell between “Good” and “Excellent” on average. Stratified analysis revealed that only the S group had scores in the “OK-Good” range, while the remaining groups were classified as “Good-Excellent”. Overall, stroke survivors rated the system higher than therapists, as shown in Fig. 4.

Semi-structured interview results

All 10 rehabilitation therapists agreed that the system’s operating process was reasonable and expressed willingness to incorporate the FLEXO-Arm1 into their rehabilitation protocols. They noted no issues with the M, M-M, and M-S groups but reported challenges with securing the distal end of the affected limb in S group patients. Stroke survivors reported smooth and continuous motion trajectories and expressed openness to using the system for rehabilitation. S group survivors, however, noted difficulty donning the device, particularly during hand fixation.

Two stroke survivors from the M group stated that they preferred using RAT over traditional therapist-led rehabilitation. In contrast, one stroke survivor from the S group indicated a lack of interest in RAT, favoring comprehensive therapy conducted entirely by a therapist. Thirty-seven survivors preferred RAT as a supplementary option alongside traditional rehabilitation sessions.

Discussion

Stroke remains a leading global health challenge, ranking as the second most common cause of death and the third leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [21]. The aftermath of stroke often leaves individuals with significant impairments in upper limb movement, coordination, sensation, and flexibility, severely impacting their ability to perform daily activities such as bathing and dressing [22]. Among these impairments, upper limb dysfunction is one of the most distressing long-term consequences of stroke, making its rehabilitation a critical focus for patients, caregivers, and clinicians alike [23]. The rise of artificial intelligence and technological advancements has underscored the potential of innovative rehabilitation technologies to enhance post-stroke recovery. To effectively address clinical rehabilitation needs, upper limb rehabilitation robots must continually refine their control methods, particularly emphasizing both active and passive control strategies [24].

The FLEXO-Arm1 upper limb rehabilitation robot, developed by Shanghai Electric Intelligent Rehabilitation Medical Technology Co., Ltd., employs a PD control strategy integrated with bio-force signals for passive motion, facilitating PMT. Its active control strategy leverages impedance control with feedforward compensation, ensuring smooth and adaptive movements. These improvements foster personalized, continuous motion trajectories during both passive and assisted actions. This study was conducted to evaluate the device’s usability following the establishment of its clinical efficacy, with the goal of assessing its potential for widespread adoption in clinical practice.

Usability is paramount in clinical rehabilitation devices, encompassing aspects such as ease of learning, usability, and fault tolerance. Effective devices should demonstrate high efficacy, efficiency, ease of learning, and a satisfying user experience during interactions [25]. Comprehensive usability evaluations are essential for assessing clinical rehabilitation equipment and driving iterative improvements. The SUS and UEQ are common subjective evaluation methods for medical devices. In this study, the SUS analysis revealed that overall mean scores for rehabilitation therapists and stroke survivors fell within the “Good-Excellent” range, except for the severe dysfunction (S) group, which rated in the “OK-Good” range. Notably, stroke survivors rated the device’s usability higher than that of rehabilitation therapists. Based on Jeff Sauro’s extensive database [26], of 466 studies (over 5000 user feedback on SUS), the SUS rating of B- for therapists (approximately 67%) indicates that the FLEXO-Arm1 outperforms about 67% of comparable products. Conversely, stroke survivors rated the usability as A- (approximately 87%), outperforming 87% of products. These results suggest that the FLEXO-Arm1 is generally usable for stroke survivors and therapists, although usability challenges persist for those with severe upper limb dysfunction, particularly related to the difficulties of donning the device, as highlighted in subsequent semi-structured interviews.

The UEQ corroborated these findings, revealing positive evaluations from both stroke survivors and therapists, with survivors consistently rating their experience higher than therapists. Comparative analysis against UEQ benchmarks showed above-average evaluations. Stroke survivors particularly excelled in ratings for attractiveness, clarity, and stimulation, indicating strong endorsement of the device. However, both groups rated the novelty item lower, which may be attributed to the device’s focus on optimizing control methods rather than introducing innovative movement paradigms. This emphasis on control optimization aligns with established rehabilitation techniques, such as the Bobath method [27] and motor relearning theory [28], which prioritize comfort in movement over new movement strategies. While stratified analysis revealed no significant differences in user experience ratings among groups, survivors with severe dysfunction rated efficiency lower, echoing insights from semi-structured interviews regarding the challenges of using the robotic arm.

Insights gained from semi-structured interviews confirmed that the FLEXO-Arm1 system delivers rehabilitation movements that are generally accepted and endorsed by both therapists and survivors. However, stroke survivors with severe upper limb dysfunction encountered difficulties in securing the distal end of the affected limb, particularly those at Brunnstrom stages I-II with limited hand function. Therapists suggested implementing elastic fixation straps to mitigate these challenges. Interestingly, stroke survivors expressed a preference for utilizing RAT as a supplementary approach following therapist-led sessions, which is significant for clinical practice. The successful completion of both the 10-minute teaching training test and the 20-minute therapeutic training test by all stroke survivors underscores the feasibility of the training program and sets the stage for future research into clinical efficacy.

Usability testing plays a crucial role in the optimization of medical devices, particularly in rehabilitation. Identifying shortcomings through usability evaluations allows for targeted improvements to better meet patient needs. For instance, a study by Professor Rejdak’s team at the Medical University of Lublin, Poland [29], assessed the perceptual appeal and usability of a multimodal robotic assistant for elderly patients with memory impairments, finding high ratings for social impact but neutral ratings for usability, indicating a need for enhancement. Similarly, Professor Shin’s team at the National Rehabilitation Center in Korea [30] evaluated two movement methods for an exoskeleton upper limb rehabilitation robot, discovering higher usability ratings for passive movements, thus highlighting areas needing optimization. In a study by Professor Climent’s team at the Monnied Biological Research Institute in Córdoba, Spain [31], usability assessments of a home-based upper limb MERLIN robot for stroke patients revealed it to be safe, useful, feasible, and motivating for home-based rehabilitation. Our findings similarly indicate that both stroke survivors and rehabilitation therapists rated the FLEXO-Arm1 robot positively in terms of overall usability and user experience, while also pinpointing areas for improvement for those with severe upper limb impairments.

Building on established clinical efficacy from prior studies [9, 10], the primary focus of usability evaluation is on efficiency, ease of learning, and user satisfaction. The optimized control strategy of the FLEXO-Arm1 robot allows for personalized training and smooth human-robot collaborative interaction during TOT, effectively simulating a therapist’s approach. Positive evaluations from both rehabilitation therapists and stroke survivors regarding the system’s usability and user experience highlight its rational operational workflow and effective rehabilitation exercises. This preliminary validation supports the feasibility of the upper limb rehabilitation robot in clinical stroke rehabilitation, indicating that stroke patients are increasingly receptive to integrating robot-assisted rehabilitation into their post-therapy regimens. Improvements have been implemented in response to feedback collected during testing, ensuring the system evolves to meet the needs of all users effectively.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First and foremost, the fixed order of the training conditions—where PMT was always administered immediately before TOT without a rest period—may have introduced bias into the usability assessments. Factors such as participant fatigue, learning effects, or carryover from the PMT session could have influenced the perceived usability and workload of the subsequent TOT mode. Consequently, this design limits our ability to evaluate the independent usability of each mode in isolation, as the experience of TOT was inherently contingent upon the prior PMT. While this sequence was chosen to simulate a continuous clinical rehabilitation workflow, it prevents a direct and separate comparison of the two modes’ usability, which is a valuable aim for future research. Furthermore, while the total sample size was adequate for the primary usability evaluation, the number of participants within each subgroup (based on impairment severity) was relatively small. This limited our statistical power to conduct robust comparative analyses between these groups. Future studies should employ a counterbalanced design with a larger, specifically powered cohort to independently assess the usability of PMT and TOT, as well as to confidently compare usability differences across diverse patient demographics.

Conclusion

This study highlights the usability of the FLEXO-Arm1 upper-extremity rehabilitation robot in the context of post-stroke rehabilitation. The findings indicate that both stroke survivors and rehabilitation therapists rated the device positively, reflecting its effectiveness in facilitating personalized motion therapy and enabling smooth human-robot interaction. Despite varying usability ratings based on the severity of upper limb dysfunction, the overall user experience was encouraging, with most participants acknowledging the potential of the device as a valuable complement to traditional therapy. The challenges faced by individuals with severe impairments, particularly in the fixation of the robotic arm, underscore the need for continued optimization of the device.

Building on these findings, future research should prioritize employing a counterbalanced study design to independently evaluate the usability of the PMT and TOT modes. This approach is essential to eliminate potential biases introduced by the fixed order and fatigue effects inherent in the current study design, thereby obtaining a clearer, mode-specific understanding of the system’s usability. Furthermore, longitudinal investigations are warranted to explore how these independently assessed training modes contribute to long-term functional outcomes and enhance clinical rehabilitation practices for stroke survivors.
