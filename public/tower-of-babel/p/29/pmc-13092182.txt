Estimating the Need for Naloxone Distribution from United States Emergency Departments
Authors: Alexandra OK Mannerings, Donald E Stader, R Gentry Wilkerson, James Neuenschwander, Scott G Weiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesNaloxone is a key intervention for opioid overdose, and emergency departments (EDs) are an important setting for its distribution. Existing estimates rely only on overdose diagnoses, likely missing many patients with an increased risk of opioid overdose (referred to as “at-risk” visits or patients “at risk” throughout this paper), before that point. We sought to create an evidence-based, expanded definition and estimation of at-risk ED visits to better quantify opportunities and volume for naloxone distribution.MethodsWe produced an expert-reviewed set of diagnosis codes that identified claims for patients with evidence-based risk factors for opioid overdose, beyond the opioid overdose codes currently in use. Using the 2021 Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Emergency Department Sample, we applied linearized survey analysis with stratified weights to quantify the frequency of claims with any matching primary or secondary diagnosis codes and provide a national estimate of qualifying visits. We cross-referenced this estimate with Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) overdose surveillance data for validation.ResultsWe estimate that United States EDs saw at least 3.87 million visits by at-risk patients, compared with 226,000 for treatment of opioid overdose alone. Of these, approximately 3.64 million visits were not classified as overdose cases. Over 900,000 visits involved nonopioid drug use, and more than 40% of visits were covered by Medicaid. At-risk visits represented over 3% of all ED visits nationally.ConclusionOur estimate represents a significant increase over previous approaches. We believe this expansion more accurately represents need, can contribute to expanded distribution of naloxone, and will better serve agencies quantifying naloxone in EDs.Keywords: naloxone, opioid epidemic, emergency department, overdose prevention

The Bottom Line

This analysis sought to redefine and re-estimate how many patients with elevated risk for opioid overdose visit American emergency departments (EDs), as publicly available estimates only account for patients treated for overdose. We estimate in 2021 that EDs treated 3.87 million patients who were more likely to experience an overdose, a number that is critical to understand if we are to prevent deaths through interventions like naloxone distribution.

Introduction

Background

Over 80,000 people died in the United States (US) from opioid-related overdose in 2022 as part of a public health epidemic that continues unabated.1 Naloxone, which can reverse opioid overdoses, is part of the US Health and Human Services’ 4 pillars to address the opioid crisis.2 One state found 81% of patients who died from opioid overdose interacted with the health care system in the year before their death; in addition to treating patients with opioid overdose, US emergency departments (EDs) also see approximately 40% of people who ultimately die from opioid overdose prior to their demise.3 Only 10% of these visits to the ED prior to death are for nonfatal opioid overdose.

Importance

States such as Washington that now mandate naloxone distribution from EDs for at-risk individuals must determine how much naloxone is needed.4 This information helps policymakers and insurers budget for purchase of naloxone and enables quality measures to track distribution. But how do they estimate who is at increased risk for opioid overdose? To date, public health entities such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have based their estimates on the number of patients treated for overdose5; with only 10% of health care visits prior to overdose deaths due to overdose,3 relying solely on overdose encounters may miss 90% of at-risk patients. Reports of fentanyl contamination in nonopioid drugs are increasing,6,7 and approximately half of patients experiencing opioid overdose intended to use nonopioid substances.8 Despite these risks to nonopioid users and at-risk patients alike, programs and analyses often limit naloxone outreach to overdose cases. For example, one study measured success of a new take-home naloxone program in their ED only by naloxone distribution to overdose patients.9 However, diagnosis codes alone do not ensure clinicians act on these opportunities; naloxone distribution in EDs remains highly variable and restricted to overdose cases, raising questions about whether gaps reflect naloxone supply constraints or clinician under-recognition risk. Overall, current estimation methods focus narrowly on overdose cases, missing most at-risk patients.5,9 By quantifying visits where best practice recommends naloxone,4,5 this approach supports planning and protocols to ensure ED naloxone distribution reaches patients before overdose occurs.

Goals of This Investigation

We sought to create an expanded definition of “at-risk” to capture any patient at increased risk of opioid overdose, not only those who experience overdose. We sought to improve estimates at-risk patient visits to EDs to better quantify opportunities and volume for naloxone distribution, and to validate the use of this approach with data available to agencies looking to conduct such estimations for naloxone distribution planning. This work is intended for public health agencies, policymakers, and hospital administrators involved in planning, funding, and implementing naloxone distribution, particularly within emergency care settings.

Methods

This study was determined not to be human subjects research by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Data Source

We used the 2021 Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS),10 which includes 30,099,368 ED visit records. This dataset is formed by a stratified probability sampling of the full State Emergency Department database and the State Inpatient database, representing inpatient admissions from the ED and ED-only visits from 993 hospitals across 41 states. The strata used for sampling are US census region, trauma center designation, urban-rural location of the hospital, ownership, and teaching status.

CDC’s Drug Overdose Surveillance and Epidemiology (DOSE) data were used to compare existing opioid overdose-related ED visit estimates. DOSE data are derived from a national electronic medical record (EMR) surveillance program, using preliminary ICD-10 codes as well as the free-text “chief complaint” field. The ICD-10 code definitions for opioid overdose virtually match between the CDC DOSE definitions and our opioid overdose code set. A record is also included in DOSE, if the chief complaint text indicates opioid involvement in an overdose.

ICD-10 Code Selection

Codes for opioid use, “abuse”, and dependence formed the core of the at-risk codes, but nonopioid drug use, “abuse,” and dependence were also included as nonopioid use still indicates a risk of polysubstance use or fentanyl exposure from adulteration, an increasingly common cause of accidental overdose (eSupplement 1 for a full list of codes included in this analysis). We used an iterative expert consensus approach to develop this curated list of ICD-10 codes. We began with the CCS substance use categories from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), which were then reviewed by a coding specialist to identify any missing or inappropriate codes. This initial list of 605 codes was iteratively reviewed and refined by a multidisciplinary panel of 4 addiction medicine physicians and academic researchers; this team was recruited by A.O.K.M. from subject-matter experts, board members and colleagues already engaged with naloxone distribution nonprofits and research. Final code selections were made through unanimous consensus, with all panel members agreeing on each inclusion or exclusion. The decision to include a code was based on meeting 3 criteria:

1.Does scientific evidence exist to support that the medical condition represented by this code is associated with increased risk of opioid overdose?

These 3 criteria meant that a code, indicating a condition that had evidence for increased risk of opioid overdose but could regularly also represent situations which would not qualify a patient as at increased risk, would not be included. This approach was taken to ensure that claims that were included in the estimate, had a very high probability of representing a visit by a patient truly experiencing an increased risk of opioid overdose. Overall, 567 ICD-10 codes were ultimately retained in the final approved code list, spanning 19 CCS categories such as opioid-related disorders, poisoning by drugs, and adverse effects of drugs. These were combined into opioid and nonopioid related code groups for analysis. eSupplement 2 presents specific examples of evidence for each code set, included in the final approved code list.

Statistical Analysis

From the NEDS, we flagged ED visits with any diagnosis code for opioid use, opioid dependence, opioid poisoning/overdose, or long-term opioid pain management (eSupplement 1) to estimate the number of ED visits by individuals at increased risk for opioid overdose.

We conducted a descriptive analysis of at-risk patient visits by key patient characteristics, including age, income, payor, race, sex, visit type, and perinatal status. Linearized survey analysis in STATA v18 was used to calculate appropriate stratified proportion and weighted count estimates from the NEDS sample, considering clusters and sample weights. HCUP provides the weights, strata, and cluster values in alignment with their sampling strategy, with discharge weights calculated by stratum. Per the HCUP documentation, “hospital-owned EDs were stratified in a manner similar to that for universe hospital-weight calculations. Within stratum s for hospital i, the universe weight for each visit in the NEDS sample was calculated as follows:

DISCWT = DWis(universe) = [DNs(universe) ÷ ADNs(sample)] ∗ (4 ÷ Qi)

where DWis(universe) was the discharge weight; DNs(universe) is the number of ED visits from community, nonrehabilitation hospitals in the universe within stratum s; ADNs(sample)) is the number of adjusted ED visits from sample hospitals selected for the NEDS; and Qi represents the number of quarters of ED visits contributed by hospital i to the NEDS (usually Qi = 4). Thus, each discharge's weight (DISCWT) is equal to the number of universe ED visits it represents in stratum s during that year”.11

Results

In 2021, US EDs saw an estimated 3.87 million visits (95% CI, 3.62 million to 4.1 million) by patients at increased risk for opioid overdose, over 3% of all ED visits (Fig. 1). At 1,152 per 100,000 US residents, this finding is substantially higher than the CDC estimate of 65.5 nonfatal opioid overdose ED visits per 100,000 persons.5 The estimate from NEDS of actual opioid overdoses treated in EDs in 2021 was 226,453, which aligns with the CDC estimate of 217,446. Thus, of the estimated 3.87 million at-risk visits, approximately 3.64 million were not classified as overdose cases. Nonopioid drug use is the most common reason for a patient to be considered at increased risk, making up over 23% of all estimated visits. Long-term opioid use was the second most common reason at 18% of visits.

Limitations

The HCUP dataset is a rigorous, well-documented claims dataset that has been used in numerous studies to provide estimates of the prevalence of various conditions.12 However, claims data are generated to facilitate payments rather than research and have known limitations. Diagnosis codes may be excluded if not documented to the necessary specification or if not relevant to payment. Additionally, our ICD-10 code selection was decided by an expert panel for this investigation; a different group of subject-matter experts could have produced a different code list. In favor of a more iterative, discussion-based process, our expert panel did not use any formal method to quantify agreement metrics, using a more established consensus framework, such as the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method or a modified Delphi approach, could have provided more rigor to the code list development. Diagnostic codes are administrative data that may not fully reflect provider intent (for example, ED physicians often do not enter final codes) or capture the broader clinical context. Racial demographic data is especially incomplete, with no consistent protocol for how a patient’s race is determined or recorded.

Conversely, the likelihood of overcounting is very low. A claim identified as “at-risk” very likely represents a visit by an at-risk patient because codes cannot be added to claims without required documentation and the expanded code list only included codes that unambiguously represented a condition related to increased risk of opioid overdose based on existing research. We recognize that patients with substance use disorder (SUD) typically have more visits to the ED than those without SUD, and thus, this estimate includes multiple visits by the same patient. However, patients at increased risk of opioid overdose are not the only people who visit EDs who may benefit from naloxone distribution; patients with family members or friends affected by opioid use disorder (OUD), or those who work in settings where overdoses more commonly occur, could also benefit from having naloxone.

Discussion

Our investigation found that approximately 3.64 million at-risk visits were not classified as overdose cases, underscoring that most opportunities for naloxone distribution occur outside of overdose encounters. These findings, based upon a nationwide sample of ED visits, provide a new estimation of how much naloxone is needed for at-risk patients. The findings are based not just upon the number of visits for overdose, but a more holistic consideration of risk factors and diagnosis that are empirically associated with increased risk for overdose. Our expansion of codes captures those who are at risk but are seen in the ED for something other than opioid overdose. In one study, patients in OUD treatment with chronic pain, and thus on long-term opioid therapy, were 55% more likely to experience an overdose than patients without chronic pain.13 CDC estimates that the rate of ED visits for people with SUD is 74.4 per 100,000 population14 compared with 47.3 for the general population.15 We believe those numbers to be too low compared with our expanded estimate, as patients with a diagnosis of long-term opioid use made up the second-largest proportion of visits by patients with increased risk of opioid overdose; and yet, such patients are excluded from public estimations until they experience an overdose. The ED offers a key opportunity to identify individuals at risk of opioid-involved overdose, even when presenting for unrelated conditions, consistent with social emergency medicine principles and prior work on ED-based screening and intervention. By focusing on conditions that indicate a significant risk of opioid overdose, rather than once we have missed a chance to intervene, we hope to increase the likelihood of interventions like naloxone and other opioid reversal agents, distribution that could reduce the threat of death.

There are concerns about the use of claims data for estimating the prevalence of conditions. The comparison of opioid overdose visits from this analysis to existing CDC estimates suggests that claims may be a viable and broadly available source of data for estimating at-risk visits to hospitals. CDC estimates of ED visits for opioid overdose leverage preliminary EMR surveillance data, using a similar code set as well as free text “chief complaint” data, and yet aligned closely with the counts generated from HCUP claims. The increase in at-risk visits compared with other methodology represents the expansion of the definition of “at-risk”, which is necessary to properly capture the scope of need for naloxone. Patient willingness to accept naloxone may differ between those with OUD and those at risk through other factors (eg, chronic opioid prescription, fentanyl-contaminated nonopioid substances), which has implications for ED-based distribution efforts. If we assume that someone only needs naloxone after being treated in the ED for an overdose, we miss a critical opportunity to give at-risk individuals naloxone prior to overdosing.

Further, these estimates are likely still undercounts because of incomplete documentation, gaps in patient history, and sensitivities in sharing drug use history. Still, they provide a novel and more precise way for states and insurers to better estimate the amount of naloxone and expense needed to provide this life-saving medication to at-risk individuals.

In summary, by expanding the definition of who is considered “at risk” for opioid overdose-related ED visits (beyond opioid overdose cases) and applying expert-reviewed ICD-10 codes to national claims data, we estimate 3.64 million at-risk visits where naloxone could be indicated, far exceeding prior counts. This approach offers a practical, evidence-based method for health systems and policymakers to better plan naloxone distribution and intervene before overdose occurs.
