Associations of lactate-to-hematocrit ratio with short- and long-term prognoses in critically ill patients with respiratory failure: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhipeng Li, Xiao Yang, Xianzhang Shan, Qionghua Lv, Huiling Yan, Songlin Li, Ziyun Yang, Yancui Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple studies have confirmed that the lymphocyte-to-high-density Lipoprotein ratio (LHR) is associated with disease outcomes. However, the prognostic value of LHR in critically ill patients with respiratory failure remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between LHR and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with respiratory failure. Hospitalized patients with severe respiratory failure were identified from the MIMIC-IV database and categorized into tertiles according to LHR. The outcomes included short-term and long-term mortality after admission. Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to evaluate the association between LHR and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with respiratory failure. A total of 10,379 patients were included in the analysis. The 30-day, 120-day, and 360-day mortality rates were 31.59%, 34.90%, and 35.49%, respectively. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis demonstrated that elevated LHR was significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality. After adjustment for confounding factors, elevated LHR remained significantly associated with 30-day, 120-day, and 360-day mortality. Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a progressively increasing risk of short-term and long-term all-cause mortality with increasing LHR. LHR is significantly associated with both short-term and long-term mortality in critically ill patients with respiratory failure. These findings suggest that LHR may help identify critically ill patients with respiratory failure who are at high-risk of all-cause mortality.

1. Introduction

Respiratory failure is a common cause of severe illness worldwide, resulting in more than 3 million hospitalizations in the United States alone in 2017 and posing a substantial threat to human health.[1] Owing to its complex etiology, respiratory failure can be precipitated by infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure, and other conditions.[2–4] Despite advances in medical technology, including the use of noninvasive mechanical ventilation, high-flow nasal oxygen therapy, and prone positioning, which have substantially improved the management of respiratory failure,[5–7] critically ill patients with respiratory failure continue to face a high-risk of adverse outcomes. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify early prognostic indicators in patients with respiratory failure to facilitate risk stratification, optimize treatment strategies, and improve clinical outcomes.

Blood lactate, a product of anaerobic metabolism that rises in the setting of tissue hypoperfusion and severe hemodynamic compromise, has been shown to correlate with prognosis in critical illness. Lower lactate levels are associated with improved outcomes in critically ill patients, supporting lactate as an important prognostic marker.[8] This association is further supported by a prospective observational study in patients with sepsis.[9] Hematocrit (HCT), which reflects red blood cell (RBC) volume and oxygen-carrying capacity, is often reduced in critically ill patients with respiratory failure, particularly in the context of infection-related sepsis.[10] Changes in HCT are a clinically relevant prognostic marker in sepsis, suggesting potential prognostic value in critically ill patients with severe respiratory failure. In addition, inflammation and hypovolemia in critically ill patients with respiratory failure may also influence HCT values. Thus, lactate and HCT both reflect disease severity, although each has limitations when considered in isolation. Recent studies have highlighted the utility of composite biomarkers, such as the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index[11] and the lactate-to-albumin ratio.[12] Accordingly, Demir et al proposed the lactate-to-hematocrit ratio (LHR) as a novel and potentially sensitive indicator for prognostic assessment in trauma patients[13]; however, its value in respiratory failure has not been well characterized.

Accordingly, this study aimed to determine whether LHR predicts outcomes in critically ill patients with respiratory failure, including short-term and long-term all-cause mortality, using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study patients

This retrospective study utilized de-identified clinical data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1), a rigorously curated, large-scale database developed by the MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology that contains detailed ICU records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center.[14] Database access was approved for Li Zhipeng, who performed the data extraction; institutional review board approval was waived because all data were fully de-identified. Study inclusion was limited to ICU admissions coded for respiratory failure according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision. The following exclusion criteria were applied sequentially: age <18 years at the time of first ICU admission; repeated ICU admissions for the same patient, with only the first admission retained; documented end-stage renal disease, liver cirrhosis, or active malignancy; ICU length of stay <3 hours, and missing lactate or hematocrit measurements within the first 24 hours after ICU admission. In the absence of established clinical cutoff values for LHR, tertiles were used to facilitate risk stratification and improve interpretability. After applying these criteria, 10,379 patients remained and were stratified into tertiles according to LHR (Fig. 1).

Patient inclusion process in the study.

2.2. Data extraction

Data extraction and processing were conducted using PostgreSQL (v14; PostgreSQL Global Development Group [Open Source]). The following variables were extracted or derived. Demographic variables included age, sex, ethnicity, and body weight. Comorbidities included COPD, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart failure. Illness-severity was assessed using the simplified acute physiology score II, sequential organ failure assessment, Acute Physiology Score III, Oxford acute severity of illness score, Glasgow Coma Scale, and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Vital signs included heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, percutaneous pulse oxygen saturation, and body temperature. Laboratory variables included RBC count, white blood cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, red cell distribution width (RDW), total calcium, glucose, potassium, sodium, total CO2, partial pressure of carbon dioxide, partial pressure of oxygen, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen. Comorbidities were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision codes in MIMIC-IV (v3.1). Hematological parameters and illness-severity scores were obtained from measurements recorded within the first 24 hours after ICU admission. Variables with <20% missing data were imputed using multiple imputation with a random forest method implemented in the R package “mice,” with models informed by the remaining observed covariates.

2.3. Study endpoint

The primary endpoint was 30-day in-hospital all-cause mortality, and the secondary endpoints were 120-day and 360-day all-cause mortality.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, [IQR]), depending on their distribution, whereas categorical variables were presented as proportions. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Group comparisons were performed using t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Survival probabilities across LHR tertiles were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves, and between-group differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify predictors of all-cause mortality, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated for the association between LHR and outcomes after stepwise adjustment for confounders. Covariates with univariate associations at P < .05 were retained, and additional clinically relevant prognostic variables were incorporated into the multivariable models. Model 1 was unadjusted, whereas Model 2 was adjusted for age and body weight. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for age, body weight, sequential organ failure assessment, APS II, simplified acute physiology score II, oxford acute severity of illness score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, heart rate, noninvasive arterial pressure, percutaneous pulse oxygen saturation, body temperature, hypertension, hemoglobin, platelet count, RDW, RBC count, white blood cell count, total calcium, glucose, potassium, total CO2, PaO2, PaCO2, creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen. LHR was modeled both as a continuous variable and as an ordinal categorical variable, with the lowest tertile serving as the reference group. P values for trend were calculated across LHR tertiles. Restricted cubic spline regression with 3 knots was applied to evaluate potential nonlinear associations between admission LHR and mortality at 30, 120, and 360 days. Stratified analyses were further performed according to sex, race, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), heart failure, and COPD to assess the consistency of the prognostic value of LHR for the primary outcome. Interactions between LHR and stratification variables were examined using likelihood ratio tests. A two-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using DecisionLink software (version 1.0; Statsape Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China, https://www.statsape.com/).[15]

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

This study included 10,379 critically ill patients with respiratory failure. The median age of the cohort was 68 years IQR, 57–79), with 5832 men (56.2%) and 4547 women (43.8%). The median LHR among all participants was 0.06 (IQR, 0.04–0.09). The 30-day, 120-day, and 360-day mortality rates were 31.59%, 34.90%, and 35.49%, respectively. Baseline characteristics of critically ill patients with respiratory failure stratified by LHR tertiles are presented in Table 1. Compared with patients in the lowest LHR tertile, those in higher tertiles generally had greater body weight, higher illness-severity scores at admission, higher heart rate, lower noninvasive arterial pressure, higher respiratory rate, lower hematocrit and hemoglobin levels, higher lactate levels, lower platelet counts, higher RDW, lower calcium levels, higher glucose and potassium levels, lower total CO2 and PaCO2, higher PaO2, higher creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels, and a higher prevalence of T2DM. Compared with the lowest LHR tertile, the highest tertile exhibited significantly higher 30-day (20.06% vs 26.54% vs 48.17%), 120-day (22.09% vs 29.49% vs 53.11%), and 360-day (22.41% vs 30.33% vs 53.74%) mortality (all P < .001).

3.2. Primary outcome

Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by LHR tertiles illustrated the incidence of the primary outcome across groups (Fig. 2). Patients with higher LHR exhibited significantly higher in-hospital mortality at 30, 120, and 360 days (all log-rank P < .0001). Variables plausibly associated with outcomes, selected on the basis of clinical guidelines and expert consensus, were included as covariates in the Cox regression models (Table 2). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), each unit increase in LHR was associated with an HR of 78.07 (95% CI, 65.03–93.72; P < .001). After adjustment for age and body weight (Model 2), the HR increased to 89.39 (95% CI, 74.41–107.37; P < .001), whereas in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), the HR was attenuated but remained significant (HR, 12.38; 95% CI, 8.83–17.37; P < .001). When comparing the highest with the lowest LHR tertile, the HR for 30-day mortality was 3.075 (95% CI, 2.815–3.360; P < .001) in Model 1, 3.017 (95% CI, 2.761–3.297; P < .001) in Model 2, and 1.617 (95% CI, 1.453–1.799; P < .001) in Model 3. Similar patterns were observed for 120-day and 360-day mortality, with LHR retaining a statistically significant, although modestly attenuated, association across all models (Table 2). These findings underscore the prognostic value of LHR as a robust indicator of both short-term and long-term mortality in critically ill patients with respiratory failure. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a nonlinear increase in short-term and long-term mortality risk with increasing LHR (nonlinearity P < .001 for 30-, 120-, and 360-day mortality, respectively) (Fig. 3).

3.3. Subgroup analysis

Further subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint demonstrated that LHR retained significant prognostic value for both short-term and long-term mortality across multiple patient subgroups, including sex, ethnicity, hypertension, T2DM, heart failure, and COPD. For 30-day mortality, the prognostic effect of LHR differed across subgroups, with stronger associations observed in patients with COPD and in those of White ethnicity. Specifically, for COPD status, the HR for 30-day mortality was 12.20 (95% CI, 8.47–17.59) in patients without COPD and 13.16 (95% CI, 4.36–39.70) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .034). Similarly, LHR showed a stronger association with 30-day mortality in White patients (HR, 14.10; 95% CI, 8.50–23.07) compared with Black patients (HR, 8.63; 95% CI, 2.81–26.50) and patients of other ethnicities (HR, 10.64; 95% CI, 6.12–18.49) (interaction P = .041; Figure 4). For 120-day and 360-day mortality, the prognostic association between LHR and mortality was consistently stronger in patients with COPD. For 120-day mortality, the HR was 10.77 (95% CI, 7.56–15.35) in patients without COPD and 14.33 (95% CI, 5.00–41.10) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .01). For 360-day mortality, corresponding HRs were 10.66 (95% CI, 7.50–15.17) in patients without COPD and 15.09 (95% CI, 5.30–42.92) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .01; Figures 5 and 6). Notably, the subgroup analyses, particularly the observation in patients with COPD, should be interpreted with caution. These analyses were exploratory and aimed to assess consistency across subgroups rather than to provide confirmatory evidence of subgroup-specific effects.

4. Discussion

We examined the association between admission LHR and outcomes in a U.S. ICU cohort of patients with respiratory failure. Higher LHR was associated with increased short-term and long-term mortality, and this association persisted after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that LHR provides independent prognostic information in this high-risk population.

Previous studies have suggested that lactate and hematocrit are prognostic indicators of mortality in critically injured populations [13]; however, the prognostic value of LHR in critically ill patients with respiratory failure has not been reported. Lactate is an important marker of metabolic status and is widely used in the management of critical illness and trauma.[16] Prior studies have shown that elevated lactate levels are associated with the pathophysiology of respiratory diseases.[17] However, lactate alone may be insufficient for prognostic assessment because levels are influenced by multiple patient- and treatment-related factors. These factors include commonly used medications, such as metformin and salbutamol, which may alter lactate levels. Hepatic function also affects lactate clearance. Moreover, clinically severe illness may be present despite normal venous lactate levels. Collectively, these factors may lead to misclassification of risk during prognostic assessment.[18] Hematocrit is also commonly used to assess hemodilution and RBC volume status. However, chronic comorbidities and clinical interventions can substantially influence hematocrit, thereby weakening its correlation with true RBC volume. Therefore, we evaluated LHR as a composite biomarker to improve prognostic assessment in patients with respiratory failure.

Demir et al[13] reported that LHR predicted in-hospital mortality in critically injured patients more accurately than lactate alone, and Staziaki et al[19] showed that the combination of elevated lactate and low hematocrit was associated with a higher risk in trauma settings. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of lactate and hematocrit in reflecting disease severity. Accordingly, LHR may facilitate more precise risk stratification in critically ill patients with respiratory failure and inform clinical decision-making.

Furthermore, subgroup-based risk stratification analyses were performed. Subgroup analyses showed that LHR consistently predicted 30-day, 120-day, and 360-day mortality in both male and female patients. No significant association was observed between LHR and short-term or long-term all-cause mortality among patients with baseline T2DM, heart failure, or hypertension. This finding may partly reflect reverse causality, as patients with these comorbidities are more likely to receive standardized treatment and closer clinical management. Consequently, their prognosis may be partially mitigated despite an intrinsically elevated baseline mortality risk. In addition, the prognostic association between LHR and 30-day mortality differed across COPD status and ethnic subgroups. Specifically, for COPD status, the HR for 30-day mortality was 12.20 (95% CI, 8.47–17.59) in patients without COPD and 13.16 (95% CI, 4.36–39.70) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .034). Similarly, LHR showed a stronger association with 30-day mortality in White patients (HR, 14.10; 95% CI, 8.50–23.07) than in Black patients (HR, 8.63; 95% CI, 2.81–26.50) or patients of other ethnicities (HR, 10.64; 95% CI, 6.12–18.49) (interaction P = .041). For 120-day and 360-day mortality, the prognostic association between LHR and mortality was consistently stronger in patients with COPD. For 120-day mortality, the HR was 10.77 (95% CI, 7.56–15.35) in patients without COPD and 14.33 (95% CI, 5.00–41.10) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .01). For 360-day mortality, corresponding HRs were 10.66 (95% CI, 7.50–15.17) in patients without COPD and 15.09 (95% CI, 5.30–42.92) in patients with COPD (interaction P = .01). These findings suggest that COPD-related disease management may modify the prognostic performance of LHR for all-cause mortality. This heterogeneity may reflect the chronic nature of COPD, whereby patients frequently receive standardized treatment and develop effective long-term management strategies. Observed ethnic differences may also reflect unmeasured genetic, socioeconomic, or healthcare-related factors. Previous studies have also reported relatively well-established approaches to COPD treatment and control.[20–22] Overall, our analyses demonstrated that LHR was robustly associated with both short-term and long-term mortality, highlighting its potential utility in identifying ICU patients with respiratory failure at the highest risk of death. Accordingly, maintaining appropriate lactate and hematocrit levels and avoiding excessive elevations in LHR may be clinically relevant for mitigating adverse outcomes. Collectively, these findings indicate that LHR should be considered not as a standalone diagnostic index but as an adjunctive metric that, when integrated with other clinical and laboratory variables, enables a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic status and supports risk stratification in routine clinical practice.

A key strength of this study is the confirmation, in a U.S. ICU cohort, that an elevated lactate-to-hematocrit ratio (LHR) is independently associated with higher mortality among mechanically ventilated patients. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference; despite multivariable adjustment and stratified analyses, residual confounding may still bias the estimated associations. Moreover, several key clinical variables were unavailable in the database and could not be accounted for, including detailed respiratory failure subtypes (e.g., hypoxemic vs hypercapnic respiratory failure), treatment-related factors such as ventilation strategies, and causes of death. These unmeasured factors may have influenced patient outcomes and potentially modified the observed associations. In addition, only baseline LHR was analyzed, as time-varying measurements during hospitalization were not consistently available. Therefore, the prognostic value of longitudinal changes in LHR should be evaluated in future studies.

5. Conclusion

In summary, our findings extend the potential application of LHR to critically ill patients with respiratory failure and support its use as a tool for risk stratification of short- and long-term mortality after ICU admission. Monitoring the LHR may support clinical risk assessment and patient management; however, further prospective studies are needed to validate its prognostic utility and to clarify its role within comprehensive risk prediction frameworks.
