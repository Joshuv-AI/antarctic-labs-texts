Uptake of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances in Mixed Forages on Biosolid-Amended Farm Fields
Authors: Thomas L Simones, Chris Evans, Caleb P Goossen, Richard Kersbergen, Ellen B Mallory, Susan Genualdi, Wendy Young, Andrew E Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractModels to predict perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) concentrations in livestock based on soil concentrations are essential to guide decisions surrounding food testing and farm management. A key parameter in modeling soil-to-livestock exposure pathways is the plant transfer factor (TF) from soil into forages. Uptake of PFOS and other individual per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) were examined in perennial mixed grasses and legumes on PFAS-contaminated farm fields. In a field plot study, PFOS TFs were similar within each plot over three consecutive years but varied 10-fold among the four plots with mean TFs ranging from 0.026 to 0.27. In a multifarm field survey study, mean PFOS TFs ranged from 0.039 to 0.37. Increasing concentrations of two PFOS precursors in soil were significantly associated with increasing PFOS TFs. These data represent a substantial increase in empirical observations of PFAS TFs for grass-based forages for use in modeling soil-to-livestock exposure scenarios.Keywords: PFOS, PFAS, forage, hay, transfer factor, plant uptake

Introduction

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) are a class of chemicals with widespread use in industrial processes, food packaging, and various consumer products. This has led to the detection of PFAS in various waste streams including biosolids with several studies reporting high levels of PFAS in biosolids.1−4 Due to the long-term practice of land application of biosolids as a soil amendment, PFAS have been introduced to farmlands across the USA.5,6 The 2001 U.S. EPA National Sewage Sludge Survey estimated that the load of 13 measured PFAS in U.S. biosolids applied to agriculture land was 1,375 to 2,070 kg per year.4

The State of Maine has a decades-long history of applying biosolids to agricultural land. This, unfortunately, led to PFAS soil contamination at several active farms in the state, and spurred soil testing for PFAS on all fields with known historical land application of biosolids or other waste residuals in the state.7 PFAS can readily move through the environment and enter the food chain.8,9 Since 2016, the State of Maine has identified several impacted dairy farms with perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS)-contaminated milk ranging from 45 to >20,000 nanograms/liter (ng/L).10,11 Despite the presence of a number of PFAS at elevated levels in soils, the primary PFAS detected in cow’s milk in Maine has been (PFOS).10,11 Sampling of soils from the farms’ fields used to grow forage for these dairy herds found field-wide average PFOS concentrations ranging from 29 to 1080 nanograms/gram (ng/g) on a dry weight (dw) basis and PFOS concentrations in collected hay samples ranged from 9.6 to 40 ng/g dw basis.11 Models that relate soil PFOS levels to livestock forage crops and ultimately dairy and beef animals have been developed.12−14 A key input into these models is a transfer factor (TF) for PFOS (also referred to as a bioaccumulation factor or bioconcentration factor) from soil into plant material used as feed, e.g., grass-based forages. There is a dearth of studies on PFAS uptake into grass-based forages from soils contaminated with PFAS from the land application of biosolids. Yoo and colleagues measured PFOS and other PFAS in soil and colocated grasses from several biosolid-spread farm fields in Decatur, Alabama.15 Several other studies have measured uptake in grasses from soils contaminated by aqueous film forming foam (AFFF) or spiked PFAS in potted plant studies.14,16−18 These studies report PFOS TFs ranging from 0.015 to 0.4.

In this study, our primary objective was to collect soil and grass-based forages at PFAS-impacted farms in Maine to better assess uptake of PFOS from soil in perennial grass-based forage crops from fields with a history of biosolids application. Our focus on PFOS was motivated by the observation that it is the predominate PFAS contaminating livestock food products at farms with a history of biosolids application. In recognition of the lack of published literature regarding the uptake of other PFAS into grass-based forages on biosolid amended agricultural fields, a secondary objective was to calculate TFs for additional PFAS to the extent feasible based on detections in soils and plant tissue. A more robust estimate of the PFOS TF from soil to forages is needed to help refine modeling efforts of soil-to-forage-to-cow’s milk and beef exposure pathways used to establish soil screening levels, and to provide information to farmers to make management decisions to limit the risk of PFOS contamination in milk or beef tissue.

Methods

Study Design

PFAS uptake into mixed, perennial grass and legume swards, hereafter referred to as “sward”, were investigated in a plot study at a single farm (farm A) with replicate sampling over three years, and in a broader field survey study across different farms (farms B, C, D, and E) sampled once at a single time point. In both studies, sward composition was dominated by grasses with a minor alfalfa and/or clover component (Table 1). Most of the fields sampled were utilized for hay/silage production, with a small portion of the fields or areas of fields used for pasture grazing.

The plot study was set up in 2021 at farm A, a dairy farm with a known history of biosolids application. Four study plots were set up in different fields on the farm with previous field-wide composite soil PFOS concentrations ranging from 44 to 232 ng/g dw. The fields provided forage for the dairy operation with most swards established approximately 5 to 6 years before 2021. Individual plots in each field were 30.5 × 4.6 m2 in size. Co-located soil and sward (soil/sward) samples in the four study plots were initially collected in June of 2021, with sampling repeated in June of 2022 and June of 2023. During each sampling event, soil/sward samples were collected in triplicate in each plot. In year one, the three soil/sward samples were collected across the 30.5 × 4.6 m2 plot area. In years two and three, all triplicate soil/sward samples in each plot were collected within a 7.6 × 4.6 m2 subsection of the original plot as the remaining area was reseeded to monospecific plantings of grass and legume species for future studies. In all three years, the soil/sward samples were collected approximately at the date of the farmer’s standard practice of first crop hay harvest, and all four plots were sampled on the same day.

In the second and third years of the plot study, plots were mowed and cut sward raked off the plot areas after the first sample collection to allow the plots to regrow for evaluation of PFAS uptake in a second cutting of sward. Here the second cutting refers to sampling of the aftermath forage growth following the mowing of the plots. On September 21, 2022, 91 days after the first sample collection and 68 days after the mowing and raking, a second set of triplicate soil/sward samples in each plot were collected of the aftermath growth. The 2022 second cut sampling took place shortly after a mechanical seeding in adjacent areas of the plots and in plot A-2, it was noted during the sward sample collection that there was visible dust on the above-ground portion of the plant. In 2023, on August 28, 75 days after the first sampling and 59 days after the mowing, a second set of triplicate soil/sward samples were collected in each plot.

The broader field survey study was conducted on four farms (B, C, D, and E) each with confirmed histories of biosolids application, previous site characterization and field-wide composite soil sampling that found elevated soil PFAS concentrations. The intent of the field survey study was to investigate PFAS uptake into grass-based forages across several locations in the state with concomitantly varying soil PFAS concentrations, biosolid sources, and other soil features. Where sampling took place, each of the four farms had fields maintained for grass-based forage production. Replicate soil/sward samples collected from a particular field were located between 10 and 130 m apart. Apart from one field, at the time of sampling, the fields had not been cut or harvested previously that year, and sampling represented harvest of first growth, referred to here as “first cut”. In 2023, the June sampling at farm B took place several months after the field had been mowed with a rotary mower. Table 1 provides the sampling dates and predominant types of plants sampled in the two studies. All farms were accessed with landowner permission.

Co-Located Soil and Sward Sample Collection

The same soil and sward collection method was employed to collect soil/sward samples for both the plot study and field survey study. Individual colocated soil and sward samples were collected from within a 30.5 × 30.5 cm2 quadrat in all instances. Sward samples were collected from the entire quadrat, cut approximately 5 to 8 cm above the ground with stainless steel scissors, inspected for any visible signs of soil adherence to the surface of the plants, and placed in Ziploc or Hefty brand polyethylene bag. Immediately afterward, three separate soil core samples were collected from the quadrat at a depth of 0 to 15 or 20 cm, using a 5 cm diameter stainless-steel hammer probe (AMS, ID). The three soil cores were hand-mixed in a stainless-steel bowl to create a composite sample, and visible rocks, roots, and biota were removed. Subsamples were collected from each composited soil sample for targeted PFAS analysis and total solids determination and for soil fertility analysis. In the field, both soil and sward samples for PFAS analysis were placed in coolers with ice immediately following collection.

All field sampling followed the Maine Department of Environmental Protection (Maine DEP) PFAS sampling standard operating procedure.19 Briefly, nitrile gloves were worn by all samplers when collecting and handling soil and sward samples. Sampling equipment that was reused in the field between replicate sample collection was wiped down or rinsed initially with laboratory-provided PFAS-free water or retail bottled water, washed with 1% Liquinox soap, rinsed with laboratory-provided PFAS-free water, and wiped dry with a clean paper towel in the field.

Soil and Sward PFAS Measurement

Commercial laboratories performed PFAS quantification of all soil and sward samples. Soil samples collected from farm B in 2020 for the field survey study were sent to Battelle (Norwell, MA, USA) for targeted PFAS analysis with their DoD QSM 5.3 Table B-15 isotope dilution method for 28 PFAS. All other soil samples were sent to Pace (Westborough, MA, USA) for targeted analysis of 28 PFAS using their laboratory-defined isotope dilution method. Method summaries are provided in the Supporting Information. All PFAS soil results were reported on a ng/g dw basis. Battelle was the primary lab for preparation and PFAS analysis of sward samples. The entire contents of individual bagged sward samples were cryoground to obtain a homogeneous sward sample mixture. Targeted PFAS analysis for 28 PFAS was performed with subsamples from the cryoground homogenate using Battelle’s DoD QSM 5.3 Table B-15 isotope dilution method. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition, College Park, MD) laboratory quantified PFAS in sward samples from the third year of sampling in 2023 in the plot study. The third-year sward samples were ground at the FDA laboratory using a Cryo-blade cryogenic grinder from Cole Palmer (Vernon Hills, IL, USA). The FDA laboratory used an isotope dilution method developed for their total diet study survey analysis (C-010.02).20,21PFAS measured in sward samples were reported on ng/g wet weight (ww) basis. Battelle provided percent moisture measurement for all individual sward samples, except for the third-year plot study sward samples in which moisture content was determined by Dairy One (Ithaca, NY, USA) from a subsample of the plant material sent for forage analysis. Method detection limits and reporting limits for targeted PFAS in soil (ng/g dw) and sward (ng/g ww) are provided in Table S1.

In the absence of established and interlaboratory validated methods for targeted PFAS analysis in plant material, an initial set of sward samples was analyzed by both Battelle and the FDA laboratory for an interlaboratory comparison. There was good agreement between the two laboratories for the targeted PFAS measured in the shared homogenate of sward samples regardless of which laboratory performed the cryogrinding. The details of the interlaboratory study are presented in the Supporting Information.

Quality Control and Quality Assurance

In addition to the interlaboratory comparisons, field blanks and equipment blanks were collected during sampling events with laboratory-provided PFAS-free water.19 Laboratories also ran a lab blank with each sampling batch. PFAS concentrations in the majority of the field and equipment blank samples were below the laboratory limit of detection (LOD). When individual PFAS were detected in field blanks the concentrations were <1.3 ng/L and when detected in equipment blanks <5 ng/L. For the laboratory blanks, nearly all blanks had PFAS concentrations reported as < LOD. Each laboratory provided percent surrogate recovery for PFAS with surrogate standards. Results of the mean and standard deviation of % recovery are provided in Table S4.

Soil Fertility Measurement

The Maine Soil Testing Service (Orono, ME, USA) at the University of Maine measured percent organic matter, pH, and cation exchange capacity on a subset of soil samples. Organic matter contents were determined by the loss on ignition method, pH was measured in water and Mehlich buffer, available nutrients were extracted with a Modified Morgan extract and measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectroscopy.22

Statistical Analysis

The PFOS TFs from the three years of sampling in the plot study were compared across study years by one-way ANOVA and a nonparametric rank sum test to determine if the TFs were significantly different within plots between the study years. A mixed model controlling for plots as a class variable with repeated measures for “year” and “cutting” was used to assess whether the PFOS TFs varied by first and second cuttings in the plot study. Bivariate linear mixed model regression between PFOS TF and PFOS soil concentration, perfluorooctanesulfonamide (PFOSA) and 2-(N- ethylperfluorooctanesulfonamido) acetic acid (NEtFOSAA) soil concentrations, and several soil fertility parameters (percent organic matter, cation exchange capacity, and soil pH) was conducted using a linear mixed model. Except for soil pH, soil PFAS concentrations and soil fertility parameters were natural log (ln) transformed for regression analyses. Initially, both individual farms and fields/plots were used as class and random effect variables to control for nonindependent clustering, “farm” and “field” terms, respectively. Farm was a nonsignificant (p > 0.05) random term in all bivariate regressions and was removed for the final analysis. Field was a significant (p < 0.05) random term in all bivariate regressions and was retained as a random effects term. In the bivariate linear regression analyses the second cut sample results were not included. For soil samples with PFOSA or NEtFOSAA concentrations below the method detection limit (MDL), a multiple imputation method was utilized to generate imputed values for the bivariate plots and regression analyses.23 Additional regression analyses were performed using 1/2 the MDL as imputed PFOSA or NEtFOSAA soil concentrations reported as < MDL (Table S8). Descriptive statistics and regression analyses were conducted in SAS (version 9.4) with a p value <0.05 as a significance level. The multiple imputation method was implemented in R statistical software (version R 4.4.1).

Results

PFAS Soil Profiles

The soil PFAS results for colocated soil/sward samples from the four plots in the plot study and the 11 fields in the field survey study are summarized in Figure 1 and in greater detail in Table S5. Briefly, PFOS was detected in 100% of the soil samples and was the predominate PFAS found in soil samples from all plots and fields apart from farm C, where 8:2 fluorotelomer sulfonic acid (8:2 FTS) was the most abundant PFAS, and plot A-4 where NEtFOSAA was found in higher concentrations in sampling years one and three (Figure 1). In the first year of sampling of the plot study, mean soil PFOS concentrations of the plot replicates differed less than 3-fold between the four plots with a range of 66 ng/g dw to 153 ng/g dw with similar ranges in year two and year three samplings (Table 2). Across the fields sampled in the farm field survey study mean PFOS soil concentrations varied across fields by more than 200-fold, with the lowest mean concentration of 3.0 ng/g dw and the highest at 769.3 ng/g dw. Overall, soil PFOS concentrations in individual colocated soil/sward samples ranged from 2.7 to 1040 ng/g dw among all the plots and fields sampled (Figure S2). For three of the four plots at farm A and the two fields from farm B, the next most abundant PFAS were the PFOS precursors NEtFOSAA and PFOSA followed by perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA) and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnA). PFOS Sward TFs

Mean PFOS concentrations for the soil and sward samples and calculated TFs from the plot study are summarized in Table 2 and in greater detail in Tables S5–S7. Mean sward concentrations of the plot replicates differed 10-fold between the four plots, ranging from 2.8 ng/g dw to 36.7 ng/g dw. This resulted in calculated PFOS TFs that differed 10-fold between the four plots from 0.02 to 0.4 (Table 2). The PFOS TFs were similar between plots A-1 and A-2 but higher in plots A-3 and A-4. This finding of variable PFOS TFs between the plots was confirmed with similar results from the second and third years of sampling (Table 2). Although the PFOS TFs for the plots tended to be higher in the third year of sampling, across the three sampling years, there was no statistically significant difference between PFOS TFs within each plot. Combining the three years, mean and standard deviation PFOS TF for plots A-1, A-2, A-3, and A-4 were 0.026 ± 0.011, 0.056 ± 0.033, 0.27 ± 0.095, and 0.26 ± 0.15, respectively.

Mean PFOS soil and sward concentrations and TFs from the field survey study are summarized in Table 3 and in greater detail in Tables S5–S7. The mean sward PFOS concentrations from the colocated replicates varied by more than 600-fold between farm fields, ranging from a low of 0.3 ng/g dw on farm C to a high of 192.5 ng/g dw on farm B. Similar to the plot study results, PFOS TFs from the field survey varied nearly 10-fold, with a mean range by field of 0.039 to 0.37 (Table 3). The combined mean and standard deviation for PFOS TFs across all plot study and field survey study results was 0.13 ± 0.10 (n = 15) with an 95% upper confidence limit of 0.17.

Exploration of Variation in PFOS TFs

Several linear regression analyses were performed to examine possible explanatory variables for the nearly 10-fold variation in PFOS TFs among sampled plots and fields. Percent soil organic matter in soil samples ranged from 3.0 to 9.6, cation exchange capacity ranged from 7.2 mequiv/100 g to 15.9 mequiv/100 g, and soil pH ranged 5.9 to 7.7 (Figure S2). When above the limit of detection, PFOSA and NEtFOSAA soil concentrations in individual colocated soil/sward samples ranged from 0.6 to 113 ng/g dw and 0.5 to 421 ng/g dw, respectively. In a linear mixed model, the bivariate relationships between PFOS TF and ln transformed percent organic matter, ln transformed cation exchange capacity, or soil pH did not reach statistical significance (Table 4). There also was no significant relationship between ln transformed PFOS soil concentrations and PFOS TF (p value = 0.99), however, there was a significant association between PFOS TF and ln transformed PFOSA and NEtFOSAA soil concentrations with p values <0.05 (Table 4).

PFOS TFs by Cutting

Second cut sampling, performed in years two and three of the plot study found PFOS TFs were generally higher in the aftermath growth (second cut) as compared with the initial growth (first cut) (Figure 2). In the second year of the plot study, the mean PFOS TF was approximately 2- to 4-fold higher in the second cut colocated soil/sward samples in three of the four plots as compared with the first cut samples. Similar results were observed in the third year between the first and second cut samplings (Figure 2). Overall, across the two years, PFOS TFs in the second cut were significantly higher than those at first cut (p < 0.032).

TFs for Other PFAS

The presence of other PFAS in soils and sward allowed for TF calculation of 16 other PFAS (Figure 3). For PFAS with measurable concentrations in both soil and sward, PFAS TFs decreased with increasing chain length for perfluorocarboxylic acids (PFCAs) (Figure 3). Perfluorosulfonic acids (PFSAs) followed a similar trend with TFs for perfluorobutanesulfonic acid (PFBS), perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), and perfluoroheptanesulfonic acid (PFHpS) greater than PFOS. Other than PFOS, PFSAs were not detected frequently in both soil and sward samples. The PFOS precursor PFOSA, while found in several soil samples, was only detectable in sward samples from farm B, which had the highest soil concentrations, and from plot A-4 in the third year of sampling and resulted in TFs of around 0.01 (Figure 3, Table S7). NEtFOSAA was only detected in sward samples from farm B with higher soil concentrations and demonstrated the lowest PFAS TF of <0.01. The fluorotelomer sulfonates 6:2 FTS and 8:2 FTS were taken up by sward samples mostly from farms B and C (Figure 3, Table S7). Discussion

Our study’s findings represent a major expansion of the available data on PFOS uptake by grass-based forage crops in a field setting on soils amended with biosolids and provides additional data on the uptake of other PFAS. To our knowledge the Yoo et al. 201115 study is currently the only other study in the published scientific literature to report PFAS uptake in forage plants in situ on biosolid-amended farm fields. Yoo and colleagues found average PFOS TFs in grass samples of Kentucky blue grass, tall fescue, and Bermuda grass ranging from 0.035 to 0.13 from fields with a known history of biosolids application in Alabama, USA.15 The mean and standard deviation for PFOS TF across all biosolid-impacted fields in the Yoo and colleagues study was 0.07 ± 0.04. We found greater variation in PFOS TFs at the field level (0.03 to 0.4) as well as at the farm level (0.06 to 0.3). The overall mean and standard deviation across all farms and fields for our study was 0.13 ± 0.10 which was higher than that reported by Yoo et al. in 2011.15 At an AFFF-impacted farm site in Australia, Mikkonen et al. in 202314 reported an overall mean PFOS TF of 0.03 with mean values ranging from 0.015 to 0.06 for paired soil/grass samples from grazed pastures.

Our PFOS TFs are generally consistent with observations from several potted plant studies that have grown perennial grasses in either contaminated soils removed from a site or uncontaminated soil spiked with PFAS.16−18 In a potted plant study with well-mixed soils obtained from biosolids-amended fields at an agricultural experimental site in China, Wen and colleagues reported a PFOS TF from soil-to-ryegrass shoots of 0.18 with mean soil PFOS values of 154 ng/g dw.18 In a potted plant study, Stahl and colleagues grew ryegrass in spiked soils with increasing concentrations of 250, 1,000, 10,000, 25,000, and 50,000 ng/g dw of PFOS along with PFOA.17 While the study authors did not calculate soil-to-grass TFs, they provided the grass and soil concentrations on a dw basis allowing calculation of a TF value. Calculated PFOS TFs varied by soil concentration with TFs of 0.048, 0.12, 0.076, 0.16, and 0.23 in the 250, 1,000, 10,000, 25,000 and 50,000 ng/g dw soils, respectively, in the first cutting of the ryegrass. The average PFOS TF across these five PFOS soil concentrations is 0.13. The average PFOS TFs from the Wen et al. and Stahl et al. potted plant studies are remarkably similar, given the differences in study type and PFAS sources. Bräunig et al. in 201916 grew wheat grass in soils that were contaminated by AFFF from a fire training site and found PFOS TFs of 0.2 and 0.4 in two soils containing an average PFOS concentration of 13,400 ng/g dw or 2,200 ng/g dw, respectively. These PFOS TFs are higher than the average PFOS TF of 0.13 across all fields in our study but are within the upper range of observed TFs from individual fields.

The factors underlying the nearly 10-fold variation in PFOS TFs for grass-based forages collected from fields on five different farms in different areas of the State are unclear. Soil properties such as organic matter, cation exchange capacity, and pH have been reported to play a role in PFAS soil sorption and influence uptake into plants, particularly organic matter which may serve as a reservoir for the more hydrophobic PFAS.24−28 Yet we saw little evidence that PFOS TFs were strongly influenced by these three soil properties individually over the ranges present in these soils. While not reaching statistical significance, we did observe the expected trend of decreasing PFOS TFs with increasing percent organic matter. The variation in PFOS TF appears to be independent of PFOS soil concentration as there was no significant relationship between PFOS TFs and ln transformed PFOS soil concentrations despite soil PFOS ranging from 3 to just over 1000 ng/g dw across all the fields sampled. The body of literature on PFOS plant uptake from soils largely supports this finding of no trend between soil PFOS concentrations and PFOS TFs.26,28 Notably, we observed higher PFOS TFs of >0.2 when there were higher, >10 ng/g dw, PFOSA and NEtFOSAA soil concentrations. We also found a positive association between PFOS TFs and PFOSA and NEtFOSAA soil concentrations in bivariate regression analyses. Albeit a weakness in assessing this relationship is the presence of soil concentrations reported as < MDL but still having a measurable PFOS TF. There is published evidence that higher soil levels of the PFOS precursors PFOSA and NEtFOSAA can contribute to higher PFOS TFs.29,30 Wen et al. (2018)30 in a potted plant study with biosolid-amended soils spiked with NEtFOSAA, found PFOSAA, PFOSA, and PFOS concentrations increased in ryegrass samples over a 60-day growth period. As we did not observe any relationship between soil PFOS concentrations and PFOS TF, the observed association for soil NEtFOSAA or PFOSA and PFOS TF may indicate that precursor transformation to PFOS is occurring within the plant tissue or soil/root interface. We found little uptake of PFOSA and NEtFOSAA into above ground plant tissue, but we did not sample roots where there may be the presence of these precursors.30 Overall, further study is warranted to better understand the presence of PFOS precursors in soil and their impact on PFOS uptake in grass-based forages.

Our results represent uptake of PFAS by mixed forage species in mature perennial forage crops dominated by grasses (timothy, tall fescue, orchardgrass, and Kentucky bluegrass) with varying amounts of legumes such as alfalfa and clover. It is possible that differences in the botanical composition of the sward samples may underlie some of the differences in PFOS TFs reported in our study as well as differences with other field studies measuring PFAS plant uptake.14,15 In our plot study we found a similar and reproducible 10-fold variation in PFOS TFs among the four study plots three of which were dominated by timothy. The one plot that was dominated by tall fescue, A-3, had similarly high PFOS TFs as plot A-4 where timothy was the predominate grass. The difference in plant species would likely not explain the variation in PFOS TFs among the plots, at least for the plot study and the types of sward sampled. To minimize potential weather-related impacts and timing of harvest within a field season, all four study plots were always sampled on the same day. There were notable differences in rainfall amounts31 among the three years the plots were sampled but there was no statistically significant difference in PFOS TFs comparing across years within the same plot. Exterior soil contamination is likely not a substantial factor driving the observed variability as soil PFOS concentrations in the plot study are similar between some plots with the lowest and highest PFOS TF and we found no dependence of PFOS TF with ln soil PFOS concentration. Additionally, our method of collecting sward samples by hand avoided soil disturbance.

It is plausible that the explanation for the observed variation in PFOS TFs is multifactorial. Our regression analyses were not multivariable and did not attempt to account for covariance between soil properties or interactions between soil concentration of PFOS precursors such as PFOSA and NEtFOSAA. Further analyses are underway using more robust multivariable statistical modeling methods.

The PFOS TFs in this study largely represent TFs for a first cutting, primary growth of hay. In the plot study, our findings from a second cut sampling in years two and three of our multiyear study suggest there is higher PFOS uptake in aftermath growth than in the initial growth for the season. There is mixed evidence in the literature of potential higher uptake in subsequent cuttings of grass.17,32,33 In the potted plant study by Stahl and colleagues, the mean and standard deviation PFOS TF across the five PFOS soil concentrations was 0.13 ± 0.073 for the first cut, 0.52 ± 0.26 for the second cut, 0.23 ± 0.13 for the third cut, and 1.43 ± 1.62 for the fourth cut.17 In a report on a field plant uptake study conducted in Germany there was minimal evidence of higher uptake in a second cutting of ryegrass.32,33 This study obtained PFAS-contaminated soils from an agricultural area where the PFAS source was the application of a contaminated industrial fertilizer. The factors responsible for higher uptake in a second cutting of grass in our study are currently unclear. There are known changes in forage quality in aftermath growth.28,34 The leaf-to-stem/pseudostem ratio is typically higher in aftermath growth following a cutting and PFAS accumulation in plants tend to follow a leaf > stem pattern.26−28 If there is a greater proportion of leaf in a second cutting this could underlie the higher observed TF in the second cut samples. As uptake into the above ground portion of plant is likely dependent upon transpirational flow,26 changes in the transpiration rate with potentially higher rates during warmer months following the first cut may also help explain the finding of higher PFOS TFs in the aftermath growth samples. Changes in root structure after a cutting may also impact uptake.28 In our study, the observation of visible soil dust on plant material from one of the four plots (A-2) when the second cut samples were collected in 2022 field season may in part have contributed to the higher PFOS TFs. However, visible soil dust was not noticeable on plant material with any other sward samples so this seems unlikely to be an explanation of our observed difference between cuts. More study is warranted to determine if there is a consistent increase in PFOS and other PFAS uptake in grasses and legumes following sequential harvests on farm fields.

The presence of additional PFAS measured in both soil and sward allowed for TF calculations for other PFAS. PFAS concentrations in the sward samples from the four plots and other farm fields generally followed what has been observed in other studies measuring soil and plant PFAS with chain length-dependent uptake.26−28 For PFCAs, uptake decreased with increasing chain length. Generally, the TFs for PFCAs with 7 carbons and fewer were above 1. The TFs for PFOA were close to 1 or below and then decreased with increasing carbons to PFUnA with TF < 0.1. This observation of decreasing PFAS TFs with increasing chain length is likely the result of increased soil sorption with increasing PFAS chain length and increased mobility with decreasing chain length within the plant.26−28 Apart from PFOS, PFSAs were detected less frequently than PFCAs in both soil and sward from farm fields with a history of land application of biosolids and it is difficult to draw a firm conclusion on whether PFCAs had higher uptake than PFSAs with the same chain length. When measurable in both soil and sward samples PFSAs did display lower mean TFs as compared to PFCAs with the same number of fully fluorinated carbons. Higher uptake of PFCAs has been observed in a number of other plant uptake studies likely due to the more hydrophobic nature of PFSAs and stronger binding to soil.26−28

A primary aim of our study was to better assess uptake of PFOS from soil by perennial grass-based forage crops from fields with a history of biosolids application for use in modeling to develop soil screening levels for a soil to forage crop to livestock exposure scenario. The substantial variation in PFOS plant TFs reported here suggests the need for a site-specific assessment of PFOS plant TFs when soil screening levels for livestock food products are exceeded. Furthermore, our reported variation in PFOS plant TFs among fields on the same farm coupled with the observation that a second cutting of a grass-based forage crop may have considerably higher TFs than the first cut highlights the importance of dynamic models of PFOS exposure in beef cattle proposed by Mikkonen et al. in 2023.13,14 These dynamic models relating water and soil levels to livestock tissue levels can account for both spatial and temporal variation in forage PFOS levels coupled with a toxicokinetic model that can incorporate both accumulation and depuration periods resulting from these potential significant changes in exposure.
