Arthroscopic Remplissage With Knotless All‐Suture Anchors and Concomitant Bankart Repair: Technical Considerations
Authors: Michael Nocek, Lorenz Fritsch, Colby C Wollenman, Ayham Jaber, Alan D Villegas Meza, Katelyn L Kirwood, Peter J Millett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior shoulder instability is common in active patients. Traumatic dislocations may produce Bankart and Hill‐Sachs lesions that increase recurrence risk. In high‐demand individuals, isolated Bankart repair may be insufficient for off‐track or near‐track Hill‐Sachs lesions. We describe arthroscopic Bankart repair with knotless all‐suture anchors in the beach‐chair position with adjunct knotless remplissage for a near‐track Hill‐Sachs lesion. This reproducible technique restores stability with minimal morbidity.

Anterior shoulder dislocations account for most glenohumeral dislocations and frequently affect young, active individuals. 1 Recurrent instability is driven by a combination of soft‐tissue injury (Bankart lesion) and posterolateral humeral head bone loss (Hill‐Sachs lesion). 2

Arthroscopic Bankart repair is an effective option in active patients after first‐time traumatic dislocation, 3 but isolated repair may be insufficient in the setting of high‐risk Hill–Sachs lesions. 4 , 5 , 6 Remplissage has become a reliable adjunct to Bankart repair in this population. 7

Here, we describe the senior author's technique for arthroscopic Bankart repair using knotless all‐suture anchors combined with arthroscopic remplissage using a knotless linked construct (Video 1).

Arthroscopic Bankart repair with knotless all‐suture anchors and staged remplissage in a high‐demand 40‐year‐old firefighter with anterior shoulder instability and a borderline on‐track (near‐track) Hill–Sachs lesion (18 × 23 × 5 mm). The right shoulder is positioned in the beach‐chair position, with a posterior portal used for viewing and instrumentation and anterosuperior and anteroinferior portals serving as additional working portals. The video shows diagnostic arthroscopy, debridement of the Hill–Sachs defect, posterior placement of two humeral‐sided knotless anchors through the infraspinatus, capsulolabral repair with capsular shift using multiple all‐suture anchors, and delayed tensioning of the linked remplissage construct after completion of the Bankart repair. Key teaching points include portal placement, management of the anteroinferior capsulolabral complex, and anchor trajectory and tensioning to optimize stability while minimizing soft‐tissue disruption. SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Evaluation

Preoperative assessment focuses on recurrence risk factors (age, sport, hyperlaxity, number of prior dislocations) and imaging of bone and soft‐tissue pathology. 4 , 8 Radiographs and magnetic resonance iamging are obtained to evaluate the capsulolabral complex, rotator cuff, and humeral head defect. 9 Glenoid track concepts are applied to classify Hill–Sachs lesions as on‐ or off‐track and guide the need for remplissage or bone augmentation. 10 , 11

The patient is positioned in the beach‐chair position after an interscalene nerve block and induction of general anesthesia. A thorough examination under anesthesia is performed to assess laxity and direction of instability, including anterior and posterior translation with the arm abducted to 90° and comparison to the contralateral side. The operative extremity is placed in a pneumatic arm holder, and the shoulder is prepped and draped in sterile fashion.

A standard posterior portal is established, and a 30° arthroscope is introduced. An anterior portal is created under spinal needle guidance in a superior position to optimize visualization of the Hill–Sachs lesion and anterior labrum while preserving space for a low anteroinferior portal within the rotator interval. Diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to assess the labrum, capsule, rotator cuff, and bony structures, and synovitis or labral fraying is debrided with a motorized shaver.

Remplissage of Infraspinatus (Part 1)

Attention is directed to the Hill‐Sachs lesion. The arthroscope is transitioned to the anterior portal, and a posterior working cannula is inserted through the infraspinatus tendon to allow direct access to the defect (Figure 1). The Hill‐Sachs lesion is debrided with a shaver and burr to create a bleeding bony surface, taking care to preserve the subchondral plate (Figure 2A and B). The cannula is then withdrawn into the subacromial/subdeltoid space and maintained there to prevent any superficial soft tissue bridge. The direction for anchor placement is determined using a switching stick or spinal needle. Using a 2.6 mm knotless all‐suture anchor (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) guide with a trocar, a perforation is made in the infraspinatus tendon at the posteroinferior margin of the Hill‐Sachs lesion, and the guide is advanced to bone (Figure 3A and B). After the first anchor is placed with the cannula maintained in the subacromial space, the cannula is redirected anterosuperiorly to create a second perforation in the infraspinatus, and a second anchor is placed in similar fashion (Figures 4A and B, 5A and B). The cannula is removed, and the anchor sutures are left untensioned and clamped for later shuttling. Placing both anchors through the same cannula helps avoid a superficial soft‐tissue bridge.

Anterior Labrum Repair With Capsulorrhaphy

With the arthroscope redirected to the posterior portal, a second anterior portal is created just above the subscapularis, the anterior glenoid rim is debrided to bleeding bone, and the capsulolabral complex is mobilized. A 1.8‐mm knotless all‐suture anchor (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed at the 5:30‐6 o'clock articular margin via the anteroinferior portal, and a curved suture passer is used to capture the inferior capsule and labrum and shuttle the repair suture, which is secured with the knotless mechanism using simple sutures to restore the labrum (Figure 6A and B). Additional anchors are placed sequentially from 5 to 2 o'clock in similar fashion to complete the repair and achieve capsular shift (Figure 7).

This process is repeated sequentially to complete Bankart repair along the anterior glenoid rim. Remplissage of Infraspinatus (Part 2)

After labral repair, the arthroscope is moved to the anterosuperior portal and the sutures from the two humeral anchors are separated. Using the opposite anchor's shuttle limbs, repair sutures are crossed and tensioned sequentially to compress the posterior capsule and infraspinatus into the Hill–Sachs defect (Figure 8A and B). Because both anchors were placed through a single cannula, no superficial deltoid or subacromial tissue is captured. Sutures are cut flush, leaving a low‐profile construct with secure infraspinatus seating within the Hill–Sachs defect (Video 1).

(A and B) Arthroscopic view of the right shoulder from the anterosuperior portal. Following anterior labral repair the remplissage is completed. The repair suture from each anchor is shuttled through the opposite anchor's shuttle suture and tensioned, compressing the captured infraspinatus tendon into the Hill‐Sachs lesion. Tensioning is performed under direct visualization from the anterosuperior portal, creating a broad, low‐profile zone of infraspinatus tendon compression that fills the defect and prevents engagement. (HH, humeral head; HSL, Hill‐Sachs lesion; IS, infraspinatus.)

Closure

All portals are irrigated and closed with No. 3‐0 Monocryl (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) suture. Sterile dressings are applied, and the patient is placed in an abduction sling. Key pearls and pitfalls are summarized in Table 1.

Postoperative Protocol

Patients are maintained in sling immobilization for 4 weeks, with passive range of motion limited to forward elevation <120°, abduction <90°, and external rotation <30°. Internal rotation is limited to the body to avoid stressing the posterior remplissage. From weeks 4‐6, active motion is initiated as tolerated. At 8 weeks, resistance strengthening begins. Return to full activity without restrictions is permitted at 4 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Arthroscopic Bankart repair with remplissage is an attractive, stability‐enhancing option for anterior shoulder instability in high‐risk patients with engaging or select near‐track Hill–Sachs lesions and subcritical glenoid bone loss. 4 , 7 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Across the literature, variability exists in patient positioning (beach‐chair vs lateral decubitus), sequencing (remplissage before vs after Bankart), anchor type (knotted vs knotless all‐suture), portal strategy (expanded posterior incision vs cannula‐based passage), and the remplissage construct (simple mattress, double‐pulley, double‐row variants). 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Comparisons with alternative techniques are summarized in Table 2.

Present Technique Versus Alternative Techniques

Positioning and Exposure

Both beach‐chair and lateral decubitus positions have been reported with comparable outcomes. 22 , 25 Beach‐chair simplifies setup and facilitates conversion to open procedures when needed, whereas lateral decubitus improves circumferential visualization and access to the inferior/posterior labrum. 18 , 26 We use beach‐chair to optimize setup efficiency. Unlike approaches that expand the posterior incision into the subdeltoid space for extracapsular knot management, ours maintains a cannula‐based intracapsular workflow, minimizing soft‐tissue disruption.

Sequence: Staged Remplissage With Delayed Tensioning

Sequencing of Bankart repair and remplissage remains variable in the literature, with authors describing remplissage‐first, Bankart‐first, and staged approaches. 15 Our two‐stage remplissage is intended to provide reliable anchor positioning while the humeral head is easily positioned, maintain unobstructed anterior visualization during labral repair, and achieve final humeral‐sided containment once the glenoid is restored. This approach also aims to reduce the risk of disrupting a fresh Bankart repair when remplissage is performed last in a constrained field. 27

Fixation Strategy: Knotless, Low‐Profile Linked Construct

Traditional remplissage uses extracapsular mattress sutures that provide broad tendon compression but can create knot stacks that may contribute to irritation and require additional dissection. 20 , 23 Our technique links two 2.6‐mm knotless all‐suture anchors to provide broad, low‐profile footprint compression with minimal bone removal, consistent with knotless labral repair principles of bone preservation and controlled tensioning, 19 , 28 , 29 and has shown high patient satisfaction, low recurrence, functional and radiographic outcomes comparable to knotted constructs, and potentially shorter operative times. 29 , 30 , 31

Anterior Stabilization: Capsulolabral Repair and Access

Inferior labral access is a recurring technical hurdle. Prior authors have proposed curved guides and, when needed, low anteroinferior portals or trajectories to achieve a true 5:30‐6:00 position on the glenoid. 32 , 33 Our method similarly emphasizes inferior anchor placement with capsular shift, using small‐diameter knotless all‐suture anchors through curved guides to allow multiple fixation points while preserving bone stock—an advantage in young athletes and in shoulders that may require revision. 18 , 34

Indications and Bone Loss

We favor this technique for off‐ or near‐off‐track Hill–Sachs lesions, on‐track lesions in high‐risk patients, and cases with subcritical glenoid bone loss. Bone augmentation (e.g., Latarjet or bone block) is reserved for glenoid defects >13.5%. 13 , 35 Because glenoid augmentation alone can fail in the setting of concurrent off‐track Hill–Sachs lesions, adjunctive remplissage may be considered in those cases. 36 In the present case—a highly active firefighter with a near‐track defect and subcritical bone loss—remplissage was added to reduce engagement and recurrence risk.

Rehabilitation Protocol

Our rehabilitation protocol accelerates recovery compared with more conservative 4‐6‐month programs, 34 and therefore requires careful patient selection, counseling, and adherence.

Limitations

This approach has disadvantages that warrant emphasis, including increased implant utilization and cost with a two‐anchor construct and the potential for anchor‐related failure (e.g., pullout or inadequate compression) if trajectory, defect preparation, or bone purchase is suboptimal. Remplissage may also reduce external rotation or contribute to posterior tightness, particularly if over‐tensioned, and it should not be used as a substitute for bony augmentation in clinically significant glenoid or humeral bone loss. 37 Key advantages and disadvantages are summarized in Table 3. In summary, this technique offers a joint‐preserving option for anterior instability with high‐risk Hill–Sachs lesions, bridging isolated soft‐tissue repair and glenoid augmentation. It is best suited to young, active patients with off‐ or near‐off‐track (high‐risk on‐track) Hill–Sachs lesions and subcritical glenoid bone loss. In these patients, staged knotless remplissage combined with capsulolabral repair can provide durable shoulder stability and reduce the risk of recurrent instability.
