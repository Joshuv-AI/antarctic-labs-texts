Factors associated with in-hospital mortality and treatment withdrawal in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Nandu Luo, Hui Li, Guangyue Luo, Fei Wang, Shuanglin Li, Lijun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere pneumonia requiring mechanical ventilation (MV) remains associated with substantial mortality and treatment failure in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs). Although several prognostic scoring systems have been developed for critically ill children, readily available laboratory biomarkers that may facilitate early prognostic assessment in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia have not been well established. Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to identify laboratory factors associated with in-hospital mortality and treatment withdrawal in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included children diagnosed with severe pneumonia who required MV and were admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) of People’s Hospital of Qiannan Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture between June 2022 and June 2025. Laboratory biomarkers obtained within 24 hours of PICU admission were analyzed. Poor outcomes were defined as in-hospital mortality or treatment withdrawal because of critical illness, with survival status confirmed by follow-up after discharge. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with poor outcomes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted as an exploratory assessment of the discriminative performance of the identified prognostic factors.ResultsA total of 185 eligible children were included, among whom 15 experienced poor outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that absolute neutrophil count (ANC), platelet count (PLT), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were independently associated with poor outcomes. Exploratory ROC analysis showed that the combination of these laboratory markers demonstrated better discriminative performance than any individual marker. Given the limited number of outcome events, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger prospective studies.ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort study, elevated ANC and ALT levels together with reduced PLT levels were independently associated with poor outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia. These routinely available laboratory biomarkers may provide complementary prognostic information during early clinical assessment. However, because of the retrospective single-center design and the limited number of poor outcome events, the present findings should be regarded as exploratory and require prospective validation in larger multicenter cohorts before clinical implementation.Keywords: Severe pneumonia, mechanical ventilation (MV), prognosis, children

Highlight box

Key findings

• Elevated absolute neutrophil count (ANC), reduced platelet count (PLT), and elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were independently associated with poor clinical outcomes, defined as in-hospital mortality or treatment withdrawal due to critical illness, in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia.

• The combination of these routinely available laboratory biomarkers showed better apparent discriminative performance than individual biomarkers in exploratory analysis.

What is known and what is new?

• Children with severe pneumonia requiring mechanical ventilation (MV) remain at high risk of in-hospital mortality despite advances in pediatric intensive care. Existing prognostic scoring systems are useful but require multiple physiological variables and specialized clinical assessment.

• This study specifically investigated mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia, a clinically distinct but understudied population. ANC, PLT, and ALT were identified as independent prognostic factors for poor clinical outcomes; An exploratory subgroup analysis suggested that reduced PLT remained associated with poor outcomes among children receiving invasive MV.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Routinely available laboratory biomarkers may complement existing prognostic assessment and facilitate early risk stratification, particularly in resource-limited settings.

• Because of the retrospective single-center design and the limited number of poor outcome events, these findings should be considered exploratory and require prospective multicenter validation before routine clinical application.

Introduction

Pneumonia remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among children worldwide, particularly in infants and young children (1). Although advances in antimicrobial therapy and pediatric intensive care have substantially improved survival, children with severe pneumonia who require mechanical ventilation (MV) continue to experience high rates of multiple organ dysfunction, prolonged pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) stay, treatment withdrawal, and in-hospital mortality (2,3). These mechanically ventilated children represent a clinically distinct subgroup characterized by greater disease severity, higher risks of organ dysfunction, and substantially poorer outcomes than children with severe pneumonia managed without invasive respiratory support (4). Therefore, early identification of children at increased risk of in-hospital mortality or treatment withdrawal is clinically important for optimizing intensive monitoring, individualized supportive therapy, and resource allocation in the PICU. Reliable and readily available prognostic indicators are therefore needed to facilitate early risk assessment and support timely clinical decision-making in this vulnerable population.

MV is an essential life-support intervention for children with severe pneumonia complicated by respiratory failure (2,4). However, children requiring MV represent a clinically distinct subgroup with substantially greater disease severity than the overall population of children with severe pneumonia. These patients are more likely to develop severe hypoxemia, systemic inflammatory responses, cardiovascular instability, coagulation abnormalities, and multiple organ dysfunction, resulting in markedly increased risks of treatment withdrawal and in-hospital mortality (5,6). Furthermore, MV itself may contribute to ventilator-associated lung injury, secondary infections, and hemodynamic instability, further complicating clinical management (2). Consequently, prognostic factors identified in the general population of children with pneumonia may not be directly applicable to mechanically ventilated patients, highlighting the need for dedicated investigations focusing on this particularly vulnerable population.

Several prognostic scoring systems, including the Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction-2 (PELOD-2), Pediatric Critical Illness Score (PCIS), and Pediatric Risk of Mortality III (PRISM III), have been developed to assess disease severity and estimate mortality risk in critically ill children (7). These scoring systems provide valuable prognostic information and are widely used in PICUs (7,8). However, they require the integration of multiple physiological and laboratory variables, repeated clinical assessments, and trained personnel, which may limit their routine application in emergency settings and resource-limited healthcare facilities. Consequently, there remains considerable interest in identifying simple, objective, and readily available biomarkers that can complement existing scoring systems and facilitate early prognostic assessment. Among routinely available laboratory parameters, inflammatory and organ injury biomarkers have attracted increasing attention because they reflect key pathophysiological processes involved in severe pneumonia. Absolute neutrophil count (ANC) reflects the magnitude of the host inflammatory response, platelet count (PLT) is closely associated with coagulation disorders and systemic inflammation, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) serves as a marker of hepatic injury secondary to systemic hypoxia or inflammatory insults (9,10). These laboratory markers are inexpensive, rapidly obtainable, and routinely measured in critically ill children, making them attractive candidates for early prognostic evaluation, particularly in resource-limited settings (9-11). Nevertheless, evidence regarding the prognostic significance of these biomarkers in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia remains limited. Furthermore, the changing epidemiology of pediatric severe pneumonia, including the increasing contribution of Mycoplasma pneumoniae and its associated dysregulated inflammatory responses, may alter the prognostic value of traditional laboratory indicators (10,12,13). Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to identify routinely available laboratory biomarkers independently associated with in-hospital mortality and treatment withdrawal in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia.

Despite advances in pediatric critical care, evidence regarding readily available laboratory biomarkers for early prognostic assessment in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia remains limited. In particular, it is unclear whether routinely measured laboratory indicators can provide clinically useful prognostic information in this high-risk population in addition to existing clinical assessment tools. Therefore, the present retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate the associations of routinely available laboratory biomarkers with in-hospital mortality and treatment withdrawal among mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia and to identify factors independently associated with poor clinical outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0327/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study included children diagnosed with severe pneumonia who required MV and were admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) of People’s Hospital of Qiannan Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture between June 2022 and June 2025. The diagnosis of severe pneumonia was established according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria (14). Children were excluded if death was clearly documented in the medical record as being primarily attributable to an underlying disease unrelated to severe pneumonia or its complications, as determined by the treating clinical team. Patients with underlying comorbidities whose clinical deterioration was considered to be related to severe pneumonia remained eligible for inclusion. Eligible patients were identified through the hospital electronic medical record system, and demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, microbiological results, treatment information, and clinical outcomes were retrospectively collected using standardized data collection forms. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of People’s Hospital of Qiannan Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture (approval No. 2025-qnzy-44). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Definitions

Severe pneumonia: diagnosed according to internationally recognized guidelines (14). Poor clinical outcomes: defined as death occurring either during hospitalization or following family-requested withdrawal of treatment due to the child’s critical condition, with death confirmed by follow-up. MV criteria: non-invasive ventilation (NIV) included continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP); invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) required endotracheal intubation. Initiation criteria included persistent hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% despite supplemental oxygen), respiratory acidosis (pH <7.25 with PaCO2 >60 mmHg), or clinical signs of respiratory failure (severe retractions, apnea, or altered mental status). Children were eligible if they were younger than 14 years of age, fulfilled the WHO diagnostic criteria for severe pneumonia, were admitted to the PICU, and received either NIV or IMV during hospitalization.

The decision to initiate NIV or IMV was made by the attending pediatric intensivists according to the patient’s clinical condition and routine PICU practice. As this study aimed to investigate laboratory factors associated with poor outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia rather than to compare ventilation strategies, patients receiving NIV and IMV were analyzed as a single cohort. The potential clinical heterogeneity associated with different ventilation modalities was considered when interpreting the study findings.

Data collection

We collected clinical data of all children with severe pneumonia who met the inclusion criteria; data collection process: two investigators (G.L., H.L.) independently reviewed medical records using a standardized data collection form. All data were double-entered and cross-checked. This encompassed general information (such as, sex, age, ethnicity, route of admission, length of hospital stay), clinical manifestations (such as, cough, tachypnea, wheezing, cyanosis, retractions), and laboratory parameters were measured at admission [such as, complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), liver function tests, myocardial enzyme profiles, Mycoplasma pneumoniae detection, and sputum culture]. Due to the retrospective design, data collection was limited to information routinely recorded in medical records. Some clinically relevant variables—including detailed comorbidities, oxygenation indices, comprehensive hemodynamic parameters, organ dysfunction scores, and precise ventilation duration—were not consistently documented and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators (G.L., H.L.) using a standardized case report form. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a senior investigator (N.L.) to ensure data accuracy and consistency.

Microbiological identification

Respiratory specimens, including sputum, endotracheal aspirates (from intubated patients), were collected aseptically within 24 hours of admission or intubation. For bacterial culture, specimens were inoculated onto appropriate media and incubated at 37 ℃ for 24–48 hours. Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the VITEK® 2 Compact automated system. Mycoplasma pneumoniae was detected via real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays on throat swabs, when clinically indicated. All quality control procedures were conducted in accordance with the clinical laboratory standards of People’s Hospital of Qiannan Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was poor clinical outcome, defined as a composite endpoint of in-hospital mortality or discharge against medical advice following treatment withdrawal because of expected poor clinical outcomes. In People’s Hospital of Qiannan Buyei and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, treatment withdrawal refers to situations in which the patient’s legal guardians elected to discontinue active treatment and requested discharge after being informed by the treating PICU physicians that the child’s condition was critical and survival was considered highly unlikely. Therefore, these patients were classified as having poor clinical outcomes based on predefined study criteria. All outcome data were obtained from the complete electronic medical records and discharge documentation during the index hospitalization; no post-discharge follow-up was performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Quantitative data were described as mean ± standard deviation (x¯±s), with comparisons made via the unpaired t-test. Non-normally distributed data were represented as median and interquartile ranges [M (Q1, Q3)], and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The normality of clinical data variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Categorical data were expressed as n (%) and analyzed using Chi-squared test. The stepwise logistic regression (the primary outcome of the present study was a binary in-hospital composite outcome rather than time-to-event survival. Therefore, logistic regression was considered appropriate for evaluating factors associated with poor clinical outcomes) was applied to explore factors associated with poor clinical outcomes in pediatric patients with severe pneumonia requiring MV. Given the limited number of poor outcome events, a sensitivity analysis using multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression was additionally performed to reduce potential small-sample bias and evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed as an exploratory assessment of the discriminative performance of the independently identified prognostic factors. The present study was not designed to develop or validate a clinical prediction model. P<0.05 was set as the statistical significance of bilateral tests.

Results

Basic clinical information

A total of 433 children diagnosed with severe pneumonia were initially screened for eligibility. Among them, 248 cases were excluded due to the following reasons: not receiving MV (n=216), discharge requested by the patient (n=4), incomplete medical records (n=23), deaths primarily attributable to underlying diseases unrelated to severe pneumonia (n=5). Ultimately, 185 eligible children were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). The study comprised 102 males (55.14%) and 83 females (44.86%). The majority of patients were aged ≤6 months (n=140, 75.68%), while 45 children (24.32%) were older than 6 months. Ethnically, the Bouyei group was the most represented (n=80, 43.24%), followed by Han (n=44, 23.78%), Miao (n=29, 15.68%), Shui (n=18, 9.73%), and other ethnicities (n=14, 7.57%). Regarding respiratory support, NIV was administered to 163 patients (88.11%), whereas 22 children (11.89%) received IMV. The primary routes of admission were the emergency department (n=95, 51.35%) and outpatient clinic referrals (n=79, 42.70%), with a smaller proportion transferred from other hospitals (n=11, 5.95%). The length of hospital stay exceeded one week for 158 children (85.41%), while 27 patients (14.59%) were discharged within one week. In terms of prognosis, the majority of children had a favorable outcome (n=170, 91.89%), whereas 15 patients (8.11%) experienced poor clinical outcomes (Table 1).

Microbial analysis of severe pneumonia in mechanically ventilated children

Respiratory specimens, including sputum, endotracheal aspirates (from intubated patients), and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (when clinically indicated), were collected aseptically within 24 hours of admission or intubation. Microbiological analysis of specimens obtained from the 185 mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia revealed a diverse spectrum of pathogens. Gram-positive bacteria were the most commonly identified, accounting for 24.86% of cases, with Staphylococcus aureus being the predominant species (78.26%). Gram-negative bacteria were identified in 22.16% of cases, mainly Escherichia coli (24.39%), Haemophilus influenzae (21.95%), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (19.51%). Mycoplasma pneumoniae was detected in 21.08% of the cohort. co-infection with gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria was observed in 5.41% of cases, and mycoplasma-bacterial co-infection in 20% of cases. Notably, no pathogens were identified in 24.32% of specimens, and microbiological testing was unavailable in four patients (2.16%) (Figure 2 and Table 2).

Comparison of clinical characteristics between children with favorable and poor clinical outcomes

This study compared the clinical characteristics of children with favorable clinical outcomes (n=170) and poor clinical outcomes (n=15). No significant differences were observed in respiratory symptoms or hospitalization costs (all P>0.05). However, significant differences were identified in age and length of hospital stay. Children in the poor outcome group were significantly older than those in the favorable outcome group [5.0 (2.8–71.0) vs. 2.20 (1.52–4.80) months, P=0.003]. In contrast, the length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in the poor outcome group [4.0 (1.0–10.0) vs. 11.0 (9.0–14.0) days, P<0.001]. No significant differences were observed in sex, respiratory symptoms, or hospitalization costs (all P>0.05) (Table 3).

Factors associated with poor clinical outcomes in children with severe pneumonia requiring MV

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified several variables associated with poor outcomes, including age, WBC, ANC, ALT, AST, CK-MB, PLT, and ALB. In the multivariable model, ANC [odds ratio (OR) =1.490, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.160–1.914], PLT (OR =0.992, 95% CI: 0.986–0.999), and ALT (OR =1.020, 95% CI: 1.002–1.039) remained independently associated with poor clinical outcomes (Table 5). To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a sensitivity analysis using multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression was conducted. The results remained materially unchanged, with ANC, PLT, and ALT continuing to show independent associations with poor clinical outcomes (Table S1).

Exploratory ROC analysis

To further explore the laboratory factors independently associated with poor clinical outcomes, ROC curve analysis was performed. ANC, PLT, and ALT demonstrated individual discriminative ability for poor outcomes. The combined model demonstrated an apparent area under the curve (AUC) of 0.898 in the study cohort (AUC =0.898, 95% CI: 0.845–0.938), with a sensitivity of 80.0% and a specificity of 90.6%. Given the retrospective design, limited sample size, and small number of poor outcome events, these ROC findings should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as representing a validated prognostic model suitable for routine clinical application (Figure 3 and Table 6).

Subgroup analysis of children receiving IMV

Within the IMV subgroup, we compared the three key prognostic factors identified in the main analysis—ANC, PLT, and ALT—between patients with favorable and poor outcomes. PLT remained significantly lower in the poor outcome group {305 [171–401] vs. 424 [354–652] ×109/L, P=0.03}, consistent with the findings from the full cohort analysis. ANC showed a trend toward higher values in the poor outcome group [12.92 (5.29–15.24) vs. 5.81 (3.40–12.96) ×109/L]; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.45). ALT also exhibited a trend toward higher levels in the poor outcome group {36 [25–234] vs. 22 [19–41] U/L}, but the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.11).

Discussion

Severe pneumonia may experience acute complications such as respiratory failure and heart failure, and in some cases, the condition may rapidly progress to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). In particular, patients whose hypoxemia cannot be adequately corrected by conventional oxygen therapy are more likely to develop severe complications and experience poor clinical outcomes. Therefore, timely recognition of respiratory deterioration and appropriate initiation of MV remain essential components of supportive management. In this study, the overall rate of poor clinical outcomes was 8.11%, which was comparable to that reported in previous studies (9.3% and 8.7%) (15,16). We acknowledge that viral pathogens, which are a leading cause of pediatric pneumonia, were not systematically tested in this study, reflecting the routine diagnostic practices in our resource-limited setting where viral testing is not consistently available. Furthermore, higher ANC, lower PLT, and higher ALT levels were independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in this cohort. Notably, exploratory ROC analysis suggested that the combination of these three variables demonstrated better apparent discriminative ability than each individual marker within this cohort, with both demonstrated apparent discriminative ability within this cohort. However, it is important to emphasize that our findings are exploratory in nature. These findings provide preliminary evidence that routinely available laboratory parameters may provide complementary prognostic information during early clinical assessment. However, whether their incorporation into clinical management improves patient outcomes requires validation in larger prospective studies.

In this study, we analyzed the etiological profile of 185 mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia and found that gram-positive bacteria, gram-negative bacteria, mycoplasma pneumoniae, and co-infections were major contributors. Gram-positive bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus remained important pathogens, consistent with previous findings in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) (17,18). However, their proportion was lower than that in non-critical cases, likely due to increased exposure to hospital flora and prior antibiotic therapy. Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 22.16% of isolates, with Escherichia coli, Haemophilus influenzae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae being predominant. Previous studies have reported that gram-negative bacilli are major pathogens in critically ill children, especially among those exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics or prolonged MV (15,18). Interestingly, Mycoplasma pneumoniae remained an important pathogen. Although traditionally considered a mild and self-limiting pathogen, recent studies have demonstrated that Mycoplasma pneumoniae can trigger severe or refractory pneumonia through immune-mediated injury and excessive cytokine activation rather than direct bacterial invasion (19,20). The persistence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae in our study indicates that this pathogen remains relatively common even among children with severe pneumonia. Its detection may assist clinicians in selecting appropriate antimicrobial therapy and contribute to a better understanding of the etiological profile of severe pneumonia in this high-risk population. Given the diversity of pathogens identified, our findings emphasize the necessity of timely and comprehensive microbiological testing. Such approaches are essential for optimizing antibiotic stewardship and improving outcomes in pediatric intensive care settings. While our study focused on host-derived inflammatory markers, we acknowledge that the relationship between these markers and outcomes may be modified by pathogen type. For example, certain pathogens—such as Staphylococcus aureus or Klebsiella pneumoniae—may elicit more intense neutrophil responses or greater hepatocellular injury than others. Conversely, the prognostic value of ANC, PLT, and ALT may differ between bacterial and viral etiologies, or between single infections and co-infections. Future studies with larger sample sizes should explore these potential interactions and assess whether pathogen-specific prognostic models are warranted.

Higher ANC was independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia (OR =1.490, 95% CI: 1.160–1.914, P=0.002). Specifically, each 1×109/L increase in ANC was associated with 49% higher odds of poor clinical outcomes. Elevated ANC levels typically indicate a heightened inflammatory response and increased activation of the innate immune system. Although neutrophils are essential for infection control, their excessive accumulation may exacerbate tissue damage through the release of proteases, reactive oxygen species, and neutrophil extracellular traps (21-23). These processes amplify pulmonary inflammation and disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier, ultimately worsening lung pathology. A cohort study involving 334 children with pneumonia demonstrated that a higher ANC was a significant risk factor for severe disease [8.91 (5.21–11.10) vs. 3.53 (2.45–5.85), P<0.01, AUC =0.760] (24). Similarly, a study of 557 pediatric pneumonia patients found that elevated ANC was independently associated with the development of plastic bronchitis (OR =3.113, P=0.04) (25). Collectively, these findings suggest that an increased ANC reflects an exaggerated systemic inflammatory response and is associated with poor clinical outcomes in severe pneumonia. These findings further support the biological plausibility of ANC as a routinely available laboratory marker associated with disease severity.

Lower PLT was independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia (OR =0.992, 95% CI: 0.986–0.999, P=0.02). Specifically, each 1×109/L decrease in PLT was associated with 0.8% higher odds of poor clinical outcomes. Lower PLT levels often arise from inflammation-related platelet consumption and compromised marrow production. In addition to hemostasis, platelets exert important immunomodulatory effects by secreting cytokines and chemokines that engage both leukocytes and endothelial cells, ultimately enhancing inflammatory responses (26,27). In a study of 156 children with severe pneumonia, platelet levels at admission ranged from approximately 200 to 450 ×109/L, and lower counts were closely associated with greater disease severity (10), which aligns with our findings. Similarly, a large cohort study of 12,905 patients demonstrated that the mean PLT at admission was around 250 ×109/L, and platelet levels between 151–175 ×109/L were significantly associated with lower 30-day mortality compared with the 250–275 ×109/L reference group (28). Furthermore, several studies have confirmed that thrombocytopenia independently correlates with adverse outcomes in children with severe pneumonia (29,30). Collectively, these findings suggest that a decreased PLT is not merely a secondary indicator of inflammation but may actively contribute to disease progression by promoting coagulopathy, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular injury. Therefore, continuous monitoring of platelet abnormalities may provide complementary prognostic information during routine clinical assessment. However, whether interventions targeting thrombocytopenia can improve clinical outcomes remains to be determined in future prospective studies.

Higher ALT levels were independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia (OR =1.020, 95% CI: 1.002–1.039, P=0.03). Specifically, each 1-U/L increase in ALT was associated with 2% higher odds of poor clinical outcomes. Elevated ALT levels may reflect hepatic involvement secondary to hypoxia, systemic inflammation, or drug-induced injury during severe infection. Proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α can induce hepatocellular injury and impair hepatic microcirculation, leading to increased ALT release into the bloodstream (31,32). A pediatric study reported that children aged 28 days to 1 year with pneumonia were most prone to liver injury, with mild elevations in ALT and AST being the predominant manifestations, both correlating with pneumonia severity (33). Similarly, another study demonstrated that survivors with abnormal liver function had significantly longer hospital stays compared with those with normal liver function (9.7 vs. 5.8 days, P=0.006) (34). Overall, these findings suggest that elevated ALT is associated with systemic inflammatory response and multiorgan stress in severe pneumonia. The underlying pathophysiology may involve cytokine-mediated hepatocyte injury, hypoxic stress, and impaired hepatic perfusion. Therefore, serial monitoring of ALT levels may provide complementary prognostic information during routine clinical assessment, although its clinical usefulness requires prospective validation.

Our study further demonstrated that poor clinical outcomes were associated with older age, shorter hospital stays, lower absolute LYM, elevated CRP, AST, and CK-MB levels, as well as lower ALB concentrations. Although these parameters did not remain statistically significant in the multivariable regression analysis, they may still hold clinical importance and warrant careful consideration in routine assessment. In contrast, other parameters—including sex, cough, wheezing, and blood gas values—showed no significant differences. These findings collectively demonstrate that several routinely available laboratory parameters were associated with poor clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia. In addition, the sensitivity analysis using multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression yielded results consistent with those of the primary analysis, providing further support for the robustness of the identified associations despite the limited number of outcome events. Careful monitoring of these parameters may provide complementary prognostic information during routine clinical assessment. Exploratory ROC analysis demonstrated that the combination of ANC, PLT, and ALT achieved better apparent discriminative ability than each individual laboratory marker within this cohort. Although the combination of ANC, PLT, and ALT demonstrated good apparent discriminative ability in exploratory ROC analysis, the primary objective of this study was to identify prognostic factors rather than to develop or validate a clinical prediction model. Given the limited sample size and the small number of poor clinical outcomes events, the observed AUC may overestimate the discriminative ability of the combined laboratory markers because of overfitting related to the limited number of outcome events. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as establishing a validated prognostic model suitable for routine clinical application but rather as providing preliminary evidence to guide future prospective multicenter validation studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the retrospective study design inherently carries risks of selection and information bias, including incomplete documentation and variability in data collection. Because data collection was limited to information routinely recorded in medical records, several clinically relevant variables—including detailed comorbidities, oxygenation indices, comprehensive hemodynamic parameters, organ dysfunction scores, and precise duration of MV—were not consistently available and therefore could not be included in the analysis. These missing variables may have resulted in residual confounding. Second, the limited etiological characterization, particularly the absence of viral pathogen testing, reflects the routine diagnostic practices in our resource-limited setting and may have resulted in unmeasured confounding. Third, the apparent AUC of 0.898 observed for the combined laboratory markers may reflect overfitting related to the limited number of outcome events rather than true discriminative ability. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require external validation in larger independent cohorts before routine clinical application. Fourth, the IMV subgroup analysis supports the clinical relevance of our findings among children receiving IMV. However, the small sample size of this subgroup (n=22) limited statistical power. Therefore, the non-significant associations observed for ANC and ALT should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect type II error rather than the absence of true associations. Further validation in larger multicenter cohorts of children receiving IMV is warranted. Fifth, as a single-center investigation conducted at a regional hospital in southwestern China, the study population may not fully reflect the clinical characteristics or management practices of other regions or institutions, limiting generalizability. An additional limitation is the relatively small number of poor clinical outcomes (15 events), resulting in a limited events-per-variable ratio in the multivariable logistic regression model. Although the number of variables included in the final model was intentionally restricted to minimize model complexity, the possibility of overfitting and unstable regression estimates cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the identified associations should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. External validation in larger prospective cohorts is required before these findings can be generalized. Another limitation is that children receiving NIV and IMV were analyzed together as a single cohort. Because the choice of ventilation modality was determined by the patient’s clinical condition rather than by random allocation, these two groups may differ in disease severity and physiological characteristics, introducing potential clinical heterogeneity. Owing to the relatively small number of patients receiving IMV, further subgroup analyses were unlikely to provide robust statistical estimates. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes should evaluate prognostic factors separately according to ventilation modality. Finally, although we excluded patients whose deaths were clearly attributable to underlying diseases unrelated to severe pneumonia, some degree of selection bias cannot be completely excluded because severe pneumonia may interact with pre-existing comorbidities to influence clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Higher ANC, lower PLT, and higher ALT were independently associated with poor clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated children with severe pneumonia. These routinely available laboratory parameters may provide complementary prognostic information for early clinical assessment. However, given the retrospective design, limited sample size, and lack of external validation, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger prospective multicenter studies before routine clinical application.
