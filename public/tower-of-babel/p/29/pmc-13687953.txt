An unexpected atypical atriofascicular pathway presenting with sudden cardiac arrest: Mechanistic insights
Authors: Olubadewa A. Fatunde, Sam Stevens, Michael B. Liu, Oluwaseun Adeola, Komandoor Srivathsan, Win-Kuang Shen
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Atypical conduction anatomy: The identification of an atrioventricular pathway with a nondecremental, fascicular extension to the distal His/proximal right bundle represents a rarely described variant with aggressive conduction properties.•Multiplicity of pathways: The presence of multiple accessory pathways underscores the necessity for comprehensive postablation evaluation to confirm complete pathway elimination and exclude fusion conduction.•Synergistic risk: The coexistence of structural heart disease (eg, Loeffler’s endocarditis) and Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome may create a unique arrhythmogenic substrate predisposing to malignant arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (SCD).•Preventive strategy: Early electrophysiological examination in patients with known or symptomatic WPW may enable timely ablation and prevent catastrophic outcomes such as pre-excited atrial fibrillation leading to SCD.

Introduction

Patients with accessory pathways (APs) may remain asymptomatic and undiagnosed until preexcitation is incidentally detected on an electrocardiogram (ECG). When symptomatic, presentations can range from benign palpitations to sudden cardiac death (SCD).

Several electrophysiologic characteristics have been associated with malignant APs, including the pathway’s anatomical location, a shortest pre-excited RR interval <250 ms during atrial fibrillation (AF), an AP effective refractory period <240 ms, and the presence of multiple pathways.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 We report the comprehensive electrophysiological assessment and successful ablation in a young patient with Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) syndrome who presented with SCD in the context of Loeffler’s endocarditis—a rare and unexpected coexistence (93%-95% of patients with WPW have structurally normal hearts).1 The following case demonstrated 2 APs, with 1 AP exhibiting atypical electrophysiological features that provided additional mechanistic insights into this unusual case.

Case report

A man, aged 22 years, with a known history of WPW syndrome, diagnosed at the age of 12 years, had previously declined electrophysiological examination. From middle school through college, he reported infrequent palpitations without presyncope, syncope, or prior cardiac arrest and had participated in competitive athletics without limitation.

While traveling, the patient experienced a witnessed collapse at an airport and was found to be in ventricular fibrillation. He was successfully resuscitated by emergency medical services en route to a local hospital. Unfortunately, the rhythm was not captured in its entirety. Coronary angiography indicated normal coronary anatomy. Laboratory evaluation revealed marked eosinophilia and profound metabolic acidosis (pH 6.6). The patient required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support and targeted temperature management, and subsequently, full neurologic and hemodynamic recovery was achieved.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40%, with circumferential basal subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement in a noncoronary distribution, multiple left ventricular thrombi, and hypointense T2 signal—findings consistent with fibrotic-stage Loeffler’s endocarditis. Anticoagulation, prednisone, and spironolactone therapy was initiated in the patient.

An initial electrophysiology (EP) study at an outside institution failed to eliminate the AP, and a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was implanted. The patient was subsequently referred to Mayo Clinic Arizona for definitive ablation.

EP study

Baseline 12-lead ECG and fluoroscopic catheter positioning are shown in Supplemental Figures 1 and 2. The ECG was suggestive of a right posterior to posterolateral AP. Mapping was performed using the CARTO 3 system (Version 8, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). A 3.5-mm irrigated radiofrequency catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch DF, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) was used for ablation.

Mapping and ablation

During atrial pacing from the proximal coronary sinus (CS), activation mapping localized the manifest AP (AP1) to the right posterior tricuspid annulus (Supplemental Figure 4A and 4B), consistent with the baseline ECG pre-excitation pattern. A discrete AP potential was recorded, and radiofrequency delivery caused immediate loss of pre-excitation within 3 seconds (Supplemental Figure 4C). Consolidation lesions were applied.

Postablation testing revealed a change in the earliest retrograde atrial activation from CS 13‒14 to CS 17‒18 (Supplemental Figure 5A), suggesting a second concealed AP (AP2).5 A brief short VA tachycardia (VA interval = 99 ms) was inducible (Supplemental Figure 5B), consistent with orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia. Programmed stimulation with EP maneuvers was not feasible owing to subsequent noninducibility. Mapping during ventricular pacing (Supplemental Figure 6A) localized AP2 to the right posterolateral tricuspid annulus, approximately 2 cm from AP1 (Supplemental Figure 6B).

Radiofrequency ablation during right ventricular pacing abolished VA conduction (Supplemental Figure 7) within 7 s. Final testing confirmed no dual AV nodal physiology, no inducible arrhythmia, and absence of pre-excitation (Supplemental Figure 8).

In general, atriofascicular and atrioventricular fibers are distinguished by their distal insertion sites, determined by the earliest recorded bipolar ventricular electrogram relative to the onset of the QRS complex during tachycardia or atrial pacing. The insertion is considered F (atriofascicular AP) when the earliest ventricular activation is localized to the right ventricular apical region near the distal right bundle branch, and ventricular (atrioventricular AP) when the earliest activation occurs near the atrioventricular ring.6

The electrophysiological features of AP1 in this case clearly differentiate it from the typical atriofascicular (Mahaim) pathway, which is characteristically long and decremental, and conducts exclusively in the antegrade direction.6,7 In contrast, AP1 showed nondecremental, bidirectional conduction with an F branch inserting into the His–Purkinje system at the distal His or proximal right bundle (Figure 2), consistent with an atypical atrioventricular pathway with F extension (Table 1). The retrograde conduction observed at baseline likely represented fusion between AP1 and AP2, given no ventriculoatrial conduction was evident after successful ablation of both APs. Because Mahaim pathways typically produce a left bundle branch block morphology with left-axis deviation, the differential diagnosis includes antidromic tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy, and nodoventricular or nodofascicular pathways. Typical antidromic tachycardia is characterized by rapid, nondecremental conduction over an AP inserting at the annulus. Ventricular tachycardia is characterized by absence of atrial and AV nodal participation in the circuit, AV dissociation, and lack of response to premature atrial beats. In supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy unrelated to an AP, a His-refractory PVC does not affect the subsequent His potential or terminate the tachycardia. By comparison, nodoventricular and nodofascicular pathways may cause advancement of the next His potential during His-refractory PVC placement, and AV dissociation can occur. A further contributing factor was Loeffler’s endocarditis, a rare eosinophilic cardiomyopathy characterized by endocardial fibrosis and mural thrombus formation.8,9 The most frequently reported arrhythmia in Loeffler’s syndrome is AF, whereas SCD owing to pre-excited AF in this context is exceedingly uncommon.8,9 It is plausible that endocardial and myocardial remodeling associated with Loeffler’s disease facilitated AF initiation, which, in the presence of a rapidly conducting AP, precipitated ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest. The coexistence of eosinophilic endomyocardial disease likely facilitated AF initiation but does not explain the malignant conduction properties of the APs. The arrhythmic risk in this patient was therefore primarily driven by AP physiology rather than myocardial disease alone.

Although WPW syndrome most commonly occurs in structurally normal hearts, it has been reported in association with both familial genetic mutations (ie, PRKAG2 mutations, BMP2 microdeletions)10 and congenital structural heart disease (eg, Ebstein anomaly, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries, etc).11 Structural heart disease (ie, Loeffler’s endocarditis) likely further accentuated the already elevated risk in this patient with WPW syndrome and malignant features indicated on EP study. At 12-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with preserved ventricular function, no recurrence of arrhythmia, and no implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapies (Supplemental Figure 9).

Conclusion

Typical risk factors may coalesce with atypical risk factors to create a unique arrhythmogenic substrate. This case illustrates the identification of an atrioventricular pathway with a non-decremental, F extension to the distal His/proximal right bundle, a rarely described variant. Systematic and comprehensive post-ablation evaluation is critical to confirm complete pathway elimination, exclude fusion conduction, and identify the presence of multiple accessory pathways. The risk of malignant arrhythmias and SCD may be heightened with the coexistence of WPW syndrome and structural heart disease (eg, Loeffler’s endocarditis), which is a rare association. Early electrophysiologic evaluation in patients with known or symptomatic WPW may enable timely ablation and prevent catastrophic outcomes such as pre-excited AF leading to SCD.
