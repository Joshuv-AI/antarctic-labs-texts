Life‐Stage–Specific Hematological Profiles in Yellow Mealworm Beetles (Tenebrio molitor): Reference Intervals and Hemocyte Characterization
Authors: Simon Maheu, Houda Ben‐Miled, Dominique Marullo‐Masson, Colin Favret, Carolyn Gara‐Boivin, Marie‐Odile Benoit‐Biancamano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe edible insect industry, driven by the nutritional and environmental benefits of mealworms, faces increasing challenges from pathogens affecting Tenebrio molitor farming systems. Hemocyte‐based health assessments could provide valuable diagnostic insights, yet standardized hemolymph processing protocols and hematological reference intervals (RIs) are currently lacking for this species.ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop practical protocols for hemolymph sampling, refine hemocyte classification, and establish hematological RIs for both larval and adult stages of T. molitor .MethodsAn optimized tearing method, involving capillary collection of hemolymph from a proleg transected at the femoral‐tibial joint, was used to sample healthy individuals. Total and differential hemocyte counts were determined using a hemocytometer and Wright‐Giemsa–stained smears, respectively, and from distinct insect populations (n = 41–43 samples per group). Hemolymph was analyzed in pools due to the small size of T. molitor . Transmission electron microscopy was conducted on a multinucleated giant hemocyte (MGH) isolated from a histologic slide. RIs were calculated following American Society for Veterinary Clinical Pathology (ASVCP) guidelines, and life‐stage differences were statistically assessed.ResultsGranular cells and plasmatocytes predominated, whereas prohemocytes and oenocytoids were rare. MGHs, newly identified in T. molitor , were characterized as a fifth hemocyte population. Significant life‐stage differences were observed, warranting separate RIs for larvae and adults. The established RIs were relatively wide, reflecting natural fluctuations in invertebrate hemolymph volume and the unsexed, age‐diverse reference population.ConclusionsThis report of hematological RIs for T. molitor lays the groundwork for implementing hemocyte‐based health surveillance in yellow mealworm beetle farming.Keywords: Coleoptera, cytology, entomopathology, hematology, hemolymph, reference parameters

Introduction

Entomophagy, the consumption of insects, has long been practiced across various regions of the world. In recent years, this tradition has gained momentum in Western countries, driven by the rapid expansion of the edible insect industry [1]. Larvae of several species, such as the yellow mealworm beetle ( Tenebrio molitor ), are widely reared for their high protein and fatty acid contents. These insects display unmatched feed conversion rates, emit fewer greenhouse gases, and require far less water and land compared with traditional livestock [1, 2, 3]. Moreover, T. molitor and other species hold great potential in the circular economy due to their capacity to upcycle organic waste [1, 3, 4].

Infectious diseases pose a growing threat to the edible insect industry. Even well‐established insect farming systems—such as those of the honeybee ( Apis mellifera ) and the silkworm ( Bombyx mori ), once considered resilient—have increasingly suffered from viral and parasitic pathogens [5, 6]. Likewise, commercial rearing of house crickets ( Acheta domesticus ) has been plagued by recurrent mass mortalities worldwide due to densovirus outbreaks [7]. In T. molitor , pathogens including Serratia marcescens and Bacillus thuringiensis are frequently associated with elevated mortality rates, and a highly virulent densovirus has recently been identified [8, 9, 10]. Despite the scale and impact of these health challenges, the industry still lacks accessible diagnostic tools and sanitary surveillance systems to effectively monitor and control disease outbreaks.

Hematology, a routine veterinary diagnostic approach widely used for infectious disease screening in vertebrates, may provide a promising solution—provided several technical hurdles are overcome. Protocols for hemolymph collection and processing, the circulatory fluid of insects, remain poorly described for hard‐shelled beetles and are often impractical for clinical applications [11, 12]. Additionally, while plasmatocytes, granular cells, prohemocytes, and oenocytoids have been morphologically characterized in T. molitor , the classification of hemocytes, the circulating cells of hemolymph, remains controversial and requires further refinement [13, 14]. Lastly, to the authors' knowledge, reference intervals (RIs) for total and differential hemocyte counts—aligned with the guidelines of the American Society for Veterinary Clinical Pathology (ASVCP) [15]—have yet to be established for T. molitor , limiting the clinical applicability of hematology in this species.

The objectives of this study were therefore to develop practical protocols for hemolymph sampling, refine hemocyte type classification, and establish ASVCP‐compliant hematologic RIs in both larval and adult stages of T. molitor .

Material and Methods

Although compliance with the Canadian Council of Animal Care (CCAC) guidelines is not required for insects, we followed institutional ethical standards and applied an ethical approach to all animal‐involving procedures throughout the study.

Reference Population

Insects and Husbandry

The reference population of T. molitor (Figure 1) was obtained from TriCycle Entotechnologies (Montreal, Canada). Insects were reared on a substrate of oatmeal, bran, and spelt and were continuously provided with a variety of organic vegetables, mainly carrots and lettuce. Rearing trays measured 600 × 400 × 145 mm and were maintained at 25.5°C–27°C and approximately 55% relative humidity under natural daylight conditions. Larvae used for hemolymph smears (n = 42) were 10 weeks old (mostly final instar), whereas those used for hemocytometer counts (n = 227) ranged from 7 to 11 weeks of age (mostly final or late instars). Adults had undergone metamorphosis 6–12 weeks prior to sampling (n = 80 for smears and n = 208 for hemocytometer counts). All larval and adult samples were collected between June and August 2023.

Life stages of Tenebrio molitor. (A) Larva (juvenile stage), (B) pupa (intermediate, sessile stage), (C) adult yellow mealworm beetle (imaginal stage). Scale bar = 10 mm.

Health Assessment and Exclusion Criteria

The reference population had no known history of diagnosed or suspected disease. Bacteriological and mycological cultures were performed on 16 larvae from the same colony as the reference population and did not yield clinically significant growth. Health status was continuously monitored by recording metamorphosis events and daily mortality; any transient increase in deaths resulted in postponed sampling until resolution. Individuals with missing limbs, wounds, or signs of melanization were considered unhealthy and were excluded from the study.

Hemolymph Collection and Processing

Prior to hemolymph collection, T. molitor larvae and adults were anesthetized for 5 min in a dry ice chamber [16]. Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of response to tactile stimuli. To minimize bacterial contamination, individuals were immersed in 70% ethanol for 30 s and then patted dry.

Three hemolymph collection methods were evaluated to determine the most efficient approach—defined as the method yielding the highest hemolymph volume while remaining reproducible and practical.

Aspiration method: This method used either a 25G × 16 mm needle mounted on a 1 mL syringe or a 0.5 mL insulin syringe with a fixed 30G × 8 mm needle. The ventral pro‐mesothorax articulation, cervical membrane, and base of the coxa were tested as collection sites on at least five individuals of both life stages.

Tearing method: Hemolymph was collected from an exuding wound created by removing an appendage with aseptic tweezers or puncturing the body wall. The hemolymph was then collected using micro‐hematocrit capillary tubes or pipettes [11]. In adults, antennae, cervical membrane, proximal elytra, base of the coxa, and femoral‐tibial joint were tested in at least five individuals. Larvae were similarly evaluated at the same sites except the elytra.

Net centrifugation method: Insects, from which an antenna or a proleg had been amputated, were placed inside a truncated Eppendorf tube with a punctured bottom. As described by Niu et al. [11], this truncated tube was inserted into an intact Eppendorf tube, with a 15 μm nylon mesh (Tisch Scientific, Cleves, USA) placed between them. The assembly was then centrifuged under approximately 40 different regimens, ranging from 600 to 5400×g for 0.5–4 min. Each regimen was assessed in duplicate in both larval and adult stages.

Following exsanguination, euthanasia was confirmed by immersion in 70% ethanol. Since it was not always feasible to obtain sufficient hemolymph from a single T. molitor for hemocyte analyses, hemolymph from 3 to 6 larvae or 3 to 7 adults was pooled for hemocytometer samples, depending on availability and yield. Likewise, for the preparation of direct smears, hemolymph from a single larva or from 1 to 2 adults was collected within the same micro‐hematocrit capillary tube.

Smears were air‐dried for 30 min before Wright‐Giemsa staining with an Aerospray HematologySTAT automatic stainer (Elitechgroup Manufacturing and Distributors, Puteaux, France). To reduce hemocyte aggregation and preserve cell morphology, all equipment (micro‐hematocrit capillary tubes, transfer pipettes, Eppendorf tubes, hemocytometer, and slides) was pre‐chilled at 4°C, and all samples were processed within 2 min of collection. Different anticoagulants—EDTA, heparin, and sodium citrate—were tested in our smear preparation and hemocytometer counting protocols prior to sampling to evaluate their hemocyte preservation and anticoagulant properties. Clot formation in the dilution solution and/or the appearance of large hemocyte aggregates during hemocytometer reading were considered indicators of inadequate anticoagulation.

Hemolymph Analysis

Total hemocyte counts (THCs) and differential hemocyte counts (DHCs) were performed by a single operator—a veterinary student (SM)—under the supervision of a diplomate of the American College of Veterinary Pathologists (ACVP) (CGB).

THCs: Counts were performed manually with a Bright Line Neubauer hemocytometer (Hausser Scientific, Horsham, Pennsylvania, USA). A 4 μL aliquot of pooled hemolymph was diluted in 40 μL of chilled solution consisting of physiological saline with anticoagulant buffer, as previously described [17]. Phenylthiourea (3 mM; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was added to the anticoagulant buffer to inhibit melanization. Hemocytes were counted following standard red blood cell grid protocols, as shown in Figure 2 [18]. To enhance contrast for translucent hemocytes, the latter were tallied at 500× magnification with oil immersion, applying oil to the external surface of the specialized coverslip and verifying preservation of the chamber height—no traces of oil wicking at the chambers' edges. Moreover, hemocytes were allowed to settle and were counted at the bottom focal plane. For inter‐laboratory harmonization, we recommend using standard 400× high‐dry magnification for routine use [18]. The THC (109/L) was calculated using the formula: THC=C¯×110.2×0.1×1000, where C¯ is the average count of both chambers, 11 is the dilution factor, 0.2 is the counting area (mm2), 0.1 is the chamber depth (mm) and 1000 is the unit conversion factor [18]. Samples were excluded if clotting occurred or if the inter‐chamber difference exceeded 25%.

Schematic representation of a hemocytometer chamber illustrating the areas used for total hemocyte counts (THCs). Counting areas are highlighted in green and labeled H; chamber depth = 0.1 mm.

DHCs: Differential counts were performed on Wright‐Giemsa–stained smears by classifying 200 hemocytes with a 50× immersive objective. Cells were initially grouped into four categories: granular cells, plasmatocytes, prohemocytes, and oenocytoids. Based on new findings, a fifth population was later added. Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was also performed to refine classification prior to DHCs. Although paired DHC and THC analyses using the same pooled hemolymph were initially attempted, separate pools were ultimately prepared for each procedure to minimize handling effects: DHCs are therefore presented as percentages. Indeed, THC analysis requires longer processing times, which induce morphological changes on smears and introduce uncertainty in subsequent hemocyte classification.

Preparation of Histological Sections

After euthanasia by immersion in 70% ethanol, T. molitor adults were fixed in Schaffer solution (1:2 ratio of 37% formaldehyde to 90% ethanol). To soften the cuticle, a depilatory cream (Nair; Church & Dwight, Ewing, USA) was mixed with Schaffer solution in a 1:1 ratio during the first 4 h. Heads, thoraces, and appendages were removed to allow precise sectioning. Samples were processed with an automated tissue processor (Tissue‐Tek VIP 5; Sakura, Torrance, USA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned longitudinally, starting dorsally, into 3 μm slices. Slides were stained with hematoxylin–phloxine–eosin–saffron (HPS) [19]. A histological slide was submitted to the Western College of Veterinary Medicine (WCVM) Imaging Centre, where transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a giant multinucleated hemocyte (MGH) that was microdissected from a histological section.

Statistical Analysis

Reference intervals (RIs) were established according to ASVCP guidelines [15]. Reference Value Advisor (v2.1) and R Commander (v4.4.0) were used for data computation and statistical analyses, respectively [15, 20]. Unless otherwise specified, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reference intervals (RIs) were determined using the parametric method when the distribution was Gaussian, while non‐parametric approaches were applied for non‐Gaussian populations [21]. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, using a p‐value > 0.20 as the threshold, as recommended for small sample sizes [21]. When transformations were required to achieve normality, log transformations were first attempted, followed by Box–Cox transformations, in accordance with the principle of parsimony. Outliers were screened using Tukey's test, though retention was preferred given the defined reference population [15]. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare total and differential hemocyte counts between life stages.

Results

For RI determination, adult T. molitor used for total and differential hemocyte counts measured 13.0 ± 1.1 mm and 13.3 ± 1.0 mm in length, respectively. Larvae measured 23.8 ± 2.2 mm for THCs and 25.9 ± 1.8 mm for DHCs. No mortality spike in the colony supplying the reference population was recorded throughout sampling.

Hemolymph Collection and Processing

Although hemolymph collection methods have been described in beetles [11], they remain poorly documented for hematologic analyses. In this study, different collection sites and techniques were evaluated to identify those providing optimal volume and sample quality.

The aspiration method—using either a needle‐mounted syringe or an insulin syringe, and regardless of the collection site—produced volumes consistently below the needle capacity and was therefore abandoned. Among the various anatomic sites and techniques tested, the tearing method, involving transection of the prothoracic legs at the femoral‐tibial joint with forceps, followed by direct aspiration of the exuding hemolymph into micro‐hematocrit capillary tubes, proved to be the most efficient. This approach yielded the highest volumes at both developmental stages, with 4–8 and 8–16 μL collected per adult and larva, respectively. In contrast, sampling at the base of the elytra, cervical membrane, or antenna resulted in inconsistent hemolymph recovery, ranging from minimal to moderate yields. Although collection at the base of the coxa produced similarly high yields, the femoral‐tibial joint consistently resulted in fewer visible signs of contamination with digestive tract material. Accordingly, this approach was used for sample collection in this study.

In our hands, Niu et al.'s net centrifugation method [11] yielded lower hemolymph volumes, required longer processing time, and presented higher contamination rates with gut content, compared with the tearing method, irrespective of the centrifuge speed and other parameters; all tested protocols performed similarly poorly.

Following collection, hemolymph coagulation occurred almost instantly, posing challenges for hemocytometer readings. Several samples were excluded due to clotting when processing was not performed rapidly enough. Furthermore, hemocytes frequently underwent crenation and lysis within minutes. Among the tested anticoagulants—EDTA, heparin, and sodium citrate—none helped preserve hemocyte morphology on smears or prevent clotting on their own. However, sodium citrate seemed to provide a modest reduction in clotting when used in combination with chilled materials—citrate alone did not offer sufficient anticoagulation. Ultimately, cooling proved to be the critical determinant for reliable performance of both total and differential hemocyte counts, as uneven cooling of capillary tubes likewise resulted in rapid hemocyte degradation. Smear quality was unaffected by heparinized capillary tubes (Fisherbrand, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) but was negatively impacted by excessive handling and suction tools.

Hemocyte Classification

Light microscopy revealed five distinct hemocyte populations: granular cells, plasmatocytes, oenocytoids, prohemocytes, and multinucleated giant hemocytes (MGHs). Their morphological features and staining characteristics are shown in Figure 3.

(C) Hematoxylin–phloxine–eosin–saffron (HPS)–stained histological section showing a multinucleated giant hemocyte (MGH) and other hemocytes circulating within the dorsal vessel. (A) and (B) taken with a 100× oil‐immersion objective; (C) with a 50× oil‐immersion objective. Gr, granular cell; MGH, multinucleated giant hemocyte; Oe, oenocytoid; Pl, plasmatocyte; Pr, prohemocyte.

Granular cells displayed a homogenous, basophilic cytoplasm containing distinct, intensely eosinophilic granules and round‐to‐slightly ovoid nuclei staining purple with coarse chromatin on Wright‐Giemsa staining. Under PAS staining, the cytoplasm displayed diffuse cytoplasmic PAS‐positivity, while the nucleus and granules were unstained (PAS‐negative).

Plasmatocytes displayed an elongated/fusiform shape with a moderately basophilic‐to‐amphophilic cytoplasm with occasionally multiple clear vacuoles and ovoid nuclei with heterogeneous/moderately coarse chromatin with Wright‐Giemsa staining. PAS staining was variable with occasional faint pink dusting within the cytoplasm and PAS‐negative nucleus.

Prohemocytes were smaller and round in shape, displayed deeply basophilic cytoplasm with an often centrally placed circular nucleus, a high nucleus‐to‐cytoplasm ratio, and finely granular to coarse chromatin. PAS staining was variable within the cytoplasm and a PAS‐negative nucleus.

Oenocytoids presented larger oval cells with abundant homogeneous, lightly basophilic‐to‐amphophilic cytoplasm, a low‐to‐moderate nucleus‐to‐cytoplasm ratio with an eccentric round‐to‐ovoid nucleus with finely granular chromatin, occasionally small round and distinct nucleoli, and no evidence of cytoplasmic granules or extensions under Wright‐Giemsa staining. With PAS staining, the cytoplasm remained uniformly green with a PAS‐negative nucleus.

Multinucleated giant hemocytes (MGHs) were distinguished by their multinucleation and comparatively larger diameters (13.6 ± 2.4 μm) with an estimated nucleus‐to‐cytoplasm ratio of 0.8, prominent large nucleoli, and a broadly rounded outline with mild convex margins. On Wright‐Giemsa staining, the cytoplasm was deeply basophilic, and round‐to‐ovoid variably sized magenta nuclei with granular chromatin, with variation in nuclear size and numbers. PAS staining is largely negative (green) with scant focal pink staining, and nuclei are negative. MGHs frequently underwent endomitosis and exhibited highly variable diameters and nuclear counts (Figure 4). Although artifacts limited evaluation of overall morphology, nuclear structures could be characterized under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure S1). The nuclei were ovoid, with heterogenous chromatin resulting in a moderately stippled pattern, and nucleoli were round and homogeneous. MGHs regularly appeared in low numbers on adult hemolymph smears but were only exceptionally identified in larvae.

Photomicrographs of Wright‐Giemsa–stained hemolymph smears depicting multinucleated giant hemocytes (MGHs) in adult Tenebrio molitor: (A) smaller MGH, (B) midsize MGH undergoing endomitosis, and (C) larger MGH. All images taken with a 100× oil‐immersion objective.

Reference Intervals

Reference intervals for total and differential hemocyte counts are presented in Tables 1 and 2, while differences between life stages are illustrated in Figures 5 and 6. Significant differences in total hemocyte counts (THCs) were found between life stages (p = 0.000004). Regarding differential hemocyte counts (DHCs), significant differences were observed for granular cells (p = 0.006), plasmatocytes (p = 0.028), oenocytoids (p = 0.005), multinucleated giant hemocytes (MGHs) (p = 0.025), and for the granular cell‐to‐plasmatocyte ratio (p = 0.009). In contrast, no significant difference was observed for prohemocyte counts between life stages (p = 0.478).

Discussion

This study established hematological reference intervals (RIs) for the yellow mealworm beetle T. molitor . Significant differences in both total hemocyte counts (THCs) and differential hemocyte counts (DHCs) were observed between adults and larvae, underscoring the need for life‐stage–specific hematological RIs.

The tearing method identified in this study proved to be both user‐friendly and time‐efficient, consistently yielding samples suitable for hematological analyses. A similar preference for this technique has been reported in larvae of Tribolium castaneum (a darkling beetle, like T. molitor ) for hemocyte analyses [22]. Difficulties with aspiration techniques, also described in the walking stick Eurycantha calcarata, likely arise from the rapid coagulation of invertebrate hemolymph [12, 14, 23]. Attempts to use fixed needles to minimize hemolymph loss within the needle were unsuccessful due to insufficient negative pressure, resulting in minimal hemolymph flow.

Differences between our results and those reported using the net centrifugation method of Niu et al. [11] may be attributable to variations in centrifugation regimens. Because the original g‐force parameters of Niu et al. were unavailable, we tested several protocols of our own, which may have been less effective. Moreover, micro‐hematocrit capillary tubes were not evaluated in their tearing method. Although T. molitor and Saperda populnea (a longhorn beetle) are of similar size, the stouter build of the yellow mealworm beetle may have contributed to the observed disparities.

Invertebrate hemolymph is known to respond poorly to anticoagulants commonly used in vertebrates, such as EDTA and sodium citrate, consistent with our findings [12, 24]. Although formalin has shown anticoagulant effects in bivalve and chelicerate hemolymph, its extensive alteration of hemocyte morphology led us to exclude it from this study [12, 24, 25]. Instead, careful chilling of both the hemolymph and the equipment, coupled with rapid processing, proved crucial.

Pooling averages individual differences and therefore narrows the observed dispersion, with variance reduced roughly in proportion to pool size. Hence, analytical imprecision remains unaffected, although minor pooling error may contribute a small additional component of variance. Consequently, the THC RIs reflect pool‐level values appropriate for batch or colony surveillance, whereas single‐insect THCs may show wider variability than our intervals suggest. We report the pool‐size distribution to aid comparability and encourage future work to derive individual‐level THCs where feasible.

Under light microscopy, the four hemocyte types previously reported in T. molitor —granular cells, plasmatocytes, oenocytoids, and prohemocytes—were identified [13, 17]. In addition, a fifth blast‐like hemocyte population, referred to here as multinucleated giant hemocytes (MGHs), was also observed. Artifacts were ruled out, as this hemocyte type was visible within the dorsal vessel on a histological section (Figure 3C) and was consistently identified in multiple smear samples. Romanowsky‐stained smears revealed cytological properties similar to MGHs described in Bactrocera dorsalis (oriental fruit fly): multiple nuclei of varying sizes, prominent nucleoli, a mostly round shape with indentations, and the absence of cytokinesis or spindle formation [26]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of an MGH confirmed the presence of ovoid nuclei with heterogeneous chromatin and uniform nucleoli, although contrast limitations due to sample preparation hindered further ultrastructural characterization (Figure S1) [27, 28].

MGHs have previously been described in flies, particularly in the Drosophila ananassae subgroup, where they emerge during parasitoid infection and act as specialized effector cells [26, 27, 28]. Their primary role appears to involve the encapsulation of large foreign bodies, such as endoparasitoid eggs or larvae [27, 28]. To the authors' knowledge, this study represents the first report of MGHs in beetles as well as in the adult stage. While MGHs are predominantly described in infected larvae, low numbers of these cells have also been observed in naïve larvae of the drosophilid fly Zaprionus indianus, a finding consistent with our observations [28].

Among hemocyte types, oenocytoids and MGHs were the easiest to identify, owing to their eccentric and multinucleated nuclei, respectively. Granular cells, although often degranulated or lysed, were recognizable by their evidently eosinophilic granules—or their purple nuclei in instances of degranulation—under Wright‐Giemsa staining. However, distinguishing plasmatocytes from prohemocytes proved challenging, as reported for Hysterocrates gigas (Cameroon red baboon spider) [29]. Hemocytes frequently clustered on smears, causing uneven staining, while plasmatocyte morphology was often altered, with the fusiform shape becoming less apparent. In such cases, nuclear chromatin patterns (more heterogeneous in plasmatocytes) and nuclear shape (ovoid in plasmatocytes vs. circular in prohemocytes) were used for classification. Prior PAS staining further aided in differentiating ambiguous plasmatocytes from prohemocytes due to their distinct carbohydrate distribution. Similar variability in PAS staining among hemocytes has been reported in other arthropods [13, 30, 31].

Our findings regarding PAS staining differed from those of Vommaro et al. [13]. While both studies reported variable PAS responses for plasmatocytes and prohemocytes, we found oenocytoids to be PAS‐negative and granular cells to be consistently PAS‐positive. Vommaro et al., by contrast, described oenocytoids as PAS‐positive and granular cells as PAS‐variable.

Total hemocyte counts (THCs) were successfully measured in sufficient samples to establish reliable RIs according to ASVCP guidelines [15]. Jones [32] previously reported THCs ranging from 20 to 36 × 109 hemocytes/L (mean = 28) for 25–30 mm larvae (n = 10). Urbański et al. [17] also reported a mean THC of 8 × 109 hemocytes/L in larvae and 30‐day‐old adults, as well as approximately 15 × 109 hemocytes/L in 4‐day‐old adults (n ≥ 11 for each group). Our RIs for THCs showed a similar but slightly higher range, possibly due to the anticoagulant effects of chilling. Notably, formalin‐based anticoagulation has been shown to increase THCs by four‐ to six‐fold in freshwater mussel hemolymph [24]. Values obtained through heat fixation methods for THC are not discussed as their calculation involves sessile hemocytes [14, 33].

Significant differences in THCs between larval and adult stages were observed, as also reported in the ground beetle Carabus lefebvrei [34]. Urbański et al. [17] observed increased THCs in 4‐day‐old adults compared to larvae, but not in 30‐day‐old adults. We suggest that the higher THC observed in adults may be relative, reflecting lower hemolymph volumes in holometabolous imagines, which concentrate circulating hemocytes [14, 35]. This interpretation is supported by our own observations of consistently lower hemolymph yields in adults. In flies, centralized hemocytopoietic tissues such as the larval lymph gland undergo histolysis after pupation, releasing hemocytes and increasing absolute hemocyte counts [36]. Although the structure of hemocytopoietic tissues in T. molitor differs from that of flies and persists in adults, its functional dynamics during pupation remain poorly described [14, 37].

The sample size in our study was also adequate for determining RIs for DHCs in both larval and adult stages [15]. Granular cells and plasmatocytes represented the majority of hemocytes, while prohemocytes and oenocytoids were scarce, as previously reported in other beetles [13, 17, 34, 38]. The granular cell‐to‐plasmatocyte ratio was significantly higher in adults compared to larvae, a pattern observed in C. lefebvrei [34] but contrary to the findings of Urbański et al. [17] in 30‐day‐old adults. Additionally, whereas Urbański et al. [17] reported prohemocytes accounting for 10%–15% of DHCs, we observed only 0%–1%, further emphasizing the differences between studies.

These discrepancies may reflect differences in age distribution. Our larvae were near pupation, while Urbański's were immature. Similarly, our adults (6–12 weeks post‐metamorphosis) were older than those in Urbański's study (4‐ and 30‐day‐old adults). Age‐related shifts in the granular cell‐to‐plasmatocyte ratio have been reported, with notable decreases occurring in the first month following pupation [17]. This may result from the unidirectional differentiation of plasmatocytes into granular cells [14]. Prohemocyte numbers also decline with age in insects [39]. Although the dynamics of hemocyte populations across the full lifespan of T. molitor remain uncharacterized, age is likely a key factor influencing DHC profiles [33].

Moreover, the distinction between immature plasmatocytes and prohemocytes—already challenging under light microscopy—may have been influenced by differing classification criteria between studies, potentially further impacting DHC results [14]. In our study, applying consistent morphologic criteria together with PAS cytochemistry reduced ambiguity. Lastly, the lower number of oenocytoids in adult samples may be due to hemolymph pooling, as this hemocyte type is particularly prone to lysis [40]. Overall, our findings highlight the need for hematological reference parameters that are not only species‐specific but also stage‐ and age‐specific.

Few measurands had 90% confidence intervals (CIs) narrower than 0.2 times the RI width, indicating that a larger sample size is needed [15]. The broad range observed in THCs was expected, as invertebrates exhibit pronounced fluctuations in hemolymph volume [14]. However, additional limitations likely contributed to RI variability. The absence of sexing and the imprecise age determination due to housing conditions increased biological variability, as both age and sex can affect hemocyte counts [33]. Rapid hemocyte lysis during hemocytometer readings also imposed time constraints, limiting the number of fields that could be tallied. While hemocytes are comparable in size to vertebrate white blood cells, which typically require larger counting areas for accurate quantification, we relied on the standard grid areas used for red blood cell counts [18]. Fewer cells counted per chamber increased inter‐chamber variability, necessitating a 25% threshold for acceptable differences between chambers, higher than the recommended 10% standard [18]. Although reduced dilutions could have mitigated this variability, we excluded all samples with any evidence of clotting to maintain data reliability.

In summary, the tearing method developed here is well‐suited for hematological analyses of T. molitor hemolymph. This study, to the author's knowledge, provides the first life‐stage–specific hematological RIs for this species and expands hemocyte classification in beetles by reporting MGHs for the first time.
