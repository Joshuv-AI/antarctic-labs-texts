Exploring individual and structural factors influencing healthcare seeking behavior in the context of the Senegal’s Universal Health Coverage program: a multilevel analysis from the ANRS-12399 Soignants Sénégal study
Authors: Ousmane Diop, El Hadji Ba, Gwenaëlle Maradan, Marion Mora, Bruno Spire, Richard Lalou, Marwân-al-Qays Bousmah, Cheikh Sokhna, Luis Sagaon-Teyssier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough Senegal’s Universal Health Coverage (UHC) program has improved access to care, it has sometimes led to overuse of health services. Moreover, the program’s substantial financial debt to health facilities (HFs) has exposed them to organizational and financial problems, making it difficult to renew drug stocks and pay salaries on time. The multiple effects of the UHC program, both on individuals and on HFs, may discourage individuals from seeking for care in HFs. This study analyzed structural and individual factors associated with healthcare-seeking behavior in the context of Senegal’s UHC program.MethodsWe used data from six HFs in the mostly rural Fatick region that participated in two different two surveys: ANRS 12399 Soignants Sénégal and CMUtuelleS. We performed a multilevel logistic regression model to identify individual and structural factors influencing healthcare-seeking behavior.ResultsAmong the 313 individuals included, 63.3% were female and median age was 52 years (IQR [41⎯63]). Half the participants (50.5%) sought health services after experiencing a health problem in the two months prior to the survey. HFs, which the National Agency managing the UHC program (33.3%) owed most to, were better equipped and staffed, but also the most negatively affected by UHC implementation. Despite this, individuals sought care in these facilities just as frequently as in others, suggesting that being better equipped and staffed helped them to be more resilient to the negative effects of the UHC program. Individuals were less likely to seek care in smaller HFs. Instead, they were more likely to seek care in HFs providing HIV and tuberculosis care. Voluntary (i.e., paying) members (vs. other members) of community-based health insurance organizations, and individuals with a chronic illness (vs. without), were more likely to seek care. Persons with a disability (vs. without) and those experiencing food insecurity (vs. no food insecurity) were less likely to seek care.ConclusionBoth the financial support provided to individuals by the UHC program and structural characteristics of the healthcare system were associated with seeking healthcare. Taking greater account of these structural aspects when implementing UHC could enhance the program’s effectiveness and help achieve its objectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13611-x.Keywords: UHC, Senegal, Health system, Healthcare seeking behavior, Health facilities, Individual factors, Structural factors, Community-based mutual health insurance organizations (CBHI), Multilevel logistic regression, Healthcare workers (HCWs).

Background

Universal Health Coverage means that all people have access to the full range of quality health services they need without financial hardship. The UHC service coverage index (SCI) score, which measures the overall coverage of essential health services for all individuals, increased from 45 in 2000 to 68 in 2019, remaining steady through 2021 [1]. Despite this progress, approximately two billion people worldwide still experience financial hardship when accessing health services. For 344 million of these, the costs incurred for treatment in healthcare facilities (HFs) leads them into poverty [2]. Globally, the proportion of people whose households spent more than 10 per cent of their income on out-of-pocket (OOP) healthcare costs (i.e. catastrophic health expenditure), gradually increased from 9.6 per cent in 2000 to 12.6 per cent in 2015, and reached 13.5 per cent in 2019 [1]. A significant proportion of this population is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, especially countries in Sub-Saharan Africa [2, 3]. In addition to financial issues, other individual factors, such as having multiple comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, disability), not having health insurance coverage, a low education level, and certain cultural aspects (e.g., spiritual beliefs, use of traditional medicine) can all discourage the utilization of health services [4–13]. Moreover, factors related to the health system (structural factors hereafter) play an important role in healthcare seeking behavior (HSB). These include geographic location, healthcare-related costs, the availability of specific equipment (e.g., CT-scanners, X-ray machines, biological analysis laboratories) and how well HFs welcome patients [4, 8, 14]. Both individual and structural barriers, may result in unmet healthcare needs (UHN), i.e., care services required but not received due to these barriers [15]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, consequences of these unmet needs have been documented at the individual level, with worsened health status; and at the structural level, with increased healthcare expenditure and reduced economic productivity in the long term [16–18].

In Senegal, as in many other developing countries, such as Ghana, Burkina Faso and Kenya [4, 6, 13, 19], the abovementioned individual and structural obstacles seem to have a greater impact on HSB than in high-resource countries [20–23], and considerable efforts have been implemented since 2000 to mitigate them. Concerning individual factors, one of the major health-related initiatives implemented in recent years in Senegal is the UHC program, which was adopted in 2013. This program focuses on reducing OOP health expenditures, which accounted for 58% of total health spending in Senegal when it was introduced in 2013 [24]. In 2014, 16.5% of the population faced catastrophic health expenditure at the threshold of 10% [25], highlighting the urgent need for financial protection. This program is managed by the National Agency of the Universal Health Coverage (ANACMU). It comprises two components; one provides free access to healthcare for children under 5 years, adults over 60 years (Plan SESAME), patients on dialysis, and pregnant women requiring cesarean sections. The second component is based on community-based health insurance schemes/organizations (CBHI hereafter), which include two categories of beneficiaries [26]; the first consists of individuals who voluntarily join a CBHI and pay for membership in order to benefit from partial or full coverage for their medical expenses, depending on the health services they use. The second includes beneficiaries of the National Family Security Grant Program (PNBSF) which provides support to the most financially vulnerable households, and to beneficiaries of the Equal Opportunities Card program (CEC), which provides aid to people with disabilities. Beneficiaries in these programs are enrolled for free in a CBHI and receive 100% coverage, meaning no out-of-pocket expenses [27–29]. Since the launch of the UHC program in 2013, notable improvements have been observed including the reduction of UHN for family planning from 30% to 19% in the 2010–2023 period [30]; OOP health expenditures decreasing from 58% in 2013 to 36.4% in 2021 [25]; and the proportion of the population facing catastrophic health expenditures at the 10% threshold dropping from 16.5% in 2014 to 6.9% in 2018 [1]. Moreover, health risk coverage increased from 20% in 2013 to 53% in 2020, although this figure is still lower than the national target of 75% initially set for 2017, and which has been maintained for 2027 [31, 32].

Concerning structural factors, Senegal has developed programs for the recruitment of health care workers (HCWs); one example is Plan Cobra, which focuses on improving healthcare delivery in rural areas [33]. It has also invested considerably in training and in the construction of HFs. For example, between 2016 and 2023, the number of HCWs at the national level increased by 58% [34]. More specifically, between 2012 and 2023, Senegal constructed five hospitals, 15 health centers and 300 health posts [34, 35]. During that period, the national health budget was increased by 122% [36, 37]. Despite these structural efforts, the impact on health system and its outcomes have been modest. The maternal mortality ratio decreased from 392 to 236 per 100,000 live births, and neonatal mortality from 29 to 23 per 1,000 live births [34]. Furthermore, the UHC SCI rose from 37 in 2010 to 50 in 2021, indicating that significant gaps remain in achieving the various goals of UHC in the country [1]. In addition, medical equipment and the number of qualified HCW are insufficient in the country, with only 0.1 doctors and 0.4 nurses and midwives per 1,000 inhabitants [38]. This shortage is more pronounced in rural areas [21]. For example, in 2022, Dakar, the capital city, had a surplus three dental surgeons and 96 doctors in health centers, while the Fatick region, which is predominantly rural [39], has a deficit of four dental surgeons and five doctors [21].

These figures suggest that the healthcare system is not meeting the needs of a large proportion of the population of rural areas [31, 34]. In particular, the UHC program’s lack of effectiveness, especially in terms of health risk coverage, is partly due to its lack of financial autonomy. The program largely relies on state subsidies often leading to chronic delays in reimbursements to HFs, which in turn compromises its sustainability [32, 40–42]. These financial problems are the source of major organizational and management difficulties for HFs. For example, late reimbursements mean HFs have lower revenue to provide free care [43, 44], while still having to purchase medicines from suppliers and pay HCWs’ salaries in order to ensure the supply of free-of-charge UHC initiatives [26, 41, 45]. In addition, access to free care under UHC unexpectedly led to an overuse of health services [32, 40]; this may lead to an increase in HCWs’ workload, ultimately worsening the already poor working conditions these professionals face. The consequences of all these real and potential structural consequences is that the quality of care is compromised. Furthermore, the financial constraints of the UHC program undermine its ability to provide adequate financial protection to the population; in reality therefore, individuals are sometimes charged for services that are supposed to be free [13, 45]. This complex situation weakens trust in the health system [13], and accordingly individuals’ motivation to seek care.

Despite this context, to our knowledge, no study to date has jointly investigated the individual and structural factors associated with HSB in Senegal, especially in the context of the country’s UHC program. Accordingly, the present study aimed to provide a better understanding of these factors, and to assess whether or not they are related to effects induced by UHC on individuals and HFs. We hypothesized that the UHC program weakens HFs’ financial and organizational dimensions, particularly those at the bottom of the health pyramid (i.e., health posts and health centers). Consequently, while the program aims to increase utilization of health services, paradoxically, it negatively affects these same services.

Materials and methods

The present cross-sectional quantitative study used data from two different sources: the ANRS 12399 Soignants Sénégal study and the CMUtuelleS study. The former is a mixed-methods research project conducted between October 2022 and May 2023 in Dakar and in the rural Fatick and Kédougou regions. It focused on the activity of health structures and HCWs’ working conditions (i.e., structural aspects). Specifically, it collected data on HF organization, human and material resource availability, and the range of services provided. Additionally, it explored the availability of UHC initiatives in HFs and the organizational changes these initiatives induced. Data were collected using questionnaires administered to HF managers.

CMUtuelleS is a cross-sectional health survey conducted between 2019 and 2020 in the Fatick region. It collected data on individual and household-level socio-demographic characteristics, household expenditure (including health expenditure), health insurance status, HSB, access to healthcare and participants’ healthcare needs, as well as participant-perceived quality of the HF services they had previously used [42, 46].

Participants and data collection

Data were collected from 313 individuals who visited six HFs in the Fatick region (one regional hospital in the town of Fatick, two health centers (located in the towns of Fatick and Niakhar), and three health posts (located in the villages of Toucar, Ngayokhème, and Diohine Public)). All six HFs were common to both the ANRS 12399 Soignants Sénégal and CMUtuelleS studies.

Specifically, from the CMUtuelleS database, which contained 1,787 adults, we identified 418 individuals who reported they had fallen ill in the two months preceding our survey. Of these, the 313 who reported that they either consulted for that illness in one of the six selected HFs or that they usually sought care in one of them, were retained in the analysis.

Eligibility criteria were as follows: was ill in the two months preceding the survey and visited one of the six selected HFs for this illness or did not visit any of them for this illness, but usually visited one of them when sick.

Outcome

The study outcome was seeking care in the two months prior to the survey. It was based on CMUtuelleS participants’ being ill in the previous two months (yes = 1, no = 2) and on whether they had visited a HF for this illness (yes = 1, no = 2). Individuals were considered to have sought care if they had been ill in the two months preceding the survey and had visited an HF for that illness (= 1); otherwise they were considered to not have sought care (i.e., ill two months previously but did not seek care) (= 0).

Statistical analysis

We first conducted descriptive analyses of the ANRS Soignants Sénégal and CMUtuelleS datasets.

The pertinence of using a multilevel logistic model was tested by comparing level 2 (i.e., individual and structural levels) and level 1 (i.e., individual level only) of the null model using the LR-test, which tested to see if differences in HSB were explained by individual and/or structural characteristics. We estimated the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess the amount of variability in the outcome due to differences between the six HFs. Each explanatory individual variable was subsequently tested at the individual level in a univariate multilevel logistic regression. Using the stepwise forward procedure, the final multivariable model for individual characteristics was selected based on both p-values and the AIC criterion. This final model was used to perform a bivariate analysis of the structural factors. The eligibility criteria used for individual factors (i.e., a p-value < 0.20 and the AIC criterion) were also applied to structural variables in order to construct the final multilevel regression. All analyses were conducted using STATA 17.1 software.

Health facility characteristics

Size and availability of healthcare services

Three of the six HFs operated 24/7 and had existed for less than 40 years. Five offered immunization services and a pharmacy, three had an ultrasound machine, and only one had a CT-scanner. In terms of type of HF, the health center in Fatick town had more medical equipment, more qualified HCW and support staff, and provided a wider range of health services, particularly in HIV and tuberculosis services than the health center in Niakhar. The same was true for the health post in Ngayokhème with respect to the other two health posts. More details about the characteristics of all six HFs are described in Appendix C.

Concerning the ‘Size and availability of healthcare services’ overarching variable reflecting ‘small-scale’ and ‘large-scale’ HRs (see above), the first two dimensions explained 85.1% of the cumulative variance (62.6% for dimension one and 22.5% for dimension two). The contribution of the variables to the dimensions is detailed in Appendix D. All three health posts were categorized as small-scale (less equipped, provided fewer services and had fewer qualified HCW), while the regional hospital and two health centers were categorized as large-scale. The vast majority of participants (92.7%) sought care in small-scale HFs (p < 0.001). Appendix E presents the profiles of the six HFs based on the MCA and the HCA.

UHC-related services

Concerning UHC initiatives, all six HFs provided health services which did not require specialized skills or ‘sophisticated’ medical equipment (e.g., dialysis, an operating room). For example, all provided free health care to adults over 60 years old and children under 5. Moreover, all six consulted CBMI members. In contrast, only two HFs (regional hospital and health center in Fatick town) offered dialysis and only one (regional hospital) provided cesarean sections (Appendix C).

Concerning the ‘UHC-related services’ variable reflecting ‘limited’ and ‘comprehensive’ UHC services, the MCA showed that the first two dimensions explained 77.3% of the cumulative variance (64.3% for dimension one and 13% for dimension two) (Appendix C). Four HFs (all three health posts and the health center in Niakhar) were categorized as ‘limited’ as they offered only three UHC initiatives, while the other two HFs (the regional hospital and the health center in Fatick town) were categorized as ‘comprehensive’, as they provided at least four UHC initiatives (Appendix E). The vast majority of individuals (96.2%) sought care in HFs with limited UHC services, suggesting that these basic services were sufficient to meet their health needs (p < 0.001).

UHC effects on HFs

The implementation of the UHC program had major effects on HFs, especially on their financial and organizational dimensions. In particular, three HFs (Fatick regional, health center in Fatick town, and health post in Ngayokhème) were owed over 10 million CFA francs by ANACMU. In addition, four HFs (the three health posts and the health center in Fatick town) reported a long delay in reimbursement. Three of them (the health center in Fatick town, and the Ngayokhème and Diohine Public health posts) had seen their organization deteriorate because of the UHC program. Moreover, the increased health demand induced by the program in the health post in Ngayokhème led to understaffing. To mitigate this, the health post hired new HCWs to ensure UHC initiatives would be adequately covered. New HCWs were also taken on in the health center in Fatick town because of increased demand, and some personnel were reassigned there to ensure UHC coverage (Appendix C).

Concerning the ‘UHC effects on HFs’ variable reflecting HFs that were either ‘slightly’ or ‘strongly’ negatively affected by UHC, the first two dimensions explained 82.9% of the cumulative variance (71.8% for dimension one and 11.1% for dimension two) (Appendix D). Four HFs (the health posts in Toucar and Diohine Public, the health center in Niakhar town, and the regional hospital) were categorized as ‘slightly’ affected (Appendix E), while the health post in Ngayokhème and the health center in Fatick town were categorized as ‘strongly’ affected. Despite these serious negative effects on the two latter HFs, 50.8% of all the participants sought care there after falling ill in the previous two months, or usually sought care there (p < 0.001). This is most likely because they were better equipped and staffed with more qualified HCWs than their counterpart structures.

HIV and tuberculosis services

The health center in Fatick town was the only HF with comprehensive HIV and tuberculosis services. These services were significantly less available in the Toucar and Ngayokhème health posts. Specifically, the health post in in Toucar only provided tuberculosis care, while the health post in Ngayokhème only provided had two HIV services: screening and educational talks. (Appendix C).

Regarding the HIV and tuberculosis services overarching variable reflecting ‘limited’ and ‘comprehensive’ services, the two dimensions explained 84.3% of the cumulative variance (79.5% for dimension one and 4.8% for dimension two) (Appendix D). Two HFs (the health posts in Ngayokhème and Toucar) were categorized as ‘limited’ (i.e., providing a maximum of two HIV and tuberculosis services). The other four HFs were categorized as ‘comprehensive’ (i.e., providing at least three or more of these services). Most participants (78.3%) sought care in HFs with limited services (p < 0.001) (Appendix E).

To summarize, apart from the regional hospital, the health center in Fatick town and the health post in Ngayokhème were the two best-equipped and most staffed (i.e., in terms of qualified HCWs) HFs in their respective categories (i.e., type of facility). However, they were also the two HFs most negatively affected by the UHC program, facing long reimbursement delays, organizational challenges, and significant ANACMU-related debt. Despite these difficulties, half the participants (50.8%) sought care there after falling ill in the previous two months, or usually sought care there.

Individual and structural factors affecting the patients seeking-care behavior

The LR-test comparing level 2 and level 1 of the null model (LR = 9.85, p = 0.002) concluded that the observed variability in HSB was due to both individual characteristics and differences in HF structural characteristics, as indicated by the ICC, with structural differences accounting for 18% of the total variability in HSB.

The univariate analysis highlighted that people belonging to a household with at least one person who was a voluntary (i.e., paying) member of a CBHI were more likely to seek care after being ill in the two months prior to the study (p = 0.005). Participants who reported a deterioration of their health status in the previous 12 months (p < 0.001) and those with a chronic illness (p = 0.001) were also more likely to seek care. In contrast, persons living with a disability (p < 0.001) and those with food insecurity were less likely to seek care (p < 0.001). This pattern was also observed for individuals who found it difficult to find transportation (p < 0.001) and for those who reported that the distance from a HF was a problem (p = 0.049). Concerning structural factors, participants were more likely to seek care in small-scale HFs (p < 0.001) and HFs that provided comprehensive HIV and tuberculosis services (p = 0.060). See Appendix F for more details on the univariate analysis.

The multivariate analysis (Table 2) highlighted that the variance of structural factors (i.e., the ICC) was close to zero, indicating that differences among HFs were not significantly associated with differences in HSB. Despite receiving 100% health coverage free of charge, PNBSF beneficiaries were not more likely to seek care. Individuals from a household with at least one member of a CBMI were 76% more likely to seek care after becoming ill (OR = 1.76 [1.06─2.91], p = 0.029) compared to persons from a household with at least one PNBSF beneficiary (not significant, p = 0.129) and persons with no health insurance (i.e., reference group).

The LR-test statistic comparing level one and level two of the null model was 9.85. The null hypothesis indicating that two-level variability was equal to zero (H0: τ00 = 0) was rejected, as the LR-test statistic was higher than the value for the χ2(1) distribution at the 95% confidence level (3.84).

Furthermore, participants who found it difficult to find transportation to a HF (OR = 0.58 [0.44─0.76], p = < 0.001) and those reporting food insecurity (OR = 0.55 [0.34─0.91], p = 0.020) were less likely to seek care. Health status was also associated with HSB. Specifically, participants with self-perceived poorer health over the past year (OR = 2.40 [1.81─3.18], p = < 0.001) and those living with a chronic illness were more likely to seek care (OR = 1.93 [1.16─3.22], p = 0.012). Conversely, individuals living with a disability (visual, hearing or physical) were less likely to seek care (OR = 0.41 [0.31─0.53], p < 0.001).

Concerning structural factors, individuals whose self-declared preferred HF was one of the three small-scale structures identified, were 86% less likely to seek care than those who preferred a large-scale HF (OR = 0.14 [0.10─0.19], p = < 0.001). Participants were 24% more likely to seek care in the two HFs strongly negatively impacted by UHC (i.e., the health post in Ngayokhème and the health center in Fatick town) (OR = 1.24 [1.01─1.52], p = 0.036). In addition, people were more likely to seek care in HFs with comprehensive services HIV and tuberculosis care services (OR = 2.05 [1.66─2.54], p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our main finding was that structural factors play an important role in HSB in rural Senegal, particularly in a context where the UHC program has created major financial and organizational difficulties for HFs, especially for health posts and health centers. Specifically, four of the six HFs (the three health posts and the health center in Fatick town) reported long reimbursement delays [40, 45] – something also observed in similar contexts in Ghana [13] and Niger [43] – and three reported debts owed to them by the ANACMU exceeding 10 million FCA francs. These two major negative effects of UHC could perhaps be offset by ensuring higher numbers of qualified human and material resources in different types of HF. For example, despite being the two HFs most negatively affected by the UHC program, more people sought care in the health center in Fatick town and the health post in Ngayokhème either after falling ill in the previous two months, or in general (accounting for 50.8% of the sample vs. 49.2% for the other four structures combined). The greater utilization of these two HFs may reflect the mandatory care pathway the ANACMU’s imposes on its beneficiaries if they wish to be covered by a CBHI or to benefit from free care. Members of a CBHI must first consult a HF that has a signed agreement with that CBHI. They may subsequently be referred to a hospital for complementary care. Similarly, beneficiaries of free healthcare initiatives, particularly children under five years old and adults over 60, must first visit a health post or health center before seeking care in hospital, except in an emergency [26]. This situation limits the choices of UHC program beneficiaries compared to other individuals; the latter can go wherever they want for treatment as long as they pay. Accordingly, the two HFs in question most likely adapted to the mandatory care pathway by increasing recruitment and equipment. These various findings illustrate how critical it is for Senegalese authorities to provide greater support to rural HFs, both financially [21, 48] and in terms of the number of qualified HCWs [21, 49, 50], in order to prevent the current negative effects of the UHC program. Such support is also crucial to address the resulting UHN, which are identified as a direct consequence of structural deficiencies related to the lack of equipment and insufficiently qualified staff [17].

Concerning health insurance status, surprisingly, PNBSF (i.e., free care) beneficiaries were not more likely to seek care that the other participants. This finding is consistent with other studies in Senegal [11, 42], in Rwanda [51], and in Burkina Faso [19], suggesting that, eliminating financial barriers alone for the poorest populations is not enough to increase access to healthcare. These persons survive on a day-to-day basis, and the decision to seek care means having to choose between going to hospital or going to work to earn money. This lack of effectiveness of the PNBSF program may also be explained by the fact that its beneficiaries are not sufficiently well informed about the benefits of community-based health insurance membership [42]. Moreover, this program is dependent on state subsidies and external financing [41], which ultimately leads to long delays reimbursing HFs and the recurrent suspension of HF services for its beneficiaries [32, 41]. In addition, despite the fact that CBHI have limited financial means and that subsidies for PNBSF beneficiaries are not paid to them on time, these beneficiaries still use the services which these insurance organizations provide: this adds to the costs of care for CBHI, ultimately increasing their financial vulnerability. These challenges often translate into CBHI being reluctant to enroll PNBSF beneficiaries [11] or even excluding them outright from their services through risk selection processes. This limited access to CBHI for PNBSF beneficiaries discourages them from seeking care. In contrast, our results show that individuals who were relatively less poor than PNBSF beneficiaries and who paid for membership in a CBHI were more likely to seek care, suggesting that expanding health risk coverage in Senegal could rely primarily on CBHI in the long term for persons with sufficient means to afford membership fees. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of adapting health insurance schemes to the different socioeconomic profiles, at the same time that public policy is constructed to address structural and individual barriers. Indeed, a comprehensive approach should be adopted as the use of health insurance is a be key determinant of UHN, especially among the poorest individuals and those without financial protection [16, 17]. Our analysis highlights the significant challenge Senegal faces in reducing poverty, particularly in rural areas. In our sample, 41.6% of the participants were living in poverty, as evidenced by self-reported food insecurity. This rate is close to the 46.5% previously observed in the whole Fatick region [20]. This close relationship between poverty and a lack of access to HFs has already been well-documented, especially in West African countries such as in Burkina Faso, Togo, Nigeria and Ghana [52–55]. It is also reflected in the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development’s goals, where reducing poverty and UHC are closely interconnected [56, 57].

Proximity to HFs played an essential role in the use of healthcare services in our study, reflecting results from a study in Sierra Leone [58]. We found that persons facing the greatest transportation difficulties to go to HFs (i.e., 45% of the sample) were 42% less likely to use health services. Insufficient road infrastructure [59] and the limited number of HFs, especially well-equipped ones [21], significantly contribute to this issue. Residents in the Fatick region must travel an average of 15.6 km to reach a health center or 46.7 km to reach a hospital. For health posts, the distance is shorter, but is still more than three times the average distance people face in Dakar city (4.2 km vs. 1.2 km) [21]. Despite being relatively closer to the rural population, in our study, 86% of the sample were less likely to visit this type of HF, which suggests they do not meet the population’s needs. Health posts are generally small in size and typically have fewer qualified HCWs and less medical equipment. One potential solution, as suggested by our results, could be increasing basic human and material resources in health posts.

Facilities with comprehensive services for HIV and tuberculosis were more frequented by patients in our study. This expertise in infectious disease management probably contributes to a higher perceived level of trust among the general population. Other factors may also explain this result, including the fact that these HFs receive more funding and equipment for these infectious diseases. Some of this funding may translate into better general organization, and therefore better quality of care. This highlights the critical need to integrate these healthcare services into the broader health offer in order to optimize resource mobilization and utilization.

Our study demonstrates that health problems – in particular disability, poor self-perceived health, and chronic illness – were significantly associated with higher or lower HF utilization depending on the health problem. Moreover, despite having greater health needs [5, 6], people living with a disability were 59% less likely to use health services, reflecting findings from a study in Uganda [6]. This disparity may be attributed to financial vulnerability, inadequate transportation, stigma, and discrimination the latter population may experience [8, 60]. Moreover, HFs, even in developed countries, are not purposely designed to accommodate individuals with, for example, mobility impairment [14, 61]. Senegalese authorities should pay more attention to this often-marginalized population by upgrading health facilities to meet required accessibility standards.

This study has limitations. First, only data on individuals and healthcare facilities from the mostly rural Fatick region were used. Accordingly, our results cannot be considered representative of urban areas. However, one can hypothesize that difficulties for HFs and individuals may be more acute in rural areas located further away from the capital Dakar. Second, the selection process we chose meant that a proportion of participants in the CMUtuelleS study were included, which may have resulted in a loss of information. However, the similarity between our results and findings in previous studies using the same data (i.e., from the CMUtuelleS study) [42], particularly concerning CBHI, suggests that the selection process did not induce significant bias. Third, the analysis of structural factors was based on only six of the region’s 305 HFs, potentially reducing the representativeness of these structures. To address this limitation and to better reflect the reality of healthcare provision and use in Fatick, when constructing the HF profiles, in addition to the six participating HFs, we also took into account the characteristics of two other HFs (specifically health posts) in the region, which were part of ANRS 12399 Soignants Sénégal. In addition, the number of individuals included in the analysis was relatively small (n = 313), which may affect the robustness of the multilevel modelling and limit the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, CMUtuelleS and ANRS 12399 Soignants Sénégal were conducted in different periods (2019–2020 and 2022–2023. respectively). However, the 2019 and 2022 Annual Health Map Monitoring Reports showed no major changes in care provision [21, 62]. Fifth, although we attempted to collect data on the activity volumes of UHC initiatives to better reflect the utilization of these services, relatively persistent challenges to accurate archiving and traceability in the Senegalese health system [63] made it difficult to obtain detailed and high-quality data. Consequently, we opted not to ask HF managers to retrieve this information, as the related significant burden on in terms of the time and energy could have resulted in inaccurate data being provided and associated desirability bias. Finally, the cross-sectional study design did not allow us to examine the dynamic interactions between healthcare-seeking behavior and the evolving health system. Future longitudinal studies would be useful to address this limitation and inform long-term policy planning.

This work also has several strengths. First, it is the only study in Senegal to date to simultaneously analyze individual and structural factors influencing care-seeking behavior in the context of UHC implementation. Second, our results highlight that while the UHC program has facilitated HSB for individuals, it has also imposed significant structural challenges on HFs, particularly due to financial constraints for HFs themselves, as well as CBHI, and the state-sponsored PNBSF program. Third, our results underscore the need for a multi-sectoral approach to healthcare in Senegal to ensure that all populations, especially financially and physically vulnerable groups, can access healthcare without facing discrimination, transportation barriers, or, for the most precarious, the difficult choice between health and food security.

Conclusion

Our study shows that besides the need for adequate financial support, access to healthcare in rural Senegal depends on various socioeconomic factors, on individuals’ health status, and infrastructure quality. Despite the UHC program’s significant results in reducing financial barriers, our findings suggest that structural barriers hinder the access to care. In addition, it seems that the UHC program is not attractive for the poorest part of the population suggesting important UHN especially in rural areas. Our findings also question the broader effectiveness of Senegal’s health system, as it does not seem to sufficiently address inequalities among the most vulnerable populations both in health and financial terms. As conventional methods to facilitate access to care for these populations appear to be ineffective, tailored interventions grounded in a deeper understanding of the specific determinants of their healthcare utilization are needed.
