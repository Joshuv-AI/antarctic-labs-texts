Diagnostic Pitfalls in Hereditary Neurological Disorders: Machado–Joseph Disease Presenting as Charcot–Marie–Tooth Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Enoch Chi Ngai Lim, Chi Eung Danforn Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a 60‐year‐old Chinese woman with Machado–Joseph disease (MJD/SCA3), initially managed as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease due to distal sensory loss, pes cavus, and areflexia. Later cerebellar, bulbar, and ocular signs, together with ATXN3 CAG expansion, clarified the diagnosis and highlighted the need to revisit atypical hereditary neuropathy diagnoses.

Introduction

Machado–Joseph disease (MJD), or spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3), is the most common autosomal dominant ataxia worldwide [1], with a prevalence of 0.3–2.0 per 100,000 [2]. It results from a CAG repeat expansion in the ATXN3 gene, producing an abnormally long ataxin‐3 protein. MJD causes progressive cerebellar ataxia and may also include pyramidal signs, dystonia, parkinsonian features, ophthalmoplegia, bulbar dysfunction, and peripheral neuropathy [3]. Age of onset is variable: cases before 25 often show dystonia and rigidity, while onset after 50 more commonly involves peripheral neuropathy and distal muscle wasting [3]. No curative treatment exists, but supportive multidisciplinary care can improve safety and quality of life [3, 4].

Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT) disease consists of inherited peripheral neuropathies and is among the most common inherited neurologic conditions [5]. CMT commonly presents with slowly progressive length‐dependent neuropathy, distal weakness and atrophy, distal sensory loss, absent reflexes, and characteristic deformities such as pes cavus and hammertoes [5, 6]. Unlike MJD, typical CMT does not produce progressive cerebellar or major brainstem signs.

Diagnostic overlap can occur when MJD presents predominantly with peripheral neuropathy and minimal early ataxia, mimicking CMT [7]. We report a woman initially suspected to have CMT2 who was later recognized as having MJD/SCA3. This case emphasizes the importance of integrating family history, central neurological signs, imaging, and targeted genetic testing. Case History

A 60‐year‐old Asian woman of Chinese heritage presented with a 5‐year history of bilateral foot numbness and gait instability. She first noticed numbness in the soles of her feet in her mid‐50s. At that time, she also experienced difficulty maintaining balance on uneven surfaces. Walking on rocky ground was especially difficult. She reported episodes where her ankles turned outward (foot eversion) without warning, leading to near‐falls. To compensate, she adopted a wider stance when walking. Supportive high‐top shoes provided her with improved stability. She denied chronic back pain, radicular pain, or persistent burning sensations in the feet. She also reported no dizziness or vision changes. There were no complaints of hand clumsiness, speech difficulty, or swallowing problems at the time of presentation.

Medical and Family History

The patient had a history of gastroesophageal reflux disease and hypercholesterolemia. She was not taking any long‐term neurotoxic medications. A family history of neurological disease was noted. Both her mother and maternal grandfather had reportedly suffered from progressive “cerebellar atrophy.” This phrase reflected the patient's own description of what she had been told about her relatives; no primary medical records for those relatives were available. At age 53, she was evaluated by an independent neurologist (the first specialist she had seen) due to her family history and emerging symptoms. She underwent a brain MRI and genetic testing. She recalled being told she had a “positive gene for a neurological condition,” but she did not understand the name or significance of the disease. No follow‐up genetic counseling was provided.

Initial Neurologic Examination

In 2018, a specialist general physician (the second specialist she had seen) performed a neurological examination. Cranial nerve functions were intact. Eye movements were normal and there was no nystagmus. Motor strength was full (MRC [Medical Research Council] grade 5/5) in all muscle groups of both upper and lower limbs, with no marked asymmetry. Tone was normal with no spasticity or hypertonia. Coordination testing showed mild impairment. Finger‐to‐nose and heel‐to‐shin tasks were accurate, but tandem gait was abnormal. She could not walk heel‐to‐toe in a straight line. Her stance was slightly wide‐based, though she could turn without difficulty. Deep tendon reflexes were absent at the ankles and reduced at the knees. Reflexes in the upper limbs were normal. Sensory testing showed decreased light touch and proprioception in the feet, but vibration sense was not recorded. Examination also revealed bilateral high‐arched feet (pes cavus) and mild distal muscle wasting in the feet. No upper motor neuron signs were present. There were no tremors or involuntary movements.

The working impression favored a distal peripheral neuropathy because the sensory symptoms, pes cavus, distal wasting, and absent ankle reflexes aligned with hereditary neuropathy; the isolated tandem gait difficulty was not initially sufficient to override that impression.

Initial Investigations

Two brain MRI scans, spaced 5 years apart in 2013 and 2018, were reported as normal. No cerebellar atrophy, brainstem lesions, or demyelinating changes were seen. Routine blood tests were normal, including vitamin B12, thyroid function, HbA1c, and syphilis serology.

In 2021, she was referred to another neurologist (the third specialist she had seen) due to relocation of the second specialist's practice. Nerve conduction studies and electromyography showed an axonal sensorimotor peripheral neuropathy, most marked in the legs. Sural sensory responses were absent or low‐amplitude. Peroneal motor responses had reduced amplitudes with normal conduction velocities, consistent with axonal rather than demyelinating pathology. The available neurophysiology report did not document upper‐limb sensory action potentials or proximal sensory studies, limiting the ability to distinguish a sensory neuronopathy/ganglionopathy from a length‐dependent axonal polyneuropathy at that time. These results, together with pes cavus and distal wasting, supported a provisional diagnosis of Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 2 (CMT2). Genetic testing for CMT was discussed but deferred. The patient had no children and believed that genetic confirmation would not alter management. Supportive care was initiated. She was counseled on fall prevention and referred for specialty footwear. Physiotherapy for balance and strengthening was started. Over the next few years, her symptoms progressed slowly, and she attended periodic follow‐up visits.

Progression and Re‐Evaluation

By 2024, at age 60, her condition had worsened. She reported frequent falls, often once per week, especially when turning or walking on slippery ground. She required support from her spouse or a friend for longer walks. A cane provided only partial benefit. She also developed new symptoms. Her hands became clumsy, making fine‐motor tasks such as button fastening difficult. Speech became slurred over the past year. She reported urinary urgency, occasional incontinence, and bowel irregularities.

On neurological examination in 2025, clear cerebellar and brainstem features were observed. Speech was dysarthric. She had mild dysphagia, with occasional choking on liquids. Eye movement testing revealed saccadic pursuit and mild ocular dysmetria, though the range of motion remained full. Facial and tongue weakness were present, with fasciculations on the tongue. Motor testing showed mild proximal weakness (4/5 at the shoulders and hips) in addition to distal weakness. Upper limb coordination was impaired with mild intention tremor and dysmetria. Truncal ataxia was pronounced; she swayed even with a wide stance and needed support to prevent falls. Gait was broad‐based and unsteady. She could only walk a few steps with a cane before requiring assistance. Tandem gait was not possible. Deep tendon reflexes showed a mixed pattern. Upper limbs were brisk (3+). Knee jerks were reduced, and ankle jerks were absent. Babinski responses were negative. Sensory testing confirmed length‐dependent sensory loss extending to mid‐calf. This mixed central and peripheral pattern prompted reconsideration of the CMT diagnosis.

Diagnostic Reevaluation and Genetic Findings

The combination of cerebellar, brainstem, and peripheral findings prompted reconsideration of the diagnosis. The family history of “cerebellar atrophy” was reviewed again. A multisystem autosomal dominant ataxia was suspected, with MJD/SCA3 high on the differential. During organization of repeat genetic testing, the laboratory identified an old medical record showing that DNA testing had already been performed in 2013. The result confirmed MJD/SCA3 with an expanded ATXN3 allele of approximately 65 CAG repeats (normal reference range 12–44 repeats; the other allele contained 14 repeats). The patient had not understood this result, and it had not been communicated to her family, her general practitioner, or the neurologists and specialist physicians she had seen between 2013 and 2025. For years, she was managed under the assumption of CMT.

A repeat brain MRI in 2025 showed mild cerebellar atrophy and subtle T2 hyperintensity in the pons, possibly an early “hot cross bun” sign. These changes were absent in earlier scans. Repeat nerve conduction studies remained consistent with chronic axonal neuropathy in the legs. Upper‐limb and proximal sensory responses were not available in the reviewed reports. Overall, the clinical course, family history, imaging evolution, and ATXN3 expansion fitted the known spectrum of MJD/SCA3.

Discussion

This case illustrates a diagnostic pitfall in hereditary neurology: anchoring on a peripheral neuropathy diagnosis when early MJD/SCA3 is dominated by neuropathic features. The provisional CMT2 diagnosis was understandable because the patient presented with foot numbness, pes cavus, distal wasting, absent ankle reflexes, and normal early brain MRI. However, two clues were underweighted: a family history described as “cerebellar atrophy” and impaired tandem gait despite preserved limb strength. The absent lower‐limb reflexes and foot deformities likely anchored interpretation toward hereditary neuropathy and delayed reconsideration of spinocerebellar ataxia.

Peripheral nerve involvement is now recognized as a frequent manifestation of MJD/SCA3, with studies of genetically confirmed cohorts reporting sensory, sensorimotor, and small‐fiber involvement [11, 12, 13, 14]. Electrophysiological and pathological investigations indicate degeneration of dorsal root ganglia neurons and distal axons [11, 13, 14]. Because a sensory neuronopathy/ganglionopathy pattern can occur in MJD/SCA3, assessment of upper‐limb sensory action potentials and proximal sensory segments is important when attempting to distinguish MJD‐related sensory neuronopathy from a standard length‐dependent polyneuropathy [14]. In this case, the lack of documented upper‐limb and proximal sensory studies was a limitation of the early neurophysiological assessment.

Small‐fiber involvement has also been demonstrated in clinical and neurophysiological studies [12]. In contrast, CMT2 results from pathogenic variants in genes directly affecting axonal transport or mitochondrial dynamics, including MFN2, and modern genetic panels can identify many axonal CMT subtypes [8, 9]. Natural history reviews emphasize that CMT usually remains a slowly progressive peripheral neuropathy rather than a multisystem central neurodegenerative disorder [10]. These mechanistic and clinical differences explain why new cerebellar, pyramidal, ocular, or bulbar signs should prompt reconsideration of a CMT diagnosis.

There have been reported cases of MJD presenting as an isolated peripheral neuropathy for several years before ataxia becomes evident [7]. Sugiyama et al. described very late‐onset SCA3 in which 7 years of sensory neuropathy symptoms preceded clear cerebellar signs [7]. Similarly, in this case, several years elapsed while neuropathic features predominated. In retrospect, the family history of progressive cerebellar disease and early tandem gait difficulty should have prompted earlier review of the prior genetic result or repeat ATXN3 testing.

The 2025 MRI finding of mild cerebellar atrophy with subtle pontine T2 hyperintensity also requires careful interpretation. The “hot cross bun” sign is classically associated with multiple system atrophy‐cerebellar type (MSA‐C), but it is not specific to MSA‐C and has been reported in genetically confirmed spinocerebellar ataxias, including SCA2 and SCA7 [15]. In this patient, the autosomal dominant family history and confirmed ATXN3 expansion support MJD/SCA3; the pontine signal change is best interpreted as part of pontocerebellar tract degeneration within the genetic ataxia spectrum rather than as evidence of a separate sporadic MSA‐C diagnosis. Urinary urgency was therefore considered in the context of confirmed MJD/SCA3 and overall clinical evolution.

The educational value of this case extends beyond the CMT mimic. MJD/SCA3 is phenotypically heterogeneous and can mimic other neurological disorders, including a parkinsonian phenotype [16], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or motor neuron disease [17], and dystonia that may respond to levodopa [18]. Recognizing this breadth is important when patients have a mixed phenotype or when a single diagnostic label does not explain the evolving examination.

Conversely, although typical CMT is peripheral, rare CMT presentations can include central or movement‐disorder features. CMTX1 has been reported with recurrent stroke‐like CNS episodes triggered by high‐altitude exposure [19], and genetically confirmed CMT2A has been reported with postural tremor [20]. These exceptions reinforce the need for genotype–phenotype correlation rather than relying only on a central‐versus‐peripheral distinction. In the present case, progressive cerebellar, brainstem, and bulbar involvement, together with the ATXN3 expansion, outweighed the CMT‐like neuropathy phenotype.

Another lesson is the importance of communication after genetic testing. The correct genetic diagnosis had been made in 2013, but the patient did not understand its significance and subsequent clinicians managed her under a different diagnostic framework. Genetic results should be explained in clear language, with interpreter support where needed, and communicated to the patient, general practitioner, and relevant treating specialists. For adult‐onset neurogenetic disorders, counseling should also address prognosis, cascade testing, and the possibility that early symptoms may not reflect the full disease spectrum.

Given the overlap between MJD‐associated neuropathy and CMT2, several red flags should prompt reconsideration of an initial CMT diagnosis: impaired tandem gait or dysmetria; ocular pursuit abnormalities; pyramidal signs such as brisk upper‐limb reflexes; dysarthria or dysphagia; a family history suggestive of autosomal dominant cerebellar degeneration; onset after 40–50 years; and neurophysiology that does not fully match a typical hereditary neuropathy profile [3, 10, 11, 14]. When these red flags are present, clinicians should consider MJD/SCA3 or another spinocerebellar ataxia and proceed with targeted repeat‐expansion testing where appropriate.

Conclusion

MJD is a diagnostically challenging disorder when early manifestations overlap with peripheral neuropathy features common to CMT disease. This case shows how MJD/SCA3 can initially be mistaken for CMT2 when neuropathic symptoms, pes cavus, and areflexia dominate the presentation. Clinicians should reconsider spinocerebellar ataxia in patients with atypical or late‐onset neuropathy, especially when there is a suggestive family history, impaired tandem gait, ocular or bulbar signs, or progression beyond a peripheral neuropathy pattern. Targeted ATXN3 repeat‐expansion testing and clear communication of genetic results are essential to prevent diagnostic delay and to guide counseling and supportive care.
