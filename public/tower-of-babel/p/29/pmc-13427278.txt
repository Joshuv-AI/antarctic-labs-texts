Comparing the incidence of epistaxis during nasotracheal intubation with Ring-Adair-Elwyn tubes using virtual versus flexible nasal endoscopy in patients undergoing elective oral and maxillofacial surgeries – A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Chanjeeviram Sugantha Priya, Chitra Rajeswari Thangaswamy, Lenin Babu Elakkumanan, Balasubramanian Krishnan, Krishnan Nagarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Nasotracheal intubation is commonly used in maxillofacial surgeries but often causes complications, with epistaxis being the most frequent. Selecting the more patent nostril and appropriate tube size can help reduce bleeding. Flexible nasal endoscopy (FNE) is traditionally used for this assessment but is invasive. Virtual nasal endoscopy (VNE), a non-invasive imaging technique, may offer an alternative. This randomized controlled study aimed to compare the incidence of epistaxis during nasotracheal intubation when guided by VNE vs FNE.Material and Methods:Sixty-four patients were enrolled. Preoperatively, VNE using OsiriX Lite software identified the more patent nostril and endotracheal tube (ETT) size. On surgery day, FNE was performed to assess the same. Patients were randomized into Group V (virtual) or Group E (endoscopy), with nostril side and Ring-Adair-Elwyn (RAE) tube size chosen accordingly. Incidence and severity of epistaxis during laryngoscopy and before throat packing, ease and time of insertion, manipulations, and number of attempts were recorded.Results:The incidence and severity of epistaxis were significantly less in group V than group E at laryngoscopy (18.8% vs 59.4%, P < 0.001) and during throat packing (31.2% vs 84.4%, P < 0.001). The time taken for ETT insertion, tube impingement, and manipulations was also significantly reduced in group V.Conclusion:Our study showed that the incidence of epistaxis was significantly less in VNE than in FNE-guided intubation.

Introduction

Nasotracheal intubation is routinely performed in patients undergoing oromaxillofacial surgeries, with epistaxis being the most common complication.[1] The reported incidence ranges from 18% to 77%.[2] The risk increases with excessive force, repeated attempts, and abnormal nasal anatomy. Selecting the more patent nostril and an appropriately sized endotracheal tube (ETT) are key factors in minimizing epistaxis.

Although nasal patency can be assessed through patient history and various bedside tests, these methods have a high diagnostic failure rate in detecting intranasal abnormalities.[345] Flexible fibreoptic endoscopy offers direct visualization of both anterior and posterior nasal cavities, facilitating the selection of the more patent nostril.[56] However, it requires technical expertise and specialized equipment. Hence, there is a need for an alternative technique to identify the patency of the nostril.

Morra et al.[7] demonstrated that virtual nasal endoscopy (VNE) offers clearer visualization of the nasal cavity than conventional endoscopy. Han et al.[8] further noted that virtual endoscopy can access narrow regions, such as the meatus, which are often difficult to visualize with standard nasal endoscopy.

Hence, we planned to study the feasibility of using VNE to select the more patent nostril and appropriate ETT size. To our knowledge, no randomized controlled trials have compared virtual and flexible nasal endoscopy (FNE) for evaluating the incidence of epistaxis. We hypothesized that VNE would result in a lower incidence of epistaxis compared to FNE when nostril side and tube size are determined by these methods. The primary objective was to assess the incidence of epistaxis. Secondary objectives included ease of intubation, required manipulations, number of attempts, postoperative pain and nasal obstruction.

Material and Methods

This prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at a tertiary care institute after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (JIP/IEC/2021/015) and registration with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2019/04/018347). Eligible patients were recruited after obtaining written informed consent in their native language, following a detailed explanation of the study procedure. Adult patients scheduled for nasal intubation as part of elective oral and maxillofacial surgeries, and who had undergone a CT scan of the head with 1.5 mm sections, were included. Patients with a history of restricted mouth opening (less than 3 cm), anticipated difficult airway, skull base fracture, or recurrent epistaxis were excluded from the study.

All patients underwent routine preanesthetic evaluation. In addition, a detailed history was obtained regarding nasal symptoms, including the degree of nasal obstruction (fully patent, partially obstructed, or completely obstructed), history of sinusitis, and nasal pain. Nostril patency was assessed at the bedside in all patients using the conventional spatula test. CT images were reconstructed at 1 mm intervals in the axial plane, with coronal reconstructions obtained perpendicular to the axial plane. In all patients, VNE was performed by a consultant anesthesiologist on the day prior to surgery, using OsiriX Lite software (Pixmeo SARL). The more patent nostril was identified through VNE [Figure 1]. The narrowest distance between the inferior turbinate and the nasal septum was measured on the CT images [Figure 2]. Based on this measurement and the external diameter of the appropriate Ring-Adair-Elwyn (RAE) ETT, the most suitable tube size was selected.

Patients were premedicated on the day prior to surgery with tablet famotidine 20 mg and tablet diazepam 5 mg. Nasal preparation was carried out by instilling xylometazoline nasal drops in both nostrils. On the day of surgery, injection glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg was administered intramuscularly. In the operating room, a consultant anesthesiologist who was blinded to the VNE findings, performed flexible fibreoptic nasal endoscopy after administering a nasal decongestant in both nostrils. The nasal mucosa, presence of polyps, deviated nasal septum, nostril patency, nasal cavity structures, and any anatomical abnormalities were examined and documented. Endoscopic images were captured and recorded. Based on the fibreoptic findings, the more patent nostril was identified. Both VNE and flexible fibreoptic nasal endoscopy were performed in all patients, and data were noted.

Following evaluation, patients were randomized into one of two groups. Block randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number table with a fixed block size of four. The randomization sequence was prepared by a person not involved in the study to avoid bias. Allocation concealment was maintained using serially numbered, opaque, and sealed envelopes, which were opened only after patient enrollment. In group V, both the nostril selection and the size of the RAE ETT were determined based on findings from the VNE. In group E, nostril selection was guided by findings from the flexible fibreoptic nasal endoscopy, while the RAE ETT size was chosen based on standard sex-based guidelines: 6.5–7.5 mm internal diameter for males and 6.0–7.0 mm for females.

Under standard monitoring, including non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), pulse oximetry (SpO2), and electrocardiography (ECG), patients were preoxygenated with 100% oxygen for three minutes. Anesthesia was induced with intravenous fentanyl 2 mcg/kg, thiopentone 5 mg/kg, and vecuronium 0.1 mg/kg. Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane and nitrous oxide to achieve a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 1. A third anesthesiologist with more than 3 years of experience had performed the nasotracheal intubation and was blinded to the preoperative virtual and FNE measurements.

The ETT cuff was checked for integrity and deflated. The RAE ETT was pre-warmed and lubricated with a water-soluble jelly. The tube was introduced into the assigned nostril, directed along the floor of the nasal passage, and advanced into the nasopharynx up to 10 cm. Then, video laryngoscope was performed to guide the ETT into the trachea, with or without the aid of Magill’s forceps.

Epistaxis was assessed visually via the video laryngoscope. If the tube could not be advanced into the nasopharynx, additional maneuvers such as rotation or alteration of angulation were attempted. A maximum of two intubation attempts was allowed. Failure to intubate within two attempts was considered a failed technique, and such patients were withdrawn from further study participation. In these cases, the attending anesthesiologist selected an alternative ETT size or changed the nostril side at their discretion. Vital parameters were continuously monitored and recorded until 15 minutes after intubation.

Epistaxis was evaluated at two time points: during laryngoscopy and prior to throat packing. The severity of epistaxis was classified into four grades based on visual assessment of the posterior pharyngeal wall: no bleeding (no blood observed), mild bleeding (presence of blood), moderate bleeding (blood collected), and severe bleeding (pooling of blood in the pharynx). The ease of intubation, which was categorized as either smooth or impinged, and the time taken to advance the ETT up to 10 cm into the nostril were recorded. Any manipulations required during insertion were documented, including the type of maneuver (e.g., change in angulation or tube rotation). The number of intubation attempts (defined as the removal and reinsertion of the ETT into the nostril) was also recorded. Postoperative nasal pain was assessed immediately after extubation and on postoperative day one. Postoperative nasal obstruction was evaluated at the same time points and graded as fully patent, partially obstructed, or completely obstructed. Additionally, the correlation between VNE and FNE findings was analyzed.

The sample size was calculated using the Sample Size Calculator for Clinical Research app (version 2.0, June 2014, developed by Manon Girard, United States), based on the incidence of epistaxis as the primary outcome variable. A previous study by Sugiyama et al.[9] reported an epistaxis incidence of 50% with the Portex tube, and we expected the incidence to be 25% in the test group. To detect a 50% reduction in the incidence of epistaxis, with a power of 80%, an alpha error of 0.05, and a superiority margin of 0.5, a minimum of 29 patients was required in each group. To account for potential dropouts, three additional patients were included per group, resulting in a total sample size of 64 patients.

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., USA). Analyses were performed under the assumptions that the two groups were independent and that variances within each group were equal. Continuous variables, including age, weight, time taken for ETT insertion, pulse rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables, such as gender, ETT size, nostril selected, preoperative history of sinusitis, nasal pain, nasal obstruction, incidence of epistaxis, ease of intubation, need for manipulation, postoperative nasal pain, and postoperative nasal obstruction, were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Independent t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between groups, except for respiratory rate, which was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normal distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Agreement between virtual and FNE in terms of nostril and ETT size selection was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 88 patients were assessed for eligibility, out of whom 64 patients were randomized into two groups, with 32 patients in each group. Data from all 64 patients were collected and included in the statistical analysis [Figure 3]. Demographic parameters such as age, weight, and gender were comparable between the groups. Preoperative nasal obstruction and nasal pain were comparable between the groups [Table 1].

The incidence of epistaxis at laryngoscopy was significantly less in group V than in group E [18.8% (six patients) vs 59.4% (19 patients), P-value < 0.001]. In both groups, no patients had moderate or severe epistaxis. At throat packing, patients in group V had significantly less incidence of epistaxis than group E (31.2% vs 84.4%, P value < 0.001). In group V, 10 (31.2%) patients had mild epistaxis, and in group E, 11 (34.4%) patients had mild, and 16 (50%) patients had moderate epistaxis. No patient had severe epistaxis in group E [Table 2].

Comparison of incidence and severity of epistaxis

Patient hemodynamic parameters were comparable between the two groups. The correlation of ETT size and nostril selection based on the two methods was found to have slight agreement. The percentage of agreement was 37.5% and 59.38% with a kappa value of 0.02 and 0.16, P value of 0.64 and 0.09, respectively, which were statistically insignificant. The time taken for insertion was significantly less in group V (5.3 ± 1.5 vs 7.6 ± 1.7 seconds, P value < 0.001). In group V, 29 patients were intubated in the first attempt, whereas in group E, 23 patients were intubated in the first attempt, P value of 0.05.

Additional manipulations were required significantly more in group E than in group (90.6% vs 65.6%, P value = 0.01). Among these, in group V, 16 required rotation, and five patients required rotation with a change in angulation. In group E, 17 patients required rotation alone, 12 patients required rotation with a change in angulation, P value = 0.02 [Table 2]. Postoperative nasal pain and postoperative nasal obstruction were comparable between the two groups.

On subgroup analysis, epistaxis at laryngoscopy among patients who were intubated in the first attempt was significantly less between group V and group E (4 vs 14 patients had mild epistaxis, P value = 0.00). Epistaxis at the time of throat packing among patients intubated in the first attempt was significantly less in group V as compared to group E (eight patients had mild epistaxis vs nine patients had mild and 10 patients had severe epistaxis, P value = 0.00).

Discussion

In our study, we had observed that the incidence of epistaxis was significantly less in VNE than in FNE, both at laryngoscopy and at the time of throat packing. In subgroup analysis, among patients who were intubated in the first attempt, the incidence of epistaxis at laryngoscopy and at the time of throat packing was significantly less in VNE.

Previous studies had shown that the incidence of epistaxis in nasotracheal intubation ranged from 18% to 77%.[2] In our study, we used xylometazoline nasal drops, softened the ETTs by immersing in warm saline, and well-lubricated ETT tips to reduce the epistaxis in both groups. These factors might have prevented the occurrence of severe epistaxis in our study. Özkan et al.[10] found that the north polar tube reduced the incidence of epistaxis when compared to the spiral tube. In our study, we had used the RAE north-facing ETT for nasotracheal intubation. The hemodynamic parameters, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiratory rate were comparable between the two groups. Kwon et al.[11] observed that there was no statistical difference in postoperative complications. In our study, the postoperative nasal pain and nasal obstruction were not statistically significant between the two groups.

In our study with flexible nasoendoscopy, we noted inferior turbinate hypertrophy, mucosal congestion in a few patients. Even though it gave us an idea about the patency, there could have been some amount of observer bias. The size of the ETT was calculated using the VNE. However, the ETT size was fixed for males at 6.5–7.5 and for females at 6–7 in FNE. Because it was not possible to measure the distance between the inferior turbinate and the nasal septum by fibreoptic nasal endoscopy.

Morra et al.[7] mentioned that VNE clearly demonstrated the anatomical details of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, which were not possible by nasal endoscopy. A few of the trials also showed similar results.[1213] The advantages of VNE were that it was obtained from preoperative CT data, not subjected to any procedure, a non-invasive procedure, lack of additional radiation exposure, more patient comfort, and devoid of any complications. In our study, the virtual nasal endoscopic method provided a highly magnified three-dimensional image. The field of view was superior when compared to fibreoptic nasal endoscopy. The more patent nostril was selected by using VNE. The distance between the inferior turbinate and the nasal septum was measured in the two-dimensional CT images. Thus, the nostril side and ETT size are selected objectively.

In our study, the less incidence of epistaxis is explained by the selection of a more patent nostril and ETT size by the virtual endoscopic-guided measurements. The time taken for the insertion of an ETT up to 10 cm into the nasopharynx was significantly less in VNE. The rate of ETT impingement was significantly less in VNE. Also, it required significantly less maneuvers (both rotation and angulation) as compared to flexible fibreoptic guided assessment. Significantly more patients were intubated in the first attempt using the VNE technique. Though additional maneuvers were required in both groups, the intubation was done through the selected nostril. Hence, both techniques were comparable.

However, our study has limitations. First, we need to first develop an objective method to measure ETT size by FNE; only then we can compare ETT size selection between the two techniques. Secondly, the ETT size was selected based on the two-dimensional images. However, the VNE was reconstructed from these images. The mucosal congestion due to trauma could have influenced the VNE findings, resulting in underestimation of nasal patency. This could have been reduced by topical vasoconstrictors. Both VNE and FNE were done at different time points. Thirdly, VNE suggested a smaller size ETT comparatively. The ventilatory parameters due to small-sized ETTs were not studied. Fourthly, postoperative nasal pain was assessed postoperatively. The influence of postoperative surgical pain contributing to nasal pain was not assessed. Hence, we could not differentiate whether the pain was due to trauma by nasal intubation or postoperative pain.

Conclusions

We conclude that the selection of the ETT size and patent nostril by the VNE method has reduced the incidence of epistaxis. VNE was comparable to FNE in identifying the more patent nostril. However, further trials are needed for comparing the selection of ETT size between the two techniques.
