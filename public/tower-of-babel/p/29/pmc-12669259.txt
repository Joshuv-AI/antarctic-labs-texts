The Efficacy and Safety of Beta-blockers and Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors in Patients with Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Kota Tokunaga, Yu Fujiwara, Reo Omori, Takumi Sato, Manmeet Singh Ahluwalia, Sarbajit Mukherjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now standard for various cancers, and preclinical studies suggest beta-blockers (BBs) may boost the efficacy of ICIs. However, prior clinical studies had small sample sizes, requiring large-scale validation.ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining BBs with ICIs in patients with solid tumors through a systematic review and meta-analysis.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase was conducted for studies on BBs plus ICIs for solid tumors up to July 2024. Outcomes of interest were overall survival, progression-free survival, and adverse events. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals were pooled using a random-effects model meta-analysis, with heterogeneity assessed by I2 statistics.ResultsOverall, 12 clinical studies involving 4293 patients with solid tumors were included, with 1463 patients receiving both BBs and ICIs and 2830 patients receiving ICIs alone. The combination of BBs and ICIs was not associated with a longer overall survival (hazard ratio 1.02; 95% confidence interval 0.84–1.23; p = 0.87; I2 = 69%) or progression-free survival (hazard ratio 0.98; 95% confidence interval 0.80–1.20; p = 0.81; I2 = 71%). Subgroup analyses by cancer types and BB types showed no significant heterogeneity in the hazard ratios for overall survival across different cancer types (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.44) and BB types (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.67). The combination of BBs plus ICIs did not seem to increase toxicity.ConclusionsDespite positive preclinical findings, this meta-analysis showed that adding BBs to ICIs was not associated with longer survival. We await the results of ongoing prospective trials assessing this strategy.PROSPERO RegistrationCRD42024574043.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-025-01184-y.

Key Points

Introduction

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized cancer treatment by eliciting durable responses across various malignancies through blocking immune checkpoints such as programmed death-1, programmed death-ligand 1, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 [1]. However, despite these advancements, most patients do not respond or develop resistance to ICIs, highlighting the need for strategies to enhance antitumor immunity in combination with ICI therapy [2].

Beta-blockers (BBs) are mainly used to manage cardiovascular conditions in clinical practice, but recently attracted attention in oncology for their potential effect in augmenting anti-tumor immunity. Preclinical studies suggest that beta-adrenergic receptors promote cancer progression by reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment, recruiting immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells, while simultaneously impairing the function of CD8⁺ T cells [3–8]. These mechanisms provide a strong rationale for investigating the efficacy of ICIs in combination with β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR) blockade [9–12]. Specifically, preclinical data suggest that β2-AR blockade reduces myeloid-derived suppressor cells, enhances CD8⁺ T-cell activity, upregulates programmed death-1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages, and demonstrates synergistic effects when combined with ICIs [7, 13–15]. Additionally, recent findings have identified a potential role of β1-AR blockade in antitumor immunity as well, demonstrating that blocking β1-AR signaling can enhance CD8⁺ T-cell function and improve immune responses when combined with ICIs [16]. These findings support the potential therapeutic benefit of using beta-blockers alongside ICIs in cancer treatment.

Building on promising preclinical findings, research interest in the clinical potential of combining BBs with ICIs has grown, yet clinical results remain mixed. One retrospective study reported a longer progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with non-small cell lung cancer treated with the BB-ICI combination compared with ICI therapy alone [17]. In contrast, another study demonstrated shorter PFS and overall survival (OS) of patients treated with the combination therapy in the same cancer type [18]. Several previous meta-analyses assessing the efficacy of the combined use of BBs and ICIs in patients with solid tumors found no statistically significant effects on OS and PFS [19–21]. Furthermore, although safety is an essential consideration in addition to efficacy, comprehensive safety data are currently lacking, underscoring the need for further investigation into potential adverse events (AEs) in the combination of BBs and ICIs.

Given the emerging data on the efficacy and safety of the BB and ICI combination over recent years, an updated and more comprehensive evaluation is needed. This study aims to provide updated evidence on the clinical impact of combining BBs with ICIs and to identify patient populations that may benefit from this approach, hoping to offer insights for future research and clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search Strategy

The literature search process is illustrated in Fig. 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). This research followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. The literature focused on the efficacy and safety information in patients with solid tumors undergoing BB and ICI therapy. PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase, with the search cut-off date being 27 July, 2024, were searched for a systematic review. The search terminology and strategy are available in Table 1 of the ESM. To ensure comprehensive coverage, supplementary issues and conference abstracts were also reviewed and included. The protocol of this research was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024574043).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria are (1) patients with solid tumors; (2) clinical trials, case-control, or cohort studies assessing the efficacy and/or safety of ICIs in combination with BBs; and (3) studies reporting hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for OS and/or PFS, or the incidence of AEs. The objective response rate was not analyzed, as fewer than five studies provided relevant data, which would not allow for a statistically reliable pooled analysis. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table 2 of the ESM.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

KT and RO independently performed a systematic review and extracted data from the selected articles. Outcomes of interest were OS, PFS, and AEs. The extracted data included the name of the first author, publication year, study design, cancer type, ICI name, ICI type, BB type, HRs for OS and PFS, and the incidence of AEs. Disagreements between the two reviewers (KT and RO) were discussed and resolved with independent authors (YF and TS). When both univariable and multivariable HRs were available, we collected both data and prioritized the multivariable model for the meta-analysis. We also used the univariable HRs for sensitivity analyses. The quality of non-randomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale [23]. Funnel plots were applied to assess and visualize publication bias. Certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach. Results of individual studies and pooled analyses were tabulated and visually displayed using summary tables and forest plots. All analyses were performed at a significance level of 0.05.

Statistical Analysis

For studies that reported only Kaplan–Meier survival curves without directly providing HRs or CIs, HRs were estimated from the published Kaplan–Meier curves using R software and DigitizeIt [24, 25]. The study by Yan et al. included both a meta-analysis and a retrospective validation cohort evaluating the efficacy of BBs and ICIs, and only data from the validation cohort were incorporated into our pooled analysis [19]. Hazard ratios with 95% CIs for OS and PFS were pooled using a random-effects model meta-analysis. Statistical analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 [26], with heterogeneity assessed by I2 statistics. Subgroup analyses were performed based on cancer types, BB types, and analysis methods (multivariable and univariable). We conducted sensitivity analyses by using univariable analysis results from each study to assess the robustness of the entire meta-analysis.

Results

General Study Characteristics

The database search found 513 relevant records, and after removing duplicate studies (n = 49), irrelevant topics (n = 437), and studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria (n = 12), we identified 15 articles on 12 studies involving 4293 patients with solid tumors that evaluated the efficacy and/or safety of ICI therapy combined with BBs, with 1463 patients receiving both BBs and ICIs and 2830 receiving ICIs alone (Fig. 1 of the ESM). Details of excluded studies after a full-text review, with reasons for exclusion, are provided in Table 3 of the ESM. Study characteristics including cancer type, the subtype of ICIs and BBs, and evaluated outcomes in each study are summarized in Table 1. The most common cancer type was lung cancer in eight studies, including non-small cell lung cancer in five studies. This was followed by melanoma in four studies and renal cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in two studies, respectively. Meta-analysis of OS

In total, nine studies were included in the meta-analysis of OS. The combination of BBs and ICIs was not significantly associated with a longer OS compared to ICIs alone (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.84–1.23, p = 0.87, I2 = 69%) (Fig. 1a). A subgroup analysis by cancer types did not identify the cancer type associated with longer OS with the combination of BBs and ICIs compared with ICIs alone (lung cancer: HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.84–1.43, p = 0.48; melanoma: HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.16–1.64, p = 0.26; HCC: HR 1.11, 95% CI 0.90–1.37, p = 0.32). No significant heterogeneity was observed across cancer types (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.44) (Fig. 1b). A subgroup analysis by BB types did not show significant differences in OS between the combination of specific BB types plus ICIs versus ICIs alone (beta-1 blocker [B1B]: HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.52–1.56, p = 0.70; non-selective beta-blocker (nsBB): HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.40–1.40, p = 0.36), with no significant heterogeneity across BB types (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.67) (Fig. 1c).Fig. 1Forest plots of hazard ratios (HRs) for overall survival (OS): a analysis including all studies, b subgroup analysis based on cancer types, c subgroup analysis based on beta-blocker (BB) types. Meta-analysis of PFS

In total, seven studies were included in the meta-analysis of PFS. The combination of BBs and ICIs was not associated with a longer PFS compared to ICIs alone (HR 0.98; 95% CI 0.80–1.20; p = 0.81; I2 = 71%) (Fig. 2a). In a subgroup analysis, no significant difference in the HR for PFS was observed across cancer types (lung cancer: HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.65–1.36, p = 0.74; HCC: HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.48–2.21, p = 0.94), with no significant heterogeneity among cancer types (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.83) (Fig. 2b). A subgroup analysis by types of BBs was also performed, although it did not show a significant difference in PFS when compared to ICI monotherapy, for neither B1B (HR 1.16, 95% CI 0.42–3.26, p = 0.77) nor nsBB (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.66–1.28, p = 0.62). Sensitivity Analysis

First, we performed subgroup analyses by categorizing the included studies into univariable and multivariable analyses and found no significant heterogeneity across multivariable or univariable models for both OS (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.96) and PFS (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.58) (Fig. 2 of the ESM). Subsequently, we performed further sensitivity analyses by prioritizing univariable outcome data from the included studies, and the subgroup analyses based on analysis models demonstrated no heterogeneity for OS (I2 = 0%, p for heterogeneity = 0.47) and low heterogeneity for PFS (I2 = 35.3%, p for heterogeneity = 0.21) across analysis models (Fig. 3 of the ESM). Overall, the sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the primary findings for both OS and PFS.

Safety

Among six studies reporting AEs in patients receiving ICIs with BBs, two studies compared general toxicity between groups with and without BBs, and both studies reported no significant differences in the incidence of AEs [27, 28]. Two studies specifically investigated cardiovascular AEs, including cardiovascular mortality, heart failure admissions, acute coronary syndrome, and atrial fibrillation, and found no significant differences between the combination therapy group and the ICI-alone group [18, 29]. However, one study noted a higher incidence of sepsis in the combination group compared with the ICI monotherapy group (8.1% vs 4.5%, p = 0.03) [29]. In contrast, another study found that pre-operative use of BBs with ICIs was associated with a reduced risk of post-operative death within 30 days compared with ICIs alone (odds ratio 0.34, 95% CI 0.13–0.87, p = 0.024) [30]. A phase I trial of combined use of propranolol and pembrolizumab has reported incidences of treatment-related AEs, such as rash, fatigue, and vitiligo (44% each, n = 4/9), with no increase in severity at higher propranolol doses [31]. Risk of Bias, Publication Bias, and Certainty of Evidence Assessment

All 12 studies were non-randomized, consisting of 11 retrospective studies and one prospective phase I study. Thus, we used the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale to assess the quality of each study. No studies scored below six points, indicating the high quality of the data used for the meta-analysis (Table 4 of the ESM). No apparent asymmetry was observed in funnel plots for both OS and PFS (Figs. 4 and 5 of the ESM). Because of the limited number of included studies for OS and PFS (fewer than ten), we did not perform any statistical analysis to assess publication bias. Certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE approach, which indicated low certainty for both OS and PFS because of study design limitations, heterogeneity, and imprecision (Table 5 of the ESM).

Discussion

Despite promising preclinical results, our updated systematic review, one of the largest to date, did not show significant differences in HRs for OS or PFS with the concurrent use of BBs and ICIs in clinical practice. This result was consistent across subgroups based on the cancer types and classifications of BBs. In addition, the combined use of BB and ICI therapy did not obviously increase the risk of AEs. However, given the retrospective design and heterogeneity of the included studies, these results should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. Nonetheless, our study represents the most updated meta-analysis exclusively focusing on this novel combination strategy, providing further insight on the efficacy and safety of such a strategy.

Previous retrospective studies have suggested that when combined with ICIs, BBs may be associated with better disease control or objective response rates compared with ICIs alone [19, 32, 33]. However, our findings indicate that the combined use of BBs and ICIs does not appear to contribute to longer PFS or OS, aligning with previous meta-analyses that included fewer studies than ours (four, seven, and 11, respectively) [19–21]. Additional sensitivity analyses using univariable results from each study demonstrated consistent outcomes with the multivariable analyses, further supporting the robustness of our findings despite inter-study variability. Similarly, a post-hoc analysis of the KEYNOTE-054 trial suggested that the combination of propranolol, an nsBB, and pembrolizumab was not significantly associated with longer recurrence-free survival compared to pembrolizumab alone (HR 0.67; 95% CI 0.38–1.19) [34]. However, only one clinical trial assessing the efficacy of BBs and ICIs in a prospective manner, reported by Gandhi et al., demonstrated a high objective response rate of 78% in patients with melanoma treated with propranolol and pembrolizumab, along with changes in interferon signaling, including increased interferon-gamma and decreased interleukin-6 levels in responders [31]. These findings indicate an immunomodulatory effect of BBs in combination with ICIs, even in a clinical study. Several ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the efficacy of this strategy, which will provide further insights on the immune profiles and clinical benefits of this approach (Table 3). Our subgroup analyses stratified by cancer types were more comprehensive than previous studies, encompassing lung cancer (seven studies in our study vs three studies in prior analyses), melanoma, and HCC (two studies in our study vs none in prior meta-analyses). In our analysis, combining BBs with ICIs was not associated with longer OS or PFS in lung cancer, HCC, or melanoma compared to ICIs alone, and our findings were consistent with smaller meta-analyses published previously [19–21]. We also found minimal heterogeneity in HRs for OS and PFS across these malignancies, indicating that the results were consistent among cancer types. Given that β-AR levels vary among tumor types [35], additional factors beyond β-AR density may influence how BBs affect antitumor immunity. Given that all included studies were retrospective, these findings should be viewed as preliminary pending results from ongoing clinical trials that may further elucidate the therapeutic role of BBs combined with ICIs across malignancies.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to conduct subgroup analyses based on BB classifications. Our result showed that the combination of specific BB types with ICIs was not associated with longer survival, and the pattern of this ineffectiveness was consistent across BB types, including B1Bs and nsBBs. This is in contrast to findings of previous research suggesting differential effects of B1B and nsBB on cancer progression. For instance, nsBBs reduced tumor proliferation by 66% (p < 0.0001) in early-stage breast cancer over 3 weeks with propranolol, while B1Bs did not show such a reduction [36]. Additionally, in patients with ovarian cancer receiving chemotherapy, those taking nsBBs achieved significantly longer OS compared with those taking B1Bs (median 94.9 vs 38 months, p < 0.001) [37]. However, interpretation of the BB subgroup findings requires caution. For example, in one included study by Wu et al., the analyses were unadjusted for important confounders, and the BB cohort included patients with more advanced liver disease (e.g., higher Child–Pugh class and portal vein thrombosis) [28], which may have biased the outcomes. As the investigation of the synergistic effect of BBs with ICIs in clinical settings is still immature, further exploration of this strategy across BB subclasses will be crucial for understanding their potentially distinct effects on antitumor immunity.

Overall, our clinical findings suggest that the addition of BBs to ICIs does not improve survival, in contrast to the favorable results observed in preclinical studies. The discrepancy between promising preclinical findings and neutral clinical outcomes may reflect several factors. In animal models, BBs are often administered at higher doses and under homogeneous tumor conditions [13], which may amplify their immunomodulatory effects. In contrast, human populations are highly heterogeneous in terms of cancer stage, BB subtype, comorbidities, and concurrent ICI regimens, many of which cannot be fully adjusted for because of the retrospective nature of available studies. However, following the promising results of an early-phase prospective trial [31], further prospective studies incorporating pharmacodynamic and immune profiling are warranted to better elucidate the mechanism by which BBs modulate antitumor immunity in humans.

In addition to efficacy, safety is a critical consideration when combining BBs with ICIs, yet it was not evaluated in previous systematic reviews. Among the studies in our meta-analysis, one reported a favorable outcome of reducing 30-day postoperative mortality [30], while another noted a worse outcome of an increased risk of sepsis with the combination compared with ICIs alone. However, other studies included in our analysis indicate that overall AE profiles, including cardiovascular events, are similar between patients receiving ICIs with and without BBs, suggesting the overall AE profile of adding BBs to ICIs seems acceptable [18, 27–29]. An important consideration for the interpretation of safety is that most studies are retrospective, and many BB users may already have cardiovascular diseases, which may complicate AE assessments, even after adjusting for potential confounders in each study. These factors highlight the need for further studies reporting the detailed safety profile from the combination therapy in a prospective manner by considering clinical factors such as comorbidities and medication interactions that potentially affect the safety profile. Currently, prospective clinical trials are investigating the combination of BBs with ICIs, with some specifically focusing on AEs (Table 3). These studies will help us understand more about the toxicity information on this novel combination.

Our study has several limitations. First, the included studies in our meta-analysis did not have sufficient information on the treatment line, the concurrent use of other antineoplastic agents, the duration of treatment, or ICI types, precluding further subtype analyses. Second, the dosing and timing of BBs varied across these studies, which may have been insufficient to effectively influence the tumor immune microenvironment, as suggested by several clinical trials that use much higher BB doses to achieve desired outcomes [31, 38]. Third, the number of eligible studies and patients remains limited, and we adopted a random-effects meta-analysis model because of the high heterogeneity observed, which may have reduced the overall statistical power of our findings. This issue is even more pronounced in several subgroup analyses, where small sample sizes further limited the robustness of the results. Moreover, most studies included in our meta-analysis were retrospective, and several did not perform a multivariable adjustment to balance clinical factors between the BB and non-BB cohorts, which could introduce potential biases as patients may have more comorbidities, especially cardiac diseases, which are typical indications for BB therapy. Furthermore, inconsistencies in the reported safety outcomes in each study hindered our ability to perform a quantitative synthesis. Further study is needed to evaluate the efficacy in a prospective manner, and more studies need to report the toxicity profile in detail to verify the safety of this combination strategy.

Conclusions

While preclinical evidence supports the potential synergistic effect of BBs and ICIs, our meta-analysis showed that the combined use of BBs and ICI therapy was not associated with prolonged OS or PFS compared to ICI therapy alone. The safety profile appears manageable but remains inconclusive because of methodological limitations. Given the retrospective nature of the included studies, these hypothesis-generating findings warrant validation through ongoing and future prospective trials.
