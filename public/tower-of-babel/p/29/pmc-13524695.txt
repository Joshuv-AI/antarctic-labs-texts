Granulomatous Interstitial Nephritis With Perivascular Involvement in Carbamazepine-Induced DRESS Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Naoto Okubo, Hanae Wakabayashi, Tomoka Tsuchida, Hiroko Inoue, Tadaki Suzuki, Katsuhiko Asanuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) syndrome is a severe hypersensitivity reaction caused by certain medications, including allopurinol, antibiotics, and antiepileptic drugs. It is characterized by a widespread rash, fever, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, and multiorgan dysfunction, including hepatic and renal impairment. DRESS syndrome differs from typical drug eruptions in that clinical symptoms may continue or worsen even after the causative medication has been discontinued. We report the case of a 75-year-old Japanese woman who developed DRESS syndrome after taking carbamazepine. She subsequently experienced severe acute kidney injury. Despite drug discontinuation, renal function progressively deteriorated over 2 months. Renal biopsy revealed granulomatous interstitial nephritis characterized by granulomatous lesions confined to the perivascular areas of the arcuate and interlobular arteries. Additionally, lymphangiogenesis was observed. Administration of prednisolone 0.8 mg/kg/d resulted in partial improvement in renal function; however, chronic kidney disease persisted. This patient showed perivascular granulomatous interstitial nephritis, a rare histological finding in DRESS syndrome. This finding shows that granuloma formation may involve crosstalk between persistent interstitial inflammation and lymphangiogenesis. Therefore, delayed treatment initiation may negatively affect renal outcomes, underscoring the essence of the prompt recognition and treatment of DRESS syndrome.

Introduction

Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) syndrome is a rare but severe drug reaction characterized by fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, and organ dysfunction. Common causative agents include antiepileptic drugs, allopurinol, sulfasalazine, and vancomycin.1 It typically develops 3-12 weeks after exposure and may progress despite withdrawal. The pathogenesis remains unclear but involves interactions between herpesvirus reactivation and drug-specific immune responses.1 Although most patients improve after drug discontinuation and corticosteroid therapy, mortality remains approximately 10%.2, 3, 4 Hepatic dysfunction is the most frequent complication, followed by renal involvement.2,5 Interstitial pneumonia, pleuritis, and myocarditis have also been reported.6 Gastrointestinal involvement is uncommon.1 Myocarditis ranges from treatment-responsive acute eosinophilic myocarditis to acute necrotizing eosinophilic myocarditis, with mortality exceeding 50%.6 These complications affect prognosis and highlight the importance of appropriate management.1

Reports describing the histological findings of renal lesions are limited because DRESS syndrome is a rare disease. Most biopsy-confirmed patients had acute interstitial nephritis.3 In this case, the kidney biopsy findings showed interstitial nephritis with limited perivascular granulomatous lesions. To date, only 1 case of granulomatous interstitial nephritis has been reported in relation to DRESS syndrome.7 This rare histological finding expands the spectrum of renal pathology in DRESS syndrome and may offer insights into immune-mediated renal injury. This case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and timely therapeutic intervention in patients with DRESS syndrome experiencing severe renal involvement.

Case Report

A 75-year-old Japanese woman with a history of papillary thyroid carcinoma underwent thyroidectomy and received levothyroxine and vitamin D supplementation. She was also taking calcium channel blockers and statins to manage her hypertension and dyslipidemia. Carbamazepine was prescribed to treat trigeminal neuralgia. Eight weeks later, she developed an itchy erythematous rash that covered over 50% of the body surface area; the day on which it appeared was defined as day 0 of onset. On day 2, laboratory tests revealed serum creatinine 0.9 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase 115 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 147 U/L, and eosinophilia (1,584 cells/μL, 18% of total leukocytes). A drug eruption was suspected, and all medications except levothyroxine were discontinued. Antihistamines and glycyrrhizin were initiated; however, the rash persisted.

By day 30, she showed improvements in liver function and rash (aspartate aminotransferase 15 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 15 U/L); however, renal function worsened (serum creatinine 3.9 mg/dL), and eosinophilia persisted. The serum immunoglobulin (Ig)G level was low (448 mg/dL), indicating hypogammaglobulinemia (Fig 1A). On day 33, computed tomography scan revealed significant renal enlargement compared with the scan on day 82 (Fig 1B and C). Upon transfer to our hospital on day 41, her serum creatinine concentration was elevated at 6.5 mg/dL, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was elevated at 6,700 U/mL. Bone marrow aspiration conducted on day 50 revealed no signs of malignant lymphoma. Urinalysis revealed no proteinuria or hematuria, with only mild elevations in tubular markers (β2-microglobulin, N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminidase). Urine output was preserved, and dialysis was not required. Serological testing was negative for myeloperoxidase and proteinase 3 antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, angiotensin-converting enzyme, antinuclear antibodies, and other autoantibodies, ruling out vasculitis, sarcoidosis, and autoimmune disease. Considering the extensive rash, hepatic and renal dysfunction, and eosinophilia, the RegiSCAR score was 5, meeting the diagnostic criteria for DRESS syndrome.8 Quantitative polymerase chain reactions for human herpesvirus [HHV]-6, HHV-7, and cytomegalovirus antigens were negative. Subsequent human leukocyte antigen genotyping identified HLA-A∗31:01 as a risk factor for carbamazepine-induced hypersensitivity. A kidney biopsy on day 45 showed no immunofluorescence deposits (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C1q were all negative) with preserved glomeruli (Fig 2A). The interstitium and tubules demonstrated diffuse infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytes (Fig 2B-D). Notably, epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells (CD68+ cells) formed granulomatous lesions that were confined to the perivascular areas around the arcuate (Fig 2A, E, F) and interlobular arteries. Based on renal biopsy findings, prednisolone 30 mg/d (0.8 mg/kg) was initiated on day 80. Consequently, renal function gradually improved. By day 95, serum creatinine had decreased to 1.4 mg/dL, sIL-2R had normalized to 600 U/mL, and prednisolone was tapered to 20 mg/d. Renal function partially recovered; however, chronic kidney disease persisted (Fig 1A).

Discussion

The pathogenesis of DRESS syndrome remains unclear; however, several interrelated immunological mechanisms have been proposed. One key initiating event is the drug-specific T cell activation in genetically susceptible individuals, especially those carrying certain human leukocyte antigen alleles.9,10 Excessive activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells triggers widespread cytokine release and multiorgan inflammation. Additionally, transient expansion of regulatory T cells occurs during the acute phase, which suppresses excessive inflammation but concurrently impairs antiviral immunity.11 This regulatory T cell-associated immune suppression has been linked to hypogammaglobulinemia observed in the early phase of DRESS syndrome, which may further predispose patients to herpes virus reactivation.4,12 Consistent with this finding, our patient exhibited transient hypogammaglobulinemia (Fig 1A). However, regulatory T cells have been reported to become dysfunctional during the recovery phase, potentially contributing to persistent tissue inflammation and the development of autoimmune symptoms.12 These dynamic and temporally shifting immune abnormalities distinguish DRESS syndrome from severe drug eruptions.

HHV-6 reactivation usually occurs 2-3 weeks after the onset of DRESS syndrome, with viremia lasting for approximately 1 week.13 HHV-6 has been detected in various organs, including the skin, lymph nodes, liver, and kidneys, during DRESS syndrome episodes.12,13 These findings suggest that HHV-6 is directly associated with rashes, lymphadenopathy, and organ dysfunction. However, HHV-6 DNA was not detected in either serum or kidney specimens of our patient. HHV-6 may have already been eliminated, because the evaluation was conducted 8 weeks after symptom onset. Notably, active tubulointerstitial nephritis was present despite the absence of the virus in the tissues. This indicates that renal interstitial inflammation may continue independently of ongoing viral replication.

In DRESS syndrome, histopathological renal data are limited; only 18 biopsy-confirmed cases have been reported, with tubulointerstitial nephritis in approximately 80% of cases and vasculitis in approximately 10%.3 Renal involvement in DRESS syndrome most commonly presents as acute interstitial nephritis, characterized by interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration with eosinophils and lymphocytes, and typically manifests as acute kidney injury.3 In this case, diffuse tubulointerstitial nephritis and localized perivascular granulomatous changes were observed. The interstitial infiltrating cells were primarily T cells, with the CD4+ cells being predominant. This finding supports the idea that T cells are essential in mediating tissue injury in DRESS syndrome. Furthermore, consistent with the histological predominance of T cells, sIL-2R levels are significantly elevated during the acute phase. sIL-2R is a recognized marker of T cell activation and has been reported to increase in parallel with viral reactivation and systemic inflammation.14 In this case, the sIL-2R levels decreased concurrently with improvements in renal function, indicating that sIL-2R may show the degree of immune activation and disease activity. Although not specific, sIL-2R may be a useful biomarker for monitoring the clinical course of DRESS syndrome.

Diffuse T cell infiltration was observed in the tubules and interstitium (Fig 2B); nevertheless, granulomatous changes were localized around the blood vessels with perivascular clusters of epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells (Fig 2A and E). Granulomatous changes are typically caused by sustained activation of antigen-specific CD4+ effector T cells. Cytokines, including interferon γ and tumor necrosis factor, produced by antigen-specific CD4+ effector T cells, induce the differentiation of macrophages into epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells.15 The precise antigenic trigger remains unclear; however, the presence of focal granulomas in the context of diffuse T cell infiltration indicates that persistent and localized immune stimulation contributes to granuloma formation.

A notable finding was the presence of D2-40+ lymphatic vessels surrounding the granulomatous lesions (Fig 2F). Lymphatic vessels are abundant in the renal cortex and become sparser toward the periphery. They drain interstitial fluid, proteins, cytokines, and immune cells into regional lymph nodes.16,17 During inflammation, lymphangiogenesis is driven by vascular endothelial growth factors C and D. Newly formed lymphatic vessels help clear inflammatory mediators; however, increased levels of vascular endothelial growth factor C, tumor necrosis factor α, and interleukin 1β can enhance lymphatic permeability, thereby facilitating immune cell migration and amplifying inflammation.16 Therefore, persistent interstitial inflammation promotes lymphangiogenesis, and the reconstructed lymphatic network may contribute to the accumulation of perivascular mediators. This process promotes the development of granulomatous changes.

Renal impairment in DRESS syndrome is increasingly recognized as a significant clinical complication, and most patients respond to corticosteroid therapy; however, a subset of patients progress to chronic kidney disease.3 A recent international consensus recommended systemic corticosteroids for severe cases based on a 4-domain severity classification, with prednisolone administered at 0.5-2 mg/kg/d and tapered over 3-6 months.18 In our case, prednisolone was initiated approximately 12 weeks after onset, as the renal parameters showed partial spontaneous improvement. Renal function partially improved; nevertheless, recovery was incomplete, indicating that delayed treatment may lead to irreversible renal damage.

This case highlights the possible interaction among persistent interstitial inflammation, lymphangiogenesis, and granuloma formation, which reflects the pathogenesis of DRESS syndrome. Renal damage caused by DRESS syndrome persists owing to interstitial inflammation, even after discontinuing the causative medication; therefore, early diagnosis and timely treatment are essential to prevent irreversible renal damage.
