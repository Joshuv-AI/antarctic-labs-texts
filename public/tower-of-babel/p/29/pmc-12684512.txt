Impact of Heart Failure Guideline Publication on Medicare Drug Coverage Policies: A Quasi‐Experimental Analysis
Authors: Amrita Mukhopadhyay, Nathalia Ladino, Tyrel Stokes, Aparna Narendrula, Stuart D Katz, Harmony R Reynolds, Allison P Squires, Rishi K Wadhera, Donglan Stacy Zhang, Samrachana Adhikari, Saul Blecker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with heart failure (HF) often have difficulty obtaining life‐saving medications due to coverage barriers, such as prior authorizations and high out‐of‐pocket costs. To promote better coverage for high value therapies and inform policymakers about cost effectiveness, the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America added Value Statements to HF guidelines. We assessed whether these guidelines influenced Medicare drug coverage policies for 2 life‐saving, costly HF medications: angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI–guideline “high value”) and sodium glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i–guideline “intermediate value”).MethodsWe performed an observational study using Medicare prescription drug plan formulary files from April 2020 to April 2023 to separately assess for changes in coverage barriers to ARNI and SGLT2i after Value Statement publication (April 2022), and subsequent Medicare plan online update (October 2022). The primary outcome was the percentage of plans each month with any barrier to drug coverage (prior authorizations, tier ≥3 out‐of‐pocket cost‐sharing, step therapy, or no coverage). Analyses used interrupted time series and difference‐in‐differences approaches. Difference‐in‐differences analyses used direct oral anticoagulants as a control due to their comparable cost and use as ARNI and SGLT2i, but without a Value Statement.ResultsAmong 7396 Medicare drug plans, monthly rates of any coverage barrier ranged from 94.3% to 97.4% for ARNI and 93.2% to 96.6% for SGLT2i. Most barriers were due to tier ≥3 out‐of‐pocket cost‐sharing requirements (ARNI: 94.3%–95.8%; SGLT2i: 93.2%–95.6%). Coverage barriers remained stable in April 2022 and declined slightly in October 2022. In difference‐in‐differences analyses, the presence of a Value Statement was associated with a ~1 percentage point decline in coverage barriers for both ARNI (difference‐in‐differences estimate, −1.07% [95% CI, −1.44% to −0.70%]) and SGLT2i (−1.32% [95% CI, −1.63% to −1.00%]).ConclusionsCoverage barriers to ARNI and SGLT2i were common and changed only slightly after publication of Value Statements in HF guidelines. There is a critical need for robust strategies to improve access to life‐saving HF medications.Keywords: copayment, drug coverage, guideline‐directed medical therapy, heart failure, prior authorizationSubject Categories: Cost-Effectiveness, Ethics and Policy, Health Services, Heart Failure

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Drug coverage barriers to 2 life‐saving heart failure medications (ARNI [angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors] and SGLT2i [sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors]) were common and did not change much in response to Value Statements in the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America heart failure guidelines.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

There is a critical need for robust strategies to improve access to life‐saving heart failure medications among patients with Medicare.

Angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have both been shown to reduce mortality and hospitalization for patients with heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite their benefits, these medications are not prescribed for >80% of eligible patients, 4 , 5 and even when prescribed, are not regularly filled by patients >60% of the time. 6 The marked underuse of ARNI and SGLT2i is partially due to drug coverage barriers, such as cost sharing and prior authorization requirements, which can contribute to these shortfalls by complicating prescribing workflows and decreasing patient adherence. 7 , 8

In order to promote high value care and provide guidance to those making coverage decisions, 9 cost considerations were included for the first time in the updated American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA) HF guidelines in April 2022. 10 These guidelines included “Value Statements,” which rated treatments based on high‐quality cost‐effectiveness studies. For HF with reduced ejection fraction, the guidelines assigned strong class I clinical recommendations for both ARNI and SGLT2i but different value statements: ARNI “high value,” SGLT2i “intermediate value.”

Little is known about whether the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Value Statements influenced drug coverage decisions. On the one hand, current US policies lack guidance on how to apply value assessments to coverage decisions. This is in contrast to other countries, such as Canada and the United Kingdom, which use cost‐effectiveness determinations as a formal part of the drug approval and pricing process. 11 On the other hand, a few select US organizations have been successful at influencing drug coverage policies. For example, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review is a nonprofit organization whose recommendations have been used by a variety of private and public US payers. 12 , 13 Of note, prior studies of the impact of AHA/ACC guidelines on drug coverage policies have revealed inconsistent results, but these prior guidelines did not include Value Statements. 14 , 15

Therefore, in this study, we assessed whether publication of the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines with Value Statements influenced Medicare plan‐level trends in coverage barriers for ARNI and SGLT2i. We hypothesized that the guidelines would have minimal or no impact, given the lack of formal requirements on US payers to consider cost‐effectiveness guidelines when making coverage decisions.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This was a retrospective, longitudinal, observational study using plan‐level data from the Medicare Prescription Drug Plan Formulary and Pricing Information Files, which are publicly available for download (https://data.cms.gov/provider‐summary‐by‐type‐of‐service/medicare‐part‐d‐prescribers/monthly‐prescription‐drug‐plan‐formulary‐and‐pharmacy‐network‐information). We included monthly data for all Medicare Advantage and Part D Standalone Prescription Drug Plans from April 2020 to April 2023. To examine changes in coverage barriers before and after the publication of HF guidelines with Value Statements, we used an interrupted time series design and separately analyzed ARNI and SGLT2i. Further, to assess the changes in coverage barriers as compared with the change in a control medication class with no associated Value Statement, we used a difference‐in‐differences design. All data were at the plan level, and therefore, this study did not meet the definition of human subjects research and did not require institutional review board approval or informed consent.

Exposure Time Points of Interest

For our primary analyses, the time point of interest was the initial publication of the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF Guidelines with Value Statements in April 2022. 10 We also conducted secondary analyses, in which the time point of interest was the first drug coverage policy update deadline for Medicare after AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guideline publication in October 2022.

Medication Selection

We characterized monthly coverage policies separately for ARNI and SGLT2i. For ARNI, we evaluated sacubitril/valsartan, which is the only available medication in this drug class. For SGLT2i, we evaluated dapagliflozin and empagliflozin as these medications are endorsed by the 2022 HF guidelines, 10 and have demonstrated mortality benefit in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction in randomized trials. 2 , 3

Control Medications

For difference‐in‐differences analyses, we used the direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) apixaban and rivaroxaban as control medications. We specifically chose apixaban and rivaroxaban as a control medications because they do not have an associated Value Statement but are otherwise similar to ARNI and SGLT2i in that they also have a class I clinical recommendation in national cardiovascular guidelines, 16 demonstrate benefit in multiple randomized trials, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 and are widely used by Medicare beneficiaries with similar cost burdens. 21 All of the medications in this analysis ranked in the top 10 in terms of cost burden to Medicare, and were selected for Medicare price negotiation (to go into effect in 2026). 22

Plan Coverage Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite of any barrier to drug coverage (prior authorization requirement, tier ≥3 cost‐sharing amount, step therapy requirement, or no coverage), as described in prior studies. 14 , 15 For each month, we computed the number of plans with any barrier to drug coverage over the total number of plans available for that month. For SGLT2i and DOACs, a coverage barrier needed to be present for both medications in each class in order to be considered (Data S1). Key secondary outcomes included assessing each barrier separately, and the estimated out‐of‐pocket costs for a 30‐day supply for each plan, which was calculated using copayment, coinsurance, and pricing information from formulary data (Data S1). For plans with missing outcome data for some months, we conducted simple imputation of values based on the prior month and next month’s values only if both values were identical, which occurred for the majority of plans with missing data. Plans with missing values that we were unable to impute were excluded from the analysis.

Other Descriptive Plan Characteristics

We extracted additional information on other plan characteristics, such as plan type (Medicare Advantage, Part D Standalone Prescription Drug Plan), region (Northeast, South, Midwest, West, US Territories), and special needs plans (dual‐eligible for both Medicaid and Medicare, chronic/disabling condition, and institution special needs plans).

Statistical Analysis

Plan characteristics were tabulated for descriptive purposes. Primary and secondary plan coverage outcomes were tabulated for each month and each medication class to allow for qualitative visualization of trends.

Interrupted time series analyses were conducted to quantify changes in primary and secondary plan coverage outcomes before and after April and October 2022. These analyses used generalized additive regression modeling with restricted cubic spline to account for preintervention trends and an indicator for intervention time point. Separate regression analyses were conducted for the primary and secondary time point (April 2022, October 2022), each outcome of interest, and each medication class (ARNI, SGLT2i). We reported changes in the proportion of plans with barriers to drug coverage using the estimated rate difference and 95% CI. For the 30‐day out‐of‐pocket cost outcome, the estimated differences in dollars and 95% CIs were reported. All models assessing out‐of‐pocket costs used the double resampling bootstrap method 23 to estimate 95% CIs given the distribution of data (see Data S1; Figures S1 and S2 for out‐of‐pocket cost distributions and details on this methodology). To assess for auto correlation, we also created autocorrelation plots for each time series model (Figure S3).

Difference‐in‐differences analyses were conducted to assess for the impact of Value Statements on drug coverage policies, using DOACs apixaban and rivaroxaban as control medications. To assess the parallel trends assumption, we visually inspected pretreatment trends for ARNI, SGLT2i, and DOACs (Figure S4). We then fit separate linear regression models to estimate the primary outcome and out‐of‐pocket costs. Our models included time period (pre‐Value Statement, post‐Value Statement), medication class (SGLT2i/ARNI versus control), and an interaction term (time period*medication class). The coefficient of the interaction term represented the difference‐in‐differences estimate or the differential change in outcomes attributable to the Value Statement. Separate linear regression models were created for the primary and secondary time point (April 2022, October 2022) and for each of the following comparisons: ARNI (“high value”) versus DOAC (no Value Statement control), SGLT2i (“intermediate value”) versus DOAC (no Value Statement control), and ARNI (“high value”) versus SGLT2i (“intermediate value”).

RESULTS

Plan Inclusion and Characteristics

From April 2020 to April 2023, there were a total of 7407 Medicare Advantage and Standalone Medicare Part D drug plans. Of these, 11 (0.14%) were excluded due to no available formulary information during the entire study period, resulting in a total of 7396 plans for primary analysis (Figure 1). Formulary information was imputed for 1126 (15.2%) plans for ≥1months with missing data using simple imputation of values from the prior and subsequent months, which were identical 100% of the time. For out‐of‐pocket cost analysis, an additional 7 (0.09%) plans were excluded for ARNI, 55 (0.74%) plans for empagliflozin, and 820 (11.1%) plans for dapagliflozin due to lack of available data. Prevalence of Coverage Barriers to ARNI and SGLT2i

Over the course of the study period, monthly rates of any coverage barrier ranged from 94.3% to 97.4% for ARNI (Figure 2A) and 93.2% to 96.6% for SGLT2i (Figure 2B). Most coverage barriers for both ARNI and SGLT2i were due to Tier ≥3 cost sharing requirements (ARNI: 94.3%–95.8%; SGLT2i: 93.2%–95.6%). For ARNI, prior authorization was required for 0.0% to 28.1% of plans in any given month, and zero plans required step therapy throughout the study period. For SGLT2i, zero plans required prior authorization throughout the study period, and 0.0% to 8.0% required step therapy for both SGLT2i medications assessed in any given month. A small percentage of plans did not cover either SGLT2i (0.0%–0.1%, Figure 2). Average 30‐day out‐of‐pocket cost ranged from $49.41 to $53.11 for ARNI and $47.14 to $50.57 for SGLT2i (Figure 3).

Changes in Coverage Barriers After Guideline Value Statement Publication

After the initial 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF Guideline Value Statement Publication in April 2022, 10 there was no significant change in coverage barriers for any of the primary or secondary outcome measures in interrupted time series models (Table 2). Small changes were noted a few months later in October 2022, when plans were required to submit drug coverage policy updates for the year (Table 2). Specifically, the percentage of plans with any coverage barrier to ARNI decreased by 1.6 percentage points after October 2022 when compared with April 2020 to September 2022 (estimated rate difference, −1.6% [95% CI, −2.0% to −1.2%], P<0.001). This was mostly due to fewer Tier ≥3 cost sharing requirements (−1.3% [95% CI, −1.6% to −1.1%], P<0.001). For SGLT2i, the percentage of plans with any coverage barrier decreased by 2.2 percentage points after October 2022 (−2.2% [95% CI, −2.4% to −2.0%], P<0.001), also mostly due to fewer Tier≥3 cost sharing requirements (−2.2% [95% CI, −2.4% to −2.1%], P<0.001). Out‐of‐pocket cost for a 30‐day supply decreased slightly for both ARNI (−$2.07 [v−$2.10 to −$0.80], P<0.001) and SGLT2i (−$3.58 [95% CI, −$3.26 to −$2.20], P<0.001) after October 2022.

Comparison With Control (No Value Statement)

Difference‐in‐differences models revealed that both “high value” (ARNI) and “intermediate value” (SGLT2i) designations in the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines were associated with an approximate 1 percentage point decline in coverage barriers as compared with control (DOAC, all P<0.01, Table 3). Interestingly, compared with control (DOAC), a “high value” designation (ARNI) was associated with an increase in 30‐day out‐of‐pocket cost by approximately $1, and an “intermediate value” designation (SGLT2i) was associated with a decrease in 30‐day out‐of‐pocket cost by approximately $1 to $2 (all P<0.001, Table 4). Consequently, an “intermediate value” (SGLT2i) designation was associated with a decrease in 30‐day out‐of‐pocket cost by approximately $2 to $3 when compared with a “high value” (ANRI) designation (all P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective, quasi‐experimental analysis of over 7000 Medicare drug coverage plans, we investigated the impact of the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guideline Value Statements on drug coverage policies for ARNI and SGLT2i, two mortality‐reducing, costly medications for HF. We found that coverage barriers to ARNI and SGLT2i were present in >90% of plans and were most commonly in the form of tier ≥3 cost sharing requirements. In the initial period following guideline publication (April 2022), we observed no change in the percentage of plans with any coverage barrier. A few months later, when plans were required to submit drug coverage policy updates (October 2022), we observed an overall small, yet statistically significant, 1 to 2 percentage point decrease in the percentage of plans with any coverage barrier. Findings were similar for both ARNI and SGLT2i. Overall, our findings demonstrate limited impact of the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guideline Value Statements on Medicare drug coverage policies.

Drug coverage barriers, such as prior authorization and cost‐sharing requirements, are ideally intended to promote the use of lower cost, equivalent alternatives. However, when applied to highly effective medications without generic options, such as with ARNI and SGLT2i, coverage barriers can contribute to decreased medication use, worsened clinical outcomes, and exacerbated inequities. 7 , 24 , 25 Value Statements were included in the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines in part to influence those making coverage decisions and promote access to high value care. 9 Unfortunately, our findings suggest that the guidelines were minimally influential to Medicare policymakers when determining drug coverage barriers. Although statistically significant, a change in coverage for 1% to 2% of plans is unlikely to have meaningful impact for the majority of Medicare beneficiaries. This finding is consistent with prior studies assessing the impact of other AHA/ACC guidelines, specifically the publication of the 2013 prevention guidelines and the 2014 atrial fibrillation guidelines, which were followed by either no change, inconsistent changes, or even increases in drug coverage barriers for Medicare patients. 14 , 15 Of note, these prior guidelines did not include Value Statements, and these prior studies used less granular, annual data.

One consideration to interpreting our findings could be the lack of guidance in the United States on how to apply value assessments to coverage decisions, and the lack of payer incentives to do so. In qualitative interviews and surveys, US payer representatives report placing increasing importance on value assessments when making coverage decisions. 12 , 26 In 1 survey, 86% of payer representatives reported broad familiarity with available value assessment frameworks, and 79% indicated that cost‐effectiveness reports often or occasionally influenced coverage decisions. 12 However, despite increasing acknowledgement of the importance of value assessments, definitions of value are inconsistent across payers, 26 there is currently no standard process for payers to incorporate value into decision‐making, 26 and Medicare is restricted from setting formal cost‐effectiveness thresholds to guide coverage decisions. 27 Moreover, the AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines do not provide direct recommendations to policy‐makers to accompany Value Statements, leaving interpretation and implementation up to the individual stakeholders. This is in contrast to other countries, in which the organizations creating value assessments, such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in the United Kingdom and the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health, are empowered to provide recommendations for policymakers, and policymakers in turn, use these recommendations when determining coverage and negotiating prices. 28 Importantly, new US legislation (the Inflation Reduction Act) has allowed Medicare to directly negotiate prices for all of the medications in this analysis (to go into effect in 2026). This ability to negotiate drug prices could provide new opportunities to use clinical guidelines and value assessments to improve access to these and other medications.

An additional reason for our findings could be a lack of payer familiarity with AHA/ACC Value Statements as a resource for cost‐effectiveness information. Currently, US payers incorporate value assessments from several different organizations, the most useful being from the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, followed by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. 12 Both of these tools have been available for almost a decade or longer, suggesting that more time may be required for penetration of AHA/ACC Value Statements into policy. Additionally, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review provides direct policy recommendations and has developed collaborative relationships with entities such as the Veterans Affairs and New York State Medicaid. Overall, improved uptake of AHA/ACC value assessments likely requires a combination of national guidance and procedures for incorporating value assessments into drug coverage decisions, empowerment of organizations such as the AHA/ACC to provide direct policy recommendations, and awareness by policymakers of the AHA/ACC Value Statements as a resource for this type of information.

Interestingly, we observed that decreases in out‐of‐pocket costs were greater for SGLT2i, which had an “intermediate value” designation, as compared with ARNI, which had a “high value” designation, and had a relative increase in price compared with DOAC control. This is counterintuitive; in an ideal scenario, higher value medications would have fewer coverage barriers in order to promote medication adherence. One possible explanation for this could be an incentive for pharmaceutical companies to reduce drug prices in order to improve their value designation. For example, regarding dapagliflozin, the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines state that a small “reduction in drug cost would lead to … [meeting] the threshold for high value in this guideline.” 10 Therefore, although our findings suggest limited influence of the guidelines on Medicare drug coverage policy‐makers, it is possible that Value Statements could influence other stakeholders, such as pharmaceutical companies or pharmacy benefit managers, to change their prices in order to obtain a high‐value designation. Alternatively, the presence of 2 different SGLT2i on the market could promote a decrease in drug prices, as compared with ARNI, which has only 1 formulation. Of note, the magnitude of the changes in out‐of‐pocket costs we observed was minimal, at approximately $1 to $2 for a 30‐day supply.

Limitations

Our reported findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations inherent in the study design. Our data were limited to Medicare drug coverage plans, and our findings may not be generalizable to commercial plans or Medicaid. Our analysis was conducted at the plan‐level and does not necessarily reflect the experience of individual patients. Other relevant influences, such as the strength of clinical evidence or the publication of original cost‐effectiveness studies may also have affected drug coverage policies. Of note, SGLT2i are also indicated for diabetes and chronic kidney disease and have been included in the respective clinical guidelines for these conditions since before the study period, 29 , 30 with updates throughout the study period. 31 , 32 Therefore, our findings on SGLT2i may not be specific to HF. Unfortunately, our data set did not allow for us to assess indication‐specific coverage policies. We did not include information on premiums and deductibles in this analysis, nor did we adjust for inflation. Tafamidis was not included this in analysis and could be considered in future studies given its class 1 clinical recommendation, combined with low value designation in the 2022 guidelines. 10 Although the use of causal inference methodology (difference‐in‐differences analysis) is a strength of this study, differences in coverage patterns for the control medication (DOACs) may be influenced by expanding indications and target populations. Moreover, although we accounted for nonlinear trends in the outcome using restricted cubic splines, models were limited to estimating the mean difference pre and post guidelines, and there is a possibility of nonlinear change in the post period that was not explored. Our 1‐year follow‐up period may not have been long enough to capture changes in drug coverage policies in response to guideline publication. Of note, a prior similar study also assessed drug coverage policies the year following guideline publication. 14 Although a longer follow‐up period can allow for more time to assess the policy effect, a longer follow‐up can also make it difficult to attribute the change to the guideline itself, as many other external forces may also be at play. Future studies will use more data to capture the longer‐term or lagged effects of guideline publication when data become available.

CONCLUSIONS

Although Value Statements were introduced into guidelines to promote high‐value, cost‐effective policy decisions, we found minimal changes in the prevalence of drug coverage barriers to ARNI and SGLT2i following Value Statement publication. Overall, our findings suggest limited initial impact of the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines on Medicare drug coverage policies.
