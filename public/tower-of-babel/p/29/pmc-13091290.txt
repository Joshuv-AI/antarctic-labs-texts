Short-chain fatty acids regulate macrophage-mediated immune responses in intestinal inflammation: Implications for pigs
Authors: Junlei Chang, Hua Zhao, Gang Tian, Jingyi Cai, Xiaoling Chen, Jiayong Tang, Gang Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntestinal inflammation poses a significant challenge to pig production, as it impairs gut health, compromises growth performance, and results in substantial economic losses. Therefore, preventing and controlling intestinal inflammation in pigs is essential for maintaining intestinal health and production efficiency. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are gut microbiota metabolites that play an important role in alleviating intestinal inflammation by regulating macrophage function. Mechanistic insights largely derived from murine and in vitro studies have revealed that SCFAs regulate macrophage mediated inflammation through mechanisms involving histone deacetylases (HDACs), nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), and other pathways. Moreover, nutritional strategies such as dietary fiber, prebiotics, and fermented feed for alleviating intestinal inflammation in pigs are discussed. This review aims to provide a theoretical foundation for developing SCFA-based nutritional interventions to improve gut health, immune regulation, and production performance in pigs.Keywords: Short-chain fatty acid, Macrophage, Pig, Intestinal inflammation

Introduction

In recent years, intestinal inflammation has become a common challenge in both human health and animal agriculture. While inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) have gained increasing attention in humans due to their rising prevalence (Kotla and Rochev, 2023), intestinal inflammation is also highly prevalent in pig production. This largely results from the ban on antibiotic feed additives and the higher animal density in intensive farming systems. In pigs, this condition is defined by a range of pathological features including diarrhea, impaired barrier function, microbial dysbiosis, activation of inflammatory pathways, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (He et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). For instance, weaning stress in piglets can induce intestinal inflammation, resulting in diarrhea and reduced weight gain (Tang et al., 2022). Furthermore, concurrent bacterial or viral infections exacerbate intestinal injury and markedly increase the risk of mortality in pigs (van Beers-Schreurs et al., 1992). Therefore, the prevention and treatment of intestinal inflammation are crucial not only for human health but also for livestock production and industry development.

The gut is both a key site of nutrient absorption and a vital immune organ. It contains 60%–70% of the body's peripheral immune cells and hosts a diverse population of microbiota, including bacteria, archaea, fungi, and other microorganisms (Gilbert et al., 2018). These microorganisms are capable of producing bioactive metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), secondary bile acids, and lactate, by processing and utilizing dietary components (e.g., dietary fibers and proteins) or host-derived endogenous substances (e.g., bile acids) (Ma et al., 2024b). Considerable research has demonstrated that these metabolites play a vital role in protecting against pathogen invasion and suppressing inflammation by regulating immune responses and maintaining barrier integrity, among other mechanisms (Ma et al., 2024b; Xin et al., 2022). In the intestinal immune system, macrophages serve as essential agents of innate immunity, contributing significantly to defense against foreign pathogens, regulation of inflammation, and tissue repair (Locati et al., 2020; Wynn et al., 2013). They are involved in the phagocytosis and clearance of cellular debris and pathogens, while also contributing to epithelial cell renewal, the initiation and resolution of intestinal inflammation, and physiological functions such as gut motility and secretion (De Schepper et al., 2018b; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

As microbiome research advances, SCFAs as key gut microbiota metabolites, have attracted considerable interest due to their immunoregulatory functions. Recent studies in murine models suggest that SCFAs can modulate the physiological activities of intestinal macrophages, thereby influencing intestinal inflammation (Sun et al., 2017). For example, butyrate enhances the antimicrobial capacity of macrophages and suppresses the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation (Chang et al., 2014; Schulthess et al., 2019). Because detailed in vivo and in vitro mechanistic investigations are challenging in large animals, many breakthrough discoveries on SCFA–macrophage interactions have been made in mice. Notably, despite interspecies differences, the core functions of macrophages such as phagocytosis, cytokine production and polarization are highly conserved across mammals (Li et al., 2023c; Mass et al., 2023). Therefore, this review synthesizes compelling evidence from murine models on the mechanisms by which SCFAs modulate macrophage function to alleviate intestinal inflammation, and critically discusses these findings for developing novel nutritional strategies in pigs. This not only contributes to the prevention and treatment of intestinal diseases but also provides a theoretical basis for the application of SCFAs in livestock production.

Macrophages and intestinal inflammation

Macrophages are a vital component of the gastrointestinal immune system, playing key roles in maintaining and regulating intestinal homeostasis. They form a dense and highly specialized network throughout the gastrointestinal tract and are distributed across multiple layers, including the lamina propria, submucosa, and muscularis (Delfini et al., 2022). Among these, lamina propria macrophages are the most abundant and serve as the first line of defense against luminal antigens and microorganisms (Viola and Boeckxstaens, 2020). Moreover, the muscularis layer also contains a small number of macrophages, which play a crucial role in regulating intestinal motility and repairing tissue damage (De Schepper et al., 2018a; Hori et al., 2008).

In response to intestinal inflammation, neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes are mobilized to initiate an immune reaction against immunogenic triggers (Hegarty et al., 2023). Macrophages can sense environmental stimuli through Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), thereby triggering subsequent immune responses (Chen et al., 2023; Na et al., 2019). Intestinal inflammation induces macrophage accumulation in the intestinal mucosa and elevated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-23 (IL-23) (Bain et al., 2013; Martin et al., 2019; Thiesen et al., 2014). However, macrophages are also essential for resolving inflammation and promoting tissue repair. Autophagy in macrophages can enhance the clearance of pathogens and apoptotic cells, promote the resolution of inflammation, and facilitate tissue repair (Wang et al., 2023a). Fritsch et al. (2023) suggest that macrophages located near the colonic crypts can promote the proliferation of crypt epithelial cells and alleviate intestinal damage during colitis through a mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)-arginase-1 pathway. Moreover, macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors, including interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and tissue repair molecules such as Wnt1, which maintain intestinal epithelial cell integrity and stimulate intestinal stem cell proliferation (Cao et al., 2022). Consequently, macrophages play a dual role in both promoting and resolving intestinal inflammation, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets for intestinal inflammatory diseases.

Regulation mechanism of SCFAs on macrophages

Short-chain fatty acids are fatty acids with 1 to 6 carbon atoms, generated by gut microbiota fermenting indigestible carbohydrates, including dietary fiber, resistant starch, and endogenous substrates like mucins (Dalile et al., 2019). In the porcine intestine, SCFAs are primarily produced in the cecum and colon, with acetate being the most abundant, followed by propionate and butyrate (Gao et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023b), and these three SCFAs have been the most extensively studied. Therefore, this review primarily concentrates on the impact of butyrate, acetate, and propionate on macrophage function.

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) pathway

Histone deacetylases are a class of enzymes that regulate gene expression primarily by deacetylating lysine residues on histones to condense chromatin's structure (Shanmugam et al., 2022). The family of 18 HDACs also targets non-histone proteins, extending their influence to processes such as lipid metabolism. Furthermore, HDAC activity is critically involved in disease pathogenesis, including infections by porcine enteric viruses such as porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) and transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) (Xu et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2024).

Recent research has highlighted the critical role of HDACs in immune regulation, especially in macrophage-driven inflammatory responses, by modulating the expression of key inflammatory factors and participating in the inflammation process (Table 1). Butyrate, one of the most extensively studied immunomodulators, has been observed in both humans and animals to improve immune responses, regulate inflammation levels, and play a crucial role in maintaining gut health (Stoeva et al., 2021). Studies in murine models have shown that butyrate reduces the secretion of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and interleukin-12 (IL-12) in macrophages by inhibiting HDAC activity (Chang et al., 2014). Similarly, Li et al. (2024b) reported that butyrate alleviates intestinal barrier dysfunction by suppressing HDAC3 expression in macrophages, thereby decreasing IL-6 and TNF-α production in mice. Besides butyrate, propionate also alleviates inflammation via HDAC modulation. Kang et al. (2024) demonstrated that engineering probiotic strains to produce propionate increased intestinal propionate levels, which, in turn, inhibited HDAC1 expression via the macrophage G protein-coupled receptor 43 (GPR43), promoting IL-10 secretion and alleviating mouse intestinal inflammation. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production is one of the key mechanisms by which macrophages eliminate pathogens (Rendra et al., 2019). Chen et al. (2024b) found that butyrate, by inhibiting HDAC3, significantly enhanced the ROS generation capacity of macrophages, thereby improving their antibacterial activity and effectively controlling Leptospira infection in mice, also contributing to the regulation of macrophage osteogenic differentiation (Yang et al., 2019). Furthermore, butyrate has been shown to upregulate antimicrobial peptide gene expression via HDAC3 inhibition in both mice and pigs, further enhancing macrophage antibacterial capacity (Schulthess et al., 2019; Xiong et al., 2016). These findings suggest that SCFAs enhance macrophage antibacterial capacity through multiple mechanisms acting in concert. Notably, the regulatory effect that SCFAs have on macrophages extends to the domain of iron metabolism. During intestinal inflammation, iron homeostasis is disrupted, leading to anemia and immune dysfunction (Huo et al., 2022). Xiao et al. (2024) demonstrated that butyrate alleviates mouse intestinal inflammation and anemia by inhibiting HDAC, upregulating the expression of ferroportin (FPN) in macrophages, promoting iron efflux, and reducing TNF-α secretion. In summary, SCFAs alleviate intestinal inflammation by inhibiting HDAC activity in macrophages, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, enhancing antimicrobial capacity, and modulating iron metabolism (Fig. 1).

Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway

Nuclear factor-kappa B is a dimeric transcription factor expressed in nearly all animal cells. It is essential in inflammation and immune responses, mainly through the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. Furthermore, NF-κB stimulates immune cell activation and proliferation, thereby contributing significantly to the body's immune defense mechanisms (Barnabei et al., 2021; Capece et al., 2022).

Nuclear factor-kappa B signaling has been shown to be significantly activated in drug-induced intestinal inflammation models in mice, as well as in pigs with inflammation triggered by bacterial or viral pathogens (Mukherjee et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025). This highlights NF-κB as a promising nutritional target for alleviating intestinal inflammation and improving gut health in animal production systems. Research has shown that butyrate alleviates colitis in mice by suppressing NF-κB activation and HDAC expression in macrophages, and that this anti-inflammatory effect is independent of IL-10 (Lee et al., 2017). Additionally, butyrate can suppress NF-κB activation and protein kinase B (PKB) phosphorylation by activating the G protein-coupled receptor 109A (GPR109A) receptor on macrophages, thus decreasing the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α and promoting intestinal mucosal repair (Chen et al., 2018). Short-chain fatty acids also confer immunomodulatory benefits against infectious pathogens such as S. aureus, which induces intestinal inflammation in pigs (Li et al., 2022). In vitro evidence demonstrates that butyrate and propionate alleviate this inflammation in macrophages by suppressing the NF-κB/HDAC axis and excessive nitric oxide (NO) production (Park et al., 2019), highlighting their potential as nutritional interventions against S. aureus infection in pigs. High-fat diets (HFD) disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis and immune balance, leading to reduced production of SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate, which in turn elevates circulating lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels and triggers intestinal inflammation (Li et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2020). Wang et al. (2024a) demonstrated that supplementation with arctigenin increased intestinal SCFA levels, thereby alleviating HFD-induced metabolic disorders and intestinal inflammation in mice. Further in vitro experiments revealed that butyrate and propionate mitigate adipose tissue inflammation and insulin resistance by inhibiting NF-κB activation in mouse macrophages (Li et al., 2024a). Similarly, a HFD promotes more rapid resolution of inflammation through acetate-mediated NF-κB suppression in mouse immune cells (Vieira et al., 2017). Consequently, these findings suggest that dietary interventions can modulate SCFA production, which in turn shapes macrophage-mediated immune responses, particularly through the regulation of NF-κB signaling pathways.

Macrophage polarization

The phenotype and function of macrophages exhibit significant heterogeneity, allowing them to dynamically adjust their characteristics in response to specific microenvironmental cues or stimulatory factors. Based on distinct activation pathways and biological effects, macrophages are primarily categorized into two functional subsets: M1 macrophages (pro-inflammatory, classically activated pathway) and M2 macrophages (anti-inflammatory, alternatively activated pathway). This process, referred to as macrophage polarization (Fig. 2), is essential for maintaining immune homeostasis (Luo et al., 2024; Shapouri-Moghaddam et al., 2018). Upon exposure to LPS, TNF-α, and other stimuli, resting macrophages (M0) polarize into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages; interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) drive the polarization of M0 macrophages into anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (Cutolo et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2024). Depending on the specific cytokines involved, M2 macrophages can be further classified into four distinct subtypes: M2a, induced by IL-4 and IL-13; M2b, activated by immune complexes and TLR agonists; M2c, differentiated in response to IL-10 and glucocorticoids (GC); and M2d, induced by TLR agonists and IL-10 (Boutilier and Elsawa, 2021; Mantovani et al., 2004). Under normal physiological conditions, M1 and M2 macrophages coexist and collectively regulate tissue homeostasis. When the tissue microenvironment undergoes changes, these two phenotypes can undergo bidirectional transitions via epigenetic modifications and signaling pathway reprogramming, enabling them to adapt and respond to inflammation in vivo.

The immune response within the intestine is continuously active and dynamically regulated, playing a crucial role in maintaining gut health and homeostasis. Once this balance is disrupted, excessive activation or suppression of the immune system may lead to aberrant inflammatory responses, resulting in diarrhea and exacerbated intestinal inflammation in pigs and mice (Li et al., 2023a; Tang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Low birth weight (LBW) piglets exhibit prolonged growth retardation and increased mortality due to impaired intestinal development and metabolic disorders (Ferenc et al., 2014). Further investigations have identified that macrophage polarization, towards the pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, plays a critical role in driving intestinal inflammation in LBW piglets and mice (Yan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Therefore, shifting M1 macrophages toward the M2 macrophages represents a crucial strategy for alleviating intestinal inflammation.

Converging evidence from murine and in vitro models establishes the reprogramming of macrophage polarization as a key anti-inflammatory mechanism of SCFAs. Propionate targets mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling to inhibit M1 polarization in murine colitis models, while butyrate counters HFD-induced inflammation via the same mechanism (Shao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Moreover, SCFAs alleviate colitis in mice by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and promoting M2 polarization via the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3/forkhead box O3(JAK/STAT3/FOXO3) signaling pathway (Zhao et al., 2024). In vitro experiments further confirmed that butyrate facilitates M2 macrophage polarization and enhances the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to intestinal mucosal recovery (Liang et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings highlight macrophage polarization as a key mechanism by which SCFAs exert anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, recent studies have shown that microbial metabolites such as ursodeoxycholic acid can alleviate intestinal inflammation in piglets and mice by promoting macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Pi et al., 2023). In addition, lactate has also been reported to induce M2 macrophage polarization and mitigate intestinal inflammation in piglets (Wang et al., 2023c). These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the exploration of how SCFAs regulate macrophage polarization to mediate anti-inflammatory responses and alleviate intestinal inflammation in pigs.

NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3)

NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 functions as a critical intracellular sensor that detects infections and cellular damage. Upon activation, it forms an inflammasome complex with apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) and caspase-1, leading to caspase-1 activation and subsequent maturation and release of IL-1β and interleukin-18 (IL-18), which trigger an inflammatory response (Huang et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022). This pathway is a well-established contributor to intestinal inflammation in piglets, with activation documented in cases of bacterial or viral infection and exposure to dietary antinutritional factors (Bao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Ge et al., 2022). Notably, the alleviation of intestinal inflammation in animals is frequently accompanied by the suppression of NLRP3 activation (Shen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023b).

Dietary butyrate supplementation can alleviate HFD-induced intestinal inflammation in mice by suppressing the NLRP3/caspase-1 pathway in macrophages via GPR109A (Shao et al., 2023). Singh et al. (2023) further confirmed this mechanism, showing that butyrate and propionate reduce S. aureus–induced inflammation in mice by inhibiting NLRP3 activation in macrophages. Moreover, studies have revealed that acetate promotes NO production in alveolar macrophages by activating NLRP3, thereby enhancing bactericidal capacity in mice (Machado et al., 2022). These results suggest that SCFAs regulate inflammation and immune responses via NLRP3 expression regulation in macrophages. Interestingly, other research has demonstrated that activation of the medium-chain fatty acid receptor GPR84 in mice colonic macrophages exacerbates intestinal inflammation by stimulating NLRP3 activation (Zhang et al., 2022), which suggests that excessive intake of medium-chain fatty acids in animal diets may negatively affect gut health. Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of NLRP3 in mediating the immunomodulatory effects of SCFAs in macrophages, and suggest that targeted nutritional modulation of the NLRP3 pathway could be a potential strategy to alleviate intestinal inflammation in pigs.

Nutritional interventions targeting SCFAs

Dietary fiber

It is well established that dietary fiber is an essential nutrient in swine diets, and SCFAs are mainly generated through microbial fermentation. As a result, both the composition and amount of dietary fiber in feed can significantly affect SCFA production (Fig. 3). Studies have demonstrated that high-fiber diets markedly increase SCFA concentrations in the intestines of sows, while also reducing inflammatory responses in both sows and piglets (Liu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). Similarly, supplementing piglet diets with alfalfa fiber or cellulose has been shown to boost the abundance of SCFA-producing gut microbiota and mitigate LPS-induced intestinal inflammation (Sun et al., 2021). Moreover, oral administration of xylo-oligosaccharides to piglets has been found to considerably increase the abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Ruminiclostridium, along with raising SCFA levels (González-Solé et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2019). Notably, soluble fiber, relative to insoluble fiber, more profoundly modulates the gut microbiota and enhances SCFA production. Collectively, these studies indicate that appropriate inclusion of dietary fiber in animal feed can effectively enhance intestinal SCFA production and promote gut health. Previous studies have shown that dietary fiber may enhance immune function by regulating macrophage activation via SCFA signaling (Trompette et al., 2018, 2014). Therefore, increasing dietary fiber levels in pig diets may represent an important nutritional strategy to prevent and alleviate intestinal inflammation through the SCFA–macrophage regulatory axis.

Fermented feed

Fermented feed refers to microbial fermentation products obtained by fermenting feed ingredients with probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bacillus species under appropriate conditions. With the global prohibition of antibiotic growth promoters in animal feed, fermented feed has attracted growing interest as a sustainable alternative that may help reduce antibiotic use, enhance gut health, and improve production performance in pigs. Studies have shown that during the fermentation process, components such as dietary fiber in the feed can be degraded by microbes, leading to increased concentrations of SCFAs, including butyrate and propionate (Chen et al., 2024a). For example, feeding fermented Radix puerariae residue to sows has been shown to improve nutrient digestibility and raise total intestinal SCFA levels (Luo et al., 2021). Similarly, incorporating fermented feed into the diets of weaned piglets has been found to increase colonic acetate and significantly reduce diarrhea incidence (Hung et al., 2019; Xin et al., 2021). During the fermentation process, a variety of beneficial microorganisms, such as Bacillus, Lactobacillus, and Weissella species proliferate and contribute to SCFA production, serving as key microbial players in gut fermentation (Kusumo et al., 2019; Wongsurawat et al., 2023; Xin et al., 2021). Additionally, fermented feed is rich in bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and ferulic acid, which have been shown to regulate macrophage function and exert anti-inflammatory effects (Han et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2022). These findings suggest that fermented feed improves SCFA levels not only through microbial content but also by providing functional metabolites that influence immune responses. Therefore, fermented feed represents a promising nutritional strategy for improving intestinal health in animals via the SCFA–macrophage axis.

Probiotic preparations

Probiotic preparations are live microbial products that contain a high concentration of beneficial bacteria and exert positive effects on the host when administered appropriately. Previous studies reported that supplementation of certain probiotic strains, including Lactobacillus, Bacillus, and Clostridium butyricum, can increase SCFAs concentration and the abundance of SCFA-producing microbiota. For instance, dietary Lactobacillus plantarum supplementation in piglets has been reported to alleviate Escherichia coli induced intestinal inflammation and increase the abundance of butyrate producing bacteria Anaerotruncus and Faecalibacterium (Wang et al., 2018a). Moreover, the supplementation of Bacillus subtilis to the diets of piglets has been found to improve fecal microbial diversity and increase SCFA levels in the hindgut (Duddeck et al., 2023). Probiotics may also directly influence macrophage polarization. Studies have shown that probiotic administration in mice suppresses M1 macrophage polarization and promotes M2 macrophage polarization, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation (An et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). Furthermore, extracellular vesicles derived from probiotics have been demonstrated to regulate macrophage polarization and alleviate E. coli-induced intestinal inflammation in piglets (Tao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023a, Li et al., 2023b). Collectively, these findings suggest that dietary supplementation with probiotics can promote SCFA production and subsequently modulate macrophage polarization to alleviate intestinal inflammation in pigs.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This review summarizes how SCFAs regulate macrophage function via the HDACs, NF-κB, and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, as well as by modulating macrophage polarization, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation. Although most mechanistic insights are derived from mouse models, given the functional conservation of macrophages across mammals, these findings provide a strong theoretical basis for understanding the beneficial role of SCFAs in porcine gut health. Moreover, preliminary pig studies support this hypothesis, emphasizing the considerable potential of SCFAs in swine nutrition and intestinal health management.

However, although macrophages are highly conserved in their functions, the mechanisms by which SCFAs regulate porcine macrophages remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, integrating dietary intervention studies with analyses of porcine gut SCFA profiles and macrophage phenotypic shifts will help clarify the potential roles of SCFAs in modulating intestinal immune homeostasis and alleviating inflammation in pigs. Elucidating these mechanisms will provide a scientific basis for precision nutritional strategies aimed at regulating intestinal SCFA levels through diet to optimize gut immune function and health in pigs.
