Large PPM Lead–Associated Thrombus in a JAK2-Positive Patient
Authors: Dean M Nelson, Cassandra Spanos, Timothy J Roberts, Mark Page, Andrew MacIsaac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntracardiac thrombi are a complication of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) and device leads. While the risk of pulmonary embolism exists, clinically significant pulmonary embolism is rare, possibly because of low embolism rates or small embolic material. Larger thrombi pose greater concern, yet guidelines regarding investigation and management are limited.Case SummaryWe describe the investigation of a large and mobile CIED-related thrombus in a patient with previous infectious endocarditis and myeloproliferative neoplasm, including serial multimodal imaging assessment, individualized management considerations, and anticoagulation.DiscussionLarge intracardiac thrombi have the potential to cause significant morbidity and mortality. The investigation and management of large intracardiac thrombi is an area of cardiology that is largely evidence free. Current literature suggests strategies may involve medical or procedural approaches, but evidence is restricted to case reports.Take-Home MessagesLarge intracardiac CIED-related thrombi remain clinically significant but poorly studied. Anticoagulation is the mainstay of treatment, however decisions are highly individualized.Key words: anticoagulation, cardiac pacemaker, echocardiography, thrombus

History of presentation

A 78-year-old woman underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for palpitations and transient ischemic attack. She was otherwise asymptomatic, without exertional intolerance or features suggestive of heart failure.

Past medical history

The patient had a significant cardiac history, including Streptococcus anginosus native mitral valve (MV) infective endocarditis 3 years earlier after a dental procedure, on a background of posterior leaflet prolapse. This was complicated by multiple embolic phenomena. Surgical MV repair with left atrial appendage ligation and excision, biatrial cryoablation, and closure of patent foramen ovale followed. She subsequently developed complete heart block, with dual-chamber permanent pacemaker (PPM) insertion. She also had mild to moderate nonobstructive coronary artery disease, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

The patient's noncardiac history included JAK2-positive myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN), diagnosed 1 year earlier, as well as diverticulosis, hyperthyroidism, and gastroesophageal reflux. Medications included aspirin, statin, hydroxyurea, atenolol, perindopril, spironolactone, frusemide, rosuvastatin, amlodipine, and pantoprazole. Surveillance TTE was performed 10 months before the current presentation (Figure 1, Video 1).

Surveillance Transthoracic Echocardiography Performed 10 Months Before Admission

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal appearance of the right ventricular permanent pacemaker lead with no lead-adherent mass.

Investigations

TTE (Figure 2, Video 2) demonstrated a dense 5.0 × 2.8 × 2.9 cm right atrial (RA) mass that appeared attached to the right ventricular (RV) pacing lead. The mass moved with the lead into the RV, without affecting tricuspid valve function or causing obstruction, and it appeared separate to the valve itself. RV size and function was otherwise normal. The left ventricle was moderately dilated, with low to normal function (ejection fraction: 50% ± 5%), and satisfactory function of MV repair with mild mitral regurgitation. Abnormal septal motion in keeping with RV pacing was noted.

Transthoracic Echocardiography Demonstrating Large, Mobile, Echodense Material Adherent to Right Ventricular Lead

The material moved into the right ventricle without compromising tricuspid valve function.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiogram demonstrated a large filling defect within the RA, through which the RV lead traversed (Figure 3). No definitive RV thrombus was observed within the limitation of the study modality. No pulmonary embolism was identified. Incidental pulmonary nodules were observed.

Findings on Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiogram

Serial blood cultures were negative.

Differential diagnosis

RV lead–associated thrombus potentially related to JAK2-positive MPN was the preferred differential. Lead-associated vegetation in the setting of previous infective endocarditis and implanted material was not favored given that blood cultures were negative and symptoms did not fulfill Duke criteria. RA solid lesion was considered.

Management

The patient was commenced on Clexane (enoxaparin; 1 mg/kg twice daily) for presumed thrombus. The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary heart conference, with favored diagnosis of PPM lead–associated thrombus, and a decision was reached for outpatient transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and anticoagulation with warfarin (target international normalized ratio: 2-3). Warfarin was chosen over direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) given its known efficacy in PPM-associated thrombus and MPN.

TEE performed 2 weeks later demonstrated a large, irregular, and highly mobile echogenic mass measuring 5.0 × 3.3 × 1.7 cm in the RA and prolapsing into the RV (Figure 4, Video 3). No significant reduction in size compared to prior TTE was observed despite Clexane bridging and therapeutic international normalized ratio. Differential diagnosis of vegetation or atrial mass remained of concern. The patient was again discussed at a multidisciplinary conference, with plan to repeat TTE after a further period of anticoagulation, prior to considering further investigation or PPM lead extraction given high morbidity/mortality concerns with this procedure in chronically implanted leads.

Outcome and follow-up

TTE was repeated (Figure 5, Video 4) 1 month later (7 weeks after anticoagulation) and demonstrated complete resolution of the RA mass, confirming PPM lead–associated thrombus. The patient had not experienced symptoms or signs suggestive of embolism. She will be continued on aspirin and warfarin indefinitely.

Transthoracic Echocardiography After Period of Anticoagulation

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated resolution of right ventricular lead–associated echodense material, confirming permanent pacemaker lead–associated thrombus.

Discussion

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), including PPMs, are widely used, safe, and potentially lifesaving for patients. Long-term complications such as infection and thrombus formation contribute to patient morbidity and mortality. Intracardiac thrombi associated with CIED leads are increasingly recognized, particularly in patients undergoing lead extraction or ablation, with reported incidences of up to 30% on intracardiac echocardiography and similar rates in postmortem series.1, 2, 3

Risk factors for CIED-associated thrombus aligns with Virchow's triad. Endothelial injury may occur during implantation or lead movement, while venous stasis can arise in setting of atrial arrhythmia, chamber dilation, or altered flow patterns. Hypercoagulability remains an important contributory role, including in MPN, as seen in the current case.4 However, established risk factors for venous thrombosis, alongside PPM lead number and anticoagulation status, have inconsistently been associated with CIED-lead thrombus.2 These mechanisms highlight the need for careful assessment of intracardiac masses in patients with CIEDs.

Although CIED-related thrombus has potential to embolize, clinically significant/symptomatic pulmonary embolism occurs infrequently, possibly related to low embolism rates or small embolic burden. Most thrombi and embolic events are clinically silent. TTE and TEE are recommended in identifying CIED-lead vegetations, masses, and valvular involvement in suspected infections. Lead masses in asymptomatic individuals may be observed, and they do not predict CIED-related infective endocarditis over long-term follow up.5 Once identified, careful clinical assessment is required to exclude infection or nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis, including serial echocardiography or additional imaging.

Excluding infection is critical but can be challenging, requiring the integration of blood cultures, echocardiography, and clinical risk profiling. In our case, absence of bacteremia, not fulfilling Duke criteria, and presence of an alternate mechanism (given MPN) supported a diagnosis of thrombus over vegetation. TEE-guided transvenous biopsy has been reported to effectively differentiate vegetation from thrombus, but it was not required here.

Consensus guidelines for the management of CIED-related thrombus are lacking, however anticoagulation remains the mainstay of treatment.6 Heparin, warfarin, and DOACs all have limited evidence as therapy. Evidence comparing anticoagulant classes is limited, however case reports have described the development or progression of thrombus on DOAC therapy,7 whereas warfarin has been associated with resolution in multiple cases, including ours.8,9 Duration of anticoagulation is also variable, with reported resolution ranging from days to several months.9 A treatment period of approximately 3 months with close monitoring before reassessment appears reasonable in many cases. Further evidence to guide anticoagulant therapy, duration, and escalation to invasive approaches is required.

Invasive approaches, including lead extraction, surgical thrombectomy, or mechanical aspiration, are generally reserved for cases with persistent, large, or highly mobile thrombi, or where embolic events occur despite adequate anticoagulation.2,5,10 These interventions carry procedural risk, especially with chronically implanted leads, and must be weighed carefully against the probability of spontaneous resolution. Extraction of leads with incidental mobile thrombi <2 cm has been demonstrated as safe,2 and the 2020 European Heart Rhythm Association guidelines5 recommend consideration of open lead extraction for thrombi >2 cm.

Our case reinforces the importance of multimodality imaging, careful exclusion of infection, and recognition of underlying hypercoagulable states in the diagnosis of CIED-related masses. It also demonstrates that even large, mobile thrombi may resolve completely with systemic anticoagulation alone when managed with close clinical follow-up. Significant morbidity and mortality risks exist in the removal of chronically implanted CIED leads, alongside the risk of peri-interventional pulmonary embolism where there is associated thrombus. In our case, avoiding unnecessary operative management was a priority.

Further research to establish optimal investigative and therapeutic strategy, particularly anticoagulant selection/duration and the role of interventional therapies, is required. Tailoring management to thrombus size and mobility is warranted to balance the risk of undertreatment with unnecessary invasive interventions.
