A van der Waerden-free proof of Rado’s theorem
Mauro Di Nasso, Lorenzo Luperi Baglini

Abstract
. We present a proof of the sufficiency of Rado’s condition for the partition regularity of linear Diophantine equations that avoids any use of van der Waerden’s theorem. The proof is based on fundamental properties that are common knowledge in combinatorics of numbers and is entirely “elementary,” with the sole exception of a standard application of the compactness principle.



Introduction

A fundamental result in arithmetic Ramsey Theory is
Rado’s Theorem

that characterizes the linear Diophantine equations
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}=0

that are partition regular as those where
a partial sum of the coefficients equals zero.
This latter property is known as “Rado’s condition”.
The original proof of Rado’s Theorem is grounded on the observation that
if Rado’s condition holds, then long enough arithmetic progressions contain solutions
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=0
; to complete the proof,
one then applies van der Waerden’s Theorem about the existence of arbitrarily long arithmetic solutions.
At the best of our knowledge, no elementary proof of the sufficiency of
Rado’s condition avoiding any use of van der Waerden’s Theorem has ever been given, where by “elementary proof” we mean a proof that does
not appeal to any advanced machinery coming from other mathematical areas
and that are often used in arithmetic number theory,
such as ergodic theory, discrete Fourier analysis, algebra on the space
of ultrafilters, or nonstandard analysis.
This paper is aimed at filling this void.

We aim to keep this paper self-contained,
so in the first two sections we recall the few notions and properties that we need,
and in Section 3 we give our proof of Rado’s Theorem. Section 4 is dedicated
to a short discussion about possible generalizations of our work.

1.
Terminology

Let us now recall the basic notions and fix terminology.

Definition 1.1
.

A finite
coloring
of a set
X
is a finite partition
X=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}
.
The pieces
C_{i}
of the partition are called
colors
.

Given a finite coloring as above, a set
F
is called
monochromatic

if all elements of
F
have the same color,
i.e.

F\subseteq C_{i}
for some
i
.

For simplicity, if not specified otherwise,
in the following all considered families
\mathcal{F}
are assumed to be upward closed,

i.e.
if
G\supseteq F\in\mathcal{F}
then also
G\in\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 1.2
.

A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
is called
partition regular

on
X
if for every finite coloring of
X
there exists an element
F\in\mathcal{F}
which is monochromatic.

A family
\mathcal{F}
is called
strongly partition regular

if for every
A\in\mathcal{F}
and for every coloring

A=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}
, one of the colors
C_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
.

In what follows, we will be interested in the case where
X=\mathbb{N}
is the set of natural numbers; we agree that in what follows
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of positive integers,
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
denotes the set of non-negative integers.

Historically, the first example of a partition regular property involving equations was provided by
a result of Schur’s

(Schur)
.

For every finite coloring of
\mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}

a,b,a+b\in C_{i}
.

A fundamental result in this area was then proved by van der Waerden

With an ingenious and rather intricate double induction procedure, he proved the following.

Theorem 1.4

For every

Theorem 1.5

(van der Waerden – Brauer)

For every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and for every coloring of
\mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}

there exists a monochromatic
\ell

a,d,a+d,\ldots,a+(\ell-1)d\in C_{i}
.

We remark that also the multiplicative versions of the above results hold,
where additive Schur’s triples are replaced by multiplicative triples “
a,b,ab

and where arithmetic progressions are replaced by geometric

a,ad,ad^{2},\ldots,ad^{\ell-1}
, respectively.

Since its publication, van der Waerden’s result
has become one of the cornerstones of arithmetic Ramsey theory,
and a fundamental tool that is found in many proofs.
A typical example is given by Rado’s Theorem (see below)
about partition regular equations.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We say that the equation “
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
”
is
partition regular
on
\mathbb{N}
to mean that the family of solutions

\mathcal{F}_{f}=\{\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{k}\mid f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=0\}
is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.
(This means that for every coloring
\mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}
there exist
monochromatic
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in C_{i}
which are a solution
f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=0
.)

In this case, by abusing terminology, sometimes we will directly say
the function
f
is
partition regular
on
\mathbb{N}
.

Partition regularity can be seen as a property that
indicates the existence of a large and “well-spread”
set of solutions, in the sense that in every given finite partition of the natural numbers
one always find elements of the same color that are a solution.

Note that Schur’s Theorem states the partition regularity of the equation
x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}=0
.
A few years later, in 1933 R. Rado
[
11
]
found a simple full characterization of
all linear Diophantine equations that are partition regular.

Theorem 1.7

(Rado)
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=0
is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

The following “Rado’s condition” holds:

There exists a nonempty
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}=0
.

The necessity of Rado’s condition can be proven directly via elementary
arithmetic arguments; see for instance
[
7
, §3.2]
for details.
About the converse implication, the original proof is grounded on the observation
that Rado’s condition suffices to show that
long enough arithmetic progressions contain solutions
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=0
; the partition regularity property then
follows by an application of van der Waerden’s Theorem, as refined by Brauer. Our goal is to provide an “almost-elementary” proof of the sufficiency of Rado’s condition that avoids the use of van der Waerden’s Theorem. Our only “non-elementary” tool
is the compactness principle, which is however widely used and common knowledge of researchers in combinatorics.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we will recall all the needed basic notions and properties
involved in our proof of the sufficiency of Rado’s condition.
In order to make this paper self-contained, and to show that all the arguments we need
are indeed elementary, we will also include most of the proofs of these properties.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
the
leftward additive shift
of
A
by
n

and the
rightward additive shift
by
n
are the following sets, respectively:

A-n:=\{m\in\mathbb{N}\mid n+m\in A\}\,;\quad n+A:=\{n+m\in\mathbb{N}\mid m\in A\}.

The
leftward
and
rightward multiplicative shift
are defined similarly:

A/n:=\{m\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\cdot m\in A\}\,;\quad n\cdot A:=\{n+m\mid m\in A\}.

Shifts can be used to introduce several
elemental notions in combinatorics of numbers.

2.1.
Thick, syndetic, and piecewise syndetic sets

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.

•

A
is
additively thick
if it includes arbitrarily long intervals;
equivalently, if every finite set
F=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}

has an additive rightward shift included in
A
,
i.e.
there exists
x\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x+F:=\{x+n_{1},\ldots,x+n_{k}\}\subseteq A.

•

A
is
multiplicatively thick
if it includes arbitrarily large
sets of multiples; equivalently, if every finite set
F=\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}

has a multiplicative rightward shift included in
A
,
i.e.
there exists
x\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x\cdot F:=\{x\cdot n_{1},\ldots,x\cdot n_{k}\}\subseteq A.

•

A
is
additively syndetic
if it has “bounded gaps”,

i.e.
if there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every interval
I

of length
|I|\geq k
one has that
A\cap I\neq\emptyset
.

•

A
is
multiplicatively syndetic
if it has “bounded gaps on multiples”,

i.e.
if there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{N}

one has that
A\cap\{x,x\cdot 2,\ldots,x\cdot k\}\neq\emptyset
.

•

A
is
additively piecewise syndetic

if it is the intersection of an additively thick set with
an additive syndetic set.

•

A
is
multiplicatively piecewise syndetic

if it is the intersection of a multiplicatively thick set with
a multiplicatively syndetic set.

We itemize below the first basic properties.
Proofs are directly obtained from the definitions and are omitted.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. Then

(1)

A
is additively syndetic if and only the complement
A^{c}
is not additively thick
if and only if
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}(A-n_{i})
is a finite union of
leftward additive shifts of
A
.

(2)

A
is multiplicatively syndetic if and only the complement
A^{c}
is not multiplicatively thick
if and only if
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}A/n_{i}
is a finite union of
leftward multiplicative shifts of
A
.

A well-known relevant property of piecewise syndetic sets that is neither satisfied
by thick nor by syndetic sets, is partition regularity.

Proposition 2.3
.

The family of additively (or multiplicatively) piecewise syndetic sets
is strongly partition regular.

Proof.

Below, we do not specify whether we are considering the additive or the multiplicative notion,
since the arguments used in the proof are exactly the same for both cases.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be piecewise syndetic.
By induction on
r
we will show that in every
partition
A=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}
one of the pieces
C_{i}
is piecewise syndetic.
The base case
r=1
is trivial.
At the inductive step
r>1
, let
C^{\prime}:=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r-1}
.
We note that, using the inductive hypothesis, it suffices to show that one
of
C^{\prime}
and
C_{r}
is piecewise syndetic.

Pick a syndetic set
S
and a thick set
T
such that
A=S\cap T=C^{\prime}\cup C_{r}
.
We distinguish two cases.
If
S\setminus C^{\prime}
is syndetic, then

C_{r}=(S\setminus C^{\prime})\cap T
is piecewise syndetic as the intersection of a syndetic and a thick set;
if instead
S\setminus C^{\prime}
is not syndetic then the complement

(S\setminus C^{\prime})^{c}=S^{c}\cup C^{\prime}
is thick, and in this case

C^{\prime}=S\cap(S^{c}\cup C^{\prime})
is piecewise syndetic.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The above result is a particular case of a more general property.
Precisely, along the same lines, one can prove the following:

•

For every nonempty family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
, the family

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\{A\cap B\mid A\in\mathcal{F},\ B^{c}\notin\mathcal{F}\}

is partition regular on
X
.
3
3

3

The family
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{B^{c}\mid B\in\mathcal{F}\}
is usually called the
dual family
of
\mathcal{F}
.

2.2.
Delta-sets

A notion of largeness that is widely used in combinatorics of numbers is
obtained by considering sets of differences.

Definition 2.5
.

The
Delta-set
of a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is defined as:

\Delta(X)=:\{x^{\prime}-x\mid x<x^{\prime}\ \text{in}\ X\}.

A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
Delta-large
if
A\supseteq\Delta(X)

for an infinite
X
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Every thick set is Delta-large.

Proof.

Let
T
be a thick set. We recursively define an increasing sequence
(x_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N})

such that
x_{m}-x_{n}\in T
for all
n<m
.
Pick any
x_{1}\in T
. At the inductive step,
pick an interval
I=[y,y^{\prime}]\subseteq T
of length
y^{\prime}-y>x_{n}
, and let
x_{n+1}=y^{\prime}
.
Then one has
x_{n+1}-x_{n}\in T
for every
i\leq n
because

y<y^{\prime}-x_{n}\leq x_{n+1}-x_{i}<y^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

The family of Delta-large sets is strongly partition regular.

Proof.

This is a straight application of Ramsey’s Theorem.
Indeed let
A=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}
be a finite coloring of
a Delta-large set
A
. Pick an infinite
X=\{x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots\}

such that
\Delta(X)\subseteq A
, and define the coloring
of the pairs
[\mathbb{N}]^{2}=D_{1}\cup\ldots\cup D_{r}
by letting
\{n<m\}\in D_{i}\Leftrightarrow x_{m}-x_{n}\in C_{i}
.
By Ramsey’s Theorem there exists an infinite
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
[H]^{2}\subseteq D_{i}
is monochromatic.
This means that if we let
X^{\prime}:=\{x_{n}\mid n\in H\}
then
\Delta(X^{\prime})\subseteq C_{i}
,
and hence
C_{i}
is Delta-large.
∎

2.3.
Banach density

One more basic notion that we will use is that of Banach density, sometimes called “uniform density”.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The
upper Banach density
(or simply the
Banach density
) of
A

is defined by setting:

\text{BD}(A)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|(A-x)\cap[1,n]|}{n}\right)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|A\cap[x+1,x+n]|}{n}\right).

The above limit superior can be proved to be an actual limit. Indeed, one can show that

\text{BD}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|(A-x)\cap[1,n]|}{n}\right)=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|(A-x)\cap[1,n]|}{n}\right).

Remark 2.9
.

A more commonly used notion of density is that of
upper asymptotic density
,
which is defined by setting:

\overline{d}(A)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap[1,n]|}{n}.

Note that
\text{BD}(A)\geq\overline{d}(A)
since in Banach density one
considers arbitrary intervals instead of just initial segments
[1,n]
.
It is not difficult to find examples of sets
A
such that
\overline{d}(A)=0
but
\text{BD}(A)=1
.

Clearly
0\leq\text{BD}(A)\leq 1
. Note that Banach density is invariant under additive shifts;
besides, multiplicative shifts preserve non-zero density.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

(1)

\text{BD}(A-n)=\text{BD}(n+A)=\text{BD}(A)
.

(2)

\text{BD}(n\cdot A)=\frac{\text{BD}(A)}{n}
.

In general Banach density is not additive; indeed one easily finds
examples of disjoint sets
A\cap B=\emptyset
such that

\text{BD}(A\cup B)<\text{BD}(A)+\text{BD}(B)
.
However, similarly to upper asymptotic density, subadditivity holds.

Proposition 2.11
.

For all
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
one has
\text{BD}(A\cup B)\leq\text{BD}(A)+\text{BD}(B)
.

Proof.

The proof is obtained by observing that for every
x
one has

\frac{|(A\cup B)\cap[x+1,x+n]|}{n}\leq\frac{|A\cap[x+1,x+n]|}{n}+\frac{|B\cap[x+1,x+n]|}{n}.\qed

As straight consequences of subadditivity, we obtain the following

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

If
\text{BD}(A)=\text{BD}(B)=0
then also
\text{BD}(A\cup B)=0
.

(2)

If
\text{BD}(A)=0
then its complement
\text{BD}(A^{c})=1
.

Banach density behaves like a finitely additive measure in the case
of families of pairwise disjoint shifts of a given set.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and let
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{k}
.
If
(A-x_{i})\cap(A-x_{j})=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j

then
\text{BD}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(A-x_{i})\right)=n\cdot\text{BD}(A)
.

Proof.

Let us prove the property when
k=2
; the general case then easily follows by induction.
For every
n
, pick an interval
I_{n}
of length
n
such that

a_{n}:=|A\cap I_{n}|=\max_{x\in\mathbb{N}}|(A-x)\cap[1,n]|
, so that
\text{BD}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{n},\eta_{n}
be such that
|(A-x_{1})\cap I_{n}|=a_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}

and
|(A-x_{2})\cap I_{n}|=a_{n}+\eta_{n}
. Clearly
|\varepsilon_{n}|\leq x_{1}
and
|\eta_{n}|\leq x_{2}
,
and so we have the following chain of inequalities:

\text{BD}(A)+\text{BD}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}+a_{n}}{n}=\\
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{|(A-x_{1})\cap I_{n}|}{n}+\frac{|(A-x_{2})\cap I_{n}|}{n}+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}+\eta_{n}}{n}\right)\leq\\
\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|(A-x_{1})\cap I_{n}|}{n}+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|(A-x_{2})\cap I_{n}|}{n}+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{n}+\eta_{n}}{n}\leq\\
\leq\text{BD}(A-x_{1})+\text{BD}(A-x_{2})+0=\text{BD}(A)+\text{BD}(A).\qed

We conclude this subsection by recalling a well-known fact that
connects sets of positive Banach density
and Delta-sets.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
have Banach density
\text{BD}(A)=\alpha>0
.
If
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
contains more than
1/\alpha
elements,
then
\Delta(A)\cap\Delta(X)\neq\emptyset
.
In particular, if
\text{BD}(A)>0
then
\Delta(A)\cap\Delta(X)\neq\emptyset

for all infinite
X
.

Proof.

Consider elements
x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{n}
in
X
where
n>1/\alpha
.
If we show that there exist
i<j\leq n
with
(A-x_{i})\cap(A-x_{j})\neq\emptyset
then
we are done. Indeed in this case we can pick
y\in(A-x_{i})\cap(A-x_{j})
, and
if
a,a^{\prime}\in A
are such that
y=a-x_{i}=a^{\prime}-x_{j}
then
x_{j}-x_{i}=a^{\prime}-a\in\Delta(A)\cap\Delta(X)
.
So, assume towards a contradiction that
(A-x_{i})\cap(A-x_{j})=\emptyset
for all
i<j\leq n
.
Then, by Proposition
2.13
we would have

\text{BD}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(A-x_{i}))=n\cdot\alpha>1
, a contradiction.
∎

2.4.
The principle of compactness

A well-known tool in combinatorics is given by the compactness principle,
a general property that connects infinitary and finitary properties.
There are several different formulations of that fundamental principle;
the one we will use in the sequel is the following.
A proof can be found,

Theorem 2.15

(Compactness principle)

Assume that

\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{P}(Y)

r
-partition regular on

By “

restricted to
r

The crucial tool we will need is a sort of universal property of the piecewise syndetic
sets with respect to partition regularity.
To the author’s knowledge, it was first proven in
[

by using compactness of the topological spaces

but has since been considered several times in the literature;

[

which was proven using the ultrafilter method.
We give here a short proof as a straight application of combinatorial compactness.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
be a partition regular family.
If
\mathcal{F}
only contains finite sets then
every additive (or multiplicative) piecewise syndetic set includes an
additive (or multiplicative, resp.) shift of an element of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

We consider here only the multiplicative case,
because the additive one is proved by using exactly the same arguments.

Given a multiplicatively piecewise syndetic set
A
,
let
r\in\mathbb{N}
be such that the union
T:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}A/i
is multiplicatively thick.
Since
\mathcal{F}
only contains finite sets, by compactness
there exists
n
such that for every
r
-coloring of
[1,n]

there exists a monochromatic set
F\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{P}([1,n])
.
By multiplicative thickness,
there exists
m
such that the multiplicative shift
m\cdot[1,n]\subseteq T
.
Now consider the
r
-coloring
[1,n]=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}

where for every
x\in[1,n]
one lets
x\in C_{j}
if and only if
j=\min\{i\leq k\mid mx\in A/i\}
.
Let
F\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{P}([1,n])
be monochromatic, say
F\subseteq C_{i}
.
Then, by definition, for every
x\in F
we have
mx\in A/i
,
and hence the multiplicative shift
(im)\cdot F\subseteq A
, as desired.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

With essentially the same proof, the result above can be extended

The central role of multiplicatively piecewise syndetic sets
for our purposes is shown by the following property, which was first
pointed out in
[
9

The proof given there uses algebra in the space of ultrafilters,
but it is shown here as a direct consequence of the
universal property of piecewise syndetic sets that we have seen above.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
be a homogeneous polynomial.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The equation
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

For every multiplicatively piecewise syndetic set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}

there exist elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
that are a solution,
i.e.

P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=0
.

Proof.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
directly follows from the partition regularity
of multiplicatively piecewise syndetic sets (see Proposition
2.3
).

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Consider the family

\mathcal{F}=:\left\{\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{k}\mid P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=0\right\}.

By the hypothesis,
\mathcal{F}
is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
and so, by the previous Proposition
3.1
,
there exists a set
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}
and
y\in\mathbb{N}

such that the multiplicative shift

y\cdot\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\subseteq A
.
If we let
a_{i}:=y\cdot b_{i}\in A
then we have

P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=y^{\ell}\cdot P(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})=0
,
where
\ell
is the degree of homogeneity of
P
.
∎

Combining the two previous properties, one
obtains a valuable tool that allows combining different
equations in such a way as to preserve partition regularity
(see
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume the homogeneous polynomials
P_{i}\left(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,k_{i}}\right)

are partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Then in every multiplicatively piecewise syndetic set
A

one finds elements
b
and
a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,k_{i}}

such that
P_{i}\left(b,a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,k_{i}}\right)=0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

For
i=1,\ldots,N
, consider the set:

\Gamma_{i}:=\{y\in A\mid\forall y_{2},\ldots,y_{n_{i}}\in A\ P_{i}(y,y_{2},\ldots,y_{n_{i}})\neq 0\}.

We observe that
\Gamma_{i}
is not multiplicatively piecewise syndetic because it does not contain
any solution of
P_{i}
, and therefore neither is the finite union

\Gamma:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq A
.
By strong partition regularity, it follows that

A\setminus\Gamma
is multiplicatively piecewise syndetic, and hence nonempty. Finally, by definition,
if
b
is any element of
A\setminus\Gamma
then
for every
i=1,\ldots,N
there exist elements
a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,n_{i}}\in A

such that
P_{i}(b,a_{i,2},\ldots,a_{i,n_{i}})=0
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 3.5

For every

Let
\mathbb{N}=C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{s}\cup C_{s+1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{r}

Remark 3.6
.

The idea of using density arguments combined with the strong partition regularity of
Delta-sets is certainly not new. To the authors’ knowledge, the first use of such arguments
goes back to Bergelson’s paper
[

The crucial property we will use to complete our proof is the following.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume the homogeneous polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.
Then for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
also the polynomial

P_{q}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k},x_{k+1}):=P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k}+qx_{k+1})

is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a multiplicatively piecewise syndetic, and let
q=\frac{d}{c}

where
c\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
By the previous proposition, the
homogeneous polynomial
Q(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=cy_{1}-cy_{2}-dy_{3}
is partition regular.
Now apply Proposition
3.4
to the polynomials
P
and
Q
,
and obtain the existence of elements
b
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},b_{2},b_{3}
in
A
such that

P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},b)=0
and
Q(b,b_{2},b_{3})=cb-cb_{2}-db_{3}=0
.
Since
b=b_{2}+qb_{3}
, the elements

a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},b_{2},b_{2}+qb_{3}\in A
are a solution of

P_{q}
, as desired.
∎

We are finally ready to prove the sufficiency of Rado’s condition.

Theorem 3.8
.

If the linear homogeneous polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]

satisfies Rado’s condition, then it is partition regular on
\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By renaming the variables if necessary, without loss of generality we can assume
that
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}x_{i}

where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=0
for some
1\leq m\leq k
.
The polynomial
Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=:c_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}x_{m}
is trivially partition regular, as it is solved by every constant
m
-tuple.
Then, by the previous lemma, also the following polynomial is partition regular:

Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1},x_{m}+\frac{c_{m+1}}{c_{m}}x_{m+1})=c_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+c_{m}x_{m}+c_{m+1}x_{m+1}.

By iterating the procedure for
k-m
steps, we finally
obtain that
P
is partition regular.
∎

4.
Conclusions

The version of Rado’s Theorem presented here is a special case of
the general formulation that characterizes the partition regularity on
\mathbb{N}
of
arbitrary (finite) systems of Diophantine equations.
The natural generalizations of Propositions
3.3
,
3.4
, and
3.5
,
are valid in the context of systems of equations, but reproducing the arguments of our proof
in this context would require a much stronger version of Lemma
3.7
.
In fact, such a strengthened lemma would have to handle multiple equations and
different added coefficients simultaneously, and we do not know whether such a property holds.
Besides, also the problem of the “injectivity” of the monochromatic solutions
as provided by Rado’s Theorem, is usually proved by using Van der Waerden’s Theorem, or by reformulating the problem in terms of systems on linear equations, see
[
4
]
.
Therefore, we close our note with the following questions:

Question 1:

Is it possible to give an elementary proof of the general Rado’s Theorem
for systems of linear equations, without resorting to Van der Waerden’s Theorem?

Question 2:

Is it possible to give an alternative elementary proof of Rado’s Theorem that proves also the
existence of injective monochromatic solutions
(
i.e.
, solutions whose coordinates are pairwise distinct)?
