Myostatin‐dependent detrusor remodelling mediates contractility impairment in diabetic rats’ bladder – a maximal elastance analysis
Authors: Cheng‐Yuan Lai, Hui‐Hsuan Lau, Ming‐Chun Hsieh, Hsien‐Yu Peng, Dylan Chou, Tsung‐Hsien Su, Tzer‐Bin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of myostatin, an endogenous myokine mediating the pathological progression of myopathy, in contractility impairments of the chronically diabetic bladder remains unclear. This study examined whether maximal elastance (mE) reliably and validly assesses bladder contractility in vivo and investigated the role of myostatin‐associated tissue remodelling in the contractility deficits in diabetic bladder. Eighty‐three female Sprague–Dawley rats were assigned to control (CON), streptozotocin‐treated (STZ; 30 mg/kg twice, i.p.) and STZ with non‐specific IgG (STZ + IgG; 2 mg/kg, i.p.) or Myo‐029 (a myostatin‐neutralizing antibody; STZ + MYO; 2 mg/kg, i.p.). The slope of the end‐emission pressure–volume relationship obtained from pressure–volume analysis was calculated as mE. mE remained relatively constant under altered preload and afterload but increased dose dependently with muscarine infusion (10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min, i.a.) and decreased with atropine infusion (10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min, i.a.). Compared to CON the STZ group showed decreased mE, reduced muscle‐to‐collagen ratio, increased myostatin expression, decreased myosin heavy chain 11 (MHC 11) and smoothelin expressions, as well as upregulated α‐smooth muscle actin (SMA) and collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1) expressions. Treatment with Myo‐029 in STZ animals reversed the reductions in the MHC 11 and smoothelin expressions, muscle‐to‐collagen ratio and mE, as well as the STZ‐upregulated SMA and COL1A1 expression. Collectively mE effectively assessed bladder contractility in vivo. Moreover myostatin contributes to diabetes‐associated deficits in bladder contractility by regulating detrusor remodelling, suggesting that myostatin may represent a potential therapeutic target for diabetes‐associated cystopathy. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a common health problem associated with an earlier onset and increased severity of bladder dysfunction (Brown et al., 2005). Functional deficits in the diabetic bladder progress time dependently (Daneshgari et al., 2009). At the early stage diabetes‐associated polyuria leads to compensatory bladder hypertrophy with myogenic and neurogenic alterations. As time progresses and damaging metabolites accumulate structural and functional decompensation occurs, resulting in diabetic cystopathy, manifested as impaired sensation, increased capacity and decreased contractility (Golbidi & Laher, 2010).

Although the details remain to be fully elucidated, studies investigating the development of diabetic cystopathy have linked myogenic dysfunction in the detrusor to impaired bladder contractility (Deli et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2011). Diabetes alters the abundance of phosphorylated myosin light chain in bladder detrusor muscle (Han et al., 2013). In decompensated bladders the detrusor displays altered contractile characteristics associated with changes in the composition of myosin II isoforms (Peters et al., 2006). Furthermore the pathological progression in detrusor cells derived from the bladders of patients with end‐stage lower urinary tract dysfunction involves a diminished amount of myosin heavy chain (Salemi et al., 2023). These findings collectively suggest that impaired bladder contractility involves adaptive changes in the composition and expression of contractile proteins in the detrusor.

Myostatin, an endogenous myokine also known as growth differentiation factor 8 (GDF8), negatively regulates the growth and differentiation of skeletal muscle (Salemi et al., 2023). Myostatin knockout mice exhibit greater muscle mass and grip strength than their wild‐type littermates (Lee et al., 2022), and a natural mutation of myostatin in a child led to extraordinary muscle mass in the thighs and upper arms (Schuelke et al., 2004). In addition to its well‐characterized role in skeletal muscle atrophy myostatin‐mediated tissue remodelling is implicated in cardiomyopathy of heart failure patients (Berezin et al., 2021) and in the progression of atherosclerosis in the vascular smooth muscle of damaged aortic walls (Verzola et al., 2017). Notably myostatin reduces the transcription of contractile genes, mediating contractility impairment in detrusor cells derived from children with end‐stage lower urinary tract dysfunction (Salemi et al., 2023). These findings suggest that beyond skeletal muscle myostatin contributes to tissue remodelling underlying pathological contractility impairments in bladder detrusor muscle. Nevertheless its potential role in the diabetes‐associated contractility deficits has not been adequately investigated.

This study aimed to investigate whether myostatin contributes to the development of contractility deficits in long‐standing diabetic bladders by promoting tissue remodelling in detrusor muscle. Furthermore because clinical trials have established therapeutic strategies for skeletal muscle myopathy by targeting myostatin (Whittemore et al., 2003) this study also sought to develop a potential protocol for treating diabetes‐associated contractile impairment through pharmacological inhibition of myostatin activity.

Contractility is the intrinsic ability of muscle to generate force independently of loading conditions (Wijayasiri et al., 2012). Although both animal (Gonzalez et al., 2021) and clinical (Sekido, 2012) cystometry records detrusor pressure as a surrogate for in vivo contractility, this equivalence holds only during isovolumic contraction when no energy conversion occurs between detrusor pressure and urine flow (Griffiths, 1995). Moreover detrusor pressure is strongly affected by the bladder's loading conditions: preload (the filling volume before contraction initiation) (Hermieu et al., 2023) and afterload (the outlet resistance that must be overcome during contraction) (Sekido, 2012). Therefore a methodology capable of more precisely assessing bladder contractility in vivo is warranted to explore the underlying pathophysiology of bladder contractility impairments.

Building upon pressure–volume analysis (PVA) (Frank, 1897; Suga, 1979), which illustrates the thermodynamic process of the cardiac cycle (Walley, 2016), maximal elastance (mE) – representing the end‐systolic pressure–volume relationship under isovolumic conditions – sensitively measures changes in the inotropic state of the ventricle with minimal dependence on loading conditions (Mirsky et al., 1987). Thus mE is widely accepted as a reliable and valid parameter for measuring ventricular contractility in vivo (Walley, 2016). Based on equivalent thermodynamic principles PVA of the bladder has been developed to characterize the thermodynamic and viscoelastic processes of voiding in both rodents (Peng et al., 2020, 2021) and humans (Lau et al., 2022, 2023, Lau, Lai et al., 2024c, 2026). Given that PVA effectively analyses the thermodynamic work of the bladder in a manner analogous to the ventricle we hypothesized that mE could also be derived from bladder PVA to assess in vivo bladder contractility for physiological/pharmacological investigations.

To test this we established PVA for the bladder in animal models. After the end‐emission pressure–volume relationship (EPR) was constructed to represent the pressure–volume relationship during isovolumic bladder contraction, the mE of the bladder was calculated as the slope of the EPR. The effects of altered preload, afterload and inotropic status were analysed to examine the validity and reliability of mE as a measure of in vivo bladder contractility.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All animal experiments in this study complied with the guideline of and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University, Taipei, Taiwan (LAC2024‐0370). The care and use of all animals adhered to institutional and national standards and the journal's policies regarding animal experiments.

Animals

Adult female Sprague–Dawley rats obtained from BioLASCO (BioLASCO, Taipei, Taiwan) were housed with ad libitum access to water and food in air‐conditioned rooms at 25 ± 2°C with a 12 h light–dark cycle turned on at 7:00 AM until animals were used for surgery or experiments. Eighty‐three rats (200–300 g) were randomly assigned to one of four groups: control (CON), streptozotocin‐treated (STZ), STZ with Myo‐029 (STZ + MYO) or STZ with non‐specific IgG (STZ + IgG).

Diabetes induction

Diabetes was induced by administering two intraperitoneal injections of STZ (30 mg/kg, i.p., 1 week apart; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Vehicle solution was administered at the same time points to age‐matched control animals. Using a portable glucometer (GM700S, RIGHTEST, Taipei, Taiwan) blood glucose levels were measured after an 8 h fast at 6 weeks after the final STZ/vehicle solution injection. Animals displaying fasting blood glucose levels higher than 250 mg/dL were recognized as diabetic. All STZ‐treated animals exhibited fasting blood glucose levels above 250 mg/dL (STZ, 425.700 ± 109.897 mg/dL; STZ + IgG, 401.100 ± 103.027 mg/dL; STZ + MYO, 443.700 ± 92.988 mg/dL. Each N = 10 rats), whereas control animals maintained glucose levels below 250 mg/dL (149.300 ± 62.507 mg/dL, N = 10 rats), confirming successful induction of a diabetic phenotype.

Cystometry and pressure–volume analysis

At 6 weeks after the last STZ or vehicle injection cystometry was performed under urethane anaesthesia (1.2 g/kg, i.p., Sigma‐Aldrich). During recording depth of anaesthesia was routinely checked, and supplementary doses of anaesthetics would be given on the occurrence of the paw pinch reflex. The bladder was continuously infused with normal saline (0.04 mL/min) to induce rhythmic voiding contractions (Fig. 1A , top). Urodynamic parameters were analysed when the tracings displayed at least two uniform contractions. From the cystometric recordings PVA was generated by plotting intravesical pressure (IVP) against intravesical volume (IVV) (Fig. 1A , bottom). The PVA trajectory proceeded counterclockwise, forming loops corresponding to voiding cycles.

Cystometric recordings used to construct the EPR were primarily obtained by increasing the infusion rate from 0.04 to 0.10 mL/min (in some experiments by partial urethral clamping). Because voiding cycles showed slight fluctuations following the increase in infusion rate at least two consecutive cycles with consistent end‐emission pressure and volume were selected for EPR construction. The EPR (Fig. 1B , orange dashed line) was defined as the regression line of end‐emission points (left corners) of at least two coincident pressure–volume loops. The slope of the EPR was calculated as the mE of the bladder.

For experiments with modified preload the infusion rate was increased from 0.04 to 0.10 and 0.16 mL/min. For experiments with modified afterload both the mid‐line of the genitalia and a quarter‐line between the mid‐line and the right border were marked along the genitalia prior to recording. Afterload was manipulated by applying a rat arterial clip to the mid‐point of the genitalia at one of three positions: 0 (no clamping), 1/4 (quarter‐line) or 1/2 (mid‐line). Mean bladder pressure, mean urethral flow and mean urethral resistance during voiding under the different afterload conditions were analysed offline to confirm increased afterload (Fig. A1). To examine the effects of inotropic modulation animals received intra‐arterial infusions of muscarine (MC; 10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min) or atropine (AP; 10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min) via the left femoral artery. After the cystometry recording animals were killed by injection of overdose urethane (5 g/kg, i.p.).

Drugs administration

Drugs used in this study were as follows: STZ (30 mg/kg (dissolved in ice‐cold 100 mm citrate saline, pH = 4.5), i.p., twice 1 week apart; Sigma‐Aldrich), MC (10 and 100 µM, i.a., 0.002 mL/min (dissolved in ddH2O); Sigma‐Aldrich), AP (10 and 100 µM, i.a., 0.002 mL/min (dissolved in ddH2O); Sigma‐Aldrich), as well as Myo‐029 and non‐specific IgG (both 10 mg/kg (both dissolved in ddH2O), i.p., 2 weeks after the last STZ injection; MCE, Junction, NJ, USA).

Histological analysis

At 6 weeks after the final STZ or vehicle injection rats were perfused under deep anaesthesia with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Bladder tissues were harvested and postfixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 4°C). For haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining paraffin sections (5 µm) were dewaxed, hydrated and soaked in haematoxylin and eosin. For Masson's trichrome staining paraffin sections (5 µm) were stained using a Masson's trichrome staining kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (ab150686, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Images were quantified and analysed using image‐processing software. Mean values from three representative areas per animal were analysed.

Western blot analysis

At 6 weeks after the final STZ injection tissue samples were dissected under deep anaesthesia. Samples were homogenized in lysis buffer containing Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After shaking (0°C, 1 h) lysates were centrifuged (4°C, 14,000 rpm, 20 min), and the supernatant was then collected. Protein concentrations were measured using a commercial assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After heating (95°C, 5 min) equal amounts of protein were loaded onto SDS–PAGE gels (Bio‐Rad, California, CA, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked and incubated (4°C, overnight) with primary antibodies: anti‐myostatin (1:2000, GeneTex), anti‐smoothelin (1:200, Santa Cruz), anti‐MHC 11 (1:1000, Santa Cruz), anti‐α‐smooth muscle actin (1:2000, GeneTex), anti‐Col1A1 (1:1000, Santa Cruz) and anti‐GAPDH (1:5000, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA). After washing membranes were incubated with peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (25°C, 30 min): goat anti‐rabbit IgG (1:5000, GeneTex) and goat anti‐mouse IgG (1:5000, Millipore). Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit (Millipore) and quantified with an analysis system (ChemStudio PLUS Touch, Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD. Wilcoxon's signed‐rank tests were used to compare values of measurement, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

PVA of voiding

As the first step to establish mE for assaying bladder contractility in vivo we constructed PVA from continuously infused cystometry (Fig. 1A , top, 0.04 mL/min) by plotting IVP versus IVV. Consistent with the periodic voiding contractions observed in the cystometry the trajectory in the PVA proceeded counterclockwise, forming repetitive loops that represented voiding cycles (Fig. 1A , bottom). Each loop could be divided into four phases: isovolumic contraction (IVC), emission (E), isovolumic relaxation (IVR) and filling (F).

Maximal elastance of the bladder

We next established the EPR of the bladder by increasing the infusion rate from 0.04 to 0.10 mL/min. Adapted from cystometry tracings (Fig. 1B , top) the PVA demonstrated that an elevated infusion rate shifted the loops upwards and to the right (Fig. 1B , bottom). The EPR (orange dashed line) was defined as the regression line of end‐emission points (i.e. the upper‐left corners) of the loops, and the maximal elastance (mE), which characterizes bladder contractility, was calculated as the slope of the EPR.

Maximal elastance is independent of altered preload

To examine whether mE is affected by loading conditions we first challenged the bladder with increased preload (infused volume) by elevating the infusion rate from 0.04 to 0.10 and 0.16 mL/min. Cystometry tracings and PVAs (Fig. 2A ) showed that increasing infusion rates markedly increased the filling volume (the volume difference between the left and right borders) and progressively shifted the loops upwards and to the right. Nevertheless the EPR established from infusion rates of 0.04 and 0.10 mL/min (EPR0.04–0.10, orange dashed line) closely overlapped with that obtained from 0.10 and 0.16 mL/min (EPR0.10–0.16, blue dashed line). Statistical analysis showed no significant difference between the mEs derived from EPR0.04–0.10 and EPR0.10–0.16 (Fig. 2B ; P = 0.238, N = 10 rats), indicating that variations in filling volume did not affect the EPR; that is, mE was relatively independent of preload changes.

Maximal elastance is independent of altered afterload

Next we modified the afterload, that is outlet resistance, by clamping the urethra at 0 (no clamping), 1/4 and 1/2 of its width. Stepwise urethral clamping significantly increased urethral resistance (Fig. A1). Cystometry tracings and PVAs (Fig. 3A ) showed that progressive urethral clamping shifted the loops upwards and to the right. However the EPR obtained from 0 and 1/4 clamping (EPR0–1/4; orange dashed line) closely matched that from 1/4 and 1/2 clamping (EPR1/4–1/2; blue dashed line). Statistical analysis showed no significant difference between the mEs derived from EPR0–1/4 and EPR1/4–1/2 (Fig. 3B ; P = 0.446, N = 10 rats), indicating that changes in outlet resistance did not affect the EPR; that is mE was relatively independent of afterload.

Maximal elastance is sensitive to positive inotropic status

Having observed that mE remained stable under altered preload and afterload we next examined whether mE could sensitively reflect changes in bladder contractility. To this end EPRs were established using infusion rates of 0.04 and 0.10 mL/min, and bladder contractility was first pharmacologically enhanced by administering a positive inotropic agent MC (10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min) locally to the bladder via the femoral artery. Arterial infusion of MC did not significantly affect systolic, diastolic and mean blood pressure measured from carotid artery of animals (Fig. A2). Representative cystometry tracings and PVAs (Fig. 4A ) showed that compared to the preinstillation control (CON) EPRs progressively shifted upwards and to the left with increasing MC concentrations (MC10 and MC100), whereas the vehicle solution produced negligible effects (VEH). Statistical analysis confirmed that in contrast to the vehicle (Fig. 4B ; VEH, P = 0.678, N = 10 rats) MC significantly increased mE (MC10 and MC100, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively, both N = 10 rats) compared to the control before instillation (CON). Curve fitting with the dose–response model revealed EC50 of muscarine was 1.262 × 10−6 m (Fig. A3)

Maximal elastance is sensitive to negative inotropic status

We next pharmacologically reduced bladder contractility by administering a negative inotropic reagent, AP (10 and 100 µM, 0.002 mL/min) locally through the femoral artery. Arterial infusion of AP did not significantly affect systolic, diastolic and mean blood pressure measured from carotid artery of animals (Fig. A4). Representative cystometry tracings and PVAs (Fig. 5A ) showed that although the vehicle solution produced little effect (VEH), the EPR progressively shifted to the right and downwards with increasing doses of AP (AP10 and AP100) compared to the preinstillation control (CON). Statistical analysis confirmed that AP significantly decreased mE (Fig. 5B , AP10 and AP100, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively, both N = 10 rats) relative to the preinstillation control (CON), whereas the vehicle solution exhibited minimal effect (VEH, P = 0.699, N = 10 rats). Dose–response analysis revealed IC50 of atropine was 2.200 × 10−8 m (Fig. A5). Together with the findings above these results demonstrate that mE is a sensitive parameter for assessing bladder contractility in vivo with minimal dependence on loading conditions.

Diminished mE in diabetic bladder

Given that mE was validated as an indicator of bladder contractility in vivo we assayed mE of control and diabetic animals. Compared to the control group (Fig. 6A , CON) PVAs from STZ‐treated animals (STZ) showed EPRs markedly shifted to the right and downwards, indicating decreased bladder contractility. Statistical analysis confirmed this impairment, showing that mE was significantly reduced in STZ‐treated animals relative to controls (Fig. 6B ; P = 0.019; N = 6 rats).

Tissue remodelling in diabetic bladder

To explore whether the impaired bladder contractility in diabetic animals was associated with tissue remodelling of the detrusor muscle we examined bladder wall morphology. First H&E‐stained images (Fig. 7A ) showed no obvious tissue injury in the urothelium or lamina propria of the bladder in either group, suggesting that epithelial or submucosal damages were less likely to account for the contractile deficit. On the contrary representative images of Masson's trichrome staining – where muscle appears red and collagen blue – revealed that compared to controls (Fig. 7B , CON) the bladders of diabetic animals displayed sparse muscle bundles with excessive collagen and connective tissue deposition within the bundles (STZ). Statistical analysis confirmed diabetes‐associated tissue remodelling, showing a significantly decreased muscle‐to‐collagen ratio in the STZ group compared to controls (Fig. 7C ; P = 0.006, N = 4 rats).

Fibrosis with diminished contractile proteins

We next explored whether diabetes‐associated tissue remodelling was accompanied by altered expression of essential contractile and/or collagen proteins in bladder tissue. Representative (Fig. 8A ) and summarized (Fig. 8B–F ) immunoblot analyses demonstrated that both the abundances of myosin heavy chain 11 (Fig. 8B , MHC 11) and smoothelin (Fig. 8C , STL) were significantly decreased in the STZ group compared to controls (P = 0.001 and 0.046, respectively, both N = 4 rats), confirming that detrusor remodelling involved a reduction in contractile elements. In addition expression levels of α‐smooth muscle actin (Fig. 8D , SMA; P = 0.036, N = 4 rats) and collagen type I alpha 1 (Fig. 8E , COL1A1; P = 0.029, N = 4 rats) significantly increased in the STZ group compared to controls, indicating the involvement of fibrosis in detrusor remodelling.

Upregulated myostatin expression in diabetic bladder

Having confirmed that diabetes‐induced bladder contractility impairment was accompanied by detrusor muscle remodelling we next analysed myostatin expression in bladder tissue, as myostatin has been implicated in the pathological progression of bladder wall remodelling through promoting detrusor muscle fibrosis (Salemi et al., 2023). Representative (Fig. 8A ) and summarized (Fig. 8F ) immunoblot analyses revealed that myostatin abundance was significantly upregulated in STZ‐treated animals compared to controls (P = 0.045, N = 4 rats), suggesting a role for myostatin in diabetes‐associated detrusor remodelling.

Myo‐029 reversed changes in proteins expression

To further assess the involvement of myostatin and explore a potential therapeutic strategy for diabetic cystopathy we administered Myo‐029, a recombinant antibody that neutralizes myostatin activity (Singh et al., 2016), to diabetic animals. Representative (Fig. 8A ) and summarized (Fig. 8B–F ) immunoblot analyses showed that Myo‐029 (STZ + MYO), but not non‐specific IgG (STZ + IgG), significantly restored the expression of the contractile proteins MHC 11 and smoothelin (P = 0.006 and 0.039, respectively, N = 4 rats), as well as depressed SMA and COL1A1 upregulation (P = 0.047 and 0.041, respectively, N = 4 rats) in the bladder tissue compared to the STZ group.

Myo‐029 attenuated detrusor remodelling

Consistently images of Masson's staining demonstrated that compared to the STZ‐treated group (Fig. 9A , STZ) administering diabetic animals with Myo‐029 (STZ + MYO) markedly attenuated detrusor muscle degeneration and collagen/connective tissue deposition, whereas non‐specific IgG (STZ + IgG) had little effect. Quantitative analyses confirmed that Myo‐029 (Fig. 9B ; P = 0.002, N = 4 rats), but not IgG (P = 0.141, N = 4 rats), significantly increased the muscle‐to‐collagen ratio in the bladder wall compared to untreated diabetic animals.

Myo‐029 ameliorated contractility deficits

Finally to confirm the functional significance of myostatin inhibition voiding functions of animals were assayed. Cystometry and PVA measurements (Fig. 10A ) demonstrated that Myo‐029 increased bladder contractility of STZ‐treated animals, as evidenced by an EPR shift upwards and to the left (STZ + MYO), whereas IgG produced negligible effects (STZ + IgG). Statistical analysis (Fig. 10B ) further verified that Myo‐029 (P = 0.003, N = 6 rats), but not non‐specific IgG (P = 0.844, N = 6 rats), significantly increased the mE of the bladder compared to STZ‐treated animals, confirming that the inhibition of myostatin by Myo‐029 effectively ameliorates detrusor remodelling and restores bladder contractile function in diabetes.

Discussion

Assuming that no energy conversion occurs between developed pressure and emitted flow (Griffiths, 1995) both animal (Gonzalez et al., 2021) and clinical (Sekido, 2012) studies have used detrusor pressure as an urodynamic parameter representing bladder contractility in vivo. However because detrusor pressure is not recorded exclusively at isovolumic contraction and is highly dependent on the bladder's loading conditions – including preload (Hermieu et al., 2023) and afterload (Sekido, 2012) – a protocol capable of more precisely assessing bladder contractility in vivo is warranted.

Building on previous basic (Peng et al., 2020, 2021) and clinical (Lau et al., 2022, 2023; Lau, Lai et al., 2024a, 2024 b, 2024 c; Lau, Su et al., 2024; 2026) studies exploring bladder function using PVA the present study, for the first time, established a procedure to measure the mE of the bladder in vivo through the EPR. The EPR represents the pressure–volume relationship during isovolumic contractions in which energy conversion between developed pressure and urinary flow is minimal. Urodynamic data in this study demonstrated that mE is a loading‐independent parameter, as it remained relatively constant under varying preload (filling volume) induced by stepwise increases in filling rate, and under altered afterload (outlet resistance) achieved through gradual urethral clamping. Furthermore experimental modulation of bladder contractility showed that mE is sensitive to contractility changes: mE significantly increased in response to a positive inotropic condition induced by pharmacological activation of muscarinic receptors – the principal excitatory receptors on the bladder wall that mediate detrusor contraction – and conversely decreased under negative inotropic conditions produced by muscarinic receptor antagonism. Together these findings indicate that mE is a contractility‐sensitive but loading‐independent parameter. Thus despite voiding being a thermodynamic process highly influenced by loading conditions mE – analogous to maximal elastance in assessing ventricular contractility (Walley, 2016) – represents a promising parameter for accurately evaluating bladder contractility in vivo. The protocol developed in this study may therefore facilitate future research into the pathophysiology and pharmacotherapy of voiding dysfunction.

Diabetes is a prevalent health problem, and over half of diabetic patients develop bladder dysfunction (Bansal et al., 2011), particularly symptoms and signs related to contractility deficits (Kaplan & Blaivas, 1988). Therefore accurately assessing bladder contractility in vivo in diabetic patients would provide valuable insights for clinical decision‐making. Using the protocol developed in this study we found that mE was significantly reduced in diabetic animals compared to controls 6 weeks after diabetes induction. This finding aligns with previous clinical (Elsakka et al., 2023) and basic (Kaya‐Sezginer et al., 2019) studies demonstrating that chronic diabetes is associated with impaired bladder contractility. Therefore our results further support the validity of mE as a reliable parameter for evaluating contractility deficits in the bladder.

Myostatin, a member of the transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) superfamily, was first identified as a potent negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass in mammals (McPherron et al., 1997; Zimmers et al., 2002). Beyond skeletal muscle emerging evidence indicates that myostatin is also expressed in cardiac myocytes (Sharma et al., 1999), vascular smooth muscle (Verzola et al., 2017) and bladder detrusor muscle (Salemi et al., 2023). Consistent with these findings immunoblotting analyses in the current study confirmed the presence of myostatin in bladder tissue. Notably compared to their control littermates diabetic animals exhibited significantly elevated myostatin expression in the bladder. Similar to previous reports demonstrating that myostatin mediates pathological remodelling in cardiomyocytes of heart failure patients (Berezin et al., 2021) promotes atherosclerosis in vascular smooth muscle (Verzola et al., 2017) and impairs bladder function in patients with end‐stage lower urinary tract dysfunction (Salemi et al., 2023), our findings link myostatin to the pathological progression of contractility impairment in long‐standing diabetic bladders.

This hypothesis is further supported by histological examination, which revealed marked tissue remodelling in diabetic animals characterized by excessive collagen and connective tissue deposition, sparse smooth muscle bundles and a decreased muscle‐to‐collagen ratio. Moreover immunoblotting results showed that the expression levels of essential contractile proteins – smoothelin and MHC 11 – were downregulated. Moreover the abundance of SMA, a key marker of myofibroblasts, and COL1A1 were upregulated in the bladders of diabetic animals. Consistent with reports describing altered contractile characteristics and myosin II isoform composition in decompensated bladders (Peters et al., 2006) these histological and biochemical findings collectively indicate that elevated myostatin levels in diabetic animals are associated with tissue remodelling leading to impaired contractility.

The causal role of myostatin in diabetes‐associated detrusor remodelling was further investigated by administering diabetic animals with Myo‐029, a recombinant antibody that binds to myostatin with high affinity and neutralizes its biological activity (Singh et al., 2016). Unlike non‐specific IgG pharmacological inhibition of myostatin markedly attenuated tissue remodelling, reversing muscle bundle shrinkage, collagen deposition and the decreased muscle‐to‐collagen ratio. Additionally Myo‐029 restored the expression of smoothelin and MHC 11, as well as impeded upregulation of SMA and COL1A1 in bladder tissue. Importantly compared to untreated diabetic animals exhibiting impaired bladder contractility Myo‐029 administration significantly increased bladder mE, indicating the recovery of contractile function. Collectively these findings identify myostatin as a negative regulator mediating diabetes‐associated detrusor remodelling and diminished bladder contractility. Thus myostatin represents a potential therapeutic target for treating diabetes‐associated cystopathy.

It is noteworthy that negative regulators of skeletal muscle mass, including myostatin and activin A, signal via activin type IIA and IIB receptors (ActRIIA and ActRIIB), which complex with transmembrane activin receptor‐like kinases (ALK4 or ALK5) (Han et al., 2021). Engagement of these receptors activates the phosphorylation of Smad2/3 proteins, which regulate downstream gene expression (Han et al., 2021). Therefore in addition to antibody‐based inhibition of myostatin future pharmacological studies targeting ActRIIA/B receptors or downstream signalling molecules could further validate the role of myostatin and provide additional therapeutic options for diabetic cystopathy.

Previous animal and clinical studies have proposed the ‘temporal theory of diabetic bladder dysfunction’ (Daneshgari et al., 2009), which suggests that in the early stage hyperglycaemia‐induced polyuria causes compensatory bladder hypertrophy accompanied by myogenic and neurogenic changes. Over time as toxic metabolites accumulate structural and functional decompensation occurs, leading to a hypocontractile or atonic bladder in the late stage (Golbidi & Laher 2010). Because the bladder deficits and therapeutic effects in the present study were evaluated at 6 weeks after STZ injection future research is needed to clarify the early‐stage pathological progression and to perform longer follow‐ups to confirm the lasting therapeutic effects of myostatin‐targeted interventions.

It is well established that diabetic neuropathy develops at a relatively early stage of the disease (Diabetes Control and Complications Trial Research Group et al., 1993). Considering that late‐stage diabetic bladder dysfunction is characterized by both sensory impairments and contractile deficits – conditions secondary to visceral nerve fibre damage within the bladder wall (Golbidi & Laher, 2010; Kebapci et al., 2007) – the potential involvement of neuropathy in diabetes‐associated contractility deficits warrants further investigation because intact bladder function depends on the co‐ordinated integration of afferent input, central regulatory centres and efferent motor output that activates the effector, namely the detrusor muscle (Sakakibara et al., 2023). Moreover in addition to myostatin‐associated tissue remodelling of the detrusor studies have demonstrated that the mechanical properties of the bladder are largely determined by the urothelium (Birder, 2006) and involve mechanosensitive ion channels present in both the urothelium and the detrusor (Araki et al., 2008). Therefore the potential roles of urothelium‐derived bioactive modulators and their associated ion channels in diabetes‐associated contractility impairment warrant further investigation.

In contrast to type 1 diabetes, which is commonly modelled in rats using STZ administration, the predominant form of diabetes in humans is type 2 diabetes (Ahmad et al., 2022). Notably alterations in bladder contractility in type 1 diabetes are largely driven by hyperglycaemia‐induced polyuria, characterized by compensatory bladder hypercontractility in the early stage and decompensated hypocontractility in the late stage (Ahmad et al. 2022). In contrast bladder dysfunction in type 2 diabetes frequently coexists with obesity, hypertension and vascular disease, which contribute to chronic bladder ischaemia and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to detrusor overactivity (Uzun et al., 2012). Whether the impairment in bladder contractility observed in the type 1 diabetes–like animal model in this study differs from that in type 2 diabetes models warrants further investigation.
