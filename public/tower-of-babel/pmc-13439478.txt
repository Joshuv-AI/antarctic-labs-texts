A physical-numerical approach to predicting tension-induced rock mass collapse using reciprocal tilt rate
Authors: Hang Liu, Zheng He, Mowen Xie, Man Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTension-induced rock masses at steep slope fronts undergo progressive failure under self-weight tensile stress, characterized by deep rear-edge fractures that propagate subcritically over decades before catastrophic collapse. Conventional monitoring systems frequently fail to provide adequate warning because the measurable deformation phase typically lasts only hours to days, despite decades-long crack incubation. We develop an integrated framework combining physical similarity modeling, smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) simulation, and field validation to systematically investigate failure evolution. High-frequency MEMS accelerometers (1000 Hz) capture dynamic tilt progression through spatial vector analysis of gravitational acceleration components. Water-induced weakening at fracture tips simulates natural stress corrosion, accelerating time-dependent deformation under controlled conditions. Results reveal distinct two-stage evolution: prolonged quasi-static deformation followed by rapid acceleration, where reciprocal tilt rate exhibits linear decay toward zero before failure, enabling quantitative prediction. SPH simulations with an elastoplastic-mixed damage model demonstrate that fracture geometry significantly influences prediction window duration, with central horizontal fractures providing optimal early warning conditions. Field validation confirms method robustness: reciprocal tilt rate approached zero linearly across all scenarios, with prediction accuracy within 0.9% and a 38-hour acceleration phase in prototype scale, providing practical lead time for risk mitigation. This integrated physical-numerical approach advances understanding of brittle rock mass failure mechanisms and provides reliable tools for implementing effective early warning systems in rockfall-prone regions.Keywords: Tension-induced fractured rock mass, SPH simulation, Physical model experiment, Reciprocal tilt rate methodSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Mountainous and hilly regions comprise over 65% of China’s land area, where complex geological structures and active exogenous forces have resulted in widespread development of tension-induced hazardous rock masses. Survey data from the Ministry of Natural Resources indicate that collapses of hazardous rock masses account for over 20% of all geological disasters in China each year, ranking second only to landslides, and this proportion has shown an increasing trend in recent years. These failures predominantly occur in deeply incised canyons of southwest China, including the Jinsha River, Yalong River, and Three Gorges Reservoir regions, as well as along weathered rock slopes of the southeastern coast. A diagnostic feature of these rock masses is the development of steep rear-edge tension cracks, often exceeding 70% of the total height, which highlights the strong structural control and the high instability potential1. Under gravity-dominated conditions, the crack tips undergo continuous subcritical propagation driven by stress corrosion2, causing hazardous rock masses to evolve from stable states to catastrophic collapse over decades, while the measurable deformation stage immediately preceding failure typically lasts only a few hours to several days3. This long-term incubation–sudden outbreak characteristic poses significant challenges for disaster early warning.

Large rock masses exceeding 100 m³ in volume exhibit three primary failure modes—sliding, toppling, and falling— distinguished by their mechanical behavior. Conventional early warning strategies are generally adapted from methods used for plastic-failure hazards, such as soil landslides, and rely on indicators including displacement measurements, stress–strain responses, and environmental triggers like rainfall or seismic activity. Nevertheless, these indicators prove inadequate for brittle failures. The interval between detectable acceleration and collapse for rock masses often spans merely minutes to seconds. The fundamental mechanism in hazardous rock mass instability involves progressive rock bridge degradation; when cumulative damage within these rock bridges reaches a critical threshold, catastrophic failure occurs instantaneously4. Both formation and failure are controlled by internal factors (rock mass structure, discontinuity characteristics) and external triggers (rainfall, seismicity, freeze-thaw cycles, anthropogenic activities)5. The tension-induced hazardous rock masses investigated herein typically exhibit upper structural openings along unloading or tectonic fractures, with aperture decreasing progressively downward until closure. Consequently, elucidating failure mechanisms and developing reliable prediction methods constitute critical priorities for geological hazard mitigation6.

Recent research has significantly advanced our understanding of rock mass failure mechanisms and early warning techniques. For instance, studies have integrated fracture mechanics with experimental techniques like Digital Image Correlation (DIC) to quantify fracture toughness degradation under environmental factors such as freeze-thaw cycles7. Concurrently, numerical simulations, including the discrete element method8, have been employed to investigate the time-frequency characteristics and dynamic response of hazardous rock masses under seismic loads. In addition to these methods, the Extended Finite Element Method (XFEM) has been widely applied in rock fracture research. XFEM can effectively simulate crack propagation paths and stress concentration phenomena, especially for complex crack networks9. For example, a study used XFEM to model short crack initiation and propagation in polycrystalline structures, capturing the variability in crack behaviors using random grain morphology10. Another study proposed an improved XFEM-VCCT (Virtual Crack Closure Technique) algorithm to address the issue of overestimating fatigue life in complex structures, demonstrating good engineering applicability11. Peridynamics (PD) is another emerging method that has shown great potential in rock fracture modeling. PD can describe the fracture process from a microscopic perspective and is suitable for simulating crack initiation and propagation12. For instance, a recent study employed an advanced peridynamic approach to simulate equidistant fracture formation in layered rock masses, revealing the significant influence of layer thickness on fracture dynamics13. Another study developed a fast method for quasi-static fracture modeling using peridynamics, showing stable crack evolution under different loading conditions14. Furthermore, physical model experiments, such as granular column collapse tests15, have provided valuable insights into collapse dynamics and impact effects, offering theoretical support for hazard prediction.

The fundamental challenge in predicting these failures lies in their distinctive temporal evolution: decades-long subcritical crack propagation followed by catastrophic collapse within minutes to hours. This long-term incubation–sudden outbreak behavior severely limits conventional monitoring approaches adapted from plastic-failure hazards. Traditional displacement-based methods and environmental indicators often fail to capture the brief acceleration phase preceding brittle failure events. The existing monitoring technologies for hazardous rock mass disasters exhibit scale-dependent limitations. At the regional scale, spaceborne InSAR and ground-based radar (GB-SAR) can identify potential hazards16, and they perform well in the large-scale monitoring of landslides and large-scale hazardous rock mass groups, but are still inadequate for monitoring the localized deformations of individual rock masses. At the local scale, terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) can achieve millimeter-level surface displacement measurements17; however, its typical sampling intervals (hours), risk missing transient precursors. At the sensor scale, MEMS inclinometers offer theoretical accuracy of 0.001°18 but suffer from significant temperature drift. For instance, field tests documented offsets up to 0.15° across − 20 °C to 40 °C thermal ranges, requiring complex compensation algorithms19. More fundamentally, current monitoring approaches prioritize static indicators, such as displacement and environmental factors, over dynamic indicators sensitive to rock bridge degradation (e.g., natural frequencies, acoustic emission). Conventional precursor-based models prove insufficiently responsive for brittle failures, while emerging techniques (acoustic emission, microseismic monitoring) remain challenged by signal-to-noise ratios and lack validated early warning criteria for field applications.

Numerical simulation offers complementary capabilities for investigating failure mechanisms beyond observational constraints. Continuum-based approaches (e.g., FLAC3D) enable the analysis of coupled hydro-mechanical processes under complex loading scenarios (rainfall, seismicity). In addition, Block theory20 provides an indispensable geometric–kinematic framework for analyzing discontinuity-dominated rock masses by idealizing them as assemblages of discrete rigid or semi-rigid blocks bounded by joint sets. It focuses on the removability, kinematics, and mechanical stability of key blocks that govern overall instability, enabling the identification of detachable blocks and tension-controlled failure units. This approach complements continuum and particle-based simulations by providing geometric constraints and failure mode characterization essential for understanding hazardous rock mass behavior21. Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH)22 is particularly suited for simulating tensile failures in rock masses initiated by an abrupt loss of tensile strength. As a mesh-free method, it naturally handles the transition from a continuum to a discontinuum, capturing tensile crack propagation and brittle fragmentation without the numerical issues associated with mesh distortion23. For tension-induced failures specifically, SPH’s tensile-shear hybrid formulations reproduce subcritical crack trajectories24, capturing stress-induced tortuous propagation that complements experimental observations. However, the failure process of hazardous rock masses is highly complex and stochastic, with each site exhibiting distinct geological conditions, structural characteristics, and external environmental triggers, making it challenging to establish a unified warning criteria. Physical experiments and field monitoring, while essential, remain cost-prohibitive and time-intensive, precluding comprehensive parametric exploration. Moreover, systematic validation of numerical models against controlled experiments remains limited, particularly for the specific case of tension-dominated rock mass failures. Consequently, integrated physical-numerical frameworks can overcome individual method limitations, enabling parametric analysis infeasible through experiments alone while ensuring model fidelity through rigorous validation.

This study addresses these gaps through three primary contributions: (1) an integrated physical-numerical framework validating that reciprocal tilt rate exhibits linear decay toward zero before failure, enabling quantitative collapse prediction. Physical similarity experiments (1:100 geometric scale, 1:10 temporal scale) with high-frequency MEMS monitoring (1000 Hz) capture complete failure evolution, while water-induced weakening at fracture tips simulates natural stress corrosion under controlled conditions. (2) An elastoplastic-mixed damage SPH model adopted to quantify how fracture geometry (position, orientation) influences failure mode transitions and prediction window duration. The model couples Drucker-Prager plasticity with Grady-Kipp tensile damage, reproducing experimentally observed crack trajectories and two-stage deformation patterns. (3) The framework is demonstrated across laboratory experiments, parametric simulations, and field monitoring. Collectively, this integrated approach advances understanding of brittle failure precursors in tension-induced rock masses and provides validated tools for early warning system implementation in rockfall-prone regions.

Experiment on the tension-induced rockfall failure model

Physical model

To address the challenge that the evolutionary process of tension-induced rock mass extends over several decades to centuries, making it difficult for in situ monitoring to capture the complete precursor sequence, this study employs a physical similarity model test to overcome spatiotemporal scale limitations. Based on similarity theory (geometric ratio 1:100, temporal ratio 1:10), a composite material mixture of barite powder, quartz sand, and gypsum in a proportion of 6:4:1 was selected to reproduce the mechanical behavior of the natural rock mass while ensuring experimental feasibility. The physical model for hazardous rock (Fig. 1) featured dimensions of 20 cm thickness × 30 cm height × 28 cm width, with 25 cm free-edge length and 21 cm initial fracture depth. This configuration represents typical tension-induced rock masses with deep rear-edge cracks approaching marginal stability conditions.

The laboratory physical model showing tension-induced rock mass configuration with monitoring system layout.

In this test, homogeneous materials were employed to simulate the uniformity of the hazardous rock body and the bedrock, thereby restoring the mechanical characteristics of rear-edge tensile-fracture failure under natural conditions. This design focuses on the failure mechanism dominated by rear-edge fracture propagation, ensuring that the fracture process of the model is consistent with the actual failure behavior of tensile-fractured hazardous rock masses.

Design of tilt deformation data monitoring

High-frequency tilt monitoring system

Tilt monitoring is a critical approach for identifying precursors of tension-induced hazardous rock mass failure. In this study, a MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems)-based tilt monitoring system was developed using triaxial accelerometers operating at 1000 Hz sampling frequency. By precisely measuring variations in the components of gravitational acceleration, the cumulative tilt angle of the hazardous rock mass was calculated. Using gravity as a reference, under quasi-static conditions the acceleration components detected along the sensor’s sensitive axes are approximately considered as projections of gravitational acceleration. When the hazardous rock mass tilts, the gravitational acceleration components along the sensor’s sensitive axes change. The system calculates cumulative tilt angles through spatial vector analysis of gravitational acceleration components. Design of collapse induction method

To simulate the natural, gravity-driven stress corrosion while accelerating the time-dependent deformation evolution of the hazardous rock mass, water-induced weakening was employed by continuous dripping at fracture tips. A drip tube delivered deionized water continuously to the fracture tip, minimizing contact area to avoid mechanical disturbances or significant mass fluctuations. This method accelerates subcritical crack growth under controlled conditions without introducing external mechanical loading or altered boundary conditions, enabling observation of complete failure sequences within feasible experimental timeframes. In the physical test, the initial fracture depth (21 cm, 70% of height) approached marginal stability to ensure measurable acceleration phases. Post-failure inspection (Fig. 3) revealed that tensile fractures comprised > 90% of the failure surface area, confirming tension-dominated mechanisms consistent with design objectives.

Analysis of experimental monitoring data

As shown in Fig. 4a, the collapse evolution of the tensile-fractured hazardous rock mass model spanned 555.7 min (9.3 h), with cumulative tilt reaching 0.061°before collapse. The tilt deformation evolution exhibited distinct phase transitions. In the stage I (Quasi-static, 0–335 min), the tilt increased continuously at an extremely slow rate of 0.0002°/h, representing stable subcritical crack propagation detectable with high-precision instrumentation. In the stage II (Acceleration, 335–555.7 min), once the tilt reaches a critical threshold, the deformation rate sharply increased to 0.04°/h, persisting for 220.7 min (3.7 h) until collapse. Based on the temporal scaling ratio of the physical model (1:10), the 220.7 min laboratory acceleration phase of this hazardous rock mass under natural conditions corresponds to 37.6 h at natural scale. This indicates that such rock masses in nature may have a measurable accelerated deformation window of nearly 38 h. This duration enables feasible implementation of monitoring-based early warning systems with sufficient lead time for intervention measures. Both the physical experiment and numerical simulation comprehensively reproduce the complete evolution of the hazardous rock mass from quasi-static deformation to accelerated failure. Both exhibit the characteristic transition from long-term slow deformation to short-term rapid collapse. The cumulative deformation curves show consistent acceleration patterns, and the reciprocal tilt-rate–time relationship maintains a clear linear decay trend. The rate evolution in both cases presents distinct linear segments that allow quantitative identification of pre-failure timing. Together, these results form a coherent framework linking simulation and experiment, providing complementary evidence for early warning and instability mechanism analysis of hazardous rock mass. Figure 4b illustrates the reciprocal tilt rate (1/ω) evolution during the acceleration phase of the tensile-fractured hazardous rock mass in the experiment.

Experimental monitoring data showing: (a) cumulative tilt angle evolution exhibiting two-stage behavior and (b) reciprocal tilt rate demonstrating linear decay toward failure.

In the initial acceleration stage (approximately 330.7–425.7 min), the reciprocal tilt rate is relatively high with large fluctuations, reflecting transitional instability as the system enters acceleration. During this period, the curve exhibits a steep linear trend. At 425.7 min, an update in the linear trend occurs, after which the curve gradually decreases and fluctuations diminish, indicating continuously accelerating tilt rates and a progressively shortening time to failure. After 500 min, rapid convergence toward zero signals imminent collapse. Overall, this concave downward trend aligns with the theoretical expectation of the reciprocal tilt rate method, in which the reciprocal tilt rate approaches zero linearly in the final approach to failure. Moreover, using the reciprocal tilt rate method, the predicted collapse time can be calculated. Linear extrapolation of the final trend yields predicted failure time 550.28 min versus observed time 555.7 min, with an error of only 0.9%, confirming the validity of the model. Earlier linear trend predictions underestimated the failure time, indicating that real-time monitoring should continuously update linear fits when transitions in the reciprocal tilt rate linear trend occur, refining the data is necessary to obtain more accurate predictions.

Time-dependent characteristic analysis—reciprocal tilt rate analysis

The SPH numerical results exhibit a high degree of consistency with the experimental monitored parameters (Fig. 11). Both datasets exhibited two-stage patterns, with the transition from stable deformation to accelerated failure occurring at approximately 60% of the total pre-collapse duration in the experiment (at 335 min) and 87% in the simulation (at 0.87 s). Although the onset of acceleration occurred slightly earlier in the experiment, the two results show strong temporal consistency. Moreover, the corresponding cumulative tilt at the onset of acceleration—0.0034° in the experiment and 0.0043° in the simulation—demonstrates close quantitative agreement, confirming that the SPH simulation effectively reproduces the critical deformation threshold observed in experiment.

Both numerical and experimental curves exhibited slow growth at low deformation levels, followed by a sharp rise once the cumulative inclination reaches the threshold, with inflection points at θ ≈ 0.0043° in the simulation and θ ≈ 0.0034° in the experiment, marking stable-to-unstable transitions. Both simulation and experimental results exhibit a monotonic decrease that approaches zero just before failure, in agreement with the theoretical expectations of the rate-reciprocal method. The SPH model predictions align closely with the experimental observations, with only negligible deviations, thereby confirming the universality and reliability of this method in both numerical and experimental tests.

Overall, despite temporal scaling differences, the SPH simulation reproduces all critical precursors, including the two-stage deformation patterns, the exponential tilt rate acceleration, and the linear reciprocal rate decay. However, this distinction does not compromise the method’s applicability. The linear regression algorithm inherent to the reciprocal rate analysis acts as a universal filter, effectively smoothing out both deterministic numerical oscillations and stochastic physical fluctuations to extract the underlying failure trend. Consequently, the SPH model provides numerical support for rock mass instability prediction and serves as a valuable complement and extension to physical experiments in engineering applications .

Parametric Analysis To assess reciprocal tilt rate method applicability across varying fracture geometries, SPH simulations examining the fracture location effects and fracture orientation effects were conducted. These analyses quantify prediction sensitivity to structural configurations commonly encountered in field conditions.

Parametric analysis

Fracture location effects

To further investigate the influence of crack location on the collapse characteristics of tension-induced hazardous rock masses, this study preset a single crack at different positions within the block. The rock mass geometry and scale were kept constant, while only its horizontal distribution was varied and parametrically incorporated into the SPH model. Five fracture positions spanning the rock mass width (x = 0.385, 0.420, 0.455, 0.490, 0.525 m from left boundary) were tested while maintaining constant geometry with depth 21 cm and vertical orientation.

Figures 12 and 13 illustrate the influence of prefabricated crack position on the failure mode and precursor evolution of the hazardous rock mass. The results indicate that fracture location significantly affects both the timing and rate of acceleration as well as the overall instability mechanism. Under identical weakening conditions, cracks closer to the free surface (e.g., x = 0.420 m and x = 0.490 m) trigger earlier acceleration and rapid displacement growth, facilitating premature failure. In contrast, cracks located farther from the free boundary (e.g., x = 0.525 m) delay the onset of acceleration, resulting in a gentler deformation trend and later collapse. When the crack is centrally positioned (x = 0.455 m), shear-dominated failure occurs, and the displacement–tilt curve transitions into the accelerated stage earliest, exhibiting the steepest slope. Correspondingly, the reciprocal tilt rate curve displays a sharp concave-downward decay with a short linear segment, narrowing the early-warning window. By comparison, tensile-dominated failures associated with non-central cracks produce smoother decay curves, aligning well with the theoretical assumptions of the reciprocal tilt rate method. Although certain configurations (e.g., x = 0.525 m) exhibit oscillatory or non-monotonic decay patterns, successive trend fitting still yields accurate predictions. These findings confirm that crack location governs not only the failure mode and timing but also the reliability of precursor-based instability prediction, with the reciprocal tilt rate method remaining robust across varying crack geometries.

Instability process of hazardous rock mass with different fracture locations.

Effect of crack defect location on reciprocal tilt rate evolution and collapse behavior.

In summary, crack location not only governs the ultimate failure mode of the hazardous rock mass38, but also directly influences the predictability of precursor curves. Cracks located near the central part of the block shorten the prediction window, whereas cracks positioned farther from the center may weaken precursory signals or increase fluctuation intensity. This behavior is consistent with observations from physical model tests, where tilt rate reciprocal curves display similar trends. The SPH numerical simulation successfully reproduces this entire evolutionary process.

Crack angle

To investigate the influence of prefabricated crack inclination on the collapse characteristics of tensile-type hazardous rock masses, four representative fracture orientations of − 20°, − 10°, 0°, and 10° from vertical at fixed central location (x = 0.455 m) isolated angular effects on failure evolution (Fig. 14). The results indicate that initial crack inclination angle significantly governs the crack propagation trajectories, velocities, and failure modes. Analysis of reciprocal tilt rate curves reveals distinct patterns: at − 20°, the rock mass exhibits extended energy accumulation with delayed tensile crack development and postponed collapse; at larger angles (− 10° and 10°), rapid early-stage fluctuations indicate accelerated crack propagation and premature failure; near 0°, retarded propagation reflects enhanced pre-instability energy storage. In all simulated cases, the reciprocal tilt rate exhibit consistent evolution with strong pre-collapse linearity, validating the proposed predictive equation. Deformation characteristics vary markedly with inclination angle. At − 20°, limited cumulative tilt deformation and subtle acceleration complicate monitoring and early warning. At 10°, the compressed accelerated deformation window necessitates higher monitoring frequencies to capture precursory signals.

Different initial crack angles on reciprocal tilt rate evolution and collapse behavior.

These findings are consistent with previous experimental and numerical studies, which have demonstrated that crack inclination significantly alters the proportion of tensile–shear failures and the evolution of fracture paths39, thereby influencing the timing of rock mass collapse. In conclusion, crack inclination critically determines failure mechanisms, deformation patterns, and early warning characteristics in tension-dominated rock masses. However, at minimal angles where shear failure dominates, applicability decreases, requiring complementary monitoring strategies for reliable early warning. Within optimal ranges where tensile failure predominates, the reciprocal tilt rate method demonstrates predictive accuracy.

Case study

Field monitoring

Field monitoring was conducted on a typical hazardous rock mass in Hubei Province. The host slope exhibits steep gradients (65°–80°), comprising weathered metamorphic rock with poor mechanical properties and low weathering resistance. The rock mass exhibits well-developed fractures and joints, with approximate dimensions of 3.1 × 3.3 × 3.4 m. A rear tension crack is present, accompanied locally by weak zones and compression evidence. Figure 15a illustrates the rock mass and monitoring system configuration. MEMS inclinometer were installed on the rock surface to acquire tilt data. The monitoring system integrates data acquisition, transmission, and analysis, utilizing low-power long-distance communication and centralized cloud-based processing. Integrated analysis of multi-source data provides a basis for rock mass stability assessment and risk early warning. The system commenced operation on May 3, 2020, and successfully captured the collapse event at 02:26 on July 19, 2020, completely recording pre- and post-failure tilt evolution.

Field photograph of the hazardous rock mass.

Based on the field investigation and monitoring data, a numerical model was constructed using SPH to analyze the failure evolution characteristics of the rock mass. The model geometry was simplified according to the measured contours, with boundary conditions reflecting actual topography. The rock mass was discretized into particles as shown in Fig. 15b. Material parameters were referenced from laboratory tests and existing literature, taking into account the low strength, high permeability, and poor durability of weathered metamorphic rock. Joint planes and tension cracks were implemented to simulate their controlling effects on stability. This model enables quantitative analysis of crack propagation, displacement responses, and progressive instability under gravitational loading, providing validation against the field observations.

Analysis of simulation results and field monitoring data

The numerical simulation captures the progressive evolution of the hazardous rock mass, from the initial fracture propagation to ultimate collapse (Fig. 16). In the early stage, rear fractures propagate gradually penetrating deeper into the rock mass and diminishing the constraint between the detached block and the underlying bedrock. As fractures develop, the rock mass exhibits pronounced tilting and rotation, accompanied by opening at the frontal edge, reflecting a typical tensile–shear failure mechanism. Upon complete penetration of the critical fracture plane, the rock mass loses stability and collapses rapidly. This evolutionary process reveals two fundamental characteristics: (1) the failure progression exhibits distinct phase transitions—from gradual fracture growth through progressive tilting to critical instability; (2) deformation accumulation accelerates nonlinearly, culminating in catastrophic failure. These observations are consistent with the prolonged nucleation preceding instantaneous failure behavior recorded in the field, confirming the dual progressive-catastrophic nature of hazardous rock mass instability.

Numerical simulation of the progressive collapse sequence of the Hazardous Rock Mass.

Both field monitoring data and numerical simulation results (Fig. 17) exhibit a characteristic two-stage evolution of cumulative tilt during the pre-collapse phase: a quasi-static deformation stage characterized by minimal deformation rates, followed by a rapid accelerated failure stage preceding overall instability. Field measurements indicate cumulative tilt increased only from 0.0° to 0.6° over approximately 60 days. During the subsequent quasi-stable stage (approximately 31 days), the tilt rapidly increased from 0.6° to 1.95°, accompanied by a sharp rise in tilt rate. Although the SPH simulation compresses the timescale significantly (total duration 0.6 s), it accurately captures the same tilt evolution pattern observed in experiments, with a gradual increase to about 0.5° within 0–0.3 s, followed by a sharp rise to 1.95° within 0.3–0.6 s. Both datasets indicate that upon reaching approximately 25% of the ultimate cumulative tilt, the system transitions into the accelerated evolution. Notably, the maximum cumulative tilt in both configurations remain within 1.5°–2.0°. These findings demonstrate that the simulation results not only reproduces the phase characteristics of the physical processes but also captures critical transition points, thereby validating the capability of the model to simulate the transition from prolonged incubation to instantaneous failure.

Test monitoring curve of cumulative tilt angle versus time.

As shown in Fig. 18, both experimental and numerical results reveal that, the tilt rate exhibits a pronounced nonlinear amplification with increasing cumulative tilt, approximating exponential growth. During the early stages of deformation, the fracture propagation proceeds quasi-steadily with gradual curve ascent. Upon fracture coalescence forming a critical surface, the deformation rate escalates sharply, marking the transition from stable evolution to rapid instability. This stability-to-acceleration transition is consistently reflected in both field and simulation data, confirming its reliability as a precursor indicator for the accelerated stage.

Cumulative tilt angle and tilt rate.

Experimental and numerical results of reciprocal tilt rate evolution (Fig. 19) exhibit gradual temporal decay approaching zero prior to instability, conforming to the reciprocal tilt rate criterion and providing a unified framework for failure prediction. In field measurements, the reciprocal value decreased from about 400 h·°⁻¹ to around 10 h·°⁻¹ during approximately 60 h preceding collapse, forming a stable quasi -linear descending segment, with the extrapolated result closely approximating actual failure timing. Despite the substantially compressed simulation timescale, a comparable linear segment was observed within 0.20–0.30 s, where the reciprocal value dropped rapidly from 50 s·°⁻¹ to near-zero with extrapolated failure time exhibiting minimal deviation from the simulated collapse point. A consistent pattern in both cases is that once the system enters the acceleration stage, the reciprocal curve forms a “prediction window” suitable for linear fitting, with the window length determining the effective lead time and the slope and intercept governing the prediction accuracy. The high consistency between experimental and simulated results validates the universality of this method across different scales and conditions, establishing the reciprocal tilt rate method as a robust criterion for short-term early warning of tension-dominated dangerous rock masses.

Field monitoring validation showing reciprocal tilt rate evolution with linear prediction window.

Overall, the simulation reproduces the macroscopic failure patterns, deformation evolution trajectories, and critical predictive indicators observed in the field. This confirms the SPH model’s efficacy in capturing the progressive failure processes of hazardous rock masses and demonstrates the practical reliability of the rate-reciprocal method for early-warning and collapse prediction applications.

Conclusions

This study addresses the challenge of early warning for tension-type hazardous rock masses, which are widely distributed in mountainous regions of China. Such rock masses exhibit a characteristic two-stage failure pattern: decades-long subcritical crack propagation coexisting with accelerated instability occurring over hours to days. This spatiotemporal behavior renders conventional monitoring methods based on displacement or environmental factors insufficient to capture the short-lived precursors of brittle failure. By integrating physical model experiments, SPH numerical simulations, and field monitoring, we established a unified framework for failure prediction spanning experimental, numerical, and in-situ observations. Innovatively, the reciprocal tilt-rate is employed as a quantitative early-warning indicator, showing a linear decay to zero prior to collapse and enabling accurate prediction of failure time (error < 0.9%). The reliability of this method is verified through both physical experiments and SPH simulations, which further reveal that crack geometry (position and inclination) significantly influences the duration of the warning window, with central horizontal cracks being most favorable for early detection. These consistent results confirm that, despite temporal scaling differences, the numerical model successfully reproduces the critical tilt conditions and failure mechanisms observed in experiments. The proposed physical–numerical coupling approach overcomes the limitations of single-method monitoring, providing a low-cost, highly reliable early-warning tool for tension-type hazardous rock masses and a portable, verifiable framework for landslide and rockfall disaster prediction in mountainous regions.
