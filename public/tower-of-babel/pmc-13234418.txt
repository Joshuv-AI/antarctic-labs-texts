The role of outpatients’ medical history on current herbal medicines use: a cross-sectional multicenter study
Authors: Chinonyerem O Iheanacho, Agneta Ehianeta Elimian, Joy Chinaza Etebefia, Obiamaka Vivian Emma-Ogbuefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerbal medicine use is widespread globally, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. This study aimed to assess the independent predictors of herbal medicine use among outpatients, focusing on medical history. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 1250 outpatients from three tertiary hospitals in Nigeria, selected for geographical diversity. Data were collected using a pretested, interviewer-administered questionnaire. Analyses included descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of herbal medicine use and related outcomes, using SPSS at 95% confidence interval (p < 0.05). Current use of herbal medicines was reported in 48.4%. Having a chronic disease significantly increased the odds of current use (AOR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.31–2.26, p < 0.001) while illness duration > 5 years had 95% higher odds of current use (AOR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.22–3.11, p = 0.005). However, taking prescribed medication reduced the odds of current use (AOR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.77, p < 0.001), but more than doubled the odds of combining herbal and orthodox therapies (AOR = 2.35, 95% CI 1.78–3.10, p < 0.001). This combination behavior was linked to an 82% increased risk of reported side effects (AOR = 1.82, 95% CI 1.38–2.40, p < 0.001). Specific conditions like gastrointestinal disorders (AOR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.08–2.14, p = 0.017) and hypertension (AOR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.06–1.88, p = 0.019 for combination use) were also independent predictors of herbal use. Herbal medicine use was significantly driven by the presence and duration of chronic illness. The high rate of concurrent use with conventional drugs and the associated increased risk of side effects highlight a critical public health concern regarding unmonitored herb-drug interactions. These highlight the urgent need for healthcare providers to routinely inquire about outpatients’ herbal medicine use, and provides evidence for policies that promote the integration and safe regulation of herbal practices within the national health system.Keywords: Nigeria, Herbal and complementary medicines, Medical history, Outpatients, Chronic diseases, Illness durationSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Herbal medicine use is widespread globally, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. A significant concern is the concomitant use of herbal and conventional medicines which poses adverse health risks potentially resulting in adverse effects such as organ toxicity and reduced drug efficacy. Ahmed et al. reported that 34.1% of adult outpatients used herbal and orthodox medicines concurrently1, yet over 60% and 82.4% did not disclose herbal use to healthcare providers1,2, primarily because doctors did not inquire or patients believed it was unnecessary to disclose. Despite 58.8% being aware of potential harmful interactions, the concurrent use without proper clinical communication was observed in the study, posing risks for adverse herb–drug interactions and complicated clinical management1. Therefore, understanding the influencing factors and determinants of herbal medicine use in patients is critical to informing clinical guidelines, public health strategies, ensuring patient safety, and integrating evidence-based traditional practices into the modern healthcare delivery systems.

Despite advances in modern healthcare, herbal medicine use in Nigeria remains pervasive, with up to 70% of the population relying on traditional remedies for the prevention and treatment of various ailments3. Among patients with chronic conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, and HIV/AIDS, almost 40% report using herbal products alongside prescribed medications4. In paediatric populations, caregivers often administer unstandardized herbal concoctions without clinical oversight, further enlarging the scope of exposure across all age groups5. The public health implications of uninformed, concomitant herb drug use are profound. Herb–drug interactions can alter drug metabolism, either inhibiting therapeutic action or precipitating toxicity, and have been linked to adverse outcomes such as treatment failure, organ damage, and hospital admissions6. For example, St. John’s wort has been shown to reduce plasma levels of antiretrovirals, risking viral rebound in HIV patients6. Compounding these risks is patients’ nondisclosure of concomitant use, a gap that undermines patient safety, hampers accurate diagnosis, and delays appropriate interventions.

The co-use of herbal and orthodox medicines is particularly concerning in tertiary facilities which treat patients with complex health illnesses such as hypertension, diabetes, and cancer, often requiring strict pharmacological management. Therefore, this study is expected to highlight illness-related factors that influence herbal medicine use, to provide evidence for safe and effective patient care. The study included three tertiary hospitals in three regions of Nigeria, for geographical diversity, capturing larger cultural, social and demographic makeup, unlike previous studies that focused on single facilities or regions7–9. Additionally, this study is timely and relevant in the context of Nigeria’s evolving health policy landscape. The Federal Ministry of Health has endorsed the integration of traditional medicine into the national healthcare delivery system, yet data on usage patterns and patient preferences in relation to medical history remain sparse10. Findings from this study can inform evidence-based policy formulation, guide public health interventions, and contribute to safer, more integrated healthcare practices in tertiary hospitals across the country. Importantly, appropriate and targeted drug information is especially relevant in improving medication use, and this can be effectively achieved through a good understanding of the scope of herbal medicines use in outpatients. This study will provide evidence of the scope of concurrent herbal medicine use with modern medicine on a larger scale. Findings from the study are hence, expected to enhance medication information strategies and the overall patient care approach. This study aimed to assess the independent predictors of herbal medicine use among outpatients, focusing on medical history.

Methods

Study design

A hospital-based cross-sectional study design was adopted for data collection between July 30 and November 16, 2025. The study utilized a quantitative, interviewer-administered survey. This design was chosen because it allows for the collection of data at a single point in time from a large and diverse population, thereby providing a snapshot of the magnitude of herbal medicine use in the selected settings. The design was appropriate for identifying relationships between medical history and herbal medicine use without manipulating any variables.

Study area

The study was conducted in the Adult and Children Outpatient Clinics of three tertiary hospitals located in Abuja, Gombe, and Anambra States, Nigeria. These facilities included the University of Abuja Teaching Hospital (UATH), the Federal Teaching Hospital Gombe (FTH Gombe), and the Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu University Teaching Hospital (COOUTH). Each hospital serves as a major referral center within its respective geopolitical region and provides both primary and specialized healthcare services to large urban and peri-urban populations. The selection of these sites was based on their high outpatient attendance rates, diverse patient demographics, and their regional representation of the North-East, North-Central, and South-East zones of Nigeria, thereby ensuring geographical diversity and generalizability of findings.

Study population and sample size determination

The study population comprised patients of all age groups who attended the outpatient clinics of the included hospitals during the data collection period.

The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula for descriptive studies11, where, n represents the required sample size, Z is the z-score corresponding to the desired confidence level, P is the estimated proportion of the population using herbal medicine, and e is the margin of error. Using a 50% prevalence (to allow for maximum variability), a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, a minimum of 384 respondents was calculated for each study site. To accommodate potential non-response and incomplete data, a total of approximately 1,250 participants were recruited across the three hospitals.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were clinically stable and able to communicate effectively in English or in the predominant local languages (Igbo or Hausa). The study included adults aged 18 years and above, as well as children aged 0–17 years whose parents or guardians provided informed consent and responded on their behalf. Only outpatients who were receiving care or consultation at the clinics (and not admitted) were included.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they were mentally impaired, unable to provide informed consent, clinically unstable (e.g., in severe pain, distress, or requiring emergency care), or if their condition otherwise made participation inappropriate or unsafe.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed on Google Forms and adapted from previous studies on herbal medicine use in Nigeria2,4. The use of structured interviewer-administered questionnaires enhanced data accuracy and completeness, particularly among participants with varying literacy levels. The instrument was designed in English Language to collect quantitative data relevant to the study’s objectives and was divided into three main sections comprising sociodemographic characteristics, medical history and herbal medicine usage patterns. The use of Google Forms enabled real-time electronic data entry, validation checks, and automatic synchronization, thereby enhancing data quality, minimizing transcription errors, and ensuring secure cloud-based storage.

Description of questionnaire sections

Section A

Demographic information gathered background characteristics of respondents, including patient category, age, sex, marital status and state of origin.

Section B

This section on patients’ medical history and current health status sought to collect data on respondents’ existing medical conditions, duration of illness, presence of chronic diseases, and use of prescribed medications. This section was intended to assess the relationship between patients’ health status and their inclination toward herbal remedies, as well as potential overlaps or interactions with conventional treatments.

Section C

This section on herbal medicine use focused on capturing current experiences with herbal medicines. It included questions on frequency, duration, types of herbal preparations used, combinations with conventional drugs and side effects. A Yes/No response scale was used to assess the presence of side effects, and an open ended question was used to obtain the exact effect patients experienced. The aim was to determine the prevalence, usage patterns and outcomes of herbal medicine among the target population.

Pretesting, validity, and reliability of the study tool

The questionnaire was pretested among 10% of the estimated sample (approximately 115 participants) in a health facility not selected for the main study but with comparable characteristics. The pretesting helped identify ambiguous questions, assess the logical flow, and estimate the time required for completion. Face and content validity were established through expert review by a five-member panel comprising professionals in Public Health, Clinical Pharmacy, and Medical Research. The experts evaluated the tool for clarity, cultural appropriateness, and coverage of study objectives. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of the pilot data to verify item clustering under intended constructs. Reliability analysis was conducted using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.82, indicating acceptable internal consistency12. Test–retest reliability was performed on a subsample after two weeks, resulting in an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.79, confirming stability over time13.

Data collection procedure

Data collection took place between July 30 and November 12, 2025, across all study sites. Trained research assistants were stationed at the outpatient clinics daily from Monday to Friday during official clinic hours. Eligible participants were identified after their clinical consultations, and the study’s purpose was explained to them. Interviews were conducted in private sections of the waiting areas to ensure confidentiality and minimize response bias.

Each interview lasted approximately 5–8 min. Data were collected using electronic tablets preloaded with the questionnaire to facilitate real-time data entry and minimize transcription errors. Supervisors conducted weekly reviews of the collected data to check for completeness and consistency, providing corrective feedback or refresher guidance when needed. This continuous quality control ensured uniformity of data collection procedures across sites and enhanced reliability.

Outcome measures

The study endpoints were the current herbal medicine use, and the independent predictors of herbal medicine use among outpatients, focusing on medical history.

Data analysis methods

Data from the electronic questionnaire were extracted from Google Forms, cleaned, and coded in Microsoft Excel 2019 before being exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, patterns of herbal medicine use, types of herbs used, Multivariable binary logistic regression models were performed to identify independent predictors of herbal medicine use. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF > 10 treated as evidence of problematic collinearity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committees of the Federal Teaching Hospital Gombe (reference: FTHG/HREC/S.13/415), the University of Abuja Teaching Hospital (reference: FCT/UATH/HREC/20,185), and the Anambra State Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committee (reference: MP/PRS/1244/Vol.205). Administrative permission was also secured from the management of each facility prior to data collection. Participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits before consenting. Participation was voluntary, and respondents retained the right to withdraw at any stage without repercussions to their care. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing all participant identifiers and securely storing data in password-protected electronic files accessible only to the research team. The study adhered strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the National Health Research Ethics Code of Nigeria, ensuring respect, beneficence, and justice in the conduct of human subject research. Written informed consent was obtained before participation.

Medical history and current health status

A wide range of medical conditions was reported, with malaria (25.0%) and hypertension (14.7%) being the most common. Notably, a substantial proportion of respondents (28.5%) reported other health problems such as HIV/AIDS, cancer, ulcer-related conditions, and other chronic or infectious diseases. One-third of respondents (33.0%) had been ill for less than one month, while 26.2% reported illness lasting between one and six months. Approximately 16.9% had experienced symptoms for 6–12 months, 16.2% for 1–5 years, and 7.7% for more than five years, indicating that both acute and chronic conditions were represented. About one-third of respondents (34.2%) reported having a chronic medical condition. Among those with chronic illnesses (n = 427), hypertension was the most prevalent chronic condition (33.3%), followed by diabetes (22.9%), asthma (17.3%), and HIV/AIDS (13.1%). Other chronic conditions, including ulcer disease, arthritis, and sickle cell disease, accounted for 13.4%, underscoring the presence of multiple non-communicable and long-term health problems in the study group. Most respondents (71.7%) were currently taking prescribed medications at the time of the survey. Among those on treatment, the most frequently reported medications were pain relievers (24.3%), antibiotics (18.5%), antihypertensives (16.6%), and antidiabetic drugs (7.8%). Additionally, 6.1% reported the use of antiretrovirals or chemotherapy agents, and 26.7% reported taking other medications not explicitly listed. (Table 2).

The most commonly used were herbal teas and infusions (31.4%), herbal powders (29.6%), mixed herbal concoctions (29.0%), roots and barks (28.0%), and leaves (27.4%). Concomitant use of herbal and conventional medicines was prevalent, 51.1% of respondents reported combining the two, and 48.7% indicated simultaneous use. Nearly half (48.6%), reported experiencing side effects from herbal medicines. The most common purposes for herbal medicine use included treatment of acute illnesses (15.0%), management of chronic conditions (14.1%), illness prevention (13.5%), boosting immunity (13.4%), general wellness (13.4%), and detoxification (13.2%), with 13.0% identifying other reasons. Sources of herbal medicines were similarly diverse. Family and friends (11.4%) and traditional healers (11.3%) were the primary sources, followed by market vendors (10.4%), pharmacies (10.3%), and personal preparation (10.2%). Self-prescription of herbal medicines was most common (21.4%), followed closely by recommendations from family/friends (20.9%) and traditional healers (20.3%), with healthcare workers accounting for 19.3% of recommendations. Table 4 presents predictors of current herbal medicine use. The presence of a chronic condition substantially increased the odds of current use by 72% (aOR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.31–2.26, p < 0.001). The duration of illness remained a strong factor, with significantly higher odds for those ill for 1–5 years (aOR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.15–2.36, p = 0.006) and over 5 years (aOR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.22–3.11, p = 0.005). Taking prescribed medication was a strong negative predictor, associated with a 41% reduction in the odds of current use (aOR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.77, p < 0.001). Furthermore, specific medical conditions independently predicted use; having gastrointestinal disorders increased the odds by 52% (aOR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.08–2.14, p = 0.017), and having musculoskeletal problems increased the odds by 63% (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.02–2.61, p = 0.042).

Discussion

Current herbal medicine use was reported in almost half of the participants in several patterns. The presence of a chronic condition substantially increased the odds of current use, while illness duration remained a strong factor, with significantly higher odds for those ill for over 5 years. Taking prescribed medication was a strong negative predictor, associated with reduction in the odds of current use. Furthermore, specific medical conditions such as gastrointestinal disorders and musculoskeletal problems independently increased the odds of herbal medicines use. The strongest predictor of combined use of herbal and comventional medicines was taking prescribed medication, which more than doubled the odds of combination use. Also, having a chronic illness significantly increased the odds of combined use of herbal and conventional medicines as did longer illness durations of over 5 years. Among specific illnesses, hypertension was a significant independent predictor, increasing the odds of combined use of herbal and conventional medicines. Taking prescribed medication was associated with a significantly increased odds of reporting side effects with herbal medicines. The presence of a chronic illness and longer illness duration of 1 -5 yeas and over 5 years were also significant risk factors for reporting side effects with herbal medicines. Specifically, patients with hypertension and gastrointestinal disorders reported significantly more side effects with herbal medicines. The presence of a chronic illness dramatically increased the odds of long-term herbal medicines use. Patients with over 5 years duration of illness had increased odds of long-term use of herbal medicines. In a reversal from the combination use model, taking prescribed medication was associated with a reduction in the odds of long-term use. Hypertension was, again, the only specific condition significantly linked to long-term use.

Almost half of the study population were currently and frequently using herbal products at the time of the survey, demonstrating that herbal medicine use was both common and sustained within the outpatient population. The observed duration of use also suggests that many respondents were long-term or habitual users of herbal remedies. This findings suggest that herbal medicines were not limited to episodic illness-related use but is integrated into the routine health practices of many respondents corroborating previous studies in Nigeria14,15. The diverse forms of the reported herbal medicines reflect the rich cultural and traditional diversity of herbal practices among Nigerians, as well as the widespread accessibility of various forms of herbal preparations. The presence of side effects alongside high levels of combined or simultaneous use highlights a critical clinical and public health concern regarding the safe integration of herbal and conventional therapies and corroborates previous findings9,16. Respondents reported multiple motivations for using herbal medicines, suggesting that herbal medicines were used for both curative and preventive purposes, which reflects deeply held cultural beliefs about the holistic benefits of traditional remedies. The increasing benefits of herbal medicines are previously reported17, and the World Health Organisation associates them with relevant potentials for chronic disease prevention and management, increasing its relevance in the major challenge facing the conventional health care system18.

Our findings showed that persons with chronic illnesses, longer illness durations, and specific disease profiles (particularly hypertension and gastrointestinal disorders) were more likely to use herbal medicines. This findings corroborate previous findings in Nigeria, Ethiopia and the United States12,17,19,20,21, suggesting that medical need is a primary driver of herbal medicines use. In Nigeria, a high prevalence of herbal use has been documented among patients with chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension12. A systematic review and meta-analysis in Ethiopia reported that chronic disease, duration of illness greater than five years and complications were significantly associated with herbal medicines use20,21, echoing the increased odds of use observed in our study. Internationally, a study in Turkey report higher herbal and CAM use among chronic disease patients compared to general populations21, indicating that chronic disease status and longer disease duration are global predictors of herbal use. A previous study also suggest that longer disease duration fosters experimentation with non‑conventional therapies, as patients search for additional relief beyond standard treatments16. This practice increases the risk of drug-herb interactions, drug adverse events and pharmacokinetic therapeutic failure, critically underscoring the relevance of routine enquiries of herbal medicines use from these category of patients by their healthcare providers.

A critical finding was the high rate of combined use of herbal and conventional medicines which raises concerns about potential herb–drug interactions and patient safety. This is mirrored by both African and global evidence, for instance, studies in Nigeria and Ethiopia highlight that patients receiving conventional treatment often adopt additional herbal therapies, either to enhance outcomes or address persistent symptoms12,22. Our finding also echoes earlier works in Nigeria and sub-Sahara Africa8,9,23, where concurrent use is especially common among persons managing chronic illnesses such as hypertension and diabetes. Similarly, a Nigerian‑based survey among hypertensive patients identified substantial co‑use of herbal and conventional medicines17. Internationally, similar co-use patterns have been reported in China24, and even among chronic disease patients in North America and Europe25. As in our study, evidence from these regions highlight concerns about associated risks of herb–drug interactions, risks of therapeutic failures and toxicities that may result in worst outcomes, reflecting the need for deliberate and comprehensive medication use enquiries by healthcare providers. This finding also brings relevance to the recent brief by the World Health Organization calling for the integration of traditional medicines into primary care services18. Although not currently implemented in Nigeria, appropriate integration would create treatment plans that transcend conventional boundaries, while providing closely monitored and safe use of herbal and conventional medicines.

The use of prescribed medicines, having chronic diseases, particularly hypertension, increased the likelihood of combining herbal medicines and conventional medicines. Using prescribed medications reduced exclusive herbal use but was the strongest predictor of combining herbal and conventional medicines in our study, reflecting complex health‑seeking behaviors with critical safety concerns. Research from other African settings also show high rates of concomitant herbal use in patients on conventional treatments, with many failing to inform healthcare providers21. Concurrent use raises concerns about herb–drug interactions and adverse event risks due to unregulated herbal products and polytherapy23. International surveys further support that chronic illnesses such as hypertension are significant drivers of herbal medicine engagement among adults worldwide26. This findings suggest that many patients do not replace conventional (prescribed) medicines but supplement it. Similarly, global CAM use research reports strong associations between prescription drug use and concurrent herbal or supplement use, particularly among middle‑aged and older adults managing chronic illnesses23, affirming this as a common health‑seeking behavior across contexts. Other global reports likewise indicate that individuals using prescribed medication often supplement with herbal therapies, leading to significant overlap between conventional and alternative use23, underscoring the need for routine inquiry by healthcare professionals during patient hospital visits. The findings also suggest the need for intentional probing into patients’ current herbal medicine use in patients with chronic illnesses to ensure appropriate care. Further, the implementation of community-based interventions, such as routine community engagements involving the locals and their leaders, is a critical approach toward appropriate prevention of unsafe concurrent use of herbal and conventional medicines.

The concurrent use of herbal medicine and conventional medicines was linked to an extensive increased risk of reported side effects, especially among patients with hypertension and gastrointestinal disorders, which were independent predictors. Similarly, a previous study observed that Nigerian hypertensive cohorts report clinically evident adverse effects in co‑users of herbal and conventional medicines7. Gastrointestinal symptoms is also noted to frequently motivate herbal use across various populations, reflecting broader global patterns of symptom‑specific herbal adoption16. In other Nigerian and Malawian populations (e.g., HIV patients), adverse reactions related to herbal use have also been documented, although at varying frequencies27. Globally, studies highlight that herb–drug interactions can lead to significant adverse effects, particularly with herbs that have multiple active constituents28, reinforcing our findings on observed risks of side effects. In Nigeria, assessments of potential herb–drug interactions among diabetic patients identified significant risks due to unregulated concomitant use and a lack of patient awareness9. Similar patterns of increased adverse reaction reporting with combined therapy are identified in other African research, where patients on anti‑retroviral or anti‑tuberculosis treatments also report side effects when using herbal medicines concurrently22. Internationally, herb–drug interactions involving antihypertensives and anticoagulants illustrate how co‑use of herbal products can exacerbate adverse outcomes23. The substantial proportion of participants reporting side effects further reinforces the importance of routine clinical inquiry into herbal use. The association between prescribed medication use and increased odds of experiencing side effects (via concurrent herbal use) aligns with pharmacovigilance concerns, and underscores the critical relevance of deliberate actions at facility and national policy levels.

Also, it was identified that chronic illnesses (particularly > 5 years) elevated the risk of side effects from concurrent use of herbal and conventional medicines, highlighting vulnerability in long-standing illness populations. African systematic reviews identify complications and longer disease courses as key determinants of increased herbal use and related safety concerns20, suggesting that cumulative exposure increases interaction risk. In Nigeria and Malawi, poorly regulated herbal product quality and frequent non‑disclosure potentially contribute to adverse outcomes in chronic disease populations29. Similarly, global CAM research reports that chronic disease patients are more likely to experience adverse effects from multiple therapies due to complex pharmacological interactions16,28. In Nigeria, hypertensive patients show high rates of herbal use and notable co‑use with prescribed antihypertensives, carrying documented side effects and potential interactions7, highlighting the critical need to integrate routine targeted education and increased pharmacovigilance.

The observed association of chronic disease, longer illness duration of 1–5 years, and hypertension with concurrent use of herbal and conventional medicines fits well with evidence from both African studies and research in other regions. In Nigeria and Ethiopia, chronic conditions are repeatedly linked with higher odds of using both conventional and herbal medicines15,20. Internationally, surveys show hypertension often predicts greater CAM and herbal engagement among patients globally, with substantial proportions using herbal remedies alongside prescribed antihypertensives21,26. These cross-regional patterns underscore that multiple therapies are an entrenched component of chronic disease management for many patients, regardless of the setting. This observation is expected to improve clinical guidance and patient outcomes. More so, findings from this study further highlight evidence supporting regular medication counseling of patients treated for chronic illnesses. This is especially relevant in the light of increasing prevalence of chronic illnesses in low and middle income countries such as Nigeria.

Although the study presents findings that will potentially improve healthcare, it is associated with some limitations. Among which is the reliance on self-reported data which may have introduced recall bias or underreporting, particularly regarding sensitive behaviours such as concurrent use of herbal and pharmaceutical products. The risk of self-reporting bias associated with the Hawthorne effect, is also a potential limitation of this study. The cross-sectional design also limits the ability to draw causal inferences, and because the study was conducted in tertiary outpatient clinics, the findings may not fully represent patterns in primary care settings, rural communities, or other geographical regions. Meanwhile, the adjustments for confounders strengthens the study findings.

Conclusion

Herbal medicine use was significantly driven by the presence and duration of chronic illness. The high rate of concurrent use with conventional drugs and the associated increased risk of side effects highlight a critical public health concern regarding unmonitored herb-drug interactions. These highlight the urgent need for healthcare providers’ routine inquiry into outpatients’ herbal medicine use, while providing evidence for policies that promote the integration and safe regulation of herbal medicine practices within the national health system. Findings will also aid in the strategic integration of herbal medicines in conventional healthcare facilities in Nigeria and other similar settings.
