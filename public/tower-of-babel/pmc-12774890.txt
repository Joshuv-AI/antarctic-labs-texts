Association between metabolic syndrome and chronic kidney disease in the Brazilian population, a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Yasmim dos Anjos Ribeiro, Ana Carolina Barbosa Franco, Luciana Saraiva da Silva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study to evaluated the associations between the presence of metabolic syndrome (MetS), the number of MetS components, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) in a Brazilian adult population.This was a cross-sectional study using a database of laboratory tests from the National Health Survey, 2014—2015 (n = 8952). MetS was classified based on the presence of three or more of the following components: hyperglycemia, defined by glycosylated hemoglobin ≥ 5.7%; high blood pressure, defined by systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg; abdominal obesity, defined by waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women; low HDL, defined by HDL cholesterol < 50 mg/dl in women and < 40 mg/dl in men; and hypertriglyceridemia, defined by triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dl. CKD was defined by a glomerular filtration rate ≤ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables were evaluated. Analysis of the association between MetS, number of MetS components and CKD was based on odds ratio estimates using logistic regression.The prevalence of CKD was 5.3% and the prevalence of MetS was 47.13%. The components present in the largest number of individuals in the general population were abdominal obesity, low HDL cholesterol, and high blood pressure. Logistic regression analysis showed that the odds of CKD were 1.95 times higher in individuals with MetS than in those without. An accumulation of 3, 4 or 5 MetS components increased the odds of CKD by 2.96, 3.15 and 5.05 times, respectively. Individuals with MetS were 95% more likely to have CKD than those without MetS. Introduction

Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) is a global public health problem1, represented by a set of metabolic alterations capable of increasing morbidity and mortality from various chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), as well as all-cause mortality2–4. According to the definition adopted by the National Cholesterol Education Program’s Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III), recommended by the I Brazilian Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Metabolic Syndrome (I-DBSM)5, MetS is defined by the presence of three or more of the following components: waist circumference (WC) > 88 cm for women, and > 102 cm for men; arterial hypertension (AH), represented by a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg; hypertriglyceridemia with triglyceride (TG) levels ≥ 150 mg/dl; hyperglycemia defined by fasting glucose ≥ 110 mg/dl; and high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) levels < 40 mg/dl in men and < 50 mg/dl in women5.

Approximately 12.5 to 31.4% of the world’s population is estimated to be affected by MetS, which varies according to the diagnostic criteria adopted6. On the American continent, and specifically in developing countries such as Brazil, the prevalence of MetS is widespread6,7, reaching 33% of the country’s adult population, according to a recent meta-analysis8. These rates are likely to increase in the coming years, not only because of the growth and aging of the population but above all because of the increasing prevalence of AH, diabetes mellitus (DM), obesity, and dyslipidemia9–12.

In addition, MetS can lead to the development of several associated diseases, such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), which is also a serious public health problem13–17. Previous studies have suggested that MetS directly influences the onset18,19 and progression of CKD to the terminal stage20. CKD affects approximately 9.5% of the world’s population and is one of the leading causes of illness and mortality in this century21,22. Estimates indicate that CKD could become the fifth leading cause of premature death worldwide by 204023.

In this context, a constant concern is that, in addition to being prevalent, health risk factors, more specifically, the components of MetS, are highly interrelated24,25. Considering that the simultaneous occurrence of risk factors increases the chance of developing adverse health conditions, studies have focused on determining how these factors impact individuals. The evidence suggests that the simultaneity of different risk factors can have a synergistic effect, thus resulting in a multiplicative deleterious effect rather than an additive effect of each behavior24–26.

Therefore, given the importance of MetS and CKD in the current epidemiological scenario and considering the scarcity of studies with laboratory data from a representative sample of the Brazilian population, this study aimed to evaluate the associations among the presence of MetS, the number of MetS components and CKD in the Brazilian adult population.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study uses a database of laboratory tests from the National Health Survey (PNS), which were collected between 2014 and 201527. The PNS is a nationwide survey carried out on a probabilistic sample of households and is the result of a partnership between the Ministry of Health (MS), the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (FIOCRUZ) and the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE)28.

Sampling

The PNS is a household survey that uses cluster sampling in three stages: census tracts or groups of tracts are the primary sampling units, households are the second-stage units, and residents aged 18 and over are the third-stage units.

Initially, 64,348 households were selected, and 60,202 adult individuals were identified for the PNS. After a subsample of 25% of the census tracts was selected to make up the laboratory testing database, a nonresponse rate of 20% was accounted for, in addition to losses that occurred throughout the process, resulting in a total sample of 8952 individuals for the collection of samples for laboratory testing27.

Data collection

Participants 18 years of age or older selected in the third stage of sample selection were interviewed and answered questions about household characteristics, characteristics of individuals living in the household (including education, work status, income, access to services and health insurance), and individual characteristics (such as work, self-perceived health, history of accidents and violence, self-reported diagnosis of chronic diseases, oral health, history of medical care, access to prenatal care, health data, and women’s lifestyle)29.

Then, measurements such as body weight, height, WC, and blood pressure (BP) were taken from these selected participants. Subsequently, between 2014 and 2015, a subsample of 8952 individuals also had blood and urine samples collected to obtain laboratory data27.

Study variables

For this study, the following self-reported sociodemographic variables from the PNS database were analyzed: sex (male and female); age (years); education (incomplete primary school; completed primary school; completed high school; completed higher education); and race/color (white; black; brown; and others such as yellow and indigenous). The race/color categories used in the PNS followed the ethnic-racial identification criteria adopted by the IBGE, one of the institutions responsible for developing this household survey30.

The clinical variables used in this study included a diagnosis of AH, DM, and excess weight. To define the presence of established AH, self-reported use of antihypertensive medication or the presence of SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg data was considered, according to the criteria of the Brazilian Society of Cardiology31. The diagnosis of DM was defined by glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C) ≥ 6.5%, according to the criteria of the Brazilian Diabetes Society32, or by self-reported use of hypoglycemic medications/insulin. Excess weight was assessed based on body mass index (BMI) data ≥ 25 kg/m2 in adults (aged 18 to 59) and > 27 kg/m2 in older adults (aged 60 or older). Finally, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) was assessed based on self-reported previous medical diagnosis.

In addition, among the behavioral variables, the presence of smoking was analyzed by self-reported use of tobacco products, considering the smoker/nonsmoker categories, and excessive alcohol consumption was defined on the basis of self-reported consumption of five or more doses of alcoholic beverages in the past 30 days33. To assess the presence of physical inactivity, individuals who engaged in at least 150 min of physical activity per week were considered active, taking into account four domains: leisure, work, commuting, and household activities. Individuals who engaged in less than 150 min of physical activity per week in these domains were classified as inactive/sedentary34.

Definition of chronic kidney disease

CKD was defined by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) ≤ 60 ml/min/1.73 m2, according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification35. The CKD-EPI 2021 equation was used to calculate GFR, considering parameters such as sex, age, and serum creatinine36. The serum creatinine concentration of PNS participants was assessed through initial blood sample collection in gel tubes, centrifugation at 3,200 rotations/minute (rpm) for 12 min, and subsequent measurement using a non-deproteinized Jaffé kinetic test, taking into account standardized methods and reagents for all laboratories responsible for collecting and analyzing the biological material for the survey37,38.

Definition of metabolic syndrome

Considering the laboratory data from the database used in this study, MetS was classified based on the presence of three or more of the following five components: 1) HbA1C ≥ 5.7%, which characterizes the presence of hyperglycemia32 (replacing fasting glucose originally proposed in the classification due to the absence of fasting prior to SNP sample collection), or self-reported use of hypoglycemic agents; 2) SBP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 85 mmHg, which characterizes the presence of high blood pressure levels, or self-reported use of antihypertensive agents; 3) WC ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women, characterizing abdominal obesity (according to the cutoff point for South Americans); 4) HDL-c < 50 mg/dL in women and < 40 mg/dL in men, characterizing low HDL-c levels; and 5) TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, characterizing the presence of hypertriglyceridemia (levels estimated by the Friedewald formula39, due to the absence of direct measurements of TG levels in the database). This definition is based on the harmonized consensus proposed by the International Diabetes Federation on Epidemiology and Prevention, National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute, American Heart Association, World Heart Federation, International Atherosclerosis Society, and International Association for the Study of Obesity40. The proposed replacement of fasting glucose levels used in this study was based on the classification previously used by Oliveira et al. in a study with the same PNS sample41.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive analysis, the following variables were considered according to the presence of CKD: gender; age; educational level; race/color; AH; DM; CVD; physical activity; smoking; excessive alcohol consumption; excess body weight; MetS and its components; SBP; DBP; HbA1c; TG; HDL-c; WC, and GFR.

Sociodemographic, clinical (including MetS and its components), and categorical behavioral variables were described as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). An additional descriptive analysis of categorical variables was performed, stratifying data by sex (men and women) and age group (18–59 years and ≥ 60 years) (Supplementary Material). The two groups (with and without CKD) for categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous data on age, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, TG, HDL-c, WC, and GFR were tested for normality of data distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of data histograms. Normally distributed data were presented as means and standard deviations and compared between groups using the Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared between groups using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test.

The analysis of associations between MetS, the number of MetS components, and the risk of CKD was based on odds ratio (OR) estimates using logistic regression and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A crude analysis and an analysis adjusted for sex, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and excess weight were performed.

The overlaps between the components of MetS in the study population were evaluated using the statistical package “vennbar”. A Venn diagram was constructed using the InteractiVenn tool42 to illustrate the distribution of MetS components and their overlaps among participants.

An additional sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of different proposed substitutions for the hyperglycemia and hypertriglyceridemia components on the study results. We repeated the analyses of the prevalence of MetS and its components, using TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, replacing the estimated triglyceride levels from the main analysis. Furthermore, we evaluated the same prevalence results, replacing HbA1c values ≥ 5.7% with the estimated mean blood glucose ≥ 117 mg/dL (non-fasting), available in the database (Supplementary Material).

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software version 15.1, with a significance level of 5%, and the effects of complex sampling of the PNS were considered by weighting the sample. The weighted analyses were performed using Stata’s “survey” package, which is suitable for complex sample designs such as the PNS’s design, except for the analysis of overlaps between MetS components, where data weighting could not be performed because the command used did not allow non-integer sample weights.

Results

General characteristics of the participants and comparisons with CKD status

The majority of the study participants were female (51.70%), with a mean age of 44 years (± 16.46). They had a low level of education, represented by incomplete primary education (41.82%), and were predominantly white (46.80%) or brown (42.66%). The prevalence of CKD was 5.3%.

When sociodemographic variables were compared according to the presence of CKD, statistically significant differences were found in age, education, and race, with individuals with CKD being predominantly older (64.76 ± 17.00 years), having less education (74.24%), and being predominantly brown (47.20%).

Regarding clinical and behavioral variables, there was a higher prevalence of AH (73.46%), DM (21.04%), MetS (75.89%), and the individuals components high BP (63.00%), hyperglycemia (41.10%), hypertriglyceridemia (76.47%), and abdominal obesity (82.64%) in individuals with CKD. The proportions of physically inactive individuals (31.51%), smokers (14.21%), and excessive alcohol drinkers (14.90%) were mostly present in individuals without CKD. CVD, excess weight, and low HDL-c were statistically similar between the groups with and without CKD (Table 1).

Prevalence, distribution, and intersection of MetS components

The overall prevalence of MetS in the total sample was 47.13% (Table 1). The number of MetS components most prevalent in the general study population was one component (17.90%), two components (26.11%) and three components (24.78%). Compared with individuals without CKD, individuals with CKD had a greater prevalence of three or more MetS components (p < 0,001) (Fig. 1).

Prevalence of MetS components according to CKD status, Brazil, PNS, 2014–2015. *defined by the presence of 3 or more components.

The findings of the sensitivity analysis revealed that replacing the TG component ≥ 150 mg/dL with TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, and choosing the estimated mean blood glucose (non-fasting) ≥ 117 mg/dL (replacing the HbA1c ≥ 5.7% data adopted in the main analysis), led to the identification of a lower prevalence of individuals with a greater number of MetS components (four and five components). Consistently, lower overall prevalence and lower proportions of MetS were found in the groups with and without CKD, when considering these other possible substitutions (p < 0.001) (Table S3, S4—Supplementary Material).

The distribution of each MetS component in the sample was quantified, revealing the intersection between them (Fig. 2). The components present in a greater number of individuals in the general population were abdominal obesity (n = 5637), low HDL-c (n = 4311), and hypertriglyceridemia (n = 4927). Some individuals had hypertriglyceridemia, abdominal obesity, and low HDL-c together (n = 869). Others had hypertriglyceridemia, high BP, abdominal obesity, and low HDL-c simultaneously (n = 670). Only a small proportion had all five MetS components at once (n = 499) (Fig. 2).

Associations between MetS, the number of components, and the presence of CKD

Figure 3 shows the unadjusted and adjusted ORs for each number of MetS componentes associated with the odds of CKD. The unadjusted model (or crude model) revealed that individuals with MetS were 3.72 (95% CI 2.74–5.04) times more likely to have CKD than individuals without MetS. In addition, the number of components, approximately 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 components of the MetS, increased the individual’s odds of having CKD by 4.48 (95% CI 1.71–11.78), 5.74 (95% CI 2.30–14.30), 11.45 (95% CI 4.73–27.68), 19.21 (95% CI 7.91–46.68), and 35.78 (95% CI 14.44–88.68) times, respectively, compared with the reference category (Fig. 3A).

In the model adjusted for sex, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and excess weight, MetS remained associated with CKD, with 95% (95% CI 1.24–3.09) greater odds of CKD in individuals with MetS than in those without. In addition, having 3, 4, or 5 components of MetS increased the odds of CKD by 2.96 (95% CI 1.21–7.29), 3.15 (95% CI 1.24–8.02), and 5.05 (95% CI 1.95–13.12) times, respectively, compared with the reference category (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

In this study, the presence of MetS and a higher number of individual MetS components were associated with a higher probability of CKD. In addition, a higher prevalence of MetS was found in the group of individuals with CKD. These findings corroborate that MetS and CKD are two closely related conditions. On the one hand, MetS is characterized by risk factors, including abdominal obesity, high blood pressure, changes in lipid profile, and insulin resistance. On the other hand, CKD can be caused by multiple conditions, including DM and AH, which are conditions shared with individuals with MetS43. Thus, it is possible to understand the relationship between MetS and CKD as a pathway in which one can lead to the development of the other.

Before exploring the outcomes related to the burden of MetS and CKD and their relationship in this analysis, it is first pertinent to explore the different clinical and behavioral characteristics of the individuals analyzed in this sample. Individuals without CKD in this study had higher rates of physical inactivity, smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption. Since this is a cross-sectional analysis in which we cannot measure when the individual is exposed to or develops the outcome, these findings may highlight the pattern of abandonment of such risk behaviors by individuals with CKD in this study upon receiving a diagnosis of the disease. Clinical aspects such as the higher prevalence of DM, AH, MetS, and their components among the population with CKD in this study are consistent with established evidence that points to a greater burden of these diseases in this group, as a consequence or cause of the decline in renal function13. Furthermore, when we stratified these results by gender, we found that the burden of most clinical and behavioral risk factors did not differ between genders in the CKD group, while in the non-CKD population, behavioral risk factors and high blood pressure levels were predominant in men. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that men exhibit lower adherence to healthy lifestyle practices and reduced healthcare-seeking behaviors44. With respect to age group, the results corroborate the extant evidence establishing a higher incidence of chronic diseases among the elderly, a characteristic that can be attributed to the role of aging in the greater predisposition to chronic diseases45. The presence of behavioral risk factors and excess weight, which were more prevalent among younger individuals in the study in both groups (with and without CKD), also aligns with a pattern observed in previous findings46.

Despite the extensive population-level research on MetS in Brazil, particularly based on the analysis of large contemporary national surveys, this topic had already been addressed in studies conducted prior to the last decade based on the analysis of small samples. In these studies published before the last ten years, the findings related to the prevalence of MetS are not consistent with the general prevalence found in our study. In 2011, Grooner et al.47 identified a prevalence of MetS ranging from 35.9 to 43.2% in a population of adults living in a Brazilian city in the state of São Paulo. In 2012, Dutra et al.48 estimated a prevalence of MetS among adults in the Brazilian Federal District of 32% of individuals living with the disease.The variability in results found between these studies can be attributed to the different established diagnostic criteria, as well as the different population profiles of morbidity and access to health care present in different regions of the country49. Beginning in 2015, with the publication of epidemiological surveys such as the PNS, studies assessing the prevalence of MetS in Brazil began using nationally representative samples. In 2018, Ramires et al.50 reported a prevalence of MetS in Brazil of 8.9%. However, the PNS data utilized by the authors encompassed solely self-reported information for the diagnosis of MetS, thereby precluding the incorporation of laboratory data to ascertain blood glucose and TG levels, for instance. This may explain why this value was significantly lower than that found in our study. In a subsequent study published in 2020 with PNS data, this time using the laboratory measurement database to define MetS, Oliveira et al.51 found an estimate that was also lower than the results of our study, representing 38.4% of the Brazilian population. However, because hypertriglyceridemia data were replaced by hypercholesterolemia data51, these findings may have underestimated the presence of MetS. Finally, a systematic review and meta-analysis of cross-sectional studies in Brazil, published in 202252, estimated a prevalence of MetS in the country of 33%, which is lower than that found in the present study. This discrepancy can be partially explained by the heterogeneity of the included studies52. Although most of the cited studies present results that allow generalization to the general population of the entire country, the use of different classifications and cutoff points for the MetS components limits the comparison of these results with the findings of our study.

Regarding the prevalence of MetS in the population with CKD, this study revealed a 75.89% prevalence of MetS among individuals with CKD. Considering this other scenario, the publication of studies on the MetS landscape among individuals with CKD in the country has only been a reality in the last ten years. Some existing Brazilian observational studies on the subject, in line with our findings, can be cited, such as the 2014 study carried out by Vogt et al.53, which evaluated the prevalence of MetS in patients with terminal CKD followed in the hemodialysis service of the Botucatu School of Medicine, in the state of São Paulo, reporting a prevalence of MetS very close to our results, referring to 75.3% of the sample, when considering the same diagnostic criteria adopted in our analysis. In another Brazilian study, published in 2017 by Greffin et al.54, aiming to investigate the role of MetS and CKD as risk factors for CVD in individuals followed by primary health care in Niterói, in the state of Rio de Janeiro, a prevalence of MetS of 74.7% was reported in the population with CKD, also very close to the prevalence found in this study. In contrast to our findings, Comini et al.55 identified a prevalence of MetS among a sample of individuals with CKD of 17.7%, which is significantly lower than the rate found in our study. Finally, another more recent study from 202456, conducted in Brazil with CKD patients from the Nephrology Reference Center of São Luís, in the Brazilian state of Maranhão, identified a prevalence of MetS among the study subjects of 45.8%, using a machine learning algorithm; however, the rate found remained lower than that reported in the present study.

An analysis of these studies suggested that the presence of MetS may increase the likelihood of developing CKD18,57,58. Many mechanisms and pathophysiological hypotheses have been described to explain the relationship between MetS (and its individual components) and CKD. Previous studies suggest that obesity may lead to increased activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and increased sympathetic activity, resulting in greater sodium retention and hyperfiltration in the kidneys. This, combined with damage to the podocyte cells of the glomeruli caused by excess weight, may contribute to proteinuria and glomerular damage. Insulin resistance and increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines have been described in previous studies as agents capable of triggering damage to the vascular endothelium of vessels, which can lead to renal fibrosis. These associated aspects can significantly increase blood pressure and albuminuria and damage the kidney parenchyma. In addition, changes in the lipid profile can lead to impaired mitochondrial function, which can also impair kidney function59.

Some studies reported in the literature, such as those by Comini et al.55, Xiao et al.60, and Raikou and Gavriil61, have shown that individual components of MetS or the accumulation of components are significantly associated with CKD. In this study, participants with CKD had a higher prevalence of three or more MetS components, and the odds of CKD were significantly higher if the individual had three, four, or five MetS components, confirming the findings of previous studies62. In addition to these findings, the presence of MetS increased the odds of CKD in the study population by 95%, after adjusting for confounding variables such as sex, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and excess weight. Sex is an important variable, as it interferes with the burden of CKD and influences biological mechanisms that may contribute to the disparities in the burden of chronic diseases44. Behavioral factors are already considered variables of interest in this relationship, as they impact both the development of MetS components63 and the decline in renal function17. Finally, age deserves emphasis for its role in mediating the links between MetS and CKD. The aging process is a potential factor in triggering multiple metabolic disorders, such as DM and AH45, due to characteristics such as cellular senescence, telomere wear, and worsening oxidative stress64. Many of these senescence-related metabolic conditions are common components of MetS, which explains the greater impact of MetS among the elderly65. In addition, it is suggested that age may influence the development of CKD, since aging has an impact both on the triggering of degenerative processes in structures of the renal system, such as reduction in the volume of nephrons and glomeruli, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis and atherosclerosis of the renal artery, and on the functionality of the kidneys, leading to reduced filtration, increased vascular resistance and impairment in the endocrine function of the kidneys66.

On the other hand, kidney disease can also contribute to the development of MetS since kidney dysfunction can lead to a decrease in sodium and water excretion, triggering an increase in blood pressure and, consequently, the development of secondary hypertension67. In addition, kidney disease can also lead to changes in glucose and lipid metabolism, which can contribute to the development of insulin resistance and an altered lipid profile68,69.

Considering the findings of this study and the growing prevalence of NCDs and their risk factors in the population12,70, it is essential to strengthen policies for screening, early diagnosis, prevention, and appropriate treatment of these conditions. In Brazil, starting in 2021, the government implemented the “Strategic Action Plan to Confront Chronic Diseases and Noncommunicable Injuries in Brazil 2021–2030” to establish goals and actions for the development and advancement of public policies focused on the prevention and control of NCDs and their modifiable risk factors71. The plan includes actions focused on components of MetS, including the monitoring, detection, and management of AH, dyslipidemia, obesity, DM, and other associated conditions, such as CVD. Furthermore, lifestyle risk factors related to NCDs, such as smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and poor diet, are already associated with MetS72, which are also the focus of these actions71. Although CKD is not directly included in the actions and goals established in this Brazilian Plan, it is directly associated with NCDs, such as DM and AH70, which are addressed in most of the actions stipulated in this document71. Policies for the adequate management of CKD have been implemented in Brazil, such as the "National Policy for the Care of People with Kidney Disease," established by Ordinance 1,168 of June 15, 2004, which has as one of its objectives the expansion of coverage for individuals with DM and AH within the country’s health system73. In contrast, in the global context, the inclusion of CKD in the global public health agenda began only in 2025, when the World Health Organization (WHO) recognized CKD on the NCD agenda through a resolution on kidney health74. This belated recognition emphasizes long years of lack of collective and comprehensive efforts to address CKD as a global public health priority. Therefore, it is concluded that understanding the importance of CKD assessment in individuals with MetS is crucial due to the impact on health and survival that can occur when these conditions coexist20,75,76. Furthermore, the multimorbidity associated with the coexistence of these conditions can be costly for the health system, impacting its proper functioning77. This highlights the need for more public policies focused on screening for both conditions in the population worldwide.

In the current study, the strengths include the use of a nationally representative sample that is randomly selected and has objective measures (laboratory results) of the Brazilian population, which allows for consistent measures.

In terms of limitations, first, the study’s cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences from being made, allowing only the development of hypotheses to be confirmed in subsequent studies. Second, the use of unweighted analyses to identify overlaps between MetS components in the study sample may lead to a loss in expanding estimates of these intersections to the general Brazilian population. Another limitation of the study may be related to the use of HbA1c and estimated TG data, instead of directly measured fasting glucose and TG data, as defined in the adopted diagnostic criteria40. Because laboratory samples were collected from PNS participants at different times of the day, without the need for prior fasting27, TG data are not available in the database, and non-fasting glucose data would not be appropriate for the established cutoffs for the MetS definition chosen for this study. Several previous studies have proposed HbA1c as an alternative to fasting glucose in the diagnosis of MetS78,79. Ong and colleagues78 tested the usefulness of HbA1c as a surrogate for fasting glucose in diagnosing MetS among Americans and found good agreement between the two measures in diagnosing MetS, although it should be noted that fewer participants with MetS were identified using HbA1c. In another study, published by Park and colleagues79, the authors found that HbA1c is a more sensitive criterion for identifying MetS in Koreans, especially when considering the more appropriate HbA1c cutoff for this population, leading to a 17% increase in MetS detection when using it as a surrogate for fasting glucose. However, while HbA1c may be a potential surrogate for fasting glucose in diagnosing MetS, it is important to emphasize that its use in this case may lead to some biases. The lack of standardized HbA1c cutoff points for MetS diagnosis in each population, and the influence of factors such as high vitamin C and E intake, can lead to low HbA1c values80 and an underestimation of identified MetS cases. Other conditions, such as hyperbilirubinemia, severe hypertriglyceridemia, iron deficiency anemia, chronic excessive alcohol consumption, and uremia in patients with CKD and renal failure (due to the presence of carbamylated hemoglobin associated with these cases), can lead to biased measurements, causing increased HbA1c levels80 and consequently overestimating the presence of MetS. Finally, regarding the indirect estimation of TG levels using the Friedewald formula, its use may also potentially bias the findings of this study. The Friedewald formula was developed as a tool for the indirect estimation of LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) levels39, but its expanded use for the estimation of other lipid profile parameters remains underexplored. Jude et al.81 tested the usefulness of the Friedewald formula in estimating TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG in a population of individuals over 50 years of age. The indirect estimate of TG levels showed a strong correlation with the directly measured levels of this lipid parameter; however, the total errors attributed to this estimate in this study population were above the error limit established by the NCEP. This previous finding may indicate a possible bias in the indirect assessment of TG levels and, consequently, an impact on the diagnosis of MetS performed in our study. However, this limitation can be mitigated by identifying some limitations found in the study by Jude et al.81, such as the small sample size and the population characteristics, which differ from those present in our country. These aspects identified in a single study may not be sufficient to preclude the use of indirect TG estimation, especially considering the challenges encountered in assessing laboratory parameters in Brazilian surveys, such as a lack of supplies and insufficient funding. Furthermore, it is important to consider that, after evaluating the sensitivity analysis performed in our study, we found that the option of including estimated TG levels and HbA1c as components of MetS, to the detriment of other possible substitutions, led to the identification of a higher prevalence of the disease, coming closer to the findings found in previous observational studies in Brazil, mainly related to the prevalence rates of MetS among individuals with CKD53,54.

Given this scenario, individuals with these conditions need to be monitored so that they receive appropriate treatment to prevent or delay the progression of kidney disease and reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications. Measures such as controlling AH, treating DM, and adopting a healthy lifestyle help improve the outlook for the disease. In addition, screening for both MetS and CKD is necessary so that preventive measures can be taken early59, especially considering the often late diagnosis of CKD82. In this context, the assessment of CKD markers (such as creatinine, the GFR, and albuminuria) in individuals with MetS is timely. In addition, the use of MetS components in CKD prediction models is relevant, which is reinforced by previous studies designed to test the effectiveness of models for early diagnosis of CKD with the inclusion of some MetS components, demonstrating that the use of these models is an imminent and effective scenario for clinical decision-making83–85.

Conclusion

In this study, individuals with MetS were 95% more likely to have CKD than those without MetS. This finding highlights the importance of considering MetS as a significant indicator in identifying risks to kidney health. Furthermore, the accumulation of MetS components significantly increased the likelihood of an individual experiencing CKD, indicating that the combination of multiple metabolic factors may be strongly associated with CKD. Therefore, the results of this study highlight the importance of integrated approaches to kidney health management, considering the implementation of public policies and interventions aimed at preventing and controlling MetS, which can play a crucial role in reducing the incidence of CKD.
