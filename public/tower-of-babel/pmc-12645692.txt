Risk factors of nosocomial infection after percutaneous coronary intervention: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ting Xu, Siyu Lin, Shulan Liu, Heyao Xu, Xiaodi Bai, Xinyao Zhou, Yunlan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo summarize the risk factors for Nosocomial Infection(NI) in patients after Percutaneous coronary intervention(PCI). MethodsThe databases of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Biological Medical Database (CBM), Chinese Wanfang Database, and China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP) were searched for relevant articles from its inception to November 2024. This meta-analysis is based on RevMan5.4 software. The results' robustness was assessed using sensitivity analysis, and publication bias was assessed using Egger's test and funnel plot. The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale.ResultsTwenty-six studies were included, involving 18,825 patients. The meta-analysis showed that: combined diabetes(Patients with post-PCI infection had diabetes) [OR = 2.17, 95%(1.52,3.10)], mechanical ventilation [OR = 4.39, 95%(2.95,6.53)], indwelling urinary catheter [OR = 4.28, 95%(2.66,6.89)], age ≥ 60 years [OR = 1.96, 95%(1.50,2.57)], invasive procedures [OR = 2.72, 95%(1.99,3.71)], somking [OR = 1.88, 95%(1.18,2.99)], cardiac function classification of III to IV [OR = 3.14, 95%(2.03,4.88)], implantation of ≥ 2 stents [OR = 2.36, 95%(1.13,4.93)], and hospitalization days ≥ 14 [OR = 2.36, 95%(1.01,5.52)] were the predictive factors for the development of NI in post-PCI patients. The most common infecting bacteria were Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The sites of infection were predominantly respiratory and urinary.ConclusionsThis systematic review discusses the risk factors that lead to NI in post-PCI patients. Healthcare professionals may combine these factors to develop targeted interventions to reduce the incidence of infections and promote patient recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-04882-7.

Background

Cardiovascular disease(CVDs) is the leading cause of death worldwide, and an estimated 17.9 million lives are taken each year, accounting for 32% of all global deaths [1]. Ischemic heart disease(IHD) is known as the "number one killer" in CVDs, which is characterized by ischemia of the coronary arteries of the heart and secondary myocardial damage [2]; it mainly includes acute myocardial infarction(AMI), chronic stable angina pectoris, chronic IHD and heart failure caused by IHD [3].

PCI is a minimally invasive catheter-based procedure that restores myocardial blood flow by revascularizing stenotic or occluded coronary arteries. Due to its benefits of reduced trauma and faster recovery, PCI has become the primary treatment for IHD in patients with localized severe coronary artery disease, serving as an effective method of blood flow reconstruction [4–6]. The likelihood of post-PCI infection is generally not high [7, 8]. However, influenced by the characteristics of the interventional procedure, the age and the immune status of patients with CVDs, patients undergoing PCI are at risk of NI [7, 9]. And are often associated with poorer clinical outcomes [10]. Infections during hospitalization, particularly pulmonary infections, are a severe complication in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients undergoing PCI. These infections are associated with a tenfold increase in 30-day mortality [8]. In one study, infection was the third leading cause of postoperative mortality in PCI patients; moreover, compared to infection-free patients, those who developed postoperative infections during hospitalization had a higher risk for severe infections within 90 days of discharge [7].

Additionally, the inflammatory reactions brought on by the invasion of pathogenic microorganisms place further medical strain on patients, resulting in more extended hospital stays, higher healthcare costs, and delayed recovery of heart function [7, 11]. NI burdens patients and healthcare facilities significantly, necessitating proactive identification of risk factors and implementation of preventive measures. However, comprehensive assessments of NI risk factors following PCI remain limited. This study systematically evaluates these risk factors to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12]. The PROSPERO database registered the study protocol (Registration ID: CRD42024530743) (Supplementary Table 1).

Literature search strategies

PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, CBM, VIP and the Chinese Wanfang Database were searched for relevant articles from its inception to November 2024. The search terms used in this study were ("infection" OR "hospital infection" OR "Cross Infection" OR "Nosocomial Infection") AND ("influencing factor" OR "risk factor" OR "associated risk" OR "associated factor" OR "predictor") AND ("Percutaneous Coronary Intervention" OR "Percutaneous Coronary Revascularization" OR "percutaneous coronary endoluminal angioplasty" OR "Angioplasty, Balloon, Coronary" OR "Transluminal Coronary Balloon Dilation" OR "coronary stenting" OR "PTCA" OR "PCI"). This strategy searches with a combination of subject terms and free words. (Supplementary Table 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) study type: case–control study and cohort study, (2) study population: Patients who undergo PCI, (3) outcome indicators: patients with NI after the operation. Exclusion criteria: (1) reviews or conference abstracts; (2) raw data could not be extracted or converted; (3) no relevant data.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two independent researchers de-duplicated all of the retrieved literature using EndNote X9 software. Then, the studies were screened by reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria. If there were disagreements regarding the inclusion of the studies, they were resolved after consulting with a third researcher. Extracted data included first author, publication year, country, sample sizes, participants' age, disorder type, gender distribution, and risk factors reported in ≥ 3.

Quality assessment of included studies

The methodological quality of case–control and cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [13]. This tool evaluates three domains: (1) selection of study populations, (2) comparability between groups, and (3) assessment of exposure or outcomes. The NOS employs a star-based scoring system across eight items. Studies were categorized as follows: low quality (0–4 stars), moderate quality (5–6 stars), and high quality (7–9 stars).

Data analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and STATA 15.0 software. We pooled adjusted OR with corresponding 95% CI derived from multivariate analysis in the original studies. Given that the potential unmeasured confounding may exist in observational studies, a random-effects model was used for all analyses. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic; if the heterogeneity was high (I2 > 50%), the potential factors that may lead to heterogeneity were explored by regression analysis (risk factors reported by ≥ 10 studies) or the leave-one-out method (risk factors reported by < 10 studies). To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out method. This involved iteratively excluding each included study and recalculating the pooled effect size for every risk factor. Assessed publication bias for risk factors reported in ≥ 10 studies using Egger's test and funnel plots, with P > 0.05 suggesting no significant bias. Meta-analysis was conducted for factors reported in ≥ 3 studies with consistent quantitative data. Descriptive analysis was employed for factors unsuitable for quantitative pooling.

Assessment of the level of evidence

The quality of evidence for each risk factor was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations(GRADE) framework [14, 15]. All observational studies begin with a "low" evidence rating following the GRADE methodology. The evidence level ("high," "moderate," "low," or "very low") could be downgraded based on five criteria: (1) risk of bias, (2) inconsistency of results, (3) indirectness of evidence, (4) imprecision, (5) publication bias. Three factors could upgrade the evidence: (1) large magnitude of an effect, (2) dose–response gradient, and (3) effect of plausible residual confounding.

Result

Literature screening process and results

The systematic search identified 2,992 potentially relevant articles. After duplicate removal (n = 887), 2,105 articles underwent title and abstract screening by two independent researchers, excluding 2,022 records. A full-text assessment of the remaining 83 articles resulted in 26 eligible studies, with 21 included in quantitative analysis and 5 in qualitative analysis. Preoperative factors

Combined diabetes

Eight studies [16, 18, 19, 21, 22, 25, 27, 37] identified diabetes as a significant risk factor for NI post-PCI [OR = 2.17,95%(1.52,3.10), P < 0.05]. Due to substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 85%), we explored sources of heterogeneity using the leave-one-out method. However, heterogeneity remained high regardless of which study was removed (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Forest plot

Forest plot

Age

20 studies [16–19, 21, 23–29, 31, 33–35, 37, 38, 40, 41] examined age as a potential risk factor, though with varying definitions: ≥ 75 years [27], ≥ 80 years [23], > 75 years [33], ≥ 70 years [25, 31], and 6 studies [17, 18, 24, 26, 37, 41] used a continuous variable to describe age. Meta-analysis of 9 studies aged ≥ 60 years [16, 19, 21, 28, 29, 34, 35, 38, 40] revealed that advanced age significantly increased post-PCI infection risk [OR = 1.96,95%CI(1.50, 2.57), P < 0.05]. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 73%), which decreased to I2 = 38% after excluding Wang et al.'s study [29]. The different gender distribution (> 50% female in Wang et al. versus < 50% in others) could be the cause of the heterogeneity (Fig. 2).

Smoking

4 studies [19, 24–26] identified smoking as a significant risk factor for NI post-PCI [OR = 1.88,95%(1.18,2.99), P < 0.05]. Due to high heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 64%), the leave-one-out method pinpointed Zhang et al. [19] as the primary source (post-exclusion I2 = 38%). It may be because the infected patients in the other three studies were all aged ≥ 60 years. In contrast, more than 1/4 of infected patients in this study were at < 60 years, thus possibly leading to heterogeneity (Fig. 2).

Cardiac function classification

10 studies examined cardiac function classification as a potential risk factor [18, 25, 27, 31, 33, 35, 36, 38–40], though assessment tools varied: 1 study [18] used the New York Heart Association (NYHA) Classification, 4 studies [31, 35, 38, 39] did not specify assessment tools.5 studies employed the Killip classification; however, 1 of the studies [33] differed in their grading of cardiac function. Meta-analysis of 4 studies [25, 27, 36, 40] with consistent Killip classification demonstrated that Killip class III-IV significantly increased post-PCI infection risk [OR = 3.14,95%CI(2.03,4.88), P < 0.05], and with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative factors

Invasive procedures

14 studies [18, 20, 24, 27–32, 34–38] indicated invasive procedures as a significant risk factor for post-PCI infection [OR = 2.72,95%CI(1.99,3.71), P < 0.05]. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 83%). Meta-regression analyzing sample size, female proportion, and publication year as covariates failed to identify significant sources of heterogeneity. Also, it was unable to find a source of heterogeneity through the leave-one-out method (Fig. 2, Table 3). Mechanical ventilation

Six studies [16, 22, 25, 30, 32, 36] demonstrated a significant association between mechanical ventilation and increased post-PCI infection risk[OR = 4.39,95%CI(2.95,6.53), P < 0.05], with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). (Fig. 2).

Indwelling urinary catheter

Four studies [16, 22, 30, 36] identified indwelling catheterization as a significant risk factor for post-PCI infections[OR = 4.28,95%CI(2.66,6.89), P < 0.05] and no heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%). (Fig. 2).

Stent implantation count

Four studies [22, 28, 31, 35] reported stent implantation count. A meta-analysis was conducted for 3 studies with ≥ 2 stents [22, 31, 35]. The analysis revealed that implantation of ≥ 2 stents significantly increased NI risk post-PCI [OR = 2.36,95%CI(1.13,4.93), P < 0.05]. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 90%), which decreased to I2 = 21% after excluding Lv et al. 's study [35] (I2 = 21%). It is potentially due to this study's notably higher proportion of non-elderly patients than others (Fig. 2).

Postoperative factors

Length of hospitalization

Eight studies [16, 19–21, 28, 30, 35, 38] investigated hospitalization duration. But with different definitions: length of hospitalization was > 10 days in 2 studies [28, 38], ≥ 10 days in 1 study [30], > 1 week in 1 study [20], and ≥ 15 days in 1 study [35]. These were unsuitable for meta-analysis due to definitional variance. Analysis was conducted of 3 studies [16, 19, 21] which used ≥ 14 days. The result showed that length of stay ≥ 14 days was a risk factor for NI following PCI[OR = 2.36,95%CI(1.01,5.52), P = 0.05], but with high heterogeneity (I2 = 77%). Exclusion of Zhang et al. [19] reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Zhang et al.'s study spanned China's 2011–2013 national antimicrobial drugs stewardship campaign [42], while other studies collected data post-campaign. This nationwide intervention standardized antimicrobial use and enhanced hospital infection control protocols, potentially leading to differences in the postoperative infection situations that may explain the observed heterogeneity (Fig. 2).

Other factors

Four studies [22, 31, 38, 39] recognized procedure time as a risk factor for post-PCI infection; 2 studies [22, 39] reported that a surgical duration > 2 h was associated with a higher risk, while 2 other studies [31, 38] indicated that a duration > 6 h. 7 studies [28, 29, 31, 34, 35, 38, 39] demonstrated that patients with more comorbidities were at elevated risk for postoperative NI following PCI. 3 studies [21, 34, 36] examined the association between prophylactic antibiotic use and postoperative infection risk. 2 studies [21, 36] reported increased NI risk with prophylaxis compared to no antibiotic use. While another study [34] found contradictory results. 3 studies [19, 23, 32] identified left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) as a risk factor for NI post-PCI PCI. 2 studies [19, 32] reported increased NI risk with LVEF ≥ 40%, while 1 study [23] did not specify the value. However, none of the above studies provided sufficient quantitative data for meta-analysis.

Seventeen studies [16, 18–20, 22, 23, 28–31, 34–38, 40, 41] reported NI sites post-PCI. Primarily involving the respiratory and urinary systems. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

We conducted sensitivity analyses for all risk factors using the leave-one-out method. The results showed good robustness except for stent placement and length of hospitalization. Egger's test and funnel plot were conducted for invasive procedures (t = 8.61, p < 0.05). The analysis revealed the presence of publication bias for this factor. After applying the shear compensation method to adjust for invasive procedures, the correction effect size was [OR = 1.87,95% CI (1.42,2.47), P < 0.05]. The effect estimates remained statistically consistent before and after adjustment, confirming the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2 , and Supplementary Fig. 3).

GRADE quality assessments

Using the GRADE approach, we evaluated the quality of evidence for all meta-analyzed risk factors: very low for age ≥ 60 years, invasive procedures, combined diabetes, and smoking; and low for mechanical ventilation, indwelling urinary catheterization, cardiac function class III-IV, stent implantation ≥ 2, and hospitalization ≥ 14 days (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Many factors influence postoperative PCI infections. Our meta-analysis identified 9 significant risk factors: combined diabetes, age ≥ 60 years, smoking, Killip class III-IV, invasive procedures, mechanical ventilation, indwelling urinary catheterization, stent implantation ≥ 2, and hospitalization ≥ 14 days.

Patients with diabetes exhibited a 2.17-fold higher risk of NI post-PCI compared to non-diabetic patients. This elevated risk likely stems from hyperglycemia's effects on neutrophil function and pathogen value-added, impaired neutrophil migration to the surgical site due to atherosclerosis-associated vasculopathy, and delayed wound healing [43]. A systematic review of coronary stent infections revealed that diabetes was present in 35% of infected cases [44]. A meta-analysis demonstrates that intensive glycemic control reduces postoperative infection risk in patients [45]. The randomized controlled trial by Kyi et al. [46] demonstrated that early implementation (within 24 h of admission) of continuous glucose monitoring and optimized glycemic control significantly reduced extreme glycemic fluctuations, reducing the absolute risk of NI by 4%. Notably, patients with severe hyperglycemia (mean blood glucose > 270 mg/dL) had a 62% reduction in infection risk [47]. These findings support the early implementation of optimized glucose management for diabetic patients to minimize postoperative NI.

Studies by Mancone et al. [48] and Lian et al. [49] identified advanced age as an independent predictor of post-PCI infection, which is consistent with our findings. Two studies [50, 51] based on the National Readmission Database explored the correlation between age and readmission post-PCI. The results showed that elderly patients have significantly higher risks of infection-related unplanned readmission than younger patients. These findings highlight the significant impact of advanced age on infections in PCI patients. Age-related physiological decline, including immune deficiency, organ and tissue dysfunction [52], and vascular aging [53] – likely contributes to the elevated post-PCI infection risk in elderly populations. Therefore, to reduce infection risks in elderly PCI patients, clinicians should minimize nonessential invasive procedures, maintain strict hand hygiene practices, and use antibiotics appropriately [54].

This study revealed that advanced cardiac dysfunction (Killip class III-IV) increased NI risk, consistent with prior reports [7, 8]. Poor cardiac function correlates strongly with myocardial ischemia risk. Myocardial ischemia induces chemokine release that recruits leukocytes and chemotaxis them to clear necrotic cells, this process releases reactive oxygen species and other mediators, exacerbating the local inflammatory response in the ischemic myocardium and exacerbating tissue damage [55]. Worse cardiac function delays recovery and elevates infection risk. Branche [56] et al. demonstrated that heart failure patients face 4–33 times higher respiratory infection rates than non-failure patients. Thus, we recommend enhanced postoperative infection monitoring, early bed exercises, and respiratory exercises for patients with poor cardiac function. Nutritional optimization in patients and improved autoimmunity. And timely antibiotic adjustment based on clinical status.

Our study revealed invasive procedures, including urinary catheterization and mechanical ventilation, as significant risk factors for NI post-PCI. Depending on the severity of the disease, some patients require interventions after PCI. During the procedure, if disinfection is inadequate or the skin and mucosal barriers are inadvertently damaged, it creates favorable conditions for pathogen invasion and significantly increases the risk of infection. Post-cardiac surgery patients exhibit a 40.5% incidence of catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI) [57]. Urinary catheters can suppress the natural defense mechanisms of the urethra, increasing the risk of bacterial colonization or biofilm formation on the catheter surface. Moreover, with prolonged indwelling of the urinary catheter, the pathogens may migrate along the catheter surface into the body, making the host more susceptible to infection [58]. The primary prevention of CAUTI is to avoid nonessential catheterization, minimize indwelling duration, and monitor for infection based on the facility's risk assessment [59].

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is one of the most common complications of endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation [60]. A meta-analysis revealed a 10% mean incidence of VAP in cardiac surgery patients [61]. Increasing to 35.9% for mechanically ventilated ≥ 48 h [62]. The tube and cuff compress the respiratory mucosa, compromising its integrity and reducing its barrier function. The body's natural defense mechanisms are compromised. Meanwhile, exogenous pathogens may migrate from the oropharynx to the trachea, causing pulmonary infections [63]. For mechanically ventilated patients, regular oral care can reduce oropharyngeal colonization by pathogenic organisms [64]. If possible, try to use high-flow oxygen or noninvasive mechanical ventilation instead of intubation; reducing sedation, promoting spontaneous breathing, and promoting early extubation and mobility are also important [60].

This study demonstrates that prolonged hospitalization increases NI risk, consistent with Taye et al.'s findings [65]. Hospital environments harbor diverse pathogens on "high-contact" surfaces with patients, including door handles, ward faucets, and the hands of healthcare personnel, which serve as primary transmission vectors for NI-causing organisms [66]. The longer patients are exposed to such an environment, the greater the possibility of NI. It is essential to ensure a "clean environment."A systematic review demonstrated that using ultraviolet light to disinfect the environment, using hydrogen peroxide to wipe down surfaces, and training and monitoring the environmental services staff through feedback could reduce NI rates [67].

Our study found that multiple stent implantation increases infection risk. The survey by Herce et al. [68]similarly concluded that using more than one lead was an independent predictor of infections. It may be related to the length of the procedure. Multiple stent implantation extends operation time, increasing wound exposure duration and bacterial contamination risk. One study reported that the likelihood of infection increased by 13%, 17%, and 37% for every 15, 30, and 60 min of surgery time, respectively [69]. To minimize surgical duration, there are some recommendations: (1) preoperative planning to reduce intraoperative decision-making times, (2) surgical team familiarization with procedures and equipment; in addition, in high-risk patients, it may be cost-effective to administer appropriate doses of antibiotics during surgery [69].

Our study confirmed cigarette smoking as a risk factor for NI following PCI. A systematic review concluded higher postoperative infection rates among smokers compared to non-smokers [70]. A meta-analysis also showed that smoking is one of the risk factors for developing lung infections after cardiac surgery [71]. It may be due to smoking impairing the mucociliary escalator. Also, the antimicrobial and proinflammatory functions of alveolar macrophages, which are further reduced during anesthesia and surgery, make it easier to get infected [72]. Patients who smoke should be urged to quit as early as possible, and patients could wear masks to minimize the risk of inhaling contaminated particles.

This study found the respiratory (accounting for 55.42%) and urinary systems as primary NI sites post-PCI, potentially associated with endotracheal intubation and urinary catheterization. The most common Gram-negative organisms among the susceptible bacteria were Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In contrast, the most common Gram-positive organisms were Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, with fungal infections occurring less frequently. Clinically, microbiological and antimicrobial susceptibility testing should be performed as early as possible, and appropriate medications should be taken based on the testing results while avoiding the overuse of antibiotics.

Strengths of the study

The strengths of this study are as follows: Firstly, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis of risk factors for post-PCI infections, which may provide some evidence-based input for developing targeted interventions in the future. Secondly, adopting the GRADE rating system improves the objectivity and transparency of evidence quality assessment, facilitating more reliable conclusions for clinical decision-making.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. Firstly, Significant heterogeneity existed for some risk factors, potentially due to clinical characteristics, different regions, different adjustment variables in the logistic regression in the included articles, etc. Although sensitivity and meta-regression analyses were employed, they did not fully account for all heterogeneity sources. Secondly, the study population is predominantly Chinese, which may result in selection bias, thus limiting the generalizability of our findings. Thirdly, most of the study participants were elderly patients, and whether the conclusions of this study apply to patients in other age groups requires further validation. Lastly, there were significant variations in sample sizes across studies, and it is not clear what type of surgery all surgeries are, whether they are emergency or elective, which may have affected the heterogeneity and bias of the research. Future studies should include larger, multicenter, high-quality research to validate the conclusions further.

Conclusion

In summary, risk factors for NI in post-PCI patients include mechanical ventilation, indwelling urinary catheter, advanced age, invasive procedures, combined diabetes, cardiac function classification of III to IV, implantation of ≥ 2 stents, a hospital stay of ≥ 14 days, and smoking. The most common bacteria were Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. The primary sites of infection were the respiratory and urinary systems. Healthcare providers should implement targeted interventions to address these risk factors to reduce the incidence of postoperative NI and improve patient outcomes.
