Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov. and Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov., two novel species isolated from the breast milk of Senegalese women
Authors: Salematou Sarr, Ousmane Ndour, El Hadj Seck, Anissa Idrissa Abdoulaye, Mamadou Beye, George Diatta, Hubert Bassene, Malick Diop, Sara Bellali, Rayan Semmar, Stephane Alibar, Meriem Boukili, Idir Kacel, Nicholas Armstrong, Carine Couderc, Clemence Thiakane, Maryam Tidjani Alou, Cheikh Sokhna, Makhtar Camara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo Gram-negative, aerobic, motile and non-spore-forming bacilli, designated strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T, were isolated from the breast milk of nursing mothers in Senegal. Because both strains failed to be identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, a comprehensive taxonogenomic approach was employed to determine their taxonomic positions. The complete genomes consist of single circular chromosomes of 4,577,173 bp (63.65 mol% G + C) for strain Marseille-QA0332T and 4,658,126 bp (60.22 mol% G + C) for strain Marseille-QA0892T. The 16S rRNA gene analysis revealed a sequence similarity of 97.98% between strain Marseille-QA0332T and Pseudomonas ceruminis BML-PP028T, while Pseudomonas muyukensis COW39T was identified as its closest relative based on digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH 26.7%) and whole genome phylogeny on Average Nucleotide Identity (ANIb 81.3%). For strain Marseille-QA0892T, the 16S rRNA gene sequence showed 99.00% similarity to Pseudomonas matsuisoli JCM_30078T, its closest relative, with an ANIb of 75.0% and a dDDH value of 21.1%. All pairwise genomic values remained well below the established thresholds for bacterial species delineation, and whole genome phylogeny confirmed the stable placement of both novel lineages within the genus Pseudomonas. Based on these polyphasic results, we propose the description of two novel species, Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov. (Marseille-QA0332T = CSUR QA0332 = CECT 31294; GenBank accession number JBUCKV000000000) and Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov. (Marseille-QA0892T = CSUR QA0892 = CECT 31318; GenBank accession number JBUCKW000000000).Keywords: Severe acute malnutrition, Breast milk, New species, Pseudomonas senegalensis, Pseudomonas millioni, Taxonogenomics, Bacteria, Microbiota, Senegal

Introduction

First identified in 1894 by the German mycologist Walter Migula [1,2], the genus Pseudomonas comprises Gram negative, aerobic, rod-polar bacteria with flagella [3]. Originally defined as a group of considerable metabolic and phylogenetic heterogeneity [4], it has expanded with successive microbiological discoveries, to encompass numerous species inhabiting diverse environments [5]. The genus Pseudomonas is one of the most diverse and adaptable bacteria genera, occupying an exceptional range of ecological niches, from soil [6] and aquatic habitats [7] to animal and plant hosts [8,9]. Advances in molecular identification have led to frequent revisions of its taxonomy [10,11] and 381 species are validly published according to the List of Prokaryotic names with Standing in Nomenclature (LPSN; (https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/pseudomonas); last accessed July 30th, 2026) with Pseudomonas aeruginosa as the type species of this genus.

Members of this genus play significant ecological and functional roles of environmental, agricultural, biotechnological [12,13] and medical importance [14,15]: some contribute to plant-growth promotion and bioremediation [16,17], whereas others act as formidable opportunistic pathogens in humans, animals or plants, posing major public health and biosecurity challenges [18].

Among the many niches colonized by this genus is human milk, a biological fluid whose microbiota contributes to the early seeding of the neonatal gut and to infant immune and metabolic development. This community remains only partially characterized at the species level, although Pseudomonas is nonetheless a frequent member of milk, and in some populations dominant [19]. African populations microbiota milk is markedly under-studied. This gap, together with the reliance of most milk-microbiota surveys on culture-independent methods that do not yield isolated, motivated a culture-based, genome-resolved investigation.

In this context, two previously uncharacterized Pseudomonas strains were isolated from the breast milk of Senegalese women during a study of the milk microbiota in relation to infant nutritional status, from mothers of malnourished and healthy children. A taxonogenomic approach, integrating phenotypic, morphological, biochemical and genomic characterization, was used to classify and describe two novel species represented by strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T were isolated from breast milk samples collected from Senegalese nursing women in Niakhar, after informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethical and scientific Committee of the Senegalese Ministry of Health and Public Hygiene (MSHP) (approval number 00000233/MSAS/CNERS/SP, September 11, 2024) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Nagoya protocol. After obtaining informed consent, 10–15 mL of breast milk was collected from nursing mothers of both malnourished and healthy children in Niakhar, Senegal. These samples were then transported at +4 °C to the French National Institute for Sustainable Development (IRD) laboratory in Dakar, aliquoted into cryotubes, and stored at −80 °C. They were subsequently shipped on dry ice to the Institut Hospitalo-Universitaire (IHU) Mediterranean Infection in Marseille for culturing, sequencing, and biological analysis.

Sterility controls, Isolation and identification of strains

For every culture medium used, sterility was verified before inoculation: an aliquot of each uninoculated medium was subcultured onto 5% sheep-blood Columbia agar (COS; bioMérieux, Craponne, France) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h, and only media showing no growth were used. Any subsequent growth therefore originated from the milk sample.

Strain Marseille-QA0332T was first obtained by direct plating of the breast milk sample during colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration, on Columbia agar (COS; bioMérieux, Craponne, France) after 24 h of aerobic incubation, before any enrichment step. Strain Marseille-QA0892T was isolated using culturomics approach; breast milk was inoculated at day 0 into several liquid enrichment media under aerobic and anaerobic conditions at 37 °C, and each enrichment was subcultured onto COS agar, after serial dilution, at seven scheduled time points (days 1, 3, 7, 10, 15, 21 and 30). Strain Marseille-QA0892T was recovered at the day-15 subculture from an aerobic blood-culture bottle containing MacConkey broth (Becton Dickinson, Heidelberg, Germany) corresponding to a scheduled sampling of the culturomics protocol. For both strains, five to eight colonies were subcultured onto COS agar (bioMérieux) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C to obtain pure isolates.

Mass spectra were recorded using a Microflex system (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) equipped with MBT Compass software. Identification reliability was interpreted according to standard manufacturer criteria: a log-score value of 2.0 or higher associated with a recognized species name was considered reliable for species-level identification, whereas scores ranging from 1.7 to less than 2.0 allowed identification restricted to the genus level. Scores falling below 1.7 were deemed unreliable. All acquired profiles were matched against the BDAL 9607 MSP database (version 13, encompassing 12,438 entries), which is regularly updated with data from the MEPHI repository (accessible at (https://www.mediterranee-infection.com/diagnostic/collection-de-souches/).

Phenotypic and biochemical characteristics

To determine the phenotypic and biochemical properties of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T, a combination of traditional and modern methods was used. Gram staining was performed to determine cell morphology and membrane composition, and fresh preparations were examined to assess the motility of the strains. Sporulation tests were carried out using two pure suspensions of colonies belonging to the two strains, diluted separately in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and heat-shocked at 80 °C for 20 min. Catalase activity was determined using hydrogen peroxide (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Etoile, France), while oxidase activity was evaluated using ID-ASE discs (MASTDISCS™ ID, Mast Group). Optimum growth conditions for both strains were assessed across a range of temperatures (20 °C, 28 °C, 37 °C, 45 °C and 56 °C) and under different atmospheric conditions including aerobic, anaerobic and microaerophilic environments, enriched in CO2 using GENbag, anaer and microaer systems (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France). Growth was further tested in media adjusted to various pH values (5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, 8.5) and in the presence of increasing NaCl concentrations (5.0%, 7.5%, 10%, 15% and 20%), to evaluate environmental tolerance.

Additional biochemical characterization was conducted using, API 20 NE, API 50 CH and API ZYM strips (bioMérieux, Marcy, L'Etoile, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions enabling the assessment of enzymatic activities and metabolic capacities.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to the EUCAST 2025 guidelines (https://www.eucast.org/bacteria/clinical-breakpoints-and-interpretation/clinical-breakpoint-tables/), last accessed on 2026) using Etest strips (Biomérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France). The antibiotics panel included molecules relevant for Gram-stain negative bacteria: amoxicillin, amikacin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, colistin, doxycycline, fosfomycin, gentamicin, imipenem, nitrofurantoin, oxacillin, rifampicin, tobramycin, and cotrimoxazole. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were then determined in accordance with the recommendations [20].

Finally, the fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) profiles of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T were analyzed using gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-FAME) [21].

Morphological characterization by scanning electron microscopy

The morphological features of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T were analyzed using a Hitachi SU5000 scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Japan). Fresh colonies were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, United States) prepared in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States). Following fixation, samples were stained with 10% phosphotungstic acid (PTA; pH 7.4; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min to enhance cellular contrast. The cell suspensions were then deposited onto glass slides by cytocentrifugation at 800 rpm for 7 min using a Cytospin 4 R centrifuge (Thermo Electron Corporation - Shandon, UK). Subsequently, samples were sputter-coated with platinum-palladium using an MC1000 Ion Sputter Coater (Hitachi High-Tech, Japan). SEM micrographs were acquired at magnifications ranging from 1,000× to 20,000x. Detailed acquisition parameters, including instrument type, accelerating voltage, working distance, magnification, and detector mode, are provided for each corresponding micrograph.

Genome extraction, sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted after resuspension of the cells in G2 buffer (Qiagen-EZ2 DNA tissue kit), mechanical lysis with glass beads (G4649-500g Sigma) on a FastPrep-24™ 5G Grinder (MP Biomedicals) and lysozyme treatment (Sigma), using EZ2 DNA tissues kit on an EZ2 advanced XL biorobot (Qiagen), with a 50 μL elution. DNA was quantified with a Qubit fluorometer and the dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit (Thermofisher Scientific). Short read sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq (Illumina Inc, San Diego, California, United States), using the paired-end strategy. Librairies were prepared with the Nextera XT DNA sample preparation kit (Illumina) from 1 ng of input DNA tagmented, amplified by limited-cycle PCR (12 cycles) with dual-index barcodes, purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Inc, Fullerton, California, USA) and normalized before pooling. Sequencing was run with the MiSeq Reagent kit v2 (500 cycles) in 2 x 250 bp paired-end. Long-read sequencing was performed on an Oxford Nanopore GridION with an R9.4.1 flow cell. Librairies were prepared without PCR from 1 μg of genomic DNA using the SQK-LSK109 kit and native barcoding (EXP-NBD196), with end-repair and DNA repair (NEBNext modules New England Biolabs), barcode and adapter ligation, and final purification, following the manufacturer's instructions. Both short-and long-read assembled into a single circular chromosome for each strain.

Taxonogenomic analyses and delineation criteria

The quality of the raw sequencing data generated by the MiSeq platform was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 [22]. Low-quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [23], which considerably improved the overall quality of the reads. Quality control of Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) reads was carried out using the Nanoplot [24], facilitating visualization of length distribution and reading quality. Based on the quality and length of the ONT reads, those of first choice were selected via Filtlong (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong). Filtered reads were assembled using Unicycler v0.4.8 [25]. To eliminate potential contaminants, sequences of less than 800 bp were excluded [26]. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka v1.13 [27]. Putative functions were assigned using BLASTp [28], after comparing predicted bacterial protein sequences with the GenBank [29] and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) databases [30]. Transmembrane helices were detected using TMHMM [31], while signal peptides were predicted using SignalP [32]. CRISPR-Cas systems were distinguished using CRISPRCasFinder [33], while ABRicate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) was used to predict antibiotic resistance genes. In this study, the TYGS (Type Strain Genome Server) online tool [34] was used to calculate dDDH (digital DNA-DNA hybridization, formula d4) values for genomes, using a 70% similarity threshold to support species delimitation [35]. In addition, JSpecies [36] was used to estimate the average nucleotide identity (ANI) between genomes, while GCView [37] facilitated final genome visualization.

Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters

Biosynthetic gene clusters were predicted with antiSMASH 8.0.4 using relaxed detection stricteness, with KnownClusterBlast, MIBiG cluster comparison, Cluster Blast, ActiveSiteFinder, RREFinder and TFBS analysis enabled [38]. Clusters were assigned to a candidate metabolite only when the similarity to a characterized MIBiG cluster was reported with high confidence; the corresponding MIBiG accession is given. Clusters with low or absent similarity are reported by predicted type only.

Virulence and antimicrobial resistance determinants

Virulence-associated genes were screened using ABRicate (GitHub, Galaxy version 1.4.0) [39] against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) at 80% identity [40]. Acquired resistance determinants were screened using ABRicate against ResFinder [41] and ARG-ANNOT [42], while intrinsic chromosomal determinants were evaluated using CARD [43]. A targeted search for class C beta-lactamases (AmpC) was also performed from Prokka annotation [26] of each genome and confirmed by BLASTp against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database. Candidate proteins were verified for three Amber class C catalytic motifs (S-x-x-K, Y-x-N and K-T-G). Plasmid replicons were searched in both assemblies with PlasmidFinder version 2.1 implemented within the Galaxy environment (tool version 2.1.6+galaxy2) [44].

Strains analysis by integrated microbial NGS platform (IMNGS)

The presence, prevalence and abundance of the two strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T in different human and animal ecosystems were determined by consulting the IMNGS's raw metagenomic sequencing archives (https://www.imngs.org). (Last accessed in December 2025) [45].

Result and discussion

Phenotypic and biochemical characteristics identification

Strain Marseille-QA0332T

Strain Marseille-QA0332T was able to grow under aerobic and anaerobic conditions at all tested temperatures. Optimal growth was observed at 37 °C under aerobic conditions on COS agar (bioMérieux). No growth was observed at NaCl concentrations ranging from 7.0% to 20%. However, strain Marseille-QA0332T was able to grow in media with a basic pH (7.0 to 8.5). Microscopic examination after Gram staining revealed that strain Marseille-QA0332T is a Gram-stain negative bacillus with cells measuring approximately 1.5 μm in length and 1 μm in width, with colony diameters ranging from 0.2 to 1 μm (Fig. 1; Table S1). Colonies are circular, convex, yellowish and opaque, with a regular, smooth border and mucous consistency. Additionally, the strain is motile and non-spore-forming. Results of biochemical profiling using API 50 CH (biomérieux) demonstrated a broad carbohydrate metabolism. The strain was able to utilize various sugars including D-glucose, D-xylose, L-arabinose, D-galactose, D-mannose, arbutin, salicin, D-trehalose, inulin, starch, and gentiobiose. Several enzymatic activities were detected using API ZYM and 20 NE strips, namely oxidase, catalase, arginine dihydrolase, urease, β-glucosidase, alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), leucine arylamidase, trypsin, valine arylamidase, acid phosphatase, α-chymotrypsin, naphthol-AS-Bi-phosphohydrolase and β-galactosidase. Assimilation tests showed utilization of D-glucose, capric acid, malic acid, and trisodium citrate. Both oxidase and catalase tests yielded positive.

Scanning electron micrographs of bacilliform, flagellated cells of strains Marseille-QA0332T (Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov; A, B) and Marseille-QA0892T (Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov.; C, D) observed with a Hitachi SU5000 SEM.

Strain Marseille-QA0892T

Strain Marseille-QA0892T exhibited growth under aerobic conditions at room temperatures, 28 °C, 37 °C and 56 °C and under anaerobic conditions at 45 °C. However, significant growth of strain was observed at 28 °C, particularly in media with an alkaline pH. No growth was observed in NaCl media. Microscopic examination following Gram staining identified strain Marseille-QA0892T as a Gram-negative bacillus. Electron microscopy shows rod-shaped bacterium with flagella cells, measuring approximately 1.6 μm (1.0 to 1.7 μm) in length and 1 μm (0.5 to 1.7 μm) in width. Colony diameters ranging from 0.2 to 1.1 cm (Fig. 1; Table S1). Macroscopic evaluation of strain Marseille-QA0892T revealed that early-stage colonies were mucous and translucent punctiform. As they matured, the colonies became dry, filamentous and opaque form, with a flat rough surface, and filamentous contours. Biochemical identification using the API 20 NE and 50 CH systems showed a positive reaction for esculin (β-glucosidase). The strain is positive for catalase and oxidase. It also reduces nitrate to nitrite and was able to utilize D-glucose, L-arabinose, D-mannose, D-mannitol, potassium gluconate, capric acid, malic acid, citrate and phenylacetic acid. The enzymatic profile obtained from API ZYM testing indicated positive activity for alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), leucine arylamidase, trypsin, valine arylamidase, phosphatase acid, α-chymotrypsin, naphthol-AS-Bi-phosphohydrolase, β-galactosidase and N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase. The phenotypic and biochemical characteristics of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T were subsequently compared with those of phylogenetically related species (Table 1, Table 2).

Fatty acids analysis

Analysis of the cell wall composition of strain Marseille-QA0332T showed that the two major fatty acids were hexadecanoic acid (33.1%) and cis-9,10-methylene-hexadecanoic acid (19.2%). Minor quantities of unsaturated, branched, and saturated fatty acids were identified. As for strain Marseille-QA0892T, cellular fatty acid composition was dominated by four fatty acids with similar proportions: two unsaturated ones with 9-octadecenoic acid (26.6%) and 9-hexadecanoic acid (18.7%), and two saturated ones with hexadecanoic acid (21.9%) and dodecanoic acid (18.7%). Several 2- or 3-hydroxyl structures were also identified. Small quantities of other saturated and unsaturated fatty acids were detected. These distinct fatty acid profiles, characteristic of each strain, were subsequently compared with those of closely related species, as shown in Table S2.

Antibiotic tests analysis

Antibiotic susceptibility testing of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T were performed using Etest strips method (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) in accordance with EUCAST guidelines [48]. The results are summarized in Table S3.

Strain identification

Despite several attempts, MALDI-TOF MS failed to provide a reliable identification of the isolated strains Isolates that could not be identified using MALDI-TOF MS with scores falling below 1.7, underwent whole genome sequencing to determine their taxonomic status by extraction of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene. Analysis and comparison of the 16S rRNA gene sequences in GenBank using BLASTn revealed that strain Marseille-QA0332T shared its highest similarity of 97.98% with Pseudomonas ceruminis BML-PP028T, whereas strain Marseille-QA0892T exhibited its closest similarity (99%) with Pseudomonas matsuisoli JCM 30078T (Fig. 2). However, whole-genome comparisons demonstrated that both strains represent lineages distinct from currently described species (Fig. 3). Strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T are proposed as novel species of the genus Pseudomonas, within the phylum Pseudomonadota. The draft genome sequences of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T have been deposited in the GenBank database (JBUCKV000000000 and JBUCKW000000000) respectively.

Genomic properties comparison

The genome of strain Marseille-QA0332T spans 4,577,173 base pairs with a G + C content of 63.65 mol% (Table 3). It encodes a total of 4173 genes of which 4079 are protein-coding genes and 94 are RNA genes (19 rRNA, 74 tRNA and 1 tmRNA) (Table 3). The closest relative by 16S rRNA gene similarity is Pseudomonas ceruminis BML-PP028T (97.98%) (Fig. 2) although genomic comparison showed that strain Marseille-QA0332T shares an ANIb value of 81.1-81.4% with its closest relatives (P. muyukensis COW39T, P. peradeniyensis BW13M1T, P. mosselii PtA1T) [49] (Fig. 4), which is well below the 95-96% species delineation threshold [50], indicating that it represents a distinct species. Furthermore, a dDDH value of 26.7% was obtained with P. muyukensis COW39T (its closest relative by dDDH and whole-genome phylogeny), below the 70% species threshold [51] (Fig. 5). Taken together, these data confirm that strain Marseille-QA0332T represents a novel species within the genus Pseudomonas, for which the name Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov. is proposed.

The genome of strain Marseille-QA0892T comprises a single circular chromosome of 4,658,126 bp with a G + C content of 60.22 mol% (Table 3). It contains 4327 genes, of which 4255 are protein-coding genes and 72 are RNA genes (12 rRNA, 59 tRNA, 1 tmRNA) (Table 3). The 16S rRNA gene showed 99.00% similarity with P. matsuisoli JCM 30078T (GenBank accession number KJ720680) and whole-genomic phylogenetic analysis confirmed P. matsuisoli JCM 30078T as its closest neighbor (Fig. 3); however, whole-genome comparisons revealed substantial divergence, with an ANIb value of 75.0% (Fig. 4) and a digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) value of 21.1% (Fig. 5), both well below species thresholds (95-96% and 70% respectively). This apparent contrast—a high 16S rRNA similarity alongside much lower whole-genome relatedness—reflects the limited resolving power of the 16S rRNA gene within the genus Pseudomonas rather than any genuine conflict. Overall, strain Marseille-QA0892T represents a distinct, novel species within the Pseudomonas genus for which the name Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov. is proposed.

Of the predicted protein-coding genes, 2928 (71.78%) in strain Marseille-QA0332T and 2981 (70.06%) in strain Marseille-QA0892T were assigned to COG functional categories; their distribution across these categories is shown in Fig. 6, Table 4, and is dominated strains, excluding the poorly characterized categories by genes involved in amino-acid transport and metabolism, transcription, inorganic ion transport and metabolism and energy production and conversion, consistent with a metabolically versatile, free-living lifestyle.

The circular chromosome maps of the two strains (Fig. S1 and S2) display the distribution of coding sequences on both strands, the RNA genes, the G + C content and G + C skew along each chromosome; the single closed circular replicon of each strain, without any separate smaller replicon, is consistent with the absence of plasmids reported below.

Biosynthetic gene clusters

To investigate the secondary-metabolite potential of the two novel species, both genomes were mined with antiSMASH, which revealed diverse putative biosynthetic gene clusters and a clear contrast between the two repertoires: eleven candidate regions in strain Marseille-QA0332T and eight in strain Marseille-QA0892T. Strain Marseille-QA0332T is rich in non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) clusters, foremost a non-ribosomal peptide metallophore cluster showing high-confidence similarity to the pseudomonine biosynthetic gene cluster (MIBiG BGC0000410; ten matched genes, mean 74% amino-acid identity, 94% coverage), the strongest cluster match in either genome and consistent with a siderophore-mediated iron-acquisition system. Strains Marseille-QA0892T encodes no NRPS cluster but betalactone clusters and a region related to phenazine biosynthesis. Both genomes share a carotenoid cluster (BGC0000642; mean 62% identity), a hydrogen cyanide cluster (matching the three hcnABC genes of BGC0002345; 63% identity) and an N-acetylglutaminylglutamine amide (NAGGN) osmoprotectant cluster. Two caveats bound these predictions; the phenazine-related region of strain Marseille-QA0892T contained only the phzB core marker and not the complete phzABCDEFG operon, so a functional phenazine pathway cannot be inferred; and all clusters are in silico predictions of biosynthetic potential that have not been chemically validated.

Antimicrobial resistance virulence determinants and clinical relevance

To assess the resistance and pathogenic potential of the two novel species, the complete genome of strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T was screened for antimicrobial resistance genes (ResFinder, ARG-ANNOT and CARD) and for virulence factors (VFDB) with ABRicate.

Screening against VFDB, at both 80% and 50% identity, detected none of the determinants underlying Pseudomonas aeruginosa pathogenicity: no type III secretion system or effectors, no exotoxin A, elastase, phospholipases and no recognized high-risk pathogenicity island in either strain. The putative virulence-associated genes detected corresponded to conserved housekeeping functions and to flagellar, motility, adherence, type VI secretion, alginate and lipopolysaccharide machinery that are widely distributed among environmental and commensal pseudomonads.

No acquired resistance gene from the ResFinder or ARG-ANNOT databases was found in either strain indicating the absence of horizontally acquired resistance genes of clinical concern such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamases or carbapenemases. CARD detected only intrinsic chromosomal determinants: four in strain Marseille-QA0332T, namely the resistance-nodulation-division efflux components mexF (mexEF-OprN) and mexB (mexAB-OprM) and the regulators rsmA and cpxR, and three in strain Marseille-QA0892T, namely mexF, rsmA and the accessory protein yajC. These are baseline efflux and regulatory elements typical of the genus Pseudomonas and are not transferable.

A beta-lactam-resistant phenotype was nonetheless observed for strain Marseille-QA0892T (resistant to ceftazidime). Because this phenotype was not accounted for by any of the resistance databases used, a targeted search for class C beta-lactamases (ampC) was undertaken. This search, confirmed by BLASTp against the NCBI non-redundant database, identified in this strain a chromosomal class C beta-lactamase whose closest characterized homologue is the class C beta-lactamase of Pseudomonas matsuisoli (71% amino-acid identity, 96% coverage) and which bears the three Ambler class C catalytic motifs; this intrinsic AmpC-type cephalosporinase accounts for the ceftazidime resistance.

The corresponding locus in strain Marseille-QA0332T encodes instead a protein of the serine-hydrolase (estA) family that lacks the catalytic motifs of a functional class C cephalosporinase, so that the elevated penicillin MICs of this strain are attributable to intrinsic efflux and outer-membrane permeability rather than to a beta-lactamase. Neither strain therefore carries any acquired or transmissible resistance determinant.

Overall, the absence of acquired resistance and of toxin repertoire indicates a low biosafety risk and is consistent with commensal opportunistic organisms adapted to human-associated niches, although pathogenic potential cannot be formally excluded in the absence of an infection model.

IMNGS analysis

The IMNGS analysis revealed the presence of strain Marseille-QA0332T in various human metagenomes: 0.69% of skin (29/4213), 0.019% in nasopharyngeal (6/31,372), 0.015% in human metagenome and 0.95% in gut metagenome out of 75,337 metagenomes (Table S4). Present at a low prevalence in various human metagenomes, the IMNGS analysis revealed that strain Marseille-QA0892T is found in 0.50% of blood metagenome (662/133,134), in 1.53% of human gut metagenome (37,791/2,465,262), in 0.066% of human metagenome (4299/6,558,079), 0.069% of human skin metagenome (2907/4,188,929), and 0.0009% of oral metagenome (1/107,658) (Table S5).

Discussion

The recovery of Pseudomonas from a low-biomass matrix raises the question of whether these organisms are genuine milk-associated bacteria, transient colonizers, or contaminants. Two arguments exclude a laboratory or reagent origin: both were obtained as viable cultured isolated rather than as sequencing reads (strain Marseille-QA0332T by direct plating before any enrichment), and media sterility was verified before inoculation of Marseille-QA0892T so that any growth originated from the milk. The report on Pseudomonas in human milk further indicates that its recovery is expected [19].

Consistent with a host-associated lifestyle, screening of public metagenomes with IMNGS detected strain Marseille-QA0332T predominantly in human-associated ecosystems, most frequently in the human gut (3749 of 392,886 metagenomes; 0.95%) and on human skin (29 of 4213; 0.69%), and at lower frequency in nasopharyngeal (0.019) and other human metagenomes (0.015%) (Table S4); its low overall prevalence indicates a host-associated but non-abundant lineage. Strain Marseille-QA0892T showed a similar distribution, being detected most frequently in the human gut (37,791 of 2,465,262 metagenomes; 1.53%), and in human blood (0.50%), on human skin (0.069%), in other human metagenomes (0.066%), and rarely in oral metagenomes (0.0009) (Table S5). For both strains, the predominance of human-associated ecosystems, together with the low overall prevalence, is consistent with host-associated, non-abundant lineages rather than with environmental contaminants.

Beyond their formal description, the genomes of the two novel species offer a first, testable view of their adaptation to a host-associated niche, and several independent lines of evidence converge. The predicted functional repertoire is congruent with such a lifestyle. Both genomes encode an NAGGN osmoprotectant cluster and, in strain Marseille-QA0332T a pseudomonine-related metallophore cluster; osmoadaptation and siderophore-mediated iron acquisition are precisely the traits expected of an organism competing in a iron-limited fluid such as milk. The hydrogen cyanide cluster in both strains, and the phenazine-related locus in strain Marseille-QA0892T suggest a capacity for interbacterial competition; as these metabolites are double-edged, associated with biocontrol in environmental pseudomonads but also with virulence in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, their role is not extrapolated to the present species.

The carotenoid cluster is particularly instructive. Carotenoids are photoprotective, antioxidant pigments generally linked, in host-associated bacteria, to resistance to oxidative stress and to persistence at exposed sites such the skin, which would fit the human-associated distribution observed here. Yet the cluster is encoded in both genomes, but only strain Marseille-QA0332T forms visibly pigmented (yellowish) colonies. This dissociated between genotype and phenotype is a direct, observable demonstration that the presence of a biosynthetic cluster does not establish its expression, and it tempers any functional inference drawn from genome mining alone: the ecological benefit a carotenoid might confer can be claimed only for the strain that produces it.

Conclusion

Based on a comprehensive polyphasic approach integrating phenotypic, biochemical chemotaxonomic, and genomic analyses, strains Marseille-QA0332T and Marseille-QA0892T can be clearly distinguished from all currently recognized Pseudomonas species. Strain Marseille-QA0332T exhibits a 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity of 97.98% and dDDH values below 70% when compared to its closest phylogenetic neighbors. These values fall below the accepted threshold for species delineation supporting the proposal of a novel species within the genus Pseudomonas, for which the name Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov is proposed, with strain Marseille-QA0332T (= CSUR QA0332 = CECT 31294) as the type strain. Similarly, strain Marseille-QA0892T displays distinct genomic and phenotypic characteristics, along with 16S rRNA gene and dDDH values below the accepted threshold for species delineation. These findings support the classification of this isolate as representing a second novel species within the genus Pseudomonas, for which the name Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov., is proposed, with Marseille-QA0892 (= CSUR QA892 = CECT 31318) as the type strain.

Description of Pseudomonas senegalensis sp. nov

Pseudomonas senegalensis, (se.ne.ga.len.sis.N.L.fem.adj. senegalensis referring to Senegal, the country where the breast milk samples were collected).

Cells are Gram-stain-negative, aerobic motile, non-spore-forming, flagellated rods, approximately 1.5 μm long and 1 μm wide. Optimal growth was obtained at 37 °C after 24 h on Columbia agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood. Colonies are circular, convex, mucoid, yellowish, and opaque, with smooth margins, 0.2 to 1 cm in diameter. Oxidase-and catalase-positive. Biochemically, strain Marseille-QA0332T is positive for arginine dihydrolase, urease and β-glucosidase activity (esculin hydrolysis). It is capable to assimilated D-glucose, capric acid, malic acid, and trisodium citrate. Enzymatic activity is positive for esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), leucine arylamidase, trypsin, valine arylamidase, acid phosphatase, α-chymotrypsin, β-galactosidase and naphthol-AS-Bi-phosphohydrolase. The majors’ cellular fatty acids are hexadecanoic acid (33.1%) and cis-9,10-methylene-hexadecanoic acid (19.2%). The G + C content of the type strain genome is 63.65 mol%.

The type strain Marseille-QA0332T (= CSUR QA0332 = CECT 31294) was isolated from human breast milk collected in Niakhar, from a Senegalese mother breastfeeding a child with severe acute malnutrition. The complete genome sequence, including the 16S rRNA gene, is available in the GenBank database under accession number JBUCKV000000000.

Description of Pseudomonas millioni sp. nov

Pseudomonas millioni (mil.lio'ni.N.L.gen.n. millioni, named in honor of Pr. Matthieu Million in recognition of his contributions to microbiological research on human microbiota and malnutrition). Morphologically, bacteria of this species appear as Gram-stain-negative, motile, non-spore-forming, rod, measuring approximately 1.6 μm in length and 1 μm in width. Strain Marseille-QA0892T (= CSUR QA0892 = CECT 31318) was isolated from a breast milk sample collected from a mother nursing a healthy child. Optimal growth occurs under aerobic conditions at room temperature, 28 °C, 37 °C, and 56 °C. Optimal growth is observed at room temperature and 28 °C, particularly in media with an alkaline pH. Colonies initially appear mucous, translucent, and punctiform. Upon maturation, they become dry, opaque, and filamentous, with a flat profile, rough surface, and filamentous, irregular margins. Colony diameters range from 0.2 to 1.1 cm. Strain Marseille-QA0892T tests positive for esculin hydrolysis (β-glucosidase activity), reduces nitrate to nitrite, and assimilates D-glucose, L-arabinose, D-mannose, mannitol, potassium gluconate, capric acid, malic acid, citrate and phenylacetic acid. Enzymatic activity includes alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C4) esterase lipase (C8), leucine arylamidase, trypsin, valine arylamidase, acid phosphatase, α-chymotrypsin, naphthol-AS-Bi-phosphohydrolase, β-galactosidase and a positive reaction for N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase. Both oxidase and catalase tests are positive. Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) analysis shows a profile dominated by four major fatty acids 9-octadecenoic acid (26.6%), 9-hexadecenoic acid (18.7%), hexadecanoic acid (21.9%), and dodecanoic acid (18.7%). Several hydroxylated fatty acids (2- and 3-hydroxy structures) and minor amounts of other saturated and unsaturated fatty acids were also detected. The genomic G + C content of the type strain is 60.22 mol%.

The type strain, Marseille-QA0892T (= CSUR QA0892 = CECT 31318), was isolated from a breast milk sample collected from a mother nursing a healthy child. The 16S rRNA gene sequence and the whole-genome sequence of strain Marseille-QA0892T have been deposited in the GenBank database under accession number JBUCKW000000000.
