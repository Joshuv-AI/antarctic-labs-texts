Mechanism of methyltransferase METTL14 mediating m6A modification and regulating SIRT5 expression to promote ferroptosis and repress gastric cancer progression
Authors: Haoruo Zhang, Yi Jia, Kaiqing Guo, Zhe Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGastric cancer (GC) ranks as the third leading reason of cancer-associated deaths worldwide. This study investigates the mechanism by which the m6A methyltransferase methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) enhances ferroptosis and inhibits GC progression through regulation of sirtuin 5 (SIRT5).MethodsIn vitro, AGS cells were treated with pcDNA3.1–METTL14, si-SIRT5 and Ferrostatin-1, while NCI-N87 cells were transfected with si-METTL14. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health challenge, frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, which complicates treatment and prognosis [1]. While the overall efficacy of GC management has improved with the adoption of standardized surgical techniques, as well as targeted therapies, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [2–5], the 5-year overall survival rate of GC remains dismally low [6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to investigate the underlying mechanisms driving GC progression to identify new therapeutic targets that may improve treatment outcomes and survival rates.

Ferroptosis, a newly recognized form of programmed cell death, is distinct from cell apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis [7, 8]. Apoptosis is marked by chromatin condensation, cell shrinkage, cytoskeleton disintegration, and the formation of apoptotic bodies [9]. In contrast, ferroptosis is characterized by elevated lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron accumulation, which drive its unique metabolic signature [10, 11]. Numerous studies have linked ferroptosis to the growth and metastasis of GC [12–14], demonstrating its capacity to influence GC progression [15, 16].

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant post-transcriptional modification in eukaryotic messenger RNAs (mRNAs), is closely associated with tumor development [17]. A reduction in m6A levels has been observed in GC, suggesting a potential regulatory role in cancer progression [18]. m6A methylation relies predominantly on methyltransferases, with methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14) emerging as a critical methyltransferase responsible for catalyzing m6A modifications [19]. In GC, METTL14 is expressed at low levels, and its overexpression has been found to suppress cell invasion and proliferation via the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin pathway [18]. Interestingly, METTL14 overexpression also induces ferroptosis in endometrial and colorectal cancer (CRC) [20, 21].

Among the seven NAD-dependent deacetylase sirtuins (SIRT), sirtuin 5 (SIRT5) has gained attention for its tumor-suppressive role in GC [22]. Downregulated in GC cell lines and tissues, SIRT5 limits cell invasion, proliferation, migration, and colony formation in vitro and restricts GC tumor growth [22–24]. While SIRT5’s tumor-suppressive function is known, its potential role in ferroptosis regulation in GC has yet to be explored. In addition, prediction analysis using the SRAMP database has identified multiple m6A methylation modification sites on SIRT5, suggesting a possible link between METTL14-mediated m6A modifications and SIRT5 in promoting ferroptosis. However, the potential of METTL14 to regulate GC progression by enhancing SIRT5 expression through m6A methylation has not been investigated.

This study aims to uncover the mechanism by which m6A methyltransferase METTL14 induces ferroptosis and inhibits GC progression via SIRT5. Our findings will contribute to understanding GC pathogenesis and highlight a promising therapeutic target for GC treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Academic Ethics Committee of Shanxi Province Cancer Hospital/Shanxi Hospital Affiliated to Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences/Cancer Hospital Affiliated to Shanxi Medical University and conducted in strict accordance with the regulations for laboratory animal management and use. Efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and to reduce their suffering.

Cell culture

Human GC cell lines AGS, NCI-N87, SNU-1 and HGC-27 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), while human normal gastric mucosal epithelial cells GES-1 were purchased from Procell Life Science and Technology (Wuhan, Hubei, China). AGS cells were cultured in F-12 K medium (no.: 21127022, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). HGC-27, SNU-1, NCI-N87 and GES-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (no.: 11875093, Gibco) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C containing 5% CO2. Cells at the logarithmic growth phase (3rd passage) were detached using 0.25% trypsin and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. Once cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were harvested for subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection and grouping

Plasmids and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) including pcDNA3.1-METTL14, pcDNA3.1-NC, si-METTL14, si-SIRT5, and si-NC were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) and transfected into GC cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions.

AGS cells were treated with 2 μM Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) for 24 h following transfection with oe-METTL14 to inhibit ferroptosis acquired from MedChemExpress [25].

Real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara Biotechnology, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara) on an ABI 7900HT rapid PCR real-time system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 34 s. Each experiment was repeated three times, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal control. Data analysis was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using an Elx800 Reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

Colony formation assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 102 cells/well and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat. No.: P0099, Beyotime) and stained with 1% crystal violet (Cat. No.: C0121, Beyotime). Colony images were captured under a bright field microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Transwell assay

The transwell assay was conducted to assess cell migration and invasion abilities. For the invasion assay, transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), while chambers without Matrigel were used for cell migration assessment. Cells were starved in serum-free medium for 24 h prior to the assay, then resuspended in culture medium containing 1% FBS. Cells (1 × 105) were added to the upper chamber, and 600 μL of complete medium with 10% FBS was placed in the lower chamber. After a 24-h incubation, non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were removed with cotton swabs, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and staining with hematoxylin (C0107, Beyotime). Five random fields of view were captured, and the cells were counted to calculate the average number.

Cell cratch assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until confluent. A straight scratch was created in the cell monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. Cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated in FBS-free medium for 24 h. Five random fields of view were imaged, and the scratch closure was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (Me-RIP)

m6A modification sites on the SIRT5 mRNA sequence were predicted using the SRAMP database (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and mRNA was isolated with PolyATtract® mRNA Isolation Systems (A-Z5300; A and D Technology Corporation, Beijing, China). For immunoprecipitation, anti-immunoglobulin g (IgG) antibody (1:100, ab109489, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) or anti-m6A antibody (1:500, ab151230, Abcam) was added to IP buffer (140 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) and incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h. The antibody–bead complex was then incubated with purified mRNA in IP buffer containing protease and RNase inhibitors at 4 °C overnight. Eluted RNA was purified by phenol–chloroform extraction, and SIRT5 expression was assessed by RT-qPCR.

RNA stability assay

To analyze RNA stability, actinomycin D (5 μg/mL, MedChemExpress) was added to inhibit transcription, and cells were collected at 0-, 2-, 4- and 6-h post-treatment. Total RNA was extracted at each timepoint and analyzed by RT-qPCR.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

Cell lysates were prepared using the Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), followed by immunoprecipitation with 5 μg of anti-METTL14 antibody (1:30, ab252562, Abcam) and 50 μL of protein A/G magnetic beads. Normal rabbit IgG (1:100, ab172730, Abcam) was used as a negative control. The precipitated RNA was separated using a magnet, washed with RIP Wash Buffer, and analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Determinations of ferroptosis-related indicators

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using ROS assay kit (S0033M, Beyotime). Intracellular Fe2+ levels were determined with the Iron Assay Kit (MAK025, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Malondialdehyde (MDA) (S0131, Beyotime) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (S0101, Beyotime) concentrations were measured using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of lipid peroxidation

The lipid ROS was evaluated using the BODIPY™581/591 C11 reagent kit (D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, AGS cells were stained with a 2 μM C11-BODIPY (581/591) probe. Except for the different treatments applied to each group, the cell culture conditions were kept consistent across all groups. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Weztlar, Germany) and analyzed with Image J software (National Institutes of Health). Oxidized BODIPY (O-BODIPY) was observed under the excitation/emission wavelength of 488/510 nm (traditional FITC filter set), while reduced BODIPY (R-BODIPY) was observed under the excitation/emission wavelength of 581/591 nm (Texas Red filter set). The reduced product of BODIPY 581/591 C11 (R-BODIPY) exhibited a maximum excitation wavelength of 581 nm and a maximum emission wavelength of 591 nm, primarily emitting red fluorescence, during which the red/green fluorescence ratio was relatively high. After oxidation by lipid hydroperoxide (O-BODIPY), the maximum excitation and emission wavelengths shifted to approximately 488/510 nm, with predominant green fluorescence, resulting in a reduced red/green fluorescence ratio. The degree of lipid peroxidation was determined by the proportion of green fluorescence.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

The LDH assay kit (S0053, Beyotime, China) was used to measure LDH release as an indicator of cell death according to the manufacturer’s instructions. LDH activity was determined spectrophotometrically at 490 nm using a microplate reader. LDH release (%) was calculated as follows: LDH release (%) = (OD value of the experimental group–OD value of the blank control group)/(OD value of the control group–OD value of the blank control group) × 100%.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysate (P0013E, Beyotime). The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit (Beyotime). Proteins (30 μg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (137 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for METTL14 (1:1000, GTX637541, GeneTex, Alton Pkwylrvine, CA, USA), SIRT5 (1:1000, GTX117824, GeneTex), E-cadherin (ab15148, Abcam), N-cadherin (ab18203, Abcam), Vimentin (ab137321, Abcam) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) (1:1000, GTX54095, GeneTex) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were then washed and incubated with secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (HRP) (1:10,000, GTX26721, GeneTex) for 2 h at room temperature. β-Actin (1:1000, GTX109639, GeneTex) was used as the loading control. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence kit (ECL Plus, Life Technology, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Laboratory animals

Six-week-old male Balb/c nude mice were obtained from Shanxi Jinbo Biomedical Co., LTD (Taiyuan, China). Mice were acclimated for 1 week prior to the experiments and maintained at 24–26 °C with 50–60% humidity, with free access to food and water.

In vivo tumor growth assessment

METTL14-overexpressing cell lines were generated via lentiviral transfection. To establish a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model, nude mice were injected subcutaneously with AGS cells (titer of 1.5 × 108 TU/mL) infected with either the METTL14 Lentiviral Vector (Human) (CMV) (pLenti-GIII-CMV) (Lv-METTL14) or the corresponding empty vector (Lv-NC) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lv-METTL14 and Lv-NC were obtained from Applied Biological Materials (Vancouver, BC, Canada). Briefly, AGS cells (1 × 107) transfected with the empty vector or METTL14 overexpression vector were resuspended in 200 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline and injected subcutaneously into the right flanks of each mouse.

Mice were randomized into three groups: control, Lv-NC and Lv-METTL14 (n = 6 mice per group). Tumor width and length were measured every 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated as volume = (length × width2)/2. After 21 days, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), and xenograft tumors were excised and weighed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues were fixed, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-μm slices. After dewaxing, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies for METTL14 (1:1000, GTX637541, GeneTex) and SIRT5 (1:500, GTX117824, GeneTex). Sections were then washed and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with a secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (HRP) (1:1000, GTX26721, GeneTex). DAB (Sigma-Aldrich) was applied for nuclear staining, and hematoxylin was used as a counterstain. IHC was performed using a kit (Zhong Shan Jin Qiao Biotechnology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Positive sites appeared brownish-yellow, and images were analyzed using ImageJ software, normalized by the percentage of positive cells in the control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality, and data that met normal distribution requirements were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent sample t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

METTL14 inhibited GC cell proliferation, migration and invasion

Studies have shown that METTL14 is down-regulated in gastric adenocarcinoma tissues and cells [18]. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses showed that METTL14 expression was significantly downregulated in GC cells compared to GES-1 cells (Fig. 1A, B, all P < 0.01). AGS cells, with relatively low METTL14 expression, and NCI-N87 cells, with relatively high METTL14 expression, were selected for further experiments. AGS cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-METTL14 (oe-METTL14), and NCI-N87 cells with si-METTL14. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 1C, all P < 0.01). In the oe-METTL14 group, cell proliferation rate (Fig. 1D, all P < 0.001), colony formation ability (Fig. 1E, all P < 0.001), and migratory and invasive capacities were significantly reduced compared to the oe-NC group (Fig. 1F, G, all P < 0.001). In addition, the expression of EMT-related proteins Vimentin and N-cadherin decreased, while E-cadherin expression increased (Fig. 1H, all P < 0.001). Conversely, the si-METTL14 group showed an enhanced cell proliferation rate (Fig. 1D, all P < 0.001), increased colony formation ability (Fig. 1E, all P < 0.001), and elevated migration and invasion capacities relative to the si-NC group (Fig. 1F, G, all P < 0.001). EMT-related proteins Vimentin and N-cadherin were upregulated, while E-cadherin was downregulated (Fig. 1H, all P < 0.001). These results emphasize that METTL14 plays a key role in restraining GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

METTL14 enhanced SIRT5 mRNA stability and expression via m6A modification

Studies have found that SIRT5 is lowly expressed in GC tissues and cell lines, and up-regulation of SIRT5 can inhibit GC progression [22, 23]. Through the ENCORI database, we found that SIRT5 expression was significantly correlated with METTL14 in STAD (Fig. 2A, P < 0.001). The SRAMP database predicted several m6A methylation modification sites on SIRT5, including two high-confidence binding sites (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 1). RT-qPCR and Western blot results demonstrated that SIRT5 expression was higher in the oe-METTL14 group than in the oe-NC group, while the si-METTL14 group showed lower SIRT5 expression than the si-NC group (Fig. 2C, D, all P < 0.01). RIP analysis revealed an interaction between METTL14 and SIRT5 mRNA (Fig. 2E, all P < 0.001). Me-RIP assay results showed that SIRT5 mRNA m6A modification levels were higher in the oe-METTL14 group than in the oe-NC group, whereas the si-METTL14 group showed reduced SIRT5 m6A modification levels compared to the si-NC group (Fig. 2F, all P < 0.01). Subsequently, the stability of SIRT5 mRNA was assessed by treating cells with actinomycin D. Further assessment of SIRT5 mRNA stability using actinomycin D treatment revealed a gradual reduction in SIRT5 mRNA levels over time. Notably, SIRT5 mRNA levels remained higher in the oe-METTL14 group than in the oe-NC group and lower in the si-METTL14 group compared to the si-NC (Fig. 2G, all P < 0.001). These findings suggest that METTL14 enhances SIRT5 mRNA stability and expression through m6A modification.

SIRT5 knockdown partially reversed inhibitory effects of METTL14 overexpression on the proliferation, migration and invasion of AGS cells

We co-transfected AGS cells with oe-METTL14 and si-SIRT5, with successful transfection confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot, which showed a marked reduction in SIRT5 expression in the oe-METTL14 + si-SIRT5 group (Fig. 3A, B, all P < 0.01). Compared to the oe-METTL14 + si-NC group, the oe-METTL14 + si-SIRT5 group exhibited increased cell proliferation (Fig. 3C, all P < 0.01), higher colony formation (Fig. 3D, all P < 0.01), and enhanced migration and invasion capacities (Fig. 3E, F, all P < 0.01). In addition, there was an upregulation in EMT-related proteins Vimentin and N-cadherin, while E-cadherin expression was downregulated (Fig. 3G, all P < 0.01).

In NCI-N87 cells co-transfected with si-METTL14 and oe-SIRT5, we observed a slower cell proliferation rate (Supplementary Fig. 2 C, all P < 0.001), fewer colonies (Supplementary Fig. 2D, all P < 0.001), and suppressed migratory and invasive abilities (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F, all P < 0.001). Collectively, these findings suggest that SIRT5 knockdown partly averts the suppression of AGS cell proliferation, migration, and invasion induced by METTL14 overexpression.

Knockdown of SIRT5 partially counteracted the promoting effect of METTL14 overexpression on ferroptosis in GC cells

To further assess whether METTL14 induces ferroptosis in GC cells by mediating m6A modification to regulate SIRT5, we compared the oe-METTL14 group to the oe-NC group. Results indicated that the oe-METTL14 group displayed significantly increased ROS, Fe2+, and MDA levels and a higher cell death rate, alongside decreased GSH content, SOD activity, and GPX4 protein expression (Fig. 4A–C, all P < 0.001). BODIPY staining assessing the degree of lipid peroxidation revealed that METTL14 overexpression led to an elevated degree of lipid peroxidation in cells (Fig. 4D, all P < 0.01). However, SIRT5 knockdown partially reversed METTL14's promotion of ferroptosis in GC cells (Fig. 4A–D, all P < 0.01). These findings suggest that the promotive effect of METTL14 overexpression on ferroptosis in GC cells is partially abolished by SIRT5 knockdown.

Inhibition of ferroptosis partially reversed METTL14’s suppressive effects on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Our findings indicated that, compared to the oe-METTL14 group, the oe-METTL14 + Fer-1 group showed a notable reduction in Fe2+ and MDA levels, significant increases in SOD levels, GSH content, and GPX4-positive cells (markers of ferroptosis inhibition), along with a marked reduction in lipid peroxidation degree and cell death (Fig. 5A–C, all P < 0.05). In addition, there was an increase in cell proliferation rate (Fig. 5E, all P < 0.01), a greater number of colony formations (Fig. 5F, all P < 0.01), and enhanced migratory and invasive capacities (Fig. 5G, H, all P < 0.01). EMT-related protein expressions of N-cadherin and Vimentin were also elevated, while E-cadherin expression decreased (Fig. 5I, all P < 0.05). The results indicate that ferroptosis inhibition partially offsets METTL14’s inhibitory impact on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

METTL14 inhibited GC tumor growth by upregulating SIRT5 expression via m6A modification

Using AGS cells stably transfected with lentiviral Lv-METTL14, we established a subcutaneous xenograft tumor in nude mice to investigate METTL14’s effect on GC tumor growth in vivo. Tumors in the Lv-METTL14 group exhibited significantly reduced volumes and weights compared to those in the Lv-NC group (Fig. 6A–C, all P < 0.01). IHC analysis confirmed higher expression levels of METTL14 and SIRT5 in the Lv-METTL14 group’s tumor tissues relative to the Lv-NC group (Fig. 6D, P < 0.01). In addition, METTL14 overexpression increased SIRT5 m6A modification levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 6E, all P < 0.01). Collectively, these in vivo results support the role of METTL14 in suppressing GC tumor growth by upregulating SIRT5 expression through m6A modification.

Discussion

GC has a high mortality rate, partly due to its subtle early symptoms, making early diagnosis challenging [26]. Notably, METTL14 has emerged as a suppressor in the malignant processes of GC cells [27]. Our findings demonstrate that METTL14 enhances the stability of SIRT5 mRNA via m6A modification, increases SIRT5 expression, and promotes ferroptosis, collectively inhibiting GC progression.

The dysregulation of METTL14 is implicated in various aspects of gastrointestinal cancer, including tumor initiation, chemoresistance, progression, and metastasis [28]. For example, METTL14 has been shown to inhibit colorectal cancer (CRC) cell proliferation and metastasis by downregulating the oncogenic long non-coding RNA XIST [29]. Similarly, Gu et al. found that METTL14 limits the self-renewal of bladder tumor-initiating cells and the occurrence of bladder carcinoma via Notch1 m6A modification [30]. In breast cancer, METTL14 has been proposed as a tumor suppressor gene [31], and it inhibits the stem cell phenotype of CRC by regulating the β-catenin/NANOG pathway [32]. Importantly, decreased METTL14 expression has also been observed in GC, with its overexpression shown to limit malignant biological behaviors in GC cells [18]. Our findings align with these studies, underscoring METTL14’s role in suppressing the malignant characteristics of GC cells.

m6A modification is pivotal in cancer progression [33], with notably reduced levels observed in GC [18]. In addition, SIRT5 is often underexpressed in GC tissues and cell lines, and its upregulation has been shown to inhibit GC progression [22, 23]. This study revealed that METTL14 interacts with SIRT5 mRNA, where METTL14 overexpression increases both SIRT5 m6A modification and SIRT5 mRNA levels in GC cells, contrasting the effects of METTL14 downregulation. This research is one of the first to uncover the METTL14/SIRT5 axis, showing how METTL14 stabilizes SIRT5 mRNA via m6A modification to increase SIRT5 expression. Furthermore, SIRT5 knockdown was found to significantly enhance GC cell malignancy. Similar associations have been noted in other cancers, with SIRT5 deficiency promoting liver cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma through bile acid-triggered immunosuppression and altered glutamine metabolism [34, 35]. Altogether, these findings suggest that METTL14 restricts GC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration by modulating SIRT5 expression.

Ferroptosis is emerging as a therapeutic avenue for cancer resistance and is known to influence GC progression [15, 16]. Its role in GC is supported by various signaling pathways and genes [21, 36]. For instance, ferroptosis inhibition in GC cells can occur via lipid–ROS accumulation blockade or ALOX15 targeting [37]. In addition, activating Wnt/beta-catenin signaling can reduce lipid ROS production in GC cells, thereby restricting ferroptosis [38]. Both METTL14 and SIRT5 have been reported to promote cellular ferroptosis [20, 39], yet the regulation of ferroptosis by METTL14 via SIRT5 has not been previously reported. Our data demonstrated that METTL14 overexpression in GC cells induced ferroptosis, evidenced by elevated Fe2+, ROS, MDA levels, increased cell death, and lipid peroxidation, along with reduced SOD activity, GSH content, and GPX4 expression, though these effects were partially diminished with SIRT5 silencing. Existing literature has reported the regulatory role of SIRT5 in ferroptosis. Evidence suggests that the desuccinylation and stabilization of BCAT1 mediated by SIRT5 result in insensitivity to ferroptosis [40]. In addition, SIRT5 modulates ferroptosis to engage in ischemia–reperfusion injury in ischemic stroke via the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway [39]. Similarly, METTL14 is known to support ferroptosis through m6A-YTHDF2-dependent GPX4 destabilization, enhancing lipid peroxidation [21]. It also promotes doxorubicin-induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes by modulating the KCNQ1OT1–miR-7-5p–TFRC axis [41], while in endometrial cancer, arginine methylation of METTL14 by PRMT3 bolsters treatment resistance and malignancy [21]. Building on these findings, this study confirmed that knockdown of SIRT5 partially reverses the promotive effect of METTL14 overexpression on ferroptosis in GC cells. Furthermore, as shown in our results, inhibiting ferroptosis counteracts the suppression of GC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration induced by METTL14 overexpression. Supporting this, METTL14-mediated m6A modification of SOX4 mRNA has been shown to inhibit CRC metastasis in vitro and in vivo [42]. Finally, our in vivo experiments showed that METTL14 upregulates SIRT5 expression via m6A modification, effectively suppressing GC tumor growth.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that METTL14 enhances the stability of SIRT5 mRNA through m6A modification, thereby upregulating SIRT5 expression and promoting ferroptosis, which collectively contributes to the suppression of GC progression. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding GC pathogenesis and suggest METTL14 as a potential therapeutic target for GC treatment. However, this study still has some limitations. First, we did not analyze the expression and correlation of METTL14 and SIRT5 in clinical GC tissue samples. Second, our in vivo and in vitro experiments did not fully explore the molecular mechanism by which SIRT5 directly participated in ferroptosis to affect GC. Future clinical studies will be conducted to address this. Furthermore, we will conduct more in-depth research into the exact molecular mechanism of SIRT5-mediated ferroptosis, such as knocking SIRT5 down or overexpressing SIRT5 alone on the basis of METTL14 overexpression in in vivo experiments. In addition, in the future, we may employ advanced sequencing technologies such as Perturb–DbiT, multimodal tri-omics mapping, and high-plex protein and whole transcriptome co-mapping [43–45] to further investigate the mechanism of ferroptosis in GC. Future research will focus on modulating SIRT5 expression in animal experiments to further confirm that METTL14-mediated m6A modification upregulates SIRT5 and impedes GC progression.
