Challenges and opportunities of implementing open-source digital pathology in sub-Saharan Africa: Lessons learned from deploying QuPath for breast cancer biomarker assessment in Cameroon
Authors: Odile Léa Zobo, Clément Parfait Ndengue, Laurine Verset, Lemaire Bodo, Myriam Remmelink, George Bediang, Joseph Mendimi Nkodo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital pathology and artificial intelligence (AI) are transforming cancer diagnostics worldwide, yet their implementation in sub-Saharan Africa remains largely undocumented. The region faces a critical shortage of pathologists while bearing an increasing cancer burden, making AI-assisted diagnostics particularly relevant. However, practical deployment in resource-constrained environments raises unique technical, logistical, and educational challenges that differ substantially from those encountered in high-income settings.ObjectiveTo systematically document the technical, logistical, and practical challenges encountered during the implementation of QuPath, an open-source digital pathology platform, for breast cancer immunohistochemical (IHC) biomarker assessment at a reference pathology lab in Cameroon, and to propose actionable solutions for similar resource-limited settings.MethodsWe conducted a prospective implementation study at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun, Yaoundé, involving 39 cases of invasive breast carcinoma with IHC for estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, Ki67, and HER2. We documented all phases of the digital pathology workflow: pre-analytical slide preparation, slide digitization (performed remotely at Erasme University Hospital, Brussels), image transfer and storage, QuPath algorithm training, and automated analysis on a consumer-grade laptop (4 GB RAM, 500 GB storage). Challenges were categorized into four domains: hardware and infrastructure constraints, pre-analytical and scanning issues, software training and optimization, and human factors including the learning curve.ResultsOf 130 IHC slides, 18 (13.8%) required re-scanning due to detection failures or blurred images despite pre-scanning quality control. The absence of a local scanner necessitated international slide shipment, adding 8–12 weeks of delay and logistical complexity. Processing on a 4 GB RAM consumer laptop averaged 20 min per case (range: 5–60 min), with frequent application freezes on large tissue sections. Image files averaged 1.5 GB each at ×40 magnification, rapidly exhausting the 500 GB local storage capacity. The QuPath random tree classifier required manual annotation of representative tumor, stromal, and lymphoid regions on all 39 hematoxylin and eosin-stained cases before deployment on IHC slides, representing approximately 15–20 h of pathologist time. Exploratory concordance analysis suggested clinically meaningful agreement with expert pathologist scoring for three of the four biomarkers assessed, with detailed analytical validation reported separately.ConclusionsImplementing open-source digital pathology in sub-Saharan Africa is feasible but requires strategic planning around infrastructure, logistics, and training. We propose a practical framework addressing minimum hardware requirements, quality control protocols adapted to tropical environments, and a structured training program for pathologists. Our experience demonstrates that despite significant constraints, AI-assisted biomarker assessment can be successfully deployed in resource-limited settings, offering a pathway to improved diagnostic standardization where it is most needed.Keywords: Digital pathology, Implementation, Sub-Saharan Africa, QuPath, Breast cancer, Resource-limited settings, Artificial intelligence, Quality control

Introduction

Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden of breast cancer, with rising incidence and mortality rates compounded by late-stage presentation and limited access to diagnostic infrastructure.1, 2 Accurate immunohistochemical (IHC) assessment of biomarkers including estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), Ki67 proliferation index, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is essential for guiding treatment decisions in breast cancer, yet the region faces a critical shortage of trained pathologists estimated at fewer than one per million population in many countries.3

Digital pathology, which encompasses the digitization of glass slides into whole-slide images (WSIs) and their analysis using computational algorithms, has emerged as a promising solution to address workforce limitations and improve diagnostic reproducibility.4, 5 Artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools can assist pathologists in quantifying biomarker expression, potentially reducing inter-observer variability and processing time.6, 7 Among available platforms, QuPath stands out as an open-source solution that eliminates licensing costs, a critical consideration for resource-constrained environments.8, 9

However, the vast majority of digital pathology implementation studies originate from high-income countries with established infrastructure, reliable power supply, high-speed internet, and dedicated IT support.10, 11 The specific challenges of deploying these technologies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, remain poorly documented. A recent implementation study from Brazil highlighted similar concerns regarding low-cost scanners, storage limitations, and maintenance challenges, but the African context presents additional constraints including limited access to scanning equipment, tropical environmental conditions affecting slide quality, and extremely limited computational resources.12

In this study, we systematically document the technical, logistical, and practical challenges encountered during the implementation of QuPath for IHC biomarker assessment in invasive breast carcinoma at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun, Yaoundé. We provide a detailed account of each phase of the digital pathology workflow, from slide preparation to automated analysis, quantify the barriers encountered, and propose practical solutions for labs in similar settings. Our goal is to provide an actionable roadmap for other pathology labs in sub-Saharan Africa seeking to adopt digital pathology and AI-assisted diagnostics. The detailed analytical validation of QuPath concordance with expert pathologist scoring across the four biomarkers, including sensitivity, specificity, and inter-observer reproducibility analyses, is reported separately; the present manuscript focuses exclusively on implementation challenges and lessons learned.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective implementation study conducted between July 2021 and August 2024, documenting the deployment of a digital pathology workflow for breast cancer biomarker assessment. The study was carried out at the pathology lab of the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun, a national reference lab in Yaoundé, in collaboration with the Department of Pathology, Erasme University Hospital, Université Libre de Bruxelles, Belgium. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine and Biomedical Sciences, University of Yaoundé I.

Study population and sample selection

Thirty-nine cases of primary invasive breast carcinoma diagnosed between May and December 2021 were included. Inclusion criteria were: confirmed histological diagnosis of invasive breast carcinoma on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and availability of IHC slides for ER, PR, Ki67, and HER2 (four markers per case, plus one HE slide, totaling approximately 195 slides). Exclusion criteria included diagnostic discordance between pathologists, significant tissue loss, sub-optimal IHC staining quality, and unreadable virtual slides after re-scanning.

Phase 1: Pre-analytical preparation and quality control

All IHC staining was performed at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun using the Ventana BenchMark XT automated platform (Roche Diagnostics). ER was detected using clone SP1 (Ventana, ready-to-use) with CC1 antigen retrieval for 60 min; PR using clone 1E2 (Ventana, ready-to-use) with the same protocol; Ki67 using clone MIB-1 (DAKO, ready-to-use) with UltraView Universal DAB detection for 32 min; and HER2 using the Pathway HER2 4B5 kit (Ventana). Before digitization, glass slides underwent a structured quality control (QC) protocol: visual inspection for slide integrity (absence of cracks or fractures), proper coverslip placement, absence of ink marks or staining residues, and adequate tissue drying. Slides failing QC were either cleaned or replaced from archival material when possible.

Phase 2: Slide digitization

Due to the absence of a slide scanner in Cameroon, all selected slides were shipped internationally to the Department of Pathology, Erasme University Hospital, Brussels. Slides were packaged in protective cases with silica gel desiccants to prevent humidity damage during transit. Digitization was performed using a NanoZoomer S360 scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) at ×40 magnification (0.25 μm/pixel resolution) with z-stack acquisition mode. A set of 10 calibration slides was used for scanner standardization. The scanner performed automated QC including barcode detection, tissue detection, and focus quality assessment. Images were stored in NDPI format and visualized using NDP.view software. Digital images were subsequently transferred to Cameroon via an external hard drive.

Phase 3: Hardware and software setup

QuPath analysis was performed locally in Yaoundé on a consumer-grade laptop computer (Acer brand) with the following specifications: Full HD 1080p display, 4 GB RAM, 500 GB hard disk drive (HDD), and approximately 4 h of battery autonomy. QuPath version 0.4 was installed as a standalone application requiring no additional software dependencies. Java Runtime Environment was pre-bundled. No internet connection was required for analysis, an important consideration given inconsistent connectivity in Yaoundé.

Phase 4: Algorithm training and deployment

The QuPath analysis workflow comprised several sequential steps. First, cell detection was performed using the built-in “cell detection” command, which segments nuclei based on hematoxylin optical density and estimates cytoplasmic boundaries by circular expansion (maximum 500 μm radius) around detected nuclei. Second, a single random tree classifier was trained to distinguish three tissue classes—tumor, stroma, and lymphoid aggregates—using QuPath's “object classifier” tool. Annotations were drawn manually on all 39 H&E-stained cases, with approximately 2–4 representative regions per case (one or two tumor regions and one to two non-tumor regions, depending on tissue heterogeneity). The classifier was trained iteratively on batches of 10 cases, with progressive refinement as more annotations were added. Default QuPath parameters for the random tree classifier were retained throughout, as parameter tuning would have required computational pathology expertise unavailable locally. The trained classifier was applied to all IHC-stained slides without marker-specific re-training, reflecting the realistic deployment scenario of a single-operator workflow in a resource-limited setting. Classifier adequacy was assessed by visual inspection of the classification overlay on training images rather than by formal cross-validation; this pragmatic approach reflects the practical constraints of implementation without dedicated machine learning support, and represents one of the limitations discussed below. For each biomarker, QuPath generated quantitative scores: percentage of positive cells for Ki67, Allred-equivalent scores for ER and PR (based on proportion and intensity), and membrane staining scores for HER2.

Phase 5: Data collection and challenge documentation

Throughout the implementation process, all technical difficulties, failures, workarounds, and time expenditures were prospectively documented in a standardized log. Challenges were categorized into four domains: (1) hardware and infrastructure constraints, (2) pre-analytical and scanning issues, (3) software training and optimization challenges, and (4) human factors including the learning curve. Processing times were recorded for each case. As an exploratory secondary analysis, automated QuPath scores were compared with manual scoring performed independently by three pathologists, to verify that the implementation produced analytically meaningful results despite hardware constraints. The detailed scoring methodology, statistical analyses, and inter-observer reproducibility are reported in a separate dedicated validation manuscript.

Hardware and infrastructure constraints

The most significant infrastructure barrier was the complete absence of a WSI scanner at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun and, to our knowledge, at any pathology lab in Cameroon at the time of the study. This single constraint transformed the project from a local digital pathology implementation into a complex international collaboration requiring physical shipment of glass slides to Brussels for digitization.

The consumer-grade laptop used for QuPath analysis (4 GB RAM, HDD storage) represented a fraction of the minimum 16 GB RAM typically recommended for digital pathology image analysis. Practical consequences included: frequent application freezes when loading large WSI files (some exceeding 2 GB), inability to run multiple analyses simultaneously, extremely slow rendering of high-magnification regions, and occasional application crashes requiring restart and loss of unsaved annotations. Processing time was directly correlated with tissue section area, ranging from 5 min for small biopsy fragments to over 60 min for large surgical specimens. The average processing time of 20 min per case across all biomarkers is approximately 3–4 times slower than what has been reported on recommended hardware specifications.8

Storage capacity was rapidly overwhelmed. At an average of 1.5 GB per slide at ×40 magnification, the 130 study slides alone occupied approximately 195 GB, consuming 39% of the 500 GB total storage capacity. This left minimal space for operating system files, software, and other work, necessitating careful file management and periodic archival to external media.

Pre-analytical and scanning challenges

Despite systematic pre-scanning QC, 18 of 130 slides (13.8%) required re-scanning. Causes included tissue detection failures (the scanner could not identify the tissue region on the slide), focus errors resulting in blurred images at high magnification, and barcode reading failures. Importantly, no discordances attributable to histological section defects, staining residues, or poor digitization quality were observed in the final dataset, validating the effectiveness of the upstream QC protocol.

Environmental conditions specific to tropical settings presented additional pre-analytical concerns. Ambient humidity in Yaoundé (typically 70%–90% during the rainy season) can cause coverslip condensation and fungal contamination on stored slides. Whereas these issues did not significantly affect the study slides due to the relatively short archival period (diagnosed 2021, digitized 2024), they represent a significant concern for labs seeking to build digital slide archives in tropical environments. Slide packaging for international shipment required silica gel desiccants and rigid protective cases to prevent humidity damage and physical breakage during transit.

Software training and optimization challenges

Training the QuPath random tree classifier required substantial pathologist time and expertise. The annotation process involved manually delineating representative regions of tumor, stroma, and lymphoid aggregates on all 39 H&E-stained cases. This was performed in batches of 10 cases, with the classifier retrained after each batch to progressively improve accuracy. The total annotation time was estimated at 15–20 h of dedicated pathologist work.

Several software-specific challenges were encountered. The cell detection algorithm, while effective for nuclear markers (Ki67, ER, and PR), performed poorly for membrane-based HER2 staining. QuPath's default cell detection estimates cytoplasm as a circular expansion around the nucleus, which does not accurately capture the membrane staining pattern critical for HER2 scoring. Threshold parameter optimization required to distinguish complete from incomplete membrane staining was particularly time-consuming. The StarDist deep learning extension, which could theoretically improve membrane segmentation, was not deployable due to the computational limitations of the 4 GB RAM laptop and the lack of GPU acceleration.

A persistent issue across all nuclear markers (ER, PR, and Ki67) was the misclassification of non-tumoral cells. The random tree classifier, despite training, intermittently identified inflammatory mononuclear cells and reactive stromal cells as tumor cells, with particularly pronounced effects on PR-stained sections; the marker-specific quantitative impact of this misclassification is reported in the dedicated validation manuscript. Careful manual selection of regions of interest before automated analysis partially mitigated this bias but added processing time, reducing the efficiency gains expected from automation.

Human factors and the learning curve

The learning curve for QuPath proficiency was substantial. The primary operator (a pathology resident with no prior computational training) required approximately 4 weeks of self-directed learning to achieve competence in basic QuPath operations: image navigation, annotation tools, cell detection parameter adjustment, and classifier training. This was achieved using freely available online tutorials and documentation from the QuPath website, without formal training or mentorship in computational pathology.

Key skills that proved essential but were not immediately intuitive included: understanding the relationship between detection parameters and cell segmentation quality, interpreting classifier performance metrics, recognizing when the classifier required re-training versus when annotation errors were the root cause of misclassification, and developing efficient annotation strategies to maximize classifier accuracy while minimizing annotation time. The absence of a local community of practice or peer network in digital pathology in Cameroon meant that troubleshooting relied entirely on online forums and documentation.

Exploratory concordance with expert pathologists

As a secondary analysis to verify that the implementation produced analytically meaningful outputs despite the constraints described above, automated QuPath scores were compared with manual scoring by three independent pathologists. Exploratory concordance was clinically meaningful for three of the four biomarkers assessed (Ki67, HER2, and ER), whereas concordance for PR was limited. These results suggest that QuPath's analytical performance was relatively robust to hardware limitations, even though processing speed was substantially affected. The detailed concordance metrics, statistical analyses, and inter-observer reproducibility data are reported in a separate dedicated validation manuscript, and are not duplicated here.

Discussion

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive documentation of the practical challenges of implementing open-source digital pathology for breast cancer biomarker assessment in sub-Saharan Africa. Whereas most digital pathology implementation studies focus on analytical performance metrics, our findings highlight that in resource-constrained settings, logistical and infrastructural barriers may be more significant determinants of success than algorithmic performance. Accordingly, the present discussion focuses on implementation lessons rather than on detailed analytical performance, which is the subject of a separate validation manuscript.

The most critical barrier identified was the complete absence of a local slide scanner, a challenge shared by the vast majority of pathology labs in sub-Saharan Africa. Whereas our international collaboration with Erasme University Hospital enabled digitization, this model is neither scalable nor sustainable for routine clinical use. Recent developments in lower-cost scanning solutions, including smartphone-based microscopy adapters and mid-range scanners such as the MoticEasyScan used in a recent Brazilian implementation study,12 offer more realistic pathways. However, even mid-range scanners represent investments of $50,000–$100,000, beyond the reach of most African labs without external funding. Innovative models such as regional scanning hubs serving multiple labs, or telepathology networks with centralized digitization, deserve serious exploration.13

The feasibility of running QuPath on a consumer-grade 4 GB RAM laptop is a significant finding. Whereas processing speed was severely compromised, the workflow could be deployed to completion and produced outputs that were analytically usable for the majority of biomarkers assessed (as detailed in the companion validation manuscript). This suggests that QuPath's core algorithms are computationally efficient enough for deployment on modest hardware, though the user experience suffers considerably. Practical recommendations for labs with similar constraints include: processing cases sequentially rather than in batch, closing all other applications during analysis, working at ×20 magnification where possible (reducing file sizes by approximately 75%), and investing in additional RAM as the single most impactful hardware upgrade.8

The 13.8% re-scanning rate is higher than the 5%–8% typically reported in high-income settings,14 and can be attributed to multiple factors including slide preparation variability inherent to manual coverslipping (versus automated coverslippers used in high-throughput labs), residual staining artifacts from the IHC process, and the challenges of scanning slides prepared in a different lab from the scanning facility. Standardized pre-scanning QC protocols, such as the one we developed and applied, are essential and should be explicitly included in implementation guidelines for LMICs.

The annotation and training burden for the random tree classifier represents a significant hidden cost of open-source digital pathology. Whereas QuPath eliminates software licensing fees, the 15–20 h of pathologist time required for classifier training has a real opportunity cost in settings where pathologists are already severely overburdened. Pre-trained models, transfer learning approaches, and federated learning initiatives that enable classifiers trained in one setting to be adapted to another with minimal local annotation could substantially reduce this burden.15 The QuPath community's growing repository of shared classifiers and scripts represents a valuable but underutilized resource for LMIC labs.

Marker-specific analytical limitations were observed during this implementation, particularly for PR and HER2, and are analyzed in detail in the dedicated validation manuscript. From an implementation standpoint, the most relevant observation is that the more advanced open-source tools that could potentially address these limitations—notably the StarDist deep learning extension for improved cell segmentation—require GPU acceleration and at least 8 GB of RAM,16 placing them beyond the reach of our hardware configuration. This creates a technology gap, where the most advanced open-source tools remain inaccessible to the settings that would benefit most from them, and represents a structural barrier that licensing-free software alone cannot overcome.

Proposed implementation framework

Based on our experience, we propose a tiered implementation framework for digital pathology in sub-Saharan Africa. Tier 1 (minimum viable) requires a mid-range laptop (8–16 GB RAM, SSD), access to a scanner (local or regional hub), and one pathologist trained in QuPath basics—applicable for research and quality assurance. Tier 2 (functional clinical support) adds a dedicated workstation (32 GB RAM, GPU), local or shared scanning capacity, and a network-attached storage solution—suitable for integration into the diagnostic workflow. Tier 3 (full digital pathology) involves institutional scanner ownership, cloud storage, deep learning tools (StarDist), and formal computational pathology training—enabling primary digital diagnosis.

Alternative low-cost image acquisition

An important avenue for future work concerns alternative image acquisition strategies adapted to the financial constraints of sub-Saharan African labs. Camera-based capture directly from glass slides—using microscope-mounted cameras or smartphone adapters—is substantially more affordable, more available, and easier to maintain than dedicated whole-slide scanners, and may represent a more realistic acquisition pathway for routine deployment in low-resource settings. Whereas such a comparative experiment was beyond the scope of the present implementation study, a controlled proof-of-concept comparing camera-captured images and scanner-derived images analyzed through the same QuPath-based pipeline would provide directly actionable evidence for labs considering digital pathology adoption. We are planning such an evaluation as a logical next step of this implementation program.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of 39 cases (130 IHC slides) is modest. Although consistent with the College of American Pathologists' minimum recommendation of 20 cases for digital pathology validation studies, with a strong recommendation of 60 cases, our final sample fell below the recommended target after exclusions and no formal power calculation was performed. As an implementation feasibility study rather than a formal validation study, the present sample is sufficient to characterize implementation challenges, but should not be interpreted as analytically conclusive. Second, the necessity of international slide shipment introduces a selection bias, as only slides surviving transport were analyzed, and substantially limits the external generalizability of our workflow: labs without access to a remote scanning partnership or operating with different pre-analytical processes may face challenges of a different nature or magnitude than those reported here. Third, the single-center design at a national reference lab may not capture the full range of challenges encountered across the heterogeneous landscape of African healthcare settings, including district hospitals, private labs, and rural facilities. Fourth, processing time measurements were obtained on a single hardware configuration and may not be directly transferable to other setups. Fifth, the random tree classifier was assessed by visual inspection of the classification overlay rather than by formal cross-validation, reflecting the practical absence of computational pathology expertise during deployment; this represents a methodological limitation as well as a real-world implementation reality. Finally, the absence of a formal cost-effectiveness analysis limits our ability to quantify the economic implications of our findings, which would require dedicated health economics expertise beyond the scope of the present study.

Conclusions

Implementing open-source digital pathology in sub-Saharan Africa is feasible but requires deliberate planning around infrastructure limitations, logistics, and training. Even on severely resource-constrained hardware, the QuPath workflow could be deployed to completion, though processing efficiency was substantially compromised and analytical performance varied across biomarkers, as detailed in the dedicated validation manuscript. The principal barriers identified in this implementation are not algorithmic but logistical: access to scanning equipment, computational resources, storage capacity, and trained personnel. Addressing these challenges through regional scanning networks, tiered hardware recommendations, shared pre-trained classifiers, and structured training programs could accelerate the adoption of AI-assisted pathology across the continent, where it has the potential to significantly improve diagnostic standardization and patient care.
