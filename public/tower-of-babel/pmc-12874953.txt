Prevalence and associated factors of post-tuberculosis lung disease in Sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abraham Tekola Gebremedhn, Kidist Bobosha, Yeabsira Alemu Fantaye, Melese Yeshambaw Teferi, Ziad El-Khatib, Tsegab Alemayehu Bukate, Hawult Taye Adane, Minyahil Tadesse Boltena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) is a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where the burden of tuberculosis (TB) remains high. Only a few studies have reported the global burden of PTLD, and the associated factors of PTLD have been understudied. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence and associated factors of PTLD in SSA.MethodsThis study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic review and meta-analysis. We included studies reporting the prevalence and associated factors of PTLD among individuals with a history of pulmonary TB in SSA. A comprehensive literature search was conducted via PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and African Journal Online databases from February 25, 2025, to March 20, 2025. The pooled prevalence of PTLD was estimated using a random-effects model. Due to the lack of reports on adjusted odds ratios (aORs), the associated factors were analyzed using crude odds ratios (ORs).ResultsA total of 21 studies, consisting of 4,463 participants, were included. The overall pooled prevalence of PTLD in SSA was 43.26% (95% CI: 34.17%–52.34%). The key Factors significantly associated with PTLD included: female sex (OR: 1.57, 95% CI: 1.16, 2.11), smoking (OR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.09, 2.46), Presence of cough (OR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.03, 2.9) and fibrotic pattern (OR:3.94 (95% CI: 1.96, 7.92).ConclusionNearly half of prior TB patients in SSA develop PTLD. Being female, smoking, fibrosis, and post-treatment cough were key factors associated with PTLD. To effectively manage PTLD in SSA, it is important to implement targeted interventions for high-risk groups, strengthen screening and chronic care services, enhance healthcare system capacity, ensure equity in health resources and integrate PTLD management into national TB control programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03887-4.Keywords: Prevalence, Post-tuberculosis lung disease, Risk factors, Respiratory disease, Sub-Saharan Africa, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

Post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) is a chronic respiratory abnormality that persists in individuals after microbiological cure of pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), and can be attributed at least in part to previous TB [1]. PTLD encompasses a range of structural, functional, and symptomatic pulmonary impairments, including airflow obstruction, bronchiectasis, fibrosis, and reduced quality of life [2, 3].

Globally, TB has led to approximately 1.25 million deaths in 2024, with 10.8 million new cases reported in the same year [4]. Although effective TB treatment is widely available through national TB programs, with reported treatment success rates of 88% for drug-susceptible TB and 68% for multidrug-resistant TB in 2022. The long-term consequences of TB, including post-treatment lung disease are increasingly recognized [4]. In response to this growing concern, the first International Post-Tuberculosis Symposium (2019) defined PTLD as “an evidence of chronic respiratory abnormality, with or without symptoms, attributable at least in part to previous tuberculosis"[5]. PTLD is a major public health challenge, particularly in SSA, where the burden of TB remains high [1, 6, 7]. An estimated 4.5–5.8% of the population in the region is affected by chronic respiratory disease [4, 8, 9].

Only a few studies have reported the global burden of PTLD [8]. A recent systematic review [10] reported a high burden of PTLD among TB survivors in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). Global reviews have assessed PTLD burden, and only a few have aggregated findings by region, and none to date have comprehensively quantified associated factors exclusively within SSA [7]. Additionally, a systematic review [9] published in 2015 revealed that individuals with prior TB were approximately three times more likely to develop chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) than those without a TB history. Existing studies in SSA vary widely in their definitions, methods, populations, health systems, and resources, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions about PTLD in the region. SSA is that the region experiences a high TB burden and continues to face persistent challenges, including limited access to healthcare resources, diagnostic constraints, a fragile health system, and a high prevalence of HIV co-infection. Moreover, there is a considerable gap in evidence on the associated factors of PTLD, such as age, HIV co-infection, TB severity, smoking, nutritional status, delayed diagnosis, and clinical characteristics in the region. Recent TB treatment guidelines acknowledge the importance of evaluating the long-term health impacts of pulmonary TB [2]. Nevertheless, the lack of targeted studies involving SSA populations continues to hinder the development of context-specific policies, clinical guidelines, and interventions [11, 12].

In response to the growing recognition of PTLD as a significant but underexplored public health issue in SSA, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to synthesize available evidence to estimate the pooled prevalence of PTLD and identify associated factors among individuals previously treated for TB in the region. By addressing current evidence gaps, this study seeks to generate reliable, context-specific data that will inform policymakers in developing clinical guidelines, enhancing post-TB care strategies, and supporting evidence-based health policy development in SSA.

Method

A preliminary search was conducted in the PROSPERO database and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects (DARE) to identify any existing or ongoing systematic reviews related to the prevalence and associated factors of PTLD, to avoid duplication and ensure the novelty of the study. We performed a systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines 2020 [13]. The study protocol has been registered on PROSPERO, ID CRD42025636215.

Eligibility criteria

PTLD has gained increasing recognition as a significant post-treatment sequela of TB since 2015 [5]. Therefore, we included studies published from January 2015 onwards to ensure consistency with updated TB diagnostic standards and treatment guidelines following the launch of the WHO End TB Strategy [14]. Studies before 2015 were excluded due to differences in clinical definitions and care practices. Our study applies to TB survivors only. Observational studies such as cross-sectional, cohort, and case–control study designs were included. To ensure accessibility and accurate interpretation, only studies published in English were considered. Studies focused exclusively on extra-pulmonary TB without reference to PTLD, reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts lacking primary data were excluded. Additionally, any studies conducted before 2015 and outside of SSA were excluded to maintain the geographical, current TB management alignment and time scope of the review.

Literature search and study selection

The literature search was performed via PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and African Journal Online databases, from February 25, 2025, to March 20, 2025. Boolean operators such as “OR” and “AND” were used with search terms such as “post-tuberculosis lung disease,” “PTLD,” “chronic respiratory disease after TB”, “sub-Saharan Africa,” and “factors associated with PTLD”. The full search for each database is available in the supplementary file (S 1). The identified research articles were screened to ensure that all relevant studies were included. The literature was downloaded and imported into Mendeley reference software to maintain and manage citations and facilitate the review process. Two reviewers (ATG and YAF) reviewed the articles, and a third reviewer (MTB) resolved any discrepancies. Research articles were subsequently screened based on their titles and abstracts to exclude studies that were irrelevant to the topic, such as those not addressing PTLD. The titles and abstracts of the selected studies were reviewed for full text by two independent authors (ATG, MTB), and discrepancies were resolved by the third author (HTA) to decide whether the study was included (S 1).

Selection of studies for the pooled analysis of associated factors

Studies included for the pooled estimate of associated factors with PTLD were based on their frequency of reporting the independent variable across studies, biological plausibility, and relevance to the conceptual framework of PTLD. We initially extracted all potentially relevant variables, including age, history of prior TB, underweight status, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), duration of symptoms, indoor pollution and prolonged TB treatment. Due to inconsistencies in variable definitions, such as differing age categorizations and limited availability of data, only those factors reported in at least two or more primary studies with comparable definitions were included in the meta-analysis. Variables like age and treatment greater than six months were narratively synthesized, where pooling was not feasible due to different categorization and a lack of data across studies.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was the prevalence of PTLD among individuals with a history of TB. This was calculated by dividing the number of participants diagnosed with PTLD by the total number of individuals with prior TB. The secondary outcome focused on the factors associated with PTLD, which were assessed via odds ratios (ORs) derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables, as reported in the original studies.

Data extraction

The data extraction was conducted by MYT and YAF via a data extraction tool that consists of demographic information, including Author, year of publication, country, income status, and study setting. Independent variables, such as participant residence, age, sex, HIV status and nutritional status, were also extracted. Smoking, severity markers of TB such as fibrosis and cough, overall PTLD prevalence, spirometry findings, symptoms, and radiologic findings were extracted via a format created in a Microsoft Excel sheet as shown in the supplementary file (S2). Inconsistency due to data discrepancy was resolved by discussion with the reviewers and the third reviewer (HAT). The overall PTLD prevalence data were extracted based on symptoms, spirometry, and radiologic findings as a benchmark (S 2).

Risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included articles was assessed via the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tool [18]. This scale has 9 items with possible responses of"Yes","No", and"Unclear". The quality score presented in (Table 1) was determined by considering"Yes"as one point and"No"and"Unclear"as zero points, meaning that the higher the score is, the lower the risk of bias. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and by consulting a third reviewer to reach consensus.

Statistical analysis

The extracted data were cleaned and exported to STATA version 17.0 for analysis. A random-effects model was used to calculate the pooled prevalence and related factors of PTLD. The pooled prevalence of post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) and its 95% confidence interval, along with the pooled associations between PTLD and its related factors, were estimated. Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, which measures the percentage of variation due to heterogeneity rather than chance. I2 values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% represent no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on the WHO sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) region, World Bank income classification, study setting, and study site/residency. Meta-regression was conducted to identify statistically significant heterogeneity based on sample size and PTLD diagnostic method (spirometry, symptomatic, and radiologic abnormality). Publication bias was evaluated using both visual inspection of funnel plots and formal statistical tests, including Egger’s and Begg’s tests, with a significance level set at 5%.

Results

Selection of studies

A total of 2487 studies were identified by the search. Following the removal of 250 duplicate articles, 2237 articles were eligible for title and abstract screening. Reviewing titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of 1960 articles. After the full texts were assessed, 247 studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. 30 studies were reviewed for further quality assessment, and 9 studies were not sufficient to be included in the final analysis. Finally, 21 studies were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis of PTLD prevalence [15–35] (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews

Characteristics of the included studies

This study consists of 4463 individuals, as shown in (Table 1). The sample size ranges from 18 in Tanzania and Uganda [20, 30] to 798 reported from Uganda [18]. Fifteen studies were cross-sectional [15–21, 23, 25–28, 30, 31, 33]. Four studies were prospective cohort studies [16, 20, 22, 29, 35], and the other two studies were longitudinal [32] and comparative [24] studies by study design. In the included studies, participants'ages were distributed within all age groups, with a median age range between 8.9 and 52 years. We observed that the majority of the study participants were adults and adolescents. Sex distribution shows a slight predominance of males were observed in the study population, with approximately 52% male and 48% female participants.

Pooled prevalence of post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) in sub-Saharan Africa

The average pooled prevalence of PTLD from 21 studies conducted in SSA was 43.26% (95% CI: 34.17–52.34; I2 = 97.7%, p < 0.001) [15–35]. To check for underlying heterogeneity, meta-regression models were done by using sample size and PTLD diagnostic method (spiro-metric, symptomatic and radiologic findings), but there was no statistically significant underlying heterogeneity (p-value = 0.96), (P-value = 0.924), (P-value = 0.939) and (P-value = 0.913) respectively as shown (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed both graphically by a funnel plot (Fig. 3) and statistically by Egger’s (p = 0.9076) and Begg’s (p = 0.6077) tests. The results indicated no evidence of publication bias.

Risk of bias

More than 85% of the included studies met key quality criteria, including adequate sample sizes, clearly described study populations and settings, the use of validated and comprehensive methods for PTLD identification, and reliable outcome measurements.

Sub-group analysis

The pooled prevalence estimates include studies that used different definitions of PTLD. To account for this variability, subgroup analyses were conducted based on diagnostic methods specifically (spirometry, imaging, symptoms), geographical region within sub-Saharan Africa, study setting, and participant residency. The pooled prevalence of spiro-metric findings was 41.01% (95% CI: 31.58–50.45; I2 2: 96% p < 0.001) [16, 17, 20–31, 34, 35]. The pooled estimates of PTLD based on symptoms were 41.77% (95% CI: 29.25–54.28; I2 2: 97.8% p < 0,001) [16, 18, 19, 22–25, 28, 29, 31–33]. The pooled estimates of radiologic findings of PTLD were 60.92% (95% CI: 38.57–83.27; I2 2: 99.2% p < 0.001) [15, 16, 18, 23, 31, 32, 35] (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

PTLD is a major public health challenge, particularly in SSA, where the burden of TB remains high. The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of PTLD was 43.26% in SSA. These finding indicates that approximately 44 out of every 100 patients who complete TB treatment are likely to develop post-TB lung sequelae. This finding underscores the long-term pulmonary consequences faced by TB survivors and is consistent with previous global estimates, which suggest that over 40% of individuals recovering from pulmonary TB will develop chronic respiratory sequelae [9, 36]. The pooled estimate represents an average across heterogeneous settings; regional variation reflects disparities in TB care and diagnostic capacity. This estimate also applies to TB survivors only, and the true burden of PTLD may be higher if fatal PTLD case were included. The findings of the current study align with a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) reported that the magnitude of PTLD was 42.7% [10]. However, our estimate is lower than that of a recent global study, which reported that approximately 59.1% of TB survivors experience PTLD [6]. This variation may be attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria (e.g., use of spirometry versus clinical or radiological assessments), population characteristics such as age distribution, HIV prevalence, and nutritional status, as well as disparities in socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and treatment quality. Additionally, variations in the timing of post-TB assessment, definitions of PTLD, and broader differences in health system capacity between global and SSA settings. The findings of this study are also consistent with those of clinical reviews, indicating that PTLD affects a broad range of respiratory functions, including obstructive lung disease, bronchiectasis, and pulmonary vascular damage [37]. It is estimated that up to half of TB survivors globally suffer from some form of PTLD [1]. The subgroup analysis based on the definition of PTLD using spirometric findings, symptoms, or radiologic abnormalities showed that the prevalence of PTLD was highest when defined by radiologic abnormalities, compared to spirometric and symptomatic criteria. This highlights the importance of comprehensive assessments that include both spirometry and symptom evaluations to capture the full extent of PTLD. Pooled prevalence of PTLD by radiologic abnormalities was 60.92%, which aligns with findings from other meta-analyses that reported a prevalence of 64.6% [10] but was lower than that reported in a review that noted radiologic abnormalities in up to 86% of patients [38]. The greater prevalence of radiologic findings than spirometry-based impairments highlights the importance of thorough imaging in post-TB care, as structural damage may be present even when spirometric tests appear normal.

In this review, there were marked differences in the prevalence of PTLD across SSA. The highest prevalence was observed in Central Africa (50.87%), whereas the lowest was reported in West Africa (38.05%), which aligns with previous findings that regional disparities in PTLD are often driven by variations in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and TB management quality across SSA regions [39]. Similarly, significantly higher PTLD prevalence was observed in TB clinic attendees (54.99%) than in hospital-based studies (38.12%) may reflect a more chronic disease trajectory among those engaged in follow-up TB services. Moreover, the finding that mixed rural–urban settings had a higher PTLD prevalence (53.66%) than did purely rural areas (40.12%) suggests that environmental and occupational exposures are highest in peri-urban zones [37].

Associated factors of post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD)

PTLD is affected by multiple factors, including older age, as reported in many studies [31, 37]. Prior TB disease severity, recurrent TB, smoking, malnutrition, and HIV co-infection. Identifying these factors is essential for improving post-TB care in high-burden settings. The current systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that being female sex were 1.57 times more likely to develop PTLD than males were. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Zimbabwe and South Africa, suggesting that sex specific factors may influence disease severity and outcomes [10, 29, 40, 41]. Global studies often reported a greater incidence of TB among men than among women, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1 in many regions [42–44]. However, women may experience worse long-term outcomes, such as PTLD or extra-pulmonary manifestations, potentially due to delayed diagnosis, social stigma, or biological differences in immune responses [40, 41].

The pooled analysis revealed that patients classified as underweight were 2.19 times more likely to develop PTLD than their normal-weight counterparts. This finding underscores the impact of nutritional status on the long-term respiratory health of individuals who have previously suffered from TB. Underweight individuals often experience compromised immune function, which can hinder recovery from TB and increase susceptibility to chronic lung conditions such as PTLD. The severity of undernutrition has been linked to poorer treatment outcomes in TB patients. Underweight patients are at increased risk of treatment failure and relapse [45, 46]. For example, one study conducted in Ethiopia indicated that underweight patients had a greater prevalence of severe symptoms and complications during TB treatment, which was associated with increased risks of long-term respiratory problems [45].

The other important factor in this study was cigarette smoking, which indicates that smokers were 1.64 times more likely to develop PTLD than non-smokers were. This underscores the importance of including smoking cessation interventions in TB control practices, especially in reducing post-TB treatment complications. For example, a study conducted at 18 TB clinics in Hong Kong reported that both current and ex-smokers were significantly less likely to achieve treatment completion and had higher rates of treatment failure, with smoking contributing to approximately 16.7% of unsuccessful treatment outcomes attributed to defaulting on treatment and mortality among ex-smokers [47]. Furthermore, smoking is associated with a slower bacteriological response during TB treatment, which means that smokers are more likely to remain sputum-positive after two months of therapy. This delayed response not only increases the risk of transmission but also raises concerns about the persistence of MTB in the lungs, leading to potential complications like PTLD [48, 49]. The other factor assessed in this study was the association between HIV and PTLD. MTB infection is known to be strongly associated with HIV, and individuals living with HIV are often hypothesized to be more susceptible to developing PTLD. However, findings from the current study indicate that the pooled association between HIV status and PTLD was not statistically significant, with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.75. This suggests that while HIV is a known risk factor for TB, its impact on the development of PTLD may not be as pronounced as previously thought. Interestingly, other studies also suggest that individuals living with HIV may experience less severe forms of PTLD than their HIV-negative counterparts do [50]. This could be attributed to factors such as immunosuppression leading to less cavitary disease at the time of TB diagnosis, which may result in lower rates of lung damage [22]. Clinical presentations such as cough and fibrotic patterns are among the known features associated with PTLD. The results from the present study indicate that patients with a history of TB who present with a cough after completing TB treatment were 1.73 times more likely to develop PTLD. This association increases the need for post-treatment follow-up and structured lung health assessments, particularly in resource-limited settings where PTLD often remains undiagnosed or misclassified [5, 22]. Fibrotic patterns observed on chest radiographs or CT scans are common among TB survivors and are strongly associated with irreversible lung tissue damage, leading to reduced lung function and diminished quality of life. In this study, individuals with fibrotic changes were 3.94 times more likely to develop PTLD compared to those without. Similarly, a prospective cohort study from Malawi reported that fibrotic patterns were linked to persistent respiratory symptoms and impaired spirometry measurements among TB survivors. However, fibrosis likely reflects the severity of the initial TB infection rather than serving as an independent risk factor for PTLD. It may act as a mediator in the causal pathway between severe TB and long-term pulmonary impairment. Future studies should aim to differentiate pre-existing fibrosis from fibrosis resulting from post-TB progression to better explain its role in the development of PTLD [5, 22, 36].

One study from the DR Congo reported that anti-TB treatment for more than 6 months was significantly associated with PTLD [19]. This might be due to delayed bacterial clearance and prolonged inflammation, which can lead to irreversible lung damage. Extended treatment may also indicate more severe or drug-resistant TB, which is independently linked to a greater risk of structural lung complications such as fibrosis and bronchiectasis [51]. The persistent burden of PTLD calls for integrated care models that extend beyond microbiological cure, emphasizing pulmonary rehabilitation, spirometry-based evaluations, and long-term symptom monitoring. Moreover, further research is needed to define standardized diagnostic criteria and evidence-based interventions for PTLD.

Implications for evidence-informed policy and clinical practice

Given the high prevalence of PTLD in SSA, it is essential to integrate PTLD management into existing TB control programs. National TB programs should expand their focus beyond microbiological cure to include post-treatment rehabilitation aimed at improving the long-term health and quality of life of TB survivors. Interventions should be tailored to local resource availability, with priority given to high-burden regions and areas with existing TB program infrastructure. Improving access to diagnostic tools and establishing clear referral pathways is crucial for the timely diagnosis and management of PTLD. This includes implementing routine screening protocols such as symptom-based assessments and spirometry, where feasible for high-risk groups, including individuals with severe TB or radiologic abnormalities [52, 53]. Collaborative efforts among TB programs, pulmonologists, researchers, and community health organizations are vital to developing context-specific and sustainable care models [53].

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis employed a comprehensive search of multiple databases to identify both published and unpublished PTLD studies. Eligible studies were selected through rigorous quality assessment, and key associated factors were analyzed. Strengths include the use of subgroup analyses and meta-regression to explore heterogeneity. However, several limitations exist. Pediatric populations were under-represented, as few studies included children. To align with updated clinical standards, studies published before 2015 were excluded, improving relevance but potentially introducing bias by omitting older evidence. The review focused only on TB survivors, excluding fatal cases that may have involved undiagnosed PTLD, possibly underestimating its true burden. Crude odds ratios were used due to the lack of adjusted estimates in many studies, which limits causal interpretation. Additionally, inconsistent age classifications and missing data on treatment adherence prevented full assessment of their impact, despite their likely contribution to PTLD risk.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the high prevalence of PTLD among TB survivors in sub-Saharan Africa, with nearly half at risk of developing the condition. Key factors associated with PTLD include female sex, smoking, fibrosis, and post-treatment cough. Integrating routine PTLD screening, such as spirometry and imaging, and targeted interventions into national TB programs is essential. These interventions should be adapted to local resource availability, with priority given to high-burden regions with existing TB infrastructure. Strengthening health systems through improved diagnostic capacity, healthcare provider training, and community awareness is critical for developing evidence-based policies that address the broader impact of TB. The inconsistent association between HIV and PTLD observed across studies underscores the need for further research into underlying mechanisms and potential effect modifiers. Future studies should also distinguish between pre-existing pulmonary fibrosis and fibrosis secondary to TB, to enhance diagnostic precision and inform targeted care strategies.
