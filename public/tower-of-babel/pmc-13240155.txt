Limitations of Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis Diagnostic Scoring in a Patient With Cirrhosis on Ursodiol: A Case Report
Authors: Michelle Ngo, Derek Ochi, Denis M. Dwyre, William Ridgway
Published: 2026

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by dysregulated cytotoxic T cells and macrophages, causing multiorgan dysfunction. 1 Delayed recognition increases mortality. 2 , 3 Diagnostic frameworks, including the HLH‐2004 criteria (≥5 of 8 features) developed in pediatric populations, and H‐score (≥169 threshold) (Supplemental Table S1), may perform poorly in adults with comorbidities. 2 , 3 , 4 We present secondary HLH in a patient with primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)–related cirrhosis on ursodiol, in whom masked diagnostic features led to delayed therapy.

Case report

The patient, a 74‐year‐old man with PBC cirrhosis on ursodiol and atrial fibrillation on apixaban presented with 6 months of fevers to 102°F, 30‐pound weight loss, dyspnea with hemoptysis, and a positron emission tomography–avid parotid mass pending outpatient biopsy. On arrival, he was hypotensive (70/50 mm Hg) and with rapid ventricular response, requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission for distributive shock and intubation after bronchoscopy.

Day 1 laboratory tests revealed new pancytopenia (white blood cell count 3.1 K/μL, hemoglobin 7.0 g/dL, and platelet count 58 K/μL), ferritin 5,867 ng/mL, creatinine 1.24 mg/dL, and bilirubin 1.9 mg/d. Aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) levels were normal; alkaline phosphatase was mildly elevated (169 U/L) but improved on ursodiol. Infectious workup results (tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction, Epstein‐Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and HIV) were negative. Imaging demonstrated pulmonary edema, cirrhosis with portal hypertension, and no splenomegaly. He developed hypertriglyceridemia (181 mg/dL, day 5), declining fibrinogen (59 mg/dL, day 11), lactate dehydrogenase 687 U/L, low haptoglobin (<15 mg), and elevated soluble interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) receptor (10,980 pg/mL, day 8). CXCL9 was elevated at 4,377 pg/mL posthumously.

By day 6, he met three HLH‐2004 criteria (fever, cytopenias, and hyperferritinemia) with an H‐score of 162, narrowly below the threshold. 4 Given ongoing deterioration without infectious source, dexamethasone was initiated on day 6. Bone marrow biopsy on day 9 (Figure 1) demonstrated hemophagocytosis, increasing the H‐score to 216, and anakinra was initiated. Despite therapy, he developed refractory shock and died on day 11.

Differential diagnosis

Considerations included HLH/MAS (Macrophage Activation Syndrome), hematologic malignancy (including lymphoma involving the parotid mass), severe infection, and decompensated cirrhosis. Infectious study results were negative. Malignancy could not be confirmed.

Management and outcome

Management included broad‐spectrum antibiotics, ICU support, dexamethasone, and anakinra. Hyperinflammation progressed with neurologic deterioration and fatal shock.

Discussion

This case highlights the limitations of HLH diagnostic tools in adults with chronic liver disease. Both the HLH‐2004 criteria and H‐score rely on clinical and laboratory features that may be altered by comorbidities or medications. HLH is classically associated with transaminitis, cytopenias, splenomegaly, and markers of hyperinflammation. However, in this patient, several features were absent or misleading early in the course.

Ursodiol likely blunted hepatic markers of inflammation. Despite severe immune activation (markedly elevated soluble IL‐2 receptor and CXCL9), AST/ALT remained normal and alkaline phosphatase was near baseline. 1 This prevented accrual of H‐score points and contributed to underestimation of disease probability. Cirrhosis further confounded interpretation. Cytopenias in cirrhosis are often attributed to hypersplenism, which this patient lacked. Instead, the abrupt onset and progression of pancytopenia paralleled systemic inflammation, suggesting HLH over cirrhosis‐related mechanisms. His portal hypertension likely reflected chronic architectural disease rather than an acute driver. This underscores how treatment of liver disease can blunt hepatic markers of inflammation and HLH‐related cytopenias can be misattributed to cirrhosis.

By day 6, the H‐score narrowly missed the diagnostic threshold. The absence of AST elevation and splenomegaly accounted for lost points. Either would have increased the score above 169. Although these tools estimate probability, rigid adherence to thresholds in complex patients may delay treatment. 3

Delays in HLH‐directed therapy are associated with worse outcomes. In this case, anakinra was initiated on day 9 after progression to advanced shock. Although causality cannot be established, earlier therapy may have altered the trajectory. 2

The underlying trigger remains uncertain, with possibilities including occult lymphoproliferative disease, undetected infection, or autoimmune activation from PBC. Notably, updated adult‐focused HLH guidance (HLH‐2024), published after this hospitalization, would not have altered early classification, underscoring persistent gaps in diagnostic tools for complex patients. 5

Conclusions

This case underscores the limitations of standardized HLH scoring systems in adults with treated chronic liver disease. Ursodiol and cirrhosis masked key diagnostic features, resulting in deceptively low early scores despite evolving hyperinflammation. Reliance on scoring thresholds delayed escalation of immunosuppression. Clinicians should recognize how medications and comorbid liver disease can distort HLH scoring and consider earlier empirical treatment when clinical suspicion remains high.
