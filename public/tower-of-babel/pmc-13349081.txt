Factors influencing the accuracy of clinical decision-making in dental trauma: knowledge, experience, and educational attitudes among medical students
Authors: Tamara Pawlaczyk-Kamieńska, Olesya Marushko, Olena Marushko, Patrycja Kamieńska, Bartosz Jastrzębski, Weronika Kamieńska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic dental injuries (TDI) require prompt and appropriate first aid; however, non-dental healthcare professionals often lack sufficient knowledge to manage such cases effectively. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of clinical decision-making among medical students in dental trauma scenarios and to identify factors influencing their performance, including knowledge, experience, and educational attitudes.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional survey of 291 medical students assessed knowledge, decision-making accuracy, and educational attitudes toward dental trauma management using a structured questionnaire.ResultsOverall, clinical decision-making accuracy was limited, particularly for tooth avulsion. Only 9.6% of participants selected the correct management for avulsion, compared to 40.9% for crown fractures. General avulsion knowledge was significantly associated with decision accuracy (ρ = 0.31, p < 0.001) and emerged as the only significant predictor in regression analysis (B = 0.096, 95% CI: 0.039–0.153, p = 0.001). Domain-specific knowledge, including replantation and storage, was not independently associated with decision accuracy. Participation in formal dental trauma training was weakly but significantly correlated with better performance (ρ = 0.125, p = 0.034). Despite low self-assessed knowledge (mean = 1.64/5), students reported a high need for education (mean = 3.87/5) and willingness to participate in training (mean = 4.00/5).ConclusionsMedical students demonstrated limited preparedness for managing traumatic dental injuries, particularly in time-sensitive avulsion cases. General knowledge of avulsion was associated with more accurate decision-making, while other factors showed limited influence. These findings suggest that integrating structured, guideline-based dental trauma education into medical curricula may help improve preparedness, although further multicenter research is needed to confirm these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09447-6.Keywords: Dental trauma knowledge, Emergency management, Medical education, Tooth avulsion, Traumatic dental injuries

Introduction

Traumatic dental injuries (TDI) represent a significant public health challenge, affecting individuals worldwide, with a particularly high prevalence among children and adolescents [1, 2]. These injuries, which include fractures, luxations, and avulsions of teeth, often occur due to sports-related accidents, falls, or vehicular collisions [3, 4]. The management of TDI requires immediate and appropriate intervention, as delays or improper first aid can lead to complications such as pulp necrosis, root resorption, and even tooth loss [3, 5, 6]. The American Dental Association (ADA) defines dental emergencies as potentially life-threatening or hazardous conditions that require immediate treatment to save a tooth, stop ongoing tissue bleeding, or alleviate severe pain [7]. Despite the urgency of these cases, studies consistently report that non-dental healthcare professionals, including emergency physicians and medical students, often lack sufficient knowledge to provide adequate initial care [1, 8].

The role of medical professionals in managing dental trauma is critical, as patients with TDI frequently present to emergency departments rather than dental clinics [1]. However, research indicates that medical curriculum often inadequately covers dental emergencies, leaving future physicians unprepared to handle such cases [9, 10]. The systematic review by Tewari et al. (2021) found that fewer than 40% of non-dental healthcare providers had received formal training in dental trauma management, and even fewer were aware of evidence-based protocols for tooth avulsion or fracture stabilization. Similarly, studies among medical students revealed significant knowledge gaps, with many unable to correctly identify storage media for avulsed teeth or recognize the time-sensitive nature of replantation [8, 10].

The consequences of inadequate initial management are well-documented. Avulsed permanent teeth have the highest chance of survival if replanted within 30 min, yet many medical professionals are unaware of this critical window [6]. Additionally, improper handling, such as holding the tooth by the root or using non-physiological storage solutions (e.g., water or dry gauze), can further reduce the likelihood of successful treatment [5]. These shortcomings highlight a pressing need for improved dental traumatology education for medical students, who will inevitably encounter such cases in their careers [9].

Despite these challenges, there is evidence that targeted training can significantly improve knowledge and confidence in managing dental trauma. Educational interventions, such as workshops or digital resources (e.g., the International Association of Dental Traumatology’s Tooth SOS app), have proven effective in enhancing preparedness among non-dental professionals [9–11]. However, such initiatives remain inconsistently implemented in medical education systems worldwide.

This study aims to assess the knowledge of fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year medical students regarding the emergency management of TDI, with particular focus on tooth avulsion, tooth fracture stabilization, and appropriate first-aid protocols. By identifying gaps in training, the research aims to support the inclusion of structured dental trauma education in medical curriculum, thereby improving patient care in emergency settings. Additionally, the study evaluates the accuracy of students’ clinical decisions in hypothetical dental-injury scenarios and examines factors influencing their performance, including theoretical knowledge, prior personal experience with oral injuries, and educational attitudes.

Methods and materials

This study employed a questionnaire-based cross-sectional design to evaluate medical students’ knowledge, attitudes, and decision-making competencies regarding dental trauma management. The survey questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. The study was conducted among students at the Medical Faculty of Poznan University of Medical Sciences. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Completing and submitting the questionnaire implied informed consent. No written consent was obtained due to the study's anonymous design. The inclusion criteria were: (1) enrollment in the fourth, fifth, or sixth year of medical studies; (2) voluntary consent to participate; and (3) completion of all questions necessary for analysis. The exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of consent to participate; (2) submission of incomplete questionnaires (missing responses in sections on objective knowledge or self-assessment); and (3) participation in the pilot phase of the survey. The sample size was determined based on practical considerations, specifically the total number of eligible medical students in their fourth, fifth, and sixth years who were available during the study period. All students who met the inclusion criteria and were present during scheduled classes were invited to participate. The exact number of students absent during data collection sessions was not recorded; therefore, the response rate was estimated based on available class attendance records. A statistical power calculation was not conducted a priori. This limitation is acknowledged, especially regarding the interpretation of non-significant results.

Before the main study, a pilot evaluation was conducted with 15 medical students to assess the clarity of the questions and the adequacy of the response scales. Based on their feedback, minor editorial modifications were made. Data from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis. Prior to the initiation of the study, the Bioethics Committee of the Poznan University of Medical Sciences issued a formal confirmation that the study did not qualify as a medical experiment and granted a waiver of ethical approval. This decision was based on the anonymous, voluntary nature of participation, the use of a non-invasive survey methodology, and the minimal risk to participants.

A proprietary, anonymous, mixed-mode questionnaire was utilized, featuring multiple-choice, scenario-based, and Likert-scale items. The questionnaire consisted of 12 questions divided into three sections (see Appendix 1). Correct answers were determined according to the current guidelines of the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) [12].

Part I – Experience (4 items)

This section included closed-ended (yes/no) questions regarding participants’ previous involvement in dental trauma classes and experience of witnessing an orofacial injury; those who had witnessed an injury were additionally asked whether they felt they knew how to respond appropriately and whether they had attempted to provide first aid.

Part II – Educational Attitudes (3 items)

This section presented statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 – strongly disagree, 5 – strongly agree). The statements pertained to participants’ self-assessment of knowledge, the perceived need for education on dental trauma, and their willingness to engage in dedicated training. The Educational Attitude Index was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the three Likert-scale items, with equal weighting (range: 1–5).

Part III – Clinical Decision Accuracy and Practical Knowledge (5 questions)

This section included two scenario-based questions regarding the management of crown fractures and avulsions of permanent teeth. Additionally, there were three single-choice questions that evaluated crucial aspects of practical knowledge, including optimal timing for replantation, management of contaminated teeth, and the best storage medium for avulsed teeth. Responses in Part III were scored as follows: each correct answer according to the 2020 IADT guidelines received 1 point, while incorrect answers or responses of "I do not know" received 0 points. Clinical decision accuracy was measured by the total number of correct responses to the two scenario-based questions, with a possible score of 0–2. The practical knowledge score was calculated by summing the correct answers to the three single-choice questions, yielding a potential score of 0 to 3 points. For domain-specific analyses, avulsion-related knowledge was further categorized. "Replantation knowledge" included correct answers on both the optimal timing of replantation and the appropriate handling and cleaning of a contaminated avulsed tooth, yielding a score of 0 to 2 points. "Storage knowledge" referred to the correct identification of suitable temporary storage media for an avulsed tooth, with a possible score of 0 to 1 point. Overall, general avulsion knowledge was considered the composite of these avulsion-related items, with a total score range of 0 to 3 points. Questionnaires that had missing responses in Part III were excluded from the analysis, and no imputation procedures were used. Each item contributed to a single predefined construct and was not used across multiple scores, except where explicitly incorporated into composite indices. Descriptive results reported in the Results section were directly derived from these coded items, and consistency among questionnaire content, the coding scheme, and the reported outcomes was verified during data analysis.

Correct answers were defined according to the 2020 International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) guidelines [12]. In the crown fracture scenario, immediate referral was considered appropriate due to the need for prompt pulp assessment and restorative management. In the avulsion scenario, immediate replantation followed by referral was regarded as the optimal approach within the recommended extraoral dry-time window. For selected items, all clinically acceptable responses, such as using milk or saliva as storage media and rinsing contaminated teeth with saline or running water, were considered correct. This approach reflects real-world clinical decision-making rather than a single best-answer model.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, medians, and frequency counts, were calculated to characterize response distributions. Bivariate analyses using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (ρ) examined relationships between general and domain-specific knowledge (e.g., avulsion management), experiential factors (training attendance and injury exposure), educational attitudes, and the accuracy of clinical decisions. Nonparametric tests, such as the Kruskal–Wallis test, assessed decision accuracy across self-reported knowledge and experience levels, while chi-square tests evaluated associations between categorical variables. The dependent variable used in the primary regression analyses was defined as the total clinical decision accuracy score, calculated as the sum of correct responses to the two vignette-based questions (crown fracture and avulsion), yielding a score from 0 to 2. In addition, scenario-specific analyses were conducted in which decision accuracy for the avulsion vignette was examined separately as a binary outcome (correct vs incorrect), to provide a more detailed assessment of domain-specific performance. Prior to model selection, the distributional properties of the dependent variable 'total correct decisions' (range 0–2) were assessed. The variable suggested approximately normal distribution (skewness = −0.23, kurtosis = −0.67) with adequate response frequencies across all categories (score 0: n = 58, 19.9%; score 1: n = 128, 44.0%; score 2: n = 105, 36.1%). Linear regression was selected as the primary analytical approach for three reasons: (1) the approximate normality of the outcome distribution supports the use of ordinary least squares estimation; (2) with a sample size of 291, the central limit theorem ensures robustness of parameter estimates even with modest deviations from normality; and (3) linear regression provides directly interpretable coefficients that facilitate educational interpretation regarding the marginal effects of knowledge domains on decision accuracy.

Post-estimation diagnostics were performed to verify model assumptions. Residuals were approximately normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk W = 0.98, p =0.12) and homoscedastic, as confirmed by visual inspection of residual-versus-fitted plots. The mean residual was effectively zero (−1.2e-16), satisfying classical linear regression assumptions. As a sensitivity analysis, ordinal logistic regression was also estimated to confirm that findings were not dependent on the choice of modeling approach.

A multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of decision accuracy. A full model including all predictors was first estimated, followed by a reduced model including only statistically significant variables. Given the hierarchical relationship between composite and domain-specific knowledge variables, these predictors were included simultaneously to explore their relative contributions to decision-making accuracy. Multicollinearity diagnostics were performed to ensure model stability. Covariates included year of study, prior training exposure, and a composite Educational Attitude Index derived from three Likert items. Results with p <0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26, and model assumptions were validated. Effect sizes were reported for correlations (ρ), ANOVA (η2), and regression (β coefficients).

Results

The total number of students formally enrolled in the fourth, fifth, and sixth years during the study period was not directly accessible to the research team. Therefore, all students present during scheduled classes were invited to participate. Ultimately, 291 medical students completed the questionnaire, including 43 fourth-year students (14.8%), 116 fifth-year students (39.9%), and 132 sixth-year students (45.4%). Only 10.7% (n = 31) reported previous participation in classes dedicated to dental trauma management. Furthermore, 40.5% (n = 118) had witnessed an orofacial injury; among these, 23% stated that they knew how to respond appropriately, and 41.8% reported attempting to provide first aid.

Students’ self-assessed knowledge of dental trauma was generally low, with a mean score of 1.64 on a 5-point Likert scale. In contrast, the perceived need for additional education was high (mean = 3.87), as was their willingness to participate in training (mean = 4.00). The composite Educational Attitude Index, derived from these three variables, averaged 3.17 (IQR: 2.83–3.67), indicating a moderate overall educational attitude. Most respondents fell within the low or medium attitude categories, while only 13% suggested a high educational attitude. These findings are summarized in Table 2 and visually presented in Fig. 1.

Knowledge related to permanent tooth avulsion was evaluated across three domains: general management, replantation procedures, and storage methods. Significant positive correlations were observed between general knowledge and several aspects of correct clinical decision-making, including appropriate handling of an avulsed tooth (ρ = 0.24, p <0.001), timely replantation within 60 min (ρ = 0.20, p <0.001), proper cleaning of a contaminated tooth (ρ = 0.20, p <0.001), and selection of suitable storage media such as milk or saliva (ρ = 0.16, p =0.007). Knowledge of replantation procedures demonstrated weaker, though still significant, correlations—most notably with correct cleaning practices (ρ = 0.13, p =0.025). In contrast, knowledge of appropriate storage methods showed moderate associations with proper immediate management (ρ = 0.20, p <0.001), the correct selection of a storage medium (ρ = 0.20, p <0.001), and overall decision accuracy (ρ = 0.22, p <0.001). Among the analyzed domains, general avulsion knowledge showed the strongest association with clinical decision accuracy (ρ = 0.31, p <0.001), followed by storage (ρ = 0.22, p <0.001) and replantation knowledge (ρ = 0.13, p =0.025).

Two linear regression models were conducted to examine whether knowledge domains predicted the accuracy of clinical decisions. In the primary regression model, the dependent variable was the total clinical decision accuracy score (range 0–2), incorporating both crown fracture and avulsion scenarios. Additional analyses focusing specifically on the avulsion vignette yielded consistent results, confirming the robustness of the findings.

The model included three predictors: general avulsion knowledge, replantation knowledge, and storage knowledge. Given that general avulsion knowledge is a composite measure derived from the latter two domains, the regression results should be interpreted as reflecting both overall knowledge and its domain-specific components. General avulsion knowledge emerged as the only significant predictor (B = 0.096, 95% CI: 0.039–0.153, p =0.001), indicating that each one-point increase in general knowledge was associated with an approximate 0.1-point increase in the number of correct decisions. Neither replantation knowledge (B = −0.024, 95% CI: −0.069–0.021, p =0.300) nor storage knowledge (B = 0.042, 95% CI: −0.004–0.088, p =0.076) reached statistical significance. The overall model was significant (F (3,287) = 7.62, p <0.001) but explained a modest proportion of variance (R2 = 0.074, adjusted R2 = 0.064), suggesting that additional factors beyond the knowledge domains measured contribute to decision-making accuracy (Table 4).

For the crown fracture scenario, the dependent variable was a binary indicator of correct decision (immediate referral to dentist). Knowledge of crown fracture management was not a significant predictor (B = 0.003, 95% CI: −0.050–0.056, p =0.917), and the model demonstrated no predictive ability (R2 = 0.000, F (1,289) = 0.01, p =0.917) (Table 5).

As a sensitivity analysis, ordinal logistic regression was performed for the avulsion scenario. Results confirmed the linear model findings: general avulsion knowledge was significantly associated with higher odds of correct decisions (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.19–1.94, p <0.001), while replantation knowledge (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.70–1.10, p =0.262) and storage knowledge (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.97–1.60, p =0.082) were not significant. The consistency between linear and ordinal model outcomes supports the robustness of our conclusions.

Among the experiential and educational variables, only prior participation in formal classes on dental trauma demonstrated a significant association with more accurate decisions (ρ = 0.125, p =0.034). Other factors, including year of study, previous exposure to oral injury, perceived ability to respond, or prior attempts to provide assistance, were not significantly related to decision quality. However, a notable association was observed between knowledge and behavior: students who reported knowing what to do were significantly more likely to attempt providing first aid (χ2 = 6.40, p =0.011).

To address potential concerns regarding model specification, we focused on the relationship between composite variables and their individual subdomains. The general avulsion knowledge score is a composite measure that combines individual domain-specific items related to replantation and storage knowledge. Although these variables are conceptually linked, we included them simultaneously in the regression model to evaluate their relative contributions to clinical decision accuracy.

Discussion

Dental trauma is a “silent epidemic” affecting individuals of all ages worldwide. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), over 1 billion people suffer from oro-dental trauma, with children being the most frequent victims; 20% of those under 12 years present traumatic injuries [13]. Accidental falls, often occurring during routine activities such as sports or playground use, constitute one of the most common and neglected public health challenges. Petti et al. (2018) [2] reported that TDIs affect 15.2% of permanent and 22.7% of primary teeth globally, underscoring the scale of the issue. Despite the high incidence, dental trauma remains an under-recognized aspect of global healthcare. Deficits in physician training exacerbate the situation: Steeves et al. (2023) [14] found that although 78% of emergency physicians manage TDI cases weekly, only 7% feel “very confident” in doing so. Similar deficiencies were noted by Çalışkan et al. (2021) [15], who reported that only 18% of physicians demonstrated adequate knowledge of tooth replantation.

This study revealed significant gaps in students' knowledge of dental trauma management, despite their strong desire for additional training. This discrepancy may reflect “unconscious incompetence,” in which learners lack the foundational understanding needed to accurately assess their competence. Self-assessed knowledge did not predict correct decision-making in fracture scenarios, suggesting subjective confidence is an unreliable indicator of preparedness. This emphasizes the need for objective, competency-based assessments, including simulation and skills-based evaluations, to better capture readiness in emergency dental care. Furthermore, students' general knowledge of tooth avulsion was associated with more accurate clinical decisions, as those with a stronger theoretical understanding more frequently selected appropriate handling, cleaning, replantation timing, and storage options, consistent with IADT recommendations [5, 6]. The inclusion of both composite and domain-specific knowledge variables in the same model should be interpreted with caution. While this approach allows exploration of their relative contributions, general avulsion knowledge likely captures shared variance across domains and may therefore act as a broader indicator of conceptual understanding rather than an entirely independent predictor.

A key finding was that formal education on dental trauma was the only experiential factor linked to improved decision-making. Passive exposure, such as witnessing trauma or attempting first aid, did not correlate with higher accuracy. This reinforces the need for structured educational interventions delivered before emergency medicine rotations. Previous studies have shown that even brief teaching sessions or digital modules can significantly improve confidence and accuracy in dental trauma management [8, 16–18]. Although medical students recognize the importance of timely referral, limited first-aid knowledge may impair their effectiveness in real clinical situations. These findings align with Tewari et al. (2021) [1], who found that fewer than 40% of non-dental healthcare providers had received formal training in dental trauma management, and even fewer were familiar with evidence-based protocols. In this study, only 10.7% of students had participated in formal courses, underscoring the need for systematic integration of dental trauma education across medical curriculum.

Time-sensitive management highlights the importance of such training. Antipovienė et al. (2021) [16] reported that only 1% of patients receive appropriate care within the first hour after trauma, contributing to high rates of necrosis in both permanent and primary teeth. Çalışkan et al. (2021) [15] found that 72% of physicians would not replant an avulsed tooth and many recommended incorrect cleaning methods. This study showed that many students were unaware of the narrow time window for successful replantation; however, those with higher general knowledge were more likely to select appropriate first-aid strategies, reinforcing the need to strengthen medical education in this area.

The results echo findings by Soğukpınar Önsüren & İpek (2021) [8], who reported significant gaps in first-aid protocols for dental trauma among medical students. While students recognized the importance of prompt referral for crown fractures, limited understanding of first-aid procedures may hinder their ability to provide essential care prior to specialist intervention. Additionally, there was a notable lack of correlation between self-reported knowledge and decision-making accuracy in tooth-fracture scenarios. This contrasts with Ivkošić et al. (2020) [10], who found a positive relationship, highlighting the need for more robust educational frameworks to ensure all aspects of dental trauma management are adequately understood.

Compared with studies conducted among medical students in other countries, the present study's findings reveal a consistent international pattern. Similar knowledge gaps regarding dental trauma management have been reported among medical students in Turkey, Croatia, Saudi Arabia, and Brazil, despite differences in healthcare systems and medical curricula. Across these settings, limited exposure to formal dental trauma education and low confidence in managing tooth avulsion have been repeatedly observed. These similarities suggest that the identified deficiencies are not country-specific but rather reflect a broader curricular gap in undergraduate medical education.

The findings of the present study suggest that students may overestimate their readiness due to a lack of real clinical exposure, stress, anxiety, or limited practice, all of which are known to impair decision-making under pressure. Finally, the modest predictive power of regression models indicates that clinical decision-making in dental trauma is multifactorial and may depend on broader competencies such as situational awareness, prior emergency medicine experience, and cognitive load. Future studies should examine these psychological and contextual factors, as they may represent modifiable determinants of clinical performance.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of the present study. First, the vignette scenarios were intentionally simplified and did not include specific clinical details such as extraoral dry time or the stage of root development. This may introduce a degree of interpretative ambiguity, as clinical decision-making in traumatic dental injuries can depend on these contextual factors.

Second, the reliance on self-reported data, particularly regarding students’ perceived knowledge, confidence, and willingness to participate in training, may introduce response bias. Participants may have unintentionally overestimated their knowledge or expressed greater enthusiasm for educational engagement than would translate into real clinical behavior. Self-assessment is inherently subjective and can be influenced by factors such as social desirability, limited insight, or lack of exposure to true emergency conditions, all of which may distort accuracy.

Although the study provides meaningful insight into theoretical knowledge and decision-making tendencies, it did not assess practical skills. This limitation is particularly relevant, as competence in dental trauma management requires not only theoretical knowledge but also psychomotor skills and the ability to act under time pressure, which cannot be adequately assessed solely through questionnaire-based methods. The ability to act under time pressure, correctly handle injured tissues, or perform appropriate first-aid procedures cannot be fully captured through hypothetical scenarios. Actual performance in emergency settings may differ significantly due to stress, uncertainty, or contextual limitations.

Regarding the statistical approach, it is important to acknowledge that the dependent variable (total clinical decision accuracy score, range 0–2) was a bounded count variable. While linear regression with bounded outcomes can produce biased estimates under certain conditions, diagnostic tests confirmed the approximate normality of the distribution and the residuals, supporting the appropriateness of this approach. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis using ordinal logistic regression yielded substantively identical results, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. The modest explanatory power of the avulsion model (R2 = 0.074) indicates that clinical decision-making in dental trauma is multifactorial and likely depends on broader competencies not captured in our knowledge assessment, such as situational awareness, prior emergency medicine experience, and cognitive load under pressure. Future studies should incorporate these psychological and contextual factors and hands-on simulations, skill-based workshops, or standardized-patient assessments to evaluate real clinical competence in dental trauma management.

An additional limitation is that internal consistency or reliability testing (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not performed for the Likert-scale items, and the questionnaire was not formally psychometrically validated. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design, which captures students’ knowledge and attitudes at a single point in time. This prevents evaluating how knowledge evolves throughout medical education or whether learning is retained over time. Longitudinal research could provide valuable insight into knowledge retention, skill development, and the long-term impact of educational interventions. Additionally, because this study was conducted at a single institution, generalizability is limited. Curriculum differences, variations in clinical exposure, and diverse educational priorities across institutions may produce different results. Multicenter or cross-national studies would strengthen external validity and provide a more representative understanding of medical students’ preparedness to manage traumatic dental injuries. Moreover, the exact number of students who were absent during the survey administration was not recorded. Consequently, the response rate was estimated based on attendance records instead of a predetermined sampling frame. This approach may affect the accuracy of representativeness estimates and introduces a potential risk of selection bias. An additional limitation is the lack of analysis of gender differences. Previous research suggests that gender may influence educational attitudes and confidence levels; therefore, future studies should explore this variable to better understand its role in clinical decision-making.

This study identifies gaps in dental trauma knowledge but does not investigate the most effective methods for delivering dental trauma education. Future research should evaluate optimal pedagogical approaches, including digital learning tools, simulation-based training, and interdisciplinary workshops. Steeves et al. (2023) [14] reported strong support for TDI education among physicians and highlighted varied preferences for how such training should be offered. Yeng et al. (2019) [17] advocated integrating e-learning, while Ipek (2024) [18] recommended simulation and multidisciplinary collaboration for practical training. Similarly, Çalışkan et al. (2021) [15] found that 90% of physicians desired further training, consistent with our students’ strong willingness to learn (mean = 4.00/5.00).

Understanding barriers to implementing dental trauma education, such as curriculum overload or limited faculty expertise, will be essential in guiding curriculum reform. Evidence from Cosgun & Cosgun (2024) [9] and Ivkošić et al. (2020) [10] demonstrates that targeted educational interventions significantly improve knowledge and decision-making accuracy. Integrating structured modules early in medical education could improve preparedness for managing dental injuries in emergency settings.

Future work should focus on developing and evaluating specific educational interventions to improve the practical management of dental trauma. Simulation-based training or digital tools, including the IADT ToothSOS app, may be particularly effective in bridging existing gaps. These interventions should be assessed for their ability to improve both knowledge and real-world decision-making. Knowledge retention, however, remains a challenge: AlZoubi et al. (2015) [19] reported marked declines within six months, highlighting the need for periodic refresher training. Interdisciplinary programs that involve both medical and dental students could enhance hands-on experience, promote collaborative care, and improve the long-term retention of skills. A key direction for future research would be to evaluate the long-term effects of early dental trauma education on clinical performance after graduation.

Although significant gaps exist in medical students’ knowledge of dental trauma, their strong willingness to learn presents a valuable opportunity for improvement. Evidence indicates that even brief educational interventions can substantially enhance competence: for example, Duruk & Daşkıran (2021) [20] found that a single one-hour lecture nearly doubled students’ correct responses, with almost half achieving a perfect score. By integrating structured, evidence-based dental trauma education into medical curriculum, we can better equip future physicians to provide timely and effective care for traumatic dental injuries, thereby improving patient outcomes and reducing the risk of long-term complications.

The findings of this study have significant clinical and educational implications. Inadequate first-aid management of traumatic dental injuries, particularly avulsion, can severely impact tooth survival and long-term oral health outcomes. Since medical professionals are often the first point of contact in emergency situations, a lack of preparedness poses a serious patient safety concern. From an educational standpoint, the observed link between formal training and accurate decision-making highlights the need to integrate mandatory, guideline-based dental trauma modules into medical curriculum. Additionally, using simulation-based and interdisciplinary teaching approaches may further enhance clinical competence and improve outcomes in emergency care.

Conclusions

Medical students exhibited limited preparedness for managing traumatic dental injuries, especially in cases of permanent crown fractures and time-sensitive avulsions. While their self-assessed knowledge was low, there was a strong desire for formal training, indicating a significant educational opportunity. General knowledge of tooth avulsion emerged as the strongest predictor of accurate clinical decision-making, while experiential factors did not significantly enhance performance. The findings indicate that incorporating guideline-based dental trauma education into medical curricula could enhance future physicians' preparedness. However, these conclusions should be interpreted cautiously due to the single-institution, cross-sectional design of the study. Further multicenter studies are needed to confirm these observations.
