Ocular involvement in Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis: A review of current management and changing trends
Authors: Zeynep Akgun, Gamze Dereli Can, Ozlem Barut Selver
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Among the mucosal involvement in Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), the eye is a significant component. Severe complications may result in vision impairment and, in extreme cases, the loss of the globe. Therefore, it is essential that ocular manifestations are effectively managed. The classification of ocular SJS/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) in acute and chronic stages has evolved over time. In light of these developments, the management of the disease has also undergone significant improvements. Amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT) represents an efficacious approach to controlling acute inflammation, particularly in cases of severe or extreme severity. Practical methods, such as ProKera® or sutureless AMT, have been introduced. To successfully manage the chronic stage, it is of the utmost importance to correctly identify the underlying problem. In addition to various techniques such as minor salivary gland transplantation and punctal occlusion, the rehabilitative management of the ocular surface also encompasses the utilization of specially designed scleral lenses, namely Prosthetic Replacement of the Ocular Surface Ecosystem (PROSE). Autologous mucosal membrane grafting, a technique that has been in use for many years, has recently seen a resurgence in popularity. In the context of limbal stem cell transplantation procedures, the introduction of cultured limbal epithelial transplantation and simple limbal epithelial transplantation represents a significant advancement. Although the success rate of keratoplasty and keratoprosthesis remains low, establishing good ocular surface homeostasis beforehand significantly improves the outcome.

Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are two ends of a spectrum and result from a type IV hypersensitivity reaction. These conditions are depicted by inflammatory and vesiculobullous involvement of the skin and mucous membranes.[1] The mortality rate for SJS and TEN ranges from 0% to 35%. The annual incidence is estimated to be between 1.2 and 12.35 new cases per million.[23] The majority of cases, approximately 85%, are drug-related, with the most common agents including antibiotics, anticonvulsants, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.[45] Other causes include viral and Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections, although the exact etiology remains unclear in some cases.[6]

Among mucosal involvement, the eye has an important place. Ocular involvement is observed in 80% of cases during the early stages and 50% during the later stages.[6] Severe complications that can threaten vision and even result in the loss of the globe have been documented. It is, therefore, imperative that appropriate and effective management of ocular symptoms be employed. In light of the advances that have been made in technology, scientific literature, and ophthalmology practices, it is evident that trends in the evaluation and management of ocular SJS/TEN have undergone significant changes and evolution over time.

The objective of the present review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the assessment and management of ocular SJS/TEN, from past to present, and to highlight changing trends. Additionally, it seeks to furnish information on the current treatment algorithms.

Method of literature search

The authors conducted a search of the PubMed and Google Scholar databases using the following terminologies: SJS, TEN, and related definitions (e.g., amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT), limbal stem cell deficiency, limbal stem cell transplantation, dry eye, posterior eyelid margin keratinization, symblepharon, and keratoprosthesis). For articles not published in English or without translations, only English abstracts were used. There was no time limit set, and a total of 138 articles were included. Since the main goal of the review was to analyze evolving trends and current treatments in managing acute and chronic ocular manifestations, a specific focus was on articles from the last 20 years (2005 onward). The changing treatment trends were evaluated in comparison.

An overview of the evaluation of ocular involvement in SJS/TEN

The syndrome can be evaluated in two principal stages: the acute and the chronic.

Acute phase

It begins 4–28 days after exposure to the triggering agent and lasts 2–6 weeks.[78] Prodromal findings such as fever, anorexia, and fatigue are followed by inflammation and ulceration of the mucosa.[9] The main symptom is skin detachment, and the disease is classified based on the proportion of the total body surface area (TBSA) affected. Therefore, if the detached skin covers less than 10% of the TBSA, it is called SJS; if it covers more than 30%, it is called TEN; and if it falls between 10% and 30%, it is called overlapping SJS-TEN.[10]

Acute ocular involvement appears in 50% to 88% of cases. The underlying pathogenesis is keratinocyte apoptosis and epithelial blistering due to severe inflammation and cytokine release.[1112] The most frequently observed clinical presentation at this period is bilateral conjunctivitis, which is present in 15–75% of individuals.[13] Clinical presentation can vary considerably, from self-limited hyperemia to complete involvement of the entire surface epithelium, including tarsus and eyelid margin. Meibomitis is also seen.[211]

The first grading of the severity of acute ocular involvement in SJS/TEN was made by Power et al.[8] in 1995 and classified as mild, severe, and very severe. Mild grade was defined as conjunctival injection, eyelid edema, and/or chemosis, moderate grade as membrane formation, corneal epithelial loss greater than 30%, corneal ulceration and/or corneal infiltrates, and severe grade as fornix shortening, symblepharon formation, and/or vision loss. Nevertheless, this grading is inadequate and offers only a general evaluation. Based on this, Sotozono et al.[14] defined the Acute Eye Severity Score in 2015, which grades acute involvement between 0 and 3 by including pseudomembrane formation, which is an important parameter in acute involvement [Table 1].

Chronic phase

Although there is no definitive time when the chronic phase begins, it is usually thought to start 3–6 months after the onset of SJS.[16] Approximately 35% of individuals with ocular involvement during the acute phase will subsequently develop chronic ocular symptoms.[1317] The cause of chronic complications is complex, with severe and ongoing inflammation of the ocular surface being a major factor. Severe inflammation and membrane or pseudomembrane formation during the acute phase increase the risk of lasting problems, such as fornix shortening, early symblepharon formation, corneal ulceration, and perforation.[11] There are reports in the literature indicating that preservative-containing eye drops, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drops, and antibiotic drops used during the acute phase may increase chronic complications.[1819] Persistent conjunctival inflammation leads to membrane formation, ulceration, scarring, and squamous metaplasia. Inflammatory damage to the meibomian glands decreases lipid tear production and increases evaporation. Obstruction of lacrimal gland duct openings and destruction of goblet cells due to conjunctival scarring also impair tear film quality. Fusion of the bulbar and palpebral surfaces, which can occur from ulceration and pseudomembrane formation, leads to the development of symblepharon and ankyloblepharon. This can limit eye movements, disrupt tear distribution, and impair ocular surface homeostasis. Keratinization and scarring of the eyelid margin and conjunctiva may be associated with several complications, including eyelid malposition, ectropion, entropion, trichiasis, distichiasis, meibomian gland loss, and punctal obstruction.[202122] These alterations not only cause ocular surface discomfort but can also lead to severe vision-threatening conditions, including corneal vascularization, limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), persistent epithelial defects, infection, corneal melting, perforation, and scarring due to mechanical trauma [Fig. 1].[2324]

When comparing these two grading systems, it becomes apparent that the main parameters—eyelid margin, cornea, and conjunctiva—are essentially the same. The primary distinction is that Sharma et al.[24] utilized a more detailed grading scale from 0 to 5. This allows them to distinguish more severe cases, which would have been classified as stage 3 in the previous system, from less severe cases. They could analyze advanced-stage cases more thoroughly regarding prognosis, planned surgery type, and surgical outcomes. Additionally, they included bulbar keratinization in their evaluation while excluding superficial punctate keratopathy.

Ocular management

The specific objectives of treatment are contingent upon the phase of the disease. In the acute period, it is essential to suppress both exogenous and endogenous inflammation, protect the ocular surface, and avoid drug toxicity.[111626] Prevention/treatment of conjunctival hyperemia, epithelial defects, and eyelid margin ulcerations is aimed. In the chronic phase, prevention of dry eye disease, eyelid malposition (ectropion, entropion, lagophthalmos), misdirected eyelashes (trichiasis, distichiasis), eyelid margin keratinization, conjunctival problems (keratinization, symblepharon, ankyloblepharon), and corneal problems (epithelial defects, thinning, scarring, neovascularization, LSCD) are treatment goals.[2728] It is important to note that the underlying pathophysiology of the two phases is distinct. The primary objective is to identify the underlying reason for the pathology and develop an appropriate treatment algorithm.[229]

Management of acute ocular SJS/TEN

Acute SJS/TEN should be addressed in a multidisciplinary manner in a burn or intensive care unit.[30] Ophthalmological examination should be performed within 1–2 days of diagnosis.[4] Ocular involvement may occur before the onset of skin lesions or may be mild initially and then worsen.[31]

Topical antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended to prevent potential infection. Topical corticosteroids and cyclosporine are excellent tools to reduce inflammation.[216] Visual outcomes are significantly better in patients who received topical corticosteroids. IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 were found to be significantly upregulated in tears from patients with acute SJS, characterized by pseudomembrane and epithelial defects.[32] According to Japanese guidelines, topical steroids are considered one of the most crucial steps in managing ocular surface inflammation in cases of severe ocular involvement.[33] In a 2025 review of the management of ocular manifestations of SJS, Sotozono et al.[34] emphasize the use of topical steroids, particularly in severe cases. In addition, preservative-free topical lubricants and/or autologous serum eye drops should be used to protect the surface. Debridement of membranes and pseudomembranes is essential in all patients. Depending on the stage, therapeutic bandage contact lenses may be considered for treating corneal epithelial abnormalities.[35]

The lack of clear measures for evaluating acute ocular findings has posed a major challenge to the implementation of systematic assessments and clinical decision-making processes. Despite the increasing body of evidence indicating the efficacy of early AMT in preventing long-term sequelae, the optimal timing for AMT remains uncertain.[36] The comprehensive grading system proposed by Gregory et al.[15] in 2016 brought a systematic perspective. Accordingly, patients in the mild and moderate stages should be managed with medical therapy, as mentioned above. From the severe and extremely severe stages, in addition to topical therapy, thorough cleaning of inflammatory residues and AMT with conformer, symblepharon ring, or ProKera (Bio-Tissue, Inc., Miami, FL) should be performed within the first 10 days. However, it is important to remember that moderate-stage cases are border-zone cases. It can sometimes be challenging to clinically differentiate between moderate and severe cases, and if necessary, AMT can be utilized at earlier stages.[6162837]

Surgical intervention: Amniotic membrane transplantation

AMT is a cost-effective, rapid, practical, and easily accessible surgical option for reducing inflammation and promoting epithelial healing. It is widely utilized for treating various ocular surface disorders, including chemical injuries, persistent epithelial defects, infections, ocular surface reconstructions, and immune-mediated conditions.[3839] The use of AMT in SJS/TEN was first reported by John et al. in 2002.[40] After that, numerous case reports, case series, and randomized controlled trials have been published. All of them report that AMT provides better results than traditional topical therapy in controlling the acute phase and preventing chronic sequelae.[41424344454647] Fig. 2 shows the number of publications on AMT by year. It is evident that the advancement in the value of AMT utilization in SJS publication has been concentrated, particularly between the years 2015 and 20. Subsequently, it can be posited that AMT has become a standard procedure, particularly in cases of severity.

Reports on AMT in SJS in terms of year

Although there is no clear information on the ideal application time, Gregory et al.[15] reported that applying AMT within the first 7 to 10 days of disease onset may help avoid serious long-term consequences, especially in severe cases. Sharma et al.[6] also supported these findings and reported that corneal opacity, LSCD, symblepharon formation, and eyelid problems were less common in the AMT group at 6 months.

In the original surgical technique, the amniotic membrane is sutured to the ocular surface in the operating room. Over time, a consensus grew that using just a single layer of amniotic membrane in the fornix is not enough in severe cases. Instead, multiple layers covering the entire eye surface—from the upper eyelid to the lower eyelid and including the eyelid margins—have become more widely accepted. However, this situation extends surgical time and makes it more difficult to stabilize the amniotic membrane.[4849] Several techniques and tools have been described to resolve this issue, reducing surgical time for AMT and increasing bedside use in the intensive care unit. One of these uses the ProKera® device (Bio-Tissue, Inc., Miami, FL), which includes a polycarbonate ring conformer where a cryopreserved sheet of amniotic membrane is clipped and stretched across the ring lumen. It is a rapid, safe, and practical alternative method that can be performed at the bedside.[5051]However, this device only covers the cornea and surrounding bulbar conjunctiva, leaving the rest of the conjunctiva, fornices, and eyelid margins exposed. It is especially inadequate when the bulbar/palpebral conjunctiva and eyelid margins are involved.[52] To overcome this disadvantage without compromising ease of application, Ma et al.[53] described a surgical technique that utilizes a single, large amniotic membrane sheet (5 × 10 cm) and a custom-made fornical ring.

Furthermore, sutureless fibrin glue AMT is a quick and practical alternative technique that is just as effective as the original method in stabilizing the ocular surface and minimizing chronic complications.[54] It is important to note that even a simple pediatric nasogastric tube, along with expensive devices like the symblepharon ring and ProKera, can be effective in securing the amniotic membrane to the ocular surface.[55] In addition, AMT can be repeated, if necessary, especially in extreme cases.[2]

AMT-related complications are extremely rare, with the most common complications being microbial infection, submembranous hemorrhage, and membrane detachment.[56] Microbial infection occurred in 3.4% of patients undergoing AMT for various indications.[57] Topical antibiotic prophylaxis may be beneficial to avoid missing a potentially infectious condition, especially as it is difficult to identify anterior segment details after implantation.[58]

Thorel et al.[59] proposed an acute treatment algorithm based on the Sotonozo classification (Stage 0–4). According to this algorithm, daily care involving eye washing with saline, pseudomembrane removal, unpreserved tears, antibiotic drops, and steroids was recommended for all stages. Additionally, case-based AMT is advised for stages 2 and 3. Management of chronic SJS/TEN: Controlling of ocular sequelae and rehabilitation of vision

Ocular Surface Stabilization – Controlling inflammation is essential for successful visual rehabilitation. The chronic phase typically advances through episodic flares amid ongoing inflammation.[6061] Although high-potency topical steroids can be used to treat flares, long-term use of these medications is not recommended. Topical or, if necessary, systemic immunosuppressive treatment with cyclosporine, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, mycophenolate, and infliximab may be used, especially when inflammation is moderate to severe.[6162] Oral doxycycline or azithromycin may also be used.[16] It is also important to note that temporary systemic immunosuppression before surgery may help reduce postoperative inflammation.[63]

Dry Eye Disease and Persistent Epithelial Defects – During the chronic phase, over half of patients experience dry eyes, and all components of the tear film (aqueous, mucous, and lipid) are affected.[6465] The aqueous component is reduced by scarring of the lacrimal ducts and inflammation of the lacrimal gland. Damage to conjunctival goblet cells degrades the mucin component. The lipid component is reduced by meibomian gland inflammation, atrophy, and squamous metaplasia of the meibomian gland orifices.[112066]

Topical treatment with topical cyclosporine, which increases goblet cell density, along with artificial tears and autologous serum, is a long-term recommended, standardized first-line treatment modality.[6768] Ocular surface and lid margin scarring often result in punctal occlusion. However, in cases with open puncta, punctal occlusion may help maintain ocular surface hydration. Punctal occlusion is a well-established treatment modality that has been used for many years. In 2004, Kaido et al.[69] reported successful results in patients with SJS. A recent study has also shown that it is effective in patients with dry eye secondary to cicatricial conjunctivitis, including cases with SJS.[70]

The labial and oral mucosa contain several minor salivary glands that can be transplanted as a complex graft with the overlying mucosa. Similar to punctal occlusion, minor salivary gland transplantation has been used since the early 2000s.[7172] In 2005, Soares and Franca,[73] and in 2010, Marinho et al.[74] reported successful results in dry eye due to SJS. From the current literature, Zhang et al.[75] conducted autologous microvascular submandibular gland transplantation on a large dry eye disease series, the vast majority (118/200) of which were due to SJS, and reported successful results. Sharma et al.,[76] who performed minor salivary gland transplantation in patients with chronic SJS-related dry eye between 2022 and 2023 in Delhi, India, also reported statistically significant improvements in dry eye and ocular surface parameters at 1 year postoperatively. To summarize, salivary gland transplantation should be considered to improve the ocular surface and quality of life of patients with severe dry eye disease.

Persistent epithelial defects at any stage of SJS/TEN should be controlled immediately, as they can cause serious results, including infection and spontaneous perforation. Treatment options include tear film supplements such as artificial tears, ointments, and autologous serum; ocular surface protectors such as bandage soft contact lenses, scleral lenses, amniotic membrane, and ProKera; and surgical interventions such as tarsorrhaphy and punctal plugs.[777879] In refractory situations, autologous cultivated oral mucosal epithelial transplantation (COMET) may be used to encourage re-epithelialization.[80]

Scleral contact lenses protect the corneal surface from various microtraumas, such as eyelid margin keratinization, and facilitate the healing of persistent epithelial defects [Fig. 4]. They may offer long-term stabilization of the ocular surface and enhance visual acuity in patients with SJS.[8182] As well as scleral contact lenses, specially designed scleral lenses known as Prosthetic Replacement of the Ocular Surface Ecosystem (PROSE) are a favorable treatment choice for chronic SJS/TEN patients. PROSE lenses were approved by the FDA in 1994 for the management of irregular astigmatism and ocular surface diseases. Since the 2010s, successful results have been reported in ocular surface pathologies, especially persistent epithelial defects secondary to graft versus host disease and SJS.[708384] In 2019, Wang et al.[85] reported that it was beneficial in more than two-thirds of pediatric patients with chronic ocular surface disease associated with SJS/TEN. Liao et al. also reported a decrease in corneal opacity with PROSE treatment in 2 SJS cases.[86]

Eyelid Problems – Eyelid malpositions such as ectropion and entropion, lash abnormalities such as trichiasis and distichiasis, and lagophthalmos secondary to skin scarring cause further mechanical damage and disruption to the already compromised ocular surface.[87] Treatment of lagophthalmos includes tarsorrhaphy and skin scar release. Entropion and ectropion may be managed with lateral canthoplasty, tarsal strip, anterior lamellar repositioning, posterior lamellar tightening, or tarsoconjunctival advancement. Furthermore, successful repair of cicatricial entropion can be achieved by combining mucous membrane grafting (MMG) with terminal tarsal rotation, anterior lamellar recession, or tarsotomy alone.[88] Although mechanical epilation can resolve eyelash irregularities, recurrence is relatively rapid. Additionally, cryoablation is not an effective long-term solution for this group. Resection of trichiatic eyelashes offers a higher success rate for cicatricial ocular surface disorders.[2118789]

Epitheliopathy caused by repetitive stress from movement of the keratinized posterior lid margin can progress to corneal stromal melting and perforation. LSCD may be followed by corneal vascularization and conjunctivalization.[2090] Therefore, early and effective intervention is essential to prevent end-stage blindness. There have been reports in the 1980s and 1990s that topical vitamin A ointment helps reduce keratinization.[9192] Other options include large-diameter rigid gas permeable limbal or scleral contact lenses and the PROSE lens system [Fig. 4]. Shlager et al.[93] reported that using PROSE along with adjuvant vitamin A in a severe case of SJS facilitated the healing of the ocular surface.

When considering surgical options, autologous MMG, in which keratinized tarsal conjunctiva is replaced with labial or buccal mucosa, is a surgical alternative worth considering. MMG has been used for many years to treat a variety of ocular surface pathologies, including SJS.[8894] Iyer et al.[95] reported improvements in patient comfort, best-corrected visual acuity, and ocular surface parameters in 92.6% of eyes in their cohort. Fu et al.[96] also reported 90.9% symptomatic improvement in 11 eyes with SJS/TEN. MMG can be applied alone or in combination with the PROSE lens system to create an additive effect.[79] In addition, it is valuable in providing ocular surface stabilization prior to vision rehabilitation procedures such as cultured oral mucosal epithelial transplantation (COMET), cultured limbal epithelial transplantation (CLET), or simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET).[979899]

It is also noteworthy that, despite its long-standing reputation, MMG has recently gained prominence as a corrective technique for eyelid issues associated with SJS.[100101]

Symblepharon management – Challenging complications in chronic SJS include symblepharon, which is a partial or complete adhesion of the bulbar and palpebral conjunctiva, and ankyloblepharon, a partial or complete fusion of the upper and lower eyelids. Depending on how severe the disease is, complications can range from lacrimal duct obstruction and impaired tear drainage to decreased visual acuity, restricted eye movement, and complete eyelid closure.[102] The goal of treating symblepharon is to resolve these issues and keep the ocular surface stable. Literature reports that post-treatment recurrence rates vary from 6.2% to 40%.[103]

Surgical treatment involves lysis of symblepharon, followed by reconstruction of the tarsal/bulbar conjunctiva, as well as the ocular surface. AMT and MMG are commonly used for this purpose. Several adjunctive treatments, including conformers, symblepharon rings, mitomycin C or bevacizumab-impregnated sponge placement, and β-irradiation, are recommended to reduce the recurrence.[104] MMG may be beneficial in treating eyelid margin and conjunctival keratinization in eyes with symblepharon. MMG is known to stabilize the ocular surface, decrease corneal opacity, encourage epithelialization, and enhance vision. It can be used along with AMT.[105] COMET, which has been reported to achieve successful results in re-epithelialization and ocular surface stabilization, may also be useful in these cases. Postoperative scleral contact lens or PROSE use will help maintain ocular surface homeostasis and improve visual acuity through optical effects.[7998]

Vision rehabilitation:

Limbal Stem Cell Deficiency and Transplantation – LSCD in SJS/TEN is typically seen bilaterally. The pathogenesis of LSCD includes chronic ocular surface inflammation, destruction of limbal stem cells, and conjunctivalization of the corneal surface.[106] Both cultivated and conventional autologous or allogeneic limbal stem cell transplantation (LSCT) have been attempted in SJS. Few studies have evaluated the effects of LSCT in individuals with isolated SJS, and results are usually presented as part of a larger series reporting the results of LSCT in various ocular surface diseases.[107] However, it should be noted that LSCT success rates in this patient group are lower than in non-inflammatory pathologies due to both severe dry eye and other factors.[108]Patients with SJS/TEN are typically not candidates for autografting due to the frequent bilateral involvement. Keratolimbal allografts (KLAL), despite promising reports in the 1990s, have a high rate of failure at one year due to graft rejection, infection, glaucoma, and other complications despite prolonged and severe immunosuppression.[109110] Solomon et al.[110] divided patients who underwent KLAL into three groups and found that visual acuity was lower in patients with SJS after 2 years compared to those with chemical burns and other causes. A study examining the effects of KLAL in a diverse group of patients with LSCD, about half of whom had SJS, found that the procedure significantly reduced the ocular surface area over a 2-year period.[111] Although immunosuppressive therapy improves outcomes, success rates still remain unsatisfactory.[112]

A significant advance in LSCT occurred with the description of the CLET technique by Pellegrini et al. in 1997.[113] CLET is one of the current treatment alternatives that should be emphasized. The use of autologous CLET has the advantage of using a minimal amount of limbal tissue, thus reducing the donor area and not requiring immunosuppression.[114] This feature is particularly useful for eyes with uncommon asymmetric or unilateral involvement. The success rate of CLET in adults with LSCD of various etiologies ranges from 68% to 80%.[115116] Shortt et al.,[117] sharing their results with allogeneic CLET in aniridia and SJS, observed an improvement in mean visual acuity of 0.98 logMAR units at 6 months in four eyes with SJS. Although visual gain gradually declined over the next 30 months, a consistent mean gain of 0.35 logMAR units was seen at 36 months.

In 2012, Sangwan et al.[118] described an innovative LSCT technique called SLET that combines the advantages of conventional LSCT and CLET while avoiding the limitations of both approaches. Although SLET is a simple, equipment-free, and rapid technique, its use is often limited in bilateral SJS. Allogeneic SLET is an option in these cases. While the risk of rejection is reduced due to the placement of limbal tissue in the mid-peripheral avascular stroma, immunosuppression remains essential.[119] Shanbhag et al.[120] reported the results of 30 eyes from 29 patients who underwent allogeneic SLET, either alive or cadaveric. About half of these cases had LSCD secondary to SJS. One year after surgery, they observed statistically significant improvements in corneal conjunctivalization, vascularization, and opacification. Although not statistically significant, symblepharon also resolved clinically. Additionally, more than 60% of eyes experienced an improvement in visual acuity to 20/60 or better.

Cultivated Oral Mucosal Epithelial Transplantation – Autologous COMET has been used to restore the ocular surface in patients with chronic SJS/TEN since 2002. Autologous oral mucosal epithelial cells are first planted onto an amniotic membrane in vitro after being harvested from the buccal mucosa. These cells are then used to restore the ocular surface. COMET promotes re-epithelialization without the need for immunosuppression and stabilizes the corneal surface over time.[121122] According to Sotozono et al.,[123] COMET may provide better postoperative visual acuity than LSCT in chronic SJS/TEN. COMET has the benefit of not needing any systemic immunosuppression, though its drawbacks include being a two-step procedure and needing costly laboratory conditions for cell culture.[124]

Based on the encouraging results of COMET and combining them with the SLET principle, Doctor et al.[125] applied simple oral mucosal epithelial transplantation (SOMET), a synthesis of these two techniques. Small pieces obtained from the lip mucosa were positioned on the paracentral cornea, similar to SLET, and covered with AMT. A well-epithelialized surface improved visual acuity and increased Schirmer value were observed at 1-year follow-up.

Kengpunpanich et al.[126] evaluated three epithelial transplantation procedures (SLET, CLET, COMET) in a series of 103 eyes with 15 years of follow-up. The mean overall success ratio was found to be 62.1%, with no significant difference between the three surgical procedures. However, LSCD secondary to SJS had a significantly lower survival ratio compared to other causes (P < 0.001). In these patients, SLET had a significantly higher survival rate compared to CLET (P = 0.018) and COMET (P = 0.047).

Keratoprosthesis: End-stage – Keratoplasty success is low in SJS/TEN, and graft rejection, failure, progressive ulceration, and perforation are common. Penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) should only be considered in SJS for tectonic purposes, as these patients have a low chance of visual recovery.[211] Solomon et al.[110] reported that PKP survival is significantly lower in SJS than in other pathologies. In cases of severe corneal opacity, vascularization, and LSCD, keratoprosthesis (KPro) can restore visual function, especially in the first few years after surgery.[127128] Although KPro surgery has performed for over two centuries, significant results have only been obtained in treating complex corneal pathologies in the past two decades. However, due to its destructive nature, the success of KPro surgery in SJS, as with many other surgeries, is low.[129130131] Therefore, KPro implantation should be reserved for extreme cases. Current designs include the Boston keratoprosthesis, types I, II, osteo-odonto keratoprosthesis, and modified osteo-odonto keratoprosthesis (MOOKP). The Boston Keratoprosthesis Type I can be utilized in patients with relatively normal eyelids, conjunctival anatomy, and ocular surfaces, while the Boston keratoprosthesis type II, or MOOKP, is appropriate for dry, keratinized eyes with large fornix and lid abnormalities. These features make them the preferred options for patients with SJS.[132] While type I KPro results seem successful, maintaining a moist environment in SJS patients is difficult. Severe dry eye greatly raises the risk of device infection and extrusion. Aqueous leakage, which may require KPro replacement or penetrating keratoplasty, was reported in 4 of 16 SJS patients who underwent KPro.[133134]

In end-stage SJS, where options are quite limited, a remarkable case report on the use of KPro emphasizes the preparation of the ocular surface with minor salivary gland transplantation and mucosal grafting prior to the application of the Boston Keratoprosthesis Type 1 to achieve successful results.[135]

In summary, managing chronic SJS is a multistage process that begins with suppressing ocular surface inflammation, continues with repairing eyelid sequelae, and ultimately culminates in resolving LSCD and providing visual rehabilitation [Fig. 5]. It is important to remember that chronic sequelae are progressive and lead to morbidity and decreased visual acuity over time. Appropriate interventions can prevent this.[136137] Over the past 25 years, the treatment of chronic sequelae has shifted from conservative to interventional approaches. Iyer et al.[138] report that procedures such as punctal cauterization, MMG, fornix reconstruction, LSCT, and KPro have become more common and earlier since 2005 and contribute to improvements or stabilization of visual acuity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SJS/TEN is a rare immunologic multisystem pathology that can cause serious ocular surface sequelae. Ocular involvement can occur in both the acute and chronic stages of the disease and can lead to serious complications that may result in blindness if left untreated. The evolution of classification systems has enabled a more accurate assessment of the disease. Improvements in technology and innovations in surgical techniques have enhanced disease management and resulted in better visual outcomes.
