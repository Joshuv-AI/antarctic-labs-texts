Compassion and Self‐Compassion in Relation to Nursing Students' Attitudes Toward Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Sevgi Çağaltay Kayaoğlu, Şeyma Soyanıt Eraslan, Satu Tufan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the relationships between compassion, self‐compassion and nursing students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence.DesignA descriptive cross‐sectional study.MethodsData were collected between April and December 2025 from undergraduate nursing students enrolled at a public university. A total of 377 students participated. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the Compassion Scale, the Self‐Compassion Scale and the General Attitudes toward Artificial Intelligence Scale. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, independent samples t‐tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlation and PROCESS‐based mediation analysis. The study was reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines.ResultsCompassion showed a small but statistically significant positive correlation with the positive attitudes subscale and a similarly small positive correlation with the reverse‐scored negative attitudes subscale of the GAAIS. Because higher scores on the reverse‐scored negative attitudes subscale indicate less negative and more accepting attitudes toward artificial intelligence, this finding suggests that higher compassion was associated with both more positive and less negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Both associations reflected a limited amount of shared variance.ConclusionsCompassion may influence how nursing students interpret and evaluate artificial intelligence, whereas self‐compassion does not appear to have a direct role. These findings indicate that attitudes toward artificial intelligence in nursing education may not be explained solely by cognitive factors and highlight the relevance of affective characteristics.Implications for Nursing PracticeEducational approaches that integrate technological competencies with attention to emotional, ethical and relational aspects of care may support the development of balanced and patient‐centred perspectives on artificial intelligence.ImpactThis study highlights the role of compassion as an affective factor shaping attitudes toward artificial intelligence among nursing students, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of technology acceptance in nursing education.Reporting MethodThe study adhered to the STROBE guidelines.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution. The study involved nursing students only.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being integrated into healthcare systems, influencing clinical decision‐making, patient management and the organisation of care. These developments are expected to transform nursing practice by introducing tools that support efficiency, accuracy and access to clinical information (Cary et al. 2024; Turchioe et al. 2024).

In parallel with these technological advancements, nursing education is undergoing significant changes. Preparing nursing students to engage with artificial intelligence has become an emerging priority, as future nurses will be required to interact with digital systems while maintaining the core values of patient‐centred care (Hashish et al. 2025; Seo and Cho 2022). Understanding how students perceive and evaluate artificial intelligence is therefore essential for developing effective educational strategies.

Previous research has shown that attitudes toward artificial intelligence among healthcare students are shaped by factors such as knowledge, exposure and perceived usefulness (Hebert et al. 2022; Baraka et al. 2025). However, these approaches have largely focused on cognitive determinants of technology acceptance and have given limited attention to the emotional and professional values that are central to nursing practice.

In a profession grounded in care, compassion and ethical responsibility, it is important to consider how these values may influence responses to technological innovation. Examining attitudes toward artificial intelligence from this perspective may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how future nurses integrate technology into care practices.

To address this gap, the present study examines the relationships between compassion, self‐compassion and nursing students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence. The following section provides a focused overview of the relevant theoretical and empirical background informing these relationships.

Background

Compassion is widely recognised as a core value in nursing practice and is central to the provision of patient‐centred care. It involves sensitivity to patients' suffering, emotional resonance with their experiences, and actions aimed at alleviating distress (Younas et al. 2023). Empirical studies have demonstrated that higher levels of compassion among healthcare professionals are associated with improved patient satisfaction, enhanced therapeutic relationships and better clinical outcomes (Younas et al. 2023). Within nursing education, compassion is considered a developable competency shaped through professional socialisation, clinical experience and educational interventions (Samson‐Akpan et al. 2022).

In the context of emerging healthcare technologies, compassion may influence how artificial intelligence is perceived and evaluated. Students with higher levels of compassion may be more attentive to the potential benefits of artificial intelligence in improving patient outcomes, while simultaneously demonstrating sensitivity to ethical concerns and risks associated with technology use. Previous research suggests that healthcare professionals may evaluate artificial intelligence not only in terms of efficiency and usefulness but also in relation to its impact on humanistic aspects of care (Morrow et al. 2023).

Self‐compassion represents an internally oriented emotional resource characterised by self‐kindness, mindfulness and a sense of common humanity (Neff 2003). In nursing students, self‐compassion has been associated with reduced stress, improved emotional regulation and greater psychological resilience (Walter 2025). These characteristics may influence how students respond to emerging technologies by reducing anxiety and facilitating adaptive coping in uncertain or complex clinical environments (Li et al. 2024). However, unlike compassion, self‐compassion is primarily directed toward the self and may not directly translate into externally oriented evaluations, such as attitudes toward patient care technologies. This outward/inward distinction is theoretically grounded in Neff's (2003) original conceptualisation of self‐compassion, which explicitly differentiates self‐compassion, treating oneself with kindness, common humanity and mindfulness in relation to one's own suffering, from compassion for others, which is directed toward another person's suffering and does not necessarily require, or follow from, self‐directed kindness. Although Neff and Pommier (2013) reported a positive association between the two constructs, they emphasised that self‐compassion and other‐oriented compassion remain conceptually and empirically distinguishable rather than interchangeable expressions of a single underlying trait, supporting their treatment as separate constructs in the present study.

Several affective and psychosocial constructs, including empathy, emotional intelligence and caring behaviours, have also been examined in relation to technology acceptance among healthcare professionals. Compassion was selected as the primary focus of the present study because it captures a distinct, action‐oriented dimension that these related constructs do not fully encompass. Whereas empathy primarily reflects an affective and cognitive resonance with another person's experience, compassion extends beyond this resonance to include a motivational orientation toward relieving suffering through action (Sinclair et al. 2017). Emotional intelligence, in turn, encompasses a broader set of abilities related to recognising and regulating emotions in general, without being specifically anchored in the ethic of care that defines nursing practice, while caring behaviours represent the behavioural enactment of professional values rather than the underlying affective‐motivational disposition from which such behaviours arise. Compassion and self‐compassion were therefore considered conceptually well suited to capturing the affective and value‐based orientations that are central to nursing identity. This also distinguishes the present focus from technology‐specific constructs such as AI anxiety and technology self‐efficacy, which primarily reflect cognitive and technology‐related appraisals rather than profession‐grounded affective values, and which have already been extensively examined in the technology‐acceptance literature (Hebert et al. 2022; Baraka et al. 2025). Building on this literature, the present study aimed to test whether a professionally grounded value, compassion, together with its self‐directed counterpart, self‐compassion, contributes additional explanatory value beyond commonly studied technology‐related constructs. This positioning is consistent with recent work conceptualising self‐compassion as embedded within a broader network of ethically and morally grounded psychological constructs among nursing students, including moral intelligence and moral sensitivity, rather than as an isolated affective trait (Yarahmadi et al. 2025). Compassion may theoretically influence attitudes toward artificial intelligence through heightened ethical sensitivity and a patient‐centred orientation, which sensitise individuals to both the potential benefits of artificial intelligence for patient care and the ethical risks associated with its use, consistent with the pattern of associations observed in the present study.

Beyond cognitive and experiential determinants, a growing body of literature has examined AI‐specific anxiety as a barrier to technology acceptance among nursing students. For example, Cho and Seo (2024) found that anxiety toward using artificial intelligence mediated the relationship between nursing students' perceptions of artificial intelligence and their intention to use artificial intelligence‐based technologies, while Migdadi et al. (2024) reported that ethical awareness, anxiety and attitudes toward artificial intelligence were closely interrelated among nursing students, underscoring anxiety as a salient cognitive‐affective barrier that is conceptually distinct from professionally grounded values such as compassion. In parallel, emerging research on technology acceptance in healthcare has begun to examine the role of empathy: Cai et al. (2023) demonstrated that perceived empathy of medical artificial intelligence mediated patients' acceptance of artificial intelligence‐based healthcare technologies, suggesting that affective appraisals of technology, and not only its perceived usefulness or ease of use, shape acceptance outcomes. Taken together, this adjacent literature indicates that cognitive‐affective barriers, such as AI anxiety, and technology‐directed affective appraisals, such as perceived empathy of artificial intelligence, have been studied more extensively than professionally grounded, other‐directed affective traits such as compassion and self‐compassion, further situating the contribution of the present study. More recent work has begun to examine ethical and professional‐identity dimensions of artificial intelligence attitudes using multidimensional and interaction‐based approaches. For example, Atalla et al. (2024) found that ethical awareness moderated the relationship between nurses' attitudes toward artificial intelligence and their innovative work behaviour, indicating that the translation of positive attitudes into professional action depends on ethical competencies rather than attitudes alone. Similarly, Yang (2024) reported that moral sensitivity and digital literacy jointly predicted artificial intelligence ethics awareness among nursing students, underscoring the interplay between internal psychological traits and outward professional and technological attitudes. These findings suggest that emotional and ethical constructs may not simply add to, but interact with, cognitive and technology‐specific factors in shaping how nursing students and nurses engage with artificial intelligence, an interaction‐based perspective that the present study's mediation framework is well positioned to extend in future research.

Despite the growing body of research on artificial intelligence in nursing education, existing studies have predominantly focused on cognitive and experiential determinants, including knowledge, exposure and demographic characteristics (Hashish et al. 2025; Seo and Cho 2022). Emotionally grounded constructs that are central to nursing identity, particularly compassion and self‐compassion, remain underexplored in relation to attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

This gap persists despite a rapidly expanding body of nursing‐specific evidence. A recent systematic review synthesising 37 studies on nursing students' and nurses' attitudes, literacy and readiness for artificial intelligence found that favourable attitudes were shaped primarily by prior training, technical competence and seniority, with limited attention paid to affective or dispositional traits such as compassion (El Arab et al. 2025). Similarly, a mixed‐methods systematic review of nursing students' perceptions of artificial intelligence in nursing education, framed through the Technology Acceptance Model, found that willingness to adopt artificial intelligence was driven largely by perceived ease of use and usefulness, again leaving affective, profession‐specific constructs largely unaddressed (Li et al. 2025). Beyond nursing, the broader human–computer interaction and technology‐acceptance literature has emphasised hedonic motivation, habit and facilitating conditions as key predictors of technology acceptance (Venkatesh et al. 2012), constructs that, like the Technology Acceptance Model, are primarily cognitive and utilitarian rather than affective in orientation. Situating the present study relative to both this current nursing‐specific literature and the broader technology‐acceptance tradition underscores the relative neglect of profession‐grounded affective traits, such as compassion and self‐compassion, as predictors of artificial intelligence attitudes. From a theoretical perspective, cognitively oriented frameworks such as the Technology Acceptance Model have been widely used to explain attitudes toward technology in general populations, but these frameworks centre on perceived usefulness and ease of use and do not account for profession‐grounded affective values such as compassion. Watson's Theory of Human Caring (Watson 2008) instead conceptualises compassion and caring as central to how nurses interpret and respond to their professional environment. In this context, compassion and self‐compassion may function as affective antecedents influencing how artificial intelligence is interpreted within professional and ethical frameworks of care.

Therefore, examining these variables may provide a more comprehensive understanding of artificial intelligence attitudes in nursing education.

The Study

Aim of the Study

The aim of this study was to examine the relationships between compassion, self‐compassion and nursing students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Hypotheses

Based on the theoretical and empirical background outlined above, the following directional hypotheses were tested using PROCESS‐based mediation analysis: Compassion will be positively associated with positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Compassion will be positively associated with (reverse‐scored) negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, reflecting a more forgiving and tolerant stance toward the potential risks and drawbacks of artificial intelligence among students with higher levels of compassion. Self‐compassion will be positively associated with compassion. Compassion will mediate the relationship between self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Compassion will be positively associated with positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Compassion will be positively associated with (reverse‐scored) negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, reflecting a more forgiving and tolerant stance toward the potential risks and drawbacks of artificial intelligence among students with higher levels of compassion.

Self‐compassion will be positively associated with compassion.

Compassion will mediate the relationship between self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Methods

Design

A descriptive cross‐sectional design was used in this study. Mediation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among compassion, self‐compassion and nursing students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. This study is conceptually grounded in Watson's Theory of Human Caring (Watson 2008), which positions compassion and caring as core professional values that shape how nurses perceive, interpret and respond to their practice environment, including emerging technologies. Within this framework, compassion and self‐compassion are conceptualised as profession‐grounded affective orientations that inform how nursing students evaluate artificial intelligence within a caring, patient‐centred paradigm.

Measures

Data were collected using four instruments: a Personal Information Form, the Compassion Scale, the Self‐Compassion Scale and the General Attitudes toward Artificial Intelligence Scale. All instruments were administered as self‐report questionnaires.

Personal Information Form

The Personal Information Form was developed by the researchers based on a review of the relevant literature. The form consists of 12 items designed to assess students' sociodemographic and educational characteristics. These items include gender, age and year of study, as well as living conditions such as current place of residence and whether they had ever lived in the same house with a grandmother or grandfather. To understand the students' family context regarding caregiving experiences, the form queried the presence of individuals with disabilities in the family or among relatives. Additionally, the students' grade point average (GPA) and whether they had received prior training on artificial intelligence were included to evaluate their academic and educational background.

Compassion Scale

The Compassion Scale was developed by Pommier (2010) to assess individuals' levels of compassion. The scale consists of 24 items rated on a five‐point Likert scale and includes six subscales: kindness, indifference, common humanity, separation, mindfulness and disengagement.

Certain subscales (indifference, separation and disengagement) are reverse‐scored. For the calculation of the total compassion score, items in the reverse‐scored subscales are recoded prior to summing all subscale scores. Higher total scores indicate higher levels of compassion.

The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Akdeniz and Deniz (2016), with a reported Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.85, indicating good internal consistency. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the Compassion Scale was found to be 0.90, indicating excellent internal consistency. McDonald's omega, which does not assume tau‐equivalence among items, was also computed and was similarly excellent (ω = 0.90).

Self‐Compassion Scale

The Self‐Compassion Scale was developed by Neff (2003) to assess individuals' overall levels of self‐compassion. The scale measures six core components of self‐compassion through paired subscales: self‐kindness versus self‐judgement, common humanity versus isolation and mindfulness versus over‐identification. It is designed to generate both subscale scores and a total self‐compassion score.

The scale consists of 26 items rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘almost never’) to 5 (‘almost always’). Items in the negative subscales (self‐judgement, isolation and over‐identification) were reverse‐coded prior to calculating the total self‐compassion score. Higher scores indicate higher levels of self‐compassion.

In the original study, the overall internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.92, with subscale Cronbach's alpha values ranging between 0.78 and 0.81. The test–retest reliability coefficient was reported as 0.93. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Akın et al. (2007) among university students, and the internal consistency coefficient for the Turkish version was found to be 0.89. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the Self‐Compassion Scale was found to be 0.91, reflecting excellent reliability in this sample. McDonald's omega was also computed and was equally excellent (ω = 0.91).

General Attitudes Toward Artificial Intelligence Scale

The General Attitudes toward Artificial Intelligence Scale (GAAIS) was developed by Schepman and Rodway (2020, 2023) to assess individuals' overall attitudes toward artificial intelligence. The scale consists of 20 items and includes two subscales: positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence (12 items) and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence (8 items).

Items are rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 5 (‘strongly agree’). Possible scores range from 12 to 60 for the positive attitudes subscale and from 8 to 40 for the negative attitudes subscale. Consistent with the scoring procedure used in the Turkish validity and reliability study (Kaya et al. 2024), items on the negative attitudes subscale are reverse‐scored; therefore, higher scores on this subscale indicate a more favourable (i.e., less negative, more forgiving) attitude toward the potential drawbacks of artificial intelligence, consistent with higher scores on the positive attitudes subscale indicating stronger positive attitudes.

The original version of the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients of 0.88 for the positive subscale and 0.83 for the negative subscale. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Kaya et al. (2024). In the Turkish version, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were reported as 0.82 for the positive subscale and 0.84 for the negative subscale. Split‐half reliability coefficients were 0.77 for the positive subscale and 0.83 for the negative subscale, indicating satisfactory reliability. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.88 for the positive attitude subscale and 0.84 for the negative attitude subscale, demonstrating good internal consistency. McDonald's omega values were similarly good (ω = 0.89 for the positive subscale and ω = 0.84 for the negative subscale).

Sampling and Recruitment

A total population (census) sampling approach was adopted to include all undergraduate nursing students enrolled during the 2025 academic year at the nursing department of a public university located in the Black Sea region of Türkiye. The target population comprised 493 students. Rather than selecting a subset of participants through probabilistic sampling, all eligible students were invited to participate in the study.

Eligibility criteria included being aged 18 years or older, being currently enrolled in the undergraduate nursing programme during the 2025 academic year, and providing informed consent. Data collection was conducted between April and December 2025. Of the 493 students in the target population, 377 provided complete and valid responses, resulting in a response rate of 76.5%. Students who declined participation, withdrew after providing consent, or submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

Sample Size and Power

A total population (census) sampling approach was adopted, and the achieved sample size was therefore determined by the number of eligible students who provided complete and valid responses (n = 377) rather than by an a priori power calculation; no participants were excluded, added, or otherwise selected to reach a target sample size. For descriptive and supplementary purposes, a post hoc power analysis was nonetheless conducted using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.7) for the regression‐based mediation models actually estimated. The analysis parameters were set as follows: a Type I error rate (alpha) of 0.05, the full analytic sample size (n = 377), 11 predictors in the full model (self‐compassion, compassion and nine covariate terms, including three indicator variables for academic year), and an effect size of f 2 = 0.05, representing a small‐to‐moderate effect according to Cohen's (1988) benchmarks rather than the effect observed in this study. The analysis indicated an achieved statistical power (1 − beta) of 0.84 (84.2%). This post hoc power estimate is reported for descriptive completeness only; because the study used census recruitment rather than a pre‐specified target sample size, post hoc achieved power should not be interpreted as evidence that the sample was deliberately or optimally powered, and it does not substitute for a priori power planning.

Data Collection

Data were collected between April and December 2025 using self‐administered questionnaires. Prior to data collection, participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Questionnaires were administered in classroom settings under researcher supervision. Participation was anonymous, and no identifying information was collected.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28) and AMOS (Version 24). Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation) were calculated to summarise participants' sociodemographic characteristics and scale scores. Missing data were minimal and handled using listwise deletion with the proportion of missing data being below 5%. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values together with visual inspection of normal Q–Q plots. Values within ±1.5 for skewness and kurtosis were considered indicative of an approximately normal distribution. The Q–Q plots also indicated that the variables closely approximated a normal distribution, with only minor deviations at the tails. Independent samples t‐tests were used to compare scale scores according to binary variables (e.g., gender, place of residence, presence of a disabled family member and receipt of artificial intelligence training). One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare scores across class levels and grade point average categories. The homogeneity of variances assumption was assessed using Levene's test; Welch's ANOVA was applied when this assumption was violated, and appropriate post hoc comparisons were performed. The assumption of independence of observations was satisfied by the study's sampling design, as each participant contributed a single, independent set of responses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among compassion, self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Prior to the mediation analyses, the assumptions underlying the regression models were examined. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. For both mediation models, tolerance values ranged from 0.900 to 0.981 and VIF values ranged from 1.019 to 1.112, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. The independence of errors was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic. The Durbin–Watson values were 1.867 for the model predicting positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence and 1.931 for the model predicting negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, indicating that the assumption of independent errors was satisfied in both models.

Mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes' (2018) PROCESS macro (Model 4) for SPSS to test the hypothesised relationships among self‐compassion, compassion, and positive and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, with compassion specified as the mediator between self‐compassion and each attitude subscale. Age and other sociodemographic characteristics (gender, academic year, place of residence, presence of a disabled family member, prior experience living with grandparents and prior artificial intelligence training) were entered as covariates in each model. Academic year, a four‐level categorical variable, was entered as a set of three indicator (dummy) variables (second, third and fourth year), with first‐year students serving as the reference category, rather than as a single continuous predictor. This regression‐based approach was selected because it allows simultaneous estimation of direct, indirect and total effects while adjusting for relevant covariates within a theoretically specified mediation model. Indirect effects were tested using a bootstrapping procedure based on 5000 resamples, with 95% bias‐corrected confidence intervals; an indirect effect was considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not include zero. Direct and total effects were assessed using unstandardised regression coefficients (B), standard errors, t values and p values. Grade point average (GPA) was examined descriptively by category (Table 1) but was not entered as a covariate in the mediation models; because GPA was not part of the estimated models, the primary mediation analyses were conducted on the full analytic sample (N = 377) rather than restricting the sample to the n = 321 participants for whom GPA happened to be available. Restricting the sample to n = 321 would have excluded all 56 first‐year students purely on the basis of a variable that was not modelled, introducing systematic, non‐random selection into the analytic sample. As a sensitivity check, the mediation models were also re‐estimated on the n = 321 subsample previously used for GPA reporting; the pattern and statistical significance of all paths were consistent with the full‐sample results, and these sensitivity results are summarised where relevant. All analyses were conducted in accordance with the assumptions underlying parametric statistical procedures.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Sinop University Human Research Ethics Committee, Türkiye (Decision No: 2025/109, 28 February 2025) prior to data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional permission was also obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences. Of the three researchers involved in the study, one held a teaching role and taught courses in which some participating students were enrolled, while the other two were research assistants with no teaching or evaluative authority over the participants. To minimise the potential for coercion arising from this relationship, data collection was carried out exclusively by the two research assistants without a teaching or grading relationship with the students, rather than by the teaching researcher; the teaching researcher was not present during questionnaire administration. Participants were assured of confidentiality and anonymity and were informed that participation was voluntary. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time before submitting the questionnaire, without any academic consequences. Because no identifying information was collected, individual responses could not be linked to specific participants once the questionnaire had been submitted; consequently, withdrawal requests could not be accommodated after submission, even before data analysis had begun. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Research data and signed consent forms will be stored securely for at least 5 years following completion of the study, after which they will be securely destroyed. The data collected in this study will be used only for the purposes of the present research; no identifiable data will be shared or used for any other research without additional ethical approval, where applicable. This research received no specific grant from any funding agency in the public, commercial or not‐for‐profit sectors.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Participants

The sociodemographic characteristics of the nursing students are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 20.54 ± 2.38 years. Of the total sample (n = 377), the majority were female (69.0%). Regarding the year of study, the distribution was predominantly concentrated in the second and third years (34.2% each), followed by fourth‐year (16.7%) and first‐year (14.9%) students. The vast majority of the students resided in dormitories (91.2%). In terms of family context and caregiving experience, 84.4% reported having no disabled individuals in their family or among relatives, and 61.3% had not lived in the same household with a grandparent. Regarding academic performance, the largest group of students (46.4%) reported a grade point average (GPA) between 3.01 and 3.50. Additionally, only a small portion of the students reported having received prior training on artificial intelligence (Table 1).

Descriptive Statistics of the Scales

The mean scores, possible score ranges and internal consistency coefficients of the study variables are presented in Table 2. The participants' mean score for the Compassion Scale was 95.62 ± 13.97 (range: 24–120), indicating a relatively high level of compassion. The mean Self‐Compassion Scale score was 3.09 ± 0.65 (range: 1–5), reflecting a moderate level of self‐compassion. Regarding artificial intelligence attitudes, the mean score for the Positive Attitudes subscale (42.93 ± 8.51) was notably higher than the Negative Attitudes subscale (24.35 ± 6.08), suggesting a generally optimistic outlook toward AI integration among nursing students. Internal consistency was assessed using both Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega, the latter of which does not require the assumption of tau‐equivalence among items and is therefore considered a more appropriate estimate of reliability for composite scores used in subsequent mediation analyses. Both coefficients ranged from 0.84 to 0.91 across the scales, confirming the robustness of the scales in this sample.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis Results for the Scales

The factor structures of the Compassion Scale, the Self‐Compassion Scale and the GAAIS used in this study were tested using confirmatory factor analysis (Table 6). The analysis yielded the following fit indices: for the Compassion Scale, χ 2/df = 2.55, GFI = 0.87, CFI = 0.88, TLI = 0.86 and RMSEA = 0.06; for the Self‐Compassion Scale, χ 2/df = 2.73, GFI = 0.86, CFI = 0.86, TLI = 0.84 and RMSEA = 0.07; and for the GAAIS, χ 2/df = 2.98, GFI = 0.88, CFI = 0.88, TLI = 0.87 and RMSEA = 0.07. Following conventional benchmarks (Hu and Bentler 1999), the RMSEA and χ 2/df values indicate an acceptable model fit across all three scales, whereas the CFI and TLI values (ranging from 0.84 to 0.88) fall below the commonly recommended threshold of 0.90 and therefore reflect a less‐than‐optimal, rather than excellent, degree of fit. This pattern should be interpreted with some caution: incremental fit indices such as CFI and TLI are known to be sensitive to model complexity and to the number of indicators per factor, and values in this range are not uncommon for multidimensional psychological scales with a relatively large number of items, but they nonetheless indicate that the measurement models did not achieve optimal fit and that some degree of measurement imprecision cannot be ruled out. A small number of error covariances were specified within the GAAIS measurement model only; no such covariances were required for the Compassion Scale or the Self‐Compassion Scale. Specifically, error covariances were specified between AIS1 and AIS3, and between AIS6 and AIS10, both pairs of items belonging to the positive‐attitudes subscale. To identify and verify these prior to finalising the measurement model, an independent re‐examination of the standardised residual correlation matrix was conducted, which confirmed that these two item pairs showed the largest same‐factor residual correlations within the GAAIS model (AIS1–AIS3: r = 0.29; AIS6–AIS10: r = 0.29) and that specifying these covariances improved model fit from CFI = 0.84, TLI = 0.82, RMSEA = 0.08 to the reported CFI = 0.88, TLI = 0.87, RMSEA = 0.07. Both item pairs loaded on the same first‐order factor, suggesting that they capture closely related, narrowly overlapping content within the positive‐attitudes subscale rather than reflecting a distinct construct. Because these covariances were nonetheless identified through inspection of model residuals rather than specified a priori, they should be regarded as a post hoc, empirically and theoretically constrained adjustment, and this is noted as a limitation of the CFA reported here.

The Model for Positive Attitudes Toward AI

The mediating role of compassion in the effect of self‐compassion on positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was examined using PROCESS Macro Model 4 (Table 7). Age and other demographic variables were included in the analysis as control variables.

In the first stage, the effect of self‐compassion on compassion was assessed. The model was statistically significant (R 2 = 0.101, F(10, 366) = 4.11, p < 0.001). However, the effect of self‐compassion on compassion was not statistically significant (B = −1.095, SE = 1.118, t = −0.979, p = 0.328, 95% CI [−3.293, 1.103]).

In the second stage, the model in which self‐compassion and compassion jointly predicted positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was examined. The overall model was statistically significant (R 2 = 0.067, F(11, 365) = 2.36, p = 0.008). The results showed that compassion significantly and positively predicted positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence (B = 0.109, SE = 0.032, t = 3.349, p = 0.001, 95% CI [0.045, 0.173]). In contrast, the direct effect of self‐compassion was not statistically significant (B = −0.792, SE = 0.695, t = −1.139, p = 0.256).

Examination of the total effect model showed that the total effect of self‐compassion on positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was also not statistically significant (B = −0.911, SE = 0.704, t = −1.294, p = 0.197).

Examination of the bootstrapped indirect effect showed that the indirect effect of self‐compassion on positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was not statistically significant (B = −0.119, BootSE = 0.144, 95% CI [−0.498, 0.097]). Because the confidence interval included zero, the mediating role of compassion in the relationship between self‐compassion and positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was not supported. A sensitivity analysis re‐estimating this model on the n = 321 subsample produced a consistent pattern (indirect effect B = −0.119, BootSE = 0.160, 95% CI [−0.527, 0.130]; also non‐significant).

The Model for Negative Attitudes Toward AI

The mediating role of compassion in the effect of self‐compassion on negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence was examined using PROCESS Macro Model 4 (Table 8). Age and other demographic variables were included in the analysis as control variables.

In the first stage, the effect of self‐compassion on compassion was examined. The overall model was statistically significant (R 2 = 0.101, F(10, 366) = 4.11, p < 0.001). However, the effect of self‐compassion on compassion was not statistically significant (B = −1.10, SE = 1.12, t = −0.98, p = 0.328, 95% CI [−3.29, 1.10]).

In the second stage, the model in which self‐compassion and compassion jointly predicted negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence was examined. The overall significance of the model was not statistically significant (R 2 = 0.046, F(11, 365) = 1.59, p = 0.099). Nevertheless, compassion was found to significantly predict scores on the negative attitudes subscale (B = 0.073, SE = 0.023, t = 3.12, p = 0.002, 95% CI [0.027, 0.119]). Because items on this subscale are reverse‐scored, this positive coefficient indicates that higher compassion was associated with less negative (more forgiving) attitudes toward artificial intelligence, consistent with the pattern observed for the positive‐attitudes subscale. The direct effect of self‐compassion, by contrast, was not significant (B = −0.161, SE = 0.502, t = −0.32, p = 0.749).

Examination of the total effect model showed that the total effect of self‐compassion on negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence was also not statistically significant (B = −0.241, SE = 0.508, t = −0.47, p = 0.636).

In the mediation analysis, the bootstrapped indirect effect was found not to be statistically significant (B = −0.080, BootSE = 0.094, 95% CI [−0.313, 0.075]). Because the confidence interval included zero, it was concluded that compassion did not play a mediating role in the relationship between self‐compassion and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence. A sensitivity analysis re‐estimating this model on the n = 321 subsample produced a consistent pattern (indirect effect B = −0.090, BootSE = 0.113, 95% CI [−0.356, 0.105]; also non‐significant).

Discussion

Compassion and Attitudes Toward Artificial Intelligence

The findings of this study indicate that compassion was weakly but significantly associated with attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Compassion was positively associated with positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence and, because items on the negative‐attitudes subscale are reverse‐scored, was similarly associated with less negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Overall, these findings suggest that higher compassion was associated with more positive and less negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence among nursing students. Given that items on the negative GAAIS subscale are reverse‐scored, this finding indicates that students with higher levels of compassion held lower levels of negative attitude toward artificial intelligence; in other words, as compassion increased, participants tended to evaluate artificial intelligence less negatively. Similarly, Kaya et al. (2024), taking into account the reverse‐scoring of the negative GAAIS subscale, interpreted this pattern as suggesting that individuals who view the positive aspects of artificial intelligence more favourably may also adopt a more flexible and tolerant stance toward its potential risks. Although this finding may initially appear to conflict with concerns that technology could diminish the human dimension of care, it can be interpreted differently within the nursing context. Individuals with higher levels of compassion may perceive artificial intelligence not as a substitute for compassion but as a complementary tool intended to support more accurate assessment of patient needs and to facilitate care processes. This interpretation is consistent with Watson's Theory of Human Caring (Watson 2008), which positions technology as legitimate within nursing practice only to the extent that it remains subordinate to, and supportive of, the transpersonal caring relationship between nurse and patient. Viewed through this lens, students with higher compassion may be more attuned to evaluating artificial intelligence according to whether it preserves or threatens this caring relationship, which may help explain why compassion was associated with both more favourable and more critically aware attitudes toward the technology, rather than with a straightforwardly positive or negative evaluation. This interpretation is further consistent with literature suggesting that artificial intelligence may support clinical decision‐making, reduce workload and allow healthcare professionals to devote more time to patient care. Indeed, the broader literature indicates that the relationship between artificial intelligence and compassion is not unidimensional: studies have examined both the capacity of artificial intelligence systems to generate empathy‐ and compassion‐like responses, and the effects of these systems on compassionate care processes and how they are perceived by healthcare professionals and patients (Banerjee 2020; Morrow et al. 2023; Ovsyannikova et al. 2025).

This study also examined the mediating role of compassion in the relationships between self‐compassion and both positive and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence. A similar pattern emerged across both mediation analyses: although compassion significantly predicted both positive and negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence, the effect of self‐compassion on compassion was not significant, and the corresponding indirect effect therefore did not reach statistical significance. In other words, students' attitudes toward artificial intelligence varied with their level of compassion, but this relationship could not be explained through self‐compassion.

Effect Size and Methodological Considerations

In this study, the fact that data were collected simultaneously from the same sample using self‐report scales raises concerns regarding common method bias and social desirability bias. In particular, nursing students' tendency, given their professional role, to provide socially desirable (i.e., higher) responses to compassion items may have artificially inflated the observed associations. To evaluate this potential bias, Harman's single‐factor test was conducted by entering all 70 items from the Compassion Scale, the Self‐Compassion Scale and the GAAIS together into a single unrotated principal component analysis, as recommended for this test. The first unrotated factor accounted for 13.3% of the total variance, well below the commonly cited 50% threshold, suggesting that a single latent factor does not account for the majority of variance across the measures. However, because Harman's single‐factor test is a relatively insensitive diagnostic technique and cannot fully rule out common method bias, this potential source of bias cannot be entirely excluded and the findings should still be interpreted with some caution.

The magnitude of the observed effects also warrants caution in interpretation. The unstandardized regression coefficient linking compassion to positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was small (B = 0.109), and the regression models tested here explained a modest proportion of variance in each outcome (R 2 = 0.067 for the positive attitude model; R 2 = 0.046, non‐significant, for the negative attitude model). With an analytic sample size of 377 (and a consistent pattern in an n = 321 sensitivity subsample), even trivially small associations can reach conventional levels of statistical significance; statistical significance should therefore not be equated with practical or theoretical importance. For context, other predictors of artificial intelligence‐related attitudes and intentions among nursing students have explained considerably more variance in comparable models: hedonic motivation and habit together predicted 34% of the variance in behavioural intention to use artificial intelligence in a UTAUT2‐based study of nursing students (R 2 = 0.342; Alenazi and Alhalal 2025), positive attitude and facilitating conditions explained 30% of intention variance in another nursing student sample (R 2 = 0.30; Kwak et al. 2022), and resistance to change alone explained 14.1% of the variance in artificial intelligence anxiety among nursing and midwifery students (R 2 = 0.141; Güner and Aydın 2026). Against this backdrop, the effect of compassion observed in the present study is comparatively small and should be interpreted as one modest affective correlate among several potentially more influential cognitive and technology‐specific predictors of attitudes toward artificial intelligence, rather than as a primary determinant. The modest direct effects observed here may also reflect the mediated nature of the relationships tested: consistent with related work suggesting that self‐compassion relates to nursing student outcomes indirectly, through constructs such as academic resilience and moral intelligence, rather than through direct effects alone (Delshad et al. 2023; Nobahar et al. 2022), the present mediation framework, in which compassion is modelled as an intervening mechanism between self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence, may be better suited to capturing these relationships than a simple direct‐effects model.

The Role of Self‐Compassion

Although the direction of the relationship between self‐compassion and compassion was negative in both mediation models, this relationship was not statistically significant. This finding does not align with the positive relationship between self‐compassion and other‐oriented compassion reported by Neff and Pommier (2013). However, López et al. (2018) did not find a significant relationship between self‐compassion and compassion for others, and suggested that these two constructs may reflect distinct psychological processes. The negative direction observed in the present study should therefore not be interpreted as indicating that self‐compassion reduces compassion for others. The non‐significance of the relationship, together with a confidence interval that included zero, suggests that this direction may reflect sample‐specific random variability rather than a genuine effect. Furthermore, whereas self‐compassion reflects an individual's understanding and acceptance of themselves, compassion for others may also be shaped by interpersonal interactions, professional experiences and environmental factors. The fact that these two constructs may have distinct determinants may therefore offer one possible explanation for the absence of a significant relationship between self‐compassion and compassion for others in this sample. The absence of a statistically significant relationship between self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence in the present study can also be considered from several conceptual perspectives. One possible explanation is that self‐compassion is more closely related to internally oriented emotional regulation and inner well‐being processes. Indeed, research on self‐compassion suggests that this construct tends to show only weak or indirect associations with externally oriented, prosocial behaviours such as helping others and social orientation (Marshall et al. 2020; Widyastuti and Savitri 2023; Liu et al. 2025). In this context, the more limited association between self‐compassion and attitudes toward external technologies with social, clinical and relational implications, such as artificial intelligence, may be considered one possible conceptual explanation. However, this interpretation constitutes a post hoc evaluation developed after observing the present findings and is not based on a hypothesis specified in advance within the scope of this study. It would therefore be valuable for future, theoretically grounded studies to re‐examine the relationship between self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence.

Subgroup Findings

When compassion scores were examined by gender in this study, female students reported significantly higher levels of compassion than male students. Similar findings have been reported by Çingöl et al. (2018), López et al. (2018) and Lee et al. (2021). Consistent with this, social role theory of helping behaviour (Eagly and Crowley 1986) proposes that gender roles encourage men toward heroic forms of helping and women toward nurturing and compassionate behaviour. Women's greater socialisation, in line with traditional gender roles, toward empathy, relational orientation, caregiving and emotional expression may have fostered the development of compassion. McDonald and Kanske (2023) similarly found that empathy and compassion levels were higher among women than men, and that women showed a greater tendency toward prosocial giving and helping than men. However, evidence also indicates that these higher levels of compassion do not always serve a protective function. For example, Vu and Rivera (2025) found that women's higher levels of psychological distress relative to men during the COVID‐19 pandemic were partly mediated by their higher levels of compassion. This finding suggests that the socially attributed role of high compassion may, particularly during periods of crisis, create a situation that works against women's own well‐being. These findings therefore point to the need for gender‐sensitive approaches in compassion education, with attention both to enhancing the compassion potential of male students and to supporting women's existing levels of compassion in ways that safeguard their well‐being. In this study, the presence of a family member or relative with a disability and having lived in the same household as a grandparent were not associated with significant differences in participants' levels of compassion, self‐compassion or attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Although such caregiving experiences and intergenerational co‐residence might initially be expected to enhance compassion and self‐compassion, the present findings do not support this assumption. This suggests that compassion may be more closely related to an individual's value orientation and moral framework, and self‐compassion to personality structure and psychological characteristics, rather than being emotions that develop solely through specific caregiving experiences or co‐residence arrangements (Goetz et al. 2010; Neff 2003, 2023). Viewed in this light, it is understandable that the experience of living with a family member with a disability or an older relative did not, on its own, emerge as a determining factor for compassion and self‐compassion levels. At the same time, self‐compassion has been consistently emphasised across studies as a key resource supporting individuals' psychological adjustment in caregiving contexts. Tekinarslan and Tok (2023), for instance, found that self‐compassion and perceived social support significantly predicted subjective well‐being among mothers of children with disabilities, via psychological resilience. Similarly, Nemati et al.'s (2022) findings indicate that self‐compassion plays a central role in maternal attitudes and empathic functioning among mothers of children with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Recent research shows that empathic and supportive relationships can be transmitted across generations, strengthening individuals' capacity for emotional regulation and caregiving: maternal empathic support during adolescence has been shown to influence parenting attitudes and children's empathy levels in adulthood, and close relationships with grandparents have been shown to support children's psychosocial well‐being, indicating that compassion‐based relational patterns may be learned within the family system and transmitted across generations (Stern et al. 2024; Yuvakgil and Yavuz 2025). In the present study, attitudes toward artificial intelligence did not differ significantly according to the presence of a family member with a disability or co‐residence with a grandparent. The literature emphasises that artificial intelligence holds considerable potential as a tool for facilitating healthcare access for people with disabilities and older adults, supporting care processes, and reducing caregiving burden, functioning as a facilitator at the level of technological infrastructure and service organisation (Milella et al. 2023; Alanazi et al. 2025; Olawade et al. 2025). This may indicate that attitudes toward artificial intelligence are more closely related to individuals' own experiences with technology than to emotionally and value‐laden family caregiving experiences.

Developmental Differences Across Academic Years

When the change in students' compassion levels across academic years was examined, a wavy (U‐shaped) pattern was evident rather than a linear (monotonic) decline. Compassion scores decreased from the first year (99.11 ± 9.23) to the third year (92.06 ± 16.22), but rose again in the fourth year to reach 98.33 ± 11.99. Post hoc multiple comparison analyses also support this pattern; third‐year students' compassion scores were significantly lower than those of both first‐year (p = 0.002) and fourth‐year (p = 0.018) students. This pattern may be related to the increased clinical responsibilities and academic workload encountered in the third year, while the rise observed in the final year may reflect students' growing clinical experience and sense of professional competence. However, because the mechanisms underlying this change were not directly assessed in this study, these explanations should be regarded as tentative interpretations rather than confirmed findings. This finding does not fully align with some studies reporting a continuous decline in compassion throughout nursing education (Demirel et al. 2020; Baig et al. 2023; Keshtkar et al. 2024); instead, it suggests that compassion levels may follow a variable rather than uniformly declining pattern across the course of nursing education. In contrast, self‐compassion scores increased with class level, with first‐year students in particular showing lower self‐compassion scores. This finding may be related to the coping skills and psychological adjustment processes that students develop over the course of their education (Naidoo and Oosthuizen 2024). However, given inconsistent findings in the literature (Alquwez et al. 2021; Hiçdurmaz and Aydın 2017), this relationship warrants re‐examination in different samples.

Implications for Nursing Practice

As an important implication, our findings raise a question that nursing educators may wish to consider: might students with lower levels of compassion be more inclined to adopt artificial intelligence recommendations without sufficiently critical evaluation? It is important to emphasise that this study did not measure trust in artificial intelligence, clinical decision‐making or actual use of artificial intelligence‐based technologies; the question above is therefore raised as a hypothesis for future research, not as an implication that follows from the present findings. Because compassion involves attentiveness to others' well‐being and sensitivity to their distress, it may plausibly be related to the ethical and critical evaluation of clinical decisions (Ahmed et al. 2024; Manookian et al. 2025), and it is conceivable that students with lower levels of compassion could be less critical in their evaluation of recommendations offered by artificial intelligence. However, because AI trust, clinical decision‐making and actual AI use were not assessed in this study, this remains a speculative interpretation rather than a finding of the present research, and it warrants direct empirical testing before firmer practice or curricular conclusions are drawn from it. With this caveat in mind, it may nonetheless be beneficial for nursing curricula to address not only digital literacy and technical competencies but also compassion‐based education, ethical reflection, critical thinking and artificial intelligence literacy together (Al‐Anezi 2026). Framed within Watson's Theory of Human Caring (Watson 2008), such curricula would position artificial intelligence not as a neutral technical skill to be mastered in isolation, but as a tool whose integration into practice must be continually evaluated against its capacity to support, rather than displace, the caring relationship at the centre of nursing work.

Cultural Context

The study was conducted at a single public university in the Black Sea region of Türkiye. Bivariate correlation analyses showed that students' compassion levels were significantly and positively associated with both positive attitudes (r=0.160,p<0.01) and reverse‐scored negative attitudes toward artificial intelligence (r=0.158,p<0.01). In the regression‐based mediation framework, the overall model predicting positive attitudes toward artificial intelligence was statistically significant (R2=0.067,p=0.008), with compassion significantly and positively predicting positive attitudes (B=0.109,p=0.001). Regarding negative attitudes, although the individual path coefficient for compassion remained statistically significant (B=0.073,p=0.002), we noted that the overall regression model including control variables did not reach statistical significance (R2=0.046,p=0.099). Given that items on the negative subscale are reverse‐scored, this result suggests that students with higher levels of compassion held lower levels of negative attitude toward artificial intelligence and may be more open to adopting the technology. This finding can be considered within the context of Turkish nursing education's emphasis on relational care and Türkiye's broader cultural context. In the Turkish understanding of nursing, care is regarded not merely as the delivery of technical procedures but as a holistic process that integrates emotion, knowledge and communication to address patients' physical, psychosocial and privacy‐related needs through an empathic approach (Danacı and Erdoğan 2025). Accordingly, a relationship‐oriented approach that aims to develop students' holistic understanding of care has been recommended in the planning of nursing education (Özsezer Kaymak et al. 2021). The educational outcomes of this approach are also reflected in the literature; studies with nursing students have found high perceived caring behaviour, suggesting that a relational understanding of care may be linked to the educational process (Eminoğlu and Duruk 2024; Serçe and Vicdan 2025). In addition to educational factors, the Turkish understanding of care is also associated with local ethical values such as family ties, intergenerational solidarity and responsibility (Öztürk and Hazer 2016). Consistent with this cultural context, the fact that 38.7% of participants in this study had lived with a grandparent, alongside the generally high compassion scores observed (95.62 ± 13.97), may suggest that students were exposed to care‐oriented values from an early age; however, the relationship was tested directly: living with a grandparent was not significantly associated with compassion scores in bivariate comparisons (t = −0.758, p = 0.449). Taken together, cultural context and a relational understanding of care may be associated with how students evaluate artificial intelligence. Within this framework, students may have evaluated artificial intelligence not as a threat to the profession but as a tool that can support care processes. In particular, the prospect that artificial intelligence may take over routine tasks such as data entry and documentation, thereby allowing nurses to devote more time to patient care and to support relational care, may be considered one possible explanation for the more favourable attitudes toward artificial intelligence observed among students with higher levels of compassion.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It contributes to a limited body of empirical research examining affective correlates of artificial intelligence attitudes in nursing students. The relatively large sample size and the use of validated measurement tools support the robustness of the findings. Additionally, the use of PROCESS‐based mediation analysis allowed for the simultaneous examination of relationships among variables.

However, several limitations should be considered. The cross‐sectional design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions. The study was conducted within a single institution, in the Black Sea region of Türkiye, and the sample was therefore culturally, institutionally, and geographically homogeneous; the findings may not generalise to nursing students in other cultural, institutional, or national contexts, and cross‐cultural replication is warranted before the findings are assumed to generalise more broadly. Relatedly, although differences in compassion and self‐compassion across academic years were discussed in relation to students' educational progression, the cross‐sectional design does not permit conclusions about within‐person developmental change over time; these cross‐sectional differences between year groups may instead reflect cohort effects or other between‐group differences unrelated to the passage of time, and longitudinal designs are needed before firm developmental conclusions can be drawn. The use of self‐report measures collected simultaneously from a single sample raises the possibility of common method bias and social desirability bias, particularly for compassion items given nursing students' professional socialisation toward compassionate values, which may have inflated the observed associations; although Harman's single‐factor test suggested that common method bias did not pose a serious threat in this study, this potential source of bias cannot be fully ruled out and should be considered when interpreting the findings (Alquwez et al. 2021). In addition, this study measured attitudes toward artificial intelligence but did not assess behavioural intention or actual use of artificial intelligence‐based technologies; attitudes do not necessarily translate into behaviour, and future research should incorporate behavioural or intention‐based outcomes to clarify the practical implications of the associations observed here. Furthermore, prior exposure to artificial intelligence was measured as a dichotomous (yes/no) variable, without capturing the duration, depth, content or recency of this training; this coarse measurement likely underestimates the role of prior exposure and may have obscured a more nuanced relationship between AI‐related training and attitudes toward artificial intelligence (Seo and Cho 2022). In addition, several other variables known to influence attitudes toward artificial intelligence, including AI literacy, general digital competence and AI‐specific anxiety, were not assessed in this study and could not be controlled for; the omission of these variables represents a further limitation, as they may confound or partially explain the associations observed between compassion, self‐compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Future studies should assess prior AI exposure and related constructs using more granular, validated measures, such as AI literacy scales, digital competence inventories and AI anxiety scales (e.g., Kaya et al. 2024), alongside structured questions capturing training duration and content.

Implications for Future Research

Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to examine how compassion and attitudes toward artificial intelligence evolve over time. It may also be valuable to explore potential mediating variables such as ethical sensitivity, digital literacy or professional identity.

In addition, intervention studies that integrate compassion‐based training with artificial intelligence education could provide important insights into how educational strategies influence students' attitudes and competencies (Hashish et al. 2025; Salem et al. 2024).
