On the Universal Representation Property of Spiking Neural Networks
Shayan Hundrieser s.hundrieser@utwente.nl Philipp Tuchel philipp.tuchel@ruhr-uni-bochum.de Insung Kong insung.kong@utwente.nl Johannes Schmidt-Hieber a.j.schmidt-hieber@utwente.nl

Abstract
Inspired by biology, spiking neural networks (SNNs) process information via discrete spikes over time, offering an energy-efficient alternative to the classical computing paradigm and classical artificial neural networks (ANNs). In this work, we analyze the representational power of SNNs by viewing them as sequence-to-sequence processors of spikes, i.e., systems that transform a stream of input spikes into a stream of output spikes. We establish the universal representation property for a natural class of spike train functions. Our results are fully quantitative, constructive, and near-optimal in the number of required weights and neurons. The analysis reveals that SNNs are particularly well-suited to represent functions with few inputs, low temporal complexity or compositions of such functions. The latter is of particular interest, as it indicates that deep SNNs can efficiently capture composite functions via a modular design. As an application of our results, we discuss spike train classification. Overall, these results contribute to a rigorous foundation for understanding the capabilities and limitations of spike-based neuromorphic systems.



Keywords
: Neuromorphic Computing, Expressivity, Universal Approximation, Deep Neural Networks, Recurrent Neural Networks, Circuit Complexity

1
Introduction

Brain-inspired (neuromorphic) computing is a broad and extremely active field
(
Pfeiffer & Pfeil, 2018
;
Frenkel et al., 2023
;
Zolfagharinejad et al., 2024
)
. Interest in this domain is largely driven by the limitations of classical computers, specifically the high energy demand caused by the
von Neumann bottleneck
. This bottleneck arises because memory and processing units are physically separated and must constantly exchange data; while processing speeds have increased exponentially, memory access latencies have not improved at the same pace
(
Hennessy & Patterson, 2011
)
.

To address this, neuromorphic chips aim to more closely mimic the human brain by combining memory and processing within the same units (neurons), processing information in a distributed and localized manner. A milestone in the development of neuromorphic computing is the 2023 release of the IBM NorthPole chip that, in some scenarios, has been reported to significantly outperform comparable GPUs both in terms of processing speed and energy consumption
(
Modha et al., 2023
)
.

Spiking neural networks
(SNNs) offer significant energy efficiency. Unlike classical systems that require a steady current, SNNs operate on an event-driven basis, consuming energy only when a spike occurs. While current SNN performance still lags behind standard Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), recent advancements aim to bridge this gap
(
Neftci et al., 2019
;
Yin et al., 2021
;
Stanojevic et al., 2024
;
Zhu et al., 2024
)
. ANNs have received tremendous attention from mathematicians in the past years and the advancement of neuromorphic computing asks for a similar program to unravel the properties of SNNs.

A central point in pursuing this topic concerns the decoding scheme: Should information be encoded in the relative frequency of spikes or in the precise timing of the spikes?
Empirical evidence in biological systems argues for the latter.
The argument rests on the processing-speed in biological systems: the primate brain can recognize objects within 100ms-150ms. If this requires 10 layers, then every layer has 10ms-15ms to process the signal. During such a short time span, a neuron can release at most two spikes, see
Thorpe et al., 2001
;
Taherkhani et al., 2020
for references. We therefore adopt the viewpoint that the coding is in the spiking times of the neurons, also referred to as the spike trains of the neurons.

In this work, we consider feedforward SNNs consisting of an input layer, potentially multiple hidden layers, and an output layer. The input neurons receive input spike trains from the environment. All other neurons have an associated membrane potential that is formalized in
Section

2
. Spikes occur if the membrane potential of the neuron exceeds a fixed threshold value (integrate-and-fire). SNNs can therefore be viewed as functions mapping spike trains to spike trains. This work investigates which spike train to spike train functions can be (efficiently) represented by SNNs.

Figure 1
:
Schematic depiction of a spiking neural network acting as a classifier
. An image stream is captured by a device (here represented by an eye) and encoded into input spike trains, where higher input signals increase the probability of a spike in the corresponding spike train. These signals are processed by the SNN over a time grid. The resulting output spikes determine the class label, distinguishing between categories such as ‘Dogs’ and ‘Cats’. In
Section

5
, we provide an explicit architecture for this.

1.1
Contributions: Expressiveness of SNNs

Most of the existing mathematical theory on SNNs has been derived for time-to-first-spike
(TTFS) coded SNNs. TTFS assumes that the information is encoded in the time lag
until a neuron spikes for the first time, see
Section

1.2
. In contrast, we work under the
sequence-to-sequence (STS)
coding paradigm. This approach interprets SNNs as a system mapping input spike trains to output spike trains, thereby admitting the capacity to process heterogeneous temporal data. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Universal Representation of Temporal Functions:
We show that any spike train to spike train function,
that is
causal
(the output at time
t
only depends on the input up to time
t
) and exhibits some notion of
finite memory
,
can be
exactly
represented by an SNN (see
Theorem

3.3
and
Theorem

3.6
for the precise statements). The proof is constructive and provides insights into the expressiveness of feedforward SNNs. The result is a structural analog of the universal approximation theorem for ANNs
(
Cybenko, 1989
;
Hornik, 1991
;
Leshno et al., 1993
;
Pinkus, 1999
)
.

•

Complexity Analysis and Lower Bounds:
We establish near-optimality of the required network size by providing nearly matching lower bounds. Specifically, for functions depending on the previous
m
spikes and a single input, the construction requires
O(\sqrt{m})
neurons (
Theorem

3.3
), whereas for
d\geq 2
input neurons we require
2^{O(md)}
neurons (
Theorem

3.6
). Both bounds are optimal in a worst-case sense up to lower order terms
(Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
). This shows an interesting separation between single-input and multiple-input SNNs. Refined results are obtained under additional sparsity assumptions on the functions.

•

Compositional Functions can Avoid the Curse of Dimensionality:
While the worst-case bound for the number of neurons is exponential in the dimension, we demonstrate that functions possessing a
compositional structure
can be represented with significantly fewer neurons (
Theorem

3.11
). This result is inspired by analogous results for ANNs
(
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
;
Kohler & Langer, 2021
)
and offers theoretical intuition regarding which functions are “simple” or “difficult” for SNNs to learn, confirming that SNNs are particularly efficient at representing compositional functions composed of short memory or low-dimensional functions.

•

Classification and Examples:
We showcase various relevant functions which are representable by SNNs (
Section

4
). This includes among others Boolean logical gates, periodic functions, spike amplifiers, and memory mechanisms.
Furthermore, we discuss classification tasks, demonstrating how SNNs could be used to classify spike trains (
Section

5
).

Collectively, these results confirm that the membrane potential dynamics of a spiking neuron naturally induce a form of temporal memory. This reinforces the potential of neuromorphic systems to overcome the von Neumann bottleneck by integrating memory and computation within the neuron itself.

1.2
Related work on (Spiking) Neural Networks

The previously considered time-to-first-spike (TTFS) coded SNNs are functions that map vectors with non-negative entries to vector with non-negative entries. Distinctive features of this SNN model are that every input neuron can spike at most once and that every neuron has its own learnable delay parameter that models the travel time of the signal between different neurons. Computation of Boolean functions for a specific class of TTFS coded SNNs has been considered in
Maass, 1997b
. This model also takes the refractory period after a spike into account. Given that every input node can emit at most one spike, one wonders whether it is possible to configure the SNN weights in such a way that the output spikes if and only if the Boolean function returns one (the output is not TTFS coded). While all weights can be chosen to be one (
Maass, 1997b
, p. 1663) only the delay parameters have to be adjusted. It is shown that for specific Boolean functions, one SNN unit is sufficient. For the same task, the number of hidden units in an artificial neural network needs to grow polynomially in the input size. Under a local linearity assumption,
Maass, 1997a
shows that single-spike SNNs can arbitrarily well approximate ANNs with clipped ReLU activation function
\sigma_{\gamma}(x)=\min(\gamma,\max(x,0)),
where the upper bound
\gamma>0
is a hyperparameter. This implies then that this class of SNNs satisfies the universal approximation property. These results are also summarized in
Maass, 1996
.
Maass & Schmitt, 1999
show that the VC dimension of a TTFS coded SNN is quadratic in the number of learnable delays.
Schmitt, 1999
derives the refined VC dimension bound
O(WL\log(WL))
with
W
the number of parameters and
L
the depth.

In a recent line of work,
Singh et al., 2023
proposes to use an encoder to map the inputs to the first spiking times of the SNN and a decoder to map them back to the outputs. It is shown that this specific class of SNNs generates continuous piecewise linear functions and that they can represent deep ReLU networks with one input.
Neuman et al., 2024
demonstrate how such SNNs with only positive weights can approximate standard function classes with optimal approximation rates. The restriction to non-negative weights seems to cause a logarithmic growth of the metric entropy of deeper SNNs, see
Neuman et al., 2024
, Remark 20.

Nguyen et al., 2025
considers a temporal dynamic where the activations and the potential are updated in each time step. Via an encoder and decoder this is then transformed into a vector-to-vector map. They also prove a universal approximation theorem and study the number of regions on which these SNNs are piecewise constant. Similarly,
Dold & Petersen, 2025
;
Fishell & Honnuraiah, 2025
study the complexity of this class of SNNs through the lens of a new concept called ‘causal pieces’.
Fono et al., 2025
provides an overview of the mathematical results on this TTFS model class.

It is important to highlight the main differences between TTFS coded SNNs and our approach. TTFS coded SNNs are functions whereas in this work, we think of SNNs as operators that map spike trains to spike trains. As a consequence we obtain a universal representation theorem instead of a universal approximation theorem. While TTFS coding relies heavily on the learnable delay parameters, the only parameters in our approach are the network weights and firing happens if the membrane potential exceeds a given threshold. This models the behavior of cortical integrate-and-fire neurons. Because of the possibility to use gradients, more recent papers such as
Neuman et al., 2024
focus instead on SNNs with ReLU type activations.

Gelenbe neural networks are another abstraction of SNNs. Distinctive properties are integer-valued potentials and spiking of the neurons at random times. The signal is assumed to be in the rate/intensity at which the neurons spike. For these networks, the universal approximation property has been derived in
Gelenbe et al., 1999a
;
Gelenbe et al., 1999b
.

While not directly linked to this article, a considerable amount of work has been devoted to the learning of SNNs, see e.g.

Taherkhani et al., 2020
for a summary. Moreover, an excellent introductory overview of SNNs is provided by
Malcolm & Casco-Rodriguez, 2023
.

2
Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs)

We first formalize the spiking mechanism in terms of the membrane potential of a single neuron (Section
2.1
) and subsequently explain how multiple neurons can be combined to form a feedforward SNN (Section
2.2
). Finally, we provide a perspective to recast an SNN as a recurrent artificial neural network, which involves Heaviside and clipped ReLU activation functions (Section
2.3
).

Definition 2.1
.

A
spike train

\mathcal{I}\subseteq(0,\infty)
is a countable set of points (modeling spike times) such that any bounded time interval contains only finitely many elements, i.e.,
|\mathcal{I}\cap[0,t]|<\infty
for every
t\in(0,\infty)
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq(0,\infty)
is a spike train if it has no accumulation points. Assuming
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
, we further define for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{I}|\}
,

\mathcal{I}_{(j)}:=\tau_{j}\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{I}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots\}\text{ with }\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots

as the
j
-th spike of
\mathcal{I}
. The class of all spike trains is denoted by
\mathbb{T}.

2.1
Neuronal spiking model

For a description of the spiking mechanism, we consider one output (postsynaptic) neuron receiving signal from
d
input (presynaptic) neurons; see Figure
2
for a schematic representation. The inputs emit spikes at certain times which are then weighted with real-valued coefficients
w_{1},\dots,w_{d}
, each associated with one edge, and contribute to a certain membrane potential of the output neuron. Positive weights correspond to excitatory connections, and negative weights correspond to inhibitory connections. However, the membrane potential cannot attain negative values.

Figure 2
:
A neuron receiving signal from three input neurons and its potential.
The spike trains of all neurons and the membrane potential of the output neuron are displayed for no memory (
h=0
), finite positive memory (
h\in(0,\infty)
), and infinite memory (
h=\infty
). Each input neuron emits spikes that are weighted and stored inside the membrane potential at the output neuron. A postsynaptic spike occurs when the membrane potential strictly exceeds a threshold value after which the potential is reset to zero.

For each presynaptic neuron
N_{j}
, with
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, denote by
\mathcal{I}_{j}\subseteq(0,\infty)
the corresponding spike train.
The membrane potential
P\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is recursively defined as follows.
For
t=0
, we set
P(0)\coloneqq 0
. For
t>0
and presynaptic spikes
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
, we define

{t}^{-}\coloneqq{t}^{-}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d},t):=\max\{s\in(\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{I}_{d}),s<t\}

as the last presynaptic spike time before
t.
If no presynaptic spike occurred, this value is set to zero. In particular, since
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
are spike trains,
{t}^{-}
is well-defined and satisfies
{t}^{-}<t
for every
t>0
.
Subsequently, the
membrane potential

{P}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
and the
output spike train

\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{T}
are defined via the equations

\displaystyle P(t):=

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathds{1}\big({P}({t}^{-})\leq 1\big){P}({t}^{-})e^{-\frac{t-{t}^{-}}{h}}+\sum_{j:t\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}w_{j}\bigg)_{+},\quad\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\big\{s\in(0,\infty)\colon P(s)>1\big\}.

(2.1)

The definition of
P(t)
is recursive, as it depends on the function value at
t^{-}<t.
If the memory coefficient
h
is large, then the potential decays slowly between input spikes, meaning that spikes from the distant past still have a significant impact on the current potential. Conversely, if
h
is small, then the potential decays quickly and only recent spikes have a significant impact on the potential. To extend the formalism to
h=0
and
h=\infty
, we use the convention that

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{x}{h}\Big):=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }h=0\text{ and }x>0,\\
1,&\text{if }h=\infty\text{ or }h=x=0.\end{cases}

The above construction is formally well-defined as confirmed in
Proposition

2.3
below, and implies that an output spike can only occur at the same time as an input spike, we refer this to as
domination
.

Definition 2.2

(Domination)
.

A spike train
\mathcal{I}\subseteq(0,\infty)
is said to be dominated by another spike train
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subseteq(0,\infty)
if
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, and this is denoted as
\mathcal{I}\triangleleft\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
. Further, given a spike train
\mathcal{I}
, we denote by
\mathbb{T}(\mathcal{I})
the set of all spike trains that are dominated by
\mathcal{I}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The membrane potential
P\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
in (
2.1
) is well-defined. Further,
\mathcal{S}
in (
2.1
) is dominated by
\bigcup_{i\in[d]}\mathcal{I}_{i}
and, thus, is a spike train by
Definition

2.1
.

The proof of
Proposition

2.3
is given in Section
A.1
. Notably,
replacing
P(s)>1
in (
2.1
) by
P(s)\geq 1
constraints the expressiveness of the SNN when the interarrival time of the input spikes is large, see Remark
4.1
. The above spiking mechanism deviates from biological neurons in the sense that, immediately after a spike occurred, the neuron can build up potential and spike again. Incorporating such a refractory period likely delimits the expressiveness of SNNs and further complicates the mathematical analysis.

2.2
Feedforward SNNs

To formalize feedforward SNNs we introduce the SNN (computational) unit. This recasts the nodewise computation of an output spike train from the input spike trains as a function.

Definition 2.4

(SNN computational unit)
.

For memory coefficient
h\in[0,\infty]
and weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define the
SNN (computational) unit

\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}:\mathbb{T}^{p}\to\mathbb{T}
as

\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p}):=\mathcal{S}

with the output spike train
\mathcal{S}
defined by (
2.1
).

Several SNN units can be arranged into an SNN layer. For a given input dimension
p_{\operatorname{in}}
and output dimension
p_{\operatorname{out}}
,
the SNN layer is characterized by a weight matrix
W:=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{p_{\operatorname{out}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{in}}}
,
where the
i
-th component of
\mathbf{w}_{j}
represents the weight of the connection from the
i
-th input neuron to the
j
-th output neuron.

Definition 2.5

(SNN layer)
.

For
h\in[0,\infty]
and
a weight matrix
W:=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{p_{\operatorname{out}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{in}}}
,
we define the
SNN layer

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W}:\mathbb{T}^{p_{\operatorname{in}}}\to\mathbb{T}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}}
as

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{\operatorname{in}}}):=\Big(\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{\operatorname{in}}}),\ldots,\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{p_{\operatorname{out}}}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{\operatorname{in}}})\Big).

Remark 2.6

(Excitatory and inhibitory neurons)
.

In neuroscience, neurons are often classified as either excitatory or inhibitory based on the type of effect they have on their postsynaptic targets. Excitatory neurons increase the likelihood of the postsynaptic neuron firing an action potential, while inhibitory neurons decrease this likelihood. In our framework, this distinction can be modeled by assigning to each neuron either exclusively positive or exclusively negative outgoing weights. To simplify the notation, however, we do not impose this constraint in the formal definition of SNNs. Nonetheless, this constraint can be easily incorporated by at most doubling the number of neurons in a network.

Stacking
L
SNN layers on top of each other yields then a feedforward SNN with
L-1
hidden layers. In analogy with feedforward ANNs, the neurons after the
\ell
-th SNN layer are jointly referred to as
\ell
-th hidden layer for
\ell=1,\ldots,L-1.
The input neurons are referred to as input layer or
0
-th layer and the output neurons as output layer or
L
-th layer. The
architecture
of a feedforward SNN is given by a pair
(L,\mathbf{p})
, where
L
denotes the number of stacked SNN layers and
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{L})
is the width vector, consisting of the number of input neurons
p_{0},
the number of output neurons
p_{L}
as well as the number of neurons
p_{1},\ldots,p_{L-1}
in each of the corresponding
L-1
hidden layers. For
\ell\in[L]
, the
(\ell-1)
-st layer is connected to the
\ell
-th layer by the SNN layer

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W_{\ell}}:\mathbb{T}^{p_{\ell-1}}\to\mathbb{T}^{p_{\ell}}
.

The total number of parameters in this SNN architecture is
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}p_{\ell-1}p_{\ell}
.
We additionally impose the constraint that among these parameters at most
s\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}p_{\ell-1}p_{\ell}
are non-zero.
This means that there are at most
s
weights between neurons.
To formalize this, we write
\|A\|_{0}
to denote the number of non-zero entries of a matrix
A.

Definition 2.7

(Feedforward SNN)
.

For memory coefficient
h\in[0,\infty]
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
SNN layers, width vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{L})
and maximum number of non-zero parameters (sparsity)
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the class of feedforward SNNs (with fixed architecture) as

\displaystyle\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s):=\bigg\{\mathbb{f}:\mathbb{T}^{p_{0}}\to\mathbb{T}^{p_{L}},\mathbb{f}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{0}}):=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W_{L}}\circ\ldots\circ\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W_{1}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{0}}),

\displaystyle W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell}\times p_{\ell-1}},\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\|W_{\ell}\|_{0}\leq s

\displaystyle\bigg\}.

These SNN classes satisfy the composition property

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Big\{\mathbb{f}^{\prime}\circ\mathbb{f}:\,\mathbb{f}\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}\big(L,(p_{0},\ldots,p_{L}),s\big),\,\mathbb{f}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}\big(L^{\prime},(p_{L},\ldots,p_{L+L^{\prime}}),s^{\prime}\big)\Big\}\\
&=\operatorname{SNN}_{h}\big(L+L^{\prime},(p_{0},\ldots,p_{L+L^{\prime}}),s+s^{\prime}\big).\end{split}

(2.2)

As a collection of functions on spike trains, this space is not convex (in any traditional sense) and does not admit any additive or linear structure. Moreover, given the definition of feedforward SNNs, and noting that the output spikes of an SNN unit are dominated by its input, we conclude an analogous property for feedforward SNNs: an output spike can only occur if an input spike occurred at the same time.

Corollary 2.8
.

For any feedforward SNN
\mathbb{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
with
d
inputs and
k
output,

\displaystyle\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[k]}f_{i}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\triangleleft\bigcup_{j\in[d]}\mathcal{I}_{j}\quad\text{ for all }\;\;\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}.

2.3
Interpreting SNNs as ANNs with State-Space Block

A natural question in the study of SNNs is how they relate to artificial neural networks (ANNs). In the following, we discuss how SNNs can be interpreted as a certain class of recurrent ANNs with specific activation functions and state-space blocks, see also
(
Casco-Rodriguez, 2025
)
. This connection deviates from the approach by
Stanojevic et al., 2023
, who consider a conversion from ANNs to SNNs using time-to-first-spike coding.

To derive the correspondence, we need to restrict the input spike trains to a regular grid, i.e., we consider only spike trains dominated by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\coloneqq\{n\delta:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
for some
\delta>0
. Under this assumption, we can encode the input spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
via the respective sequential input vector
(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathbf{x}^{(t)}:=(\mathds{1}(t\delta\in\mathcal{I}_{j}))_{j\in[d]}
. This allows us to represent an SNN layer using a recurrent ANN with a state-space block consisting of two hidden neurons. One hidden neuron has the (strict) Heaviside activation function
\mathds{1}(x>1)
, and the other hidden neuron uses the clipped ReLU activation function
x\mathds{1}(0\leq x\leq 1)
, see Figure
3
for a schematic depiction. The Heaviside neuron models the output spike train, whereas the clipped ReLU neuron encodes the potential; see Appendix
A.2
for details.

Figure 3
:
SNN computational unit and equivalent recurrent ANN with state-space block.
In both SNN and ANN, the yellow and blue neurons denote input and output neurons, respectively. For the SNN unit, the input consists of spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
which are all dominated by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}
.
For the ANN, the input consists of an input stream of binary vectors,
(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
which encodes the input spike trains via
\mathbf{x}^{(t)}:=(\mathds{1}(t\delta\in\mathcal{I}_{j}))_{j\in[d]}
.
The state-space block of the ANN consists of two hidden green neurons, one hidden neuron has activation function
\mathds{1}(x>1)
and generates the output spike train and the other hidden neuron has activation function
x\mathds{1}(0\leq x\leq 1)
and encodes the potential. The output of the latter neuron is delayed by the time unit
\delta
(dashed connections).

While this argument is for SNNs with input spike times contained in a regular grid, the possibility for asynchronicity of input spike trains provides SNNs with additional flexibility and representational power.

In the degenerate case of no memory
h=0
, the membrane potential immediately drops to zero after a spike occurred. The output spikes depend only on the input spikes at the same time. In this case, the conversion shows that a feedforward SNN is then exactly equivalent to an ANN with the (strict) Heaviside activation function
x\mapsto\mathds{1}(x>1),
the same network weights, and no biases.
See
Khalife et al., 2024
and
Kong et al., 2025
for the expressivity of ANNs with the Heaviside activation function.

3
Universal Representation of Functions of Spike Trains

This section analyzes the representational power of Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs). We specifically focus on functions that map
d
input spike trains to a single output spike train and satisfy some natural constraints that are introduced below. Explicit representation theorems are provided in
Section

3.1
for a single input (
d=1
) and in
Section

3.2
for multiple inputs (
d\geq 2
). Building on these results, we then discuss implications for the representation of compositional functions in
Section

3.3
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}
be a function of
d\in\mathbb{N}
input spike trains.

(i)

The function
F
is called
input-dominated
if for any
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
it holds that

F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\triangleleft\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[d]}\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i}.

(ii)

The function
F
is called
causal
if for all
t>0
and
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
it holds that

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\cap[0,t]=F({\mathcal{I}}_{1}\cap[0,t],\dots,{\mathcal{I}}_{d}\cap[0,t])\cap[0,t].

(iii)

The function
F
has the
monotone scaling property

if for any strictly
increasing bijection
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
and any

\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
it holds that

F(\varphi(\mathcal{I}_{1}),\dots,\varphi(\mathcal{I}_{d}))=\varphi(F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}))

Intuitively, an input-dominated function can only have an output spike if an input spike occurred at that time. Causality ensures that the output at time
t
depends only on input spike that have occurred up to time
t
but not on any future input spikes. While the term causal has various meanings across different fields of mathematics, we chose it because of the close similarity with causal systems in control theory and the recently introduced related notion of ‘causal pieces’
(
Dold & Petersen, 2025
)
.

The output of an input-dominated, causal function can be very sensitive to perturbations of the input spike times. To delimit this behavior, we impose the monotone scaling property which entails that the output is independent of the size of the gaps between consecutive input spikes.
What matters is whether a neuron spikes before, at the same time, or after another neuron – and this remains invariant under monotone transformations.

These properties are natural in the context of SNNs. Indeed, any feedforward SNN
f
with memory
h\in[0,\infty]
is input-dominated and causal. Further, if
h=\infty
, then
f
satisfies the monotone scaling property. This follows from the fact that the potential is constant between spikes, and thus only the ordering of the spike timings matters. For
h<\infty
, the potential depends on the precise spike timings, and thus in general
f
does not satisfy the monotone scaling property. Remarkably though, even for the regime of finite
h\in(0,\infty)
, SNNs can still exactly represent functions satisfying the monotone scaling property.

3.1
Single-Input Universal Representation

We first focus on input-dominated, causal functions
F\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
of a single input satisfying the monotone scaling property. As a consequence of this property, such a function is fully determined by the output for the spike train
\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, for any
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\pi\colon\mathcal{I}\to\mathbb{N}
be the ordering function defined by
\pi(\mathcal{I}_{(j)})=j
for
j\in[|\mathcal{I}|]
, then

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{I})=\pi^{-1}\big(F(\pi(\mathcal{I}))\big)=\pi^{-1}\big(F(\mathbb{N})\cap[|\mathcal{I}|]\big).

(3.1)

Given that the function is fully determined by
F(\mathbb{N})
, we first turn our attention to the special cases when
F(\mathbb{N})
is finite or periodic. In both cases,
F(\mathbb{N})
is fully characterized by finitely many spikes.

Definition 3.2

(Finite and periodic functions)
.

An input-dominated, causal function
F\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
satisfying the monotone scaling property is called
m
-
finite
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
if
F(\mathbb{N})=F([m])
. Further,
F
is called
m
-
periodic
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
if

\displaystyle F(\mathbb{N})=F([m])\oplus\{0,m,2m,\ldots\},

with
\oplus
denoting the Minkowski sum (sumset). Further, if
F
is
m
-finite (resp.
m
-periodic), then it is said to be
r
-sparse for
r\in[m]
if
|F([m])|\leq r
. The class of all functions which are
m
-finite (resp.
m
-periodic) and
r
-sparse is denoted by

\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)\quad\ \big(\text{resp.}\ \ \mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)\big).

The following theorem states that both finite and periodic input-dominated, causal functions satisfying the monotone scaling property can be exactly represented by a feedforward SNNs with finitely many neurons and positive memory.

Theorem 3.3

(Single-input universal representation theorem)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N},r\in[m]
with
h\in(0,\infty]
. Then, there exists an architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
with
L=O(1+\log(m))
,
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(\sqrt{m})
and a positive integer
s=O(\sqrt{m}+r)
such that for any
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
there exists a feedforward SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with memory
h
such that

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{I})=F(\mathcal{I}),\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}.

(3.2)

If
m=4^{q}
for
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and possibly
h=0
if
m=1
, then (
3.2
) also holds for
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
.

The proof is fully constructive and detailed in
Section

6.2
. In particular, a feasible assignment of the SNN parameters that ensure representation can be explicitly derived from the represented function. The number of neurons scales with
\sqrt{m}
rather than
m
, whereas the number of weights scales with
r+\sqrt{m}
. Thus, fewer output spikes of the function enable a representation by a more sparsely connected SNN.

Complementary to the upper bounds we also derive a lower bound showing that the number of non-zero network parameters
s
cannot be improved by more than an
O(\log(m+1))
-factor when
r\geq\Omega(\sqrt{m}).

Theorem 3.4

(Lower bound for the number of SNN parameters for single-input universal representation)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\in[m]
,
L\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1},s\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in(0,\infty]
.
If every
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
can be represented by an element of
\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
, then

s\geq\frac{r}{5\log(m+1)}.

The analogous statement also holds for

\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
.

The proof relies on quantitatively upper bounding the expressive power of feedforward SNNs for a fixed input spike train (
Theorem

6.5
) and lower bounding the number of distinct functions in
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
and
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
. Details are provided in
Section

6.4
. In particular, for
r=m
, we require
s=\Omega(m/\log(m))
weights.

Let us now comment on multiple aspects of the universal representation theorem.

(i)

The construction is independent of the memory coefficient
h\in(0,\infty]
. Intuitively one may suspect that larger memory increases the expressive power. However, for monotone scaling SNNs, rescaling the time axis shows that the expressive power of SNNs is identical for all
h\in(0,\infty)
and in particular no worse than that for
h=\infty
.

(ii)

All network weights in the SNN construction are integer valued, which appears to be necessary in some instances (see
Remark

4.1
) to ensure that the SNN satisfies monotone scaling. This observation could have interesting consequences for learning. If all input spikes occurred in some bounded interval, then the weights could also be chosen as real-valued without changing the SNN output. Insofar, integer weights ensure full representative power of an SNN unit for large interarrival times. We return to this point in
Remark

4.1
.

(iii)

Depth is necessary as it is impossible to represent certain input-dominated, causal, monotone scaling functions which are
3
-finite with a
shallow
, i.e., single hidden layer, feedforward SNN (
Proposition

B.1
). However, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in(0,\infty)
, all
m
-finite functions
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,m)
can be represented on the first
m
-input spikes by a shallow SNN with
m
hidden neurons as long as the interarrival time between consecutive input spikes are sufficiently homogeneous (
Proposition

B.2
).

(iv)

The number of negative weights in the construction in
Theorem

3.3
scales with order
\sqrt{m}
, whereas the number of positive weights is proportional to
r+\sqrt{m}
. For
r=\Omega(m),
the gap in the rate is
\Omega(\sqrt{m}).
Interestingly, also in biological neural networks more excitatory neurons (positive weights) than inhibitory neurons (negative weights) occur, with the typical ratio
4:1
for cortical neurons
(
Markram et al., 2004
;
Lehmann et al., 2012
;
Sahara et al., 2012
)
. In
Theorem

B.4
we construct an SNN with
m
output neurons and
O(m(1+\log^{2}m))
total neurons and non-zero weights, of which only
2\lceil\log(m)\rceil
are negative such that for any finite input spike train
\mathcal{I}
with cardinality
m
, the
j
-th output neuron
N_{j}
for
j\in[m]
spikes precisely at
\mathcal{I}_{(j)}
. This implies that
m
-finite functions (on spike trains with cardinality
m
) can also be implemented with SNNs with similar architectural constraints.
Conversely, at least two negative weights are necessary to ensure that certain outputs are representable, see
Lemma

B.3
.

(v)

The current construction in
Theorem

3.3
is optimized for SNNs with no upper bound on the out-degree (number of descendants of a node in the underlying graph). Under the additional assumption that every neuron is connected to at most
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\geq 4
, neurons in the next layer the required number of neurons to represent
m
-finite or
m
-periodic functions changes to
O(\max(m/q,\sqrt{m}))
(
Proposition

B.6
). In particular, by
Theorem

3.4
and
Lemma

6.7
the neuron count for general
q
cannot be improved up to logarithmic terms in
m
.

3.2
Multiple-Input Universal Representation

We now focus on multi-input functions
F\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}
with
d\geq 2
(although the following is still sensible for
d=1
) which are input-dominated, causal and satisfy the monotone scaling property. Unlike the single-input case, such functions are not fully determined by
F(\mathbb{N},\dots,\mathbb{N})
, as this only describes the output if all input spikes happen at the same time. To ensure finite representability using SNNs, we therefore impose the following assumption on the behavior of the function, which restricts the dependence of the output spike at time
t
to only a bounded number of past input spikes.

Definition 3.5

(Markovian memory)
.

An input-dominated, causal function
F\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}
satisfies the monotone scaling property has
Markovian memory
of order
m\in\mathbb{N}
, or is said to be
m
-Markovian, if for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}(\mathbb{N})
with
\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap(0,t]=[t]
it holds

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\cap\{t\}=\big(F(\mathcal{I}_{1}\cap(t-m,t],\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\cap(t-m,t])\big)\cap\{t\}.

If
m=1
, the function is called
memoryless
. For
r\in[2^{md}]
, the function
F
is called
r
-
sparse
if

\left|\left\{\textstyle(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{T}([m])^{d}\colon F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\neq\emptyset,\bigcup_{i\in[d]}\mathcal{I}_{i}=[m^{\prime}]\text{ for }m^{\prime}\in[m]\right\}\right|\leq r.

The class of all
m
-Markovian functions
F
which are
r
-sparse is denoted by

\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r).

Intuitively, the output of an
m
-Markovian function can only depend on the last
m
input spikes from each input spike train to determine whether there is an output spike at time
t
. This is natural as biological neurons typically have only limited memory, and thus the output at time
t
cannot depend on arbitrarily distant input spikes.

SNNs can also exactly represent causal, monotone scaling functions with bounded Markovian memory:

Theorem 3.6

(Multiple-input universal representation theorem)
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N},r\in[2^{md}]
with
h\in(0,\infty]
or possibly
h=0
if
m=1
. Then, there exists an architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
and a positive integer
s
satisfying
L=O(1+\log(m))
,
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(dm^{2}+r)
, and
s=O(dm^{2}r)
such that for any function
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
, there exists a feedforward SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})=F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\quad\text{ for all }\;\;\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}.

(3.3)

The proof is provided in
Section

6.1
and is also entirely constructive. It relies on building a memory-mechanism (using
O(dm)
neurons) within the SNN to store the relevant past input spikes, and subsequently representing a binary function with binary inputs (using
O(r)
neurons) by essentially constructing Boolean operations. The latter is based on the classical sum-of-products (disjunctive normal form) representation of Boolean functions, and it exploits that SNNs can efficiently represent the
AND
and
OR
functions with an arbitrary number of inputs.

Complementary to the representation result, we also derive a lower bound on the required number of network weights to represent all functions in
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
.

Theorem 3.7

(Lower bound for the number of SNN parameters for multiple-input universal representation)
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
,
r\in[2^{md}]
,
L\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1},s\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in(0,\infty]
.
If every
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
can be represented by an element of
\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
, then

s\geq\frac{r\land(2^{d}-1)^{m}}{5md}.

The proof of the lower bound is similar to the single-input case and based on a counting argument provided in
Section

6.4
.
Let us now discuss some more aspects of the result.

(i)

In the absence of any sparsity, we have
r=2^{md}
which is substantially larger compared to
r\leq m
in the single-input case. In conjunction with the lower bounds on the network connectivity, this highlights the optimality of the construction in
Theorem

3.6
up to polynomial factors in
m
and
d
and
the presence of a
curse of dimensionality
in the SNN representation of the class
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r=2^{md}).
However, it is not just the dimensionality
d
which leads to large architectures but also the memory parameter
m
. This is to be expected since for multiple inputs, the number of possible input spike patterns that can be realized within the memory window scales exponentially in
md
. Meanwhile for a single input, by the monotone scaling property, all input spike patterns with
m
spikes are essentially equivalent and lead to similar outputs (up to the exact spiking time). One structural assumption which mitigates the curse, however, is that
F
yields an output spike on a small fraction of input spike patterns.

(ii)

In Theorem
3.6
, the bound on the number of required network weights is of the same order as the number of neurons. We believe that by allowing for more intricate connectivity patterns and at the expense of a significantly more involved proof, the number of neurons could be reduced further. The lower bound in
Theorem

3.7
combined with
Lemma

6.7
however asserts that the number of neurons can be at best reduced to
\Omega(\sqrt{\frac{r\wedge((2^{d}-1)^{m}-1)}{dm}})
which still scales exponentially in
dm
for maximal
r
.

(iii)

Similar to the single-input case, the construction in the proof of
Theorem

3.6
does not depend on the choice of the memory parameter
h\in(0,\infty]
and utilizes integer weights only. If one were to impose an upper bound on the interarrival time between consecutive spikes in the input spike trains, then the construction could also be carried out for real-valued weights without changing the representability result, see
Remark

4.1
.
Notably, the use of real weights allows SNNs to be provable more expressive compared to SNNs with the same architecture and integer weights,
see
Lemma

C.12
. Characterizing the full representational power of SNNs with real-valued weights is an interesting direction for future research.

We close this subsection with two remarks on how the framework of input-dominated, causal, monotone scaling functions with Markovian memory can be utilized to model time-dependent and periodic functions.

Remark 3.8

(Time-dependent functions)
.

Functions satisfying the monotone scaling property are fully determined by the order at which the input spikes arrive. By incorporating an exogenous spike train, one can still obtain functions which depend on the actual timing of the spikes and not only on their ordering. To see this, take a fixed spike train
\mathcal{D}
and consider

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\mapsto F_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d},\mathcal{D})

for some
m
-Markovian function
F_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathbb{T}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{T}
. The output spike train of this map will depend on the actual timing of the input spike trains. This construction plays an important role in the proofs. For concrete examples, see the
IS-EQUAL
, and
CEIL
m
function from
Section

4
, as well as the
CLASSIFIER
D,R
function in
Section

5
.

Remark 3.9

(Periodic functions)
.

By Lemma
E.1
, the class of input-dominated, causal, Markovian functions excludes periodic functions. Nevertheless, composing a function of finite Markovian memory with a periodic function can yield a function which is periodic from some spike onwards. For example, composing an

m_{P}
-periodic function
F_{M}\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}
with an
m_{M}
-Markovian function
F_{P}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
, yields a function
F:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T},F(\mathcal{I}):=F_{M}(\mathcal{I},F_{P}(\mathcal{I}))
which is
m_{P}
-periodic from the
(m_{M}+1)
-st overall spike onwards (Lemma
E.2
).

3.3
Efficient Representation of Compositional Sparse Functions

As a consequence of the representation results for single and multiple inputs, we also obtain refined representation statements for compositions of monotone scaling functions. To this end, we consider for
\mathbf{d}\coloneqq(d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
with
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
d_{0}=d
, and
d_{q}=1
functions of the form

\displaystyle F=F_{q}\circ F_{q-1}\circ\dots\circ F_{1},

with input-dominated, causal, monotone scaling functions
F_{\ell}\colon\mathbb{T}^{{d_{\ell-1}}}\to\mathbb{T}^{{d_{\ell}}}
for
\ell=1,\dots,q.
According to the lower bound in
Theorem

3.7
, the number of required weights to represent a general function in
F_{\ell}
grows at least with order
\Omega(d_{\ell}2^{d_{\ell-1}m_{\ell}}/(d_{\ell-1}m_{\ell}))
if
F_{\ell}
is
m_{\ell}
-Markovian. Sparsity assumptions can reduce the number of network weights. However, sparsity is not preserved if one artificially enlarges the input dimension
d_{\ell-1}
of
F_{\ell}
by adding irrelevant input spike trains:

Example 3.10
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\textup{mm}}(2,m,r)
for
m\in\mathbb{N},r\in[2^{2m}]
, and define for
d\in\mathbb{N}
the function
G\colon\mathbb{T}^{d+2}\to\mathbb{T},G(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d+2})=F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})
. Then,
G
is not
m
-Markovian, since the output will depend on spikes in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
which may not be among the last
m
spikes of
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{I}_{d+2}
. Further, for any
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}\in\mathbb{T}([m])
with
F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2})\neq\emptyset
, it follows for any
\mathcal{I}_{3},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d+2}\in\mathbb{T}([m])
that
G(\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d+2})\neq\emptyset
, resulting in
G
being at best
2^{md}
-sparse.

To remedy this issue, which would suggest unnecessarily large architectures to represent functions with few relevant inputs,
we assume that for each
\ell\in[q]
, there exist integers
t_{\ell-1}\in\{0,\dots,d_{\ell-1}\}
,
m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
r_{\ell}\leq m_{\ell}
if
t_{\ell-1}\leq 1
and
r_{\ell}\leq 2^{m_{\ell}t_{\ell-1}}
if
t_{\ell-1}\geq 2
such that
F_{\ell}=(F_{\ell,k})_{k\in[d_{\ell}]}
and each component function satisfies

\displaystyle F_{\ell,k}\in\mathbb{F}(t_{\ell-1},m_{\ell},r_{\ell})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(4^{\lceil\log_{4}(m_{\ell})\rceil},r_{\ell}),&\text{ if }t_{\ell-1}=0,\\
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m_{\ell},r_{\ell}),&\text{ if }t_{\ell-1}=1,\\
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(t_{\ell-1},m_{\ell},r_{\ell}),&\text{ if }t_{\ell-1}\geq 2.\end{cases}

This means that each component only depends on at most
\max(t_{\ell-1},1)
inputs, and that the output is characterized by the last
O(m_{\ell})
time-points. The corresponding function class of compositional functions is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(q,\mathbf{d},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{r})\coloneqq\Big\{F=F_{q}\circ\dots\circ F_{1}\;\;:\;\;

\displaystyle F_{\ell}=(F_{\ell,k})_{k\in[d_{\ell}]}\colon\mathbb{T}^{{d_{\ell-1}}}\to\mathbb{T}^{{d_{\ell}}},

\displaystyle F_{\ell,k}\in\mathbb{F}(t_{\ell-1},m_{\ell},r_{\ell})\text{ for all }k\in[d_{\ell}],\ell\in[q]\Big\},

where
\mathbf{t}\coloneqq(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{q-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{q}_{0},\mathbf{m}\coloneqq(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
,
\mathbf{r}\coloneqq(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{q})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{q}
. In Sections
4
and
5
, we illustrate how this compositional framework can be used to model relevant functions for spike train processing.

Theorem 3.11

(SNN representation of compositional functions)
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{d},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{r}
be as above with
d_{0}=d
and
d_{q}=1
with
h\in(0,\infty]
. Then, there exist parameters
L=O(\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}1+\log(m_{\ell}))
,
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{L}
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}d_{\ell}\left[m_{\ell}^{2}(\max(1,t_{\ell}))+r_{\ell}\right]\right)
and
s=O\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}d_{\ell}m_{\ell}^{2}\max(1,t_{\ell})r_{\ell}\right)

such that for any function
F\in\mathbb{F}(q,\mathbf{d},\mathbf{t},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{r})
there exists a feedforward SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})=F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\quad\text{ for all }\;\;\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}.

The proof of
Theorem

3.11
is a direct consequence of Theorems
3.3
and
3.6
by constructing for each component function
F_{\ell,k}
an SNN according to the respective theorem’s architecture and subsequently stacking the SNNs on top of each other.

Let us summarize the key insights of Theorem
3.11
.

(i)

By the universal representation theorems, a function is efficiently representable if it has small memory or few inputs.
Theorem

3.11
shows that also the composition of functions with small memory and many inputs with a function that has large memory and few relevant inputs can be efficiently represented.

(ii)

A key conceptual benefit of the compositional framework is that periodic functions with a large period can be generated by composing periodic functions with a short period. In particular, this allows efficiently representing functions which depend on a rather long history of input spikes using functions which all admit small memory. For this to be possible, information from past spikes needs to be repeated and successively condensed into a few spikes so that they can be passed on to later components. This process may be interpreted as extracting and temporarily storing relevant features from the past spike patterns.

(iii)

Composing functions also allows processing information from many input spike trains by stacking functions on top of each other, where each component function only depends on a small subset of the inputs. As small SNNs can represent the standard Boolean logical operations (
Section

4.2
), an example of such a compositional structure are SNNs that emulate Boolean circuits.

4
Examples of Efficiently Representable Functions

In the following, we provide several examples for input-dominated, causal, functions satisfying the monotone scaling property and admitting an efficient SNN representation. The representation of all these functions is detailed in Appendix
C
, some figures below already illustrate the constructions.

4.1
Finite or Periodic Single-Input Functions

The following functions operate on a single-input spike train and are either finite or periodic. Specific constructions for SNNs representing these functions are illustrated in
Figure

4
.

•

SKIP
function: Given an input spike train
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}

5
Classification of Spike Trains

In this section, the goal is to detect whether specific reference spike patterns occur in a given input spike train. If yes, then a classifier SNN should output a spike, and otherwise no spike should be produced. We believe that this is a crucial property for SNN-driven pattern recognition, temporal sequence processing, and decision-making.

In the following, we first mathematically formalize the classification task and introduce the classifier function. We only focus on the single-input case. The extension to multiple-input spike trains is straightforward, see
Remark

5.4
. Afterward, we show that the classifier function can be efficiently represented by SNNs and discuss for which classes the representation is simple/hard.

We follow the framework of input-dominated, causal, monotone scaling functions introduced in
Section

3
. However, to allow for time-dependent functions, that is, functions whose output at time
t
may depend on the absolute time
t
and not only on the relative timing of the input spikes, we additionally consider an exogenous spike train
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{T}
which serves as a time reference or temporal grid as outlined in
Remark

3.8
. For a given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider a (non-empty)
class of reference patterns

\mathfrak{R}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}.
Each element
R\in\mathfrak{R}
is a binary vector of length
m
encoding a specific pattern of one’s and zero’s, encoding the presence/absence of a spike. The respective classifier function should then assess whether the most recent
m
spikes from the input spike train correspond to one of the reference patterns in
\mathfrak{R}
and is defined as follows.

Definition 5.1

(Classifier function)
.

Given
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{T},
a positive integer
m\leq|\mathcal{D}|
, a class of reference patterns
\mathfrak{R}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m},
and an input spike train
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}
, the output
\mathcal{O}
of the
CLASSIFIER
D,R
function is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\left\{\mathcal{D}_{(n)}:\Big(\mathds{1}(\mathcal{D}_{(n-m+i)}\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}})\Big)_{i\in[m]}\in\mathfrak{R},n\in[|\mathcal{D}|],n\geq m\right\},\qquad\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\textup{CEIL}_{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D}).

The above function can be interpreted as follows. At each time point
D_{(n)}
from the dominating spike train
\mathcal{D}
, the function assesses the last
m
spikes from the ceiling counterpart
\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}
of the input spike train
\mathcal{I}
with respect to
\mathcal{D}
. If the resulting binary vector indicating whether a spike occurred at these
m
time points belongs to the reference class
\mathfrak{R}
, then the function outputs a spike at time
D_{(n)}
. Otherwise, no spike is produced. As a function of both input
\mathcal{I}
and dominating spike train
\mathcal{D}
,
CLASSIFIER
D,R
is input-dominated, causal, satisfies the monotone scaling property, and has
m
-Markovian memory.

In the following, we show that this function can be efficiently represented by SNNs.

Theorem 5.2

(Classifier universal representation theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{T}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leq|\mathcal{D}|
,
r\in[2^{m}]
and
h\in(0,\infty]
. Then, there exists an architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
and a positive integer
s
satisfying
L=O(1+\log(m))
,
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(m^{2}+r),
and
s=O(m^{2}+mr)
such that for any
\mathfrak{R}\subseteq{\{0,1\}^{m}}
with
|\mathfrak{R}|\leq r
, there exists an SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with memory
h
, called classifier SNN, such that

\displaystyle\textup{{CLASSIFIER}}_{\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{R}}(\mathcal{I})=f(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D})\quad\text{ for all }\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}.

The classifier universal representation theorem states that a pattern class
\mathfrak{R}
with elements of length
m
can be efficiently implemented if either the function itself or its complement contains only a few patterns. In particular, the index
r
can be interpreted as a sparsity index for the classifier function. Notably, we also see that depth of the network is completely independent of the complexity of the class
\mathfrak{R}
and only logarithmically dependent on the length
m
of the patterns in
\mathfrak{R}
. Conversely, this suggests that deep SNNs are capable of classifying extremely long patterns efficiently.

The proof of
Theorem

5.2
is similar to the proof of the multiple-input universal representation theorem. It relies on first utilizing the
CEIL
1
operation to construct
\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}
, then composing it with the memory module
MEMORY
m
to store the last
m
spikes from the input spike train
\mathcal{I}
, and then constructing a memoryless function which assesses whether the outputs of the
MEMORY
m
correspond to a pattern in
\mathfrak{R}
. Details are deferred to
Section

6.3
.

To demonstrate the implications of the above representation result, we provide some examples for specific classes
\mathfrak{R}
and discuss the complexity of the resulting classifier SNNs.

Example 5.3
.

We apply
Theorem

5.2
to specific classes of reference patterns
\mathfrak{R}\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
. This ensures that in all considered cases, there exists a classifier SNN with depth of order
L=O(1+\log(m))
. Below we provide bounds for the width and the number of parameters.

(i)

(Singleton class) If
\mathfrak{R}
consists of a single pattern, then,
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}+s=O(m^{2}).

(ii)

(Translational invariant class) Given
\mathfrak{R}_{0}\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
consider the translational invariant class
\mathfrak{R}\coloneqq\{(R_{i+k\bmod m})_{i\in[m]}\colon R\in\mathfrak{R}_{0},\,k=0,\dots,m-1\}.
Then,
|\mathfrak{R}|\leq m\cdot|\mathfrak{R}_{0}|,

\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(m^{2}+m\cdot|\mathfrak{R}_{0}|)
and
s=O(m^{2}\cdot|\mathfrak{R}_{0}|)
parameters.

(iii)

(Ball environment class) Given
\mathfrak{R}_{0}\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
and a radius parameter
b\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, consider the ball environment class
\mathfrak{R}\coloneqq\{R^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{m}|R_{i}-R_{i}^{\prime}|\leq b\text{ for some }R\in\mathfrak{R}_{0}\}.
Then
|\mathfrak{R}|\leq|\mathfrak{R}_{0}|\sum_{j=0}^{b}\binom{m}{j}=:M,

\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(m^{2}+M),
and
s=O(mM).

(iv)

(Complementary class) Let
\mathfrak{R}=\{0,1\}^{m}\backslash\mathfrak{R}_{0}
for some pattern class
\mathfrak{R}_{0}
. Then, the classifier SNN
f_{\mathfrak{R}}
can be realized by negating the output of the classifier SNN
f_{\mathfrak{R}_{0}}
using the
MINUS
-function (introduced in
Section

4.2
) whenever there is an input spike. Hence, efficient representation is possible if
\min(|\mathfrak{R}|,2^{d}-|\mathfrak{R}|)
is small.

We conclude this section with three remarks on extending the classification framework.

Remark 5.4

(Multiple-input spike train classification)
.

The classification framework can be naturally extended to multiple-input spike trains. In this case, the classifier function would assess whether the most recent
m
spikes from each input spike train correspond to a multidimensional reference pattern in a given class
\mathfrak{R}
. Moreover, the resulting classifier SNN would scale with order
L=O(1+\log(m))
with
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(m^{2}d+r)
where
r\coloneqq|\mathfrak{R}|
, see
Remark

6.4
. In the worst-case the size of
\mathfrak{R}
(and its complement) scales as
\Omega(2^{md})
, which leads to an architecture that is exponential in
m
and
d
. Combinatorial arguments suggest that this is unavoidable. However, under more structure in
\mathfrak{R}
, say, if
\mathfrak{R}
is characterized by some compositional nature, more efficient SNN representations are possible.

Remark 5.5

(Post-processing after classification)
.

The classifier SNN considered here spikes exactly once if it identifies that the input spike pattern of the most recent spikes belongs to the reference class
\mathfrak{R}
. This output can be immediately used to trigger further processing in a downstream task. Via the
REPEAT
k
function introduced in
Section

4.3
, the output can also be repeated
k\in\mathbb{N}
times to reinforce the classification decision. This approach would also reconcile the two perspectives described in the introduction how the brain may process information, that is, whether information is encoded in the timing of the spike trains or the frequency of spikes. On the one hand, for a spike train to be classified to lie in a specific class, the specific spiking times of the input spike train are relevant. On the other hand, once an input spike train is identified to belong to this class, an optimal classifier SNN spikes as often as possible. Meanwhile, the classifier should be able to stop spiking (and thus forget) after some time, which is captured by the fact that the classification output is only repeated finitely many times.

m
-Markovian function.

To show the assertion for
h\in(0,\infty]
, we denote the spike trains for the
d
inputs by
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
and apply the function
MEMORY
m
from Lemma
6.1
to them. By (
4.1
), we end up with
dm
neurons with spike trains
(\mathcal{J}_{j}^{i})_{i\in[d],j\in[m]}
where

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{i}=\textup{DELAY}_{j}\textstyle\left(\mathcal{I}_{i},\bigcup_{k\in[d]}\mathcal{I}_{k}\right).

Further, define the binary function
J_{j}^{i}(t)\coloneqq\mathds{1}(t\in\mathcal{J}_{j}^{i})
for
t\in(0,\infty)
. By causality and the monotone scaling property, we may, without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}(\mathbb{N})
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{i}=\mathbb{N}
, and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
F
is
m
-Markovian and input-dominated, for any
t
, the indicator
\mathds{1}(t\in F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d}))
depends only on the spike trains
(\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap(t-m,t])_{i\in[d]}
, which are completely determined by the binary vector
(J_{1}^{1}(t),\ldots,J_{m}^{1}(t),\ldots,J_{1}^{d}(t),\ldots,J_{m}^{d}(t))
. Hence, there exists a Boolean function
F_{\mathrm{B}}:\{0,1\}^{md}\to\{0,1\}
with
F_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathbf{0})=0
such that for all
t\in(0,\infty)
it holds

\displaystyle t\in F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\quad\iff\quad F_{\mathrm{B}}\big(J_{1}^{1}(t),\ldots,J_{m}^{1}(t),\ldots,J_{1}^{d}(t),\ldots,J_{m}^{d}(t)\big)=1.

We now use
Lemma

6.2
(applied with input dimension
md
) to represent
F_{\mathrm{B}}
with an SNN
f_{\mathrm{B}}
with constant depth
4
,
O(md+r_{\mathrm{B}})
neurons, and
O(mdr_{\mathrm{B}})
non-zero weights, where
r_{\mathrm{B}}:=|\{y\in\{0,1\}^{md}:F_{\mathrm{B}}(y)=1\}|
. Since
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
is
r
-sparse, we have
r_{\mathrm{B}}\leq r
. Composing the memory module
MEMORY
m
from
Lemma

6.1
with the SNN
f_{B}
which represents
F_{B}
yields an SNN
f
whose output neuron spikes at time
t
if and only if
t\in F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})
. Hence,
f(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})=F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})
. By
Lemma

6.1
and
Lemma

6.2
, the network
f
has depth
L=O(1+\log(m))
, a total number of neurons
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(dm^{2}+r)
, and at most
s=O(dm^{2}r)
non-zero weights.

Finally, for the case
h=0
and
m=1
,
one can directly apply
Lemma

6.2
to the input spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
to obtain an SNN with the desired representation property.
∎

6.2
Proof of the Single-Input Universal Representation Theorem

For the proof of
Theorem

3.3
we rely on the following lemma, which shows that the
SPIKE
1
and
CEIL
1

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
h\in(0,\infty]
, there exist feedforward SNNs
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1},s_{1})
and
g\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L_{2},\mathbf{p}_{2},s_{2})
with bounded architecture,

\sum_{i\in[2]}L_{i}+\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{1}+s_{i}=O(1)
, such that

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{I})=\textup{{SPIKE}}_{1}(\mathcal{I}),\quad g(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D})=\textup{{CEIL}}_{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D})\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{T}.

The statement is a direct consequence of
Lemma

C.3
(i)
and
Lemma

C.7
. We now proceed with the proof of
Theorem

3.3
.
Figure

7
depicts the architecture of the constructed feedforward SNN.

:
Architecture of the single-input SNN from proof of
Theorem

3.3
for
m=16
.

F
to be represented is finite (resp. periodic), then the third block contains a componentwise
SPIKE
1
(resp.
SKIP
) function. The exact representation of

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

If
m=1
and
F
is
1
-periodic, then one can use for
h\in[0,\infty]
a SNN with a single output neuron that is directly connected to the input neuron with weight
2
if
F(\mathbb{N})=\mathbb{N}
or weight
0
if
F(\mathbb{N})=\emptyset
.

The construction for the SNN in the more general case consists of several different steps. For the proof, we define
\overline{m}\coloneqq 4^{\lceil\log_{4}(m)\rceil}\geq m
. In particular, any function which is
m
-finite and
r
-sparse is in particular also
\overline{m}
-finite and
r
-sparse. In the periodic case, we assumed
m=4^{q}
, thus
\overline{m}=m
. Further, without loss of generality, we can assume in the following that the input spike train
\mathcal{I}
fulfills
|\mathcal{I}|=\infty
. Given that SNNs are causal, this implies that the same assertions apply if
\mathcal{I}
consists of only finitely many spikes.

Step 1: Coarse splitting of the input spike train.

We first apply the composition of
\log(\overline{m})/2
(which is an integer by definition of
\overline{m}
)
ODD
functions to the input neuron and denote the output neuron by
v_{1,1}
. In parallel, we attach enough
SKIP
connections to the input neurons until the depth is synchronized, the corresponding output neuron is denoted by
v_{1,2}
. The spike trains of
v_{1,1}
and
v_{1,2}
are denoted by
\mathcal{V}_{1,1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1,2}
, respectively. By construction,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1,1}=\{\mathcal{I}_{(\sqrt{\overline{m}}\cdot k+1)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},\quad\mathcal{V}_{1,2}=\mathcal{I}.

This requires depth
O(\log(\overline{m}))
and
\sqrt{\overline{m}}
neurons and non-zero weights.

Step 2: Fine splitting.

We now apply a
CLOCK
{}_{\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
function to the neurons
v_{1,1}
and
v_{1,2}
, which leads to
2\sqrt{\overline{m}}
neurons
v_{2,1},\dots,v_{2,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
with spike trains
\mathcal{V}_{2,1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{2,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2,j}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+\sqrt{\overline{m}}(j-1)+1)}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},\quad\mathcal{V}_{2,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j}=\{\mathcal{I}_{(\sqrt{\overline{m}}\cdot k+j)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\quad\text{ for }j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}].

For
j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}]
, each spike train
\mathcal{V}_{2,j}
only spikes once in a period of length
\overline{m}
, while each spike train
\mathcal{V}_{2,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j}
spikes
\sqrt{\overline{m}}
times in a period of length
\overline{m}
.
This requires depth
O(\log(\overline{m})),
as well as
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})
neurons and non-zero weights.

Step 3: Finite or periodic functions.
If the function
F
to be represented is
\overline{m}
-finite, then we apply the
SPIKE
1
function from
Lemma

6.3
to each neuron
v_{2,1},\dots,v_{2,\sqrt{\overline{m}}},
denote the output neurons by
v_{3,1},\dots,v_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
and their spike trains by
\mathcal{V}_{3,1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
.
For the other neurons
v_{2,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+1},\dots,v_{2,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
, we attach
SKIP
connections and denote the spike trains by
\mathcal{V}_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{3,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
.
Meanwhile, if the function to be represented is
\overline{m}
-periodic, then we instead attach
SKIP
connections to all neurons
v_{2,1},\dots,v_{2,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
, and keep the notation identical. Notably, for periodic functions this step could also be omitted entirely.

The construction gives spike trains
\mathcal{V}_{3,1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{3,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}.
For
j\in[\overline{m}]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{3,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\{\mathcal{I}_{(\sqrt{\overline{m}}(j-1)+1)}\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-finite},\\
\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+\sqrt{\overline{m}}(j-1)+1)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-periodic},\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{V}_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j}=\mathcal{V}_{2,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j},

and it needs
O(1)
layers and
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})
neurons and non-zero weights.

Step 4: Representation block.
We now compose the neurons
v_{3,1},\dots,v_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
with another layer of neurons to obtain
v_{4,1},\dots,v_{4,\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
. The weight
w_{ji}
between neuron
v_{3,i}
and neuron
v_{4,j}
is set equal to
2
if
(i-1)\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j\in F([\overline{m}])
and otherwise set to zero. This ensures that the spike train
\mathcal{V}_{4,j}
of neuron
v_{4,j}
for
j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{4,j}=\begin{cases}\{\mathcal{I}_{(\sqrt{\overline{m}}(i-1)+1)}:i\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}],(i-1)\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-finite},\\
\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+\sqrt{\overline{m}}(i-1)+1)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}],(i-1)\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-periodic}.\end{cases}

In parallel, we attach a
SKIP
connection to each neuron
v_{3,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+1},\dots,v_{3,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
and denote the spike trains by
\mathcal{V}_{4,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{4,2\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
.

Overall, the representation block involves a single layer,
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})
neurons and
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}}+r)
non-zero weights where
r
denotes the sparsity index of
F
.

Step 5: Delay block.
We now aim to shift the spike trains of
v_{4,1},\dots,v_{4,\sqrt{\overline{m}}}
to the correct position, as they can currently only spike whenever
\mathcal{V}_{1,1}
spikes. To this end, we apply to each pair
v_{4,j}
and
v_{4,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j}
for
j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}]
the
CEIL
1
function from
Lemma

6.3
, where the respective inputs are selected as
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\mathcal{V}_{4,j}
and
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\mathcal{V}_{4,\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j}
. We denote the resulting output neuron by
v_{5,j}
with spike train
\mathcal{V}_{5,j}
, which by construction is given for each
j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}]
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{5,j}=\begin{cases}\{\mathcal{I}_{(\sqrt{\overline{m}}(i-1)+j)}:i\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}],(i-1)\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-finite},\\
\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+\sqrt{\overline{m}}(i-1)+j)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}],(i-1)\sqrt{\overline{m}}+j\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-periodic}.\end{cases}

This step requires
O(1)
layers,
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})
neurons and
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})
non-zero weights.

Step 6: Output.

Consider a single output neuron
o
by attaching weight
2
from each neuron
v_{5,j}
for
j\in[\sqrt{\overline{m}}]
to
o
. The spike train
\mathcal{O}
of
o
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\begin{cases}\{\mathcal{I}_{(t)}:t\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-finite},\\
\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+t)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},t\in F([\overline{m}])\},&\text{ if }F\text{ is $\overline{m}$-periodic},\end{cases}

which equals
F(\mathcal{I})
by construction. Collectively, this construction requires
O(\log(\overline{m}))=O(1+\log(m))
layers,
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}})=O(\sqrt{m})
neurons and
O(\sqrt{\overline{m}}+r)=O(\sqrt{m}+r)
non-zero weights. This concludes the proof.
∎

6.3
Proof of Classifier Universal Representation Theorem

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

We first rewrite
CLASSIFIER
D,R
into a composition of simpler functions.
Let
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}
be the input spike train and let
\mathcal{D}
be the exogenous dominating spike train. In the following, we first show that there exists a
1
-Markovian function
F_{\mathfrak{R}}:\mathbb{T}^{2m}\to\mathbb{T}
, fully determined by
\mathfrak{R}
, such that

\textup{CLASSIFIER}_{\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{R}}(\mathcal{I})=F_{\mathfrak{R}}\Big(\textup{MEMORY}_{m}(\textup{CEIL}_{1}\big(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D}\big),\mathcal{D})\Big)\quad\text{ for all }\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}.

(6.1)

To define
F_{\mathfrak{R}}
, note that the output of the memory function
\textup{MEMORY}_{m}(\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D})
, with
\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\textup{CEIL}_{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{D})
, comprises
2m
output spike trains
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{i}
for
i\in[2]
and
j\in[m]
where, by domination
\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}\triangleleft\mathcal{D}
, it holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[m]}\mathds{1}(\mathcal{D}_{(n)}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2})\begin{cases}\leq m-1&\text{ if }n\leq m-1,\\
=m&\text{ if }n\geq m.\end{cases}

Moreover, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq m
and
j\in[m]
the following equivalence is met,

\mathcal{D}_{(n)}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{D}_{(n-m+j)}\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathcal{D}}.

The function
F_{\mathfrak{R}}
is then defined as a function acting on the
2m
input spike trains
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{i}
for
i\in[2]
and
j\in[m]
via

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\left(t\in F_{\mathfrak{R}}(\mathcal{J}_{1}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{m}^{1},\mathcal{J}_{1}^{2},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{m}^{2})\right)=\mathds{1}\left((\mathds{1}(t\in\mathcal{J}_{j}^{1}))_{j\in[m]}\in\mathfrak{R}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{j\in[m]}\mathds{1}(t\in\mathcal{J}_{j}^{2})=m\right)

for all
t\in(0,\infty)
. In particular, note that
F_{\mathfrak{R}}
is
1
-Markovian since the output at time
t
only depends on the input spike trains at time
t
, and that it is
|\mathfrak{R}|
-sparse.
Combining the above three displays, we conclude that (
6.1
) is satisfied.

For the representation of the
\textup{CLASSIFIER}_{\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{R}}
-function with a feedforward SNN we first apply a feedforward SNN representing the
\textup{CEIL}_{1}
-function to the two input neurons. In parallel, we add sufficiently many
SKIP
-connections to the neuron with spike train
\mathcal{D}
. By
Lemma

6.3
this architecture has
O(1)
depth,
O(1)
neurons, and
O(1)
non-zero weights. Next, we attach the memory module
\textup{MEMORY}_{m}
from
Lemma

6.1
, which has
O(1+\log(m))
layers,
O(m^{2})
neurons, and
O(m^{2})
non-zero weights. Finally, we apply the function
F_{\mathfrak{R}}
to the output of the memory module. By
Lemma

6.2
,
F_{\mathfrak{R}}
can be represented by a feedforward SNN with
O(1)
depth,
O(m+|\mathfrak{R}|)
neurons, and
O(m|\mathfrak{R}|)
non-zero weights. Combined, this yields a feedforward SNN representing
\textup{CLASSIFIER}_{\mathcal{D},\mathfrak{R}}
with
O(1+\log(m))
depth,
O(m^{2}+|\mathfrak{R}|)
neurons, and
O(m^{2}+m|\mathfrak{R}|)
non-zero weights.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

Our proof also naturally extends to the multivariate setting with

6.4
Proof for the Complexity Upper Bound of SNN Computational Units

To prove the lower bounds in Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
, we first derive an upper bound on the number of distinct functions that a class of feedforward SNNs with fixed architecture can represent over a given set of input spike trains. A comparison with lower bounds on the number of distinct functions within the function classes
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
,
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
, and
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
, yields then lower bounds on the number of required SNN network parameters to represent all functions within these classes.

Theorem 6.5

(Expressiveness of feedforward SNNs)
.

Consider a finite collection of
d
-tuples of spike trains
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
and set
n\coloneqq|\mathbb{I}|
. Enumerate its elements by
\mathbb{I}=\left\{\smash{(\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{d})},i\in[n]\right\},
define
T_{i}:=|\bigcup_{j\in[d]}\smash{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{j}}|
, and set

T_{\operatorname{sum}}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}(T_{i}+1)^{2}
.
Then, for any
h\in[0,\infty]
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{p}:=(d,p_{1},\ldots,p_{L})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
, and
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(p_{\ell-1}\vee p_{\ell})
,
it holds

\displaystyle\big|\left\{\mathbb{f}\colon\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T}^{p_{L}}\colon\mathbb{f}\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)\right\}\big|\leq\big(8es^{2}T_{\operatorname{sum}}\big)^{s}.

If
\mathbb{I}
consists of a single spike train
(d=1)
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
spikes, the bound simplifies to
\big(8es^{2}(m+1)^{2}\big)^{s}
. This confirms that the number of distinct functions grows at most polynomially in
m
and exponentially in
s
. Likewise, if
\mathbb{I}
contains multiple spike trains, the expressiveness scales polynomially in
n
and the total number of spikes among
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
, but it scales exponentially in the number of weights.
The proof of
Theorem

6.5
is detailed in
Appendix

D
.

We now prove the lower bound on the required number of SNN parameters for single-input universal representation.

Proof of
Theorem

3.4
.

By the monotone scaling property, it suffices to consider the input spike train
\mathcal{I}:=[m]
.
For every
\mathcal{O}\subseteq[m]
with
|\mathcal{O}|\leq r
, there exists
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
such that
F(\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{O}
.
This means that the cardinality of possible output spike trains of feedforward SNNs in
\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with input
\mathcal{I}
should be at least
\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{m}{k}
.
By applying Theorem
6.5
with
d=p_{L}=1
,
\mathbb{I}=\{\mathcal{I}\}
and
T_{\operatorname{sum}}=(m+1)^{2}
, we get

\big(8es^{2}(m+1)^{2}\big)^{s}\geq\left|\big\{f(\mathcal{I}):f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)\big\}\right|\geq\left|\big\{\mathcal{O}\subset[m],|\mathcal{O}|\leq r\big\}\right|=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\binom{m}{k}\geq 2^{r},

where we used
\tbinom{m}{k}\geq\tbinom{r}{k}
for the last inequality. This yields
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and since
8e\leq 21.75<25=5^{2}
, we have

s\geq\frac{r}{\log(8es^{2}(m+1)^{2})}\geq\frac{r}{2\log(5s(m+1))}.

We thus arrive, since
r\leq m
, at

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m+1}{5}\mathds{1}\left(s\geq\frac{m+1}{5}\right)+\frac{r}{2\log\big(5s(m+1)\big)}\mathds{1}\left(1\leq s<\frac{m+1}{5}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m+1}{5}\mathds{1}\left(s\geq\frac{m+1}{5}\right)+\frac{r}{4\log(m+1)}\mathds{1}\left(1\leq s<\frac{m+1}{5}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m+1}{5}\land\frac{r}{4\log(m+1)}\geq\frac{r}{5\log(m+1)}.

The assertion for
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
can be obtained in the same way.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.7
.

We consider the set of input spike trains

\mathbb{I}:=\Big\{(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{T}([m])^{d}:\textstyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{j}=[m]\Big\}.

If
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{I}
,
then for each
t\in[m]
at least one of the input spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
should include
t
, and hence the number of possible values of
(\mathds{1}(t\in\mathcal{I}_{j}))_{j\in[d]}
is
2^{d}-1
.
This yields
|\mathbb{I}|=(2^{d}-1)^{m}
.
By applying Theorem
6.5
with
d_{0}=d
,
d_{L}=1,
and

T_{\operatorname{sum}}=\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{I}}(m+1)^{2}=(2^{d}-1)^{m}(m+1)^{2},

we get

\Big|\big\{f\colon\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T},f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{d},s)\big\}\Big|\leq\big(8es^{2}(2^{d}-1)^{m}(m+1)^{2}\big)^{s}.

Let
a(r):=r\land(2^{d}-1)^{m}
. According to Lemma
6.6
below we have

\Big|\big\{\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T},(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\mapsto F(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\;\colon\;F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)\big\}\Big|\geq 2^{a(r)}.

Hence, for universal representation it is necessary that

s\log(8es^{2}(2^{d}-1)^{m}(m+1)^{2})\geq a(r)
, which implies
s\in\mathbb{N}

Lemma 6.7

Given a feedforward SNN with architecture

A.2
Casting SNNs as Recurrent ANNs with State-Space Block

For the representation of the SNN layer in terms of an equivalent recurrent ANN with state block we make the following conventions. In the SNN layer, the input consists of spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{\operatorname{in}}}
which are all dominated by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}
.
For the ANN, the different input spike trains are encoded as a sequential input vector
(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathbf{x}^{(t)}:=(\mathds{1}(t\delta\in\mathcal{I}_{j}))_{j\in[p_{\operatorname{in}}]}\in\{0,1\}^{p_{\operatorname{in}}},\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.

Conversely, the sequential input vector
(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
can be used to reconstruct the input spike trains via

\mathcal{I}_{j}=\{\delta t:t\in\mathbb{N},x_{j}^{(t)}=1\}\quad\text{for }j\in[p_{\operatorname{in}}].

To model an SNN layer
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h,W}:\mathbb{T}^{p_{\operatorname{in}}}\to\mathbb{T}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}}
with
h\in[0,\infty]
and weight matrix
W:=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{p_{\operatorname{out}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{in}}}
, we introduce a nonlinear state-space blocks. This state-space block consists of
2p_{\operatorname{out}}
hidden neurons, where for each output neuron, one hidden neuron encodes the potential and the other models the output spike train. Concretely, it is parametrized by matrices
W^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{in}}},V\in\mathbb{R}^{2p_{\operatorname{out}}\times 2p_{\operatorname{out}}}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times 2p_{\operatorname{out}}}
. Given a sequence of
p_{\operatorname{in}}
-dimensional input vectors
(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{\infty}
, the activations of the values of the hidden neurons vector
\mathbf{h}^{(t)}
and the output neurons vector
\mathbf{o}^{(t)}
are updated according to

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{(t)}

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Big(W^{\prime}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}+V\mathbf{h}^{(t-1)}\Big),\qquad\mathbf{o}^{(t)}:=U\mathbf{h}^{(t)}

where
\mathbf{h}^{(0)}:=\mathbf{0}_{2p_{\operatorname{out}}}
and the activation function
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{2p_{\operatorname{out}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2p_{\operatorname{out}}}
acts component-wise and is defined for
i\in[2p_{\operatorname{out}}]
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(z):=\begin{cases}\mathds{1}(z>1),&\text{if }i\leq p_{\operatorname{out}},\\
z\mathds{1}(0\leq z\leq 1),&\text{if }i>p_{\operatorname{out}}.\end{cases}

To imitate the behavior of the SNN layer, we set the parameters as

\displaystyle W^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}W\\
W\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\quad V:=\begin{pmatrix}0_{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{out}}}&e^{-\delta/h}I_{p_{\operatorname{out}}}\\
0_{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{out}}}&e^{-\delta/h}I_{p_{\operatorname{out}}}\end{pmatrix},\quad U:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p_{\operatorname{out}}}&0_{p_{\operatorname{out}}\times p_{\operatorname{out}}}\end{pmatrix}.

We now prove that the
j
-th entry of the output neuron vector
\mathbf{o}^{(t)}
of the state-space block models equals one if and only if there is a spike in the
j
-th output neuron of the SNN layer at time
t\delta
. To see this, note that the
j
-th entry for
j\in[p_{\operatorname{out}}]
of the hidden neuron activation vector
h^{(t)}
at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle h^{(t)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathds{1}(r_{j}^{(t)}>1),&j\in[p_{\operatorname{out}}],\\
r_{j}^{(t)}\mathds{1}(0\leq r_{j}^{(t)}\leq 1),&j\in[p_{\operatorname{out}}]+p_{\operatorname{out}},\end{cases}\quad r_{j}^{(t)}:=\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}+e^{-\delta/h}h^{(t-1)}_{j+p_{\operatorname{out}}}.

Hence,
h^{(t)}_{j+p_{\operatorname{out}}}
for
j\in[p_{\operatorname{out}}]
equals the neuronwise potential among each computational unit
P(t)
in (
2.1
). Finally, we get

\mathbf{o}^{(t)}=(h^{(t)}_{j})_{j\in[p_{\operatorname{out}}]}=\mathds{1}\Big(t\delta\in\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{p_{\operatorname{in}}})\Big).

Altogether, this confirms that the SNN layer can be represented by the above recurrent ANN with state-space block. Feedforward SNNs are defined by compositions of SNN layers, while
recurrent neural networks are defined by compositions of nonlinear state-space blocks.
We thus confirm that
any feedforward SNN with
(L,\mathbf{p})
architecture with
\mathbf{p}:=(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{L})
can be represented by recurrent neural networks with
L
state-space blocks, and for
\ell\in[L]
, the
\ell
-th state-space block contains
2p_{\ell}
hidden units.

Appendix B
Extensions of the Single-Input Universal Representation Theorem

In the following, we derive variations of the single-input universal representation theorem (
Theorem

3.3
), by restricting to shallow SNNs, proving the necessity of negative weights, and deriving the universal representation theorem with bounded out-degree.

B.1
Universal Representation of Shallow Networks

Regarding the expressive power of shallow SNNs, i.e., one-hidden layer SNNs (
L=2
), we first show that there exist input-output spike train pairs that cannot be represented by shallow SNNs. This represents a fundamental limitation of shallow SNNs, which is different from the classical universal approximation theorems for ANNs. Complementary to this result, we then provide sufficient conditions on the input spike train that allow shallow SNNs to represent all dominated output spike trains. Note that this is equivalent to the representation of
m
-finite functions for input spike trains restricted to consist of only
m
spikes.

Proposition B.1
.

For any finite
h\in(0,\infty)
there exist two spike trains
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{T}
with
\mathcal{O}\triangleleft\mathcal{I}
such that no shallow SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(2,(1,\ell,1),s)
with
\ell,s\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
f(\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Consider the input spike train
\mathcal{I}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\}
with spiking times
\tau_{1}=1,\tau_{2}=2,\tau_{3}=3+h\ln(1+e^{-1/h}),
and define
\varphi_{1}=e^{-(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})/h}=e^{-1/h}
and
\varphi_{2}=e^{-(\tau_{3}-\tau_{2})/h}=\frac{e^{-1/h}}{1+e^{-1/h}}
. Let the output spike train be
\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\{\tau_{1}\}
.

To show the assertion denote by
H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell}
the neurons in the hidden layer. Each neuron is characterized by the respective weight
w_{i}
. If
w_{i}>1
, then the spike train of
H_{i}
equals
\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, if
w_{i}\in(1/(1+\varphi_{1}),1]
, then
H_{i}
spikes only at
\tau_{2}
, since then

\displaystyle P(\tau_{1})=w_{i}\leq 1,\quad P(\tau_{2})=w_{i}(1+\varphi_{1})>1,\quad P(\tau_{3})=w_{i}\leq 1,

where
P
is the membrane potential of
H_{i}
. Finally, if
w_{i}\leq 1/(1+\varphi_{1})
, then
H_{i}
does not spike at all, since

\displaystyle P(\tau_{1})

\displaystyle=\left(w_{i}\right)_{+}\leq 1,\quad P(\tau_{2})=(w_{i})_{+}(1+\varphi_{1})\leq\frac{1+\varphi_{1}}{1+\varphi_{1}}=1,

\displaystyle P(\tau_{3})

\displaystyle=(w_{i})_{+}(1+\varphi_{2}+\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2})=(w_{i})_{+}\left(1+\varphi_{1}\frac{1+\varphi_{1}}{1+\varphi_{1}}\right)=(w_{i})_{+}\left(1+\varphi_{1}\right)\leq 1.

Hence, all hidden neurons either admit the spike train
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\}
or
\{\tau_{2}\}
or do not spike at all. In particular, for an output spike at time
\tau_{1}
, all neurons with spike train
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\}
must have output weights that accumulate a value greater than one. However, this leads to a spike at time
\tau_{3}
, which cannot be compensated by the neurons with spike train
\{\tau_{2}\}
, as they do not contribute to the potential at time
\tau_{3}
.
∎

We now derive a positive statement in the case that the arrival times of the input spike trains are sufficiently homogeneous.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
h\in(0,\infty]
and
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}
be a spike train with
m:=|\mathcal{I}|\in\mathbb{N}

and

\displaystyle\delta_{\max}-\delta_{\min}<h\log\Big(\frac{1-q^{m}}{1-q^{m-1}}\Big).

or in case of constant gap size
\mathcal{I}_{(k+1)}-\mathcal{I}_{(k)}\equiv\delta

k\in[m-1]

\delta>0

Proof.

First fix
h\in(0,\infty)
. For

2
or

or
0

which such that the connection from input to

Define

and, for

Since

.

for all

by a simple induction. Moreover, a straight-forward computation shows by definition of

that Assumption (

) is equivalent to

1-e^{-\delta_{\max}/h}<\frac{1-q}{1-q^{m}}
and to
(1-e^{-\delta_{\max}/h})\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}q^{i}<1
.
Thus, for

A_{1}(w)<\cdots<A_{m}(w)

B.2

It is of genuine interest to wonder whether universal representation can hold with only excitatory neurons, that is, only non-negative network weights. We provide a negative answer in Lemma

(Necessity of two negative weights)

\mathcal{I}
(consisting of

\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{I}_{(1)}\}

We begin by proving the following claim: In a single-input feedforward SNN with depth

– the same three spike patterns
\{\emptyset,\{\mathcal{I}_{(2)}\},\{\mathcal{I}_{(1)},\mathcal{I}_{(2)},\mathcal{I}_{(3)}\}\}
occur for

spikes at all three times or never spikes. Hence, we may assume that

has spike train
\{\mathcal{I}_{(2)}\}

exceeds

only spikes at time
\mathcal{I}_{(2)}
it follows that along this path no neuron receives a unique negative contribution at time

\mathcal{I}_{(3)}

\mathcal{I}_{(1)}
and
\mathcal{I}_{(3)}
. Hence, the output neuron also spikes at time

, a contradiction. Concluding, at least two negative weights are required to realize

.
∎

, it follows that after the

B.3
Structural constraint: Limited out-degree

In this section, we work under the constraint that each neuron can be connected to at most
q\in\mathbb{N}
other neurons. Under this assumption, we get the following:

Proposition B.6

(Universal representation with bounded out-degree)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N},r\in[m],

h\in(0,\infty]
, and
q\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\geq 4
be the maximum out-degree. Then, there exists an architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
with
L=O(1+\log(m))
,
\|\mathbf{p}\|_{1}=O(\max\{m/q,\sqrt{m}\})
and a positive integer
s=O(\max\{m/q,\sqrt{m}\}+r)
such that for any
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{fin}}(m,r)
there exists a feedforward SNN
f\in\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(L,\mathbf{p},s)
with memory
h
and maximum out-degree
q
such that

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{I})=F(\mathcal{I}),\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}.

(B.2)

If
m=4^{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and possibly
h=0
if
m=1
, the claim also holds for
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{per}}(m,r)
.

Proof.

The proof modifies the construction in the proof of
Theorem

3.3
that is visualized in
Figure

6
. We rewire the network so that approximately
m/q
neurons enter the representation block (see
Figure

6
), each firing at distinct points only once for finite functions and once in a period
2^{\lfloor\log(q)\rfloor}\leq q
for periodic functions. The second layer of the representation block consists of
2^{\lfloor\log(q)\rfloor}
neurons which ensures the out-degree constraint. The connection weights for the modified representation block are determined by
F
, the neurons in the second layer can only spike once within a period
2^{\lfloor\log(q)\rfloor}
, and are subsequently delayed by a
2^{\lfloor\log(q)\rfloor}
-periodic
CLOCK
module, so the representation of
F
is ensured.

Let
\overline{m}\coloneqq 4^{\lceil\log_{4}(m)\rceil}\geq m
as in the proof of
Theorem

3.3
. Assume first that
q
is a power of
2
and
q\leq 2\sqrt{\overline{m}}
(if
q>2\sqrt{m}
we use the architecture from
Theorem

3.3
, which already satisfies the out-degree constraint).
Starting from the input spike train
\mathcal{I}
, we replace the coarse/fine splitting block by a deeper version. We construct

by applying the

\log(q)
times (instead of

functions where we keep both outputs. Denoting the spike train of

by

, this yields

\mathcal{V}_{1,i}=\big\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+(i-1)q+1)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\},\quad i\in\big[\overline{m}/q\big].

In parallel, we construct the
CLOCK
q
module consisting of
q
neurons
u_{1},\ldots,u_{q}
by keeping a copy of the input for the first
\log(\overline{m}/q)
layers and then applying
ODD/EVEN
blocks
\log(q)
times, each time keeping both outputs. Writing
\mathcal{U}_{i}
for the spike train of

, we obtain

\mathcal{U}_{i}=\big\{\mathcal{I}_{(\overline{m}k+i)}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\},\quad i\in[q].

This is the coarse- and fine splitting step, see Figure
6
. From this point on, the finite/periodic modification and the representation block are constructed analogously to the proof of
Theorem

3.3
, with the only difference that the last layer in the representation block now has
q
neurons (so that the representation block has
O(m)
weights and each neuron has out-degree at most
q
). The remaining parts of the argument are identical to those in the proof of
Theorem

3.3
. This construction creates the output spike train
F(\mathcal{I})
and uses
O(\frac{\overline{m}}{q}+q)=O(m/q)
neurons and well as

O(\frac{\overline{m}}{q}+q+r)=O(m/q+r)

to ensure that the

C.1
and

.

If

. The maximum out-degree of the resulting SNN architecture is bounded by

and fulfills universal representation of single-input functions. Moreover, the number of neurons is bounded by
O(\max(\frac{m}{q^{\prime}},\sqrt{m}))=O(\max(\frac{m}{q},\sqrt{m}))
while the number of non-zero weights is bounded by
O(\max(\frac{m}{q^{\prime}},\sqrt{m})+r)=O(\max(\frac{m}{q},\sqrt{m})+r)
.
∎

Lemma C.2

(Bit-path characterization of the
ODD/EVEN
-tree)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{T}
. Consider the depth-
q
binary tree obtained by consecutively applying
ODD
or
EVEN
functions to the outputs at every neuron. Specifically, take a neuron
N
of the tree at depth
\ell\in[q]
and define a binary vector
\mathbf{b}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{\ell}
by following the path from the root to
N
where an
ODD
function is coded as
b_{\ell}=0
and an
EVEN
function is coded as
b_{\ell}=1
. Then, the spike train of
N
is

\Big\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r-1\equiv\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}b_{t}2^{t-1}\!\!\mod 2^{\ell},r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\Big\}.

In particular, for each
j=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}b_{\ell}\,2^{\ell-1}\in[2^{q}]
, with
b_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}
for all
\ell\in[q]
, there exists a unique output neuron with spike train
\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r\equiv j\mod 2^{q},r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\}
that can be reached by following the path determined by the bits
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on the depth
\ell\in[q]
. For the base case,
\ell=1
, note that after one application of the
ODD/EVEN
function, one output contains precisely the spikes at the odd positions of
\mathcal{I}
, i.e.,
\{\mathcal{I}_{(1)},\mathcal{I}_{(3)},\ldots\}=\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r-1\equiv 0\mod 2,r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\}.
The other output contains precisely the spikes at the even positions, i.e.,
\{\mathcal{I}_{(2)},\mathcal{I}_{(4)},\ldots\}=\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r-1\equiv 1\!\mod 2,r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\}.
This shows the claim for
\ell=1.

For the induction step, assume the claim holds for some
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,q-1\}
. Fix bits
b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}
and let

i_{\ell}\coloneqq 1+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}b_{t}2^{t-1}.

By the induction hypothesis, there exists a neuron at depth
\ell
with spike train
\mathcal{I}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}):=\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r-1\equiv i_{\ell}-1\mod 2^{\ell},r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\}.
For
k=0,1,\ldots
, define
r_{k}:=i_{\ell}+k\,2^{\ell}
. Apply now an
ODD/EVEN
function to
\mathcal{I}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}).
The
ODD
function outputs
\{\mathcal{I}_{(r_{k})}:k\text{\ even}\}
, whereas the
EVEN
function outputs
\{\mathcal{I}_{(r_{k})}:k\text{\ odd}\}
(since we start at index

). Consequently, for the
ODD
child (
b_{\ell+1}=0
) and
k=0,1,\ldots
we have

r_{2k}-1=(i_{\ell}-1)+(2k)\,2^{\ell}\equiv i_{\ell}-1\!\mod 2^{\ell+1},

and for the
EVEN
child (
b_{\ell+1}=1
) and
k=0,1,\ldots
we have

r_{2k+1}-1=(i_{\ell}-1)+(2k+1)2^{\ell}\equiv(i_{\ell}-1)+2^{\ell}\!\mod 2^{\ell+1}.

In either case, the new residual modulo
2^{\ell+1}
is

i_{\ell+1}-1\coloneqq\Big(1+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell+1}b_{t}2^{t-1}\Big)-1=(i_{\ell}-1)+b_{\ell+1}2^{\ell},

verifying the claim for depth
\ell+1
. This completes the induction.
Taking
\ell=q
gives that the neuron along the path
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
has spike train
\{\mathcal{I}_{(r)}:r-1\equiv j-1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2^{q},\ r\leq|\mathcal{I}|\}
with
j:=1+\sum_{t=1}^{q}b_{t}2^{t-1}
, i.e. it outputs exactly the spikes with
r\equiv j\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2^{q}
.
∎

C.5
Real Weight SNNs are more Expressive than Integer Weight SNNs

Even though the previous constructions use integer weights, real valued weights allow for additional expressiveness.

Lemma C.12
.

Let
\mathbb{I}:=\{(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\colon|\mathcal{I}|=3,|\mathcal{J}|=2,\mathcal{I}_{(3)}=\mathcal{J}_{(1)}\}
. Then, the function
F:\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T}
given by
F(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})=\{\mathcal{I}_{(3)}\}
is causal, input dominated and has the monotone scaling property (on
\mathbb{I}
). Further,
F
can be represented by an SNN in the class
\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(1,(2,1),2)
with real valued weights but not by an SNN in
\operatorname{SNN}_{h}(1,(2,1),2)
with integer values.

Proof.

For
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in\mathbb{I}
write
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J}=\{\mathcal{S}_{(1)},\mathcal{S}_{(2)},\mathcal{S}_{(3)},\mathcal{S}_{(4)}\}
. Hence,
\mathcal{I}=\{\mathcal{S}_{(1)},\mathcal{S}_{(2)},\mathcal{S}_{(3)}\}
and
\mathcal{J}=\{\mathcal{S}_{(3)},\mathcal{S}_{(4)}\}
. We want the output spike train to be
\{\mathcal{S}_{(3)}\}
for input spike trains
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})
. This can be achieved by an SNN with two inputs, one output and weights
w=(w_{1},w_{2})
satisfying

0<w_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad 1-w_{1}<w_{2}\leq 1.

At
\mathcal{S}_{(1)}
the potential is

P(\mathcal{S}_{(1)})=w_{1}<1
and no spike occurs. At
\mathcal{S}_{(2)}
,

P(\mathcal{S}_{(2)})=e^{-(\mathcal{S}_{(2)}-\mathcal{S}_{(1)})/h}w_{1}+w_{1}=w_{1}\bigl(1+e^{-(\mathcal{S}_{(2)}-\mathcal{S}_{(1)})/h}\bigr)<2w_{1}\leq 1,

so again no spike occurs. At
\mathcal{S}_{(3)}
, both input neurons spike and the potential exceeds the threshold,

e^{-(\mathcal{S}_{(3)}-\mathcal{S}_{(2)})/h}P(\mathcal{S}_{(2)})+w_{1}+w_{2}\geq w_{1}+w_{2}>1.

Therefore, the output spikes. At
\mathcal{S}_{(4)}
the potential equals
w_{2}\in(1-w_{1},1]\subset(1/2,1]
, and no spike occurs. Thus, the output equals
\{\mathcal{S}_{(3)}\}
for all
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in\mathbb{I}
.

However, the same cannot be achieved with integer weights. To see this, consider weights
v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and suppose, for a contradiction, that the output spike train is
\{\mathcal{S}_{(3)}\}
for this input. As the output should not spike at
\mathcal{S}_{(1)}
, we must have
v_{1}\leq 1
. The absence of a spike at
\mathcal{S}_{(2)}
implies
v_{1}\leq 0
(for
v_{1}=1
the potential at
\mathcal{S}_{(2)}
equals
1+e^{-(\mathcal{S}_{(2)}-\mathcal{S}_{(1)})/h}>1
and
\mathcal{O}
would spike). At
\mathcal{S}_{(3)}
there is a spike, hence
v_{2}>1
. At
\mathcal{S}_{(4)}
there is no spike, hence
v_{2}\leq 1,
contradicting
v_{2}>1
. Therefore, no integer vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
can realize this specific output spike pattern.
∎

D.1
Auxiliary Statements for Expressiveness of SNNs

To quantify the expressiveness of general feedforward SNNs with a fixed architecture, we first consider the simpler case of a single SNN computational unit. To analyze its expressiveness, we start by deriving an explicit formula for the membrane potential of the output neuron.

Recall that the membrane potential from (
2.1
) at time
t
of an SNN computational unit
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}
with memory
h\in[0,\infty]
and weight vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
receiving

\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\subseteq(0,\infty)
is defined recursively as

\displaystyle{P}(t):=\Big(\mathds{1}({P}({t}^{-})\leq 1){P}({t}^{-})e^{-\frac{t-{t}^{-}}{h}}+\sum_{j:t\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}w_{j}\Big)_{+},

(D.1)

where
{t}^{-}
denotes the presynaptic last spike time strictly before
t
among all input spike trains and the output spike train. Crucial for our analysis of the expressiveness of SNN units is the following unfolding of the recursion in (
D.1
), which entails the contribution of each individual input neuron to the membrane potential for a given time frame
[r,t]
. Given input spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
and time points
0<r\leq t,
we define the vector of contributions of the
i
-th input neuron, for
i\in[d]
, to the membrane potential at time
t
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{[r,t]}\coloneqq(p_{i}^{[r,t]})_{i\in[d]}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d},\qquad p_{i}^{[r,t]}:=\textstyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap[r,t]}e^{-\frac{t-\tau}{h}}.

(D.2)

Lemma D.1

(Unfolded membrane potential)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
be
d
spike trains and let
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}
be an SNN unit with memory
h\in[0,\infty]
,
d
input neurons, and weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})
. Enumerate the spike times of
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in[d]}\mathcal{I}_{j}
by
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots
, set
\tau_{0}\coloneqq 0
, and let
r\in[|\mathcal{I}|]
. Set
j\coloneqq\max\{i\in\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}:P(\tau_{i})\notin(0,1]\}
for
P
from (
D.1
). Then, the vectors
\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{j},\tau_{r}]}
defined in (

), it holds that

\displaystyle P(\tau_{r})=\textstyle\left(\sum_{k=j+1}^{r}\sum_{l\colon\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l}\exp\big(-(\tau_{r}-\tau_{k})/h\big)\right)_{+}=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}w_{l}p_{l}^{[\tau_{j+1},\tau_{r}]}\right)_{+}=\left(\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{j+1},\tau_{r}]}\rangle\right)_{+},

where

denotes the standard inner product on

The above result reveals a close relation between the membrane potential at the spike times and the vectors

for given input spike trains

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}):=\Big\{\mathbf{p}^{[r,t]}\;\Big|\;\text{ for }r,t\in\textstyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{j},r\leq t\Big\}.

(D.3)

Lemma D.2

(Identifiability of outputs of SNN units)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
be spike trains and let
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}},\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}
be two SNN computational units with memory

and

input neurons, which are parametrized by the respective weights vectors

and
\mathbf{w}_{2}

(D.4)

then
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})=\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}).

We now proceed with an upper bound on the number of distinct functions that can be represented by a single SNN computational unit.

Proposition D.3

(expressiveness of one SNN computational unit)
.

Consider a finite collection of

-tuples of spike trains

, and denote by

an SNN computational unit with memory

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Big|\big\{\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\colon\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T},(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\mapsto\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\;\colon\;\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\big\}\Big|\leq(8eH)^{d}.\end{aligned}

Moreover,

for

Proof of

.

We write

.
By definition,

For

denotes the number of non-zero parameters in the

.

D.3
Proof of the Cardinality Bound on Causal Functions

Proof of
Lemma

6.6
.

If
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{I}
,
then for each
t\in[m]
at least one of the spike trains
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d}
includes
t
, and hence the number of possible values of
(\mathds{1}(t\in\mathcal{I}_{j}))_{j\in[d]}
is
2^{d}-1
.
This yields
|\mathbb{I}|=(2^{d}-1)^{m}
.
We enumerate the elements of
\mathbb{I}
by
\mathbf{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{I}_{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}
.

For
k\in[(2^{d}-1)^{m}]
, we define a map
\nu_{k}:\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)\to\{0,1\}
as

\nu_{k}(F):=\mathds{1}\Big(m\in F(\mathbf{I}_{k})\Big).

Based on this perspective, it follows that

(i)

For any
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}
with
\|\mathbf{b}\|_{1}\leq r
, there exists
F\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
such that
(\nu_{k}(F))_{k\in[(2^{d}-1)^{m}]}=\mathbf{b}
.

(ii)

For
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{mm}}(d,m,r)
, if

\big(\nu_{k}(F_{1})\big)_{k\in[(2^{d}-1)^{m}]}\neq\big(\nu_{k}(F_{2})\big)_{k\in[(2^{d}-1)^{m}]},

then there exists
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{I}
such that
F_{1}(\mathbf{I})\neq F_{2}(\mathbf{I})
.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\big\{\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}:\|\mathbf{b}\|_{1}\leq r\big\}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}{i}\Big(\mathds{1}\big(r\leq(2^{d}-1)^{m}\big)+\mathds{1}\big(r\geq(2^{d}-1)^{m}+1\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{r}\land 2^{(2^{d}-1)^{m}}.

(D.7)

By
(i)
,
(ii)
and (
D.7
), we obtain the assertion. ∎

D.4
Proofs for Auxiliary Statements on Expressiveness

Proof of
Lemma

D.1
.

The proof follows by unrolling the definition of the membrane potential in (
2.1
).
Specifically, we show via (reverse) induction for every
s\in\{j+1,\dots,r\}
,

\displaystyle P(\tau_{r})=\bigg(\mathds{1}\big(P(\tau_{s-1})\leq 1\big)P(\tau_{s-1})\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{s-1}}{h}\Big)+\sum_{k=s}^{r}\sum_{l\colon\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{k}}{h}\Big)\bigg)_{+}.

Choosing
s=j+1
yields the desired result.

The case
s=r
follows directly from (
2.1
). For the induction step, we assume that the claim holds for some
s\in\{j+2,\dots,r\}
, and we need to show that it also holds for
s-1
. Since
s-1\geq j+1
we know by definition of
j
that
P(\tau_{s-1})\in(0,1]
and this asserts

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathds{1}\big(P(\tau_{s-1})\leq 1\big)P(\tau_{s-1})

\displaystyle=P(\tau_{s-1})

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}\big(P(\tau_{s-2})\leq 1\big)P(\tau_{s-2})\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{s-1}-\tau_{s-2}}{h}\Big)+\sum_{l\colon\tau_{s-1}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l},

where we can drop the expression
(\cdot)_{+}
as the argument is positive. Plugging this into the induction hypothesis yields the claim for
s-1
and finishes the proof by mathematical induction:

\displaystyle\textstyle P(\tau_{r})=\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left(\mathds{1}\big(P(\tau_{s-2})\leq 1\big)P(\tau_{s-2})\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{s-1}-\tau_{s-2}}{h}\Big)+\sum_{l\colon\tau_{s-1}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l}\right)\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{s-1}}{h}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=s}^{r}\sum_{l\colon\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l}\exp\big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{k}}{h}\Big)\Bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\Bigg(

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\big(P(\tau_{s-2})\leq 1\big)P(\tau_{s-2})\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{s-2}}{h}\Big)+\sum_{k=s}^{r}\sum_{l\colon\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{l}}w_{l}\exp\Big(-\frac{\tau_{r}-\tau_{k}}{h}\Big)\Bigg).\qed

Proof of
Lemma

D.2
.

We prove the claim by mathematical induction with respect to the collection of all input spike times
\mathcal{I}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{j}\subset(0,\infty)
. Indeed, by
Proposition

2.3
an output spike can only occur at an input spike time
\tau\in\mathcal{I}
. It thus suffices to compare whether the membrane potentials from (
2.1
) at
\tau
for
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}
and
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}
either both exceed
1
or are both below
0
. We enumerate the elements of
\mathcal{I}
by
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots
, set
\tau_{0}\coloneqq 0
, and denote the potential for the output neuron associated to
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{i}}
as
P_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.

For the base case, we consider the time point
\tau_{1}
, and we need to show that
P_{1}(\tau_{1})>1
if and only if
P_{2}(\tau_{1})>1
and
P_{1}(\tau_{1})=0
if and only if
P_{2}(\tau_{1})=0
. To this end, note that

\displaystyle\textstyle P_{1}(\tau_{1})=\left(\langle\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]}\rangle\right)_{+},\quad\textstyle P_{2}(\tau_{1})=\left(\langle\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]}\rangle\right)_{+},

and the desired equivalence follows from the assumption in (
D.4
).

Let us now suppose that
\chi(P_{1}(\tau_{i}))_{i=1,\dots,k-1}=\chi(P_{2}(\tau_{i}))_{i=1,\dots,k-1}
for some
k\geq 2
, and thus that the two output spike trains are identical up to time
\tau_{k-1}
. We need to show that the output spike trains are also identical at time
\tau_{k}
.
To this end, consider

j\coloneqq\max\big(i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\colon P_{1}(\tau_{i})\notin(0,1]\big).

Hence, at
\tau_{j}
either
P_{1}(\tau_{j})=0
or
P_{1}(\tau_{j})>1
. In the first case, we have by the induction hypothesis that
P_{2}(\tau_{j})=0
,
whereas in the second case we have
P_{2}(\tau_{j})>1
. In both cases, we thus have that
P_{2}(\tau_{j})\notin(0,1]
. Moreover, for every subsequent time point
i\in\{j+1,\ldots,k-1\}
it holds that
P_{1}(\tau_{i})\in(0,1]
and thus by the induction hypothesis also
P_{2}(\tau_{i})\in(0,1]
.
Hence, we obtain

\max\big(i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\colon P_{2}(\tau_{i})\notin(0,1]\big)=j.

By applying
Lemma

D.1
for both
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}
and
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}
at time
\tau_{k}
(i.e.,
r=k
), we get

\displaystyle P_{1}(\tau_{k})=\left(\langle\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{j+1},\tau_{k}]}\rangle\right)_{+},\quad P_{2}(\tau_{k})=\left(\langle\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{p}^{[\tau_{j+1},\tau_{k}]}\rangle\right)_{+}.

The desired equivalence at time
\tau_{k}
thus follows from (
D.4
), completing the induction.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

D.3
.

By
Lemma

D.2
, two SNN computational units
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}
and
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}
with weight vectors
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have identical output spike trains for all combinations of input spike trains
(\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{I}
, and thus satisfy
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{1}}=\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}_{2}}
on
\mathbb{I}
, if

\displaystyle\chi(\langle\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{p}\rangle)=\chi(\langle\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{p}\rangle)\quad\text{ for all }\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I}),

(D.8)

where
\chi(x)=\mathds{1}(x>1)-\mathds{1}(x\leq 0)\in\{-1,0,1\}
and where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
is defined in (
D.3
).

Condition (
D.8
) has a specific geometric interpretation. For each
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
we associate two hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbf{p})
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbf{p})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by
\mathcal{H}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}\rangle=i\}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
.
These sets define proper hyperplanes because
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
, since each vector
\mathbf{p}
involves at least one contribution from a spike.
The hyperplanes divide the space into three regions: the region
\mathcal{R}_{-1}(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}\rangle\leq 0\}
below
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbf{p})
,
the region
\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon 0<\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}\rangle\leq 1\}
between the two hyperplanes, and the region
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon 1<\langle\mathbf{w},\mathbf{p}\rangle\}
above
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbf{p})
. The condition on
\mathbf{w}_{1}
and
\mathbf{w}_{2}
in (
D.8
) can thus be rephrased as follows: For every
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
, the weight vectors
\mathbf{w}_{1}
and
\mathbf{w}_{2}
must either both lie in
\mathcal{R}_{-1}(\mathbf{p})
, or both lie in
\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{p})
, or both lie in
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\mathbf{p})
.

Hence, the number of different SNN units
\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}
acting on
\mathbb{I}
for
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is upper bounded by the number of different ways to assign each
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})
to one of the three regions
\mathcal{R}_{-1}(\mathbf{p})
,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathbf{p})
, or
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\mathbf{p})
. Equivalently, it reduces the problem to counting the number of different intersections of the form
\bigcap_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})}\mathcal{R}_{r(\mathbf{p})}(\mathbf{p})
for
r(\mathbf{p})\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\{\mathbb{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\colon\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{T},(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d})\mapsto\varphi_{h,\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{d}),\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\{\bigcap_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})}\mathcal{R}_{r(\mathbf{p})}(\mathbf{p})\;\colon\;r\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})\to\{-1,0,1\}\right\}\right|.

The latter is upper bounded by so-called hyperplane arrangements, see
Lemma

D.4
below. By e.g.
Halperin & Sharir, 2017
, the number of different regions formed by the hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbf{p})
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbf{p})
for
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{I})

is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{d-i}{k-i}\binom{2H}{d-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{2r}{d-i}\sum_{k=i}^{d}\binom{d-i}{k-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{2H}{d-i}2^{d-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{2H}{i}2^{i}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{2H}{i}\leq(d+1)\left(\frac{4eH}{H\land d}\right)^{d}\leq(8eH)^{d},

where for the second inequality we used that for all
i\in\{0,\dots,d\}
,

\binom{2H}{i}\leq\binom{2H}{d}\mathds{1}(H\geq d)+\binom{2H}{H}\mathds{1}(H<d)=\binom{2H}{H\land d}\leq\left(\frac{2eH}{H\land d}\right)^{H\land d}\leq\left(\frac{2eH}{H\land d}\right)^{d}.

The last inequality follows by noticing that
(d+1)/d^{d}\leq 2^{d}
and
d+1\leq 2H^{d}
for
d,H\in\mathbb{N}
.
This proves the assertion.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

The number of partition cells formed by intersections of given half-spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{r}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and their complements is upper bounded by the number of different (connected) non-empty regions formed by intersecting the hyperplanes
\partial\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\partial\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Proof of
Lemma

D.4
.

For
i\in[r]
, define the sets
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{0}\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}_{i}^{c}},

\mathcal{H}_{i}^{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{i},

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{-1}\coloneqq\textup{int}(\mathcal{H}_{i}^{c})
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{0}\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{H}_{i}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{i}
, where the redundant definition
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{1}=\mathcal{H}_{i}
is chosen for notational convenience below. Next, define

where
\mathcal{A}
(resp.
\mathcal{B}
) encodes all non-empty, and thus convex and connected, intersections of
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in\{0,1\}
(resp.
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in\{-1,0,1\}
). By
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{0}\dot{\cup}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{-1}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots r\}
, for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we find

\displaystyle\emptyset\neq\textstyle\bigcap_{i\in[r]}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r}\in\{-1,0,1\}\\
\mathds{1}(\beta_{i}=1)=\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\,\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{\beta_{i}}

and thus there exists some
\beta_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,\beta_{r}(\alpha)\in\{-1,0,1\}
such that
\emptyset\neq\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{i}^{\beta_{i}}\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
.
Further, since two distinct
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
lead to disjoint sets
\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
and
\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}^{\prime}}
, it follows that
(\beta_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,\beta_{r}(\alpha))\neq(\beta_{1}(\alpha^{\prime}),\ldots,\beta_{r}(\alpha^{\prime}))
,
and we conclude that there is an injection
\iota\colon\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathcal{B}
, asserting
|\mathcal{A}|\leq|\mathcal{B}|
.
∎

Appendix E
Proofs for Additional Statements from Section 3

Lemma E.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
be an input-dominated, causal function satisfying the monotone scaling property. Then, the following implications hold.

(i)

If
F
is
m
-finite, then
F
has
(m+1)
-Markovian memory.

(ii)

Conversely, if
F
has
(m+1)
-Markovian memory, then either
F
or its input-wise complement
\overline{F}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T},\mathcal{I}\mapsto\mathcal{I}\backslash F(\mathcal{I})
is
m
-finite.

Proof.

By virtue of the reduction in (
3.1
), the function
F
is identified by
F(\mathbb{N})
. In particular,
m
-finiteness is then equivalent to
F(\mathbb{N})\subseteq[m]
, while
(m+1)
-Markovian memory is equivalent to the property that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
one has
t\in F(\mathbb{N})
if and only if
t\in F((t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N})
.

For
(i)
, note for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\leq m+1
that
[t]=(t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N}
, hence by causality it holds
t\in F(\mathbb{N})
if and only if
t\in F(\{1,\dots,t\})=F((t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N})
.
Moreover, by
m
-finiteness we have
t\notin F(\mathbb{N})
for every
t\geq m+1
. Hence, it follows for
t\geq m+1
that by monotone scaling that
t\notin F((t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N})=F([m+1])\oplus\{t-(m+1)\}
, and in particular,

\mathds{1}(t\in F(\mathbb{N}))=0=\mathds{1}(t\in F((t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N})),

which confirms the
(m+1)
-Markov property.

To show
(ii)
, observe that both
F
and
\overline{F}
are
(m+1)
-Markovian, and assume without loss of generality that
m+1\notin F(\mathbb{N})
(otherwise, the argument applies to
\overline{F}
). Then, by
(m+1)
-Markovian memory and monotone scaling, it holds for any
t\geq m+1
that
F(\mathbb{N})=F((t-(m+1),t]\cap\mathbb{N})=F([m+1])\oplus\{t-(m+1)\}.

This spike train does also not contain
t
since
m+1\notin F([m+1]).
Hence,
F(\mathbb{N})\subseteq[m]
. ∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
F_{M}\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}
be
m_{M}
-Markovian and
F_{P}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
be
m_{P}
-periodic, and define the input dominated, causal, monotone scaling function
F\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T},F(\mathcal{I}):=F_{M}(\mathcal{I},F_{P}(\mathcal{I}))
. Then,
it follows
for all
t\geq m_{M}+1
that

t\in F(\mathbb{N})
if and only if
t+m_{P}\in F(\mathbb{N})
.

Proof.

By (
3.1
) it suffices to consider
\mathcal{I}:=\mathbb{N}
. For
t>m_{M}
define
\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\mathbb{N}\cap(t-m_{M},t]
and
\mathcal{J}_{t}:=F_{P}(\mathbb{N})\cap(t-m_{M},t]
. By the
m_{M}
-Markovian property of
F_{M}
we have
t\in F_{M}(\mathbb{N},F_{P}(\mathbb{N}))
if and only if
t\in F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t},\mathcal{J}_{t})
. Further, note
\mathcal{I}_{t+m_{P}}=\mathcal{I}_{t}\oplus\{m_{P}\}
and, since
F_{P}
is
m_{P}
-periodic, it holds
\mathcal{J}_{t+m_{P}}=\mathcal{J}_{t}\oplus\{m_{P}\}
. Moreover,
the monotone scaling property of
F_{M}
implies
F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t+m_{P}},\mathcal{J}_{t+m_{P}})=F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t},\mathcal{J}_{t})\oplus\{m_{P}\}
, so
t+m_{P}\in F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t+m_{P}},\mathcal{J}_{t+m_{P}})
if and only if
t\in F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t},\mathcal{J}_{t})
. Applying again the
m_{M}
-Markovian property at time
t+m_{P}
yields
t+m_{P}\in F(\mathbb{N})
if and only if
t+m_{P}\in F_{M}(\mathcal{I}_{t+m_{P}},\mathcal{J}_{t+m_{P}})
. Combining these equivalences gives

t\in F(\mathbb{N})
if and only if
t+m_{P}\in F(\mathbb{N})
for all
t\geq m_{M}+1
.
∎
