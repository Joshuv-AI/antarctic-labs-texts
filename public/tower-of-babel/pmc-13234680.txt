Sapphire-Supported Ta–Pt Thin Films for Electrochemical Studies under Hydrothermal Conditions
Authors: Muna Abdulaziz, Kostyantyn Pichugin, Travis Whistle, Tetyana Maksyuta, German Sciaini, Liliana Trevani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-temperature platinum (Pt) thin films have been widely used in gas-phase devices such as temperature sensors and microheaters, but little has been done in the field of high-temperature electrochemistry, particularly under hydrothermal conditions. In this work, we explored the chemical and mechanical stability of sapphire-supported tantalum–platinum (sapphire/Ta/Pt) electrodes capped at their edges with a conformal Ta/SiO2 layer in 0.1 M H2SO4 at 200 °C under autogenous pressure. A subset of samples was annealed for 10 min in flowing O2 at 650 °C to gauge the impact of the Ta/SiO2 layer on the overall performance of the Pt electrodes in acidic solutions. The temperature and pressure investigated in this work are well above those reported in previous studies on Ta, Pt, and Pt-alloy thin films, particularly those focused on catalyst materials for energy storage and conversion devices. The electrochemically (or electrochemical) active surface area (ECSA) of the Pt electrodes was measured before and after immersion in H2SO4 at 200 °C and used as the primary durability metric, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tracked changes in surface chemistry and morphology. Results show that both unannealed and annealed electrodes perform well, retaining more than 80% of their initial ECSA after cycling the potential between 0 and 1.4 V at 500 mV/s in 0.1 M H2SO4 at room temperature, constituting a significant first step toward the development of reusable/low-cost sensors for electrochemical studies in hydrothermal systems.

Introduction

Thin-film platinum (Pt) electrodes and their Pt-alloy counterparts have been intensively studied as catalyst materials for polymer electrolyte membrane fuel-cell (PEMFC) cathodes, − biosensors, , microfluidic devices, and a range of other applications. In contrast, high-temperature uses of Pt thin films remain largely confined to gas-phase devices such as temperature sensors and microheaters. ,

In an earlier study, we assessed the chemical and mechanical stability of Pt thin films in hot aqueous environments. The films were deposited on sapphire wafers with a titanium (Ti) adhesion layer, capped with a Ti/Al2O3 insulating layer that defined the electrode geometry, and then exposed to rapid thermal annealing at 900 °C under argon for 5 min to stabilize the insulator. Despite the short annealing step, Ti diffusion through the Pt film led to pores and hillocks on the Pt surface and to the formation of a Pt3Ti alloy, altering the electrode composition and diminishing the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of the electrodes after prolonged storage in air. To mitigate Ti diffusion through Pt films in gas phase environments at temperatures approaching or exceeding 1000 °C, adhesion layers made from metals with lower mobility in Pt, such as tantalum (Ta) and zirconium (Zr), have been explored. ,, These findings are promising: Ta shows markedly lower mobility in Pt than Ti under both air and vacuum. Moreover, Ta deposited on C-plane (0001) sapphire (the substrate used here) forms an interfacial layer that improves the stability of the Ta/Al2O3 heterojunction. On the other hand, the electrochemical behavior of Ta, Pt, and Ta2O5–Pt electrodes under ambient conditions has been investigated in the past, − which facilitates the analysis of experimental data under high temperature and pressure conditions. In 0.5 M H2SO4, Ta2O5 thin films exhibit extremely low current densities in the potential range for hydrogen and oxygen oxidation/reduction region on Pt. It has also been found that the presence of Ta2O5 in nanostructured Ta2O5/Pt electrodes does not introduce any significant change in the cyclic voltammogram of Pt in sulfuric acid that, according to the authors, could indicate that the role of Ta2O5 is to allow proton transfer without preventing any reaction on the Pt catalyst surface.

In this work, we have prepared multilayer Ta/Pt electrodes on C-plane sapphire substrates using magnetron sputtering and applied a nonconductive Ta/SiO2 bilayer on top of the Pt film as electrical insulation layer. Subsequently, the electrode area was exposed using photolithography and ion milling. The chemical and mechanical stability of the films in 0.1 M H2SO4 at 200 °C after immersion periods of 4 and 14 h was investigated using a combination of electrochemical and surface characterization methods. The immersion times were deliberately selected to far exceed the contact times normally encountered in laboratory electroanalytical experiments and flow cells under ambient conditions. By using H2SO4 for the chemical stability experiments, we could also compare the performance of the Pt thin-film electrodes with studies on Pt catalyst materials for PEMFCs. ,, For consistency, ex situ cyclic voltammetry in 0.1 M H2SO4 at room temperature was also used to monitor changes in ECSA before and after high-temperature immersion. Other characterization techniques, such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), provided us with a more detailed insight into the morphological and chemical changes produced by exposure to 0.1 M H2SO4 at 200 °C. A subset of electrodes underwent an oxidative annealing step at 650 °C to enhance interlayer adhesion and improve the insulating performance of the protective Ta (Ta2O5)/SiO2 coating; the remainder were left unannealed, enabling direct comparison. This comprehensive analytical strategy is paving the way for the rational design of robust thin-film electrodes involving other materials and geometries to extend the capabilities of a recently introduced channel flow cell able to operate at temperatures over 150 °C under well-defined hydrodynamic conditions by some of the authors in this study.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Chemicals were used as received. Sulfuric acid from Sigma-Aldrich (ACS reagent, 95–98% H2SO4), and gases, argon (Ar 99.999%, Ar5.0 UH-T) and oxygen (O2 99.99%, OX4.0HC-T) from Linde Canada Inc. Solutions were prepared using ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ.cm). Other chemicals used in electrode fabrication are listed in the following section.

Electrodes Fabrication

A schematic of the thin film electrode design is shown in Figure . Thin films were deposited on 2-in. diameter, 0.5 mm thick, double-side polished sapphire wafers (AdValue Technology, SS-2WC-2-50) with C-plane orientation. Prior to photolithography, wafers were cleaned with acetone and isopropanol, followed by nitrogen drying. A negative photoresist (m-AN1410) was employed in the initial photolithography step to enable metal lift-off. Electrode patterns were defined by exposing the photoresist to a 375 nm laser at 550 mJ/cm2 using a Heidelberg MLA system, followed by development in ma-D533/S developer. Electrode fabrication utilized a multilayer structure consisting of: Ta (25 ± 3 nm) as the bottom adhesion layer, Pt (300 ± 5 nm) as the working electrode, Ta (25 ± 3 nm) as the top adhesion layer, and SiO2 (1063 ± 20 nm) as the insulating layer.

(a) Scheme of multilayer Pt films: Ta (25 nm)/Pt (300)/Ta (25 nm)/SiO2 (1 μm); (b) sapphire wafer with thin film electrodes fabrication scheme; (c) picture of Pt thin film electrode (0.04 cm2) after cutting.

Ta and Pt layers were deposited using an AJA Orion Twin Chamber Magnetron Sputter System without substrate heating. The SiO2 layer was deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) using an Oxford System 100. To evaluate improvements in chemical and mechanical stability in acidic, high-temperature environments, selected Ta/SiO2-coated electrodes underwent annealing at 650 °C for 10 min in oxygen using an Allwin-RTP Rapid Thermal Processor.

Selective opening of electrode areas was achieved through photolithography and ion milling. A positive photoresist (AZ P4620) was spin-coated, exposed with a 405 nm laser at 1000 mJ/cm2 using the Heidelberg MLA system, and developed in AZ-400 K (4:1). Ion milling was performed with the AJA IonMill system, achieving a depth of 1174 ± 14 nmsufficient to fully remove the top SiO2 and Ta layers, thereby exposing the underlying Pt electrode.

Ta and Ta/Pt electrodes were also fabricated using the same deposition process, omitting the top Ta and SiO2 layers, as reference materials. Samples have been identified using the notation given in Table .

We observed that reproducibility among electrodes cut from the same wafer was not an issue; rather, etching conditions required optimization and stringent control to ensure consistent wafer-to-wafer reproducibility. Our optimization process involved systematic ion milling calibration, achieved by regularly characterizing and adjusting the etch rate using reference samples.

During the annealing step in an oxygen environment, the formation of tantalum oxides alters the thickness of the Ta interlayer. Consequently, the etching process was optimized to ensure proper exposure of the Pt surface. To achieve this, the Pt layer was intentionally thickened and the etching depth increased, providing a sufficient process margin to compensate for potential under- or overetching. Overall, this established procedure has proven reliable for maintaining wafer-to-wafer reproducibility and is compatible with the scalable fabrication of these electrodes.

Characterization Studies on Films

Changes in film morphology after immersion in H2SO4 at high temperatures were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The studies were performed using a Hitachi FlexSEM 1000 SEM system. Grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GI-XRD) was used to investigate changes in the films’ crystallinity and the possible formation of a Pt–Ta intermetallic alloy during annealing. The studies were carried out with a Malvern-PANalytical Xpert Pro MRD diffractometer using Cu–Kα radiation source. The phase analysis was performed at an incidence angle of 0.5°, covering a 2θ range from 20 to 100° with a step size of 0.1°. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was also used to investigate changes in surface composition and functional groups upon annealing and immersion in acid media. Like in our previous study on Ti/Pt films, XPS spectra were collected using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nexsa Surface Analysis System. The monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) X-ray source was focused to a 400-μm spot size. Charge compensation was achieved with a flood gun. The chamber pressure was maintained at 7 × 10–8 mbar. Survey spectra were acquired at a low energy axis point density (1.0 eV point spacing) and low energy resolution (E-pass = 200 eV), while regional spectra were collected with 0.1 eV point spacing and higher energy resolution (E-pass = 50 eV). XPS data were analyzed using the Avantage software by Thermo Fisher Scientific, employing a Shirley function to approximate the experimental background. The XPS spectra were first calibrated using the C 1S peak at 285 eV before further analysis. To investigate the chemical profile beneath the surface layer, XPS depth profiling was used for the annealed films. Etching was performed using the ion gun on a cluster size of 2 mm. An ion energy of 1000 eV was applied at midcurrent, with an estimated sputter rate of 0.2 nm/sec on Ta2O5. Each etching cycle lasted 5 s resulting in ∼1 nm of etching per cycle after which XPS data was collected up to 9 cycles.

Chemical and Thermal Stability Studies - Electrochemical Characterization

The procedure used to assess the stability of the Ta/Pt/Ta/SiO2 films for hydrothermal work was adapted from that previously used for Ti/Pt/Ti/Al2O3 films and will therefore be briefly discussed here. The stability tests were done in commercial Parr digestion vessels with PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) liners. The vessels were filled with 0.1 M H2SO4 solution to completely submerge the samples, and before closing the autoclave, the solution was bubbled with Ar gas for 10 min. After that, the vessel was placed in a VWR forced-air convection oven at 200 °C for 4 to 14 h, as indicated in Table . The vessels were cooled down with ice and cold water after removal from the oven. The electrochemical performance of the Ta/Pt/Ta/SiO2 films was evaluated by comparing their cyclic voltammograms (CVs) before and after hydrothermal treatment. Electrochemical measurements were performed at room temperature (23 °C) in a two-compartment three-electrode cell, using a Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode using a Gamry Reference 600 potentiostat. All potential values have been referenced to the normal hydrogen electrode potential (E NHE = E Ag/AgCl + 0.199 V). Prior to the experiments, the solution (∼100 mL) was bubbled with Ar for 30 min and kept under Ar throughout the measurements. Electrodes were cycled 50 times between 0.0 and 1.4 V (vs NHE) at 500 mV/s before taking a CVs at 50 mV/s between −0.01 and 0.9 V (vs NHE) for analysis. The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) of the Pt electrodes was calculated with eq : ,, 1 ECSA=Q(μC)210μC/cm2 where 210 μC/cm2 is the charge density associated with the adsorption/desorption of a monolayer of hydrogen on polycrystalline Pt, and Q represents the charge obtained by integration of the current vs voltage region corresponding to the hydrogen desorption region from 0.0 to 0.3 V (vs NHE) after subtracting the double-layer capacitance charge. Electrochemical data were analyzed using the Echem Analyst software commercialized by Gamry Instruments.

It has been reported that tantalum oxide on the surface of Pt could dampen the reduction peak of Pt, particularly when exposed to potentials below 1.1 V vs NHE, and may be due to the tendency of Ta to be preferentially oxidized over more noble metals like Pt. This effect has also been observed in Pt films coated in thin layers of Nb oxide, where the Pt reduction peak decreases as the metal oxide layer increases. However, as the potential increases to 1.2 V and above, the Pt reduction peak becomes prominent, despite the presence of Ta on the surface as seen in previously published CVs of Pt/Ta catalysts, as Pt oxidation becomes more favorable at higher potentials. , After acid treatment, the Ta2O5 content decreases and the Pt reduction peak is clearly visible. The results also indicate that SiO2 is an appropriate protective layer for high T,p applications at temperatures up to 200 °C in acidic media conditions. Based on these preliminary results, the annealing steps do not appear to improve the electrodes’ performance significantly. Conclusions

This work explored viable approaches to preparing platinum (Pt) thin film electrodes for studies at temperatures up to 200 °C. Sapphire was chosen as the substrate material due to its various properties that make it superior to other common substrate materials such as silica, quartz, and alumina ceramics. Mechanical and physical properties such as hardness, flexural strength, and thermal conductivity make sapphire more resistant to fractures from applying high pressures or from thermal shock due to temperature gradients when comparing with other materials. Furthermore, its crystallinity provides high chemical inertness and resistance to corrosion from both acidic and alkaline media. Its optical properties also make it highly compatible with spectroscopic techniques, such as Raman spectroscopy. This is relevant as these electrodes were designed to be used as part of a high T,p spectroelectrochemical cell. Moreover, 2-in.-diameter sapphire wafers are highly cost-effective when purchased in bulk (<USD 10 per wafer). This low material cost provides significant commercial potential, particularly as nanofabrication expenses are further reduced through process optimization for mass production.

Due to its low diffusivity through Pt films, Ta was adopted as the adhesion layer, and Ta/SiO2 was used to define the Pt electrode’s geometrical area. The impact of annealing on the multilayer films was also investigated. It is worth noting that the Ta interlayer thickness is impacted by the formation of tantalum oxide species, to ensure that the etched Pt layer is of the same quality each time, a pragmatic solution has been adopted by increasing the etching depth well into the Pt layer, taking advantage of its 300 nm thickness. Thus, errors associated with insufficient etching or too much etching are avoided, provided that similar conditions for etching are used.

The ECSA of the electrodes was adopted as a primary stability measure to evaluate their performance after immersion in 0.1 M H2SO4 for 4 and 14 h, but it was necessary to cycle the potential to obtain reproducible results. The studies performed under ambient conditions, both before and after immersion, indicate that all electrodes can withstand acidic media at least under the experimental conditions of this study. The electrodes remain electrochemically active, and CVs obtained after 14 h of immersion in 0.1 M H2SO4 at 200 °C clearly show the features observed for polycrystalline Pt, despite some Pt dissolution being expected under these conditions.

The calculated ECSA in all cases increased with potential cycling and the duration of the acid treatment, but remained close to the electrodes’ geometric area, indicating good stability of the Ta/SiO2 top layer. In the case of the annealed samples, the increase in ECSA is more likely associated with the dissolution of Ta2O5 that forms on the electrode surface when stored in air, as confirmed by complementary XPS studies.

The formation of an intermetallic alloy between Pt and Ta after annealing is likely to occur, but the GI-XRD results were inconclusive. For the unannealed samples, Ta could not be confirmed by XPS, and the increase in ECSA was more likely due to increased roughness after immersion in acid media or cycling the potential, which would also affect the annealed sample results. Once the electrodes were cycled, the CVs for all the electrodes remained constant even after cycling the potential between 0 and 1.2 V at 100 mV/s for an additional 1000 cycles in both Ar and air. Both schemes demonstrated reasonable stability at high temperatures in acidic media, which is suitable for analytical applications, particularly when combined with flow cells, as the contact of the electrodes with corrosive media conditions can be minimized.

As mentioned, these electrodes are currently being used for high T,p electrochemical studies and have been reused multiple times in acidic media. Despite the stability of the films, wear can occur over time; therefore, the ECSA is measured before and after high T,p experiments to ensure their integrity throughout the process.
