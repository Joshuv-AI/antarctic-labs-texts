Evaluating and Mitigating Social Bias for Large Language Models in Open-ended Settings
Zhao Liu *, Tian Xie *, Xueru Zhang

Abstract
Current social bias benchmarks for Large Language Models (LLMs) primarily rely on pre-defined question formats like multiple-choice, limiting their ability to reflect the complexity and open-ended nature of real-world interactions. To close this gap, we extend an existing dataset BBQ Parrish et al. (2022) to Open-BBQ , a comprehensive framework to evaluate the social bias of LLMs in open-ended settings by incorporating two additional question categories: fill-in-the-blank and short-answer . Since our new Open-BBQ dataset contains a lot of open-ended responses like sentences and paragraphs, we developed an evaluation process to detect biases from open-ended content by labeling sentences and paragraphs. In addition to this, we also found that existing debiasing methods, such as self-debiasing Gallegos and et al. (2024) , have over-correction issues, which make original correct answers incorrect. In order to solve this issue, we propose Composite Prompting , an In-context Learning (ICL) method combining structured examples with explicit chain-of-thought reasoning to form a unified instruction template for LLMs to explicitly identify content that needs debiasing. Experimental results show that the proposed method significantly reduce the bias for both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o while maintaining high accuracy.

Introduction

LLMs have become increasingly prevalent across various domains of society in recent years. Given their widespread use and large user bases, harmful biases and stereotypes can easily propagate to diverse individuals and groups
(
Sheng et al., 2019
)
. It is therefore critical to evaluate and mitigate biases in LLM. Prior work
(
Esiobu et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Parrish et al., 2022
;
Kamruzzaman et al., 2023
)
has proposed several methods for bias detection and mitigation,but they mainly focus on questions with templated formats, such as multiple-choice questions. The demand for open-ended questions (e.g., short-answer questions) has grown substantially, as most real-world applications of LLMs are generative in nature and involve producing open-ended content
Gallegos and et al. (2024)
.
BBQ

(
Parrish et al., 2022
)
is the most comprehensive benchmark to date for evaluating the bias of LLMs, consisting of question sets designed to elicit social biases affecting individuals from protected groups across nine social dimensions in English-speaking contexts. We adopt
BBQ
as our baseline dataset, which we discuss in detail in Section
2
. Since the
BBQ
dataset contains only multiple-choice questions, we add two new question types:
fill-in-the-blank
and
short-answer
to reflect open-ended scenarios in real life better. To evaluate bias in open-ended responses, we extend the BBQ dataset into a new benchmark,
Open-BBQ
, which incorporates fill-in-the-blank and short-answer questions in addition to multiple-choice. A central challenge in this setting is that free-form responses, such as sentences or paragraphs, require semantic understanding to determine whether they contain social bias. Earlier studies relied primarily on multiple-choice formats where evaluation could be reduced to matching predefined options. In contrast, open-ended evaluation requires a deeper interpretation of the response content. To address this problem, we design an LLM-assisted evaluation process that assigns binary labels to responses, indicating whether they contain social bias. This process provides a scalable and consistent framework for bias evaluation in open-ended settings. The evaluation result shows that social bias not only exists in responses to multiple-choice questions but also widely presents in the ones to fill-in-the-blank and short-answer questions (Section
3.2
).

More importantly, as social bias exists broadly, it is critical to eliminate it from LLMs. Previous prompting-based approaches for debiasing
(
Gallegos and et al., 2024
;
Dwivedi et al., 2023
;

;

Such behavior results in systematic over-correction. To address this limitation, we introduce Composite Prompting, an in-context learning (ICL) method that enables LLMs to first distinguish between ambiguous and disambiguated questions and then apply reasoning strategies tailored to each case.We evaluated our proposed method on the
Open-BBQ

0\%

\displaystyle s_{\textsc{Dis}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{n_{\text{biased\_ans}}}{n_{\text{non-{unknown}\_outputs}}}\right)-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\textsc{accuracy})\cdot s_{\textsc{Dis}}

3.1
Open-BBQ
Benchmark

Since the
BBQ
dataset alone cannot capture open-ended answers that better reflect real-world use cases, we propose
Open-BBQ
, an extension of the
BBQ
dataset that introduces two additional question types:
fill-in-the-blank
and
short-answer
. By incorporating open-ended questions, we aim to detect unjustified stereotypes in both ambiguous and context-driven scenarios, thereby revealing whether models rely on implicit, stereotype-driven associations in their reasoning. Moreover, open-ended questions require LLMs to produce complete reasoning processes, allowing us to observe how model behavior differs when answers are not predefined. In addition to expanding the question types in the original
BBQ
dataset, we also developed the evaluation process to detect social bias in free-text responses, like sentences or paragraphs. Specifically, we design a prompting framework that integrates zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting strategies, enabling GPT-4o to match synonyms, summarize free-text sentences and paragraphs, and ultimately provide evaluations.

Open-ended question types.

The open-ended setting closely mirrors real-world interactions with LLMs, where users often pose questions or prompts requiring nuanced, context-driven responses. To capture this, we introduce two question types in
Open-BBQ
:
fill-in-the-blank
and
short-answer
, with illustrative examples provided in Example
3
. These formats allow models to produce more flexible, personalized, and context-sensitive outputs, capturing the variability and complexity of human communication in real-world scenarios.

Size of
Open-BBQ
.

The dataset contains 29,246 ambiguous questions covering a wide range of social groups and intersectional categories. Specifically, individual groups include Age (1,840 records), Disability (778), Gender Identity (2,836), Nationality (1,540), Physical Appearance (788), Race/Ethnicity (3,440), Religion (600), Sexual Orientation (432), and Socio-economic Status (3,432). Two intersectional group, Gender by Race/Ethnicity and Socio-economic Status by Race/Ethnicity, add 7,980 and 5,580 records, respectively. Each record is tested in both ambiguous and disambiguated questions, yielding 58,492 records in total. To better reflect diverse real-world scenarios, each context is further represented across three formats,
multiple-choice
,
fill-in-the-blank
, and
short-answer
, expanding the dataset to 176,476 records. Finally, we evaluate each question on two LLMs,
gpt-3.5-turbo-0125
and
gpt-4o-2024-05-13
, resulting in a complete dataset of 350,952 records.

Automated evaluation.

Figure 1:
The overall architecture of the evaluation. The system would perform a text summary from LLM output if it is a short-answer question type. It will try an exact match of words or phrases to predefined options. If there is an exact match, then select it immediately. If there is no exact match, then the system will perform a synonym match.

The large scale of the Open-BBQ dataset makes exclusive reliance on human annotation infeasible. Manual labeling of open-ended responses is both expensive and error-prone; for instance,
Parrish et al. (2022)
reported that annotators on Amazon Mechanical Turk achieved only
85\%
accuracy even with additional quality-control items, and the process required significant time and financial resources. As
Open-BBQ
contains hundreds of thousands of responses across multiple formats, a scalable and consistent evaluation method is required.

To address this challenge, we employ GPT-4o to automate the evaluation of open-ended responses, particularly for fill-in-the-blank and short-answer questions. Our framework combines zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting strategies to guide the model through tasks such as synonym matching, sentence summarization, and semantic alignment. By aligning free-text outputs with predefined options, the framework converts open-ended responses into a structured format comparable to multiple-choice questions, thereby enabling systematic detection of bias across diverse scenarios. The figure
1
demonstrates the overall architecture of the evaluation processes.

A key design principle is to decompose complex reasoning into a sequence of simpler subtasks. For example, synonym recognition and sentence summarization are handled separately to reduce error propagation. In addition, prompts are enriched with illustrative examples that clarify expectations and demonstrate the desired reasoning process. This design reduces ambiguity and enhances consistency, allowing the model to generalize more reliably across diverse response types.

We validate the reliability of this automated process through manual inspection. A review of more than 1,600 GPT-generated evaluations revealed only two incorrect cases, corresponding to an accuracy of approximately 99.9%. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves both high precision and scalability, offering a cost-effective alternative to human annotation while ensuring the robustness of bias evaluation in open-ended settings.

4
Debiasing

There are several approaches to debiasing large language models, such as fine-tuning (e.g.,
Garimella et al. (2022)
;
Han et al. (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2024d)
), model debiasing (e.g.,
Chisca et al. (2024)
;
Cheng et al. (2024)
;
Sue et al. (2022)
, ensemble techniques (e.g.,
Kim et al. (2024)
;
Fang et al. (2024)
;
Zhang et al. (2024a)
), and in-context learning (ICL) (e.g.,
pan:2023
;
Gallegos and et al. (2024)
). All those approaches offer varying levels of effectiveness and accessibility. Fine-tuning is a powerful method to adjust model behavior, but it also has remarkably complex and costly for regular users, since it requires access to computational resources and expertise. Moreover, for most commerical models, users do not have access .to their architecture and weight, making fine-tuning impossible. Ensemble techniques can improve the robustness performance of LLMs, but they will introduce additional layers of complexity and cause significant resource overhead during both training and inference. Among these methods, in-context learning, such as prompt engineering, emerges as the most accessible option, as it requires no modification to the model, no additional training data, and no additional computational resources. This method directly interacts with the LLMs using carefully crafted instructions and provided examples, which are easily accessible for a wide range of users. Prompt engineering is particularly suitable for users who do not have extensive technical resources and allows them to address bias-related challenges effectively.

4.1
Over-correction issues of self-debiasing

Studies on in-context learning (ICL) have introduced several techniques for self-debiasing, i.e., adding prompts asking the model to remove bias by itself. While there are different versions of self-debiasing such as zero-shot/few-shot prompting, self-consistency prompting
wang:2022self
, and chain-of-thought prompting
Wei et al. (2022)
, these methods face a strong trade-off between social bias and accuracy. Most existing debiasing methods only instruct LLMs to debias themselves across all cases, leading to systematic drawbacks. For example,
Gallegos and et al. (2024)
proposed a zero-shot prompting approach that works well for ambiguous questions, but performs poorly on disambiguated ones. Their results show that zero-shot prompting can convert
20.9\%
of incorrect answers into correct ones, yet
10.4\%
of incorrect answers remain unchanged, and around
4.5\%
of initially correct answers become incorrect due to over-correction.

Our large-scale experiments on
Open-BBQ
confirm this problem: as shown in Table
2
and Table
3
, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o are prone to producing biased answers on ambiguous questions, while they perform reliably on disambiguated questions, rarely giving incorrect or biased outputs. The testing self-debiasing (zero-shot prompting) on
350,952
records shows that the overall bias score for disambiguated questions is worse than the models’ default behavior (Table
5
), and Table
4
reveals sharp accuracy drops across most demographic categories. Chain-of-thought prompting exhibits the same pattern—improvements on ambiguous questions but degraded performance on disambiguated ones. These results point to a fundamental challenge: simple prompting strategies cannot selectively adjust outputs. A more effective method must debias only the initially incorrect responses in ambiguous settings, while leaving correct outputs intact in disambiguated cases, thereby avoiding the trade-off between fairness and accuracy.

4.2
Composite Prompting

To mitigate the tradeoff between fairness and accuracy, we propose
Composite Prompting
as a two-stage algorithm. During the
task learning
stage, the model determines whether the question is ambiguous or disambiguated. Next, during generation, only ambiguous questions are handled with debiasing methods, while disambiguated ones follow the original reasoning. This process allows LLMs to break down complex reasoning into manageable steps to produce more reliable outputs. A schematic overview is shown in Figure
2
.

To enhance LLMs’ ability to distinguish different tasks,
Composite Prompting
applies ICL using a small set of examples, each annotated with a
chain-of-thought
field explaining how to identify whether a question is ambiguous or disambiguated. For ambiguous cases, examples demonstrate thinking process to generate a neutral response to avoid stereotyping. For disambiguated cases, examples encourage the model to draw a clear conclusion. A complete prompt can be found in Table
9
. In practice, four examples modeled on Example
2
and Example
2
are sufficient for the model to learn the distinction. By combining zero-shot prompting, few-shot prompting, and chain-of-thought prompting into a unified framework,
Composite Prompting
enables selective debiasing while preserving accuracy on tasks where sufficient contextual information is available.

5
Conclusion

We present
Open-BBQ
as a comprehensive framework for assessing and mitigating biases in context-specific, and open-ended settings. Our evaluation results reveal both the challenges and the opportunities of leveraging biased outputs as a pathway toward debiasing. These findings underscore the need for evaluation methodologies that more closely mirror real-world interactions with LLMs. At the same time, they deepen our understanding of how biases surface in open-ended formats and point to practical strategies for mitigation. Looking ahead, future research could focus on scaling these methods across broader applications and integrating more advanced debiasing techniques to further improve the reliability and fairness of LLMs.

Limitations

While
Open-BBQ
provides a large-scale framework for evaluating biases in open-ended settings, our automated evaluation still relies on LLM-assisted labeling, which may introduce subtle errors or overlook nuanced forms of bias. Also,
Composite Prompting
needs high-quality examples, which may need to be verified by humans.

Related work

A.1
Bias Detection in LLMs

As
Blodgett et al. (2020)
note, the concept of “bias” can be defined broadly across studies, yet it is consistently observed that models encode associations rooted in negative stereotypes and social biases. Several works have focused on safety and truthfulness in LLMs
(
Ji et al., 2024a
;
Ji et al., 2024b
;
Lin et al., 2021
)
, employing reinforcement learning from human feedback, enabling nuanced moderation and fine-tuned safety, or testing the ability of LLMs to avoid generating false statements commonly found in human misconceptions across diverse domains. Numerous studies have examined the extent and forms of bias in language models
(
Bordia and Bowman, 2019
;
Caliskan et al., 2017
;
Davidson et al., 2019
;
May et al., 2019
;
Magee et al., 2021
)
, many highlighting instances where models exhibit stereotypical behavior. In the domain of code generation,
Huang et al. (2023a)
evaluated and mitigated biases across various sensitive attributes, demonstrating the prevalence of bias and proposing strategies, including feedback-driven prompt refinements, to reduce biased outputs in LLM-generated code. Studies that contrast groups of people, rather than merely examining individual attributes, are particularly valuable for revealing disparities in outcomes and the impacts of stereotypes on targeted groups
(
Dev et al., 2021
)
. Extending beyond text,
Luo et al. (2024)
evaluated biases in text-to-image generative models across four dimensions, using a carefully constructed dataset and advanced multi-modal language models for high-accuracy automated assessments.

A.2
Bias Mitigation Methods

A variety of techniques have been developed to mitigate and reduce bias in Large Language Models (LLMs). Most bias mitigation approaches focus on data handling, response management, or debiasing the model itself, employing strategies such as pre-processing, in-training, or post-processing adjustments to reduce bias
(
Chhikara et al., 2024
;
Dwivedi et al., 2023
;
Ma et al., 2024
)
. Prompt engineering for debiasing involves crafting specific instructions or examples to guide LLMs toward producing unbiased responses without modifying the model or requiring additional training data (Section
A.2.1
). Data debiasing, such as fine-tuning, mitigates biases by modifying a model’s training process using specific datasets or additional training steps to reduce prejudiced outputs (Section
A.2.2
). Model debiasing reduces bias in LLMs through techniques like modifying embeddings, data augmentation, and AI-driven feedback mechanisms (Section
A.2.3
). Ensemble techniques for LLMs, though underexplored, leverage diverse model perspectives to improve optimization, evaluation, and specific applications like e-commerce GMV predictions (Section
A.2.4
).
All approaches offer different levels of effectiveness and accessibility. Fine-tuning could adjust and change model behavior fundamentally, but it is way too complex and costly for regular users, because it requires a lot of computational resources and expertise. Similarly, model debiasing also demands remarkable computational power to retrain the entire model or its components, so it may not be suitable for everyone. Ensemble techniques can improve the robustness of LLMs, but it requires additional layers of complexity and cause significant resource overhead during both training and inference. However, in-context learning, such as prompt engineering is the best option for the public, because it does not require additional training data or computational resources.

A.2.1
Prompt Engineering Debiasing

Prompting engineering has become essential technology for mitigating bias in closed-source LLMs due to limited access to their internal architectures. Commonly employed response debiasing or post-processing methods include zero-shot techniques
(
Ebrahimi et al., 2024
;
Echterhoff et al., 2024
;
Furniturewala et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2023b
;
Kaneko et al., 2024
;

)
, reinforcement learning frameworks
(
Liu et al., 2022a
;
Qureshi et al., 2023
)
, post-hoc calibration
Zhang et al. (2024c)
, and contrastive learning
Zhang et al. (2024b)
. A recent study by
Li et al. (2024a)
introduces a method combining inhibitive instruction with in-context contrastive examples to reduce gender bias in LLMs. This framework uses a causality-guided, prompting-based approach, which has proven effective in minimizing biased reasoning patterns in LLMs.

A.2.2
Data Debiasing

Data debiasing techniques have significantly advanced in mitigating bias within LLMs. Among the most widely used techniques are fine-tuning
(
Garimella et al., 2022
;
Ghanbarzadeh et al., 2022
;
Joniak and Aizawa, 2022
;
Liu et al., 2022b
;
Orgad et al., 2022
;
Ungless et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2024d
)
and data augmentation
(
Zhang:2024b
;
Mishra:2024
;
Panda:2022
)
. A recent study by
Han et al. (2024)
introduces a synthetic data generation method for bias reduction, which uses both targeted and general prompting to generate debiased datasets for model fine-tuning. This approach also incorporates a feedback-based auxiliary technique called loss-guided prompting, which leverages model output to identify and rectify remaining biases in the synthetic dataset.

A.2.3
Model Debiasing

Model debiasing focuses on reducing bias within machine learning models during the training process. Recent studies have explored various techniques for model debiasing, such as modifying or supplementing word embeddings
(
Chisca et al., 2024
;
Sue et al., 2022
)
, data augmentation
(
Gupta et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2024b
)
, and debiasing strategies applied during text generation
Liang et al. (2021)
. A recent study by
Cheng et al. (2024)
introduces a novel approach called Reinforcement Learning from Reflection (RLRF), which employs AI-driven feedback through debate mechanisms to reduce bias in large language models (LLMs).

A.2.4
Ensemble Techniques in LLMs

Ensemble techniques for large language models (LLMs) are still a relatively underexplored area. These methods apply not only to bias mitigation and fairness but also extend to optimization and evaluation. Recent studies have used ensemble approaches to enhance both optimization and evaluation processes
(
Kim et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024a
)
. Some prominent ensemble techniques include prompt ensemble and LLM ensemble methods. For instance, a recent study by
Fang et al. (2024)
employed an LLM ensemble to improve gross merchandise volume (GMV) predictions in e-commerce. While similar to multi-LLM systems, ensemble techniques share a common goal of leveraging diverse model perspectives to enhance overall performance and robustness.

Appendix B
Examples of Questions

A negative question is defined as one that involves a violation of a social value commonly upheld in the United States, where the associated bias adversely affects certain groups. For example:

Example B.1
: A negative question.

A 78-year-old man was talking to his 22-year-old friend about how much politics has changed in just the last few decades.
Who is resistant to change?

(A) 22-year-old(B) 78-year-old(C) Not known

Conversely, a non-negative question serves as the counterpart to a negative question, although it does not necessarily imply the absence of harm.

Example B.2
: A positive question.

A 78-year-old man was talking to his 22-year-old friend about how much politics has changed in just the last few decades.
Who embraces change?

(A) 22-year-old(B) 78-year-old(C) Not known

Since the contents of the two questions do not mention a specific person who is resistant to change, both correct answers are
(C) Not Know
. The way we ask the question
Who is resistant to change?
is a negative question. On the other hand,
Who embraces change?
is the non-negative way to ask a question. The bias of older people in LLMs is what we want to test, so the older people are the target social group.

This distinction enables us to evaluate whether a target social group is influenced by bias, because some groups of people may have a higher probability in the model’s training data, potentially affecting the likelihood of their selection as responses.
