Contingency Management for Smoking Cessation Among Adults Experiencing Homelessness
Authors: Dian Gu, Deepalika Chakravarty, Jordan Cuby, Sharon Hall, Maxine Stitzer, Margot Kushel, Fan Xia, Maya Vijayaraghavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes an extended contingency management (CM) intervention improve cigarette smoking abstinence among adults experiencing homelessness?FindingsIn this pilot randomized clinical trial of 83 adults experiencing homelessness who were engaged in primary care in San Francisco, California, the CM group achieved a higher rate of 7-day point prevalence abstinence (carbon monoxide–verified self-report of abstinence) at 6 months compared with the control group (31% vs 12%) and higher odds of 7-day point prevalence abstinence during 6 months of the intervention.MeaningExtended CM may be an effective strategy to promote long-term smoking abstinence among adults experiencing homelessness.

Introduction

Although smoking prevalence among US adults has decreased to 9.9% in 2024, the prevalence of smoking among people experiencing homelessness is greater than 70%.1,2,3 Tobacco-caused chronic diseases are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among adults experiencing homelessness,4,5 highlighting a need for effective smoking cessation interventions.6

People experiencing homelessness face barriers to cessation; these barriers do not hinder quit attempts, but they reduce the likelihood of successful quitting.7 The quit ratio (former to ever smoking—a marker of successful quitting) is 9% to 13% among people experiencing homelessness compared with 60% in the general population.8,9,10 Limited access to health care and tobacco treatment services,4,11 as well as high rates of co-occurring psychiatric and substance use disorders, hinder cessation efforts.3,12

Clinical trials among adults experiencing homelessness that have used guideline-recommended tobacco treatment of behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy have demonstrated modest abstinence rates (9%-17%) at a 6-month follow-up.13,14,15 These trials show that while behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy are essential, additional treatment approaches may be necessary to increase the efficacy of cessation among adults experiencing homelessness. Intervention trials that have used contingency management (CM) in addition to behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy have yielded higher abstinence rates in this population (22%-48% at 4-week follow-up) than those without CM.16,17,18,19

CM is a behavior modification intervention that incorporates tangible rewards to reinforce desired behaviors.20,21,22 Based on the operant conditioning principle, the positive reinforcement from tangible rewards from CM is thought to be equal to or outweigh the immediate rewards (eg, biological pleasure) from smoking.22 CM has been used to improve smoking cessation outcomes among populations with intersecting vulnerabilities, such as people with low socioeconomic status, substance use disorders, mental health conditions, and HIV.23,24,25,26 While studies among people experiencing homelessness have demonstrated the feasibility and efficacy of short-term CM lasting 4 to 8 weeks,17,18,19,27 including a study among veterans experiencing homelessness,27 the feasibility and efficacy of extended (≥6 months) CM interventions remain unknown. Additionally, these short-term clinical trials have not been evaluated in practice-based safety net health care settings where adults experiencing homelessness seek health care.

To address this gap, we conducted an extended CM intervention for smoking cessation within a safety-net health care system in San Francisco, California. We evaluated the efficacy of escalating incentives contingent on increasing abstinence compared with fixed incentives for attendance not contingent on abstinence among adults experiencing homelessness. We hypothesized that participants in the CM group would have higher abstinence rates at the 6-month follow-up than those in the control group.

Methods

This pilot randomized clinical trial was approved by the University of California, San Francisco Institutional Review Board and followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.28 The study was independently reviewed by the San Francisco Department of Public Health. The protocol (found in Supplement 1) has been published previously.29 We obtained written informed consent from all participants using the teach-to-goal approach.30 Participants enrolled between November 8, 2021, and June 27, 2023, completed 24-week follow-ups on January 9, 2024, and completed exploratory 52-week follow-ups on May 21, 2024.

Participants and Setting

We recruited participants from 3 clinics from the largest safety-net provider of primary care, behavioral health, and acute care services in San Francisco, California. Clinic 1, where we recruited 58 participants, was a San Francisco Department of Public Health clinic, located in downtown San Francisco, and served a primarily homeless population. Clinic 2, an academic primary care clinic (6 participants) and clinic 3, an academic outpatient HIV clinic (19 participants), served a low-income, racially and ethnically diverse patient population and were affiliated with the San Francisco General Hospital.

Eligible participants included those who (1) were currently experiencing homelessness or were living in a temporary shelter-in-place hotel,31 (2) currently smoked cigarettes (defined as having smoked ≥100 cigarettes in lifetime, daily smoking in the past 7 days, and ≥5 cigarettes per day, verified by expired carbon monoxide [CO] levels ≥8 ppm [Micro+ Smokerlyzer; Bedfont Scientific Ltd]),32,33,34 (3) had an intention to quit smoking within the next 6 months; (4) were proficient in English; (5) received primary care in 1 of the 3 safety net clinics in San Francisco where study recruitment took place, and (6) were able to provide written informed consent. We initiated the study during the COVID-19 pandemic, which posed challenges for study recruitment and follow-up due to limitations in our ability to recruit and follow up from clinics. While some participants were living in congregate shelters, others moved into temporary shelter-in-place hotels. We conducted study procedures in person at an outdoor central location in the Tenderloin region of San Francisco or at their clinics.

Procedures

We recruited participants through the electronic health record (EHR), word-of-mouth referrals, flyers, and announcements in clinic waiting rooms. Within each clinic, study staff used the EHR to create a list of eligible patients for each clinician. Study staff then contacted health care clinicians to obtain permission to contact their eligible patients and made at least 3 attempts to contact each patient. Of the 1077 participants screened for eligibility (795 who met criteria based on the EHR and 282 based on primary care clinician referrals), 989 were excluded because study staff were unable to contact them by telephone or in person; 3 declined to participate because they believed the incentive amounts were not high enough; and 2 were excluded because they did not meet the criteria for current homelessness (Figure 1).

Study Flow Diagram

We used a 2-group parallel randomized clinical trial design. Using stratified block randomization, stratified on the recruiter for each clinic site and nicotine dependence levels (time to first cigarette after waking, high [≤30 minutes] vs low [>30 minutes]), we randomized participants to the CM intervention group or the control group. After completing the baseline assessment, all participants completed follow-up assessments and provided an expired CO sample at 2, 4, 12, 24, and 52 weeks after study initiation. We reimbursed participants $20 for completing the baseline questionnaire, $15 each for the 2-, 4-, and 12-week follow-up questionnaires, and $25 each for the 24- and 52-week follow-up questionnaires. Participants received an additional $5 for monthly check-in visits between 6 and 12 months.

Usual Cessation Care for All Participants

All participants had the opportunity to receive guideline-recommended smoking cessation care during their primary care encounters. Because our clinical trial was embedded within the EHR,29 once we recruited participants, we informed their primary care clinicians of their patients’ enrollment into the CM clinical trial and encouraged them to provide guideline-recommended smoking cessation treatment. Of the 83 participants, 74 had at least 1 primary care visit where they received counseling, and 40 had at least 1 encounter where they received pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation.

CM Intervention and Control Conditions

Participants assigned to the CM intervention group received incentive payments in the form of gift cards redeemable at national retail chains. We set the starting incentive at $13.00, based on prior studies among similar populations18,19,35,36 and increased by $0.50 for each CO test indicating abstinence (ie, CO level ≤5 ppm).37 Test results indicating smoking (ie, CO level >5 ppm) or missed visits reset the gift card amount to the initial value for as many as 2 visits. However, 2 consecutive negative tests restored the amount to the highest level previously earned.16,18,19,36,38 We conducted daily abstinence assessment visits during week 1, twice weekly through week 4, weekly through week 13, and monthly through week 24, for a total of 25 abstinence assessment visits. At each abstinence assessment visit, participants provided an expired CO sample and reported on whether they smoked in the past week, whether they attempted to quit smoking, and the number of days of their quit attempt. Participants in the CM group could earn a maximum of $475 in contingent incentives, in addition to the incentive they received for completing questionnaires. Control participants had the same abstinence assessment schedule as the CM group and received $5 per visit regardless of smoking status, for a maximum of $125.

Measures

We describe measures in detail in our protocol paper.29 A brief description of the variables follows.

Sociodemographic and Tobacco Use Characteristics

We assessed self-reported age, gender, race and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, or other [American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or multiracial]), and chronic homelessness.39 Age, gender, and race and ethnicity data were included because they are associated with disparities in smoking cessation6 as well as mental health screens and substances used because of their link with cessation in prior research.3,12 We assessed mean daily cigarette consumption, time to first cigarette after waking, method of cigarette purchase, receipt of a clinician’s recommendation to stop smoking, history of quit attempts, duration of the most recent quit attempt, resources used to aid quitting, and past 30-day use of other tobacco products.

Mental Health Screening, Substance Use, and Financial Incentives

We assessed (1) depressive symptoms (Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale–10 score ≥10),40 (2) anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 score ≥10),41 (3) posttraumatic stress disorder (meeting ≥3 criteria based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders [Fifth Edition]),42 and (4) psychological distress (Kessler Psychological Distress Scale score ≥17).43 For substance use, we assessed past 30-day use of opioids, amphetamines, cocaine, and cannabis. We also report the mean amount of financial incentives received overall and by study group.

Smoking Abstinence

The primary outcome was smoking abstinence at 6-month follow-up. Abstinence was defined as (1) 7-day point prevalence abstinence (PPA), self-reported during the past 7 days and biochemically verified with a CO level of 5 ppm or less44; and (2) CO-verified abstinence, measured as a CO level of 5 ppm or less at the abstinence assessment visit.17,18,19 The 2 secondary outcomes were repeated measures of the 2 primary outcomes across all 25 study visits. We also reported the median total number of CO-negative samples for each group.29

Statistical Analysis

We analyzed data between June 1, 2024, and December 31, 2025. We describe sample statistics using means (SDs) or medians (IQRs) for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. We used logistic regression models to evaluate the effects of the CM intervention on primary and secondary outcomes. Logistic model 1 included only the study group and time to first cigarette after waking. Logistic model 2 included the study group, time to first cigarette after waking, and baseline variables including age, gender, race and ethnicity, number of positive mental health screens (depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder, psychological distress), and number of substances used (opioids, amphetamines, cocaine, and cannabis) in the past 30 days. We did not include the site recruiter variable, as it was used solely for administrative purposes and not considered prognostic for cessation outcomes. We set the sample size as 90 for practical purposes and assumed equal allocation to the intervention and control groups.29

We used repeated-measures generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a logit link and robust SEs to model smoking abstinence across the 25 study visits, estimating the population mean CM intervention effect. The base GEE model 3 included study group, days from enrollment (centered at the first follow-up visit), the interaction between days and study group, and time to first cigarette after waking. The same baseline covariates in the logistic model 2 for the primary outcome are included in the GEE model 4.

Our primary analytic approach followed the convention in the field where a missed study visit indicated continued smoking.45 In supplemental analyses, we considered multiple imputation, where missing values were imputed based on nonmissing data.46 We conducted all analyses using an intention-to-treat principle. Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided P < .05. We performed analyses using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 90 expected participants, we recruited 83. Among these, 42 participants (50.6%) were assigned to the CM group and 41 (49.4%) to the control group (Table 1). The mean (SD) participant age was 47.0 (10.8) years. Fifty-three participants (63.9%) were men, 21 (25.3%) were women, and 9 (10.8%) were nonbinary. In terms of race and ethnicity, 16 participants (19.3%) were Hispanic or Latino, 30 (36.1%) were non-Hispanic Black, 21 (25.3%) were non-Hispanic White, and 16 (19.3%) were of other race or ethnicity (Table 1). Participants had a mean (SD) of 2.5 (1.4) positive mental health screens and use of 1.5 (1.0) substances at baseline. Tobacco use characteristics are presented in eTable 1 in Supplement 2.

Effects of the CM Intervention on Primary Smoking Cessation Outcomes

Follow-up data were available for 43 participants (51.8%) at 2 weeks, 36 (43.4%) at 1 month, 36 (43.4%) at 3 months, 42 (50.6%) at 6 months, and 22 (26.5%) at 12 months. Participants attended a mean (SD) of 14.3 (8.8) study visits during the 6-month follow-up. Because the 12-month outcome assessment was exploratory, we did not conduct a formal intention-to-treat analysis for this time point. The median total number of CO-negative samples throughout the first 6 months was 9 (IQR, 2-22) in the intervention group and 4 (IQR, 2-10) in the control group. The 7-day PPA at 24 weeks was 31.0% in the CM group and 12.2% in the control group (Figure 2A). The CO-verified abstinence at 24 weeks was 31.0% in the CM group and 24.4% in the control group (Figure 2B). Effects of the CM Intervention on Secondary Smoking Cessation Outcomes

After adjusting for baseline covariates in GEE model 4 (Table 3), at the first follow-up visit, the CM group had higher odds of 7-day PPA (AOR, 12.23 [95% CI, 4.61-32.45]) (P < .001) and CO-verified abstinence (AOR, 3.18 [95% CI, 1.64-6.16]) (P < .001) than the control group. The intervention effect attenuated over time for 7-day PPA (days-from-enrollment × intervention AOR, 0.99 [95% CI, 0.98-1.00]) (P = .02), with the estimated AOR declining to 6.16 (95% CI, 2.52-15.07) (P < .001) at visit 22 and 3.29 (95% CI, 1.06-10.23) (P = .04) at visit 25 (eTable 3 in Supplement 2). The intervention effect did not vary significantly over time for CO-verified abstinence (days-from-enrollment × intervention AOR, 1.00 [95% CI, 0.99-1.01]) (P = .81). eTable 4 in Supplement 2 presents the results from the multiple imputation analysis for the secondary cessation outcomes; main intervention effects were consistent with primary results.

Incentives Earned

Participants in the CM group earned a mean (SD) of $176.9 ($171.1) in incentives, while those in the control group earned $79.5 ($39.4) (Table 1). During the first 4 weeks, participants earned a mean (SD) of $94.4 ($82.0) in incentives in the CM group and $45.7 ($21.1) in the control group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first pilot randomized clinical trial that evaluated the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of an extended CM intervention for smoking cessation among people experiencing homelessness. Seven-day PPA was higher among participants in the CM group compared with the control group at 6-month follow-up and during the 6-month study duration. The results for intervention effects obtained using multiple imputation were consistent with those from the primary approach, which treated missing data as smoking. However, intervention effects were strongest in the early phase of the intervention,29 when there were frequent, reinforcing study visits, highlighting the need to sustain higher-frequency visits for a longer duration.47,48 This study provides evidence that extended CM interventions are feasible to implement and are associated with increased cessation behaviors among adults experiencing homelessness, highlighting the potential for scalability in clinical settings serving this population.

We found that the intervention’s effects varied depending on the definition of abstinence used for the primary cessation outcomes. Seven-day PPA, which included CO verification of self-reports of abstinence, may reflect a participant’s sustained intent and effort to remain smoke-free during the past week, whereas CO-verified abstinence, a measure used in prior studies with adults experiencing homelessness,17,18,19 may reflect proximal fluctuations in smoking status during the past 24 hours.44 Overall, our findings suggest that PPA may be a more stable and robust measure to use for smoking cessation studies that use CM.

We found a higher-than-expected quitting rate in the control group that received fixed $5 payments, suggesting that low-value engagement incentives may encourage participants to quit. Prior CM research indicates that the effectiveness of incentives may depend on their magnitude, as some studies among people who use drugs have shown that lower-value incentives for abstinence are less likely to yield significant behavioral changes than higher-value incentives.49,50 Our findings indicate that lower-value incentives, not contingent on abstinence, supported engagement in the control group. Future CM interventions with this population might include engagement payments in addition to payments for demonstrating abstinence.

The abstinence rate in the CM group at 6 months (31.0%) falls within the range reported in prior short-term CM trials among adults experiencing homelessness (22%-48% at 4 weeks),16,17,18,19 demonstrating comparable abstinence rates during a substantially longer follow-up period. The present finding extends prior research by demonstrating that extended CM interventions to 6 months can be effective in promoting smoking cessation in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.23,51 For adults experiencing homelessness who smoke, it may be common to internalize self-blame for poor health outcomes that are driven in large part by structural inequities stemming from deep poverty.52 In this context, repeated financial reinforcements for giving up smoking may promote feelings of autonomy, self-esteem, and confidence to quit,52 which may support ongoing engagement with a quit attempt.

Limitations and Strengths

Our study has several limitations. First, because it is a pilot randomized clinical trial, the sample size is relatively small. Replication in a larger-scale study is warranted to confirm our findings. Second, the study was conducted in safety-net clinics within a single urban area in the US, which may limit the external generalizability of the results to other settings. Third, we cannot rule out the possibility of contamination among participants from the same clinic who discuss financial incentives, but this risk is likely low because study procedures were conducted individually and at an outdoor location separate from the clinic sites.

This study also had several strengths. Despite the COVID-19 pandemic–related challenges with data collection that limited in-person recruitment and follow-up in clinics and the substantial mental health and substance use burden in this population, we achieved strong participant engagement, with a mean of 14.3 study visits attended during a 6-month period. The study used robust clinical trial methods and used engagement strategies that reached people in their communities. The CM incentives (ie, $176.9) per participant are within a plausible and implementable cost range. A future cost-effectiveness analysis is warranted to evaluate the economic value of this intervention to inform its scalability in clinical settings.

Conclusions

In this pilot randomized clinical trial of adults experiencing homelessness, an extended CM intervention integrated into safety-net health care settings was associated with higher odds of smoking abstinence than the control condition. These findings suggest that extended CM may be a promising strategy for health care settings serving adults experiencing homelessness, with attention to engagement and abstinence-contingent incentives to support cessation over time.
