Genetic insights into dabie bandavirus from a clinical case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome in Longyou County, China
Authors: Rui-jun Yang, Min Wang, Jia-ling You, Lei Lyu, Shi-teng Huang, Bing-dong Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo conduct an epidemiological investigation on a case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) reported in Longyou County, Zhejiang Province in April 2025, and to perform nucleic acid detection and genetic characterization of Dabie bandavirus (DBV) in the patient’s serum sample, aiming to provide scientific evidence and technical support for local SFTS prevention and control.MethodsAn epidemiological investigation was conducted on the case. Serum samples from the confirmed case and close contacts, as well as related vector host specimens, were collected. DBV-specific nucleic acid was detected using real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR. The viral genome was amplified by RT-PCR and subjected to whole-genome sequencing. Professional computer software (SeqMan Ultra 17.2, Clustal X 2.1, BioEdit V7.2.6.1, and MEGA V7.0.26, etc.) was used for nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignment, phylogenetic tree construction, and calculation of genetic distances and homology percentages.ResultsThe case had a history of tick bite and no history of travel outside the area. Fluorescence quantitative PCR detection showed that only the case’s serum sample was positive for DBV nucleic acid, while serum samples from close contacts and related vector host specimens were all negative for DBV nucleic acid. After amplification and sequencing, the three fragments L, M, and S were successfully obtained. Genetic evolution analysis showed that they belonged to genotypes L (C), M (C), and S (A), respectively. Compared with the closest reference strain HZ2023-16 in the phylogenetic tree, the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes were 99.74%, 99.53%, 99.66%, and 99.19%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 100%, 99.72%, 99.66%, and 99.59%, respectively. Compared with the type C reference strain AHL/China/2011 (L(C)/M(C)/S(D)), the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes were 96.87%, 96%, 93.76%, and 95.66%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 99.42%, 98.51%, 98.29%, and 99.18%, respectively. Compared with the type A reference strain JX23XSH (L(A)/M(A)/S(A)), the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes were 96.24%, 95.97%, 95.35%, and 97.02%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 99.52%, 98.14%, 99.32%, and 99.18%, respectively. The average genetic distances of the L, M, and S genes compared with the HZ2023-16 reference strain were 0.003, 0.005, and 0.005, respectively; compared with the type C reference strain AHL/China/2011 (L(C)/M(C)/S(D)), they were 0.031, 0.04, and 0.053, respectively; compared with the type A reference strain JX23XSH (L(A)/M(A)/S(A)), they were 0.038, 0.04, and 0.038, respectively. Amino acid variation analysis showed that compared with the reference strains, this strain had 0–12 amino acid substitutions in the L protein (e.g., V68I, A140V), 3–20 substitutions in the M protein (e.g., D151E, G863S), 1–5 substitutions in the NS protein, and 1–2 substitutions in the NP protein.ConclusionCombining the clinical manifestations, epidemiological history, and laboratory test results of the case, this outbreak can be determined as a local case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome. The strain is genetically closely related to human-derived reference strains and possesses specific genomic characteristics.

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick-borne infectious disease first identified in China in 2010, characterized clinically by fever and progressive thrombocytopenia and leukopenia [1]. The causative agent was initially named SFTS virus (SFTSV) and classified under the genus Phlebovirus in the order Bunyavirales. In 2020, the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) officially renamed it Dabie Bandavirus (DBV) [2]. DBV is a novel negative-strand RNA virus with a genome comprising three segments: S, M, and L. The S segment encodes two proteins: a nonstructural protein (NS) and a nucleoprotein (NP). The M segment encodes the glycoprotein precursor for Gn and Gc, which are responsible for virion formation and attachment to target cells. The L segment encodes the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDRP), which triggers viral RNA replication and transcription [2–3]. It has been reported that the key endemic areas in China include Henan, Anhui, Hubei, Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces, with confirmed SFTS cases also reported in Hunan, Xinjiang, Jilin, and Taiwan [4–8]. The disease has also been reported in South Korea, Japan, and several Southeast Asian countries [3, 4, 8], primarily concentrated in hilly or mountainous regions, exhibiting distinct geographical clustering and seasonal distribution, which may be associated with the geographical distribution and ecological niches of transmission vectors and hosts [6, 8, 9]. The first case in Zhejiang Province was reported in 2011 [10]. Longyou County, located in the central part of the Jinqu Basin in western Zhejiang Province, has a subtropical monsoon climate characterized by early springs, short autumns, and long summers and winters. On April 16, 2025, a case presenting with symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and lymphadenopathy without an obvious trigger was identified. In this study, serum samples were collected from the patient, and after laboratory nucleic acid testing and whole-genome sequencing for traceability, the case was confirmed as a DBV infection. Genetic characterization of the virus was conducted at the molecular level to provide scientific evidence and technical support for local SFTS prevention and control efforts.

Materials and methods

Case information

A patient with unexplained fever (39.3 °C), chills, poor appetite, muscle soreness in the limbs, and right inguinal lymphadenopathy was admitted to Longyou County Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine on the evening of April 15, 2025. Routine blood tests revealed thrombocytopenia and leukopenia. Given that the patient had been residing long-term in a mountainous rural area, the attending physician considered a suspected case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS).

Specimen collection and detection

Case specimen collection

A 5 mL non-anticoagulated blood specimen was aseptically collected from the patient.

Case finding

Case finding was conducted through interviews with family members and healthcare providers, as well as review of outpatient logs and inpatient medical records from the patient’s locality and surrounding healthcare facilities. Individuals who had direct contact with the patient’s blood, bloody secretions, excreta, or contaminated items, or with blood, excreta, or contaminated items from infected animals, were identified and monitored for 14 days. All sampled individuals provided informed consent.

Tick and animal surveillance and specimen collection

Tick surveillance was conducted in accordance with GB/T 36,788 “Surveillance methods for vector density - Ticks.” On April 17, parasitic tick surveillance was performed in and around the patient’s residence using the body surface examination method. Three parasitic ticks were collected from a cat, and one parasitic tick was collected from a dog. Additionally, four other dogs and one sheep were examined, with no parasitic ticks found. On April 18, questing tick surveillance was conducted in and around the patient’s residence using the flagging method, and six questing ticks were collected. An additional three questing ticks were collected on April 28. Concurrently, blood samples were aseptically collected from animals including cats, sheep, and dogs.

Specimen processing and nucleic acid detection

Tick specimens were homogenized using a tissue homogenizer and centrifuged for subsequent use. Blood specimens were centrifuged to separate serum for subsequent use. Nucleic acid extraction was performed using the Xi’an Tianlong nucleic acid extraction kit (Cqex-DNA/RNA virus (CDC) nucleic acid extraction or purification reagent, product number T333, specification 32T). Detection of Dabie Bandavirus (DBV) nucleic acid was performed using the Jiangsu Shuoshi novel bunyavirus nucleic acid detection kit (product number YJC70107N, specification 25T). Specimens were also submitted to the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention for confirmation.

Whole genome sequencing

Total viral nucleic acid was extracted using the BG-Flex-48 automated nucleic acid extraction system and its accompanying viral genome extraction kit (Shanghai Biogerm Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). Targeted amplification was then performed following the instructions of the Dabie Bandavirus whole genome enrichment kit (Shanghai Biogerm, specification 24T). Subsequently, library construction was performed on the enriched PCR products according to the operating guidelines of the whole genome DNA library construction kit (Shanghai Biogerm). Qualified libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform with PE150 sequencing.

Sequence analysis

The following computer software was used to process the determined structural gene sequences: SeqMan (Ultra 17.2) for sequence assembly; Clustal X (2.1) for multiple sequence alignment; BioEdit (v7.2.6.1) for identifying genetic locus characteristics; and MEGA (v7.0.26) with the Maximum Likelihood method (bootstrap = 1000) for phylogenetic analysis. Reference sequences were obtained from GenBank. Genotype classification was performed according to the method described in reference [11].

Results

Patient symptom description

The case was a 50-year-old male. He reported going up the mountains to dig bamboo shoots on April 1, during which he discovered a tick bite on his right foot that was engorged with blood and managed the wound himself. On April 12, he began to experience fatigue. On April 13, he developed fever (39.3 °C), chills, poor appetite, muscle soreness in the limbs, and right inguinal lymphadenopathy. On April 14, he visited Longyou County Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 3.1 × 10⁹/L and a platelet count of 91 × 10⁹/L. He was treated with cefuroxime for anti-infection and ibuprofen sustained-release capsules for fever reduction, but his temperature fluctuated. On April 14, he received intravenous lincomycin and ribavirin at a private clinic but continued to experience low-grade fever, fatigue, limb weakness, and right inguinal lymphadenopathy with pain. On the evening of April 15, he was admitted to Longyou County Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Physical examination revealed a temperature of 37.4 °C, and routine blood tests showed a white blood cell count of 1.3 × 10⁹/L, platelet count of 46 × 10⁹/L, lymphocytes 14.4%, and neutrophils 82.8%. On April 16, Longyou County Center for Disease Control and Prevention detected positive nucleic acid for Dabie Bandavirus, which was subsequently sent to Quzhou City Center for Disease Control and Prevention for confirmation. After confirmation on the evening of April 16, the patient was transferred to the Department of Infectious Diseases at Longyou County People’s Hospital. On April 17, physical examination revealed: temperature 37 °C, a pinpoint-sized erythema (tick bite site) on the medial aspect of the right lower leg, 6.0 cm above the ankle, and a palpable enlarged lymph node in the right groin measuring approximately 1.0 × 2.0 cm with significant tenderness. Routine blood tests showed white blood cell count 3.1 × 10⁹/L and platelet count 32 × 10⁹/L. Liver enzymes: aspartate aminotransferase 68 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase 566 U/L. No bleeding was observed, and symptomatic treatment with fluid replacement was continued. On April 20, routine blood tests showed white blood cell count 10.0 × 10⁹/L and platelet count 46 × 10⁹/L. On April 21, platelet count was 62 × 10⁹/L. On April 25, routine blood tests showed white blood cell count 3.4 × 10⁹/L and platelet count 131 × 10⁹/L. During hospitalization, the patient had no bleeding symptoms and remained hemodynamically stable. He was discharged on April 25.

Virus nucleic acid detection results

Case specimen

DBV detection was performed on positive controls, negative controls, and specimen nucleic acids by county, city, and provincial CDC laboratories. The case specimen (two parallel samples) showed ideal logarithmic growth curves, confirming DBV nucleic acid positivity.

Close contact, tick, and animal specimens

Specimens collected included samples from 4 close contacts, 1 sheep serum, 5 dog sera, 1 cat serum, 4 parasitic ticks, and 9 questing ticks. All tested negative for DBV nucleic acid.

Whole genome sequencing

Following amplification and sequencing by RT-PCR, the three segments—large (L), medium (M), and small (S)—of the DBV genome were obtained. The L segment was 6368 nucleotides in length, containing an open reading frame from positions 17 to 6271 of the cRNA 5’ end, encoding 2084 amino acids. The M segment was 3378 nucleotides in length, containing an open reading frame from positions 19 to 3240 of the cRNA 5’ end, encoding 1073 amino acids. The S segment was 1744 nucleotides in length and belonged to ambisense RNA, with two opposite-oriented reading frames separated by a 54-nucleotide intergenic region. One open reading frame was located from positions 29 to 910 of the cRNA 5’ end, encoding 293 amino acids (nonstructural protein NS). The other open reading frame was located in the reverse orientation from positions 1702 to 965 of the 3’ end, encoding 245 amino acids (nucleoprotein NP). After sequence assembly, the L, M, and S segment gene sequences were submitted to GenBank (accession numbers PV948530, PV948531, and PV948532, respectively) and designated as ZJH.

Sequence analysis

Phylogenetic tree analysis results

Representative DBV gene sequences from other regions of China and internationally published in NCBI were downloaded and aligned with the three gene sequences of DBV obtained in this study to construct phylogenetic trees. Analysis showed that the topological structures of the S, M, and L gene phylogenetic trees were generally consistent. The L gene belonged to the C genotype branch (bootstrap value 92) and was closest to the human-derived reference strain HZ2023-16 (PP706866.1) from Hangzhou, China, in 2023. The M gene belongs to the genotype C branch and clusters with the human reference strain HZ2023-16 (PP706930.1) isolated in Hangzhou, China, in 2023, with a bootstrap support of 100%.The S gene belonged to the A genotype branch (bootstrap value 96) and clustered with the 2023 human-derived reference strain HZ2023-16 (PP706930.1) from Hangzhou, China (bootstrap value 100), showing greater distance from other reference strains. Homology analysis

The genetic evolution analysis of this strain showed that its L, M, and S segments belonged to genotypes L(C), M(C), and S(A), respectively. Nucleotide and amino acid homology analyses were performed between this viral strain and the closest reference strains in the phylogenetic tree: HZ2023-16 (Hangzhou), AHL/China/2011 (Anhui), and JX23XSH (Nanchang). Analysis revealed that compared with HZ2023-16, the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes of this DBV strain were 99.74%, 99.53%, 99.66%, and 99.19%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 100%, 99.72%, 99.66%, and 99.59%, respectively. Compared with AHL/China/2011, the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes were 96.87%, 96%, 93.76%, and 95.66%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 99.42%, 98.51%, 98.29%, and 99.18%, respectively. Compared with the JX23XSH reference strain, the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, NS, and NP genes were 96.24%, 95.97%, 95.35%, and 97.02%, respectively, and the amino acid homologies were 99.52%, 98.14%, 99.32%, and 99.18%, respectively. Discussion

According to the Technical Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (2024 Edition) [12], combined with the epidemiological data, clinical symptoms, and laboratory test results of the case, this outbreak can be determined as a relatively typical case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS). The disease course and treatment process have certain clinical implications. The patient developed high fever (39.3 °C), progressive thrombocytopenia (91 × 10⁹/L → 32 × 10⁹/L), and lymphadenopathy approximately 12 days after the tick bite, consistent with the clinical characteristics of DBV infection. It is noteworthy that initial treatment with cefuroxime and ibuprofen sustained-release capsules, as well as intravenous lincomycin and ribavirin, failed to effectively control the condition, suggesting that primary care physicians may have insufficient awareness of tick-borne diseases. After transfer and treatment with antiviral and fluid replacement supportive therapy, the patient’s platelet count gradually recovered to 131 × 10⁹/L. However, the 24-hour delay in confirmation (admitted on April 15 → confirmed on April 16) further exposed the diagnostic capacity gaps for tick-borne diseases in primary healthcare institutions. Additionally, the elevated lactate dehydrogenase (566 U/L) suggested the need to be alert to cytokine storm [13], although this case did not progress to severe illness, possibly related to early fluid replacement therapy. In this study, phylogenetic analysis of the L, M, and S gene segments of the DBV strain revealed the virus’s unique genotype combination characteristics and evolutionary relationships. The L gene belongs to the genotype C branch, has the closest genetic distance to the 2023 human reference strain HZ2023-16 (PP706866.1), and shows a bootstrap value of 92%, indicating a high degree of relatedness on the phylogenetic tree. The M gene belongs to the genotype C branch and clusters with the 2023 human reference strain HZ2023-16 (PP706930.1) isolated in Hangzhou, China, with a bootstrap support of 100%. Furthermore, the S gene is clearly assigned to the genotype A branch, with a bootstrap value of 96%, and forms a tight cluster with HZ2023-16 (PP706994.1) (bootstrap value also 100%), but shows a greater genetic distance from other reference strains. Notably, the reference strain HZ2023-16 itself also exhibits the same genotype combination pattern of L(C) + M(C) + S(A) [14]. This L(C) + M(C) + S(A) genotype combination pattern may reflect a transitional state of the virus during host adaptation.

The L, M, and S genotyping of the virus strain in this case is L(C), M(C), and S(A), respectively, and its genetic evolutionary characteristics are generally consistent with the genetic diversity trend of DBV. Compared with the closest reference strain on the phylogenetic tree, HZ2023-16, the nucleotide homologies of the L, M, and S gene segments are all above 99% (up to 99.74%), and the amino acid homologies of their encoded proteins are close to 100% (the L protein is 100% identical), suggesting that they may belong to the same evolutionary branch and that virulence-related proteins (e.g., NP) are highly conserved. In contrast, compared with the early strains AHL/China/2011 and JX23XSH, the nucleotide homologies are significantly reduced, but the amino acid homologies remain relatively high, indicating that while nucleotide mutations have occurred during viral evolution, protein functional stability has been maintained through codon degeneracy. Additionally, the amino acid homology difference in the M gene may affect the antigenicity of the viral envelope protein, which requires further verification through epitope analysis. This result not only provides new data for the molecular epidemiological study of DBV but also suggests that attention should be paid to the impact of genetic variations in regional epidemic strains on vaccine design [15].

The L(C), M(C), S(A) genotype combination of the DBV strain in this study presents unique evolutionary characteristics, and genetic distance analysis revealed molecular differences from various reference strains. Compared with the closest reference strain on the phylogenetic tree, HZ2023-16, the average genetic distance of the DBV L gene was only 0.003, lower than that of the M gene (0.005) and S gene (0.005), indicating that the L gene is relatively conserved during evolution, which may be closely related to the functional stability of the viral polymerase. Further comparison with the genotype C reference strain AHL/China/2011 and the genotype A reference strain JX23XSH showed that the genetic distance of the DBV L gene was lower than that of the S gene, while the genetic distance of the M gene fell between the two. This difference may reflect the different evolutionary rates of different genotypes under natural selection pressure. It has been reported that the M gene segment is an important immunogen of the virus, experiencing greater selective pressure during virus-host binding, and its evolutionary rate is relatively fast [5]. Notably, the genetic distance between the DBV M gene and the genotype A reference strain JX23XSH (0.04) was slightly higher than that of the L gene (0.038) and S gene (0.038), suggesting that the M gene may be subject to different selective pressures during evolution compared to the L and S genes.

The DBV strain in this study exhibits a unique genotype combination of L(C)/M(C)/S(A), and amino acid variation analysis provides important information for understanding DBV evolution. Because the DBV genome is a segmented RNA virus lacking proofreading function during transcription and replication, it has a certain mutation rate [16]. Compared with the closest reference strain HZ2023-16, the DBV L protein showed no amino acid substitutions, while the M protein had only three amino acid mutations. Further comparison revealed that the DBV M protein had 20 amino acid differences from the genotype A reference strain JX23XSH, which was higher than the 16 differences from the genotype C reference strain AHL/China/2011, consistent with the phylogenetic result that the M gene belongs to the genotype C branch. It has been reported that the M segment has high variability [4], and the number of amino acid mutations in the M protein in this study supports this conclusion. Furthermore, the number of amino acid mutations in the NS protein was significantly lower than that in the M protein, while mutations in the NP protein remained at a low level, indicating that viral structural proteins are under stronger functional constraints during evolution. These findings provide a new perspective for understanding the molecular evolutionary mechanisms of DBV and offer an important theoretical basis for vaccine design and diagnostic reagent development based on protein variations [15]. Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick‑borne infectious disease. This case is the first reported occurrence in Longyou County. Field epidemiological investigation showed that the patient was a locally acquired case, with a clear history of tick bite, no travel history outside the area, and no contact with similar SFTS cases. All 13 collected ticks and nearby animal samples tested negative for DBV nucleic acid. Possible reasons for these negative results include: the timing of field sampling may have missed the peak viremic period in ticks or host animals (e.g., the post‑viremic phase), the limited sample size may be insufficient to detect low‑level infection, and the natural infection rate of DBV in local vectors may indeed be low. Because only a small number of animal serum specimens were collected in this study and only nucleic acid testing was performed, serological testing was not carried out. Future studies should include serological surveillance of local host animals and ticks to more comprehensively assess the circulation status of DBV in the local area. The strain obtained in this study shows high nucleotide and amino acid homology with some reference strains, but the specific transmission route remains unclear. Recent studies have indicated that SFTSV may have a risk of human‑to‑human transmission under certain conditions [17]. Although nucleic acid tests of close contacts in this study were all negative, vigilance should still be maintained in future epidemic prevention and control. The prognosis of SFTS is generally favorable; however, elderly patients, those with underlying diseases, or those who delay seeking medical care tend to have more severe illness, and critically ill patients may die from multiple organ failure. In response to this epidemic, the local health authorities have taken active measures, including conducting emergency surveillance, strengthening routine surveillance, enhancing training at the grassroots level, and raising awareness of the diagnosis and treatment of tick‑borne diseases such as SFTS. Among these, the appropriate use of antiviral drugs and optimization of the referral system are key to improving patient outcomes.
