Mpox outbreak associated with multiple sexual partnerships and HIV coinfection in Masaka District, Uganda, February-April 2025
Authors: Gertrude Abbo, Richard Migisha, Emmanuel Okiror Okello, Bridget Ainembabazi, Stella Martha Migamba, Charles Kasozi, Lilian Bulage, Benon Kwesiga, Steven Ndugwa Kabwama, Alex Riolexus Ario

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Mpox outbreak in south-central Uganda mainly affected young adults (25-34 years).•Human immunodeficiency virus infection is strongly associated with mpox infection (adjusted odds ratio 5.4).•Having multiple sexual partners increased the odds of mpox infection (adjusted odds ratio 7.3).•Close contact with individuals with a rash significantly raised infection risk.•Integration of mpox services into human immunodeficiency virus programs could reduce transmission.Keywords: Mpox, Outbreaks, Case-control studies, HIV, Risk-taking, Uganda

Introduction

Monkeypox (mpox) is a reemerging zoonotic viral disease caused by the mpox virus, an orthopoxvirus from the Poxviridae family [1]. Individuals affected by mpox experience a time interval of 7-21 days between exposure and the onset of distinctive clinical symptoms. These symptoms include fever, headache, myalgia, back pain, chills, lymphadenopathy, skin lesions, and genital or perianal lesions. Clinical manifestations can vary depending on the route of exposure and may include localized genital, perianal, or mucosal lesions [2]. The virus can first spread from an animal to a human through animal bites and scratches. Human-to-human transmission can occur through direct contact with lesions, body fluids, or contaminated materials, and through respiratory secretions during prolonged close face-to-face contact [3]. As of June 30, 2024, a total of 97,962 mpox cases had been reported across 118 countries, including 111 countries with no prior history of the disease [4].

Since 2022, mpox has expanded beyond historically affected regions, with substantial increases in reported cases in Africa [5]. Although global transmission declined following the 2022-2023 outbreak, African countries continued to experience increasing numbers of suspected and confirmed cases, partly reflecting improved surveillance and persistent challenges in laboratory confirmation [6]. In contrast, challenged by low capacity for laboratory confirmation, African countries witnessed a 78.5% increase in the number of suspected cases from roughly 8000 in 2022 to about 15,000 in 2023 [7]. By August 2024, the continent had reported over 18,000 suspected cases (including over 5000 confirmed cases) and over 600 deaths, representing a 160% increase compared with the same period in 2023 [8].

Uganda experienced a substantial increase in mpox cases during 2024-2025 with a cumulative total of 4810 confirmed mpox cases and 37 deaths, with central districts, including Masaka, reporting increasing numbers of cases. Individuals aged 25-29 years were among the most affected demographic groups, highlighting the importance of targeted risk communication and prevention efforts among young adults [9]. Masaka District reported a marked increase in confirmed cases during early 2025, prompting intensified surveillance and outbreak investigation. According to the National mpox Situation Report dated March 4, 2025, Masaka District reported 144 confirmed cases, with males being more affected [10]. The outbreak was particularly severe in the Masaka Sub-region, especially Masaka and Kyotera Districts, where 105 new cases were reported in the 2 weeks leading up to February 20, 2025 [11]. Environmental and social conditions that facilitate frequent or prolonged close contact may influence mpox transmission. In Masaka District, such conditions may include high population mobility, fishing communities, crowded households, and settings where residential and commercial activities occur within the same premises. These factors may increase opportunities for close interpersonal contact and therefore warrant consideration during outbreak investigations. Despite growing evidence that close physical contact and sexual networks may contribute to transmission, context-specific evidence describing the behavioral and environmental factors associated with mpox transmission in Uganda remains limited.

We conducted this outbreak investigation in Masaka District to describe the epidemiology of the outbreak, identify associated risk factors, and inform targeted control measures.

Methods

Outbreak setting

Masaka District is located in south-central Uganda, approximately 130 km from Kampala, with an estimated population of approximately 1,000,000 in 2025. The district includes both urban centers and fishing communities, characterized by high population mobility and dense social interactions (Figure 1). Health services are provided through a network of public, private, and faith-based facilities, including the Masaka Regional Referral Hospital, health center IVs, IIIs, and IIs.

A map of Uganda showing the location of Masaka District.

Case definitions

A suspected case was defined as the onset of a skin rash (papular or vesicular) on any part of the body, with fever and any of the following symptoms: swollen lymph nodes, headache, muscle pain, back pain, and fatigue, in a resident of Masaka District during February-April 2025. A confirmed case was a suspected case with laboratory confirmation of mpox by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The case definitions used in this study were adopted from the Ministry of Health guidelines on mpox that were in effect at the time of the investigation [12].

We conducted case finding by reviewing facility records at the treatment unit in Masaka Regional Referral Hospital. We also conducted active house-to-house case search in the communities within the district with the help of village health teams and snowballing, especially for newly confirmed mpox cases. We interviewed patients with mpox using a case investigation form and developed a line list. The case investigation form included information on demographic characteristics, clinical features such as the time of symptom onset and duration, social history, exposures, and any laboratory investigations done (Appendix 1).

Descriptive epidemiology

We conducted a descriptive analysis of data from interviews with patients with mpox. We characterized cases by person, place, and time. Person characteristics included age and sex; place was assessed by subcounty of residence; and time was assessed using the date of symptom onset. We constructed an epidemic curve using the date of symptom onset to describe the temporal distribution of cases during the outbreak. We categorized age (in years) into five meaningful categories, i.e., <15 years, 15-24, 25-34, 35-44, and ≥45 years. We used the 2024 Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS) population estimates for Masaka District [13] to calculate population-based attack rates. We used the 2024 population estimates from UBOS for Masaka District to calculate subcounty-level attack rates. We constructed choropleth maps using the Quantum Geographic Information System software to display attack rates by subcounty.

Environmental assessment

As part of the comprehensive epidemiologic investigation into the mpox outbreak in Masaka District, environmental assessments were conducted to complement case investigation data. Community observations were conducted in affected households, assessing housing conditions, sanitation, and patterns of social interaction within and between households. These assessments aimed to identify environmental and behavioral factors that may contribute to transmission risk and to evaluate the feasibility of infection prevention and control measures within the homes and surrounding environments of patients with confirmed mpox.

Hypothesis generation

We conducted 12 interviews among patients with mpox who were identified through active case search. We also interviewed members of the district health team who were part of the mpox response team, clinicians who attended to the patients with mpox, and the head of case management at Masaka Regional Referral Hospital, where most of the patients with mpox were managed. We obtained data on demographic characteristics, clinical presentation of cases, dates of symptom onset, duration, social history, exposures, and any laboratory investigations done. We used the results of the descriptive analysis of data from patients with mpox, key informant interviews, and environmental findings to generate hypotheses.

Case-control study

To evaluate the hypotheses generated from our preliminary findings and to identify risk factors associated with mpox, we conducted a case-control study. Confirmed cases were selected from health facility records and subsequently followed up in the community for interviews. A total of 86 patients with confirmed mpox who were reachable during the investigation period were included in the study. Controls were selected at a ratio of 1:2 (172 controls). We calculated the sample size using the Kelsey method, assuming that 70% of cases would have mpox infection and that the corresponding odds ratio would be 2.3, with 80% power and a 95% confidence level. The exposure assumption was based on previous mpox outbreak investigations in Nigeria, West Africa [14]. Cases included patients with confirmed mpox identified during the outbreak period who were available for interview. Controls were selected from the same communities as the cases. Following identification of a case household, eligible non-case households within the same community were enumerated. From the eligible non-case households, households were selected conveniently. Two eligible individuals were then selected from each selected household using simple random sampling. Controls were residents of Masaka District who had no history of symptoms suggestive of mpox during February-April 2025. Controls were screened for symptoms compatible with mpox using the applicable case definition and were excluded if they reported a history of compatible symptoms during the study period. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was administered to the eligible patients with mpox and controls to obtain information on their demographic characteristics, clinical presentation of cases, dates of symptom onset, duration, social history, any laboratory investigations done, and potential risk factors for mpox infection.

Data analysis

We analyzed the data using Epi Info™ Version 7 to identify risk factors associated with mpox infection among the residents of Masaka District. We used odds ratios to determine the association between different exposures and mpox infection by conducting bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Variables with P ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analysis were considered for multivariable logistic regression based on the predefined analytical approach. We used a stepwise model-building procedure, with model improvement assessed using the likelihood-ratio test. We also examined changes in the precision of estimates as variables were added or removed and considered epidemiological plausibility when interpreting the final model. The associations were only considered significant at a P-value of <0.05. We also conducted common reference group analysis to untangle the possible mixing of effects of different exposures on mpox infection among the study population. The under-15 age group was excluded from the regression analysis because of the zero cell therefore the age-group regression sample size comprised 253 participants (81 cases and 172 controls).

Descriptive epidemiology

The highest attack rate was observed in the 25-34-year-old age group (24.0 per 10,000 population), followed by 35-44-year-olds (20.4 per 10,000 population). The attack rates were similar among males and females, at 2.4 and 2.2 per 10,000 population, respectively.

Temporal distribution of confirmed mpox cases identified through surveillance, October 2024-April 2025

Routine surveillance identified sporadic confirmed mpox cases in Masaka District beginning in October 2024. The number of cases increased from late January 2025, with the highest concentration of cases occurring between February 21 and March 4, 2025. The analytic outbreak investigation and case-control study focused on cases identified during February-April 2025 (Figure 2). An increase in cases was observed shortly after the December 2024 festive season, possibly reflecting increased social interactions and travel-related exposures during that period.

A sustained rise in cases occurred from late January 2025, coinciding with the Ministry of Health’s alert and heightened surveillance in Masaka District. The outbreak peaked between February 21 and March 4, 2025, when the majority of confirmed cases were reported. In early March, the National Rapid Response Team was deployed to support outbreak field investigations and control. This was followed by risk communication and community engagement campaigns initiated in late March. From early April onward, the number of new cases steadily declined, indicating a likely effect of the public health interventions (Figure 2).

Attack rates by subcounty during the mpox outbreak in Masaka District, Uganda, February-April 2025

Attack rates varied across subcounties in Masaka District (Figure 3). Bukakata Subcounty, a predominantly fisherfolk community, recorded the highest attack rate, of 6 cases per 10,000 population. Moderate attack rates were observed in Nyendo-Mukungwe and Kimanya-Kabonera, whereas lower rates were observed in Masaka City and Kyanamukaaka.

Environmental assessment findings

Findings from the environmental assessments revealed that many of the case households operated as multipurpose venues functioning as retail shops during the day and bars during the evening. Environmental factors such as crowded living conditions, high population mobility, communal workplaces, fishing communities, and households that double as commercial premises likely facilitated close physical contact and enhanced transmission. These dual-use environments were often overcrowded, poorly ventilated, and lacked infrastructure to support effective isolation of symptomatic individuals.

Characteristics of cases and controls

A total of 258 participants were enrolled in the investigation, comprising 86 patients with confirmed mpox and 172 neighborhood controls (Table 3). The mean age of cases was 29 years (standard deviation [SD] = 9) compared to 33 years (SD = 9) among controls. Patients with mpox were more likely to be in the 25-34-year age group (45%), whereas a higher proportion of controls were aged ≥35 years (P = 0.003). Slightly more than half of the cases were female (52%), while the majority of controls were male (61%) (P = 0.046).

Geographically, most cases resided in urban settings (71%), compared to 58% of controls (P = 0.042). With respect to occupation, the largest proportion of cases were businesspersons (38%), followed by bar workers (21%) and fisherfolk (16%); occupational distribution did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.31) (Table 3).

A significant difference was observed in HIV status, where 56% of cases were HIV-positive compared to 13% of controls (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Risk Factors for mpox infection among residents of Masaka District, Uganda, February-April 2025

In multivariable analysis, mpox infection was significantly associated with close contact with persons with mpox-like symptoms, risky sexual behaviors, and HIV infection (Table 4). Individuals who reported close contact with a person with a rash had approximately threefold higher odds of infection (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 3.2; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.4-6.9). Individuals with HIV were significantly more likely to be infected than individuals without HIV (aOR = 5.4; 95% CI = 2.3-12.6). The strongest association was observed among participants reporting multiple sexual partners, who had approximately sevenfold higher odds of mpox infection (aOR = 7.3; 95% CI = 3.5-15.3) (Table 4).

Discussion

This outbreak predominantly affected young adults aged 25-34 years and was concentrated in urban and fishing communities characterized by high levels of social interaction and mobility. The findings suggest that transmission was associated with close-contact environments and behavioral risk factors. Bukakata Subcounty, a fisherfolk landing site on the western shores of Lake Victoria, experienced the highest attack rates. More than half of the patients with mpox were living with HIV. HIV infection, multiple sexual partners, and close contact with a person with a rash were associated with increased odds of mpox infection.

The strong association between multiple sexual partners and mpox infection is consistent with evidence from African and other settings indicating that sexual and close-contact networks can contribute to transmission. In this outbreak, the findings suggest that overlapping sexual networks may have facilitated transmission among affected communities [13,14]. Tailored, non-stigmatizing interventions, including peer-to-peer education, risk communication, early case detection, contact tracing, and supportive isolation, may help reduce transmission in similar settings [15]. Policies could promote collaboration with sexual health clinics to ensure these interventions are accessible and acceptable.

Close contact with symptomatic individuals was also significantly associated with infection, reinforcing the role of direct contact in transmission and the importance of early detection and isolation [16]. Similar patterns were reported in Chile [17] and African outbreaks [16], where close skin-to-skin contact facilitated mpox spread. These findings support early recognition, prompt isolation, community-based surveillance, and contact tracing, particularly in communities and settings where frequent close interpersonal contact occurs. Environmental assessments identified bars, lodges, and multipurpose households as settings where close interactions occurred; however, the investigation did not establish these settings as direct sources of transmission. Public health messaging in similar outbreak settings should emphasize avoiding direct contact with symptomatic individuals.

HIV infection was associated with approximately fivefold increased odds of mpox infection, likely reflecting immunosuppression [13]. Global evidence indicates that individuals with HIV are at higher risk for severe mpox, including hospitalization [18]. The association with HIV infection may reflect underlying immunosuppression or overlapping risk networks. This highlights the importance of integrating mpox prevention and screening within HIV programs to enable early detection and management of mpox among people living with HIV.

These findings have several implications for the mpox response in Masaka District. The association of mpox with multiple sexual partnerships, HIV infection, and close contact with symptomatic individuals supports strengthening early case detection, contact tracing, risk communication, and integration of mpox screening and prevention within HIV services. Risk communication should be non-stigmatizing and tailored to communities with frequent close interpersonal contact. Environmental assessments may also help identify settings where infection prevention measures and practical isolation arrangements are needed.

Strengths and limitations

This investigation had several strengths, including active case finding and household follow-up, which enhanced case detection beyond routine facility-based reporting and enabled assessment of behavioral and environmental exposures. However, some limitations should be considered. Information on sexual behaviors and other exposures was self-reported and may have been subject to recall and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of the questions. Stigma related to mpox, HIV infection, and sexual behaviors may also have contributed to underreporting of exposures. In addition, controls were not laboratory-tested for mpox; therefore, asymptomatic or unrecognized infections could not be completely excluded, potentially resulting in misclassification. We minimized potential information bias through private interviews, confidentiality assurances, standardized interviewer-administered questionnaires, and a defined recall period. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the observed associations.

Conclusion

The mpox outbreak in Masaka District primarily affected young adults and was associated with behavioral and clinical risk factors, including multiple sexual partnerships and underlying HIV infection. Strengthening integration of mpox interventions within HIV programs and implementing targeted risk communication strategies may help reduce transmission in similar high-risk settings.
