Blood biomarkers and penalized logistic regression for the prediction of complicated appendicitis in children: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Masanaga Matsumoto, Yohei Sanmoto, Kouji Masumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Early preoperative identification of complicated appendicitis in children is important for risk stratification. Several blood biomarkers have been proposed for this purpose, but their performance has rarely been compared directly within the same cohort. We aimed to clarify their relative diagnostic performance and to determine whether combining laboratory parameters using an elastic-net model provides incremental diagnostic value.Materials and methods:This single-center, retrospective observational study included children younger than 16 years who underwent emergency appendectomy between January 2015 and December 2024. Preoperative laboratory parameters and composite biomarkers were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analyses, and pooled area under the curve (AUC) estimates were calculated after multiple imputation using Rubin’s rules. AUC values were interpreted as measures of discriminative performance, with higher values indicating better discrimination. Pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using DeLong’s test with Holm adjustment. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2.Results:Among 308 patients, 109 (35.4%) had complicated appendicitis. The C-reactive protein (CRP)-to-albumin ratio (CAR) showed the numerically highest AUC among the evaluated biomarkers [0.852; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.800–0.892], followed by CRP (0.848; 95% CI, 0.795–0.889), chloride (0.817; 95% CI, 0.763–0.860), sodium (0.765; 95% CI, 0.703–0.818), and albumin (0.715; 95% CI, 0.649–0.773). DeLong comparisons showed no significant difference between CAR and either CRP or chloride (P = 0.19 for both), whereas CAR significantly outperformed sodium and albumin (P = 0.02 and P < 0.001, respectively). The elastic-net model achieved a pooled AUC of 0.871 (95% CI, 0.824–0.906) and provided only modest additional discrimination over CAR.Conclusion:CAR showed robust diagnostic performance and may provide a practical balance between accuracy and simplicity for early risk stratification of complicated appendicitis in children. Laboratory-only multivariable modeling provided limited additional value, suggesting that further improvement may require integration of clinical and ultrasonographic findings.

Introduction

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common surgical emergencies in children presenting with acute abdominal pain[1], with an estimated incidence of approximately 4 per 1000 person-years among school-aged children[2]. Complicated appendicitis, defined as gangrenous change, perforation, or abscess formation, accounts for nearly 30% of pediatric cases[3]. This condition is clinically significant because it is associated with longer operative time and hospitalization, increased need for postoperative imaging or additional interventions, and a higher 30-day readmission rate[3]. These factors underscore the importance of early preoperative identification and risk stratification to guide optimal perioperative management.

Several approaches have been proposed to predict complicated appendicitis in children, including imaging-based assessments[4–6], laboratory parameters[7–16], clinical scoring systems combined with additional laboratory markers[17–19], and multifactorial models incorporating clinical, imaging, and laboratory data[20,21]. However, physical examination findings can be subjective[22], particularly in young children, where reliable assessment is often limited by poor cooperation and interpretation challenges. Similarly, ultrasonography – the most commonly used imaging modality in pediatric cases – is operator dependent, and its diagnostic accuracy varies by examiner skill and inter-observer differences[4]. In contrast, blood biomarkers are objective, quantitative, and routinely available before surgery. Nevertheless, although numerous individual biomarkers and composite indices have been proposed, their diagnostic performance has varied across studies, and direct head-to-head comparisons within a single pediatric cohort remain limited[7–16,23–27]. Thus, the relative performance of readily available blood biomarkers and the incremental value of more complex multivariable models remain insufficiently defined in pediatric appendicitis.

In this study, we performed a head-to-head comparison of previously reported objective preoperative biomarkers in a single pediatric cohort to assess their ability to differentiate complicated from uncomplicated appendicitis. The analysis focused on laboratory markers that are readily available and less dependent on examiner skill, reflecting the clinical context of pediatric emergency care, in which initial evaluations are frequently performed by providers with varying levels of experience. This approach may support early risk stratification and timely surgical consultation. Furthermore, we examined whether a multivariable penalized logistic regression model could improve diagnostic performance compared with established biomarkers.

Materials and methods

Patients and data collection

This retrospective observational study included patients younger than 16 years who underwent emergency appendectomy for acute appendicitis at a tertiary pediatric surgery center between January 2015 and December 2024. Patients who underwent concurrent surgical procedures were excluded.

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and included patient characteristics (sex, age, height, body weight, body mass index, history of conservative treatment, antibiotic administration before hospital presentation, duration from symptom onset to hospital presentation, body temperature at presentation, presence of nausea and/or vomiting, signs of peritoneal irritation, time from blood sampling to surgical initiation, and presence of periappendiceal free fluid on ultrasonography); intraoperative and postoperative findings (operative time, estimated blood loss, presence of fecalith in the specimen, method of appendiceal stump closure, presence of perforation or abscess, intraperitoneal lavage, drain placement, postoperative complications with Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ II, postoperative length of stay, and 30-day readmission); and histopathological findings (presence of gangrenous appendicitis).

Preoperative laboratory parameters included white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts; hemoglobin (Hb), C-reactive protein (CRP), sodium, chloride, total bilirubin (T-bil), albumin, and fibrinogen levels; and the prothrombin time-international normalized ratio (PT-INR). In addition, composite biomarkers representing combined inflammatory and nutritional indices were calculated as follows: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio = platelet count/lymphocyte count; C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) = CRP level/albumin level; systemic immune-inflammation index = (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count; systemic inflammation response index = (neutrophil count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count; and pan-immune-inflammation value = (neutrophil count × monocyte count × platelet count)/lymphocyte count. These biomarkers were selected because they consist of routinely available preoperative laboratory parameters or composite indices derived from such parameters and have been reported in previous studies to be diagnostically useful for complicated appendicitis in pediatric populations[7–16,23–27].

Treatment strategy and surgical procedure

During the study period, emergency surgery was the standard management strategy for acute appendicitis. At our institution, appendectomy was routinely performed using the transumbilical laparoscopic-assisted appendectomy technique[28]. Appendiceal stump closure was generally performed extracorporeally; however, intracorporeal closure was used in technically challenging cases, such as those with severe adhesions or appendiceal injury that prevented exteriorization. Intraperitoneal lavage and drain placement were performed at the surgeon’s discretion.

Antibiotic management for appendicitis was consistent throughout the study period. Uncomplicated appendicitis was treated with cefmetazole at a dose of 100 mg/kg/day, whereas complicated (perforated) appendicitis was treated with meropenem (60 mg/kg/day) in combination with amikacin (8 mg/kg/day). Escalation or de-escalation of antibiotic therapy was performed when clinically indicated, based on the patient’s clinical course and microbiological findings. The duration of antibiotic therapy and the timing of hospital discharge were determined by the primary physician, based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical parameters, including resolution of fever, laboratory findings, and tolerance of oral intake.

Definitions of complicated and uncomplicated appendicitis

Patients were classified as having complicated or uncomplicated appendicitis based on intraoperative and histopathological findings. Complicated appendicitis was defined as the presence of at least one of the following features: gangrene, perforation, or abscess formation, in accordance with the 2020 World Society of Emergency Surgery Jerusalem guidelines[1]. Uncomplicated appendicitis was defined as cases diagnosed with acute appendicitis on postoperative histopathological examination that did not meet any of the above three criteria.

Handling of missing data

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations. The number of imputations (m) was set to 20 to ensure adequate relative efficiency. The imputation model included clinically relevant variables to ensure congeniality, including age, sex, height, body weight, history of conservative treatment, administration of antibiotics before hospital presentation, body temperature, the duration from symptom onset to hospital presentation, individual laboratory parameters, and disease classification (complicated or uncomplicated appendicitis).

Elastic-net model development

An elastic-net–penalized logistic regression model was constructed using multiple preoperative laboratory parameters as predictors[29]. Elastic-net regularization combines L1 and L2 penalties to enable variable selection and coefficient shrinkage, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting in the presence of correlated predictors. Nested 10-fold cross-validation was applied: the inner loop tuned α over {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} and selected the largest λ within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error, while the outer loop evaluated model performance (training:test = 9:1). Predictors were standardized within each training fold to avoid data leakage. The final model for each outer iteration was evaluated on the held-out test fold, and the area under the curve (AUC) values obtained from the outer predictions were pooled across multiply imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the diagnostic performance of preoperative laboratory and composite biomarkers for differentiating complicated from uncomplicated appendicitis. The secondary outcome was the comparison of surgical outcomes between the two groups. In addition, we performed a supplementary analysis using an elastic-net–penalized multivariable predictive model to assess whether integrating multiple laboratory parameters could improve diagnostic performance compared with established biomarkers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test or chi-squared test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For between-group comparisons of blood biomarkers, Hodges–Lehmann median differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as effect size estimates.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for individual laboratory parameters that demonstrated strong between-group differences in univariate analyses (P < 0.001) and for all composite biomarkers. Within each imputed dataset, AUC, optimal cutoff values determined using the Youden index, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were calculated. These metrics were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules after a logit transformation of AUCs, and 95% CIs were derived using the t-distribution. AUC values were interpreted as measures of discriminative performance, with higher values indicating better discrimination; qualitative labels such as “poor,” “good,” or “excellent” were not applied because such labeling systems are not standardized[30]. For the five biomarkers with the numerically highest AUCs, pairwise comparisons of ROC curves were performed using DeLong’s test, with the marker showing the highest AUC used as the reference. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. In addition, ROC analyses restricted to complete-case data were conducted as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the ROC results. Decision-curve analysis was performed to assess the clinical net benefit of the best-performing biomarker and the elastic-net model across threshold probabilities, with reference to treat-all and treat-none strategies. Notably, variables with a missingness rate exceeding 20% were not included in the multiple imputation model. This threshold was selected pragmatically to avoid unstable imputations in variables with substantial missing data. Such variables were excluded from subsequent ROC analyses and elastic-net–based modeling.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 revision) and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the participating institution (Approval No. R07-183). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because the study used clinical information obtained during routine medical care, and an opt-out option was implemented to allow participants and their guardians to decline the use of clinical data for research purposes.

Results

Participant characteristics

During the study period, 312 emergency appendectomies were performed for acute appendicitis. After excluding 4 patients who underwent concomitant procedures, 308 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 109 (35.4%) were classified as having complicated appendicitis and 199 (64.6%) as uncomplicated appendicitis. Patients in the complicated group were younger; the number of patients aged ≤ 5 years was 12 of 109 (11.0%) compared with 2 of 199 (1.0%) in the uncomplicated group (P < 0.001), with the youngest patients being 2 years of age. A history of conservative treatment was reported for 4 of 109 (3.7%) and 18 of 199 (9.0%) patients, respectively (P = 0.11). Antibiotic administration before hospital presentation was documented in 17 of 109 patients (15.6%) in the complicated group and 21 of 199 patients (10.6%) in the uncomplicated group, with no statistically significant between-group difference (P = 0.20). Patients with complicated appendicitis more frequently presented after a delayed interval of ≥24 hours from symptom onset compared with those with uncomplicated appendicitis (76.1% vs. 36.2%, P < 0.001). Body temperature at presentation was significantly higher in the complicated group (median [IQR] 38.0 [37.4–38.5] °C vs. 37.0 [36.7–37.7] °C, P < 0.001), and signs of peritoneal irritation were more commonly observed (71.6% vs. 52.3%, P = 0.001). The interval from blood sampling to surgical initiation was shorter in the complicated group (median [IQR] 3.0 [3.0–5.0] hours vs. 5.0 [3.0–9.0] hours, P = 0.010). Diagnostic accuracy of preoperative laboratory biomarkers

In ROC analysis, CAR showed the numerically highest discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI, 0.800–0.892), sensitivity of 0.814 (95% CI, 0.730–0.876), and specificity of 0.767 (95% CI, 0.704–0.821). This was followed by CRP [AUC of 0.848 (95% CI, 0.795–0.889)], chloride [AUC of 0.817 (95% CI, 0.763–0.860)], sodium [AUC of 0.765 (95% CI, 0.703–0.818)], and albumin [AUC of 0.715 (95% CI, 0.649–0.773)] (Table 4 and Fig. 1). In DeLong comparisons with Holm adjustment, using CAR as the reference marker, the AUC of CAR was not significantly different from those of CRP or chloride (adjusted P = 0.19 for both), but was significantly higher than those of sodium and albumin (adjusted P = 0.02 and adjusted P < 0.001, respectively). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to complete cases (n = 290), the overall discriminative performance of these biomarkers was largely consistent with that observed in the primary multiple imputation–based analysis, with no material changes in AUC estimates or their relative ranking across markers (Table 4). Results of the elastic-net model

Variable selection was stable across imputations, with chloride, CRP, and albumin consistently identified as key predictors (selection frequency = 100%; Table 5). These predictors were incorporated into elastic-net models whose tuning predominantly favored ridge-like regularization (α = 0 in 86.5%, 0.25 in 6.0%, 0.50 in 5.0%, 0.75 in 1.5%, and 1 in 1.0%). The elastic-net logistic regression model yielded a pooled AUC of 0.871 (95% CI, 0.824–0.906), which was numerically higher than that observed for the best-performing biomarker, CAR (Fig. 1). Decision-curve analysis demonstrated that both CAR and the elastic-net model provided greater net benefit than the treat-all and treat-none strategies across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 2). The elastic-net model showed a slightly higher apparent net benefit than CAR over much of the threshold range; however, the magnitude of this difference was limited (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study directly compared established preoperative blood biomarkers within a single pediatric cohort for discriminating complicated from uncomplicated appendicitis and further examined whether a multivariable elastic-net model could enhance diagnostic performance. Two key findings emerged. First, among all individual and composite biomarkers, CAR showed the numerically highest discriminative ability, achieving a pooled AUC of 0.852 (95% CI, 0.800–0.892). Second, although the elastic-net model integrating multiple routinely measured laboratory parameters yielded an AUC of 0.871, the incremental gain beyond CAR was modest. Taken together, these findings indicate that, within the scope of commonly obtained blood parameters, the potential for multivariable modeling to meaningfully outperform CAR in identifying complicated appendicitis may be limited.

CAR is defined as the ratio of CRP level to albumin level, integrating an inflammatory and a nutritional marker into a single index. CAR was initially proposed as a simple marker of disease severity in hospitalized medical patients and has subsequently been investigated as a prognostic and diagnostic marker across diverse clinical settings[31–35]. Evidence regarding CAR in pediatric appendicitis, however, remains limited. To date, only one study has specifically evaluated its diagnostic performance for complicated cases. In a single-center cohort of 296 children, Hou et al[24] found that CAR showed an AUC of 0.883 with an optimal cutoff of 1.39 (sensitivity, 86.6%; specificity, 84.6%), outperforming individual biomarkers such as WBC count, CRP, and albumin. The biological rationale for CAR is plausible: CRP increases as a positive acute-phase protein, whereas albumin decreases as a negative acute-phase reactant during systemic inflammation[36,37]. By combining these opposing inflammatory responses, CAR may reflect disease severity more comprehensively than either marker alone, which may explain its favorable discriminative performance for complicated appendicitis. However, the optimal CAR cutoff in the present study was 0.7, which was lower than the cutoff of 1.39 reported by Hou et al[24]. This difference may be attributable to variations in patient characteristics, disease severity, and institutional treatment or referral patterns. Thus, while the present findings support the diagnostic utility of CAR, the cutoff value should be interpreted as cohort-specific and requires external validation before broader clinical application.

Penalized logistic regression approaches, including the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and elastic net, are useful for handling multiple correlated predictors while reducing overfitting through coefficient shrinkage[29]. Ding et al[38] developed a LASSO-based nomogram for predicting complicated pediatric appendicitis and identified neutrophil count, CRP, fibrinogen, and chloride as key predictors; their model achieved 81.7% sensitivity and 82.6% specificity, and outperformed conventional clinical scores. In the present study, elastic-net modeling also consistently selected CRP, albumin, and chloride, supporting the relevance of routinely measured inflammatory and biochemical markers. However, despite stable variable selection, the elastic-net model provided only modest additional discrimination over the simpler CAR index. This finding suggests that, when prediction is based solely on routinely available laboratory parameters, combining multiple biomarkers may offer limited practical gain over a simple composite marker such as CAR. One possible explanation is that many laboratory parameters reflect overlapping aspects of the acute inflammatory response, thereby limiting the additional information gained through multivariable modeling.

Beyond commonly used laboratory markers, several novel biomarkers have been investigated for predicting complicated appendicitis[39,40]. Procalcitonin has gained interest due to its rapid kinetics and relative specificity for bacterial infection, rising within hours of systemic inflammation and peaking earlier than CRP does[41]. A meta-analysis reported that procalcitonin discriminated complicated from uncomplicated appendicitis in children with high accuracy (AUC: 0.956)[39]. Similarly, prealbumin, a negative acute-phase reactant with a short half-life, has been explored as an early indicator of inflammatory and metabolic stress[42]. The CRP-to-prealbumin ratio (CPA) has demonstrated promise as a composite index; in a study of 313 children, Long et al[40] found that CPA achieved excellent discrimination, particularly among older children aged 9–16 years (AUC: 0.919). However, these biomarkers face practical limitations because neither procalcitonin nor prealbumin is routinely included in preoperative testing, and both require additional time and cost. Therefore, despite their promising diagnostic profiles, their clinical utility may be limited compared with widely available indices such as CAR.

This study has some limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and small sample size limit external generalizability; larger, prospective, multicenter studies are needed for validation. Second, the number of enrolled patients over the 10-year study period was limited, raising the possibility of enrollment bias related to institutional referral patterns or case selection. Third, the evaluation was limited to blood parameters routinely obtained at our institution. Assays such as procalcitonin and prealbumin were unavailable, and fibrinogen was excluded from multiple imputation, ROC analyses, and model development because of substantial missing data. Notably, fibrinogen was measured more frequently in patients with complicated appendicitis than in those with uncomplicated appendicitis [44.0% (48/109) vs. 31.7% (63/19), P = 0.04], suggesting potentially non-random missingness. Therefore, the diagnostic and incremental predictive value of these markers could not be evaluated in this cohort. Fourth, variation in the timing of blood sampling relative to surgery, as well as antibiotic administration before hospital presentation, may have influenced preoperative laboratory values and introduced temporal bias. Fifth, although the elastic-net model achieved a discriminative performance that was numerically higher than that of CAR, its AUC was derived from internal nested cross-validation, yielding a more conservative estimate that may better reflect performance in real-world clinical settings. In contrast, the ROC analyses for single and composite biomarkers were conducted using the full cohort, which is known to produce optimistically biased performance estimates, as reported in prior studies. Consequently, the discriminative performance of the elastic-net model is not directly comparable to the more optimistic estimates obtained for the biomarker-based analyses. Moreover, given the relatively small sample size, the stability of model estimates may be limited, and the risk of overfitting cannot be entirely excluded despite the use of cross-validation. Finally, because this study relied solely on laboratory data, clinical findings and ultrasonographic information were not incorporated. Although this design enabled an unbiased comparison of biomarkers, the resulting elastic-net multivariable model requires calculation of multiple weighted coefficients and therefore does not function as a simple bedside tool. When developing future prediction models that integrate physical examination and ultrasonographic findings, constructing them in a simplified, user-friendly format – such as a scoring system – will be essential to ensure practical clinical implementation.

In conclusion, this study provides a head-to-head comparison of routinely available preoperative blood biomarkers and an elastic-net model for predicting complicated appendicitis in children. CAR showed the numerically highest AUC among the evaluated biomarkers, whereas the multivariable model based solely on laboratory parameters provided only modest additional discriminative gain. These findings suggest that CAR may represent a practical balance between diagnostic performance and clinical simplicity for early risk stratification in pediatric emergency settings. Further improvement in prediction may require simple, user-friendly models that integrate laboratory, clinical, and ultrasonographic findings.
