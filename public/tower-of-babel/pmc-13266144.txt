Biomimetic self-assembled nanosoldiers of paotianxiong polysaccharides and α-lipoic acid: Highly specific targeted therapy for mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis
Authors: Qiao Ren, Xiaoxi Liang, Jiaxin Jiang, Xue Wen, Yi Lin, Quan Zhang, Jin Wang, Qinwan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a highly prevalent condition in chronic kidney disease progression, mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (MsPGN) has limited therapeutic options, with the glomerular barrier substantially restricting drug efficacy. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammation, and aberrant cellular proliferation play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of MsPGN. To overcome limitations of conventional drugs and nanocarrier delivery systems in MsPGN treatment, this study proposed an interesting “drug-carrier integration, synergistic targeting' strategy. Our preliminary experiment found that Paotianxiong polysaccharides (PP) has the effect of inhibiting mesangial cells (MCs) proliferation. Novel self-assembling nanoparticles incorporating hydrophilic PP and hydrophobic α-lipoic acid (LA) were developed. This design integrates the ROS scavenging and anti-inflammatory functions of LA with the superiority of PP in inducing MCs apoptosis, thereby synergistically inhibiting the abnormal proliferation of MCs and improving bioavailability simultaneously. For the first time, nanoparticles were coated with LPS/PDGF-BB-activated MC membranes for biomimetic delivery, enabling targeted MsPGN therapy. The prepared nanoparticles (average size: 74 nm) demonstrated significant uptake in MCs. In vitro, they exhibited remarkable capabilities in inhibiting MCs proliferation, scavenging ROS, and reducing inflammation. In vivo, they can achieve the synergistic effect of active and passive targeting and effectively accumulate in glomerular MCs. Through the synergistic effects of regulating oxidative stress, inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization, and suppressing growth factor expression, they can effectively alleviate MsPGN. Furthermore, no significant adverse effects were observed during treatment. Overall, this study constructed a novel self-assembled nanoformulation for MsPGN treatment, provided a new and promising biomimetic nano-delivery strategy targeting glomerular MCs.

Introduction

Mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (MsPGN) is an immune-mediated glomerular disease characterized by intra-glomerular inflammatory cell infiltration, mesangial cells (MCs) proliferation, and mesangial matrix deposition as primary pathological features [1,2]. Hyperactivation and proliferation of MCs, accompanied by excessive extracellular matrix expression, compresses intra-glomerular capillaries causing occlusion and reduced filtration area, ultimately leading to glomerulosclerosis and renal failure [3]. Current clinical therapeutics for MsPGN are limited, typically involving angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) combined with corticosteroids to reduce proteinuria and preserve renal function [4,5]. However, these drugs carry significant side effects and are contraindicated in patients with severe renal impairment. There is some controversy as to whether hormone/immuno suppressant therapy is beneficial [6]. Therefore, discovering new effective therapeutics for MsPGN holds significant clinical implications.

Substantial evidence indicates that aberrant proliferation of MCs triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines, typically accompanied by immune cell infiltration and upregulation of inflammatory factors [1,7]. Furthermore, multiple studies demonstrate that MsPGN involves elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress, which critically contribute to disease pathogenesis by driving MCs proliferation, phenotypic transformation, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and inflammatory responses [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. ROS are closely associated with the signal transduction pathways related to cell mitosis and can promote the proliferation of MCs through multiple pathways. Inhibiting ROS to suppress ERK and attenuate the expression of TGF-β1 can improve MCs proliferation in the glomeruli [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Consequently, scavenging ROS, alleviating inflammation, and further inhibiting MCs proliferation are crucial for ameliorating MsPGN. α-Lipoic acid (LA), a naturally occurring disulfide compound with potent antioxidant properties, is abundant in flaxseeds, chia seeds, hemp seeds, walnuts, and soybeans [16]. LA effectively scavenges ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) by neutralizing free radicals, chelating transition metal ions, and regenerating antioxidants, substantially enhancing systemic antioxidant capacity [17]. Simultaneously, LA mitigates diverse inflammatory diseases by modulating macrophage polarization [[18], [19], [20]] and targeting NF-κB signaling to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine release [21]. Previous studies have confirmed that LA shows good efficacy in various kidney disease models, including diabetic nephropathy, sepsis, ischemia/reperfusion injury, and cisplatin-induced injury [22]. Notably, LA exerts significant therapeutic effects in OX-7 antibody-induced MsPGN through ROS scavenging [12].

With the in-depth exploration of the pathogenesis of MsPGN based on traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theories, TCM-based therapeutic strategies for MsPGN have achieved encouraging advances. Notably, processed Aconiti Lateralis Radix Praeparata and its derived polysaccharides possess potent bioactivities, exerting prominent therapeutic effects on renal injuries and immune-mediated diseases [23]. Studies indicate that polysaccharides from processed Aconiti Lateralis Radix Praeparata significantly alleviate MsPGN by inducing MCs apoptosis and regulating cell cycle arrest [24]. Our previous studies have shown that Paotianxiong (a processed Aconiti Lateralis Radix Praeparata) polysaccharides (PP) possess favorable biological activities and play a significant role in protecting renal function [[25], [26], [27]]. Our latest preliminary experiments revealed that PP can induce apoptosis of activated MCs at appropriate concentrations, thereby inhibiting MCs proliferation. This precisely compensates for the deficiencies of LA, making it highly suitable as both a therapeutic agent and a nano-delivery carrier for the treatment of MsPGN. Moreover, TCM polysaccharides are gaining attention in nanomedicine due to their diverse bioactivities and ideal drug carrier properties: biocompatibility, biodegradability, safety, and facile modification. Since TCM polysaccharides are water-soluble, they can be hydrophobically modified chemically or physically to produce amphiphilic derivatives, which have the structural basis for self-assembling into nanoparticles [28]. Due to the presence of carboxyl groups, the hydrophobic drug α-lipoic acid can be esterified with the hydroxyl groups of hydrophilic PP to obtain an amphiphilic polymer, which can then self-assemble into nanoparticles, enhancing the solubility and bioavailability of the drug and achieving the effect of “drug-carrier integration'. Meanwhile, this polymer is connected by pH-sensitive ester bonds and is more likely to degrade in the slightly acidic environment of the inflamed kidney area, thus achieving pH-responsive drug release [29].

Although the synergistic drug delivery mode of self-assembled nanoparticles composed of LA and PP has many advantages, poor targeting efficiency due to the glomerular barrier still limits its application in glomerulonephritis. Studies report that the glomerular filtration barrier effectively retains nanoparticles >10 nm, making particle size a critical determinant for MCs targeting [30]. The mesangium—a specialized mesenchymal tissue between glomerular capillary loops—consists of MCs and mesangial matrix. It interfaces with capillaries through fenestrated endothelial cells (70∼130 nm) [31]. Choi et al. systematically evaluated renal distribution of gold nanoparticles (25∼180 nm) post-tail vein injection, revealing 75 nm particles achieve optimal MCs targeting [32]. Additionally, the large blood flow in the kidneys and the phagocytic function of MCs create favorable conditions for the entrapment of nanoparticles in the glomeruli and subsequent targeting to MCs [33]. Natural biomimetic drug carriers represent an emerging delivery platform gaining significant research traction. Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles inherit specific functional proteins on the surface of natural biological membranes, thus stably performing functions such as ligand recognition, organ targeting, long-circulation, immune escape, and reducing nanoparticle elimination [34]. Renal tubular cell membrane-coated nanoparticles acquire homologous targeting capabilities through retained membrane proteins, enabling precise drug delivery to renal tubules [35,36]. Cell membrane-based biomimetic nanodelivery systems offer a promising strategy for precise drug delivery to pathological sites [37]. We speculate that MC membranes-modified nanoparticles (a previously unreported strategy) demonstrate exceptional MCs-targeting delivery efficacy due to their homotypic targeting mechanism.

In this study, we developed a simple and effective biomimetic nanoparticle for highly efficient MsPGN therapy. Specifically, hydrophobic LA and hydrophilic macromolecule PP were linked via pH-sensitive ester bonds to self-assemble into PPLA-NPs, enhancing their respective bioavailability and achieving “drug-carrier integration'. By integrating the functions of LA in regulating oxidative stress (ROS scavenging) and anti-inflammation (macrophage modulation) with the superiority of PP in inducing apoptosis of abnormal MCs, the abnormal proliferation of MCs is synergistically inhibited, thereby alleviating MsPGN. To improve the targeting of nanoparticles to glomerular MCs, a “synergistic targeting' strategy was proposed. For the first time, MC membranes were used to modify the nanoparticles to achieve homologous targeting characteristics. Moreover, to further enhance targeting specificity, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and plateletderived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) were used to induce MCs to highly express integrin membrane proteins such as αv and α8 integrins [38,39]. These integrins were commonly employed as targeting ligands for renal-specific and MCs-selective delivery [35,40]. This approach utilizes integrins to specifically target lesion sites by binding to receptors such as fibronectin and laminin within the expanded mesangial matrix under MsPGN pathological conditions [41]. Subsequently, the stellate MCs with potent phagocytic capacity in the mesangial matrix further facilitate cellular internalization through membrane homologous recognition and adhesion.Passive targeting was concurrently achieved by suitable nanoparticle size. Finally, the characteristics, in vitro activity, in vivo targeting, in vivo efficacy, pharmacological mechanism, and safety of the nanoparticles were evaluated through in vitro and in vivo experiments. The results demonstrated that the nanoparticles exhibit excellent glomerular MCs targeting capability, favorable activity profiles in both in vitro and in vivo settings, and satisfactory safety, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for targeted treatment of MsPGN.

Extraction of PP and synthesis and characterization of polymer materials

Extraction of PP

The extraction and purification procedures for PP strictly adhered to the experimental protocol previously published by our research group [25]. Appropriate quantity of coarse-granular processed Aconiti Radix (Paotianxiong) was subjected to triple extraction via 95% ethanol reflux. The extracted solution was discarded. Subsequently, triple extraction was performed using deionized water under reflux conditions. The combined supernatants were concentrated, followed by ethanol precipitation. After removal of starch and proteins, the solution was concentrated and lyophilized to obtain crude polysaccharides from Paotianxiong. Finally, the purified polysaccharides (PP) were obtained through DEAE-52 cellulose column chromatography, dialysis, and lyophilization. Detailed preparation parameters can be found in the supplementary materials.

Synthesis of LA-grafted PP polymer

An appropriate amount of LA was dissolved in 10 mL DMSO, whereupon carboxyl group activation was conducted for 2 h using DCC and DMAP at a molar ratio of LA: DCC: DMAP = 1:1.5:1. Subsequently, 10 mL of a DMSO solution containing PP at a mass ratio of PP: LA = 1:1.2 was added dropwise to the activated mixture. After stirring for 48 h, the product was precipitated with ethanol. Finally, the precipitate was separated, washed, and lyophilized to obtain the PPLA graft polymer.

Characterization of LA-grafted PP polymer

The PP, LA, and PPLA solutions were dissolved in DMSO-d6, respectively. 1H NMR spectra of the samples were acquired using a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker Avance Neo 600 MHz). The PP, LA, and PPLA polymer samples were uniformly mixed with KBr, dried, and pressed into tablets. The infrared absorption spectra in the range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 were recorded using a fourier transform infrared (FTIR, Cary660, Agilent, USA). The XRD patterns of the PP, LA, and PPLA polymers were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer, Bruker, Germany). The samples were exposed to Cu Kα radiation at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA, and scanned at a rate of 5°/min in the diffraction angle 2θ range from 5° to 80°. Thermogravimetric analysis was carried out using differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC, 449F5, Netzsch STA, Germany). Each sample was placed in an alumina crucible with nitrogen as the protective gas and analyzed at a rate of 10 °C/min in the temperature range of 0∼600 °C. A scanning electron microscope and energy dispersive spectrometer (SEM-EDS, HORIBA EMAX mics2, Hitachi, Japan) were used to observe and analyze the microstructure and elemental distribution of the polymers. The grafting degree of the polymers was determined using UV-vis spectrophotometry.

Preparation and characterization of biomimetic cell membrane-coated nanoparticles

Extraction of MC membranes

The activated MC membranes were obtained according to the methods reported in the literature [29,42]. First, the cultured HBZY-1 cells were incubated with LPS (1 μg/mL)/PDGF-BB (20 ng/mL) for 6 h. The activated HBZY-1 cell pellets were collected and resuspended in TM buffer solution (containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors). Then, the samples were treated with an ultrasonic cell disruptor for 5 min under ice-bath conditions. An appropriate amount of sucrose solution (1 mol/L) was added and mixed with the cell solution until the final sucrose concentration reached 0.25 mol/L. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 20,000 g for 30 min to obtain the MC membranes (pellet). The protein content of the cell membranes was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit and stored at - 80 °C for further experiments.

Preparation of PPLA self-assembled nanoparticles

An appropriate amount of PPLA polymer was dissolved in DMSO. An equal volume of pure water was added dropwise under constant stirring. After stirring for 2 h, the mixture was transferred to a dialysis bag (MWCO = 10,000 Da) and dialyzed for 48 h. The self-assembled nanoparticles of PPLA were obtained after filtration through a 0.45 μm filter membrane.

Preparation of activated MC membranes-coated nanoparticles

An appropriate amount of phospholipids was dissolved in ethanol, and nanoparticles were prepared by the thin-film dispersion method. Then, the sample was sonicated with a probe at 30% power for 6 min in an ice bath and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was mixed with the activated MC membranes (1 mg/mL protein concentration), sonicated with a probe at 6% power for 5 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane again to obtain the membrane-coated biomimetic nanoparticles (named M@PPLA-NPs). The PPLA self-assembled nanoparticles without cell membrane modification were named PPLA-NPs.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The morphology of the nanoparticles was characterized using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi HT7800, Japan). The particle size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument (Malvern ZS-90, UK). PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs were stored at 4 °C, and their particle sizes were measured daily for 7 consecutive days to evaluate the stability of the nanoparticles. The serum stability of M@PPLA-NPs was evaluated using 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The protein profiles of cell membranes and M@PPLA-NPs were detected using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by Coomassie blue staining. Western blotting (WB) was used to evaluate the change in the expression level of integrin membrane protein represented by αv integrin in MCs after induction by LPS/PDGF-BB, while simultaneously evaluating the retention of this protein in M@PPLA-NPs. In addition, a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica TSC SP8, DE) was used to conduct cell co-localization experiments. Briefly, cell membranes were labeled with DiD, and then cell membrane-coated C6-labeled nanoparticles were prepared using the labeled membranes. The formulation was co-incubated with cells. Then, the cells were washed, fixed, stained with DAPI, and observed under the CLSM. The in vitro release characteristics of the formulation were studied using the dialysis method. Sample solutions were placed in dialysis bags (MWCO = 10,000 Da), which were then immersed in corresponding release media (pH 7.4 or pH 5.5) and shaken at 37 °C. The release media were collected at different time points to detect the LA content, and the in vitro release rate was calculated.

Cell culture

The HBZY-1 cells were obtained from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, streptomycin (50 U/mL), and penicillin (50 U/mL) in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cellular uptake

An appropriate volume of SD rat blood was centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C and 3500 rpm to separate the serum and leukocyte layer. Erythrocytes were washed by adding isotonic phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 0.25× hypotonic PBS with gentle mixing. After incubation at 4 °C for 20 min, erythrocytes were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min to obtain pale pink erythrocyte membranes [43]. Then, C6-loaded red blood cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (C6-RM@NPs), C6-loaded MC membranes-coated nanoparticles (C6-MM@NPs), and uncoated nanoparticles (C6-NPs) were prepared according to the method described in Section 2.3.3.

HBZY-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and incubated with modeling agents for 6 h. Then, free C6, C6-NPs, C6-RM@NPs, or C6-MM@NPs at a concentration of 500 ng/mL were added respectively and co-incubated for 1 h or 3 h. After removing the medium and washing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI and imaged by CLSM. Flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto Ⅱ, USA) was used to quantify the fluorescence intensity of different formulations.

Furthermore, HBZY-1 cells were used as the experimental object. C6 nanoparticles coated with resting MC membranes (C6-non-activated-M@NPs) or activated MC membranes (C6-activated-M@NPs) at a concentration of 500 ng/mL were added respectively and co-incubated for 1 h or 3 h. Fluorescence microscopy was used for observation, and flow cytometry was employed to quantify the fluorescence intensity of different formulations.

Raw264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 12 h Raw264.7 cells were co-incubated with C6-NPs and C6-MM@NPs (500 ng/mL) for 1 h or 3 h. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI and observed by CLSM. Flow cytometry was used to quantify the fluorescence intensity of different formulations.

Cell proliferation

HBZY-1 cells (5 × 103 cells per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 12 h. Then, PDGF-BB (20 ng/mL) was added to induce cell proliferation. Subsequently, the cells were co-incubated with PP, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs loaded with drugs at different concentrations (2.15, 4.3, 8.6, 17.2 μM) for 24 h. Wells without cells served as the blank group, and blank medium was used as the control group. After 24 h, CCK-8 reagent was added and incubated in the dark for a period of time. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured to calculate the cell survival rate.

Cellular ROS scavenging effect

2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was used as a fluorescent dye to detect the intracellular ROS level. HBZY-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 12 h. In the presence of PDGF-BB (20 ng/mL) and LPS (1 μg/mL), HBZY-1 cells were treated with MPA (0.6 μg/mL), LA (30 μg/mL), PP, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs (4.3 μM) for 24 h. After removing free drugs, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Imaging was performed using CLSM.

In vitro anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities

HBZY-1 cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes and incubated for 12 h to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, the cells were co-incubated with PDGF-BB and LPS together with MPA, LA, PP, PPLA-NPs, or M@PPLA-NPs for 24 h. Cell precipitates were collected, and the expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, SOD, and MDA were measured using commercial assay kits.

Apoptosis of proliferating cells

HBZY-1 cells were treated with PDGF-BB combined individually with MPA, LA, PP, PPLA-NPs, or M@PPLA-NPs for 24 h. Then, the cells were collected, stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI, and the apoptosis of proliferating cells was evaluated by flow cytometry.

Macrophage polarization

Raw264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates pre-coated with cell slides and incubated for 12 h. Macrophages were stimulated with LPS and subsequently treated individually with MPA, LA, PP, PPLA-NPs, or M@PPLA-NPs for 24 h, with untreated blank medium cells as the control group. After treatment, the cells were incubated with CD86 antibody at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI, and macrophage polarization was observed under a fluorescence microscope [44]. Similarly, the above experiments were repeated, and the levels of TNF-α in cell supernatant and iNOS in cell lysate were determined using an ELISA kit.

After euthanizing the mice, pre-cooled PBS was injected into the abdominal cavity for gentle lavage. The collected lavage fluid was centrifuged, and the harvested cells were resuspended and filtered through a cell strainer to remove impurities. The cells were cultured in complete medium for mouse peritoneal macrophages, and suspended cells were eliminated by medium replacement to obtain purified primary peritoneal macrophages (PMPMs). Cells were seeded under identical experimental conditions as RAW264.7 cells. Macrophage M1 polarization was induced by treatment with 100 ng/mL LPS and 50 ng/mL IFN-γ, together with corresponding drug interventions for 24 h. Afterwards, ELISA kits were used to detect TNF-α contents in cell supernatants and iNOS levels in cell lysates, so as to assess the capacity of different agents in modulating macrophage M1 polarization.

In vivo targeting ability

DiD was used as a fluorescent probe to mimic drugs, and DiD-NPs and DiD-M@NPs were prepared according to the method described in 2.3. To track the biodistribution of fluorescent nanoparticles in vivo, MsPGN model rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3) and intravenously injected with free DiD solution, DiD-NPs, and DiD-M@NPs (200 μg/kg), respectively. Subsequently, fluorescence detection was performed at different time points (2, 6, 24 h) using the IVIS SpectrumCT in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, USA). After 24 h, the kidneys were dissected for fluorescence detection. Finally, the data were analyzed using Living Image software. To investigate whether nanoparticles can precisely localize to the glomerular mesangial region, this study examined the co-localization of nanoparticles with glomerular MCs using immunofluorescence. After sectioning the kidney tissue, MCs were stained and labeled with α-SMA antibody [29], and the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Then, imaging analysis was performed under a fluorescence microscope. Finally, semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence was conducted using Image pro plus software.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy

The anti-Thy1.1 nephritis model is currently an ideal model for studying MsPGN. An acute anti-Thy1.1 nephritis model with renal pathological features basically similar to the early lesions of human MsPGN can be established by a single tail vein injection of OX-7 monoclonal antibody [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. On Day 0, male SD rats were injected with OX-7 monoclonal antibody (1 mg/kg, C851, Leinco Technologies, USA) via the caudal vein to induce MsPGN. Thirty-six model rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6): Model group (equal volume PBS); MPA group (30 mg/kg MPA); PP group (150 mg/kg PP); LA group (20 mg/kg LA); PPLA-NPs group (150 mg/kg PPLA-NPs); M@PPLA-NPs group (150 mg/kg M@PPLA-NPs). Drug administration began 2 h post-modeling and was performed once daily. Normal control rats did not undergo model induction. A schematic diagram of the experimental operation is shown in Fig. 6A.

On day 4 of the experiment, rats were placed in metabolic cages for 24 h urine collection. Urinary protein, creatinine, and urea nitrogen levels were measured using commercial assay kits. On day 5, anesthetized rats were subjected to blood collection. Serum urea nitrogen and serum creatinine were measured. Renal tissue samples were homogenized, and levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, SOD, and MDA were determined using respective assay kits. Frozen sections were prepared from partial renal tissues for subsequent immunofluorescence analysis of ROS and 8-OHdG markers. Other kidney samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for further histological analysis. Fixed renal tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to evaluate MCs proliferation. Immunofluorescence assays were performed to assess the expression of target proteins in tissues: α-SMA, PCNA, CD86, CD68, CD80, iNOS, PDGF-BB, and TGF-β1.

Transcriptomic analysis

Kidneys obtained from the normal group, model group, and M@PPLA-NPs group were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, total RNA from the renal cortex was extracted using an RNA extraction kit. Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing analysis was used to evaluate the differences in gene expression among the groups. DESeq2 was used for analysis to identify differentially expressed genes, followed by gene set enrichment analysis. This experiment was commissioned to Sanshu Bio-technology Co., Ltd. For execution. The data analysis was performed on the cloud platform of Sanshu Bio-technology Co., Ltd.

Safety evaluation

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 12 h. Afterwards, the cells were co-cultured with PP, PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs at gradient concentrations of 0.86, 2.15, 4.3 and 8.6 μM for 24 h. Cell-free wells were set as the blank group, and cells cultured with pure medium were defined as the control group. After 24 h of intervention, CCK-8 working solution was added, followed by dark incubation for an appropriate duration. The absorbance value at 450 nm was detected to calculate cell viability, so as to evaluate the cytotoxicity and immunotoxicity of the nanoformulations.

The body weight of animals in each group was recorded daily and plotted to generate weight curves. Meanwhile, the heart, liver, spleen, and lung tissues of animals from the normal, PP, LA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs groups in the experiment were taken for HE staining analysis.

Hemolysis study

Fresh whole rat blood was centrifuged to remove the plasma and leukocyte layers. The remaining red blood cells (RBCs) were washed repeatedly with PBS until the supernatant became clear. The concentrated RBCs were diluted to a 4% suspension. Different concentrations of test formulations were mixed with an equal volume of the RBCs suspension. PBS and ultrapure water served as negative and positive controls, respectively. All samples were incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C for 3 h, followed by centrifugation and supernatant collection. Finally, the absorbance at 545 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate the hemolysis rate.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from independent experiments. Two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01). All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10.41.

Results and discussion

Characterization of LA-grafted PP polymers

For the detailed structural characterization of PP (57.9 kDa), please refer to our previously published papers [25]. PPLA was synthesized via a chemical reaction using DCC as the coupling agent. Briefly, the carboxyl group of LA was activated by DCC/DMAP, and the activated carboxyl group was then introduced into the hydroxyl group of PP. The chemical structure of the final PPLA product was analyzed using 1H NMR and FTIR. In the 1H NMR spectrum of PP, proton peaks were primarily distributed in the range of δ = 3.0-6.0 ppm (e.g., a-5.46, b-5.01, c-4.90, d-4.51). The 1H NMR spectrum of LA exhibited characteristic proton peaks at δ = 1.0-2.0 ppm (e.g., f-1.88, g-1.67, h-1.55, i-1.38), along with a carboxyl proton peak at e−12.00 ppm. In the 1H NMR spectrum of PPLA, diagnostic proton peaks of both PP (a, b, c, d) and LA (f, g, h, i) were observed, while the carboxyl peak at e−12.00 ppm disappeared. The FTIR spectrum is shown in Fig. 1B. The stretching vibration peak of the carbonyl group (C=O) of the carboxyl group in LA appears at 1706 cm−1, which is the characteristic absorption peak of the carbonyl group of free carboxylic acid. Since the carboxyl group undergoes an esterification reaction with the hydroxyl group of PP to form an ester bond, a new C=O stretching vibration peak (1739 cm−1) appears in PPLA, which is the characteristic absorption peak of the carbonyl group in the ester bond. Additionally, an absorption band at 1209 cm−1 was observed, attributed to the asymmetric C-O-C stretching vibration of the newly formed ester bond. These results indicate that the esterification reaction is successful.

The thermal properties of the polymers were investigated using DSC and TGA. As shown in Fig. 1C and D, the LA sample exhibited two intense exothermic processes at 65 °C and 290 °C, indicative of its crystalline nature. The PP sample displayed a distinct exothermic peak at 232 °C, likely attributed to the degradation of polysaccharide branches. In contrast, PPLA primarily showed a melting endotherm at 292 °C without significant exothermic peaks during decomposition, which was similar to the reports in relevant literature [50]. Enhanced thermal stability and reduced exothermicity suggest a more stable molecular structure in PPLA, resulting from the grafting of LA onto the PP polysaccharide backbone. Below 200 °C, minor weight losses in PP and PPLA samples corresponded to water evaporation. Significant weight losses of 62.89% (PP) and 61.79% (PPLA) were observed in the 200-400 °C range, attributable to polymer chain degradation. The weight loss of the LA sample was mainly due to the rapid degradation around 300 °C. Due to LA grafting, PPLA exhibited accelerated degradation kinetics similar to LA at 300 °C. This also indirectly indicated the successful grafting of LA onto PP [51].

The width and intensity of peaks in XRD patterns are typically associated with the physical form of polymers. As shown in Fig. 1E, LA molecules exhibit disordered packing and fail to form crystalline structures due to steric hindrance. The XRD pattern of PP displayed a broad diffraction peak at 2θ = 12∼30°. After grafting with LA, these peaks became narrower and more intense, indicating improved crystallinity of PPLA. Consequently, PPLA exists predominantly in an amorphous state, and this crystalline phase transition facilitates improved drug solubility [49,52].

In addition, to further verify the above-mentioned conclusions, elemental distribution and composition of PPLA were analyzed using SEM-EDS. Apparently, the three elements C, O, and S were uniformly distributed in the polymers PPLA (Fig. 1F), accounting for 61.35%, 33.92%, and 2.19% respectively (Fig. 1G). The homogeneous distribution of S—a characteristic element of LA—provides robust evidence for successful grafting of LA onto PP. Finally, the grafting ratio of LA was calculated to be approximately 12.58% ± 0.82%.

Characterization of biomimetic cell membrane-coated nanoparticles

In MsPGN pathology, Mesangial matrix exhibits significant increases in fibronectin and laminin, which serve as specific receptors for integrin binding [41]. Studies have shown that after MCs are stimulated by LPS and growth factors, the expression of integrins such as αv and α8 in MCs is significantly up-regulated [38,39]. Given the low expression of membrane proteins in resting MCs, HBZY-1 cells were stimulated with LPS/PDGF-BB for 6 h to upregulate integrins expression (Fig. S1). PPLA-NPs were prepared using the dialysis method. To obtain membrane-coated nanoparticles, cell membranes were loaded onto the nanoparticle surface via sonication. TEM was employed to observe the morphology of PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs. Both exhibited spherical structures (Fig. 1H). Additionally, M@PPLA-NPs displayed a distinct core-shell structure with a membrane-like surface morphology. The membrane thickness was approximately 7.5 nm, comparable to the thickness of cell membranes. DLS was used to measure the particle size (Fig. 1I and S2), PDI (Fig. 1J), and zeta potential (Fig. 1K and S2). PPLA-NPs showed a diameter of 62.73 ± 1.30 nm with PDI 22.10% ± 2.19%. M@PPLA-NPs measured 74.27 ± 0.86 nm with PDI 22.73% ± 4.15%. All PDI values were less than 30%, indicating good dispersibility. After coating with the cell membrane, the diameter of PPLA-NPs increased by approximately 11.54 nm, slightly larger than that of uncoated nanoparticles. Zeta potential decreased from −3.98 ± 0.31 mV (PPLA-NPs) to −8.37 ± 0.10 mV (M@PPLA-NPs). These results confirm the successful modification of PPLA-NPs with cell membranes to obtain M@PPLA-NPs. Furthermore, fluorescent nanoparticles were prepared using DiD-labeled MC membranes and C6-labeled nanoparticles. Fluorescence colocalization experiments revealed highly overlapping signals, with yellow merge zones emerging from red (cell membrane) and green (nanoparticles) fluorescence. The overall merged absorption further confirmed the successful coating of nanoparticles with MC membranes (Fig. 1L).

To investigate changes in protein composition post-membrane fusion, protein profiles were analyzed using SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. 1M–M@PPLA-NPs exhibited protein bands identical to cell membranes, while no bands were detected in PPLA-NPs. The successful transfer and retention of membrane proteins on nanoparticles indicates unimpaired protein integrity during fusion. WB experiments further confirmed the presence of αv integrin (a membrane protein) on both MC membranes and M@PPLA-NPs (Fig. 1N). These demonstrate functional preservation of homologous targeting and receptor recognition capabilities in the biomimetic platform. These properties ensure specific targeting of MCs by M@PPLA-NPs.

The storage stability of PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs was evaluated by maintaining them at 4 °C for 7 days. As shown in Fig. 1O and S3A, both particle size and PDI remained stable without significant changes. To assess physiological stability, M@PPLA-NPs were incubated in 10% FBS for 48 h. As illustrated in Fig. S3B, only minor variations in size and PDI were observed during this period, confirming the reliability of M@PPLA-NPs in the blood circulation.

Finally, the pH-responsive drug release behavior of nanoparticles was investigated in vitro. Compared to the neutral pH of healthy tissues, the inflammatory microenvironment in glomeruli exhibits mild acidity (pH ≈ 5.5) [53]. Release media were therefore set at pH 7.4 (simulating physiological conditions) and pH 5.5 (mimicking inflammatory milieu). As shown in Fig. 1P, nanoparticles demonstrated pronounced acid-responsive release kinetics. Cumulative drug release rates at 2 h reached 8.45% ± 1.56% (pH 7.4) and 18.71% ± 2.21% (pH 5.5), respectively. Cumulative drug release rates at 48 h reached 47.69% ± 6.00% (pH 7.4) and 56.55% ± 3.89% (pH 5.5), respectively. This differential release is attributed to hydrolysis of the acid-labile ester bond between LA and PP under acidic conditions. Meanwhile, compared with the LA solution, the nanoparticles showed obvious sustained-release behavior, which is beneficial for the continuous action of the drug at the lesion site. This release mechanism plays an important role in the specific accumulation of nanoparticles in inflamed tissues.

Intracellular uptake of nanoparticles

MCs express adhesion molecules (e.g., αv, α8 integrin) on their membranes [54]. Given that integrins can mediate homotypic interactions between cells [35,55], and α8 integrin typically serves as a modifying molecule for specifically targeting MCs [40], we hypothesized that nanoparticles coated with activated MC membranes could promote their accumulation in MCs. To validate this, cellular uptake experiments were performed using C6-loaded NPs: C6-NPs (uncoated), C6-MM@NPs (mesangial membrane-coated), and C6-RM@NPs (erythrocyte membrane-coated control). The fluorescence images after 1h and 3 h incubation are shown in Fig. 2A. The uptake of nanoparticles was time-dependent. Compared to free C6, nanoparticle formulations significantly increased intracellular fluorescence, indicating improved solubility/stability of hydrophobic compounds. C6-MM@NPs demonstrated markedly stronger fluorescence than C6-NPs and C6-RM@NPs at both time points, confirming membrane-specific targeting. C6-MM@NPs may be internalized into cells through multiple mechanisms, including integrin-mediated endocytosis and homologous membrane fusion [35], resulting in significantly higher cellular uptake efficiency compared to other formulations. Meanwhile, the cellular uptake was further quantitatively confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2B and S4), revealing that HBZY-1 cells exhibited the highest uptake efficiency toward C6-MM@NPs. In addition, to further verify whether the activation of MC membranes affects cellular uptake efficiency, C6-non-activated-M@NPs and C6-activated-M@NPs were applied for cellular uptake assays. As presented in Fig. S5A, the C6-activated-M@NPs group exhibited markedly higher fluorescence intensity at 1 h and 3 h compared with the C6-non-activated-M@NPs group. The cellular uptake efficiency of activated MC membranes-coated nanoparticles by activated MCs was significantly higher than that of resting MC membranes-coated nanoparticles, with an approximate increase of 19.02% at 1 h. Quantitative results from flow cytometry analysis were consistent with the above findings (Fig. S5B and C). This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that activated MC membranes inherit highly expressed adhesion molecules, integrins, chemokine receptors and inflammatory surface markers from activated cells, which enables specific homologous recognition and membrane adhesion with homotypic activated MCs. In contrast, resting MC membranes show low expression levels of related membrane proteins, leading to relatively weak cellular internalization capacity. Collectively, this strengthened cellular internalization can promote the intracellular delivery of macromolecular PP and hydrophobic LA, thereby improving the overall therapeutic efficacy. To evaluate immune evasion capability, macrophage uptake of different C6-labeled nanoparticles was investigated in vitro. In vitro cellular efficacy study

Abnormal proliferation of MCs is one of the most prominent pathological features of MsPGN. In the diseased state, multiple cytokines can promote the malignant proliferation of MCs, among which PDGF-BB is the most significant [56]. Extensive in vitro and in vivo studies have established PDGF-BB as the pivotal mitogen inducing pathological mesangial proliferation in MsPGN [57]. Therefore, PDGF-BB was selected as an inducer of MCs proliferation in vitro to mimic the disease environment of MCs in the glomerular mesangial area during MsPGN. Subsequently, the effect of drugs on MCs proliferation under proliferation-inducing conditions was investigated, and the possible mechanisms of inhibiting MCs proliferation were further explored at the level of cell apoptosis and other aspects. As shown in Fig. 2C, when the drug concentration was in the range of 2.15-17.2 μM, PP exhibited inhibitory effects on MCs proliferation. Interestingly, this effect was not concentration-dependent, which was similar to previously reported literature [58]. When the concentration of PP was 2.15 μM, it had a significant inhibitory effect, with an inhibition rate of 37.41%. Appropriate drug concentration is crucial for regulating the abnormal proliferation of MCs. When self-assembled with LA into nanoparticles (PPLA-NPs), inhibition became concentration-dependent and intensified with increasing concentrations. M@PPLA-NPs showed stronger inhibitory activity than PPLA-NPs (P < 0.05), possibly due to more efficient cellular internalization.

Subsequently, to investigate whether the suppression of MCs proliferation is achieved by influencing the apoptosis of abnormally proliferating MCs, we quantified the degree of apoptosis induced by different drugs. As shown in Fig. 2K, the apoptosis rate of cells in the control group was 5.09 ± 0.26%, and that in the PDGF-BB group was 5.12 ± 0.69%. There was no significant difference compared with the control group, indicating that PDGF-BB has no apoptosis-inducing effect. MPA or free LA treatment resulted in marginally higher apoptosis rates (MPA: 7.49 ± 0.39%, LA: 8.76 ± 0.90%) than controls, but without significant difference. PP-treated cells exhibited an apoptosis rate of 15.05 ± 2.06%—approximately twice that of MPA or LA groups. PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs induced apoptosis rates of 10.14 ± 0.48% and 20.06 ± 1.37%, respectively, with significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that PP at an appropriate concentration can induce apoptosis in abnormally proliferative MCs, thereby suppressing their excessive proliferation, which is consistent with previous studies [24]. M@PPLA-NPs showed superior efficacy due to enhanced cellular internalization. Meanwhile, it makes up for LA's shortcomings in inducing cell apoptosis.

Studies indicate that ROS serve as critical intracellular signaling molecules in mitogen-activated pathways involved in glomerulonephritis pathogenesis. A certain concentration of ROS can promote the proliferation of MCs through multiple pathways. It contributes to glomerular injury not only through direct structural damage but also by inducing ERK/NF-κB activation and MCs proliferation. MsPGN is characterized by elevated ROS and oxidative stress, driving mesangial proliferation, ECM deposition, and inflammatory responses. Inhibiting ROS suppresses ERK activation and attenuates TGF-β1 expression, thereby ameliorating mesangial proliferation [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Given the strong ROS-scavenging activity of LA, we used a ROS fluorescent detection probe to evaluate the ROS-scavenging ability of the formulations on HBZY-1 cells under inflammatory stimulation. To better mimic the inflammation and oxidative stress symptoms of MsPGN, we constructed a cell model by co-induction with PDGF-BB and LPS. As shown in Fig. 2D, model group cells exhibited intense green fluorescence, indicating the generation of a certain amount of ROS. MPA and PP treatment exerted no obvious reduction in ROS fluorescence intensity, suggesting a negligible ROS-scavenging ability, in line with previous literature [59]. LA and PPLA-NPs demonstrated significant ROS-scavenging efficacy, with M@PPLA-NPs exhibiting the strongest suppression of intracellular ROS generation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2I). These results indicate that M@PPLA-NPs inherit the ROS scavenging ability of LA. SOD maintains cellular redox balance by scavenging ROS and can reflect the intracellular antioxidant status. MDA reflects the level of lipid peroxidation. We evaluated the intracellular SOD and MDA levels in HBZY-1 cells treated with different drugs. Evidently, SOD levels slightly increased after treatment with LA and PPLA-NPs, and significantly increased after treatment with M@PPLA-NPs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2G). After treatment with different drugs, MDA levels decreased to varying degrees, with M@PPLA-NPs significantly reducing MDA levels (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2H). These data demonstrate M@PPLA-NPs' superior ability to restore cellular antioxidant defenses. Concurrently, it also compensates for PP's deficiencies in regulating oxidative stress.

Research has shown that in the pathogenesis of most human glomerular diseases (such as IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis), MCs are not only the primary affected cells but also the main participants in the pathological process. Upon injury, quiescent MCs undergo phenotypic transformation into proliferative/secretory states, driving abnormal hyperplasia. Activated MCs secrete inflammatory mediators including PDGF, TGF-β, TNF-α, and IL-1β [57], which promote autocrine/paracrine signaling loops that exacerbate proliferation, activation, and ECM accumulation. Infiltrating immune cells in MsPGN further amplify inflammation through cytokine release and complex cellular crosstalk, accelerating mesangial injury. TNF-α and IL-1β serve as core mediators within the cytokine network, synergistically driving disease development and histopathological remodeling in MsPGN [57]. We investigated the effects of different drugs on cellular inflammatory factors. The results are shown in Fig. 2E and F. The levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly increased in the model group. After treatment with different drugs, the levels of these factors decreased to varying degrees. PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory effects (P < 0.01), providing critical insights into the pharmacodynamic efficacy of M@PPLA-NPs in vivo.

Studies reveal intense macrophage infiltration in glomeruli of MsPGN animal models [1]. In MsPGN, M1 macrophages trigger inflammation-mediated renal damage by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), which facilitate MCs proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition, representing a core pathogenic mechanism. [60,61]. This study elucidates the effects of PP, LA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs on macrophage M1 polarization. As shown in Fig. 2L and S7, LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells exhibited intense CD86 fluorescence, confirming M1 polarization. After treatment with free PP, there was no obvious change in fluorescence. LA exhibits weaker inhibitory activity than M@PPLA-NPs, likely due to the poor bioavailability of free LA. PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs significantly attenuated CD86 fluorescence intensity, demonstrating potent inhibition of M1 polarization. To further verify the regulatory effect of this nanoformulation on macrophage polarization, multiple indicator verification experiments were conducted using RAW264.7 macrophages and PMPMs in this study. TNF-α and iNOS are characteristic markers of M1-type macrophages. Upon activation, M1 macrophages secrete abundant TNF-α to amplify inflammatory responses and highly express iNOS to catalyze nitric oxide production, which further strengthens pro-inflammatory effects. Therefore, detecting the expression levels of these two indicators can accurately evaluate the regulatory efficacy of nanoformulations on M1 macrophage polarization. As presented in Fig. S8A–D, both cell lines in the model group exhibited significantly upregulated expression of TNF-α and iNOS, confirming the successful construction of the M1 polarization model. The MPA exerted a strong inhibitory effect on the expression of both markers and effectively suppressed M1 macrophage polarization. In contrast, single treatment with PP showed no obvious regulatory influence on TNF-α and iNOS expression. Compared with the model group, LA, PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs markedly reduced the levels of TNF-α and iNOS. Among these groups, PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs displayed superior inhibitory effects (P < 0.05), demonstrating that nanoformulations can remarkably inhibit the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype by improving bioavailability. The above results indicated that the core efficacy of this nano-drug delivery system in inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization may be attributed to LA, and its anti-inflammatory effect could be mediated by suppressing M1 macrophage polarization. Current studies indicate that LA can inhibit LPS-induced M1 macrophage polarization via multiple mechanisms, including regulating the level of the immune regulatory factor cyclic adenosine monophosphate, blocking the NF-κB signaling pathway, and remodeling metabolic reprogramming through modulating the glycolysis axis [18,62,63].

Biodistribution study

Adequate accumulation of therapeutics in target tissues is a prerequisite for successful treatment. Although some studies have confirmed the protective effect of LA against MsPGN [12], and previous experiments have demonstrated the inhibitory effect of PP on MCs proliferation, few suitable drug carriers are available for the precise delivery of PP and LA to the glomeruli. To evaluate the in-vivo targeting behavior of the designed nanoparticles, an MsPGN rat model was established to test the targeting ability of different DiD-labeled nanoparticles to the kidneys and MCs. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, free DiD solution exhibited weak fluorescence at 2/6/24h due to rapid metabolism from short half-life and instability. The accumulation of ordinary DiD-NPs in the body was significantly enhanced, with strong fluorescence distribution from 2 h to 24 h, indicating improved in-vivo retention. However, the fluorescence signal of DiD-NPs was mainly distributed in the liver, and the fluorescence signal in the kidneys was always weak, indicating less distribution in the kidneys. In contrast, DiD-M@NPs showed predominant renal distribution, although they were also detected in the liver, which is inevitable in the case of intravenous injection. Quantitative analysis showed that among these groups, the fluorescence level of DiD-M@NPs in the kidneys remained the highest throughout. The results of the fluorescence intensity of the kidney tissues removed after 24 h were consistent with this. DiD-M@NPs showed significantly stronger fluorescence signals in the kidneys than DiD and DiD-NPs (Fig. 3C and D). To further investigate whether the nanoparticles could precisely target MCs, this study examined the co-localization of DiD-labeled nanoparticles with glomerular MCs using immunofluorescence. Kidney tissue sections were stained with α-SMA antibody to label activated MCs [29], followed by fluorescence microscopy imaging. As shown in Fig. 3F and G, green fluorescence represented MCs (α-SMA), red fluorescence indicated DiD nanoparticles, and yellow co-localization signals denoted nanoparticle accumulation within MCs. The above experimental results comprehensively verify the glomerular penetration and mesangial targeting capacity of the nanoformulation. In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that the renal fluorescence intensity increased gradually over time and reached the peak at 6∼24 h, demonstrating the progressive infiltration and prolonged retention of nanoparticles in kidney tissues. In vitro immunofluorescence colocalization analysis indicated that the nanoparticles were distributed inside glomeruli and highly overlapped with MCs, confirming their successful penetration across the glomerular barrier and precise accumulation in the mesangial region. The optimized particle size of approximately 74 nm is critical for efficient glomerular penetration, as nanoparticles with this size can pass through glomerular endothelial fenestrae (70∼130 nm) and be retained in the mesangium rather than being filtered into renal tubules [32]. Moreover, the activated MC membranes-coated nanoparticles bind to upregulated receptors such as fibronectin and laminin in the mesangial matrix under MsPGN pathological conditions via highly expressed integrins, thereby further anchoring nanoparticles within the mesangium [41]. Subsequent membrane homologous recognition and adhesion further promote the internalization of nanoparticles by MCs. Together, these results confirmed that the nanoformulation could efficiently penetrates the glomerulus and targets MCs through size-mediated passive retention and integrin-dependent specific anchoring.

MCs exhibit distinct phenotypic characteristics under different pathophysiological states. α-SMA serves as a marker of MCs activation, signifying transformation from a quiescent to proliferative/secretory phenotype. In quiescent states, ECM homeostasis is maintained with minimal α-SMA expression. During MsPGN progression, α-SMA upregulation accompanies phenotypic switching—proliferating cells secrete excessive ECM components that drive disease via autocrine/paracrine signaling [65]. Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), an auxiliary protein of DNA polymerase, localizes exclusively to nuclei of proliferating cells and serves as a biomarker of cell proliferation [66]. Thus, immunostaining of α- SMA and PCNA can reflect the activation and proliferation of MCs [67]. In normal glomeruli, α-SMA and PCNA staining was largely negative, with only faint signals detected in vascular media. In contrast, MsPGN rats exhibited strongly positive α-SMA and PCNA staining in glomeruli. Among drug-treated animals, α-SMA staining in glomeruli remained positive but was attenuated to varying degrees. Notably, MPA, PP, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs significantly reduced α-SMA fluorescence, with M@PPLA-NPs showing the lowest intensity. PCNA fluorescence was also markedly diminished in all treatment groups. M@PPLA-NPs demonstrated superior efficacy in suppressing MCs activation and proliferation compared to free PP, LA, and PPLA-NPs (Fig. 4G–I). These findings demonstrate M@PPLA-NPs significantly improve renal function while alleviating MCs activation/proliferation in MsPGN.

As a classic glomerulonephritis, oxidative stress and inflammation are pivotal in MsPGN progression. We thus assessed inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress markers in MsPGN rats. TNF-α and IL-1β levels reflect renal inflammation. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, all treatments significantly reduced TNF-α/IL-1β (P < 0.05), with M@PPLA-NPs showing superior reduction versus other groups (P < 0.05). This indicates M@PPLA-NPs effectively alleviate inflammation and prevent cytokine-driven MCs proliferation and activation via autocrine/paracrine signaling. SOD and MDA levels reflect antioxidant system integrity. In the model group, SOD decreased and MDA increased (Fig. 5C and D). MPA and PP had no significant effect on SOD or MDA, while M@PPLA-NPs treatment significantly enhanced SOD activity and reduced MDA levels (P < 0.05), suggesting protection against oxidative stress-induced renal damage. 8-OHdG serves as a key biomarker for oxidative stress-associated DNA damage [68]. Our immunofluorescence results (Fig. 5F and I) revealed differential reductions in 8-OHdG expression across treatment groups. Notably, the M@PPLA-NPs group exhibited significantly attenuated 8-OHdG expression compared to Model, MPA, PP, and LA groups (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. S9, treatment with LA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs reduced renal ROS levels, with M@PPLA-NPs demonstrating the most pronounced effect. Our in vitro results demonstrated M@PPLA-NPs inhibit M1 macrophage polarization, thereby attenuating inflammation. To assess in vivo M1 polarization suppression, immunofluorescence was performed to evaluate macrophage polarization states in MsPGN rat kidneys (Fig. 5E and H). Compared with the normal group, the red fluorescence signal of CD86 in the model-group tissues was stronger. Among treatments, LA moderately reduced M1-related biomarkers, while PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs showed more pronounced effects. Further verification was performed using additional M1 macrophage markers. The immunofluorescence results of CD80 and iNOS were consistent with those of CD86. As shown in Fig. S10A–D, PP exhibited no obvious regulatory effect, while LA reversed the expression levels of M1-related biomarkers to a certain extent. Among all treatment groups, MPA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs exerted more prominent therapeutic effects (P < 0.05). Previous analysis of renal TNF-α and IL-1β levels, two canonical pro-inflammatory markers of M1 macrophages, further validated the immunofluorescence findings. These findings confirm that M@PPLA-NPs can effectively inhibit M1 macrophage polarization to exert anti-inflammatory effects, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of MsPGN. In MsPGN pathology, multiple cytokines drive pathological MCs proliferation—with PDGF-BB being the most direct and predominant mitogen [56,57]. Mesangial matrix deposition primarily involves collagen I/IV and fibronectin-1, largely mediated by TGF-β1. To investigate whether M@PPLA-NPs suppress proliferation via these pathways, glomerular PDGF-BB and TGF-β1 expression was assessed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 5G–J and K). All treatment groups showed attenuated PDGF-BB fluorescence versus model, with PP and M@PPLA-NPs achieving the most pronounced suppression. On the other hand, a large amount of TGF-β1 was expressed in the glomeruli of model animals. While PP failed to significantly reduce TGF-β1, LA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs significantly inhibited its expression—with M@PPLA-NPs demonstrating maximal efficacy. Based on these findings, M@PPLA-NPs suppress mesangial proliferation through two mechanisms: direct inhibition of PDGF-BB expression, reducing mitogenic stimulation; and indirect suppression of TGF-β1, attenuating ECM secretion. Compared to free PP, LA, and unmodified PPLA-NPs, M@PPLA-NPs demonstrate significantly enhanced efficacy in both pathways. This superior therapeutic performance can be attributed to: (1) PPLA-NPs enhancing PP and LA bioavailability via stabilization; (2) Membrane coating enabling targeted delivery to diseased MCs; (3) Synergistic effect of PP and LA.

Transcriptomic mechanism analysis

To deeply explore how M@PPLA-NPs alleviate MsPGN, we conducted transcriptomic analysis on the renal cortices of normal rats, OX-7 antibody-induced MsPGN model rats, and M@PPLA-NPs-treated MsPGN model rats, aiming to reveal the potential molecular pathways affected by the intervention. Using Log2(Fold Change) > 1 and Padj < 0.05 as screening criteria, 129 and 18 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the control group vs. the model group and the model group vs. the treatment group, respectively. Volcano plots revealed the up-regulation and down-regulation of genes in normal rats vs. MsPGN rats and MsPGN rats vs. M@PPLA-NPs-treated rats (Fig. 6A and B). Heatmaps of these DEGs demonstrated significant upregulation of genes associated with inflammation, ROS, and ECM deposition after model induction, while M@PPLA-NPs treatment downregulated some of these genes (Fig. 6C and D). To further investigate the regulatory roles of these genes in MsPGN, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed. The results of GO analysis (Fig. 6E and F) showed that after the establishment of the MsPGN model, the biological processes (BP) in which the up-regulated genes were enriched were mainly related to key pathways such as regulation of cell population proliferation, cell adhesion, response to oxidative stress, regulation of cell-cell adhesion mediated by integrin, inflammatory response, response to growth factor, and response to ROS. For cellular components (CC), these processes mainly occurred in the extracellular matrix, basement membrane, etc. Molecular function (MF) analysis highlighted critical functions such as redox enzyme activity, integrin binding, fibronectin binding, collagen binding, and arachidonic acid metabolism. After treatment with M@PPLA-NPs, the BP in which the down-regulated genes were enriched were mainly related to pathways such as arachidonic acid metabolic process, cellular response to transforming growth factor beta stimulus, cellular response to oxidative stress, regulation of cell population proliferation, and extracellular matrix constituent secretion. For CC, these processes mainly occurred in the cytoplasm and endocytic vesicle membrane. The results of MF analysis included oxidoreductase activity, fibronectin binding, prostaglandin H2 endoperoxidase reductase activity, 17-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (NADP+) activity, monooxygenase activity, etc. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6G–I) demonstrated significant upregulation of pathways including PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, arachidonic acid metabolism, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, and ECM-receptor interaction after MsPGN induction. Upon M@PPLA-NPs treatment, pathways such as chemical carcinogenesis-ROS and arachidonic acid metabolism were significantly downregulated, while the PPAR signaling pathway was markedly upregulated. These findings indicate that MsPGN pathogenesis involves oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and ECM-receptor interactions. M@PPLA-NPs effectively suppressed ROS and arachidonic acid metabolism-associated inflammation in glomeruli while activating the PPAR signaling pathway, thereby exerting therapeutic effects against MsPGN. These are consistent with our previous experimental results.

Safety evaluation

Finally, we evaluated the biological safety of the drugs and nanoparticles by monitoring the body weight changes of the rats and performing HE staining on the heart, liver, spleen, and lung tissues after tail-vein administration. Results showed consistent body weight gain trends across all treatment groups, with no statistically significant differences compared to the control group (P > 0.05) (Fig. 7A). Correspondingly, H&E staining images revealed no detectable inflammation or abnormal pathological changes in organs of rats from PP, LA, PPLA-NPs, and M@PPLA-NPs groups compared to the normal group. The blood compatibility of PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs was evaluated using hemolysis assays. As shown in Fig. 7B and C, a red solution was clearly observed in the ultrapure water group. In contrast, the solution in the PBS group was transparent, indicating that the red blood cells remained intact and were centrifuged to the bottom of the tube. The supernatants of the PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs sample groups with a series of concentration gradients were all transparent. After calculating the hemolysis rate, all PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs groups exhibited hemolysis rates below the 5% threshold, demonstrating excellent blood compatibility and suitability for intravenous administration.

CCK-8 assays were performed to systematically evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity of nanoformulation at gradient concentrations ranging from 0.86 to 8.6 μM against macrophages. This concentration range fully covers the effective doses adopted in functional experiments, enabling an objective assessment of the biosafety of various formulations within the therapeutic concentration window. The results demonstrated that no obvious cytotoxicity was observed in all PP-treated groups at tested concentrations when compared with the control group. Cell viability in the PP group remained approximately 100% across all experimental concentrations. Similarly, the PPLA-NPs and M@PPLA-NPs groups exhibited cell viability higher than 90% within 0.86∼4.3 μM (Fig. S11). These data clearly verified that the as-prepared nanoparticles exhibited no prominent cytotoxicity toward immune cells and possessed favorable in vitro biosafety within the therapeutic dose range.

Overall, these results indicate that this nano-delivery system does not exhibit significant toxicity. M@PPLA-NPs demonstrated favorable biocompatibility and safety in vivo, indicating their potential as a promising renal-targeted drug delivery system for alleviating MsPGN.

Clinical translational potential and limitations

From a clinical perspective, the biomimetic nano-delivery system constructed in this study has good prospects for clinical translation in the treatment of MsPGN. MsPGN is a common chronic glomerular disease in clinical practice, and the existing treatment methods generally have problems such as poor targeting and obvious systemic side effects. All materials used in this system have good clinical compatibility: LA is an approved antioxidant, and PP is derived from TCM with long-term clinical application, both of which have clear biosafety. The preparation conditions of the nanoformulation are mild and easy to produce, meeting the basic requirements of industrialization. Meanwhile, the cell membrane coating biomimetic technology has been widely verified by numerous studies to have multiple advantages. The intravenous administration route is also consistent with the conventional clinical administration route for severe kidney diseases. In addition, this “drug-carrier integration + synergistic targeting' strategy can also be extended to other MCs proliferation-related glomerular diseases such as IgA nephropathy and lupus nephritis.

However, there are still several limitations in the clinical translation of this system: (1) this study is only based on the acute anti-Thy1.1 rat model, which cannot fully simulate the complex course of chronic and recurrent human MsPGN, and the long-term efficacy and safety need to be further verified in chronic progressive models; (2) the synthesis uniformity and batch-to-batch stability of PPLA copolymers need to be improved, the grafting rate and membrane coating efficiency are prone to fluctuations during scale-up production, and industrial scale-up still faces challenges; (3) the quality control standards for membrane proteins of cell membrane-coated nanoparticles have not yet been established, and standardized processes need to be improved to meet production requirements; (4) systematic research on the in vivo pharmacokinetics and long-term accumulation risk of nanoparticles is still lacking; (5) the cost control and storage stability of the formulation still need to be optimized. The above issues will be gradually improved in subsequent studies.

Conclusions

In summary, we proposed a “drug-carrier integration and synergistic targeting” strategy for the targeted treatment of MsPGN. Novel biomimetic M@PPLA-NPs were successfully constructed via the self-assembly of TCM component PP and western medicine LA, followed by surface coating with MC membranes activated by LPS/PDGF-BB. The optimized particle size (≈74 nm) and active targeting property enable the nanoparticles to effectively penetrate the glomerular barrier and accumulate in the mesangial region. These nanoparticles exert synergistic therapeutic effects via multiple pathways, including the regulation of oxidative stress (ROS scavenging), suppression of inflammation (inhibition of M1 macrophage polarization), and induction of abnormal cell apoptosis. Such actions collectively inhibit MCs proliferation and mesangial matrix deposition. Experimental results confirmed that M@PPLA-NPs could specifically accumulate in glomerular MCs, ameliorate pathological manifestations of MsPGN, and maintain satisfactory biosafety. Nevertheless, several limitations still exist in the present study. Firstly, the therapeutic efficacy was only evaluated in an acute anti-Thy1.1 rat model, which may not fully reflect the complexity of chronic or recurrent human MsPGN. Secondly, challenges remain in the synthesis uniformity of polysaccharide-grafted polymers, batch-to-batch consistency and scale-up production, which require further optimization. Thirdly, systematic pharmacokinetic investigation and long-term biosafety evaluation will be the key priorities of our future research. Overall, this study constructed a novel self-assembled nanoformulation for MsPGN treatment, provided a new and promising biomimetic nano-delivery strategy targeting glomerular MCs, and offered new insights into targeted therapy for MsPGN.
