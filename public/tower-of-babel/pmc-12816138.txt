The Distinct Monocyte Subsets and Intercellular Communication in Primary Sjögren's Syndrome Revealed by Single-Cell RNA Sequencing
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Xiangrong Song, Li Fang, Bo Zang, Jiaxiu Li, Yifei Yang, Meng Xu, Yuanwei Han, QiXuan Liu, Hong Zhu, Bin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe presence of unique monocyte subsets and sub-populations plays a significant role in the onset and progression of rheumatic diseases. This study aimed to characterize variations in monocyte subsets and sub-populations and functional roles in patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).MethodMonocyte samples from patients with pSS and healthy controls (HCs) were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). This approach identified divergent gene expression patterns, transcription factors, and immune cells interactions within monocyte subset and sub-populations, highlighting pathways potentially involved in the pathogenesis of pSS.ResultsThe scRNA-seq analysis delineated three major monocyte subsets: classical monocytes (CD14++CD16−), non-classical monocytes (CD14−CD16+), and cDC2, with further identification of sub-populations within the classical and non-classical monocyte subsets. A notable increase in the proportions of classical monocyte sub-population 2 and non-classical monocyte sub-population 2 was observed in pSS patients. Compared to the HCs, pSS patients exhibited enhanced immune cell interactions within monocyte subsets. Furthermore, in pSS patients, the dominant increased pathways within monocytes were those related to viral responses, interferon activity, and oxidative phosphorylation. Additionally, significantly elevated expression levels of IFI44, IFI44L, HBA2, LY6E, XAF1, EPSTI1, APOBEC3A, and IFIT3 were identified in pSS monocytes.ConclusionThis research revealed irregular alterations in monocyte subsets and sub-populations, transcription factors, and gene expression patterns within pSS patients, pinpointing prospective biomarkers in pSS as viable targets for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01974-z.Keywords: Primary Sjögren’s syndrome, Monocyte subsets, Single cell RNA-sequencing, Transcriptomic analyses

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is the second most common systemic autoimmune diseases (AIDs) chiefly affecting middle-aged and elderly women, notably impacting the exocrine glands and potentially every organ system with an estimated prevalence ranging from 0.1 to 5% in the general population [1]. Diagnosing pSS remains challenging due to the absence of specific diagnostic markers [2, 3]. Significant advancements in isolating its diverse aspects, pinpointing potential genes, and delineating related disease pathways provide an opportunity to see pSS. The disease pathobiology involves the activation of the innate immune system, leading to autoimmunity against exocrine tissues and significant dryness [3]. However, the exact mechanisms initiating salivary and ocular dysfunction remain undefined [4], necessitating urgent development of innovative strategies to pinpoint and cultivate potential new therapeutic targets through a deeper understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [5].

Mononuclear phagocytes (MNPs), prominent components of the innate immune system, play pivotal roles in both immunity and autoimmunity dynamics [6]. Monocytes and their derivatives of macrophages, and DCs manifest abnormalities in autoimmune disorders including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and autoimmune neuroinflammation [7–9]. Human monocytes are traditionally classified into distinct subpopulations based on their surface marker expression: classical monocytes (CD14++CD16−), which are considered pro-inflammatory cells, and non-classical monocytes (CD14−CD16+), which exhibit different functional properties. These cells actively engage in the SLE pathology through mechanisms such as the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and fostering B and T cell activation [10, 11]. Recently, an augmented focus has been directed towards understanding the function of monocytes in pSS. Within the cellular spectrum of pSS, these monocytes demonstrate heightened cytokine release, such as IL-6 and BAFF [12], exhibit increased expression of type I IFN-regulated genes [13, 14], diminished IκBα levels [15], and impaired apoptotic cell phagocytosis functions [16]. These cells embody intrinsic immune functions encompassing immunomodulation, cytokine synthesis, and phagocytosis [17]. Yet, the intricate mechanisms of monocytes in pSS remain vaguely defined.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) has become a powerful method for simultaneously examining the cellular composition and distinct transcriptional profiles of immune cells. Currently, insufficient studies have ventured into investigating the transformations in monocyte subsets in pSS patients. To ensure clarity throughout this study, we define “monocyte subsets” as the major classical and non-classical monocyte populations, while “sub-populations” refers to the further subdivisions identified within these major subsets through our analysis. This investigation endeavors to scrutinize the changes in monocyte subsets within pSS patients utilizing scRNA-seq data, delving deeper into the monocle pseudo time trajectory and transcriptomic shifts within monocytes.

Materials and Methods

Patient and Control Participants

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University General Hospital (Ethics Nos. 2018–099, 2020–313) and the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (Ethics Nos. QYFYWZLL29645), with all participants providing written informed consent. Three pSS patients enrolled from the institution’s Department of Rheumatology and were diagnosed as per the 2016 ACR/EULAR pSS criteria [18], alongside three healthy controls (HCs). From each individual, 8 ml of peripheral blood was procured, followed by PBMC isolation through density gradient centrifugation utilizing Ficoll-Hypaque. These cells were then rinsed with cold PBS, enumerated, and preserved on ice for impending analyses.

scRNA-Seq Analyses

Viable single-cell samples with more than 90% vitality were processed using Singleron platform for scRNA-seq library construction, employing the GEXSCOPE™ kit according to the manufacture’s protocol [19]. Preliminary quantification was conducted using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer. Subsequently, individual libraries were adjusted to a concentration of 4 nM, amalgamated, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X with 150 bp paired-end reads.

Quality Control, Dimension-Reduction and Clustering

A stringent filtration procedure was applied, using criteria of over 200 gene counts, fewer than 6000 UMI counts, and mitochondrial gene content below 10% to ensure high cell quality. This process retained 68,137 cells for subsequent analysis. Scanpy v1.8.2 was utilized in Python 3.7 for quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering [20]. The expression matrix for each sample dataset was filtered based on the following criteria: 1) cells with fewer than 200 gene counts or those in the top 2% of gene counts were excluded; 2) cells in the top 2% of UMI counts were excluded; 3) cells with more than 30% mitochondrial content were removed; 4) genes expressed in fewer than 5 cells were excluded. After filtering, 68,137 cells remained for downstream analysis. The raw count matrix was normalized by total counts per cell and log-transformed into a normalized data matrix. The top 2000 variable genes were selected using the ‘seurat’ method. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the scaled variable gene matrix, with the top 20 principal components used for clustering and dimensional reduction. Batch effects between samples were corrected using Harmony. Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter set to 1.2. The resulting clusters were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Cell Classification and Cell Type Annotation

Cell type identification for each cluster was determined according to the expression of canonical markers from the reference database SynEcoSysTM (Singleron Biotechnology). SynEcoSysTM includes collections of canonical cell type markers from CellMakerDB, PanglaoDB, and recent literature, which assisted in accurately classifying the cells [21].

Cell doublets were identified based on the expression patterns of canonical markers. Clusters enriched with markers specific to multiple cell types were excluded from downstream analysis to ensure high-quality data.

Monocytes were initially classified into subsets based on established surface marker expression patterns: classical monocytes (CD14++CD16−, considered pro-inflammatory cells) and non-classical monocytes (CD14−CD16+, exhibiting different functional properties). Within each major monocyte subset, further subpopulations were identified through unsupervised clustering analysis. As defined in the introduction, “monocyte subsets” refer to the main classical and non-classical monocyte populations, while “subpopulations” refer to the further subdivisions within these major subsets.

Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) Analysis

To identify differentially DEGs, we used the scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups() function based on the Wilcoxon rank sum test with default parameters. Genes expressed in more than 10% of the cells in either of the compared groups, and with a fold change >2.0 (log2 fold change >1), were selected as DEGs. The adjusted p value was calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction, with a threshold of 0.05 used to evaluate statistical significance. Only genes meeting the above criteria were considered statistically significant DEGs for downstream analysis.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To explore the prospective roles of monocyte subsets and sub-populations, Genes and Genomes pathway augmentation analysis was performed using Metascape. The R cluster Profiler tool (version 4.0.5) [22, 23] was employed to identify significantly enriched biological processes and pathways within specific monocyte subsets and sub-populations through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. For GSVA pathway enrichment analysis, the average gene expression of each cell type was used as input data.

Cell Developmental Trajectory

Monocyte pseudotime analysis was conducted utilizing the Monocle2 R package, while Cell Phone DB facilitated the intercellular interaction study [24].

Cellular Interaction Analysis

Leveraging the Cell Chat R package, we analyzed cellular interaction networks by calculating potential interaction probabilities based on single-cell expression patterns of recognized ligands and receptors. This process required the conversion of annotated Seurat objects into CellChat-compatible formats. Using the “Identify Over Expressed Genes” function, we identified overexpressed ligands and receptors within specific cell types. Subsequently, gene expression data were mapped onto the protein interaction network using CellChat’s “Project Data” function. To filter interactions where either the ligand or receptor was overexpressed, the “Identify Expressed Interaction” Cell Chat function was applied.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription

Fasting peripheral blood (5 mL) was collected from pSS patients within 24 hours of admission and from healthy controls (HCs) on the day of physical examination. Blood samples were drawn into EDTA-containing vacuum tubes and stored at 4 °C prior to processing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Cat# T9424, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a final elution volume of 20 μL. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer, where the A260/280 ratio serves as an indicator of sample purity since purine and pyrimidine rings in nucleic acids contain conjugated double bonds that strongly absorb UV light at 260 nm, enabling accurate RNA quantification. Only samples with an A260/280 ratio between 1.8–2.1 and RNA concentration exceeding 70 ng/μL were selected for downstream processing. Qualified RNA samples were reverse transcribed using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Cat# A5001, Promega) in a 20 μL reaction volume containing 2 μg of RNA input, with incubation at 42 °C for 60 minutes followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 15 minutes. Extracted RNA was stored at −80 °C, while the resulting cDNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent qPCR analysis.

Real-Time Fluorescence Quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using Platinum SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix-UDG (Cat# 11744500, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions on a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche) platform. Each reaction was performed in a 20 μL volume containing 1 μL of cDNA template, 10 μL of SYBR Green SuperMix, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (final concentration 0.5 μM), and 7.5 μL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds and 60 °C for 1 minute. Melting curve analysis was performed from 60 °C to 95 °C with increments of 0.5 °C to verify the specificity of PCR products. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and negative controls without template were included in each run. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. The relative gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene, and results are expressed as fold change relative to healthy controls. Primer sequences and specifications are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data were represented as mean ± SEM. Unpaired Student’s t test facilitated the comparison between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was utilized for multiple groups. For differential gene expression analysis, genes were considered significantly differentially expressed when meeting both fold change >2.0 and P value <0.05 criteria. A P value below 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Results

scRNA-Seq Analysis Revealed Identification of Blood Monocyte Subsets in pSS Patients

We first performed unsupervised clustering to classify monocytes into three major populations: classical monocytes, non-classical monocytes, and cDC2. As established in our methodology, classical monocytes are considered pro-inflammatory cells, while non-classical monocytes exhibit different functional properties. UMAP visualization (Fig. 1A) revealed that classical monocytes constituted the largest proportion, followed by non-classical monocytes, while cDC2 represented the smallest population. Analysis in Fig. 1B demonstrated that these three monocyte subsets were present in both PSS patients and HCs. To better understand monocyte heterogeneity and its potential role in PSS, classical monocyte subsets were further subdivided into three sub-populations and non-classical monocyte subsets into two sub-populations. Comparative analysis revealed that PSS patients exhibited a significantly higher proportion of classical monocyte sub-population 2 compared to HCs (Fig. 1C). Chi-square analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences in the distribution of both classical and non-classical monocyte sub-populations between PSS patients and HCs (both p < 0.001). Specifically, classical monocyte sub-population 2 was dramatically increased in PSS patients compared to HCs (37.82% vs 5.97%, p < 0.001), representing more than a 6-fold increase. Similarly, non-classical monocyte sub-population 2 showed a marked increase in proportion among PSS patients (Fig. 1D). Non-classical monocyte sub-population 2 was significantly elevated in PSS patients compared to HCs (26.20% vs 12.09%, p < 0.001), representing approximately a 2-fold increase. These findings demonstrated distinct differences in monocyte sub-population composition between PSS patients and HCs. Further transcriptomic analysis uncovered differential gene expression patterns across monocyte subsets and sub-populations between PSS patients and HCs. Specifically, in PSS patients: classical monocyte sub-population 1 characteristically overexpressed IFI6; sub-population 2 showed upregulation of APOBEC3A; and sub-population 3 displayed elevated expression of EPSTI1 (Fig. 1E). Among non-classical monocytes, sub-population 1 specifically overexpressed IFI44L, while sub-population 2 exhibited significant upregulation of HBB (Fig. 1F). These findings provided new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PSS.

Enrichment Analysis of Significantly Different Genes in Monocytes of pSS Patients

Through scRNA-seq transcriptome analysis, some important differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found in monocyte subsets and sub-populations of pSS patients, such as EPSTI1, APOBEC3A, HBA1, HBA2, HBB, IFI44, IFI44L, IFI6, IFIT1, IFIT2, IFIT3, ISG15. Compared to healthy controls (HCs), nine genes demonstrated particularly robust expression changes with high statistical significance: RETN (8.17-fold increase, adjusted P = 8.75E-94), HIF1A (7.39-fold increase, adjusted P = 3.85E-80), MX1 (6.97-fold increase, adjusted P = 1.87E-157), HBA2 (5.30-fold increase, adjusted P = 1.83E-100), BPSTI1 (5.12-fold increase, adjusted P = 2.90E-127), SIGLEC1 (3.18-fold increase, adjusted P = 1.48E-73), RSAD2 (2.75-fold increase, adjusted P = 4.43E-61), PLSCR1 (2.68-fold increase, adjusted P = 3.20E-60), and CD81 (2.07-fold increase, adjusted P = 4.43E-38). All these genes exceeded the 2-fold threshold for biological significance, with 77.8% showing expression changes greater than 2.5-fold (Supplementary Table 1). KEGG enrichment analysis of these significant DEGs identified EBV infection, influenza A, antigen processing and presentation, and oxidative phosphorylation as the major upregulated pathways in monocytes (Fig. 2A-E). Moreover, GSVA enrichment analysis revealed distinct pathway expression patterns across different monocyte sub-populations (Fig. 2F-G). Notably, classical monocyte sub-populations (ClassicalMo_1 and ClassicalMo_2) showed enhanced expression of Myc_Targets_V1 and Fatty_Acid_Metabolism pathways compared to ClassicalMo_3, while non-classical monocyte sub-populations exhibited differential expression of interferon-related pathways. These findings suggest that different monocyte sub-populations may have distinct functional roles, with the expanded ClassicalMo_2 sub-population (which is significantly increased in pSS patients) potentially contributing to pSS pathogenesis through enhanced metabolic and proliferative pathways.

Transcriptomic Changes in Monocytes and Cell-Cell Interactions of pSS PATIENTS

Many significantly differentially expressed transcription factors were identified in monocyte subsets of pSS patients through scRNA-seq transcriptome analysis (Fig. 3A). The heatmap clearly demonstrates upregulation of STAT2, ETV7, IRF2, ZNF189, and STAT1, and reduction of EGR1, HDAC2, STAT5B, JUN and ZBTB7C in pSS monocyte subsets compared to healthy controls. The visualization allows for direct comparison of transcription factor expression patterns between HC and pSS across different monocyte subtypes and cDC2 cells. Among them, STAT1, STAT2, HDAC2 and IRF2 have been shown to play a key role in the pathogenesis of many autoimmune or rheumatic diseases. The cellular interaction networks between pSS patients and HCs were investigated by ligand-receptor pair analysis, revealing unique characteristics of cellular communication in disease states. Our study found that ligand-receptor pair interactions between monocytes and T cells were significantly increased in pSS patients, indicating more frequent and intimate communication between these two cell types (Fig. 3B-C). Further analysis showed that monocytes showed elevated transcript levels for multiple pro-inflammatory factors (such as LTB, TNF, CCL4L2), and exhibited increased mRNA expression of a series of T cell activation factors (including TNFSF10, FAM3C, CD28, SIRPG) (Fig. 3D-E). These results suggest that the upregulated transcripts of these monocytes may contribute to inflammatory responses and immune cell infiltration through potential secretion of pro-inflammatory factors, while possibly enhancing the cytotoxicity of autoreactive T cells through the potential production of T cell activation factors, potentially forming a sustained inflammatory cascade, which might be a key mechanism promoting pSS progression.

Monocyte Pseudo Time Analysis Monocytes of pSS and HCs

To investigate monocyte differentiation and activation pathways potentially altered in pSS, a pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed to delineate the transcriptional changes between pSS and HCs. This analysis aimed to determine whether specific monocyte subsets might reflect different activation or maturation states associated with pSS status, and to uncover disease-specific transcriptional reprogramming during disease progression. Predominantly, states 1, 2, 8, and 9 harbored classical monocytes from pSS patients. Meanwhile, other phases predominantly showcased classical monocytes from both pSS patients and HCs (Fig. 4A-C). The pseudotime trajectory colored by disease group revealed a potential disease-associated progression pattern where certain trajectory regions were predominantly occupied by cells from pSS patients (Fig. 4A). Figure 4B displays the pseudotime progression scores per cell, identifying a temporal ordering of cells across inferred developmental states with a clear pseudotime gradient. Figure 4C assigns cells to 9 distinct pseudotime states based on transcriptional similarity.

Proceeding further, a differential expression study on classical monocytes from states 1 to 9, pinpointing 706 amplified and 948 diminished DEGs were conducted, as documented in supplement data. Notably, genes such as CXCL10, APOBEC3A, HLA-DRA, and MX1 appeared enriched in later pseudotime states, consistent with interferon activation pathways characteristic of pSS pathogenesis. Interestingly, while some of these genes overlap with the commonly identified differentially expressed genes in monocytes, the majority exhibited unique transcriptional changes in classical monocytes from pSS patients. The analysis revealed trajectory-dependent changes in inflammatory and antigen-presentation genes that were more prominent in specific monocyte clusters (e.g., ClassicalMo_2), suggesting that classical monocytes undergo disease-specific transcriptional reprogramming during pSS progression.

During the single-cell pseudo-time analytical process, four critical branch junctions representing cellular transformations were identified such as cell fate diversification, underscoring the paramount importance of branch event analysis. Monocle2 identifies variably expressed genes based on pseudo-time values utilizing the differential GeneTest function, categorizing genes exhibiting analogous trajectories, thereby mitigating the issue. This analysis identified the top 30 differentially expressed genes, grouping them to illustrate alterations within each gene module during the pseudo-time progression. This enabled us to graphically represent the transitional patterns of each gene module throughout the pseudo-time sequence (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, a comprehensive exploration of the transcriptional alterations in highly significant genes were embarked on including APOBEC3A, CD74, DPYD, HLA-DRA, B2M, CXCL10, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DRB1 throughout the pseudo-time continuum, enriching our understanding of their evolving roles (Fig. 4E).

Increased Expression of Key Immune-Related Genes in Monocytes of pSS Patients

Among the significant DEGs, the increased expression of IFI44, IFI44L, HBA2, LY6E, XAF1, EPSTI1, APOBEC3A and IFIT3 in the monocytes of pSS patients was identified (Fig. 1E-F). To further elucidate the transcriptional differences across monocyte subsets, we performed additional differential expression analysis comparing classical and non-classical monocytes between HC and pSS patients. Volcano plots were generated to visualize the differential gene expression patterns between these monocyte subsets (Supplementary Fig. 1). This analysis revealed distinct transcriptional signatures for classical and non-classical monocyte subsets in pSS, demonstrating that different monocyte subpopulations exhibit unique molecular alterations in the disease context. In our study, the expression levels of IFI44, IFI44L, HBA2, LY6E, XAF1, EPSTI1, APOBEC3A and IFIT3 all of which demonstrated significant DEGs in the monocytes of pSS patients were examined (Fig. 5). The volcano plot analysis further confirmed that these interferon-stimulated genes showed particularly pronounced upregulation in classical monocytes from pSS patients, while non-classical monocytes exhibited a partially overlapping but distinct pattern of differential gene expression. These findings suggest that these genes might play crucial roles in the immune dysregulation characteristic of pSS and warrant further investigation as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Discussion

The mechanisms governing monocyte involvement in pSS progression remain incompletely understood. This study investigates aberrant transformations within monocyte subsets and sub-populations of pSS patients through scRNA-seq analysis, highlighting pivotal transcriptomic alterations and differentiation pathways. Notably, two novel subsets, sub-populations, classical monocyte sub-population 2 (enriched with APOBEC3A) and non-classical monocyte sub-population 2 (enriched with IFI6 and MAX1), were significantly elevated in pSS patients. These sub-populations, alongside upregulated pathways such as viral infection, oxidative phosphorylation, and interferon-related responses, underscore critical alterations in monocytes of pSS. Meanwhile, expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as ISG15, MX1, IFI44, and IFI44L was observed markedly increased across monocyte subsets, suggesting their central role in immune dysregulation. Collectively, this study illuminates unprecedented monocyte subset and sub-population changes and identifies key gene markers with diagnostic and therapeutic potential for pSS.

Monocytes and macrophages play pivotal roles in immune modulation, inflammation and tissue repair, which are critical participant of AIDs [25]. While adaptive immune responses, such as the presence of autoantibodies and autoreactive B and T cells, are known contributors to pathogenesis of AIDs, innate immunity also plays an indispensable role [25, 26]. As established in our methodology, monocytes differentiate into classical monocytes (CD14++CD16−), which are considered pro-inflammatory cells, and non-classical monocytes (CD14−CD16+), which exhibit different functional properties [27]. Elevated levels of mature peripheral monocytes (CD14lowCD16+) have been noted in pSS patients [28], though their precise roles remain unclear. In salivary glands of pSS patients, pro-inflammatory CD14brightCD16+ monocytes, characterized by high IL-34 expression, have been implicated in monocyte growth and differentiation [29]. Our findings reveal elevated proportions of classical monocyte sub-population 2 and non-classical monocyte sub-population 2 in the peripheral blood of pSS patients, potentially driving disease onset and activity. Similar alterations in monocyte count and frequency are observed in SSc, RA, and other AIDs [6, 25, 30, 31]. Moreover, monocytes derived from SLE patients have demonstrated a propensity to differentiate into DCs, enhancing autoantigen presentation via IFN-α stimulation [32, 33]. We speculate that increased proportions of classical monocyte sub-population 2 and non-classical monocyte sub-population in pSS may have the potential to amplify inflammatory mediator secretion, monocyte proliferation, and differentiation. These sub-populations may serve as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), potentially facilitating T and B cell activation and immune response initiation, contributing to pathogenesis of pSS.

Recent research highlighted significantly increased pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in pSS patients, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18, CXCL8, and CXCL10, which were linked to heightened disease activity [28, 29, 34, 35]. Additional upregulation of IL-6, IL-18, type I IFN, and BAFF, primarily secreted by monocytes and macrophages, further supported their pathogenic role in pSS [12, 34, 36]. However, it should be noted that elevated transcript levels do not necessarily correlate directly with protein expression and secretion due to post-transcriptional and post-translational regulatory mechanisms. Previous studies have also demonstrated upregulation of IFN-related and type I IFN-inducible genes in monocytes of pSS, correlating with increased disease activity [13, 37, 38]. In our study, elevated expression of genes such as APOBEC3A, IFITM3, LY6E, IFI6, MAX1, and IFI44L in monocytes of pSS patients were revealed by scRNA-seq analysis, implying their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Further experimental validation at the protein level is essential to confirm the functional significance of these transcriptional changes.

Our results identified that expression levels of IFI44, IFI44L, HBA2, LY6E, XAF1, EPSTI1, APOBEC3A, and IFIT3 in the monocytes of pSS patients were significantly increased (Figs. 1E-F and 5). Among these, IFI44 and IFI44L, well-known ISGs, were associated with persistent activation of the type I interferon pathway, a hallmark of pSS immune dysregulation [39–41]. Elevated HBA2 and LY6E indicated that metabolic and antiviral functions in monocytes were enhanced [42, 43], while XAF1, a regulator of apoptosis, might influence immune homeostasis by promoting programmed cell death [44, 45]. Increased EPSTI1 and APOBEC3A emphasized the importance of inflammation and antiviral immunity [46, 47], and the upregulation of IFIT3 manifested its role in inflammatory processes and interferon signaling [48]. These findings exhibited the critical involvement of these genes in monocyte-mediated immune dysregulation in pSS, providing new insights into their contributions to onset and progression of pSS.

Monocytes engage in extensive intercellular interactions with NK cells, pDCs, and neutrophils in pSS, mediated by ligands such as FAM3C_HLA-C, LTB_LTBR, and CCL4L2_VSIR. Based on the transcriptional evidence, these interactions may amplify immune responses and inflammatory cascades, potentially driving pathogenesis of pSS. However, validation of actual protein-protein interactions and functional outcomes would be required to confirm these predictions. Elevated neutrophil activation pathways in monocytes of pSS showed a role for monocyte-neutrophil crosstalk in disease development. Furthermore, construction of a gene regulatory network revealed increased activity of transcription factors such as STAT1 and IRF7 in monocytes of pSS, which may regulate immune pathways and inflammation. These findings underscore the intricate signaling networks underlying pathogenesis of pSS and presenting potential therapeutic targets.

Our pseudo-time analysis conducted using Monocle2 revealed the dynamic expression of genes such as APOBEC3A, CD74, CXCL10, and HLA-DRA during monocyte differentiation. Notably, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DRB1 were highly expressed in the classical monocyte sub-population 2 subset and known to play critical roles in antigen presentation. Previous studies have reported robust correlations between HLA-DRB1 polymorphisms and severity of AIDs [49, 50]. Our findings further support the role of HLA genes in pathogenesis of pSS, offering potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

This study is limited by a small sample size. Larger cohort studies and functional experiments are preferred to validate these findings. Nevertheless, we identified novel monocyte sub-populations and key immune-related genes that may drive disease progression, offering promising diagnostic and therapeutic avenues. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanistic roles of these subsets sub-populations and genes in pathogenesis of pSS, with an emphasis on their clinical significance.
