Operator product expansions of derivative fields in the sine-Gordon model
Alex Karrila and Tuomas Virtanen and Christian Webb

Abstract
. In this article, we initiate the study of operator product expansions (OPEs) for the sine-Gordon model. For simplicity, we focus on the model below the first threshold of collapse ( β < 4 ​ π \beta<4\pi ) and on the singular terms in OPEs of derivative-type fields ∂ φ \partial\varphi and ∂ ¯ ​ φ \bar{\partial}\varphi . We prove that compared to corresponding free field OPEs, the sine-Gordon OPEs develop logarithmic singularities and generate Wick ordered exponentials. Our approach for proving the OPEs relies heavily on Onsager-type inequalities and associated moment bounds for GFF correlation functions involving Wick ordered exponentials of the free field.



1.
Introduction and main results

In this introductory section, we first review the notion of operator product expansions, give an informal definition of the sine-Gordon model (with a precise definition in Section
4
), and then state Theorem
1.1
, which is our main result about operator product expansions for derivative fields in the sine-Gordon model.

1.1.
The operator product expansion

In the theoretical physics literature, the
operator product expansion
(OPE) is a profound tool for studying quantum field theories (QFTs). The OPE was originally introduced by Wilson
[
38
]
. An OPE describes what happens to two local quantum fields evaluated at points that are allowed to merge. It is typically postulated in theoretical physics that for any QFT that has a meaningful continuum definition, for
x
in some neighborhood of
y
, the product of any two local fields
A(x)
and
B(y)
has an expansion of the form

(1.1)

A(x)B(y)\sim\sum_{i}c_{i}(x-y)C_{i}(y),

where
C_{i}
are some further local fields,
c_{i}(x-y)
are
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions that may diverge at zero, and
\sim
indicates that this may be an asymptotic expansion or that the difference of the two sides is regular at
x=y
. The existence of the OPE (
1.1
) can be viewed either as a useful tool for defining possibly more complicated fields
C_{i}
from the possibly simpler fields
A,B
, or then as a fundamental axiom of QFT needed to define interacting QFTs. For an overview of OPEs from the point of view of physics, see e.g.
[
22
,
38
]
.

OPEs have been particularly important in studying
conformal field theories
(CFTs), where OPEs are a fundamental building block of the
conformal bootstrap
approach to CFT – see e.g.
[
11
, Section 6.6.5]
and
[
34
]
. Mathematically, this approach is closely related to that of
vertex operator algebras
, which are an algebraic approach to defining
1+1
dimensional CFTs
[
25
]
.

In the past few decades, a major theme in modern probability theory has been understanding conjectures of physicists about describing scaling limits of critical models of statistical mechanics in terms of CFT. Part of this question is to understand concepts such as OPEs and the conformal bootstrap approach in probabilistic models defined for example as scaling limits of lattice models. While a general, mathematically rigorous solution to such a question seems out of reach currently, there has been progress in some particular models. For example, for the
Liouville model
, the leading order terms for the OPE were studied in
[
5
]
, and the conformal bootstrap approach was justified in
[
19
]
. For the scaling limit of the
critical Ising model
, the leading order terms of the OPE were studied in
[
8
, Section 6]
. The OPE for the scaling limit of the critical Ising model as well as that for the (compactified) massless scalar field played also an important role for proving
bosonization identities
relating Ising correlation functions to free field correlation functions in
[
6
]
. Recently, a lattice-level version of the idea of generating more complex fields from simpler ones via OPEs (in a boundary CFT) was mathematically formalized in
[
27
]
.

1.2.
The sine-Gordon model

The (Euclidean, massless)
sine-Gordon model
is a Euclidean quantum field theory, that physicists might define through a path integral with a measure

(1.2)

\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,\mu}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\mu\int\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi)\mathop{:}}\mathcal{D}\varphi,

where
Z_{\beta,\mu}
is a (possibly infinite) normalization constant known as the partition function,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta>0
, the integrals are over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\mathop{:}\cdot\mathop{:}
denotes
Wick ordering
(we return to a precise definition of this in Section
3
), and
\mathcal{D}\varphi
denotes the non-existent Lebesgue measure on the space of functions from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Before turning to a mathematically precise defintion of this model (which we present in detail in Section
4
for
\beta\in(0,4\pi)
), we mention some of the reasons that the sine-Gordon model is an important model in the study of two-dimensional QFTs.

•

On the level of the partition function and suitable correlation functions, the sine-Gordon model is equivalent to a model of classical statistical mechanics describing (in the grand canonical formalism) a gas of charged particles interacting through the two-dimensional Coulomb potential. See e.g.
[
15
]
for a mathematically rigorous discussion of this.

•

The sine-Gordon model is a prime example of a
near critical model
. It is not a CFT, but a rather mild perturbation of one (namely the free scalar field). In fact, physicists predict that despite losing the structure associated with conformal invariance, the model retains some type of integrability, and certain quantities can be computed exactly. See e.g.
[
30
,
32
]
.

•

The sine-Gordon model is a prime example of an interacting quantum field theory exhibiting a duality known as
bosonization
. The dual theory is a fermionic theory known as the
massive Thirring model
. This was first predicted in the physics literature by Coleman
[
9
]
. For mathematical results of this flavor, see
[
4
,
7
]
.

•

The sine-Gordon model is conjectured to be the scaling limit of a wide class of models of statistical mechanics, such as the
six-vertex model
. See e.g.
[
29
]
for conjectures from the physics literature and
[
31
]
for mathematical work relating the scaling limit of the
dimer model
and the sine-Gordon model.

•

The sine-Gordon model is an interesting model from the point of view of
renormalization
. One expects that the sine-Gordon model is non-trivial only in the regime
\beta\in(0,8\pi)
and that there exists an increasing sequence of thresholds
\beta=\beta_{n}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}=8\pi
such that more and more complicated renormalization (counter terms) is needed to define the model for
\beta\in[\beta_{n},\beta_{n+1})
. We will focus on the case
\beta<\beta_{1}=4\pi
, where Wick ordering is the only renormalization that is needed. See for example
[
10
,
15
,
33
]
for further details on this.

As is typical in a mathematical approach to Euclidean QFT (see e.g.
[
16
]
), the way of making sense of the path integral (
1.2
) physicists propose for the sine-Gordon model is to interpret
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}\mathcal{D}\varphi=\mathbb{P}(d\varphi)
as a centered Gaussian measure with covariance
-\Delta^{-1}
, and try to construct the interacting measure
\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,\mu}}e^{\mu\int\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi)\mathop{:}}\mathbb{P}(d\varphi)
through regularization and renormalization. The usual difficulty here is that the Gaussian measure is ill defined since the Laplacian is not invertible in the full plane. There are various ways to try to circumvent this issue, but our choice will be to focus on the
finite volume
sine-Gordon model. That is, we fix some bounded open set
\Omega
(with a nice boundary), a function
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and try to view the finite volume sine-Gordon model as the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit of the measures

(1.3)

\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,\mu,\psi}(\varepsilon)}e^{\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}x\,\psi(x)\varepsilon^{-\frac{\beta}{4\pi}}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x))}\mathbb{P}(d\varphi_{\varepsilon}),

where
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a regularization of the
Gaussian free field

\Omega
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega

is a normalization constant. We will return to this regularization in detail in Section

\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a continuous function on
\Omega
, so for each
\varepsilon>0
, the measure above is well defined.

Ideally, the way one would study the OPEs (
1.1

As we discuss shortly, we will take a slightly softer approach that avoids some technical difficulties, but still gives precise meaning to the OPE.

Ultimately, one would like to take the

1.3.
The main result

Given the fundamental importance of the concept of the OPE as well as the sine-Gordon model, it is natural to ask what is known about OPEs in the sine-Gordon model. OPEs feature in various way in studies of the sine-Gordon model in the physics literature, but as an example, let us mention the work
[
28
]
, where the OPE for the vertex fields of the sine-Gordon model plays a role in diagonalizing the Hamiltonian operator of the sine-Gordon model (in the Minkowski space/Hilbert space representation of the model). Mathematically there is not much that is known, and the purpose of this article is to initiate the study of OPEs for the sine-Gordon model.

The first question in the study of the OPEs for the sine-Gordon model is how to interpret (
1.1
) for such a probabilistic model. The typical way this is done in the physics and mathematics literature (see
[
6
,
8
,
26
]
) is to view (
1.1
) as holding within an arbitrary correlation function. That is, for
\mathcal{O}
in a suitable class
spectator observables
, we should have

(1.5)

\left\langle A(x)B(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\sim\sum_{i}c_{i}(x-y)\left\langle C_{i}(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)},

where
A,B
are either derivative fields
\partial\varphi,\bar{\partial}\varphi
, or vertex fields
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}
, and
\sim
indicates that the difference between the left and right hand sides has a limit as
x\to y
(or possibly even being an asymptotic expansion). The most natural choice for the class of spectator observables, and the one typically studied in the physics literature, is to take
\mathcal{O}
to be a product of fields evaluated at distinct points, e.g. of the form

\prod_{j=1}^{p}\partial\varphi(u_{j})\prod_{k=1}^{q}\bar{\partial}\varphi(v_{k})\prod_{l=1}^{r}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{l}\varphi(x_{l})}\mathop{:}.

The interpretation is that if (
1.5
) holds for any such
\mathcal{O}
, then (
1.1
) holds.

As we are taking the first step into rigorous analysis of OPEs for the sine-Gordon model, we choose to simplify the question of analyzing of the OPEs in various ways.

•

First of all, we consider the sine-Gordon model only for
\beta<4\pi
. In this regime, the sine-Gordon measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the measure of the GFF, which makes analysis significantly simpler.

•

The second simplification we make is that we focus only on the case where
A
and
B
are derivative fields. It seems that in our approach to the OPEs, the vertex fields are more challenging to control. To be more precise, certain high dimensional integral operators we encounter in Section
3
are more singular for the vertex fields and they are harder to control accurately.

•

Our third simplification is that instead considering spectator observables that are products of fields evaluated at distinct points, we take
\mathcal{O}
to be in a class of random variables that generate the
\sigma
-algebra of the underlying probability space. More precisely, we take
\mathcal{O}
to be an
exponential functional in the field
, namely

\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}

for some
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. At least formally, one could generate all possible correlation functions (
1.4
) from these by taking suitable limits. For example, if one would take
f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\delta_{\varepsilon}(x-x_{j})
, where
\delta_{\varepsilon}
is a suitable approximation of the Dirac delta-function of width
\varepsilon
, then multiplying
\mathcal{O}
by a suitable
\varepsilon
–dependent quantity and taking
\varepsilon\to 0
would yield vertex fields in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit. Derivative fields could in principle be generated by similar limiting arguments and series expanding the exponential for suitable choices of
f
. Controlling such limits in the OPE does not seem particularly simple though.

From the point of probability theory, this is a natural interpreation for our interpretation of the OPE since if for two integrable random variables
X
and
Y
we have
\mathbb{E}(X\mathcal{O})=\mathbb{E}(Y\mathcal{O})
for all
\mathcal{O}
in some class of random variables that generates the full
\sigma
-algebra of the probability space, we have
X=Y
almost surely.

On a technical level, this simplification bypasses the need to control the correlation functions (
1.4
) in general, and again this simplifies the analysis of certain singular integrals we encounter.

•

Finally, we consider only the singular part of the OPE (
1.5
), namely focusing on terms in (
1.5
) where
c_{i}
does not have a limit as
x\to y
, instead of trying to prove an all order expansion.

We expect that with significantly more involved analysis of the integrals appearing in Section
3
, most of these simplifications can be dispensed with, at least for
\beta<4\pi
– the extension to
\beta\geq 4\pi
requiring a slightly different approach. Fully understanding OPEs for the sine-Gordon model (involving also vertex fields, understanding the expansions to all order and all values of
\beta
) would be a very interesting question that we leave for further study.

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
bounded, connected, and open with smooth boundary,
\beta\in(0,4\pi)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
\mathcal{O}
an exponential functional in the field
\varphi
, namely
\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}
with
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the following claims hold.

(1)

For
x,y\in\Omega
distinct, the correlation functions

\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)},\qquad\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)},\qquad\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}

exist and are continuous functions on the set
\{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}:x\neq y\}
. Also, the correlation functions

\left\langle\mathop{:}e^{\pm i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(x)}\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}

exist and are continuous in
x\in\Omega
.

(2)

The following limits exist (and are finite) for
y\in\Omega

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left(\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(x-y)^{2}}\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}-\mu\frac{\beta}{16\pi}\frac{(\bar{x}-\bar{y})}{x-y}\psi(y)\left\langle\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\right),

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left(\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(\bar{x}-\bar{y})^{2}}\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}-\mu\frac{\beta}{16\pi}\frac{(x-y)}{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}\psi(y)\left\langle\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\right),

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left(\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}+\mu\frac{\beta}{8\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}\psi(y)\left\langle\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\right),

where we have written

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\langle\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y)}\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}+\left\langle\mathop{:}e^{-i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y)}\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\right).

Remark 1.2
.

We comment here on a few issues related to Theorem
1.1
.

(1)

This result can be seen as a way of defining a renormalized pointwise product of the derivatives. Note for the GFF, one would only need to Wick order the product, but for the sine-Gordon model, this renormalization is more complicated.

(2)

Closely related correlation functions were recently constructed in
[
14
]
, where the authors prove that after subtracting a suitable
\varepsilon
-dependent deterministic constant (which is divergent in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit), correlation functions involving
\partial_{\mu}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\partial_{\nu}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)
(where
\mu,\nu
index coordinates of the spatial variable
x
) converge in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit. The connection to the OPE is not discussed though.

(3)

It is instructive to compare these OPEs to the corresponding GFF ones. We discuss these in Section
2
, but the upshot is that the singular quantities in the OPEs (
1.6
)–(
1.8
) are new compared to the GFF OPEs. Moreover, the GFF OPEs never generate exponentials from the derivatives. This illustrates how OPEs in near critical models/interacting models can differ from OPEs in CFTs.

(4)

Comparing with (
1.1
), one should interpret for example (
1.6
) as saying that

\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(x-y)^{2}}+\mu\frac{\beta}{16\pi}\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{x-y}\psi(y)\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}+\,\mathrm{regular}.

Perhaps a more thorough justification of this would allow replacing the spectator observables
\mathcal{O}
by all possible fields obtained from OPEs of the gradient and vertex fields, and we expect that the result is true in this generality, but it would require a full understanding of all order OPEs for all possible fields.

(5)

In
[
3
]
, a variant of the sine-Gordon model is studied through a coupling between the GFF and the sine-Gordon field. The authors prove that for
\beta<6\pi
, one can construct a probability space, where the two fields differ by a Hölder-continuous function (continuously differentiable for
\beta<4\pi
). It would be interesting to understand the OPEs discussed here from this coupling perspective as well.

(6)

Using the bosonization results of
[
5
]
, one should be able to study gradient OPEs also for the infinite volume limit of the sine-Gordon model at
\beta=4\pi
.

(7)

It would be interesting to study similar questions for a closely related model known as the sinh-Gordon model, whose definition is similar, but
\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi)\mathop{:}
is replaced by
\mathop{:}\cosh(\gamma\varphi)\mathop{:}
. This model has recently been studied e.g. in
[
2
,
18
,
21
]
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is split into two parts: in Section
4
, we construct the relevant correlation functions, while in Section
5
, we prove the OPE. Our proof is based on proving that the correlation functions are analytic in
\mu

[
20

24
]
), with Taylor coefficients given by suitable GFF correlation functions, which we analyze in Section
3

1.1

2.
A review of the GFF and an analogue of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we review basic properties of the
Gaussian free field
(GFF), with the aim of discussing the analogue of Theorem
1.1
in the setting of the GFF. Much of what we discuss here is well known: see e.g.
[
36
]
for the construction of the GFF as well as
[
6
, Section 2]
, and
[
26
, Lecture 3]
for OPEs for the GFF (with spectator fields that are products of fields at distinct points).

We begin with a brief review of what the GFF is.

2.1.
The Gaussian free field

The two-dimensional Gaussian free field, or the Euclidean massless free scalar field, is a Gaussian process
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose covariance is
(-\Delta)^{-1}
. This of course has some issues. First of all,
\Delta
is not invertible on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To remedy this issue, we will work on a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
‚ and assume that it has a smooth boundary. In this case,
-\Delta_{\Omega}
, the Laplacian with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions, is invertible, and the inverse is an integral operator with kernel
(-\Delta_{\Omega})^{-1}(x,y)=G_{\Omega}(x,y)
, the
Green’s function of the domain
(with zero boundary conditions). For example, if
\Omega=B(0,1)
, then one readily checks that

G_{\Omega}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|1-x\bar{y}|^{-1}.

In general,

(2.1)

G_{\Omega}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x,y),

where
x\mapsto g_{\Omega}(x,y)
is harmonic in
\Omega
,
g_{\Omega}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}
for
x\in\partial\Omega
, and
g_{\Omega}(x,y)=g_{\Omega}(y,x)
.

The second issue we encounter is that
G_{\Omega}(x,x)=\infty
, so
\mathbb{E}(\varphi(x)^{2})=\infty
, which means that
\varphi
cannot be a random function with a Gaussian distribution (what is a Gaussian random variable with infinite variance?). This can be remedied by viewing
\varphi
as a
random generalized function
– e.g. as a random element of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
or a suitable Sobolev space with a negative regularity index. The upshot is that there exists a probability space on which we have a random generalized function
\varphi
such that for any collection
f_{1},...,f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the random variables
\varphi(f_{1}),...,\varphi(f_{n})
are jointly Gaussian, centered, and have covariance

(2.2)

\left\langle\varphi(f_{i})\varphi(f_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}:=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(f_{i})\varphi(f_{j})]=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f_{i}(x)f_{j}(y)G_{\Omega}(x,y).

In what follows, we will often use
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
instead of
\mathbb{E}
for expectation with respect to the probability measure on this probability space. When relevant, we write
\mathbb{P}
for the probability measure on this probability space. For a proof of existence of the GFF, see e.g.
[
36
]
. The reader can also give a quick proof of existence on a suitable space of generalized functions using Minlos’ theorem.

Note that since
\varphi
is a random generalized function, also its derivatives are well defined random generalized functions: for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have for example
\partial\varphi(f):=-\varphi(\partial f)
.

Since we are dealing with jointly Gaussian random variables, we can compute joint moments of random variables of the form
\varphi(f)
(or
\partial\varphi(f)
and
\bar{\partial}\varphi(f)
) by Wick’s theorem (also known as Isserlis’ theorem): for
f_{1},...,f_{p},g_{1},...,g_{q},h_{1},...,h_{r}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\partial\varphi(f_{j})\prod_{k=1}^{q}\bar{\partial}\varphi(g_{k})\prod_{l=1}^{r}\varphi(h_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\Pi\in P_{p+q+r}}\int_{\Omega^{p+q+r}}d^{2(p+q+r)}x\prod_{j=1}^{p}(-\partial f(x_{j}))\prod_{k=p+1}^{p+q}(-\bar{\partial}g(x_{k}))\prod_{l=p+q+1}^{p+q+r}h(x_{l})\prod_{\{a,b\}\in\Pi}G_{\Omega}(x_{a},x_{b}),

where
P_{p+q+r}
denotes the set of pairings of
\{1,...,p+q+r\}
, that is sets of the form
\{\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i
,
\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\cap\{a_{i^{\prime}},b_{i^{\prime}}\}=\emptyset
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
, and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{a_{i},b_{i}\}=\{1,...,p+q+r\}
. Note that
P_{p+q+r}
is empty (and the corresponding moment is zero) if
p+q+r
is odd, and if it is even, then we must have
n=\frac{p+q+r}{2}
.

More relevantly for our analogue of Theorem
1.1
for the GFF, we can compute mixed moments of random variables of the form
\varphi(f)
and
e^{i\varphi(f)}
. This can be done with a standard “complete the square argument”, but for the convenience of the reader, we offer a proof of this in Appendix
A
. Using Lemma
A.1
(applied to the centered jointly Gaussian random variables
\varphi(g),\varphi(h),\varphi(f)
, the polynomial
P(x)=x_{1}x_{2}
, and
z=i
) one finds that for
f,g,h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi(g)\varphi(h)e^{i\varphi(f)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=\left\langle e^{i\varphi(f)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\left\langle(\varphi(g)+i\left\langle\varphi(g)\varphi(f)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}})(\varphi(h)+i\left\langle\varphi(h)\varphi(f)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=\left\langle e^{i\varphi(f)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\bigg(\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}xd^{2}yg(x)h(y)G_{\Omega}(x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x_{1}d^{2}y_{1}f(x_{1})g(y_{1})G_{\Omega}(x_{1},y_{1})\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x_{2}d^{2}y_{2}f(x_{2})h(y_{2})G_{\Omega}(x_{2},y_{2})\bigg).

2.2.
An analogue of Theorem
1.1
for the GFF

In this section, we show the analogue of Theorem
1.1
for the GFF. The main purpose of this is to emphasize that for the sine-Gordon model, the OPE is genuinely different from the GFF one. This also serves the purpose of illustrating the general philosophy of how we define the relevant correlation functions also for the sine-Gordon model later on (though the task is much more involved there). We do however point out that there are other approaches to OPEs for the GFF – see
[
6
,
26
]
.

To formulate the result, we first need to make sense of correlation functions of the form

\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},\qquad\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},

with
\mathcal{O}
an exponential functional in the field:
\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}
with
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Note that
\partial\varphi(x)
and
\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)
are no longer honest random variables –
\partial\varphi
is a random generalized function, so it does not have a pointwise meaning. We define these correlation functions through the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a given exponential functional in the field
\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}
with
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique smooth function on
\{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:x\neq y\}
, which we write as
(x,y)\mapsto\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
, such that for any
g,h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with disjoint supports

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}xd^{2}yg(x)h(y)\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

Similar claims are true for
\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(g)\bar{\partial}\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
and
\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
.

Note that the assumption about
g
and
h
having disjoint supports is important here – apart from the
\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)
-case, these functions will not be continuous on the diagonal
x=y
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that from (
2.4
) (replacing
g
by
-\partial g
and
h
by
-\partial h
and integrating by parts), we have at least formally

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}xd^{2}yg(x)h(y)\bigg(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\partial_{x}G_{\Omega}(u,x)\int_{\Omega}d^{2}vf(v)\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(v,y)\bigg).

This means that our candidate for the correlation function is

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\bigg(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(x,y)-\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\partial_{x}G_{\Omega}(u,x)\int_{\Omega}d^{2}vf(v)\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(v,y)\bigg).

All we need to do to prove the claim is to justify the integration by parts and show that this is a smooth function on the domain in question. Integration by parts for
\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(x,y)
is justified by the disjoint support assumption on the test functions. Also, this is a smooth function on
\{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}:x\neq y\}
.

For the second term, it is sufficient for us to argue that for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the function

x\mapsto\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\partial_{x}G_{\Omega}(u,x)

is smooth on
\Omega
. This follows readily from (
2.1
). In fact, since

\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\nabla_{x}\log|u-x|^{-1}=\int_{\Omega}d^{2}u\nabla f(u)\log|x-u|^{-1},

we see by iterating this identity that

\displaystyle x\mapsto\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\partial_{x}G_{\Omega}(u,x)

is a smooth function on
\Omega
.

The claims for the correlation functions with one or both of the holomorphic derivatives
\partial
replaced by antiholomorphic ones
\bar{\partial}
are proven the same way.
∎

With this fact in hand, we turn to the analogue of Theorem
1.1
for the GFF.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}
with
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the following limits exist

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left(\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(x-y)^{2}}\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left(\left\langle\bar{\partial}\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(\bar{x}-\bar{y})^{2}}\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\right)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to y}\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

Again, we emphasize here the difference with Theorem
1.1
– neither singular terms like
\frac{\bar{x}-\bar{y}}{x-y}
,
\log|x-y|^{-1}
, nor objects like
\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi)\mathop{:}
are present for the GFF.

Proof.

We saw in the proof of Lemma
2.1
that

\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\bigg(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(x,y)-\int_{\Omega}d^{2}uf(u)\partial_{x}G_{\Omega}(u,x)\int_{\Omega}d^{2}vf(v)\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(v,y)\bigg)

and that the terms involving
f
are smooth functions on
\Omega
‚ so they have no singularities. Our task is thus to show that the limit

\lim_{x\to y}\left(\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G_{\Omega}(x,y)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(x-y)^{2}}\right)

exists. This follows from (
2.1
) since

\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{(x-y)^{2}}.

The other claims are similar, though for the cross term one notes that

\partial_{x}\bar{\partial}_{y}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}=0

for
x\neq y
.
∎

3.
Wick ordered exponentials and analysis of their correlation functions

In this section, we make sense of
Wick ordered exponentials
, namely objects of the form
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}
which are needed for our construction of the sine-Gordon model (we define
\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi)\mathop{:}
as the real part of
\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}

\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}
follows

]

3.1.
Wick ordered exponentials

Non-linear operations, such as exponentiation, are not well defined for generalized functions, so we need to construct the Wick ordered exponentials

through

For this purpose, let

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-2}\rho(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
. It follows from
[
24
, Proposition 2.3]
that if we extend
\varphi
as zero outiside of
\Omega
, then
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(the space of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
). This implies that the convolution

3

Recall that for

Thus we can view

Lemma 3.1

We have the following estimates for the covariance of the process

Proof.

(1)

We start by noting that e.g. by

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}z\,d^{2}w\,\rho_{\varepsilon}(z-x)\rho_{\delta}(w-y)G_{\Omega}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}a\,d^{2}b\,\rho(a)\rho(b)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x+\varepsilon a-y-\delta b|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x+\varepsilon a,y+\delta b)\right)

\varepsilon,\delta<\min(\mathrm{dist}(K,\partial\Omega),\frac{|x-y|}{3})

which was the first claim.

(2)

For the variance estimate, we use (

\varepsilon<\mathrm{dist}(K,\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\int_{B(0,1)\times B(0,1)}d^{2}a\,d^{2}b\,\rho(a)\rho(b)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|a-b|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x+\varepsilon a,x+\varepsilon b)\right).

Again by the mean value property and rotational invariance of

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\int_{B(0,1)\times B(0,1)}d^{2}a\,d^{2}b\,\rho(a)\rho(b)\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|a-b|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x,x),

which was the claim.

For notational simplicity, let us assume that

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega\times B(0,1)}d^{2}z\,d^{2}b\,\rho_{\varepsilon}(z-x)\rho(b)\log|z-y-\delta b|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Omega\times B(0,1)}d^{2}z\,d^{2}b\,\rho_{\varepsilon}(z-x)\rho(b)\log|z-y-\delta b|^{-1}+g_{\Omega}(x,y)

is to construct non-linear objects from it via renormalization. For Wick orderered exponentials, the approach is as follows: for

. We also include the

\alpha\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})

(3.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon,\delta\to 0}\left\langle|\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)-\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\delta}}\mathop{:}(f)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=0.

Convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
then follows from completeness of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, and the claim follows from the fact that convergence in
L^{2}
implies convergence in probability.

Let us thus try to show (
3.6
). Since we are dealing with Gaussian processes, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle|\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)-\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\delta}}\mathop{:}(f)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha^{2}c_{\rho}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)\bigg[\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)-\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y))^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}-2\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}\delta^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)-\varphi_{\delta}(y))^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\delta^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle(\varphi_{\delta}(x)-\varphi_{\delta}(y))^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}\bigg].

Note that since
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we can apply Lemma
3.1
with
K=\mathrm{supp}(f)
. First from (
3.2
), we find for
0<\varepsilon,\delta<\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(f),\partial\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}\delta^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)-\varphi_{\delta}(y))^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}\delta^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\delta}(y)^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha^{2}c_{\rho}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}.

We can of course use this estimate in the case
\varepsilon=\delta
as well, so we find for
\varepsilon,\delta<\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(f),\partial\Omega)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left\langle|\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)-\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\delta}}\mathop{:}(f)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left[e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}-2e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}+e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\delta}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}\right].

Let us study the
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
limits of the three integrals appearing here. First of all, we have for
\varepsilon<\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(f),\partial\Omega)
(by (
3.1
) and (
3.3
))

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|x-y|\geq 3\varepsilon}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{|x-y|<3\varepsilon}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|x-y|\geq 3\varepsilon}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}G_{\Omega}(x,y)}+O(\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\pi}}\varepsilon^{2}).

Since
\alpha^{2}<4\pi
, we see from (
2.1
) (say by dominated convergence) that this last integral has a limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
and the error term tends to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}G_{\Omega}(x,y)}.

Of course we also have the same result with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\delta
, so it remains to estimate the cross term in (
3.7
). For this, let us for notational simplicity assume that
\delta\geq\varepsilon
. The argument is essentially identical and we find that as
\delta\to 0

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|x-y|\geq 3\delta}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{|x-y|<3\delta}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\varphi_{\delta}(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|x-y|\geq 3\delta}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}G_{\Omega}(x,y)}+O(\delta^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\pi}}\delta^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}d^{2}x\,d^{2}y\,f(x)f(y)e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(g_{\Omega}(x,x)+g_{\Omega}(y,y))}e^{\alpha^{2}G_{\Omega}(x,y)}+o(1).

Plugging these estimates into (
3.7
), we conclude that (
3.6
) holds, and as argued at the beginning of the proof, this implies the claim.
∎

3.2.
Onsager inequalities

For the sine-Gordon model we will need to make sense of quantities like
\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
. The way we will do this is by proving that they are analytic in
\mu
with Taylor coefficients given by
\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}[\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))]^{k}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
. We will also need to make sense of the normalization constant (or partition function) of the model, which is
\left\langle e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
. This also turns out to be analytic in
\mu
with Taylor coefficients
\left\langle[\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))]^{k}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
. A formal Gaussian computation suggests that one should have for example

\displaystyle\left\langle[\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))]^{k}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=2^{-k}\sum_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{k}\in\{-1,1\}}\int_{\Omega^{k}}d^{2k}x\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\psi(x_{j})e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}g_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{j})}\right)e^{-\beta\sum_{1\leq l<m\leq k}\sigma_{l}\sigma_{m}G_{\Omega}(x_{l},x_{m})}.

To estimate such integrals and justify this approach to the sine-Gordon model, we will make use of so-called
Onsager
or
electrostatic inequalities
which bound the sum appearing in the exponential above. A similar approach was taken already in
[
20
,
24
]
. We offer a proof here since it is simple enough and we give a slightly sharper inequality in the regularized case. While we do not make use of it, we generalize the results of
[
20
,
24
]
slightly and prove the inequality in the case that
\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}
is replaced by an arbitrary
\alpha_{j}\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})
since the proof is identical. The following lemma describes these inequalities.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
K\subset\Omega
be compact,
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})
, and
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in K
.

(1)

There exists a constant
c=c_{K,\rho}
such that for
0<\varepsilon<\mathrm{dist}(K,\partial\Omega)

-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\min_{k\neq j}|x_{k}-x_{j}|}+cn.

(2)

There exists a constant
c=c_{K}
such that

-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}G_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{k})\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\min_{k\neq j}|x_{k}-x_{j}|}+cn.

We mention here that the restriction
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})
is not essential – without this restriction, the constants
c
would simply need to depend on
\max_{i}|\alpha_{i}|
. However, for the corresponding exponential to be integrable, this restriction is important.

We also mention that the main importance here is that in item (1), the constant
c
does not depend on
n
or
\varepsilon
. This will imply that all moments of
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)
are bounded in
\varepsilon
and grow at a controllable rate. Item (2) also implies that the moments of
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}(f)
grow at a controllable rate. This is ultimately what justifies analyticity of the sine-Gordon correlation function as a function of
\mu
in our approach.

Finally, before going into the proof, we mention that as in
[
24
, Proposition 3.9]
, one could at least in item (2) drop the assumption that
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in K
(and simply assume that
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in\Omega
). As we do not need this case and the proof is cosmetically more involved, we do not state the inequality in this setting.

Proof.

(1)

Let us introduce

r_{j}=\min\left(\frac{1}{4}\min_{k\neq j}|x_{j}-x_{k}|,\mathrm{dist}(K,\partial\Omega)\right)

and define the random variables

Z_{j}=\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{j})}(x_{j}).

These are of course centered jointly Gaussian random variables, and we have

\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{k})=\left\langle\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{j})}(x_{j})\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{k})}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

We note that for
j\neq k
, we always have
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\geq 3r_{j}
and
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\geq 3r_{k}
. If we also have
|x_{j}-x_{k}|\geq 3\varepsilon
and since we are assuming that
\varepsilon<\mathrm{dist}(K,\partial\Omega)
, then by (
3.1
)

\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{k})=\left\langle\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{j})}(x_{j})\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{k})}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=G_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{k})=\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

If on the other hand we have
|x_{j}-x_{k}|<3\varepsilon
, then also
r_{j},r_{k}<\varepsilon
, and we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{j}Z_{k})=\left\langle\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{j})}(x_{j})\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{k})}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

We thus have

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l}Z_{l}\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}(Z_{l}^{2})+2\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},

or

\displaystyle-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}(Z_{l}^{2}).

For
\mathbb{E}(Z_{l}^{2})
, we note from (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{l}^{2})=\left\langle\varphi_{\max(\varepsilon,r_{l})}(x_{l})^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\min(\varepsilon^{-1},r_{l}^{-1})+c\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\log r_{l}^{-1}+c.

Note that for each
K\subset\Omega
compact‚ we can find a
c_{K}
such that for
x_{1},...,x_{n}\in K

\log r_{l}^{-1}\leq\log\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq l}|x_{l}-x_{j}|}+c_{K}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}(Z_{l}^{2})\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\min_{m:m\neq l}|x_{l}-x_{m}|}+nC

for a suitable
C
depending only on
K,\rho
.

(2)

This case follows from the first one by taking
\varepsilon\to 0
and using e.g. (
3.1
).

∎

3.3.
Statements of the moment bounds

In this section, we record the main technical estimates we need in this article. These are various bounds and regularity estimates for correlation functions involving
\mathop{:}e^{\pm i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}
. We present the proofs, which rely on a suitable graphical enumeration argument originally due to
[
20
]
, later in Section
3.5
.

Our first estimate is a bound on moments of
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\psi)
that is uniform in
\varepsilon
, as well as a related bound on moments of
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}(f)
. In addition, these estimates control the growth rate of these moments. This result is not new – a variant of it is proven in
[
20
]
, and a more general result is given by
[
24
, Theorem 3.12]
. We provide a proof for it in Section
3.5
as proofs for further estimates we state shortly build on this proof. As the quantity
\max_{i}|\alpha_{i}|
appears repeatedly in what follows, we introduce the following notation for it: for
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, define

(3.8)

\|\alpha\|:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\alpha_{i}|.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
f_{1},...,f_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})
, there exists a constant
C
depending only on
\Omega
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{supp}(f_{i})
,
\max_{i}\|f_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, and
\|\alpha\|
such that for
n\geq 1
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\left\langle\left|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq C^{n}n^{\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}n}.

Moreover, we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\int_{\Omega^{n}}d^{2n}x\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}(x_{j})e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{j})}\right)e^{-\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}G_{\Omega}(x_{k},x_{l})}.

Remark 3.5
.

It will feature in our proof as well, but as we refer to this type of argument elsewhere as well, we emphasize here that (
3.9
) upgrades the convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
provided by Lemma
3.2
to convergence in
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. More precisely, (
3.9
) implies that as a function of
\varepsilon
,
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)
is bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
for any

. In particular (with an application of Markov’s inequality),
|\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)|^{p}
is uniformly integrable for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. Combining this fact with Lemma
3.2
, we can apply Vitali’s convergence theorem to deduce that
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)\to\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi}\mathop{:}(f)
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
.

For our next two results, we need to estimate integrals involving
e^{-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}G_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{k})}

K\subset\Omega

For each

Finally, we need an estimate for a similar integral where two variables are not integrated over.

For each
n\geq 3

\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in(-\sqrt{4\pi},\sqrt{4\pi})

for

Before going into the proofs, we record some of the basic notions and estimates we will need in the proofs.

3.4.
Key notions and estimates needed for the moment bounds

The first step in our proofs will typically be to make use of Onsager’s inequality, Lemma
3.3
, and deal with integrals involving

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\min_{k\neq j}|x_{k}-x_{j}|\right)^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{4\pi}}.

Given
\Omega
‚ we can always find some
R>0
such that
\Omega\subset B(0,R)
, and in the relevant integrals, we will typically make use of this bound.

The minima here make these integrals hard to estimate directly, and the way we deal with this issue is to decompose the integration region in a way that keeps track of the structure of the relative distances of the points. It turns out that this decomposition is most convenient to describe in terms of suitable graphs associated with
nearest neighbor maps
. We now define these maps and review the basic facts about them that we need.

Given a point configuration
x=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in B(0,R)^{n}
, we define a function
F_{x}:\{1,...,n\}\to\{1,...,n\}
by declaring that
F_{x}(i)=j
if
x_{j}
is the closest point to
x_{i}
. If there are several points
x_{j_{1}},...,x_{j_{l}}
at the minimal distance from
x_{i}
, we define
F_{x}(i)
to be the smallest of these indices.

3.4.1.
Nearest neighbor maps and their associated graphs

In our proofs, we will decompose the integration region according to sets where different nearest neighbor mappings are realized, so we will need to sum over the different possibilities. For keeping track of the combinatorics, it is convenient to identify the nearest neighbor mapping with a certain directed graph. This directed graph has as its vertex set
\{1,...,n\}
and it has edges from
i
to
F_{x}(i)
.

Not all directed graphs are possible since the nearest neighbor structure imposes constraints on the graph. To illustrate this, suppose that such a graph contains edges
(i\to j),(j\to k)
,
i\neq k
. This means that
x_{k}
is either strictly closer to
x_{j}
than
x_{i}
, or they are equally close and
k<i
. Now, if the graph contained a loop
(i_{1}\to i_{2}),\ldots,(i_{\ell}\to i_{1})
of
\ell\geq 3
edges then either there is a pair of consecutive edges corresponding to the “strictly closer” case in the loop, or
i_{1}>i_{3}>i_{5}>\ldots
and
i_{2}>i_{4}>\ldots
. Chaining comparisons around the loop, one would obtain either that
x_{i_{2}}
is strictly closer to
x_{i_{1}}
than
x_{i_{2}}
, or
i_{1}>i_{1}
, both a contradiction. On the other hand, by definition, every vertex has out degree one. It follows that one-directional edges of the graph of
F_{x}
form trees rooted at, and directed toward, the vertices of two-loops. See for example Figure
3.1
. We call such graphs labelled directed two-loop rooted forests, and write
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(n)}
for their set.

Conversely, given such a graph
F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(n)}
, there can be at most one nearest neighbor map
F_{x}
corresponding to
F
. In other words, the mapping from nearest-neighbour maps
F_{x}
to labelled directed two-loop-rooted forests is an injection. Since we are looking for an upper bound, it will be fine to upper bound combinatorial factors by summing over all of
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(n)}
. When we want to emphasize the number of connected components in the graph, we write
\mathcal{F}_{2,k}^{(n)}
for the subset of
\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(n)}
consisting of graphs with
k
connected components (equivalently,
k
two-loops). Note that always
k\leq n/2
. For an illustration of such a graph, see Figure
3.1
.

is recorded in the following lemma. For completeness, we provide a proof in Appendix

Lemma 3.8
.

For

3.4.2.
Estimating an integral

After performing the decomposition according to nearest neighbor maps, we will encounter integrals of the form

\int_{U(R)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}},

where

(3.11)

U(R)=\left\{(u_{1},...,u_{l+m})\in B(0,2R)^{l+m}:\sum_{j=1}^{l+m}|u_{j}|^{2}<(2R)^{2}\right\}.

The following lemma records the basic estimate we will need for these integrals. A similar estimate already appears in
[
20
,
24
]
, however, since we state and use the estimate in a slightly different form, we present a proof in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For
\alpha\in(0,4\pi)
and
R>0
, there exist a constant
C=C_{\alpha,R}>0
such that

\int_{U(R)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}}\leq C^{l+m}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+(l+m)(1-\frac{\alpha}{8\pi})-l\frac{\alpha}{8\pi})}.

We now turn to the proofs of the moment bounds we need.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Despite having introduced the main tools, the proof is still slightly lengthy so we split it into steps.

Step 1: Applying the Onsager inequality
. We begin by noting that since for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
|\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f)|\leq\varepsilon^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}c_{\rho}}\int_{\Omega}d^{2}x|f(x)|
(deterministically), it is sufficient to prove the claim for
0<\varepsilon<\mathrm{dist}(\bigcup_{j}\mathrm{supp}(f_{j}),\partial\Omega)
. Moreover, since for any random variable
X
,
\mathbb{E}(|X|)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(|X|^{2})}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\left|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\left(\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\right)^{1/2}

Fubini combined with (
3.2
) and the fact that for centered jointly Gaussian random variables
V_{1},...,V_{n}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{iV_{j}}]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(V_{j}V_{k})}
, shows that

(3.12)

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad=\varepsilon^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2\pi}}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}^{2}c_{\rho}}\int_{\Omega^{2n}}d^{2n}xd^{2n}y\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[f_{j}(x_{j})\overline{f_{j}(y_{j})}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{j})^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{j})^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times e^{-\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}(\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}+\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}})}e^{\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega^{2n}}d^{2n}xd^{2n}y\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[f_{j}(x_{j})\overline{f_{j}(y_{j})}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{j})-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(y_{j},y_{j})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times e^{-\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}(\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}+\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}})}e^{\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(y_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}.

Since the
f_{j}
are compactly supported, we see that
\max_{j}\sup_{x\in\cup_{k}\mathrm{supp}(f_{k})}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(x,x)}
is bounded. Let
R>0
be such that
\Omega\subset B(0,R)
. Then applying Lemma
3.3
(item (1)), renaming
y_{1},...,y_{n}
to
x_{n+1},...,x_{2n}
, and defining
\alpha_{n+j}=-\alpha_{j}
, we find that for some constant
C
depending only on
\beta
,
\|\alpha\|
,
\cup_{j}\mathrm{supp}(f_{j})
and
\max_{i}\|f_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq C^{2n}\int_{B(0,R)^{2n}}d^{4n}xe^{\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}\log\frac{1}{\min_{j:j\neq k}|x_{j}-x_{k}|}}.

By rescaling the integration variables, we find for some other constant
\widetilde{C}
(still depending only on the allowed quantities)

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\widetilde{C}^{2n}\int_{B(0,1)^{2n}}d^{4n}xe^{\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}\log\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\min_{j:j\neq k}|x_{j}-x_{k}|}}.

Introducing the factor
\frac{1}{2}
inside the logarithm, at the price of changing
\widetilde{C}
slightly, will turn out to be convenient later on.

Step 2: Using nearest neighbor maps for a change of variables
. We will now start using the tools from Section
3.4
. Given a graph
F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(2n)}
, we define a set
U_{F}\subset B(0,1)^{2n}
by the condition
U_{F}=\{(x_{1},...,x_{2n})\in B(0,1)^{2n}:F_{x}=F\}
. We thus find

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}^{2n}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}^{(2n)}}\int_{U_{F}}d^{4n}x\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left(\frac{1}{2}|x_{j}-x_{F(j)}|\right)^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{C}^{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{2,k}^{(2n)}}\int_{U_{F}}d^{4n}x\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left(\frac{1}{2}|x_{j}-x_{F(j)}|\right)^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}}

To estimate the integral, we perform a change of variables that depends on the structure of the graph
F
. By renaming the integration variables, we can assume that
\{1,2\},\{3,4\},...,\{2k-1,2k\}
are the vertices of the two-loops. We define for
1\leq j\leq k
,
u_{2j-1}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{2j-1}-x_{2j})
and
u_{2j}=\frac{1}{2}x_{2j}
. For the remaining vertices (
i>2k
), we define
u_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{i}-x_{F(i)})
. Note that this linear change of variables is a bijection, since we can solve each of the variables
x_{i}
in terms of the
u
-variables recursively by “climbing up a tree”.

If we write
\widetilde{U}_{F}
for the image of
U_{F}
under this change of variables, and note that the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant of the change of variables is
4^{2n}
(the linear change of variables is upper triangular in the basis corresponding to the order of the tree), we find that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{U_{F}}d^{4n}x\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left(\frac{1}{2}|x_{j}-x_{F(j)}|\right)^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}}=4^{2n}\int_{\widetilde{U}_{F}}d^{4n}u\prod_{j=1}^{k}|u_{2j-1}|^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2\pi}}\prod_{j=2k+1}^{2n}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}}.

Step 3: Modifying the integration region
. Apart from the integration region, and number of integration variables, this is of the form of Lemma
3.9
. We now show that we can enlarge the integration region to be of suitable form. For this purpose, note first of all that
|u_{j}|<1
for all
j
since
x_{j}\in B(0,1)
for all
j
. Moreover, we point out that the balls
B_{j}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|y-x_{j}|<|u_{j}|\}
, with
j=1,3,...,2k-1,2k+1,2k+2,...,2n
are disjoint. If they were not disjoint, there would be a point
y
such that
|y-x_{j}|<|u_{j}|
and
|y-x_{j^{\prime}}|<|u_{j^{\prime}}|
for some
j\neq j^{\prime}
. This would mean that
|x_{j}-x_{j^{\prime}}|<2\max(|u_{j}|,|u_{j^{\prime}}|)
. Thus for one of the points
x_{j},x_{j^{\prime}}
, the other point would be closer than its nearest neighbor, contradicting the definition of the nearest neighbor, so we see that the balls are disjoint.

Note that
B_{j}\subset B(0,2)
for all
j
(since
|x_{j}|<1
and
|u_{j}|<1
). As the balls are disjoint, this means the sum of the areas of the balls
B_{j}
must be less than the area of
B(0,2)
, or in other words,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}|u_{2j-1}|^{2}+\sum_{j=2k+1}^{2n}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq 4.

We conclude that

\widetilde{U}_{F}\subset\left\{u\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{2n}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}|u_{2j-1}|^{2}+\sum_{j=2k+1}^{2n}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq 4,|u_{2}|,|u_{4}|,...,|u_{2k}|\leq 1\right\}.

Integrating out the variables
u_{2},u_{4},...,u_{2k}
, we recover an integral of the form studied in Lemma
3.9
(recall (
3.11
) for the notation
U(1)
):

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C^{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{2,k}^{(2n)}}\int_{U(1)}d^{2(2n-k)}v\prod_{j=1}^{k}|v_{j}|^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{2\pi}}\prod_{j=k+1}^{2n-k}|v_{j}|^{-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}},

where once again,
C
is a constant depending only on the allowed parameters.

Step 4: Concluding the proof of (
3.9
)
. Applying Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
3.9
, we conclude that there exists a
C
depending only on the allowed parameters such that uniformly in
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(2n-k)!}{\Gamma(1+(2n-k)(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi})-k\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi})}

\displaystyle=C^{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(2n-k)!}{\Gamma(1+2n(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi})-k)}.

By Stirling’s approximation for the Gamma function, uniformly in
k\in\{1,...,n\}
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{(2n-k)!}{\Gamma(1+2n(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi})-k)}

\displaystyle=(2n-k)\log(2n-k)-(2n-k)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(1+2n\left(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}\right)-k\right)\log\left(1+2n\left(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}\right)-k\right)

\displaystyle\quad+1+2n\left(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}\right)-k+O(\log n)

\displaystyle=(2n-k)\log n-\left(2n\left(1-\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}\right)-k\right)\log n+O(n)

\displaystyle=n\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}\log n+O(n),

so by noting that the sum produces a factor of
n
, which we can get rid of by modifying
C
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{-i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\overline{f_{j}})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq C^{2n}n^{\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}n}

and

\displaystyle\left\langle\left|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq C^{n}n^{\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{8\pi}n}

for some constant
C
depending only on the allowed parameters. This concludes the proof of (
3.9
).

Completing the proof of (
3.10
).
Recall from Remark
3.5
that (
3.9
) implies that
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\to\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi}\mathop{:}(f_{j})
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
for each
p\in[1,\infty)
. In particular, for each
j
,
\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\in L^{p}(\mathbb{P})
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. By a repeated application of Cauchy-Schwarz, this implies that the expectation in (
3.10
) is finite and

\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

With a similar argument as in (
3.12
), we find that for small enough
\varepsilon

\displaystyle\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(f_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{n}}d^{2n}x\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(f_{j}(x_{j})e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(x_{j},x_{j})}\right)e^{-\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}.

We see that Lemma
3.1
implies that the covariances
\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
converge to
G_{\Omega}(x_{k},x_{l})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which means that if we could take the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit inside of the integral, we would have proven (
3.10
). This we can do with the dominated convergence theorem and Lemma
3.3
: we have

\displaystyle e^{-\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}\left\langle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k})\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x_{l})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}\leq e^{cn}e^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\|\alpha\|^{2}}{4\pi}\log\frac{1}{\min_{l:l\neq k}|x_{k}-x_{l}|}}.

Repeating the proof of (
3.9
), we see this to be integrable. This concludes the proof of (
3.10
).
∎

4.
The sine-Gordon model and its correlation functions

In this section, we define the sine-Gordon model and its correlation functions that we analyze for the OPEs.

4.1.
The sine-Gordon model

Recall from Section
1
that we would like to define our model as a probability measure (on a space of generalized functions) which is absolute continuous with respect to the law of the GFF: for
\beta\in(0,4\pi)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
(real valued),

(4.1)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}(d\varphi)=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}}e^{\mu\int_{\Omega}d^{2}x\psi(x)\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(x))\mathop{:}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{GFF}}(d\varphi),

where

Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}=\left\langle e^{\mu\int_{\Omega}d^{2}x\psi(x)\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(x))\mathop{:}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

Having made sense of the Wick ordered exponentials, we see that the natural interpretation for a rigorous definition of the cosine term in (
4.1
) (for real valued
\psi
) is

\int_{\Omega}d^{2}x\psi(x)\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(x))\mathop{:}=\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)).

This suggests trying to define the measure (
4.1
) rigorously as

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}(d\varphi)=\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}}e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{GFF}}(d\varphi),

where

Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}=\left\langle e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

This definition requires of course that
Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}
is finite (and non-zero). This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For each
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta\in(0,4\pi)
and
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}\in(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Note that it is sufficient to prove that

\left\langle e^{\mu|\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))|}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}<\infty

for each
\mu>0
. For this in turn, it is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\left\langle e^{\mu|\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)|}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}<\infty.

Using Fubini and Proposition
3.4
,
6
6

6

To be precise, we use Vitali’s convergence theorem to extend the bounds of Proposition
3.4
to
\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)
.
we see that

\displaystyle\left\langle e^{\mu|\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)|}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{n}}{n!}C^{n}n^{\frac{\beta}{8\pi}n},

for some
C
depending only on
\beta
and
\psi
. For example, by Stirling’s approximation, this series converges for any
\mu>0
so we are done.
∎

We have thus constructed the sine-Gordon model for
\beta<4\pi
, and for example for
\mathcal{O}=e^{i\varphi(f)}
with

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}=\frac{\left\langle e^{i\varphi(f)}e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}{\left\langle e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}}

Note that with a little bit of effort (using Lemma

where
Z_{\mathrm{sG}(\beta,\mu,\psi)}(\varepsilon)=\left\langle e^{\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
,
as suggested in Section

4.2.
The relevant smeared correlation functions for the sine-Gordon model

We will shortly need integral kernels for these correlation functions. We will express these through a series expansion in

Lemma 4.2
.

The functions

(4.2)

\displaystyle Z_{\mu}(g,h)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}\left\langle\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},

(4.4)

\displaystyle Z_{\mu}(\eta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}\left\langle\mathop{:}e^{\pm i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\eta)\mathcal{O}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}},

and

(4.5)

\displaystyle Z_{\mu}(\mathcal{O})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\sum_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}\left\langle\mathcal{O}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\partial

\bar{\partial}

Proof.

Let us first give a heuristic argument and then justify why the steps we take are allowed. Formally, if we series expand the exponential in (
4.2
) and swap the order of the expectation and summation, we find that
Z_{\mu}(g,h)
would be given by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{n}}{n!}\left\langle\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))\right)^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}.

This is almost of the form claimed in the statement of the lemma. To massage it into the required form, note that for example from Lemma
3.2
one can show that for real valued
\psi
,
\overline{\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)}=\mathop{:}e^{-i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)
, so

\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)+\mathop{:}e^{-i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi).

Thus

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{Re}\,\left(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right)\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n}\in\{-1,1\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi),

which suggests that
Z_{\mu}(g,h)
should be given by the series expansion (
4.3
) as claimed.

The only step in this argument that was not properly justified was interchanging the order of expectation and the series expansion. For this we naturally want to use Fubini. It is thus sufficient to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu|^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\left\langle\left|\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(h)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{:}e^{i\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}<\infty.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz (and the fact that
\left\langle|\partial\varphi(g)\partial\varphi(f)|^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}<\infty
since we are dealing with Gaussian random variables) and our remark that
\overline{\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)}=\mathop{:}e^{-i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)
, we see that this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu|^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\left(\left\langle\left|\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right|^{2n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Due to our uniform integrability and

convergence (see Remark
3.5
), we can use Proposition
3.4
to bound

\left(\left\langle\left|\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi)\right|^{2n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{\psi,\beta}n^{\frac{\beta}{8\pi}n}

so the question is now convergence of the series

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu|^{n}}{2^{n}n!}n^{\frac{\beta}{8\pi}n}.

By Stirling’s approximation, this would be convergent for every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
even for
\beta<8\pi
, so it certainly converges for
\beta<4\pi
. Thus our formal argument is justified and we have derived the series expansion (
4.3
).

The proofs of (

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\mathcal{O}e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

\displaystyle+\mu\frac{\beta}{8\pi}\log|x-y|^{-1}\psi(y)\left\langle\mathop{:}\cos(\sqrt{\beta}\varphi(y))\mathop{:}\mathcal{O}e^{\mu\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathop{:}e^{i\sqrt{\beta}\varphi}\mathop{:}(\psi))}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}

has a limit as
x\to y
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of Proposition
5.1
. The first difference is that
\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\partial\varphi(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
gets replaced by
\left\langle\partial\varphi(x)\bar{\partial}\varphi(y)\right\rangle_{\mathrm{GFF}}
. This is continuous as
x\to y
(since it is just
\partial_{x}\bar{\partial}_{y}g_{\Omega}(x,y)
), so it can be absorbed into the part that has a limit as
x\to y
.

The analysis of the terms corresponding to
I_{n,1},I_{n,2},I_{n,3},I_{n,4}^{1}
is completely analogous – we only used size bounds for showing that these produce continuous functions. For
I_{n,4}^{2}
, one can again (with completely analogous arguments) reduce the question to understanding the contributing asymptotics of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{B(0,\delta)}d^{2}u\frac{1}{u-(x-y)}\frac{1}{\bar{u}}.

Using the same series expansions, we see that now the only non-zero contribution comes from

\int_{|x-y|<|u|<\delta}d^{2}u\frac{1}{|u|^{2}}=2\pi(\log|x-y|^{-1}-\log\delta^{-1}).

After this, the proof continues exactly as in Proposition
5.1
, and we recover the claim.
∎

Again, by dividing by the normalization constant, we recover (
1.8
). This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Appendix A
A tool for computing Gaussian expectations

We review in this appendix a simple version of Girsanov’s theorem for a finite dimensional Gaussian distribution which we use repeatedly.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
V_{1},...,V_{n}
be centered, real-valued, and jointly Gaussian random variables with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Moreover, let
P:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be a polynomial. We then have for any
z\in\mathbb{C}

(A.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[P(V_{1},...,V_{n})e^{zV_{1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zV_{1}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[P\left(V_{1}+z\mathbb{E}(V_{1}^{2}),...,V_{n}+z\mathbb{E}(V_{1}V_{n})\right)\right]

Proof.

First of all, since
\mathbb{E}[e^{zV_{1}}]=e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}(V_{1}^{2})}
, one readily checks that the right hand side of (
A.1
) is an entire function of
z
. Moreover, since we are dealing with Gaussian random variables, we have
P(V_{1},...,V_{n})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
and
e^{R|V_{1}|}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
for each
R>0
. Thus using Fubini and Morera’s theorem, one sees that also the left hand side of (
A.1
) is an entire function of
z
. It is thus sufficient for us to verify that (
A.1
) holds for
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

The verification of this is a standard “complete the square” argument we now present. As we are dealing with centered jointly Gaussian random variables, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[P(V_{1},...,V_{n})e^{zV_{1}}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det\Sigma}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d^{n}xP(x_{1},...,x_{n})e^{zx_{1}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}x}.

Making the change of variables
x_{i}=y_{i}+z\Sigma_{1i}
, we have

\displaystyle zx_{1}-\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}x

\displaystyle=zy_{1}+z^{2}\Sigma_{11}-\frac{1}{2}y^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}y-z\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{1i}(\Sigma^{-1})_{ij}y_{j}-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{1i}\Sigma_{1j}(\Sigma^{-1})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{2}}{2}\Sigma_{11}-\frac{1}{2}y^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}y.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[P(V_{1},...,V_{n})e^{zV_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}\Sigma_{11}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det\Sigma}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d^{n}yP(y_{1}+z\Sigma_{11},...,y_{n}+z\Sigma_{1n})e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma^{-1}y}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{z^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}(V_{1}^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[P\left(V_{1}+z\mathbb{E}(V_{1}^{2}),...,V_{n}+\mathbb{E}(V_{1}V_{n})\right)\right]

which is the claim (for real valued
z
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of some facts from Section
3.4

In this appendix we provide proofs of Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
3.9
which we used in Section
B
. These are not new, and similar arguments can be found in
[
20
]
and
[
24
, Appendix A]
.

Before proving the bound in Lemma
3.8
, we prove an exact formula for the quantity
|\mathcal{F}_{2,k}^{(n)}|
.

Proposition B.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\leq n/2
, the number of directed two-loop rooted forests
F\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}
with
n
vertices and
k
two-loops is

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|=\frac{n!}{2^{k}k!}\frac{2kn^{n-2k-1}}{(n-2k)!}.

Proof.

Proofs can be found, e.g., in
[
20
]
or
[
24
, Appendix A]
. We explicate an elementary combinatorial argument and refer to
[
24
, Appendix A]
for the rest.

Let
C_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the number of rooted trees with
m
labelled vertices. As in Section
3.4
, we direct the trees toward the root. For instance,
C_{1}=1
,
C_{2}=2
(the trees
1\rightarrow 2
and
2\rightarrow 1
) and
C_{3}=9
(
6
re-labellings of the tree
1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 3
and
3
of the tree
1\rightarrow 2\leftarrow 3
). A directed two-loop rooted forest
F\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}
with
n
vertices and
k
two-loops (
1\leq k\leq n/2
) can be seen as a collection of
2k
such labelled trees, paired by the two-loops. Denoting the vertex count of the
i
:th tree by
m_{i}
(so
1\leq i\leq 2k
and
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{2k}=n
) the number of ways to distribute the labels
1,\ldots,n
to the different trees is given by the multinomial coefficient

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{2k}}=\frac{n!}{m_{1}!\ldots m_{2k}!}.

With the labels fixed, each tree has
C_{m_{i}}
possible structures. Imposing for a moment an additional order to these trees (there are
k!2^{k}
such orders, by the freedom to permute and reverse the two-loops), it is easy to compute
\#\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}
in terms of the auxiliary quantity

\displaystyle T_{m}:=C_{m}/m!.

Altogether, this reasoning gives

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2k}\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{2k}=n\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{m_{1},\ldots,m_{2k}}C_{m_{1}}\cdots C_{m_{2k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{2^{k}k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2k}\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{2k}=n\end{subarray}}T_{m_{1}}\cdots T_{m_{2k}}.

Furthermore, in terms of the generating function
T(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}T_{m}x^{m}
(a formal power series), one readily observes that the sum above is the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the power series of
(T(x))^{2k}
, denoted
[T(x)^{2k}]_{n}
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|=\frac{n!}{2^{k}k!}[T(x)^{2k}]_{n}.

The problem of finding
|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|
is thus transferred into finding
[T(x)^{2k}]_{n}
.

Lemma B.2
.

The generating function
T(x)=\sum_{m\geq 1}T_{m}x^{m}
satisfies
T(x)=xe^{T(x)}
. It is the inverse function of
\phi(x)=xe^{-x}
as a formal power series, and as a convergent complex power series in a neighborhood of
0
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from a recursive formula for
C_{m}
, derived similarly as the formula for
|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|
: a labelled rooted tree of
m
nodes can be constructed by gluing
d
smaller labelled rooted trees by directed edges to the root vertex, where
1\leq d\leq(m-1)
is the (in-)degree of the root vertex. Arguing as above, one obtains

\displaystyle C_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{d}\\
r_{1}+\ldots+r_{d}=m-1\end{subarray}}\binom{m}{1,r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}}C_{r_{1}}\cdots C_{r_{d}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad T_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{d}\\
r_{1}+\ldots+r_{d}=m-1\end{subarray}}T_{r_{1}}\cdots T_{r_{d}}.

and this identity gives

\displaystyle xe^{T(x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}x\frac{T(x)^{d}}{d!}=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}x\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{d}}T_{r_{1}}\cdots T_{r_{d}}x^{r_{1}}\cdots x^{r_{d}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}T_{m}x^{m}=T(x).

For the second claim, just compute
\phi(T(x))=T(x)e^{-T(x)}=xe^{T(x)}e^{-T(x)}=x
. Finally, to go to usual complex power series, note that
\phi(z)
has a local, complex analytic inverse
g:U\to\mathbb{C}
in some neighborhood
U
of
0
. The equation
\phi(g(z))=z
(resp.
\phi(T(x))=x
) determines recursively the power series coefficients of
g
(resp.
T
). Hence, the power series coefficients of
g
and
T
coincide.
∎

Finally, using the fact that
T^{-1}(z)=ze^{-z}
in a neighbourhood of
0
,
[T(x)^{2k}]_{n}
can be found using the Lagrange–Bürmann theorem
[
37
, Chapter VII.3]
, see
[
24
, Appendix A]
.
∎

Proving Lemma
3.8
from this explicit formula is now simple.

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

To obtain the form of the bound stated in Lemma
3.8
, note that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{2^{k}k!}\frac{2kn^{n-2k-1}}{(n-2k)!}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n-k}(n-k)!\frac{n^{n-2k}}{(n-2k)!}\qquad\text{(since $2k\leq n$ and $\tfrac{n!}{(n-k)!k!}\leq 2^{n}$)}

\displaystyle\leq C2^{n-k}(n-k)!\frac{n^{n-2k}}{(\tfrac{n-2k}{e})^{n-2k}}\qquad\text{(Stirling)}.

Writing
n-2k=s\in[0,n]
we have
\log\tfrac{n^{n-2k}}{(n-2k)^{n-2k}}=-s\log(s/n)\leq Cn
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{2,k}|\leq C^{n}(n-k)!.

∎

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

Our first remark is that scaling the integration variables by
R
, we have for a suitable constant
C=C_{\alpha,R}

\displaystyle\int_{U(R)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}}\leq C^{l+m}\int_{U(1)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}},

so we focus on this integral. Going into polar coordinates in each
u_{j}
, and then defining
t_{j}=\frac{1}{4}r_{j}^{2}
, there exists again a constant
C=C_{\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{U(1)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}}\leq C^{l+m}\int_{\mathcal{U}}d^{l+m}t\prod_{j=1}^{l}t_{j}^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}t_{k}^{-\frac{\alpha}{8\pi}},

where

\mathcal{U}=\left\{(t_{1},...,t_{l+m})\in(0,1)^{l+m}:\sum_{j=1}^{l+m}t_{j}\leq 1\right\}.

This last integral can be evaluated (note that it is closely related to the Dirichlet distribution): from
[
37
, Section 12.5]
, if
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{N}>0
, then

\int_{\mathcal{U}}d^{N}tf(t_{1}+...+t_{N})\prod_{j=1}^{N}t_{j}^{\alpha_{j}-1}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\cdots\Gamma(\alpha_{N})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N})}\int_{0}^{1}f(t)t^{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}-1}dt.

For us
f=1
, and we find the bound

\displaystyle\int_{U(R)}d^{2(l+m)}u\prod_{j=1}^{l}|u_{j}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=l+1}^{l+m}|u_{k}|^{-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}}\leq C^{l+m}\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi})^{l}\Gamma(1-\frac{\alpha}{8\pi})^{m}}{\Gamma(1+l(1-\frac{\alpha}{4\pi})+m(1-\frac{\alpha}{8\pi}))},

which is of the form we want.
∎
