Comprehensive review of the cerebral aqueduct: anatomy, embryology, imaging, genetics, molecular, and pathology
Authors: Gabrielle Dituri, Noritaka Komune, Nicolás E. Ottone, Nikola Vučinić, Carmine Antonio Donofrio, Filippo Badaloni, Antonio Fioravanti, Joe Iwanaga, C. J. Bui, Joseph Lockwood, Aaron S. Dumont, R. Shane Tubbs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cerebral aqueduct (CA), or aqueduct of Sylvius, is the narrowest portion of the ventricular system, connecting the third and fourth ventricles and providing an essential conduit for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation. Despite its small caliber, the CA transmits over 500 ml of CSF daily, and its vulnerability to obstruction makes it central to the pathophysiology of hydrocephalus. This review synthesizes current knowledge of the CA across developmental, anatomical, and clinical dimensions. We examine its embryological formation and the contribution of structures such as the subcommissural organ and Reissner’s fiber, highlighting comparative features across species, and evaluate the dynamic physiology of CSF flow in relation to age and disease states. Advances in imaging modalities, including high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging and fetal ultrasound, are summarized in the contexts of both research and clinical practice. Pathological considerations are addressed, ranging from congenital anomalies (e.g., aqueductal webs, Chiari malformations, cavernomas, and forking) to acquired lesions (e.g., gliosis, neoplasia, and trauma-induced stenosis). The genetic underpinnings of aqueductal stenosis are reviewed, emphasizing L1CAM mutations and broader molecular pathways implicated in congenital hydrocephalus. Surgical strategies, including endoscopic third ventriculostomy, aqueductoplasty, and transaqueductal stenting, are critically appraised in terms of efficacy, complication profiles, and long-term outcomes. Overall, this review underscores the CA as a nexus of neurodevelopment, neuropathology, and neurosurgical innovation, and identifies gaps in knowledge that merit future investigation.

Introduction

The cerebral aqueduct (CA), also known as the aqueduct of Sylvius, is a narrow channel within the midbrain that connects the third and fourth ventricles and plays a critical role in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation. Despite its small caliber, it serves as a key conduit through which a substantial volume of CSF passes daily, rendering it particularly susceptible to obstruction and pathological alteration.

Given its clinical importance and the evolving understanding of its anatomy, physiology, and pathology, a comprehensive synthesis is warranted. This review aims to provide an integrated overview of the CA, emphasizing its anatomy, development, function, imaging characteristics, and clinical relevance.

History and Relevant Anatomy

The CA (midbrain aqueduct, aqueduct of Sylvius) unites the third and fourth ventricles and was described in 1650 by the anatomist Franciscus de la Boe Sylvius (Fig. 1) [1, 2]. It is still remembered eponymously after Sylvius, though it had first been described by Jacopo Berengario da Carpi 25 years earlier.

The CA is in the dorsal midbrain, surrounded by the periaqueductal gray matter [3]. The third ventricle connects superiorly to it, while the fourth ventricle connects inferiorly to it (Fig. 2) [4]. The posterior commissure (Fig. 3) and lamina tecti lie posterior to the CA, while the medial longitudinal fasciculus, red nucleus, and CN III/IV nuclei lie anterior to it [3]. The CA has a cross-sectional area of approximately 0.5 mm2 in children and 0.8 mm2 in adults. Although its small size makes it the narrowest part of the CSF path, more than 500 ml of CSF pass through the channel per day in the human brain [4, 5].

The CA is formed by foldings of the ventricular wall comprising small longitudinal striae that run parallel to the median sulci [6]. Histologically, the CA and the central canal of the spinal cord are lined by a single layer of ependymal cells (Fig. 2) [7]. These cells have apical kinocilia and are firmly attached to neighboring ependymal cells [7]. They often show variant anatomy, such as a lining thicker than a single cell layer or small diverticula [3]. The subependymal glial plate can also present with many variations, sometimes in only one part of the CA, or surrounding the CA, or being indiscernible from the ependymal layer of cells, or containing ependymal cells [3, 6]. If the subependymal glial plate is indiscernible, then the CA is bordered by glial cells of the brain stem. If these glial cells proliferate, the glia can enter the canal and obstruct the flow of CSF (gliosis) (see section on Acquired Malformations) [3, 4].

Embryology

During the 3rd and 4th weeks of gestation, primary neurulation begins through a complex signaling and regulatory process that starts with an open neural plate and culminates in sections that proliferate, fold, and pinch off to form the neural tube [8, 9]. Following neural tube closure, three dilations develop on its cranial end. These embryonic brain vesicles become the prosencephalon (which later becomes the telencephalon and diencephalon), mesencephalon, and rhombencephalon (which later becomes the metencephalon and the myelencephalon). Cavities inside the vesicles later develop into the ventricles, producing CSF and facilitating its transport. The cavity of the rhombencephalon forms the fourth ventricle; the cavity of the diencephalon becomes the third ventricle; the cavities within the telencephalon develop into the lateral ventricles. The lateral and third ventricles communicate via the interventricular foramina of Monro, while the third and fourth ventricles communicate via the CA (Figs. 4, 5) [10, 11]. The ventricles reach adult size before the brain does, so the ventricular configuration is altered as the brain continues to develop [12]. During infancy, the length of the CA rapidly increases from 12.8 mm at birth to 18 mm after one year postpartum [3]. Both the lumen and the length of the CA vary among individuals.

Located at the entrance of the CA is the subcommissural organ (SCO). This structure is thought to contribute to the formation of the Reissner’s fiber (RF), a fibrous aggregation consisting mostly of SCO-secreted glycoproteins of a type of called SCO-spondins [13]. This enigmatic structure has been thought essential for proper formation of a straight body axis in vertebrates and to be significant in maintaining normal CSF circulation, because its absence or injury results in collapse of the CA leading to hydrocephalus [14-19]. However, while it is clear that the RF is important in the embryological development of the CA, more research is needed to elucidate its function and mechanism precisely (Table 1).

Cerebrospinal Fluid Flow

Usually, CSF is produced primarily in the lateral ventricles by the choroid plexus, a collection of specialized ependymal cells and capillaries [20-22]. It flows unidirectionally from the lateral ventricles through the interventricular foramina to reach the third ventricle and then travels through the CA from the third to the fourth ventricles [23]. CSF in the fourth ventricle then flows through the foramina of Magendie and Luschka into the subarachnoid space [23, 24].

CSF flow is pulsatile and is highly dependent on the cardiac cycle. It begins approximately 200 ms after the early phase of ventricular depolarization and lasts for approximately 60% of the cardiac cycle [25]. This is followed by a small amount of backflow from the fourth to the third ventricle when the brain and intracranial vasculature contract during diastole [25, 26]. CSF flow is influenced by both respiration and cardiac pulsation, and can be characterized by measurements such as pulsatility, velocity, and amplitude [27]. Many studies have shown that CSF flow through the CA is largely driven by arterial pulsation, but the peak flow velocity varies with age [28-30]. One study showed that peak CSF flow velocity ranged from 3.8 to 24.7 mm/s (mean=11.3±5.8 mm/s) in the elderly, while another study found the amplitude of peak velocity in healthy adolescent controls to be 6.6±2.3 cm/s (mean=2.6±0.9 cm/s) [28, 30]. The difference highlights the decrease in flow rate in elderly populations. Similarly, a study comparing stroke volume (the total volume of CSF displaced throughout the cardiac cycle) between younger and older patients revealed a significantly greater stroke volume in younger patients, suggesting a loss of compliance with increasing age [31].

The CSF pulse in the CA can be used to evaluate intraparenchymal structural changes and as a marker for different pathologies, increased pulsatility being linked to conditions such as normal pressure hydrocephalus and multiple sclerosis [32]. Additionally, it has been theorized that a change in pulsatile flow dynamics within the CA contributes to the development of ventricular enlargement in patients with communicating hydrocephalus [33].

Comparative Anatomy

Vertebrates have a highly conserved ventricular system [34]. Animals such as sharks, frogs, iguanas, rabbits, cats, dogs, and primates all have a CA that connects CSF flow between the third and fourth ventricles [35]. The CA connects to the optic ventricles in non-mammalian animals such as sharks, frogs, and iguanas with a prominent optic lobe [35]. Besides anatomical and spatial differences in the CA across species, its size differs as expected. For instance, in the adult rat brain, the cone-shaped CA is approximately 3.5 mm long, while in humans, the tube-shaped channel is approximately 15 mm [22, 29].

Imaging

Imaging of the CA is best accomplished using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 6). Conventional 1.5 T T1- and T2-weighted MRI provides excellent anatomical detail of aqueductal configuration and can identify structural stenosis or congenital anomalies [36]. Beyond static morphology, phase-contrast MRI has been particularly valuable for evaluating CSF dynamics, enabling quantification of velocity and pulsatile flow through the aqueduct to be quantified [37]. These measurements allow detection of subtle flow abnormalities to be detected that are not always evident on structural sequences alone, and have proven useful in distinguishing obstructive from communicating forms of hydrocephalus [37].

The aqueduct can also be assessed during early development with fetal ultrasound, where it serves as an easily identifiable landmark in the first trimester owing to its echogenic “box-like” appearance [38]. This characteristic appearance permits evaluation of midline integrity and ventricular connectivity to be evaluated, and measuring the distance between the CA to the anterior border of the occiput has been used to detect midbrain displacement in fetuses with spina bifida [38]. Such ultrasonographic findings can therefore act as an early marker of neural tube defects, supporting both diagnosis and prognostic evaluation.

Together, these imaging modalities not only define the structural anatomy of the CA but also provide insight into its functional physiology, establishing a critical role for MRI and ultrasound in both clinical practice and research.

Pathology of Aqueductal Stenosis

Aqueductal stenosis (AS) is a partial or complete blockage of the CA, which can lead to hydrocephalus (Fig. 7) [39]. A cross-sectional area as narrow as 0.1 mm2 has been reported in non-hydrocephalic patients, but how narrow the channel must be to result in hydrocephalus is unknown [3]. AS is thought to be responsible for up to 10% of adult hydrocephalus cases [40]. In AS, the third ventricle and the lateral ventricles enlarge, while the fourth ventricle typically remains normally sized [41]. The blockage can be congenital or acquired via such factors as infection, hemorrhage, or tumor (Fig. 7) [39]. Symptoms that have been described in AS include headache, diplopia, gait impairment, urinary incontinence, and mental deterioration [42]. However, symptoms and their severity vary widely among patients with hydrocephalus, including but not limited to headaches, vision problems, loss of urinary bladder continence, loss of coordination, seizures, lethargy, irritability, and muscle weakness [43].

Sylvian aqueduct syndrome is characterized by pupillary anomalies, impairment of conjugate upward gaze, nystagmus, and palsies of the extraocular muscles caused by a transtentorial pressure gradient through the CA [44, 45]. This unique constellation of symptoms indicates a periaqueductal location and can have a variety of causes, including encephalitis, trauma, multiple sclerosis, and congenital disease [46].

Congenital Malformations

Many congenital malformations result in pathology concerning the CA, including but not limited to Chiari malformations, aqueductal webs, periaqueductal cysts, tectal plate tumors, Sylvian aqueduct syndrome, and aberrant vasculature nearby.

Congenital narrowing of the aqueduct can be due to intrinsic obstruction or extrinsic compression. One example of an intrinsic obstruction is an aqueductal web, a membrane composed of ependymal cells and fibrillary neuroglia between the third and fourth ventricles, which blocks the flow of CSF (Fig. 7) [40]. If this obstruction severely restricts the flow of CSF, procedures such as an endoscopic third ventriculostomy, endoscopic aqueductoplasty, or ventricular shunting can be performed (see Surgery section) [47].

Another rare vascular malformation that can result in obstruction of the CA is a cavernoma, a cluster of capillaries that form lesions in the central nervous system (CNS) [48, 49]. This pathology is relatively rare, occurring in approximately 0.4% of people; intraventricular cavernomas constitute approximately 2.5%–10.8% of all cerebral cavernomas [48, 49]. While an aqueductal location is extremely rare, there have been cases of cavernous angiomas appearing in the CA and causing hydrocephalus [48, 50]. In contrast to this are pathologies that typically cause increased pressure on the CA, such as periaqueductal cysts and developmental venous anomalies located adjacent to the CA. Because those structures are close to the CA, these conditions have been documented to cause non-communicating hydrocephalus [51, 52]. The channel can also become kinked and compressed owing to structural abnormalities or brain masses such as tumors [53].

Another anatomical variation that can cause partial obstruction is forking of the CA. Incomplete merging of the median fissure causes a septum to form, resulting in two separate canals (forking) [22, 54]. This septum is typically located in the inferior part of the CA and is composed of fibrillary neuroglia with clumps of ependymal cells and ependymal canaliculi [3]. The canals thus formed can either lead to the fourth ventricle or be blind pouches, which can result in obstruction [22]. In patients with hydrocephalus associated with a myelomeningocele, the CA can also undergo structural changes that shorten it and lead to forking at its dorsal part [22].

While forking can arise pathologically over time, a diagnosis of developmental-based forking requires histopathological evidence of ependymal-lined channels [54]. Fetal infections and teratogens can cause pathophysiological changes in the ciliated ependymal cells, leading to such conditions as atresia and obstructed CSF flow, and forking of the CA is thought to arise from a similar developmental anomaly [4, 54].

In addition to obstruction/compression of the CA, another pathological congenital condition that can lead to AS is a Chiari malformation. There are two types of Chiari malformation, Chiari I and Chiari II. A Chiari I malformation includes downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum. A Chiari II malformation is characterized by displacement of the brainstem, cerebellar vermis, and fourth ventricle caudally [55, 56]. In a Chiari II malformation, both the fourth and third ventricles appear small, while the lateral ventricles often appear asymmetrically dilated [57]. Vimentin, an intermediate filament crucial for coordinating signaling pathways, is frequently overexpressed in ependymal cells in patients with Chiari II malformations and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both Chiari types, though the exact mechanism remains unclear [58, 59]. Both types of Chiari malformation are associated with AS and can be a congenital cause of hydrocephalus [3]. AS can also be associated with other embryological conditions such as Dandy–Walker malformation (cerebellar vermian hypoplasia) and agenesis of the corpus callosum [41, 60].

Complete CA atresia is rare and is most likely caused by abnormal infolding of the neural plate [3]. If there is a proximal CA blockage, the tectum is pushed into the tegmentum, while in a distal CA blockage the proximal part of the CA can dilate and cause edema in the periaqueductal gray [20].

These genes have been implicated in congenital AS, but many more gene pathways can also be identified in congenital hydrocephalus [62, 63]. Notably, not only have mutations in L1CAM been known to cause X-linked stenosis of the CA with resultant hydrocephalus, but they can also manifest in a wide range of pathological conditions. This syndrome, dubbed L1 syndrome, can present with isolated agenesis of the corpus callosum, corticospinal tract hypoplasia, anterior cerebellar vermian hypoplasia, thalamic fusion, adducted thumbs, and spastic paraplegia [63]. A duplication on the short arm of chromosome X in the 22.33 region, deletions in the long arm of chromosome 9, and deletions in the long arm of chromosome 12 in the 22–23.1 region have also been identified [62].

Acquired Malformations

Acquired malformations are generally thought to be a result from another process, such as trauma or illness. Gliosis is one such reactive processes in which glial cells (particularly astrocytes) proliferate and hypertrophy in response to a brain insult or trauma [64]. Histology of such gliosis can reveal disruption of the ependymal or subependymal layer. It may also be accompanied by an inflammatory process, leading to signs and symptoms of increased intracranial pressure [64-67]. In neonates, intraventricular hemorrhage and subsequent scarring is a major cause of posthemorrhagic AS. Reactive gliosis following perinatal hemorrhage has been described as a key mechanism, with astrocytic proliferation and disruption of the ependymal or subependymal layers leading to narrowing of the aqueduct and impaired CSF circulation [68]. Similarly, meningitis and other CNS infections can provoke an inflammatory reaction in ependymal lining. This inflammation often results in gliotic changes and structural alterations that compromise aqueductal patency, contributing to secondary hydrocephalus.

Neoplasias such as tectal gliomas have also been documented as an etiology for acquired AS, producing obstruction either by direct infiltration or through a mass effect on the aqueduct [61, 69]. Collectively, hemorrhage, infection, gliosis, trauma, and tumor growth are the principal acquired contributors to AS and highlight the clinical importance of early recognition and management of those risk factors.

Surgery

For patients who present with symptomatic hydrocephalus, various procedures can be performed, such as a ventricular shunt, e.g., a ventriculoperitoneal shunt [70]. One such procedure, endoscopic cerebral aqueductoplasty, is used to widen the CA to facilitate CSF flow. This procedure can correct pathologies such as AS or a trapped fourth ventricle. There are many ways to perform this procedure, including the traditional approach beginning at the coronal suture and continuing through the lateral ventricle into the third ventricle to reach the CA, and a newer foramen magnum trans-fourth ventricle approach [71]. Irrespective of approach, this is considered a highly successful procedure, with postoperative cine MRI demonstrating normal CSF flow rates and resolution of symptoms [71, 72]. Stent placement is also recommended to prevent postoperative occlusion of the CA from scarring and reclosure, which could necessitate reoperation [73, 74]. This procedure is thought to restore CSF flow more physiologically than others and to entail less risk to major arteries in the brain, such as the basilar artery and its branches [73]. However, it also carries a risk to surrounding midbrain structures, which can result in conditions such as Parinaud syndrome and various nerve palsies (CNs III and IV) [73].

A related procedure is endoscopic transaqueductal stenting, in which a stent is placed in the CA without necessarily widening it. However, like all procedures in which a foreign body is introduced, there is a chance of infection or malfunction, causing most surgeons to opt for alternatives [75].

One such alternative is endoscopic third ventriculostomy, in which surgeons create a hole in the floor of the third ventricle using an endoscope (Figs. 8, 9) [76]. This procedure is performed by creating a burr hole around the coronal suture on a line that extends from the foramen of Monro to the interpeduncular cistern [77]. Once an adequate trajectory has been analyzed and the endoscope inserted, advancement through the third ventricle can be achieved. Risks for this procedure include hemorrhage (0.21% rate of basilar rupture), hematomas, infections, CSF leaks, and subdural hygromas [78, 79]. Neurological conditions such as gaze palsy, hemiparesis, and altered consciousness have also been documented postoperatively. This procedure is not an option for children under six months of age, as there has been a high documented rate of failure. One study reported an overall complication rate of 8.5% and a lower infection rate than with to shunt placement [78, 80]. It has been well documented as a successful treatment for obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to conditions such as AS, with a success rate of 80% at two-year follow-up [79].

The CA occupies a uniquely vulnerable and clinically consequential position within the ventricular system. Its minute caliber, variable embryological development, and surrounding neuroanatomical relationships make it highly susceptible to congenital and acquired pathologies. AS remains a major contributor to obstructive hydrocephalus, with genetic, structural, and inflammatory mechanisms often intersecting. Advances in imaging have improved diagnostic precision, making earlier detection of aqueductal abnormalities and a more nuanced assessment of CSF dynamics possible. Modern surgical interventions, particularly endoscopic techniques, have shifted the therapeutic landscape by offering more physiological and durable alternatives to shunting, though risks to adjacent midbrain structures remain significant.

Conclusion

Looking forward, further elucidation of genetic and molecular pathways, coupled with innovations in minimally invasive imaging and operative technologies, could enhance prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. A comprehensive understanding of the CA is indispensable not only for clinicians managing hydrocephalus but also for neuroscientists investigating the fundamental biology of CSF flow and brain development.
