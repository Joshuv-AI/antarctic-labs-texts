Helicobacter pylori promote M1 macrophage polarization via JAK3-mediated activation of the SOCS3/IL2RA axis to suppress host immunity
Authors: Jian Chen, Li Zhou, Ling Li, Yue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a major cause of chronic gastritis and gastric cancer. Macrophages are key regulators of gastric immunity, yet the mechanisms by which H. pylori reprogram macrophage activation and suppresses host immune responses remain unclear. RAW264.7 macrophages were infected with H. pylori PMSS1. Macrophage polarization, cytokine secretion, and JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 signaling were examined by qRT-PCR, Western blotting, flow cytometry, and ELISA. Ritlecitinib was used to inhibit JAK3. Primary naive CD4⁺ T cells were treated with macrophage-conditioned medium to assess proliferation, CD25/CD69 activation, and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation. SOCS3 knockdown was used to evaluate rescue effects. H. pylori significantly promoted M1 macrophage polarization, with increased CD86, iNOS, MHC II, TNF-α, and IFN-γ and decreased CD163, Arg1, TGF-β, and IL-10 (p < 0.05). H. pylori also upregulated JAK3, STAT5, and SOCS3, while Ritlecitinib attenuated inflammatory gene expression (p < 0.05). Hp-CM significantly inhibited primary CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation, CD25/CD69 activation, and CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation (p < 0.05). SOCS3 knockdown partially restored these CD4⁺ T-cell responses (p < 0.05). H. pylori promotes M1 macrophage polarization and impairs CD4⁺ T-cell immunity through JAK3/STAT5–SOCS3-mediated immune dysregulation. This mechanism contributes to chronic immune dysregulation and supports bacterial persistence, identifying JAK3/SOCS3 as potential therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00203-026-05104-0.Keywords: H. pylori, Macrophage polarization, Immune suppression, SOCS3/IL2RA axis

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) persistently colonizes the human stomach and represents the primary etiological agent of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric cancer (Gravina et al. 2018, Elbehiry et al. 2023, Eyvazi et al. 2020). Recent epidemiological analyses have shown that global prevalence of H. pylori infection remains high, affecting approximately 40–50% of adults worldwide (Chen et al. 2024). Persistent H. pylori infection is closely associated with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric mucosal atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and gastric cancer. In particular, H. pylori is recognized as a major risk factor for non-cardia gastric cancer and contributes substantially to the global burden of infection-associated malignancies. Although eradication therapy has reduced disease risk in many patients, treatment failure, antibiotic resistance, persistent inflammation, and immune escape remain important clinical challenges (Savoldi et al. 2018, Malfertheiner et al. 2022). Therefore, clarifying how H. pylori reshape host immune responses is essential for understanding disease progression and identifying potential immunomodulatory targets. Among the innate immune cells recruited early to the gastric mucosa, macrophages are key regulators of antibacterial defense and inflammatory amplification. Increasing evidence shows that H. pylori profoundly alters macrophage polarization (Yang et al. 2021, Sit et al. 2024), but the upstream molecular signals governing this process remain insufficiently characterized.

One signaling cascade implicated in H. pylori-induced inflammation is the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway, which integrates cytokine-receptor signals into transcriptional programs that shape immune cell phenotype (Ezeonwumelu et al. 2021, Villarino et al. 2017). Multiple studies indicate that H. pylori can activate JAK family kinases and STAT signaling in gastric epithelial cells and immune cells, promoting inflammatory mediator release (Cha et al. 2015, Peng et al. 2023). However, whether JAK3 activation directly drives macrophage polarization during H. pylori infection has not been fully elucidated.

Suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), a classical negative regulator of the JAK/STAT cascade, adds additional complexity. SOCS3 expression is significantly induced during H. pylori infection (Jafarzadeh et al. 2024, Sarajlic et al. 2020), yet its function is context dependent. SOCS3 can inhibit STAT3 activity while allowing sustained STAT1-driven inflammatory responses, thereby favoring M1 macrophage polarization (Mahony et al. 2016). This dual regulatory capacity raises the possibility that SOCS3 may serve as a key checkpoint coupling JAK3 activation to macrophage inflammatory programming.

Beyond macrophage activation, the downstream impact of H. pylori-induced macrophage phenotypes on adaptive immunity remains incompletely defined. H. pylori infection has been linked to impaired T-cell proliferation and dysfunction of regulatory T cells (Tregs), both of which contribute to chronic infection and immune escape (Yu et al. 2018, Shaopeng et al. 2024). How macrophage-derived factors suppress T-cell responses, and whether JAK3/SOCS3 signaling participates in this crosstalk, is largely unknown.

In this study, we performed a series of experiments using RAW264.7 macrophages infected with H. pylori to clarify how it exploits JAK3-dependent signaling to reshape macrophage function and impair host immune responses.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and bacterial strains

The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Institute. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in medium (DMEM) (Wisen, 319-005-CL) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Wisent, 086-150) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin).

H. pylori PMSS1 strain (DSM 105294; BacDive ID 157650), a rodent-adapted CagA-positive strain, was obtained from Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The bacteria were cultured on Blood Agar Base No. 2 supplemented with 5% horse blood (Oxoid, Cambridge, UK) under microaerophilic conditions at 37 ℃. Before infection, RAW264.7 cells were seeded and cultured until they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. H. pylori were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase, resuspended in antibiotic-free medium, and added to RAW264.7 cells at an MOI of 200 for 24 h.

Isolation and culture of primary naive CD4⁺ T cells

Mouse spleens were collected from C57BL/6 mice under sterile conditions and mechanically dissociated to obtain single-cell suspensions. Naive CD4⁺ T cells were isolated using magnetic bead sorting according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of isolated CD4⁺ T cells was immediately assessed by flow cytometry. Purified naive CD4⁺ T cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

JAK3 inhibitor treatment

To determine the involvement of JAK3 signaling in H. pylori-induced macrophage inflammatory activation, RAW264.7 macrophages were pretreated with the JAK3 inhibitor Ritlecitinib (MCE, 28544) at a concentration of 1 µM for 1 h before H. pylori infection. Cells were then infected with H. pylori at an MOI of 200 for the indicated time. After treatment, cells were collected for qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses, and culture supernatants were collected for ELISA where appropriate.

Lentiviral shRNA-mediated SOCS3 knockdown

SOCS3 knockdown in RAW264.7 macrophages was performed using a lentiviral shRNA system. Briefly, 293 T cells were co-transfected with PL/shRNA/GFP-mouse-SOCS3, PMD2G, and PSPAX2 plasmids using Lipo6000 transfection reagent. After 48 h, viral supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.45-µm filter, and used to transduce RAW264.7 cells. A non-targeting shRNA lentiviral vector was used as the negative control. SOCS3 knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting before subsequent H. pylori infection and conditioned medium collection.

The target sequence of the lentiviral shRNA used for mouse SOCS3 knockdown was 5′-GGGAGTTCCTGGATCAGTATG-3′.

Preparation of macrophage-conditioned medium and treatment of primary naive CD4⁺ T cells

RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded into 6-well plates and treated according to the experimental design. For the Control-CM group, RAW264.7 cells were cultured under normal conditions. For the Hp-CM group, RAW264.7 cells were infected with H. pylori. For SOCS3 knockdown experiments, RAW264.7 cells were first transduced with lentiviral sh-NC or sh-SOCS3 and then infected with H. pylori to generate Hp + sh-NC-CM and Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM.

After treatment, culture supernatants were collected as macrophage-conditioned medium. The conditioned medium was centrifuged to remove cell debris and filtered to remove residual bacteria before being applied to primary naive CD4⁺ T cells. Primary naive CD4 + T cells were stimulated with anti-CD3 antibody at a concentration of 3 µg/mL and cultured with the indicated macrophage-conditioned medium for 72 h. After treatment, CD4⁺ T cells were collected for CFSE proliferation assay, flow cytometric analysis of CD25/CD69 activation, and detection of CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, 9108). Cells were collected according to the corresponding experimental groups. In the non-infected control group, RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured under normal conditions without H. pylori exposure. In the infected group, RAW264.7 macrophages were infected with H. pylori PMSS1 at an MOI of 200 for 24 h. In brief, cells were lysed at room temperature for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ℃ to obtain a clarified lysate. The resulting supernatant was mixed with isopropanol to precipitate RNA and subsequently centrifuged at 7500 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃. The RNA pellet was washed with DEPC-treated water, dissolved, and its quantity and purity were determined by UV spectrophotometry. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, RR047), and mRNA levels were quantified by real-time PCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (GenStar, A304) according to the manufacturers’ protocols. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene for normalization (Xu et al. 2025, Yang et al. 2025). Primers used for qRT-PCR were designed based on the corresponding mouse gene sequences obtained from the NCBI database. Primer specificity was checked using NCBI Primer-BLAST to avoid non-specific amplification. GAPDH was selected as the internal reference gene for normalization because of its stable expression under the experimental conditions used in this study. The primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Western blot

Cells were collected and lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010). Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0010) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per sample) were then separated on 8–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gels. The separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, ISEQ00010) and blocked in 5% nonfat milk (BD, 232100) prepared in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20. For Western blotting, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-JAK3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #8827, 1:1000), anti-phospho-JAK3 (Tyr980/981) (Cell Signaling Technology, #5031, 1:1000), anti-STAT5 (Cell Signaling Technology, #94205, 1:1000), anti-phospho-STAT5 (Tyr694) (Cell Signaling Technology, #9314, 1:1000), anti-SOCS3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2923, 1:1000), anti-IL2RA/CD25 (Cell Signaling Technology, #39475, 1:1000), anti-FoxP3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #12653, 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, #2118, 1:1000). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074, 1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. After thorough washing with TBST, protein signals were detected using the Tanon ECL system. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software, and the expression of each target protein was normalized to GAPDH.

ELISA

The concentrations of TNF-α (Elabscience, E-HSEL-M0009), IFN-γ (Elabscience, E-MSEL-M0007) and IL-10 (biorbyt, orb219745) in the culture supernatants were measured using commercially available mouse ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min to remove debris. Standards and samples were added to pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated for 1–2 h at 37 °C. After washing, HRP-conjugated detection antibodies were applied and incubated for 1 h. TMB substrate solution was then added, and the reaction was stopped with 2 M sulfuric acid. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch). Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated in parallel.

Flow cytometric analysis of CD4⁺ T-cell activation and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation

Flow cytometry was performed to evaluate CD4⁺ T-cell activation and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation. After treatment with indicated macrophage-conditioned medium for 72 h, CD4⁺ T cells were collected and washed with PBS. For detection of T-cell activation, cells were stained with FITC-conjugated anti-CD4 antibody (FITC-CD4; eBioscience, 53-0041-82) together with APC-conjugated anti-CD25 antibody (APC-CD25; eBioscience, 17–0251-82) or APC-conjugated anti-CD69 antibody (APC-CD69; eBioscience, 17–0691-82) at 4 ℃ in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed and resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. CD4⁺CD25⁺ and CD4⁺CD69⁺ cells were quantified as activated CD4⁺ T cells.

For analysis of FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation, cells were first stained with surface antibodies against CD4 and CD25, including FITC-conjugated anti-CD4 antibody (FITC-CD4; eBioscience, 53-0041-82) and PE-conjugated anti-CD25 antibody (PE-CD25; eBioscience, 12–0251-82). After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a FoxP3/transcription factor staining buffer set according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular staining was then performed using APC-conjugated anti-FoxP3 antibody (APC-FoxP3; eBioscience, 17–5773-82). After washing, cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. The proportion of CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ cells was calculated to assess FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation.

Observe the cell morphology under an inverted microscope

RAW264.7 macrophages from the control and H. pylori-infected groups were observed under an inverted microscope (Nikon, Ts2R). Representative fields were selected to evaluate cell size, shape, adhesion status, and cytoplasmic granularity. Images were captured for morphological comparison.

CFSE assay for CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation

The proliferation of primary naive CD4⁺ T cells was evaluated using a CFSE dilution assay. Briefly, purified naive CD4⁺ T cells were washed with PBS and labeled with CFSE (ThermoFisher, C34554) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation at 37 °C in the dark, the staining reaction was terminated by adding complete RPMI 1640 medium. The cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. CFSE-labeled CD4⁺ T cells were stimulated with anti-CD3 antibody and cultured with indicated macrophage-conditioned medium from the indicated groups for 72 h. After incubation, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. T-cell proliferation was determined based on CFSE dilution, and the total proliferating cell population was calculated according to the proportion of cells showing reduced CFSE fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as follows: p < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

Transcriptomic profiling indicates macrophage-associated immune activation during H. pylori infection

To characterize immune alterations associated with H. pylori colonization, transcriptomic data from GSE60427 were analyzed (Fig. 1A). KEGG enrichment revealed significant activation of cytokine signaling, antigen-processing pathways, Toll-like receptor cascades, and inflammatory mediator production (Fig. 1B). GO enrichment further indicated that differentially expressed genes were highly associated with leukocyte activation, MHC complex assembly, chemokine signaling, and innate immune responses (Fig. 1C).

Notably, multiple macrophage-associated signatures, including inflammatory activation, cytokine-related pathways, and antigen-presenting functions, were prominently enriched. These findings suggest that macrophages may represent major immune responders during H. pylori infection and provide a rationale for the subsequent in vitro experiments to validate macrophage polarization and related signaling mechanisms.

H. pylori drive robust M1 polarization accompanied by morphological, phenotypic, and cytokine changes

Infection of RAW264.7 macrophages with H. pylori (MOI = 200) induced rapid morphological changes consistent with M1 activation, including cell rounding, increased cytoplasmic granularity, and reduced adhesion (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometry confirmed strong upregulation of CD86 and downregulation of CD163, demonstrating a clear shift toward classical M1 polarization (Fig. 2B).

Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed increased expression of M1 markers (iNOS, MHC II) and reduced levels of M2-associated proteins (Arg1, TGF-β) (Fig. 2C). ELISA showed significantly elevated TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion accompanied by a notable reduction in IL-10 (Fig. 2D). Together, these data demonstrate that H. pylori strongly reprogram macrophages into a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by heightened cytokine output and loss of regulatory features.

H. pylori engage the JAK3/STAT5–SOCS3 regulatory module in macrophages

To investigate whether H. pylori affect JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 signaling in macrophages, the mRNA and protein expression levels of JAK3, STAT5, and SOCS3 were examined in RAW264.7 cells after H. pylori infection. qRT-PCR analysis showed that H. pylori significantly increased the mRNA expression of JAK3, STAT5, and SOCS3 compared with the control group (Fig. 3A-C).

Data are presented as mean ± SD. **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 versus the control group

Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that the protein levels of JAK3, STAT5, and SOCS3 were also increased in H. pylori-infected macrophages (Fig. 3D-G). These results indicate that H. pylori upregulate the JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 signaling module in RAW264.7 macrophages, suggesting its potential involvement in H. pylori-induced macrophage inflammatory activation.

JAK3 inhibition attenuates H. pylori-induced inflammatory gene expression in macrophages

To determine whether JAK3 activation is required for H. pylori-induced inflammatory activation, RAW264.7 macrophages were pretreated with the JAK3 inhibitor Ritlecitinib before H. pylori infection. qRT-PCR analysis showed that Ritlecitinib significantly reduced the expression of the M1-associated inflammatory genes TNF-α, iNOS, and CCR7 compared with the H. pylori group (Fig. 4A-C). In addition, IRF8 expression was markedly decreased following Ritlecitinib treatment (Fig. 4D). Conversely, the expression of the anti-inflammatory genes IL-4, IL-10, and Chi3l3 was significantly increased (Fig. 4E-G). Ritlecitinib also markedly reduced JAK3 mRNA expression in H. pylori-infected macrophages (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of JAK3 suppresses the pro-inflammatory transcriptional program induced by H. pylori while promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory macrophage-associated genes.

Hp-CM suppresses the cellular function of primary CD4⁺ T cells

To determine whether H. pylori-activated macrophages affect CD4⁺ T-cell immune responses, primary naive CD4⁺ T cells isolated from mouse spleens were treated with conditioned medium collected from control macrophages or H. pylori-stimulated macrophages. Flow cytometry showed that Hp-CM markedly reduced FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation, as indicated by the decreased proportion of CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ cells compared with the Control-CM group (Fig. 5A).

CFSE dilution analysis further showed that Hp-CM impaired CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation. Compared with Control-CM-treated cells, Hp-CM-treated CD4⁺ T cells displayed a significantly increased proportion of undivided cells, indicating reduced proliferative capacity (Fig. 5B). In addition, Hp-CM suppressed CD4⁺ T-cell activation, as demonstrated by the reduced percentages of CD4⁺CD69⁺ and CD4⁺CD25⁺ cells (Fig. 5C).

At the molecular level, qRT-PCR showed that Hp-CM significantly decreased the mRNA expression of JAK3, STAT5, IL2RA, and FoxP3, while increasing SOCS3 expression in CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 5D-H). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that Hp-CM reduced the phosphorylation levels of JAK3 and STAT5, decreased IL2RA and FoxP3 protein expression, and increased SOCS3 protein levels, whereas total STAT5 expression was not obviously changed (Fig. 5I). These results suggest that conditioned medium from H. pylori-activated macrophages suppresses primary CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation, activation, and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation, accompanied by inhibition of the JAK3/STAT5-IL2RA/FoxP3 regulatory signaling pathway and upregulation of SOCS3.

Macrophage SOCS3 knockdown reverses Hp-CM-mediated CD4⁺ T-cell dysfunction

To further determine whether SOCS3 contributes to the suppressive effect of H. pylori-activated macrophages on CD4⁺ T-cell immunity, SOCS3 was knocked down in RAW264.7 macrophages using a lentiviral shRNA system before H. pylori stimulation. Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed that SOCS3 expression was markedly reduced in the Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM group compared with the Hp + sh-NC-CM group, indicating successful SOCS3 knockdown (Fig. 6A, B).

The functional effects of SOCS3 knockdown were then evaluated in primary naïve CD4⁺ T cells treated with macrophage-conditioned medium. CFDA-SE dilution analysis showed that Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM significantly decreased the proportion of undivided CD4⁺ T cells compared with Hp + sh-NC-CM, indicating that SOCS3 knockdown in H. pylori-activated macrophages partially restored CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation (Fig. 6C). Flow cytometry further showed that Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM increased the proportion of CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ Treg cells, suggesting partial recovery of FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation (Fig. 6D).

In addition, SOCS3 knockdown restored CD4⁺ T-cell activation. Compared with the Hp + sh-NC-CM group, Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM significantly increased the percentages of CD4⁺CD25⁺ and CD4⁺CD69⁺ cells (Fig. 6E). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis showed increased mRNA expression of JAK3, STAT5, IL2RA, and FoxP3 in CD4⁺ T cells treated with Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM (Fig. 6F-I). Western blot analysis further confirmed that Hp + sh-SOCS3-CM increased JAK3 phosphorylation, STAT5 phosphorylation, IL2RA expression, and FoxP3 expression compared with Hp + sh-NC-CM, whereas total JAK3 and STAT5 were only moderately changed (Fig. 6J). These results indicate that SOCS3 knockdown in H. pylori-activated macrophages partially restores primary CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation, activation, and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation. These findings suggest that macrophage SOCS3 plays an important role in mediating the suppressive effect of H. pylori-activated macrophages on CD4⁺ T-cell immune responses.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that H. pylori drives a pronounced M1 polarization program in macrophages and disrupts CD4+ T-cell immune regulation through activation of the JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 axis. These findings align with prior evidence that H. pylori engages innate immune pathways and promotes chronic gastric inflammation (Wang et al. 2014, Lima de Souza Gonçalves et al. 2022). Transcriptomic enrichment of immune-activation signatures in infected tissues supports observations that macrophages are key coordinators of the gastric inflammatory microenvironment (Fan et al. 2024).

Differences between our findings and some previous reports may be related to variations in H. pylori strain, bacterial virulence factors, infection dose, stimulation time, and macrophage model (Mohammadzadeh et al. 2023). In the present study, RAW264.7 macrophages were infected with the PMSS1 strain, which may partly explain the strong M1-like inflammatory response observed in our model (Dyer et al. 2018). Macrophage polarization is a key determinant of the inflammatory outcome during H. pylori infection. Previous studies have reported that H. pylori infection promotes the recruitment and M1 polarization of gastric macrophages and enhances the production of inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to chronic gastritis and mucosal injury (Quiding-Järbrink et al. 2010). We show that macrophages constitute an early and dominant responder population during H. pylori exposure, consistent with transcriptomic signatures from infected gastric tissue revealing strong enrichment of antigen presentation, cytokine signalling and inflammatory amplification pathway (Minaga et al. 2018). Beyond serving as passive sensors, macrophages shape the inflammatory landscape and determine whether infection evolves toward resolution or persistent colonization. Our data demonstrate that H. pylori drive a highly polarized M1 phenotype, in line with recent observations that the bacterium actively skews macrophage function to maintain a pro-inflammatory but ultimately non-sterilizing immune environment (Mohamed et al. 2025, Dore and Pes 2024).

Given that macrophage polarization is largely controlled by cytokine-dependent intracellular signalling, we further focused on the JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 axis. The JAK/STAT pathway links extracellular inflammatory cytokines to transcriptional immune responses and plays a central role in immune-cell activation, inflammatory amplification, immune suppression and immune-microenvironment remodeling (Xue et al. 2023, Lv et al. 2024). Recent evidence has further emphasized that JAK-related signalling is closely involved in shaping immune-cell function and regulating the balance between inflammatory activation and immune escape within the local microenvironment (Liu et al. 2026). Therefore, examining JAK3, STAT5 and SOCS3 allowed us to determine whether H. pylori-driven macrophage polarization was accompanied by activation of a cytokine-responsive regulatory circuit. JAK3 was selected because it is closely associated with immune-cell signalling, STAT5 was examined as a downstream effector involved in cytokine responsiveness and immune regulation, and SOCS3 was included because it is an inducible feedback regulator that may determine the intensity and direction of macrophage-mediated immune responses. In our study, H. pylori increased JAK3, STAT5 and SOCS3 expression in macrophages, whereas pharmacological inhibition of JAK3 by Ritlecitinib attenuated M1-associated inflammatory gene expression. These findings suggest that JAK3-related signalling contributes to H. pylori-induced macrophage inflammatory activation. Meanwhile, the increase in SOCS3 may represent a feedback response to persistent inflammatory stimulation and may also participate in shaping macrophage-derived signals that suppress downstream CD4⁺ T-cell immunity (Morelli et al. 2024, Shin et al. 2024).

Previous studies have indicated that H. pylori can impair effective adaptive immune responses by altering CD4⁺ T-cell activation and regulatory programs, which may contribute to persistent colonization and chronic gastric inflammation (Fan et al. 2024, Sirit and Peek 2025). In line with these findings, our study showed that conditioned medium from H. pylori-activated macrophages significantly suppressed primary naive CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation and reduced the proportions of CD4⁺CD25⁺ and CD4⁺CD69⁺ cells. These results suggest that macrophage-derived soluble factors are sufficient to weaken CD4⁺ T-cell activation after H. pylori stimulation. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on direct T-cell responses during H. pylori infection, our findings further connect macrophage inflammatory polarization with downstream CD4⁺ T-cell dysfunction (Hu et al. 2025). Mechanistically, Hp-CM reduced IL2RA, p-JAK3, p-STAT5 and FoxP3 expression in CD4⁺ T cells, indicating that macrophage-derived signals may interfere with IL-2/JAK3/STAT5-related immune regulation. Since IL2RA encodes CD25 and is essential for IL-2 responsiveness, while FoxP3 is a key transcription factor for Treg differentiation, these changes provide a possible explanation for the reduced CD4⁺ T-cell activation and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation observed in our model. Together with recent evidence showing that helper T-cell dysfunction and altered Treg responses are closely associated with persistent H. pylori-induced gastritis, our results support a macrophage-to-CD4⁺ T-cell regulatory mechanism that may contribute to chronic immune imbalance during H. pylori infection (Wu et al. 2025).

From a clinical perspective, persistent H. pylori infection remains a major challenge because it is closely associated with chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric mucosal atrophy and gastric cancer (Malfertheiner et al. 2022). Although eradication therapy is widely used, increasing antibiotic resistance, treatment failure and persistent mucosal inflammation continue to limit clinical outcomes (Martínez-Martínez et al. 2026). Current consensus guidelines emphasize that successful management of H. pylori infection should not only focus on bacterial eradication but also consider disease risk stratification, gastric cancer prevention and the inflammatory consequences of long-term infection36. In this context, our findings provide a potential immune-regulatory explanation for why persistent H. pylori infection can maintain chronic inflammation while failing to generate effective immune clearance. The macrophage-CD4⁺ T-cell regulatory axis identified in this study suggests that targeting macrophage inflammatory polarization or JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3-related signalling may help restore immune balance during chronic infection (Shadvar et al. 2024). However, because JAK/STAT signalling broadly participates in host defense and immune homeostasis, any clinical translation of this strategy should be carefully evaluated in primary cells, animal models and patient-derived gastric mucosal samples.

Despite these findings, several issues should be addressed in future studies to strengthen the translational value of this work. The present study mainly used RAW264.7 macrophages and primary mouse CD4⁺ T cells to establish a controllable macrophage-T-cell regulatory model. Although this system is useful for clarifying the role of the JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 axis, it cannot fully reflect the cellular diversity and spatial complexity of the gastric mucosal immune microenvironment. Future studies should validate this mechanism in bone marrow-derived macrophages, human monocyte-derived macrophages, gastric mucosal immune cells and patient-derived gastric biopsy samples. In particular, evaluating JAK3, STAT5, SOCS3, IL2RA and FoxP3 expression in H. pylori-positive clinical specimens may help determine whether this axis is associated with the severity of gastritis, bacterial persistence, precancerous lesions or treatment response. In addition, our conditioned-medium model focused on soluble macrophage-derived mediators, while direct macrophage-CD4⁺ T-cell contact and tissue-level immune interactions were not examined. Further work using Transwell systems, direct co-culture models, animal infection models and single-cell analysis may help define the precise cellular communication network involved in this process. From a clinical translation perspective, the macrophage-CD4⁺ T-cell regulatory axis identified here may provide potential biomarkers for immune imbalance during persistent H. pylori infection and may also offer a basis for developing adjunctive immunomodulatory strategies alongside eradication therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, these findings suggest that H. pylori promote M1-like macrophage polarization and activates the JAK3/STAT5-SOCS3 axis, thereby impairing CD4⁺ T-cell proliferation, activation and FoxP3⁺ Treg differentiation. This macrophage-T-cell regulatory mechanism may contribute to persistent immune imbalance during H. pylori infection and provides a potential direction for future immunomodulatory studies.
