Sulfur-incorporated copper catalysts for enhanced ethylene production in membrane electrode assembly CO2 electrolysis
Authors: Juho Ha, Hyunki Kim, Seohyeon Jang, Andi Haryanto, Jungmin Yoo, Yeonghye Yun, Sunbeom Ahn, Gyeong Ho Han, Myoung Hwan Oh, Soo Young Kim, Chan Woo Lee, Inho Nam, Sang Hyun Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper sulfides typically favor formate production in CO2 electrolysis, yet their behavior under membrane electrode assembly (MEA) conditions remains largely uncharted. In this study, CuxS/Cu electrodes with tunable morphologies, including spheres, films, polygons, and cubes, were synthesized, and the surface sulfur content was systematically tuned through electrodeposition followed by controlled sulfidation. Performance evaluation in MEA systems reveals that sulfidation markedly enhances C2H4 selectivity and partial current density (PCD). For spherical Cu electrodes, sulfidation to a S/Cu surface ratio of 0.33 resulted in a 1.76-fold increase in C2H4 PCD, from 58.2 to 102.5 mA/cm2 at 3.2 V. In situ Raman spectroscopy suggests that sulfur coordination stabilizes bridge-bonded CO and promotes the formation of the CH2CHO intermediate, which is consistent with the enhanced C2H4 activity. Density functional theory calculations reveal that Cu-rich sulfide surfaces, including Cu2S and Cu31S16, stabilize *OCCO intermediates, suggesting a thermodynamically favorable C–C coupling reaction pathway through synergistic Cu–S coordination. Introduction

Electrochemical CO2 reduction (ECO2R) is a promising approach for converting CO2 into value-added chemicals [1, 2], with ethylene (C2H4) being a particularly desirable product owing to its industrial relevance [3–5]. Among monometals, copper is unique in enabling C–C coupling, a key step in forming multi-carbon (C2+) products including C2H4 [6]. Strategies for enhancing C–C coupling on copper include facet engineering, introduction of vacancies, modulation of oxidation states, incorporation of functional groups such as –NH2, and tandem catalyst design [7–12]. Among them, oxide-derived copper catalysts have exhibited improved performance, attributed to the formation of mixed-valence Cu0/Cu+ interfaces that lower the activation energy for *CO dimerization [13–15]. However, metastable Cu+ species tend to reduce to Cu0 under the negative potentials required for C2+ product formation, compromising long-term stability [10, 16]. Building on this concept, recent strategies have incorporated heteroatoms such as B and N into Cu or Cu oxide matrices to further modulate surface electronic properties and stabilize key intermediates [17–20].

In contrast, copper sulfide-derived catalysts generally promote formate (HCOO–) production during ECO2R [21–23]. This tendency arises from sulfur-modified surfaces that preferentially stabilize the *OCHO intermediate while disfavoring *COOH adsorption. Given that *COOH is essential for C–C bond formation, this mechanistic bias restricts the generation of C2+ products such as ethylene [21–23]. Nonetheless, several studies have reported enhanced C2H4 production using sulfide-derived copper catalysts under specific conditions [24–28]. Yabuki et al. proposed that lattice strain in copper sulfide films can promote C–C bond formation by creating surface geometries resembling the bonding environment of C2H4 [25]. Wen et al. proposed that, in contrast to bulk Cu2S, the Cu/CuxSy interface provides a more favorable configuration for *CO dimerization, because the spacing between the Cu0 and Cuδ+ species supports geometries conducive to coupling reactions [27]. Results obtained by density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the biphasic Cu/Cu2S interface with oxidized Cuδ+ species exhibits reduced *CO adsorption energy, facilitating CO dimerization relative to a pure Cu surface [27]. In another study on S-doped CuO catalysts, sulfur doping increased the C2H4 Faradaic efficiency (FE) from 14.9 to 48.4% [28]. This improvement was attributed to a lower free energy barrier of *COOH formation and enhanced surface hydrophobicity, which in turn reduced water contact and suppressed the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) [28].

Despite recent advances, sulfide-derived copper catalysts, akin to their oxide-derived counterparts, continue to suffer from limited partial current density (PCD) and FE toward C2H4 under prolonged electrolysis. These performance constraints stem from two principal structural instabilities: (i) the reduction-driven phase transformation from CuxSy to metallic Cu under cathodic bias; (ii) the re-adsorption of dissolved sulfide ions onto the Cu surface, which inhibits key intermediate adsorption and shifts selectivity toward HCOOH [10, 29, 30].

Such degradation mechanisms are further intensified in conventional aqueous-phase CO2 electrolysis setups, including H-type and flow cells, which constitute the predominant testing platforms for sulfide-derived copper catalysts [24–28]. To circumvent these challenges, membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzer offer a promising alternative by operating with gaseous CO2 in a zero-gap configuration, minimizing ohmic losses and enhancing stability [31, 32]. Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, sulfide-derived copper electrodes have not been systematically investigated under MEA conditions for selective C2H4 production from CO2.

In this study, Cu-rich CuxS/Cu (x < 2) electrodes were synthesized via electrodeposition, followed by solution-phase anion exchange to systematically tune the surface sulfur content. The ECO2R performance of these electrodes was evaluated in the MEA systems. Sulfidation significantly enhanced C2H4 selectivity and PCD in MEA, with a distinct volcano-type trend observed as a function of the surface S/Cu ratio. In situ Raman spectroscopy revealed that sulfur coordination stabilizes bridge-bonded CO and promotes the formation of the CH2CHO intermediate, providing mechanistic insight into the relationship between surface chemistry and electrochemical trends. Furthermore, first-principles calculations were performed on representative Cu-rich CuxS surfaces. The results revealed that sulfur incorporation stabilized *CO and *OCCO intermediates via favorable Cu–S coordination environments, thereby lowering the energy barrier for C–C coupling. These findings provide a molecular-level rationale for the enhancement in C2H4 selectivity observed experimentally under MEA operating conditions, supporting the conclusion that surface sulfur species actively modulate key catalytic pathways in ECO2R.

Experimental

Preparation of Cu electrodes

Copper electrodes with distinct morphologies were synthesized via electrodeposition using a custom-built Teflon cell configured in a three-electrode arrangement. The working electrode consisted of microporous carbon paper (MPL/CP, GDL210S, Cetech), with a graphite rod and saturated calomel electrode (SCE) serving as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. Morphological control was achieved by tuning the deposition bath composition, applied potential, and substrate pretreatment conditions. CuSO4·5H2O (99% purity, Daejung) was employed as the Cu source, with supporting electrolytes prepared using (NH4)2SO4 (99% purity, Alfa Aesar) or Na2SO4 (99% purity, Thermo Scientific) and H2SO4 (95% purity, Junsei). The electrodeposition was conducted at constant potential using a potentiostat (Autolab PGSTAT302N, Metrohm). Prior to use, the MPL/CP substrates were treated with 30 W of oxygen plasma (PDC-FMG-2, HARRICK PLASMA) to enhance surface hydrophilicity [33]. The electrolyte solution was deaerated by purging with N2 for 20 min before deposition. Following electrodeposition, the Cu electrodes were rinsed with deionized water and dried under a N2 stream. The detailed deposition parameters are summarized in Table S1.

Preparation of CuxS/Cu electrodes

The electrodeposited Cu electrodes were converted to Cu-rich copper sulfide via a sulfidation process based on anion exchange. Immediately after electrodeposition, the samples were immersed in a 1 mM aqueous Na2S·5H2O (98% purity, Daejung) solution at ambient temperature and pressure. The sulfidation time was varied between 1 and 60 min to modulate sulfur incorporation. After treatment, the electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water (18 MΩ·cm) and dried with N2.

Evaluation of ECO2R performance in MEA cell

In the MEA configuration, the as-prepared Cu-based electrode and commercial IrOx on carbon paper (C020, Dioxide Materials) served as the cathode and anode, respectively. An anion exchange membrane (Sustainion X37-50 RT, Dioxide Materials) was positioned between the electrodes, defining an active area of 1 cm2. No additional ionomer was applied to either electrode. Prior to use, the membrane was pretreated in 1.0 M KOH at 80 ℃ for 90 min. After cooling to room temperature, it was stored in 1.0 M KOH and rinsed thoroughly with deionized water immediately before cell assembly. The MEA was assembled using 200 μm-thick gaskets on both the cathode and anode sides, and the cell was tightened with a clamping torque of 60 lbf·in. Humidified CO2 was delivered to the cathode at a flow rate of 220 mL/min, whereas 0.1 M KOH was supplied to the anode at 20 mL/min. Both the humidifier and the cell were maintained at room temperature, so that the CO2 feed was saturated with water vapor, corresponding to a relative humidity of approximately 100%. All ECO2R experiments were conducted at ambient temperature and pressure without active control of the gas humidity. All results presented in this manuscript were obtained without iR compensation. Liquid products were not separately analyzed. The gas product analyses were performed three times using independently prepared electrodes at each cell voltage. The outlet of each electrolyzer was connected to a gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent 7890B) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a flame ionization detector (FID). The concentrations of H2, CO, CH4, and C2H4 were measured automatically at 30 s intervals throughout the electrolysis. Characterization

The surface morphology of the Cu-based catalysts was characterized using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Sigma, Carl Zeiss) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL Ltd.). The crystallographic structure was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), supported by Fourier transform (FFT) analyses. The bulk and surface compositions were probed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; NORAN System 7, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; K-alpha+, Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively.

In situ Raman measurements

Au@SiO2 nanoparticles, as a SERS antenna, were deposited onto the catalyst surface to enhance the surface signals. The Au@SiO2 nanoparticles were synthesized following a previously reported method [34, 35]. First, 100 mL of chloroauric acid solution (0.01 wt%, 99.99% purity, Alfa Aesar) was heated to boiling temperature, and 0.7 mL of sodium citrate solution (1 wt%, >99% purity, Daejung Chemicals and Metals) was quickly added. The reaction mixture was boiled for 30 min under reflux, and the solution was cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the Au nanoparticles were then coated with SiO2 shells. To do this, 0.4 mL of the 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilate (APTMS, 96% purity, Daejung Chemicals and Metals) solution (1 mM) was added to 30 mL of the Au nanoparticle solution and stirred for 20 min. Then, 3.2 mL of sodium silicate (0.54 wt%, pH 10.2, 28–30% SiO2, Daejung Chemicals and Metals) was added to the mixture and stirred for 3 min. The mixture was then transferred to a preheated heating mantle (90 ℃) and stirred for 20 min.

In situ Raman measurements were conducted using a Raman electrochemical flow cell (Redoxme) with a sapphire window. The counter and reference electrodes were Pt wire and Ag/AgCl electrodes, respectively. For the working electrode, Cu SP and 5S/Cu SP electrodes were cut into 4 × 4 mm dimensions and attached to glassy carbon electrode using commercial silver paste and dried in oven. After that, 10 µL of Au@SiO2 was drop-casted on the catalyst and dried at room temperature. CO2-saturated 0.5 M KHCO3 electrolyte was circulated to fill the cell by a peristaltic pump. The applied potential was controlled with Ivium potentiostat. The potentials against reference were converted to RHE using this equation: ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.1976 + (0.0591 × pH). In situ Raman measurements were repeated at least twice for each electrode, and the representative spectra are shown.

DFT calculation

The DFT calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package with the projector augmented wave method and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional under the generalized gradient approximation [36–40]. A plane-wave basis set with an energy cutoff of 400 eV was used. The bulk structures of Cu and Cu-rich copper sulfides, including high chalcocite (Cch), low chalcocite (Ccl), and djurleite (Dj), were fully relaxed and used to construct surface models for Cu(111), Cch(111), Ccl(001), and Dj(002). Brillouin zone sampling was carried out using Monkhorst–Pack grids: 9×9×9 (Cu), 7×7×7 (Cch), 2×3×3 (Ccl), and 2×2×1 (Dj) for bulk calculations, and 1×1×1 for surface calculations. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was applied in the z-direction, and the surface slabs consisted of 6–7 atomic layers. Geometry optimization was conducted until the residual force was below 0.05 eV·Å–1.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CuxS/Cu electrodes

A series of CuxS/Cu (x < 2) electrodes was fabricated with controlled morphology, particle size, and composition to investigate their influence on ECO2R selectivity. Metallic Cu precursors were deposited onto MPL/CP substrates via electrodeposition, followed by solution-phase sulfidation to incorporate sulfur into the surface structure. Morphology control was achieved by varying the electrolyte composition, applied potential, deposition time, and substrate pretreatment, as presented in Table S1.

Figure 1 presents the FESEM images of Cu deposits synthesized under distinct electrodeposition conditions, along with their corresponding morphological evolution upon sulfidation. Compared with the bare MPL/CP substrate shown in Fig. S1, the deposits exhibit polygonal (Fig. 1a), cubic (Fig. 1b), and spherical (Fig. 1c) geometries. The polygonal and spherical particles measured approximately 200–300 nm, whereas the cubic ones were larger, measuring approximately 500–600 nm. Under more negative potentials, the spherical particles coalesced into a continuous film, almost entirely covering the substrate surface (Fig. 1d). The four morphologies were designated as a polygon (PG), cube (CB), sphere (SP), and film (FL), based on their distinct geometrical features.

FESEM images of electrodeposited Cu and CuSx/Cu electrodes as a function of sulfidation time: a Cu polygon, b Cu cube, c Cu sphere, and d Cu film. The scale bar represents 500 nm

Sulfidation was achieved via a solution-phase anion exchange process, driven by the disparity in solubility product constants (Ksp) among Cu-based compounds. The Ksp values of Cu2S (2.5 × 10–48) and CuS (8.0 × 10–37) are several orders of magnitude lower than those of Cu2O (2.0 × 10–15) and CuO (1.0 × 10–20), facilitating the spontaneous substitution of O2– in native copper oxides with S2– from aqueous Na2S solution [41–43]. Sulfidation was performed by immersing the as-prepared Cu electrodes in 1 mM Na2S under ambient conditions, with the reaction time varying from 1 to 60 min. Although deliberate oxidation was not employed, unavoidable surface autoxidation occurred during sample handling, leading to native oxide formation [44, 45]. Previous studies on Cu foils have reported that Cu2O layers with a thickness up to 1.2 nm form within 10 min of air exposure [46].

The FESEM images (Fig. 1 and S2–S5) revealed that sulfidation induced a consistent morphological transformation across all electrode types. Nanoscale particulates emerged within 1 min, followed by the progressive development of vertically oriented nanosheets that expanded with time. For the Cu PG and Cu SP morphologies, nanosheet structures began to emerge after 5 min, eventually expanding to ~1 μm in size and fully enveloping the catalyst surface by 20 min (Fig. S2 and S4). At 60 min, a secondary layer of finer sheets appeared atop the primary microsheets. In contrast, Cu CB and Cu FL electrodes underwent slower evolution but followed similar trends. These transformations are consistent with prior reports on CuxS/Cu systems, where prolonged sulfidation times promote roughened, sheet- or plate-like architectures [47, 48]. Sulfidized electrodes are denoted as S#/Cu, where # indicates the sulfidation duration in minutes.

Compositional and crystallographic analyses were conducted to elucidate the structure of the CuxS/Cu electrodes. EDS and XPS were employed to quantify the bulk and surface elemental compositions following sulfidation. As shown in Fig. 2a, the bulk S/Cu ratio determined by EDS (solid symbols) increased progressively with the sulfidation time, reaching a maximum of 0.64 for the S60/Cu CB sample. The order of S incorporation was Cu CB > Cu PG > Cu SP > Cu FL. A similar trend was observed in the surface S/Cu ratios extracted from the XPS measurements on the S30/Cu electrodes (hollow symbols). The surface ratios were found to be 1.6–2.6 times higher than their corresponding bulk values, which ranged from 0.35 to 0.87, indicating the significant surface enrichment of sulfur. These data suggest the formation of Cu-rich CuxS (x > 1.5) phases near the electrode interface after 30 min of sulfidation.

a S/Cu ratio of S#/Cu electrodes obtained by EDS and XPS analysis. b XRD patterns of S#Cu electrodes. c TEM image of S5/Cu SP. d–g Elemental mapping images of S5/Cu SP. h HRTEM image of S5/Cu SP

The bulk crystal structure of the S#/Cu SP electrodes was investigated using XRD. Figure 2b presents the XRD patterns of the Cu SP electrodes and the underlying MPL/CP substrate. The pristine Cu SP exhibited distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu (111), Cu (200), and Cu (220) at 44.1, 51.2, and 75.0°, respectively. As the sulfidation time increased, the intensity of these peaks decreased, but discernible peaks corresponding to the CuxS phases were not detected. To further probe the structural and compositional changes, TEM analysis was conducted on the Cu SP electrodes before and after sulfidation (Fig. 2c–h and S6). The TEM image of S5/Cu SP (Fig. 2c) revealed a spherical morphology consistent with the FESEM observations. Elemental mapping using EDS (Fig. 2d–e) confirmed a core–shell structure, with Cu concentrated at the core and S enriched at the shell, indicating the formation of a CuxS/Cu heterostructure.

Crystallinity analyses of Cu SP and S5/Cu SP were conducted using fast FFT analysis. HRTEM images of Cu SP (Fig. S6e–g) revealed lattice fringes corresponding to Cu2O and the more oxidized Cu(OH)2 phase. Metallic Cu reflections were absent, in contrast to the XRD results, likely because of the complete oxidation of metallic Cu during sample preparation or electron beam exposure during TEM analysis.

Following 5 min of sulfidation, new lattice spacings of 0.318 and 0.269 nm emerged in the S5/Cu SP sample, which were absent in pristine Cu SP. These spacings did not match the known lattice planes of Cu, Cu2O, or Cu(OH)2, and are attributed to newly formed CuxS domains. However, precise phase assignment remained ambiguous owing to the structural complexity of the Cu–S compounds. According to the Cu–S phase diagram, five Cu-rich sulfide phases are thermodynamically stable at ambient temperature: covellite (CuS), anilite (Cu7S4), digenite (Cu9S5), djurleite (Cu31S16), and chalcocite (Cu2S) [49–51]. Comparison with the reported crystallographic parameters and representative lattice spacings of these five candidate phases shows that the experimentally observed spacings are compatible with multiple Cu–S phases, suggesting that the sulfided Cu electrode may comprise a mixture of Cu–S phases rather than a single well-defined phase (Table S2).

The evolution of the electronic structure of the S#/Cu SP electrodes with increasing sulfur content was analyzed using XPS. As shown in Fig. 3a, the Cu LMM Auger spectra of pristine Cu SP exhibits distinct peaks corresponding to Cu0 (568.3 eV) and Cu+ (569.9 eV) [22]. Following 1 min of sulfidation, the Cu0 peak disappeared, whereas the Cu+ feature became more prominent. These spectral features remained relatively unchanged up to 30 min of sulfidation. However, at 60 min, the Cu+ signal diminished, and a new Cu2+ peak emerged at 569.0 eV, suggesting a transition toward a more sulfur-rich CuxS surface. The corresponding increase in the S/Cu ratio with the sulfidation time, reaching 0.73 at 60 min (Fig. S7a), further supports this interpretation.

a Cu LMM Auger spectra, b O 1 s spectra, and c S 2p spectra of S#/Cu SP electrodes as a function of sulfidation time

The O 1 s spectra as a function of the sulfidation duration are summarized in Fig. 3b. The deconvolution of the O 1 s region for Cu SP (Fig. S7b) revealed contributions from the Cu2O, surface-adsorbed OH–, and H2O [44, 45]. As the sulfidation time increased, the Cu2O peak progressively weakened and was almost absent by 30 min, whereas the relative intensities of the OH– and H2O peaks remained stable. By 60 min, the O 1 s signal was markedly attenuated, consistent with the replacement of oxygen species by sulfur during sulfidation. This substitution underlies the observed increase in the surface hydrophobicity of copper sulfides [28]. In contrast, the S 2p spectra exhibited a gradual increase in intensity with the sulfidation time. Peaks at 162.2 and 163.5 eV, corresponding to S2– 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, respectively, appeared after just 1 min of sulfidation and intensified over 30 min without significant spectral shifts. At 60 min, the S 2p envelope broadened, reflecting a more diverse sulfur environment. Combined with the Cu LMM results, this spectral evolution suggests a phase transition from Cu-rich CuxS (1 < x < 2) to stoichiometric CuS [52].

ECO2R performance evaluation in MEA cell

The electrocatalytic CO2 reduction was investigated in a catholyte-free MEA system using Cu and CuxS/Cu electrodes (Fig. S8). As shown in Fig. 4a, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were obtained for pristine and sulfidized Cu SP electrodes under humidified N2 and CO2 flows at the cathode. The non-sulfidized electrode exhibited higher current densities under N2 compared with CO2, whereas the sulfidized counterpart delivered superior performance in the presence of CO2, suggesting enhanced catalytic activity toward ECO2 upon sulfidation.

ECO2R performance evaluation in MEA-type cell for various Cu and S#/Cu electrodes. 0.1 M KOH was supplied to the anode, while humidified CO2 was fed to the cathode. a LSV curves for Cu SP and S5/Cu SP under humidified CO2 (N2 shown for comparison). b PCD for C2H4 of S#/Cu SP as a function of sulfidation time. c Variation in FE for gas products from various Cu and S5/Cu electrodes at 3.2 Vcell. d Effect of 5-min sulfidation on C2H4 FE and PCD for each Cu electrode at 3.2 V. e PCD for C2H4 as a function of surface S/Cu ratio at 3.2 V, obtained from the XPS measurements shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S7a

Subsequent gas product analyses were conducted for the Cu SP electrode under humidified CO2 at fixed cell voltages ranging from 2.4 to 3.4 V (Fig. S9 and S10). At voltages below 3.0 V, where the current density remained under 100 mA/cm2, CO was the predominant product. In contrast, C2H4 emerged as the major product above 3.0 V. The PCD for C2H4 formation as a function of sulfidation time is shown in Fig. 4b. Across the applied voltage range, the C2H4 PCD exhibited volcano-type dependence on the sulfidation duration, with enhancements observed even at short sulfidation times (1–5 min). Importantly, this sulfidation-induced enhancement in C2H4 selectivity was not exclusive to the spherical Cu morphology but was consistently observed across various electrode architectures (Fig. S10–S13).

Figure 4c and d compares the FE and PCD enhancements across Cu electrodes with different morphologies after 5 min of sulfidation, evaluated at a constant cell voltage of 3.2 V. Although the degree of enhancement varied depending on electrode morphology, all S5/Cu variants exhibited higher C2H4 FEs relative to their non-sulfidized counterparts. The corresponding C2H4 PCD also improved, with increases ranging from 9.2 to 28.3 mA/cm2. Figure 4e plots the C2H4 PCD of the S#/Cu SP electrodes at 3.2 V as a function of the surface S/Cu ratio. Even a minor increase in S/Cu from 0 to 0.12 elevated the PCD from 58.2 to 85.8 mA/cm2. The maximum value of 102.5 mA/cm2 was reached at an S/Cu ratio of 0.33, beyond which performance declined, exhibiting a volcano-type dependence. This represents a 1.76-fold enhancement in C2H4 PCD compared with pure Cu (58.2 mA/cm2). This performance is compared with previously reported copper sulfide-based ECO2R catalysts in Table S3, which highlights the limited prior exploration of C2H4 production from copper sulfide catalysts, particularly in MEA configurations. Notably, the PCD declined further to ~73 mA/cm2 where the surface S/Cu ratio reached 0.74, which could be related to the surface converting toward more sulfided CuS, as shown by XPS (Fig. 3a).

To evaluate the long-term stability of ECO2R, a large-area S5/Cu SP electrode (3.0 × 3.0 cm2) was fabricated and tested in an MEA-type electrolyzer. Figure 5a and b present the current density profile and corresponding FEs of gas products over time. Initially, the FE for C2H4 was 38.8%, slightly lower than that obtained with small-area electrodes, despite a comparable current density of ~125 mA/cm2. Over the first hour, the current density gradually increased, consistent with a slight early-stage activation. A sudden drop from 168 to 160 mA/cm2 was observed around the 78-min mark, followed by minor fluctuations. Despite these fluctuations, the current density remained stable for approximately two hours, during which the C2H4 FE rose to a peak of 51.0%. Thereafter, the current density and C2H4 selectivity declined steadily, with the current reaching 91 mA/cm2, which is approximately 72.8% of its initial value, after 10 h, while the FE for CO increased.

a Current transient of long-term ECO2R evaluation in MEA cell with S5/Cu SP (3.0 × 3.0 cm2) at cell voltage of 3.2 Vcell. b FE for gas products during long-term ECO2R evaluation corresponding to (a). c S/Cu ratio of pristine S5/Cu SP and S5/Cu SP after 600 min of MEA operation at 3.2 Vcell. The ratios were determined by ICP-QQQ. For the post-reaction sample, the analysis was performed on the catalyst layer that remained adhered to the membrane after cell disassembly

To further investigate the origin of this degradation, the catalyst layer was examined after long-term operation. However, disassembly revealed that most of the catalyst had transferred onto the membrane, leaving few detectable CuxS deposits on the C/CP surface. This limited the utility of conventional techniques such as XRD, XPS, and SEM (Fig. S14 and S15). Instead, the residual catalyst was analyzed by dissolving the entire post-reaction MEA and performing inductively coupled plasma triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (ICP-QQQ). The S/Cu atomic ratio, which was 0.064 in the pristine S5/Cu SP, decreased to 0.032 after 600 min of operation (Fig. 5c), reflecting sulfur depletion during MEA operation.

The decline in C2H4 FE after ~2 h may be associated with the continued decrease in S/Cu ratio over the course of the test. However, an additional contributing factor cannot be ruled out. Upon disassembly of the cell, wetting was found to extend beyond the catalyst layer to the bare carbon regions of the cathode, which was not evident in short-duration evaluations. Such wetting can impede gaseous CO2 transport, and since these carbon regions lack the CuxS catalyst, any reaction occurring there would not contribute productively to C2H4 formation. Together with the fluctuation observed in current density, this raises the possibility that flooding-related issues began to develop from around this point onward, potentially compounding the effect of sulfur loss. We do not assign a specific mechanistic pathway connecting these observations, as the present data do not allow us to distinguish the relative contribution of each factor.

To gain further mechanistic insight into the sulfur-driven enhancement in C2H4 production, we next examine the reaction intermediates via in situ Raman spectroscopy and the electronic effect of sulfur incorporation via DFT calculations.

In situ surface characterization via Raman spectroscopy

In situ Raman experiments were performed on pristine Cu and S5/Cu SP in CO2-saturated 0.5 M KHCO3 to investigate the effect of sulfidation on C2H4 production (Fig. 6a–c and S16). A distinct transition in CO adsorption configurations was observed. While pristine Cu is dominated by top-bonded CO (COtop, at approximately 2080 cm–1), S5/Cu SP exhibits a complete shift to bridge-bonded CO (CObridge, at approximately 1900 cm–1). This shift is significant as CObridge provides a more favorable geometric environment for C–C coupling than isolated COtop species [53–56].

In situ Raman spectra of S5/Cu SP measured in the wavenumber ranges of a 200–1100 cm–1, b 1200–2200 cm–1, and c 2750–3600 cm–1. d Peak intensity ratio of *CH2CHO and *CObridge on Cu SP and S5/Cu SP catalysts at different potentials. e Raman spectra of Cu SP and S5/Cu SP without electrolyte. f Enlarged view of in situ Raman spectra of S5/Cu SP in the wavenumber region of 200–450 cm–1

The enhanced activity on the sulfidized surface is supported by the presence of the CH2CHO intermediate at approximately 520 cm–1. The in situ Raman spectra revealed a significantly higher CH2CHO to CObridge intensity ratio on S5/Cu SP compared to pristine Cu SP, exhibiting a monotonic increase as a function of the applied cathodic potential (Fig. 6d). This indicates that the C2 intermediate generated from the C–C coupling of adsorbed CO is more abundant on the sulfidized Cu catalyst, which is consistent with the electrochemical trends observed in MEA measurements showing higher partial current density and FE towards C2H4 production (Fig. 4b).

The structural integrity of the catalyst was further monitored by tracking the Cu–S vibration during CO2 reduction (Fig. 6e and f). The Cu–S vibration peak at 287 cm–1 was identified as a primary feature of copper sulfides. Notably, while the Cu–CO peak exhibited a characteristic redshift at more negative potentials, the Cu–S peak showed no noticeable shift, allowing for its clear distinction from the Cu–CO signal (Fig. 6f). Although a gradual decrease in peak intensity was observed under increasing cathodic bias, the Cu–S vibration remained discernible across the examined potential range.

These observations suggest that while the catalyst surface undergoes a degree of restructuring and sulfur loss under high cathodic bias, the Cu–S coordination is maintained to a discernible extent during the ECO2R. This is consistent with the post-reaction analysis, which revealed that although the S/Cu ratio decreased after long-term MEA operation, a substantial amount of sulfur remained anchored within the catalyst matrix (Fig. 5c).

Theoretical insights into CO dimerization and catalyst stability

To elucidate the origin of enhanced C–C coupling upon sulfidation, DFT calculations were performed on three Cu-rich copper sulfide phases: Cu2S with the high-temperature chalcocite structure (Cch), Cu2S in its low-temperature form (Ccl), and Cu31S16 with the djurleite structure (Dj), as shown in Fig. S17. The experimental characterization indicated Cu-rich CuxS domains without resolving a single dominant crystalline phase. In this regard, these structures were employed as representative models with distinct local Cu–S coordination environments.

To assess surface reactivity, the adsorption energies of key C2H4-related intermediates (*CO, *2CO, and *OCCO) were computed. The most favorable *CO binding sites were first identified through a systematic screening process (Fig. S18 and S19), and subsequent *2CO and *OCCO adsorptions were evaluated locally around those sites to account for the influence of neighboring Cu and S atoms. All sulfide surfaces exhibited stronger *OCCO binding relative to metallic Cu (–1.27 eV), with Cu2S (Cch) at –3.93 eV, Cu2S (Ccl) at –1.69 eV, and Cu31S16 (Dj) at –1.73 eV, as shown in Fig. 7a. These results indicate that sulfur incorporation stabilizes *OCCO adsorption across the examined sulfide structure. Moreover, the bulk formation energies were –0.15 eV per atom for Dj, –0.14 eV per atom for Ccl, and –0.02 eV per atom for Cch (Fig. 7b), indicating greater bulk thermodynamic stability of Dj and Ccl relative to Cch. The free-energy change from *2CO to *OCCO was strongly dependent on the sulfide structure, decreasing from 1.69 eV on metallic Cu to 0.12 eV on Cch and 1.21 eV on Dj, while Ccl exhibited a higher value of 2.62 eV (Fig. 7c and S20). In particular, the favorable OCCO formation energetics of Cch despite its lower bulk stability indicate that bulk thermodynamic stability and local catalytic activity are not necessarily correlated, highlighting the strong sensitivity of C–C coupling to the local coordination environment. The relaxed structures further illustrate the distinct local coordination environments associated with *OCCO stabilization on Cu and Dj (Fig. 7d). Together, these results indicate that Cu–S coordination modifies the energetics of C–C coupling in a strongly structure-dependent manner. These findings align with experimental observations and provide a mechanistic rationale for the enhanced ethylene selectivity achieved under MEA operating conditions, supporting the electronic/coordination effect of sulfur as a meaningful contributor to the experimentally observed enhancement. However, the present static slab calculations cannot determine the prevalence of this favorable Cu–S coordination environment on the dynamically reconstructed and structurally heterogeneous catalyst surface. Thus, while the results demonstrate that this local environment can promote *OCCO formation if present, its frequency and quantitative contribution to the overall catalytic activity remain unresolved and require statistical atomistic sampling combined with operando structural characterization.

a Adsorption energies of key intermediates (*CO, *2CO, *OCCO) on different catalyst surfaces: metallic Cu, Cch, Ccl, and Dj. b Normalized formation energy of Cu-rich CuxS. c Free energy diagrams for C–C coupling pathways on Cu and Dj surfaces. d Side view of relaxed atomic structures of each intermediate on the Cu and Dj surface

Overall, these ECO2R results demonstrate that sulfidation of the Cu surface substantially enhances the C2H4 FE in the MEA cell. This sulfur effect, however, is accompanied by concurrent changes in active surface area, morphology, and surface hydrophobicity (e.g., the reduced N2 current relative to CO2 for the sulfidized electrode in Fig. 4a, potentially reflecting suppressed HER [28]). We were unable to separate the relative contributions of these factors from the electronic effect captured by DFT within the present experimental design, and quantitatively resolving their individual contributions remains an important direction for future work.

Conclusions

This study elucidated the underexplored role of sulfur incorporation in tuning the C–C coupling activity of copper-based catalysts under MEA operation. Cu-rich CuxS/Cu electrodes significantly enhance C2H4 FE and PCD in MEA configurations. For Cu SP, a surface S/Cu ratio of 0.33 yielded a C2H4 PCD of 102.5 mA/cm2 at 3.2 Vcell. This corresponds to a 1.76-fold enhancement over pure Cu (58.2 mA/cm2) and follows a volcano-type trend. In situ Raman spectroscopy confirmed that this enhancement originates from the sulfur-induced stabilization of bridge-bonded CO configurations, which is associated with an increased abundance of the key CH2CHO intermediate. Although a gradual decrease in sulfur content was observed under high cathodic bias, the persistence of Cu–S coordination throughout the reaction, as evidenced by both in situ Raman and post-reaction analysis, confirms that the sulfide-derived interface remains active during peak performance. Combined with DFT calculations, the results revealed that Cu–S coordination environments stabilize *OCCO intermediates, effectively facilitating C–C bond formation. The convergence of computational and experimental results highlights surface sulfur incorporation as a key structural motif that facilitates C2H4 selectivity under MEA operating conditions in ECO2R.
