On a problem of Caro on the ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3} -Ramsey number of forests
José D. Alvarado, Lucas Colucci, Roberto Parente

Abstract
Let k k be a positive integer and let G G be a graph. The zero-sum Ramsey number R ⁡ ( G , ℤ k ) R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k}) is the least integer N N (if it exists) such that for every edge-coloring χ : E ⁡ ( K N ) → ℤ k \chi\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,E(K_{N})\,\rightarrow\,\mathbb{Z}_{k} one can find a copy of G G in K N K_{N} such that ∑ e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) χ ⁡ ( e ) = 0 \sum_{e\,\in\,E(G)}{\chi(e)}\,=\,0 . In 2019, Caro made a conjecture about the ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3} -Ramsey number of trees. In this paper, we settle this conjecture, fixing an incorrect case, and extend the result to forests. Namely, we show that R ⁡ ( F , ℤ 3 ) = { n + 2 , if F is 1 ​ ( mod ​ 3 ) regular or a star; n + 1 , if 3 ∤ d ⁡ ( v ) for every v ∈ V ⁡ ( F ) or F has exactly one vertex of degree 0 ​ ( mod ​ 3 ) and all others are 1 ​ ( mod ​ 3 ) , and F is not 1 ​ ( mod ​ 3 ) regular or a star; n , otherwise. R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\begin{cases}n+2,&\text{if $F$ is $1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$ regular or a star;}\\ n+1,&\text{if $3\nmid d(v)$ for every $v\in V(F)$ or $F$ has exactly one}\\ &\text{vertex of degree $0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$ and all others are $1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$,}\\ &\text{and $F$ is not $1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$ regular or a star;}\\ n,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} where F F is any forest on n n vertices with 3 | e ⁡ ( F ) 3\mid e(F) and no isolated vertices.



1
Introduction and main result

The celebrated Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem
[
11
]
states that any sequence of
2m-1
elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
contains a subsequence of
m
elements whose sum is zero.

Motivated by this result, Bialostocki and Dierker
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
established a connection between this theorem and Ramsey theory. Specifically, they considered colors as elements of a finite group and, instead of seeking monochromatic substructures, they focused on identifying “zero-sum substructures”. From here on, we will restrict our attention to finite cyclic groups.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
G
be a graph with
k\mid e(G)
. The
zero-sum Ramsey number

R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k})
is the smallest integer
N
such that, for every edge-coloring
\chi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E(K_{N})\to\mathbb{Z}_{k}
, there exists a copy of
G
in
K_{N}
satisfying
\sum_{e\in E(G)}\chi(e)=0
. We say that such a copy of
G
has zero-sum. It is simple to see that
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k})
exists if, and only if,
k\mid e(G)
.

Recall that the
k
-colored Ramsey number
R_{k}(G)
is the minimum
N
such that every
k
-coloring of the edges of
K_{N}
has a monochromatic copy of
G
. In particular,
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k})\leq R_{k}(G)
.

For a comprehensive review of zero-sum Ramsey problems, we recommend the survey of Caro
[
6
]
.
The case
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is now very well understood. Indeed, due to the work of Caro
[
6
]
, extending on earlier work of Alon and Caro
[
1
]
,
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
was completely determined.

Despite the complete characterization on the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the next natural cyclic group,
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, remains less understood.
In 2019, Caro
[
9
]
proposed the following conjecture for the case of trees:

Conjecture 1
.

Let
T
be a tree on
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
vertices. Then,

R(T,\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\begin{cases}n+2,&\text{if }T\text{ is the star }K_{1,n-1}\\
n+1,&\text{if }T\text{ has no vertex of degree }0\pmod{3}\\
n,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Very recently, Y. Caro and X. Mifsud
[
10
]
made significant progress on this problem. This conjecture serves as the main motivation for our work, where, after fixing an incorrect case of the original statement, namely, when the tree contains exactly one vertex of degree congruent to
0
modulo
3
and the degree of all other vertices of
T
are congruent to
0
modulo
3
, we determine the exact value of
R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{3})
when
F
is any forest.

Thus, the main result of the paper is the following.

Theorem 2
.

Let
F
be a forest on
n
vertices with
3\mid e(F)
and without isolated vertices. Then,

R(F,\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\begin{cases}n+2,&\text{if $F$ is $1\pmod{3}$ regular or a star;}\\
n+1,&\text{if $3\nmid d(v)$ for every $v\in V(F)$ or $F$ has exactly one}\\
&\text{vertex of degree $0\pmod{3}$ and all others are $1\pmod{3}$,}\\
&\text{and $F$ is not $1\pmod{3}$ regular or a star;}\\
n,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 3
.

The condition that
F
does not have isolated vertices in the theorem is added for convenience only, as it is simple to see that, if
v\notin V(G)
, then
R(G\cup\{v\},\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\max\{R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{3}),|V(G)|+1\}
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce relevant definitions, notation, and useful facts that will be used throughout the paper. In Section
3
, we establish the lower bounds for our main result. Section
4
forms the core of the paper, where we derive the upper bounds for our main result. This section is divided into several subsections, as outlined in Table 1. Finally, in Section
5
, we conclude by discussing some related open problems.

Lemma 7
.

Let
G
be a graph such that
3|e(G)
. Then, a coloring
{\chi\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,E(G)\,\rightarrow\,\mathbb{Z}_{3}}
is such that
G
is zero-sum if, and only if,
|\chi^{-1}(0)|\,\equiv\,|\chi^{-1}(1)|\,\equiv\,|\chi^{-1}(2)|\,\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

For
i\,\in\,\{0,1,2\}
, let
e_{i}
denote
|\chi^{-1}(i)|
. We have
e_{0}+e_{1}+e_{2}\,=\,e(G)\,\equiv\ 0\,\pmod{3}
.

If
e_{0}\equiv e_{1}\equiv e_{2}\pmod{3}
, then the sum of the colors of the edges of
G
is
0\cdot e_{0}+1\cdot e_{1}+2\cdot e_{2}\equiv(0+1+2)e_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. On the other hand, suppose that the sum of the edges of
G
is zero, i.e.,
0\cdot e_{0}+1\cdot e_{1}+2\cdot e_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, or, equivalently,
e_{1}\equiv e_{2}\pmod{3}
. Plugging this into
e_{0}+e_{1}+e_{2}\equiv 0
, we get
e_{0}+2\cdot e_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, i.e.,
e_{0}\equiv e_{2}\pmod{3}
.

∎

Lemma 8
.

Let

,

,

be elements of

Proof.

By contradiction, assume that this is not the case. As

∎

Lower bounds

Clearly,
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{k})\geq n
for every graph

n
vertices and every

. It remains to show lower bounds for the cases where

3.1
Type
1

If
3\nmid d(v)
for every
v\in V(F)
, a

coloring does not contain a zero sum copy of
F
. Indeed, in any embedding of
F
in this colored

, a vertex

will be assigned to the class
1
, giving exactly
d(v)\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
edges of color

0

2
. By Lemma

, this copy of

cannot be zero-sum.

On the other hand, if

is a forest where exactly one of the vertices has degree
0\pmod{3}
and the other vertices have degree

, consider a

coloring of

. There are the following
4
possibilities of embedding of

in this

: the two vertices of class

may correspond to two vertices of degree
1\pmod{3}
in

or one vertex of degree

and one of degree

; and the two vertices embedded in the class

might be joined or not. In each of those cases, it is immediate to check that the number of edges of color

and color

are not congruent modulo

: indeed, if the vertices are joined, there is

in

and

, where

4.1

In this subsection, we prove the upper bound for the type

Lemma 11
.

Let
m\geq 5
be a positive integer, and suppose

is colored in the following way: for every vertex
v\in V(K_{m})
, there are at most two colors on edges incident to
v
, and one of the colors that appear in those edges appears at most once (i.e., the pattern
aabb
is forbidden at every vertex). Then, the coloring follows one of the following patterns:

(i)

There is a color
a
such that
G[a]
is the complement of a matching (i.e., the edges colored with the two other colors span a matching)

(ii)

There is a subgraph
K_{m-1}
in
K_{m}
which is monochromatic.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\{a,b,c\}
. For a vertex
v
, let us call the
dominant color
of
v
the only color that appears more than once in the incident edges to
v
(and hence does not appear in at most one incident edge to
v
).

It is clear that if every vertex is monochromatic, then the whole graph must be monochromatic and the result follows. We may assume, then, that there is a bichromatic vertex. Let
u
be a vertex that has all but one edge of color
a
, and the only edge of color
b
incident to
u
is
uv
. Let
A
be the set of remaining vertices, i.e.,
A=V(K_{m})-\{u,v\}
.

First, assuming that
b
is not the dominant color of
v
, then all the edges joining
v
to the vertices in
A
have the same color, say,
\alpha
(which is either
a
or
c
). If
\alpha=a
, then each vertex of
A
has two neighbors of color
a
, which means that
a
is the dominant color of them. This implies that the degree of each of those vertices in colors
b
and
c
(together) is at most one, which implies that
G[a]
is the complement of a matching. On the other hand, if
\alpha=c
, then each vertex of
A
has one edge of color
a
and one of color
c
, which means that every other edge incident to this vertex has a constant color (either
a
or
c
). It follows that
A
is monochromatic, and that either
A\cup\{u\}
or
A\cup\{v\}
is a monochromatic

.

On the other hand, if
b
is the dominant color of
v
, then all vertices in
A
except possibly one are joined to
v
with an edge of color
b
. Every vertex of this set

of at least
m-3
vertices has an edge of color
a
and one edge of color
b
incident to it. As in the paragraph above, this implies that this set spans a monochromatic complete graph on
|A^{\prime}|
vertices. If
|A^{\prime}|=n-2
, then the result follows as in the previous paragraph. If
|A^{\prime}|=n-3
, let

be the remaining vertex. All the edges joining

to the vertices of

must have the color of the edges in

(as the vertices in

already have both colors appearing on its edges), which implies again that

is monochromatic and the result follows as before.
∎

In this section, we consider forests with

4.4
Colorings without alternating
C_{4}

In this section, we deal with colorings that do not contain any alternating four cycle. Perhaps surprisingly, this condition imposes a very symmetric structure to the coloring, as shown in the following lemma. This allows us to find zero-sum copies of forests in such colorings easily, which we do in Proposition
17
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and suppose
\chi
is a coloring of a

with the following property: in every

, the sums of the color of edges of the two matchings are equal, i.e., the coloring does not have any alternating

. Then,

is a CC coloring.

Proof.

The result is readily checked if

. Assume from now on that

, and let
\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\{a,b,c\}
.

By pigeonhole principle, there are three vertices
u
,
v
and

such that
\chi(uw)=\chi(vw)
. Let us assume that
\chi(uw)=\chi(vw)=a
, without loss of generality. For every other vertex

in the graph, by considering the cycle
xvwu
, it follows that
\chi(xu)=\chi(xv)
. That is,

is partitioned in three sets

,
S_{b}
and
S_{c}
with the property that

if
\chi(yu)=\chi(yv)=\alpha
. Now, the colors of every remaining edge is determined by the color of the edge

, by considering the cycle

, where
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
are vertices. It is simple to check that the resulting coloring will indeed have the desired pattern.

∎

The following proposition shows how to find zero sum copies of forests in CC colorings:

Proposition 16
.

Let

be

-colored with a

Proof.

The result is immediate if

. Assume now that

, and let

be a forest and assume it is embedded in a

,

and

to a class

(indices taken modulo

+d(u)

and a vertex

Assume first that

,

and

now in the following way: if there are at least two nonempty classes, say,

and

, one of them must have size at least two, say,

and

get in class

and
v_{1}
in class

and

or

and

to get a zero sum copy of

Now, assume that

does not have vertices of all possible degrees modulo

. By the condition in the statement,

has at least two vertices of degree

modulo

and

. Moreover, every nonempty forest has at least two vertices of degree

and

. If the coloring has at least two classes with at least two vertices in each class, say

and

and

are mapped to

and

∎

Proposition 17

in each color is congruent to
2
modulo
3
).

∎

4.6.3
Three connected components

The forests with
\alpha_{s}(F)=1
that consist of three connected components are the disjoint union of three stars. We compute their Ramsey number in the following proposition.

Proposition 34
.

For
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
,
d_{2}
positive integers, let
S(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
denote the vertex disjoint union of a star on
d_{1}
edges, a star on
d_{2}
edges, and a star on
d_{2}
edges. If
3\mid d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}
, then

R(S(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}),\mathbb{Z}_{3})=\begin{cases}d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+5,&\text{if $a\equiv b\equiv c\equiv 1\pmod{3}$;}\\
d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+4,&\text{if $a\equiv b\equiv c\equiv 2\pmod{3}$;}\\
d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+3,&\text{if $a\equiv b\equiv c\equiv 0$}\\
&\text{or $(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\equiv(0,1,2)\pmod{3}$.}\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

The first case follows from Proposition
12
, and the second follows from Proposition
13
.

If
d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\equiv d_{3}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, given a coloring of a
K_{d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+3}
, we first find a zero-sum copy of
S_{d_{3}}
in it, since
R(S_{d_{3}},\mathbb{Z}_{3})=d_{3}+3\leq d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+3
. In the remaining
d_{1}+d_{2}+2
vertices, we can find a zero-sum copy of
S(d_{1},d_{2})
, by Proposition
33
.

On the other hand, if, say,
d_{1}\equiv 0
,
d_{2}\equiv 1
,
d_{3}\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, the fact that
d_{1}\geq 3
allows us apply Lemma
28
to reduce the problem to the case
d_{3}=2
. In this case, let
p_{1}
be the parent of degree
0\pmod{3}
,
p_{2}
be the parent of degree
1
, and
p_{3}
be the parent of degree
2\pmod{3}
. Then, we are done by Proposition
27
considering the switching structures
(p_{1},l_{1},l_{2},p_{2})
and
(l_{3},p_{3})_{2}
.
∎

5
Open questions

There are many natural open questions that follow the results of this paper. It is natural to ask whether one can compute the
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-Ramsey number for all graphs, as it was done for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

[
6
]
. We note that this is far from being solved, as, for instance, the exact value of
R(K_{n},\mathbb{Z}_{3})
is not know if
n\equiv 7\pmod{9}

[
8
]
(it is known to be either
n+3
or
n+4
). On the other hand, it would be nice to have a sharp universal upper bound as a function of
n
. It is conjectured that
R(G,\mathbb{Z}_{3})\leq n+8
for every graph on
n
vertices with
3|e(G)
, with equality if and only if
G
is a triangle
[
10
]
.

Another natural future direction would be to compute the zero-sum Ramsey numbers for trees (or forests) for other groups. We believe that it might be instructive to start with
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where
p
is a prime (perhaps it would be illuminating to study the case
p=5
first). Although some isolated results are known
[
2
]
, the problem is still quite open for those groups.
