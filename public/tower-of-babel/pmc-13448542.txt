Barriers and facilitators to the implementation of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health in health services: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Julianne Machado Bonfim, Andreia Thayna Felipe Do Nascimento, Aline Braga Galvão Silveira Fernandes, Gentil Gomes Da Fonseca Filho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) represents a major milestone in understanding functioning and disability and is widely used in clinical practice, management and health research. Despite its relevance, implementation of the ICF remains inconsistent across health services and care settings. Evidence on barriers and facilitators to implementation remains fragmented, limiting the development of effective, context-sensitive implementation strategies. This scoping review aims to map barriers and facilitators influencing ICF implementation in health services and to identify implementation strategies reported in the literature.Methods and analysisThis scoping review protocol will follow the methodological recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). Qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods primary studies describing the implementation of the ICF in health services will be included with no language restrictions, published since 2001. Searches will be conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, LILACS, SciELO and PEDro databases. Studies will be selected in two stages by two independent reviewers using the Rayyan platform. Disagreements will be resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer. Data will be extracted using a standardised form. Barriers and facilitators will be mapped and categorised according to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), enabling a theory-informed synthesis of implementation determinants across settings. Findings will be summarised using descriptive statistics and narrative synthesis, including implementation contexts, strategies, barriers and facilitators.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required because this review will use data from publicly available published studies and will not involve human participants. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication, conference presentation and knowledge translation activities targeting researchers, healthcare professionals, service managers and policymakers.Trial registration number10.17605/OSF.IO/VRQPD.

Introduction

Endorsed by the WHO in 2001, the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) represented a milestone in understanding the health–disease process by moving beyond a strictly biomedical perspective focused on pathology and its consequences.1 Grounded in a biopsychosocial approach, the ICF conceptualises functioning as the result of the dynamic interaction among health conditions, activities, participation and contextual factors, including environmental and personal factors, thereby promoting a more comprehensive understanding of individuals and their health experiences.2 3 Widely recognised as a paradigm shift in the conceptualisation of health and disability, the ICF shifts the focus from disease to human functioning across different life contexts and provides a standardised and universal language for describing functioning and disability, facilitating communication among healthcare professionals, researchers, policymakers and managers. Furthermore, its application in clinical practice, education, rehabilitation and public policy has been widely advocated for supporting person-centred care, interdisciplinary decision-making and strategies aimed at enhancing functioning, social participation and the consideration of environmental influences on health.4–6

Over the past two decades, the use of the ICF has expanded substantially, reflecting the global transition to approaches centred on functioning and aligned with the biopsychosocial model, as evidenced by the increase in scientific production, strong participation of high-income countries and the predominance of topics such as rehabilitation, participation and quality of life, alongside more recent applications in ageing, epidemiology and public health.7 In clinical practice and healthcare management, the ICF has demonstrated substantial value by providing a standardised framework for describing and measuring functioning, enabling complex health conditions to be translated into comparable and clinically meaningful information. Its use facilitates interdisciplinary communication, supports clinical decision-making, promotes consistent outcome reporting and contributes to the organisation and evaluation of rehabilitation services and health systems through more integrated and person-centred approaches.4 5 8 Furthermore, the ICF has increasingly been incorporated into clinical practice, rehabilitation, health professional education, research, policy development and health information systems, contributing to person-centred care, outcome evaluation and multidisciplinary healthcare delivery.5 9 10

Evidence from different countries indicates that the implementation of the ICF has expanded considerably over recent decades, although with varying levels of integration across healthcare systems. In Europe, substantial efforts have been made to support the system-wide implementation of the ICF in rehabilitation services, including its use in clinical practice, outcome reporting, quality management, research and policy development.11 Similarly, China has undertaken national initiatives to incorporate ICF-based functioning information into routine clinical practice and health information systems, aiming to standardise the reporting of functioning across the continuum of care.12 In addition, global initiatives involving countries such as Brazil, Poland, Russia and Malawi have demonstrated the applicability of ICF-based approaches in clinical and rehabilitation settings and highlighted their contribution to multidisciplinary communication, patient-centred care and the identification of barriers and facilitators affecting functioning.13

Despite these advances, the implementation of the ICF remains heterogeneous across healthcare settings and countries. Evidence from South Africa suggests that the use of the ICF in routine clinical practice is still limited, despite recognition of its potential to improve rehabilitation service delivery and interdisciplinary communication.14 Furthermore, studies conducted in different regions have identified barriers such as insufficient professional training, lack of knowledge, limited organisational support, time constraints and difficulties integrating the ICF into existing clinical workflows.13 14 Additional challenges include the predominance of disease-oriented models of care, the complexity of ICF operationalisation, difficulties in the use of qualifiers and the limited integration of functioning information into routine healthcare processes.15 16

From an implementation perspective, these challenges reflect determinants operating at multiple levels, including healthcare professionals, organisational environments, implementation processes and broader health system characteristics. Understanding how these factors influence the adoption, integration and sustainability of ICF-based practices is essential to support functioning-oriented healthcare and guide the development of effective implementation strategies.5 10

Although previous studies have described experiences with the application of the ICF in rehabilitation, education, policy development and healthcare delivery, evidence regarding the factors that facilitate or hinder its implementation remains dispersed across different contexts and populations. Consequently, there is limited understanding of how barriers and facilitators interact across healthcare settings and influence the successful incorporation of the ICF into routine practice.

Given the breadth and heterogeneity of the available literature, a comprehensive mapping of the evidence is needed to identify implementation determinants and knowledge gaps related to the use of the ICF in health services. Such evidence may inform policy development, support service planning, guide implementation efforts and strengthen integrated practices focused on functioning and participation.

Therefore, this study aims to map barriers and facilitators to implementing ICF in health services, thereby advancing applied knowledge and supporting improvements in clinical, educational and managerial practices in rehabilitation and public health.

Methodology

A scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the methodological recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI),17 which guide this type of study. The process will follow the proposed steps: definition of the research question, establishment of eligibility criteria, database search, selection of studies, extraction and analysis of information and presentation of results, ensuring methodological rigour and comprehensiveness in the synthesis of evidence on the implementation of the ICF in health services. This protocol was developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines.18 The conduct and reporting of the scoping review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).19 Any relevant changes to this protocol after registration will be duly documented on the platform, including a description of the changes, the date and the corresponding justification.

Research question

The question is structured according to the PCC (population–concept–context).

P (population)Professionals, users of health services and health systemsC (concept)Barriers and facilitators to the implementation of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and HealthC (context)Implementation in health services at different levels of care

For the purposes of this review, barriers will be defined as any individual, organisational, contextual, process-related or system-level factor that hinders, delays, limits or prevents the implementation of the ICF in healthcare settings. Facilitators will be defined as factors that support, enable, promote or sustain the implementation of the ICF in healthcare settings. These determinants may be reported by healthcare professionals, service users, managers, policymakers or other stakeholders involved in implementation processes.

The identification and interpretation of these determinants will be informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR V.2.0), which provides a comprehensive framework for understanding factors that influence implementation across multiple levels of healthcare systems.20

Question

What barriers and facilitators are described for the implementation of the ICF in health services, across professional, user and health system perspectives?

Eligibility criteria

Primary studies published between 2001 and 2026 will be included, using qualitative, experimental, quasi-experimental, preintervention and postintervention approaches and implementation studies, without language restrictions, which describe the implementation of the ICF in any level of healthcare context.

Health services will include, but will not be limited to, hospitals, rehabilitation centres, primary healthcare services, outpatient clinics, community-based services, long-term care facilities, nursing homes, emergency services and other healthcare settings in which the ICF has been implemented in routine practice, service organisation or health system processes.

For the purpose of this review, implementation of the ICF will be defined as the systematic integration and use of the ICF framework, components or coding structure into routine clinical practice, health service organisation or health system processes.

This includes, but is not limited to the use of ICF in clinical assessment and documentation, incorporation into health records or information systems, application in care planning or multidisciplinary communication and its use to inform service organisation or policy.

Studies that refer to the ICF exclusively as a conceptual framework, without evidence of practical application or integration into health services, will be excluded.

Studies will also be excluded if they focus exclusively on the development, validation or psychometric testing of ICF-based instruments without describing their implementation in healthcare settings. Editorials, commentaries, letters and conference abstracts without full-text reports will not be included.

During full-text screening, studies will be considered eligible when they explicitly describe the practical integration, adoption, operationalisation or routine use of the ICF within health service delivery, clinical practice, service organisation or health system processes. Studies will be excluded if the ICF is mentioned only as a theoretical or conceptual framework without evidence of its implementation in real-world healthcare settings.

To ensure consistency in study selection, reviewers will use predefined operational definitions and eligibility criteria developed a priori.

Eligibility decisions will be made independently by two reviewers according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Search strategy

The research strategy will be conducted in the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, LILACS, SciELO and PEDro databases. Controlled descriptors from the MeSH and DeCS vocabularies will be used, along with keywords related to the ICF, implementation, health services and associated factors, including barriers and facilitators. The research will be conducted in May 2026, combining terms with the Boolean operators AND and OR, as appropriate, and the strategy will be adapted to the specific characteristics of each database. Study selection

The selection of studies will be carried out in two stages by two independent reviewers (JMB and ATFN). Initially, the titles and abstracts will be screened, followed by the evaluation of the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements between reviewers will be resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by a third reviewer. The reference selection and management process will be supported by Rayyan software, an online tool developed to assist systematic and scoping reviews.21

Prior to study selection, the reviewers will conduct a pilot screening of a sample of studies to ensure a consistent interpretation of the eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies identified during this process will be discussed and resolved before the formal screening phase. Throughout the review process, regular meetings will be held to ensure consistency and methodological rigour in study selection and data extraction.

The reviewers have previous experience in evidence synthesis and in research involving the ICF.

Data extraction

Data extraction will be carried out independently by two reviewers using a standardised data extraction form developed by the review team (online supplemental appendix 2, available as Supplementary Material). The form may be refined during the review process to ensure that all relevant information is captured. It will include information on the authors, year of publication and country; level of healthcare; health services; population involved; study objective and design; implementation strategies for the ICF; reported barriers; reported facilitators and implementation outcomes, when reported, such as acceptability, feasibility, adoption, fidelity, sustainability, integration into routine practice and other indicators used to evaluate the implementation process.

Implementation outcomes will include, when reported, outcomes such as acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, adoption, fidelity, penetration, sustainability, integration into routine practice, service utilisation, quality improvement indicators and other measures used to evaluate implementation processes.

Reported barriers and facilitators will also be extracted and subsequently coded according to the domains and constructs of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR V.2.0), when sufficient information is available in the included studies.

Any discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by a third reviewer GGFF.

Data synthesis

The synthesis of the data will be conducted narratively and descriptively, aligned with the objectives of a scoping review.

To address the review objective, evidence will be mapped according to country, healthcare setting, level of care, target population, implementation strategies, barriers, facilitators, implementation outcomes and CFIR 2.0 domains and constructs.

Barriers and facilitators identified in the included studies will be analysed using an inductive thematic approach.

The results will be presented through tables and narrative summaries to facilitate the identification of patterns across healthcare settings and countries.

As this is a scoping review, no primary or secondary outcomes will be predefined, as the aim of the study is to map and characterise the available evidence rather than to evaluate the effects of interventions or compare studies. However, implementation outcomes reported by the included studies, such as acceptability, feasibility, adoption, fidelity, sustainability, and integration into routine practice, will be extracted and mapped when available.

Implementation outcomes will be synthesised alongside CFIR 2.0 determinants to provide a broader understanding of factors influencing the implementation of the ICF in health services.

Patient and public involvement

The research team comprises academic researchers and healthcare practitioners, promoting the articulation between academic knowledge and healthcare practice. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this review. Nevertheless, the results of this review may indirectly contribute to improve the organisation of health services and care practices by supporting more effective strategies for the implementation of the ICF.

Timeline of study stages

The planned timeline of the scoping review stages is provided in online supplemental material 1. The activities are organised chronologically from December 2025 to November 2026 and include methodological planning, database searching, study selection, data extraction, and synthesis of findings, ensuring a systematic and structured conduct of the study.

Ethics and dissemination

This study does not require approval by a research ethics committee, as it is a scoping review based exclusively on secondary data from previously published, publicly available studies and does not involve direct data collection from humans.

The results of this scoping review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and presentation at national and international scientific events. In addition, the findings may support academic discussions, professional training and the development of institutional strategies to implement the ICF in health services.
