Gut commensal Parabacteroides goldsteinii protects against Salmonella infection in mice by enhancing innate defense function
Authors: Chien-Chang Chen, Tzu-Lung Lin, Chia-Chen Lu, Kun-Jei Chen, Shih-Ming Jung, Jong-Hwei Su Pang, Hsin-Chih Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial enteritis caused by the invasion of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium into the intestinal epithelium is often associated with the overactivation of the immune system, production of proinflammatory cytokines, disruption of the intestinal barrier and dysbiosis of gut microbiota. In this study, the gut commensal Parabacteroides goldsteinii isolated from the feces of mice and enriched after consumption of Hirsutella sinensis mycelium (HSM) was selected to evaluate its ameliorative effect on enteritis. S. typhimurium was used to establish an enteritis model in mice and the effects of orally administered heat-killed P. goldsteinii were evaluated. The results indicated that P. goldsteinii reduced the systemic burdens of S. typhimurium, restored mucosal barrier integrity, decreased overexpression of genes involved in inflammation, and modulated the composition of gut microbiota, leading to the amelioration of enteritis. A subsequent transcriptomic study in germ-free mice highlighted that P. goldsteinii administration increased the expression of α-defensins, Reg3g, lyz1, and CARD9, which are involved in mucosal defense mechanisms. The increased expression of these factors was validated in infected specific-pathogen free (SPF) mice. The results indicated that P. goldsteinii may enhance intestinal immunity, leading to a reduction in S. typhimurium pathogenicity. P. goldsteinii may be developed as a food supplement for the amelioration of S. typhimurium infection-associated enteritis.

Introduction

Salmonella, the most common foodborne pathogens, cause salmonellosis in humans, and are a major concern for public health1, animal husbandry, and the food industry worldwide2,3. Salmonella infection is a major global public health issue as it causes 93.8 million cases of foodborne illnesses and 155,000 fatalities each year4,5.

Salmonella enters the human body through the ingestion of contaminated food or water, affecting the composition of the intestinal flora, invading intestinal epithelial cells and multiplying therein6,7. During Salmonella infection, pathogen-induced epithelial damage and innate immune activation promote the recruitment of adaptive immune cells, leading to increased migration of lymphocytes and plasma cells into the ileum and colon8,9, resulting in the disruption of the intestinal barrier and allowing intestinal bacteria to access internal tissues10,11. The symptoms of Salmonella infection include diarrhea, fever, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Severe cases may result in sepsis12. Further, the resistance of Salmonella strains to antibiotics has increased worldwide, whereas the overreliance on antibiotics disrupts the gut microbiota, thus slowing recovery13.

Multiomics data strongly indicate that gut bacteria play an important role in modulating intestinal immunity and metabolism, maintaining host homeostasis, and facilitating the development of disease14. In particular, the close interactions between gut commensal bacteria and host cells, and the mechanism of action through which commensal bacteria maintain optimal intestinal integrity and immunity, have been extensively examined15. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with the development of intra- and extra-intestinal inflammation-related diseases16,17. The role of the gut microbiota in Salmonella infection has attracted greater attention than its role in classic pathogen infections over the last decade7,18. Strategies for modulating the composition of the gut microbiota and the development of next-generation probiotics (NGPs)19,20 have been intensively studied to overcome difficulties in the treatment of Salmonella infections21.

NGPs and traditional probiotics differ fundamentally in their scientific basis, functional intent, and clinical development paradigm. The distinction is particularly relevant in microbiota-targeted therapies, inflammatory diseases, and precision nutrition. Compared to traditional probiotics with limited disease-modifying effects, NGPs (typically identified using advanced methods such as high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analysis to analyze tissue samples from individuals with specific health conditions) may have stronger preventative and therapeutic effects by providing well-defined, reproducible, and disease-targeted microbial functions22. Many important NGPs have been described recently, including Prevotella copri and Christensenella minuta, which control insulin resistance23,24; Parabacteroides goldsteinii, Akkermansia muciniphila25,26, and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, which alleviate obesity and decrease insulin resistance27,28, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which protects mice against intestinal diseases29, and Bacteroides fragilis, which reduces inflammation and exhibits antitumor effects30.

Parabacteroides spp. are associated with host health and are core members of the human gut microbiota. They were previously identified in 12 human populations at an average abundance of 1.27%31–33. Among the Parabacteroides species, P. goldsteinii has been highlighted for its protective effect against inflammation-related diseases31,34. For example, P. goldsteinii ameliorates obesity and metabolic syndrome in mice that are fed a high-fat diet28,35. Daily supplementation with live P. goldsteinii decreases proinflammatory lipopolysaccharides (LPSs) plasma levels and increases the expression of tight junction proteins, resulting in an overall improvement in gut barrier function28. Moreover, P. goldsteinii isolated from mice improves the pathology of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)36. LPS is a key mediator connecting Gram-negative bacteria, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and inflammation. Its biological effects depend primarily on dosage, structure (lipid A), host environment, and epithelial barrier integrity. Further, LPS derived from P. goldsteinii has anti-inflammatory effects and significantly ameliorates COPD by antagonizing the Toll-like receptor 4 signaling pathway36.

Recent studies indicate that P. goldsteinii may function as an NGP that is associated with or involved in the reduction of chronic inflammation-related diseases31,32. The ameliorative effects of P. goldsteinii on colitis and Helicobacter pylori-induced gastric pathology in vivo37 have been reported. Moreover, P. goldsteinii is beneficial for maternal immune activation-induced autism-related behaviors38, anastomotic healing after colorectal cancer surgery39, food allergies40, and aspirin-mediated intestinal damage41. Furthermore, P. goldsteinii assists in the maturation and development of a subtype of CD4+ effector T cells, which play important roles in controlling inflammation and maintaining the homeostasis of the intestinal mucosa42.

Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that P. goldsteinii may have the potential to reduce S. typhimurium infection-induced pathology. The main functional component of P. goldsteinii (Pg-LPS) is the lipopolysaccharides36, and P. goldsteinii maintains robust anti-inflammatory activity following heat sterilization37. Its anti-inflammatory effects characterized by inhibition of NF-κB signaling and modulation of cytokine responses43. Because live bacteria may have the possibility of involving antibiotics transmission and contain potential virulence factors. To maintain its anti-inflammatory function and ensure its safety for administration, we autoclaved P. goldsteinii for the prevention or treatment of S. typhimurium-induced pathology. Therefore, we examined the impacts between P. goldsteinii and pathogenic Salmonella, as well as the effect of P. goldsteinii on the intestinal flora and mucosal immunity. This study aimed to provide information for the further application of P. goldsteinii as NGPs, and to determine the interactions among microorganisms and their relationships with the host gut.

Results

P. goldsteinii reduced the systemic S. typhimurium infection

To determine the effects of P. goldsteinii treatment on S. Typhimurium SL1344 infection, mice were fed heat-killed P. goldsteinii 12 days before intragastric S. Typhimurium infection (Fig. 1A). The mice infected with S. Typhimurium alone showed signs of disease development early (2 days) after infection, including soft or loose stools, a hunched posture, rough body hair, and weight loss (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the pathological symptoms of the mice pretreated with P. goldsteinii before S. Typhimurium infection were significantly improved. The weight of the mice infected with S. Typhimurium (STM group) was significantly lower compared with that of the normal mice (control group) (percent body weight change on day 7: 5.429 ± 0.476 in the control group and −3.949 ± 1.944 in the STM group, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1B), whereas mice pretreated with P. goldsteinii (STM + PG group) experienced significantly less weight loss (percentage change in body weight on day 7: 0.880 ± 0.840 in STM + PG group vs. −3.949 ± 1.944 in STM group, p < 0.05; Fig. 1B).Fig. 1Administration of P. goldsteinii ameliorates S. typhimurium systemic infection.The grouping and schedule of murine experiments (n = 14 each group) were indicated in (A). Next, we addressed whether P. goldsteinii treatment affects intestinal colonization and systemic S. typhimurium translocation. Following S. typhimurium infection, the bacterial loads in the fecal pellets and tissue homogenates were calculated after growing on selective Salmonella–Shigella agar plates. On days 2, 5, and 7 post-infection, S. typhimurium was observed in the stools of only eight of fourteen mice in the STM group (Fig. 1C–E), which was consistent with previous studies showing that the intestinal colonization of S. Typhimurium was inadequate in this murine model44. However, the bacterial counts in the stool were lower in P. goldsteinii-preinoculated mice (Fig. 1C–E), although the difference was not significant (p > 0.05). In terms of systemic translocation, the load of S. Typhimurium in the spleen (8.1 × 107 ± 6.4 × 107 in the STM group and 1.9 × 103 ± 1.9 × 103 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.001), liver (2.1 × 107 ± 2.1 × 107 in the STM group and not detected in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05), and MLNs (4.3 × 107 ± 4.1 × 107 in STM group and not detected in the STM + PG group, p < 0.01) were decreased in P. goldsteinii-treated mice on day 7 post-infection (Fig. 1F–H). These results suggest that P. goldsteinii may reduce systemic translocation of S. typhimurium although the changes in intestinal colonization of S. typhimuriumare not significant.

The levels of proinflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokines and chemokines in the serum were measured using multiplex ELISA. As shown in Fig. 1I–P, the mice infected with S. typhimurium presented with increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines and inflammatory mediators. Pretreatment with P. goldsteinii reduced the serum levels of the Th1 cytokines IFN-γ (148.3 ± 79.2 in the STM group and 7.9 ± 6.1 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05) and IL-12p70 (2.5 ± 1.1 in the STM group and 0.2 ± 0.09 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1I, J). The levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α (88.7 ± 60.9 in the STM group and 5.7 ± 3.4 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05), IL-1β (9.1 ± 8.7 in the STM group and 0.8 ± 0.4 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05), and IL-6 (1179.0 ± 1075.0 in the STM group and 94.3 ± 58.9 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05) were also lower in the STM + PG group than in the STM group (Fig. 1K–M). There was no significant difference in IL-10 levels between these groups (Fig. 1N). Pretreatment with P. goldsteinii reduced serum levels of CXCL10 (37.9 ± 17.7 in the STM group and 16.3 ± 6.8 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05) and CXCL9 (1310.0 ± 635.1 in the STM group and 92.8 ± 41.3 in the STM + PG group, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1O, P). These results indicate that P. goldsteinii reduces S. typhimurium-induced secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and suggest that pretreatment with P. goldsteinii ameliorates systemic S. Typhimurium infection.

P. goldsteinii reduces pathological changes in the intestine caused by S. Typhimurium infection

Next, we examined whether P. goldsteinii treatment affects the intestinal pathology induced by S. typhimurium infection. Distal colon and ileum tissues were collected on day 7 post-infection, subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining (Fig. 2A–F) and immunohistochemical examination, and were scored by a pathologist based on thickness, immune infiltration, and damage (Fig. 2G, H). Histologic examination of the colon and ileum in mice infected with S. typhimurium revealed typical pathological changes associated with bacterial infection, including wall thickening (colon: 276.3 ± 27.2 µm in the control group and 367.8 ± 31.5 µm in the STM group, p < 0.0001; ileum: 186.5 ± 28.5 µm in the control group and 278.5 ± 36.5 µm in the STM group, p < 0.0001), extensive IC infiltration (colon: 0.7 ± 0.2 in the control group and 2.5 ± 0.1 in the STM group, p < 0.0001; ileum: 1.0 ± 0.0 in the control group and 2.4 ± 0.1 in the STM group, p < 0.0001), and damage (colon: 0.7 ± 0.2 in the control group and 2.5 ± 0.1 in the STM group, p < 0.0001; ileum: 1.0 ± 0.0 in the control group and 2.4 ± 0.1 in the STM group, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B, E), compared with the colon and ileum of the control mice (Fig. 2A, D). In contrast, mice preinoculated with P. goldsteinii exhibited less severe pathological phenotypes, including mild colonic crypt elongation, less IC infiltration in the colonic LP, and a mildly uneven epithelial surface (Fig. 2C, F). The integrity and continuity of the colon were evaluated by analyzing the expression and localization of the ZO-1 protein in the columnar cells in the upper epithelium of the colon using immunohistochemistry (Fig. 2I, L). ZO-1 protein expression was lower in the colons of the STM group than in the control group (Fig. 2J, M), whereas this change was less pronounced in the STM + PG group (Fig. 2K, N). Subsequent quantification of the ZO-1-stained cells revealed that, compared with the STM group (13.0 ± 1.0), the STM + PG group (49.0 ± 1.5) had significantly more ZO-1-positive cells in the colon (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2O). In the ileum, S. Typhimurium infection caused significant epithelial damage (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2P); however, ileal epithelial integrity was significantly restored in the STM + PG group (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2P). Moreover, S. Typhimurium treatment significantly reduced the ratio of the villus height to crypt depth (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2Q), whereas pretreatment with P. goldsteinii significantly restored this ratio (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2Q). Taken together, these data suggest that P. goldsteinii pretreatment reduces pathological changes in the colon, ileum, and intestinal barrier in S. Typhimurium-infected mice.

P. goldsteinii restores the colonic transcriptomic profile in S. typhimurium-infected mice

Mouse colons from the control, STM, and STM + PG groups were collected on day 7 post-infection and subjected to transcriptomic analysis. Principal component analysis of the three groups revealed similar expression profiles for the STM + PG and control groups, which differed from that in the STM group (Fig. 3A). A volcano plot revealed 322 upregulated genes and 45 downregulated genes in the STM group compared with the control group (Fig. 3B, left panel). In contrast, the STM + PG group consisted of 56 upregulated genes and 206 downregulated genes compared with the STM group (Fig. 3B, right panel). Heatmap analysis of the DEGs identified in both comparison groups (STM vs. control and STM + PG vs. STM) revealed that the genes upregulated in the STM group were mostly downregulated after treatment with P. goldsteinii, and vice versa (Fig. 3C). The common DEGs in both comparisons (STM vs. control and STM + PG vs. STM) are illustrated in a Venn diagram, indicating that 170 genes were upregulated in the STM group; however, were downregulated in the STM + PG group, and 16 genes were downregulated in the STM group; however, upregulated in the STM + PG group (Fig. 3D). Subsequent GO biological process enrichment analysis revealed the enrichment of 170 genes in biological processes related to the immune response (immune system processes, innate immune responses, and immune effecter processes), defense responses (defense responses to other organisms, defense responses to viruses, and regulation of defense responses), interspecies interactions between organisms (responses to bacteria and viruses), and cellular responses to cytokines (interferon-α and interferon-β) (Fig. 3E). Specifically, genes associated with Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) infection and inflammation, including Lcn2 (lipocalin 2), Cxcl9 (C-X-C motif chemokine 9), Cxcl10 (C-X-C motif chemokine 10), Cd14, and Gzmb (granzyme B), were upregulated in the STM group, whereas their expression was downregulated in the STM + PG group. On the other hand, no pathways were significantly enriched in the 16 genes downregulated in the STM group; however, upregulated in the STM + PG group (Fig. 3D). Thus, the results of the transcriptomic analysis suggested that P. goldsteinii administration may affect the colonic transcriptomic profile in S. P. goldsteinii modulates the gut microbiota composition in S. typhimurium-infected mice

Beta diversity among these three groups, as determined using partial least squares discriminant analysis, revealed distinct clustering of the microbiota in each group (Fig. 4A). In contrast, alpha diversity, as determined using the Shannon and Simpson indices and observed features, was not significantly different among the control, STM, and STM + PG groups (p > 0.05, Fig. 4B). The 10 most abundant bacterial species among the three groups are shown in Fig. 4C. Significantly differentially abundant bacterial species between the groups (p < 0.05) were further analyzed using Welch’s t-test (Fig. 4D, E). Compared with those in the control group, The relative abundance of Lactobacillus johnsonii, Limosilactobacillus reuteri subsp. reuteri, Limosilactobacillus reuteri, and Faecalibaculum rodentium were significantly lower in the STM group, whereas the relative abundance of Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus and Faecalimonas umbilicata was greater compared with those in the control group (Fig. 4D). Compared with those in the STM group, the relative abundance of L. asaccharolyticus and F. umbilicata was significantly lower in the STM + PG group. Discussion

In this study, the effect of P. goldsteinii preinoculation on colonization, proliferation, or clearance of S. typhimurium after infection was examined. The results revealed that Salmonella bacterial counts were lower in the feces of P. goldsteinii-preinoculated mice compared with those of Salmonella-infected mice, indicating that P. goldsteinii may affect the growth of Salmonella. Further, the systemic spread of Salmonella from the intestine to the spleen, liver, MLNs, and blood (Fig. 1F–P) was reduced, suggesting that P. goldsteinii pretreatment reduces the S. Typhimurium burden in infected animals. The expression of inflammation- and immunity-related genes was increased in the intestine following Salmonella infection. In contrast, pretreatment with P. goldsteinii reversed the aberrant expression of these genes. Similarly, P. goldsteinii administration ameliorated pathological changes in the mucosal and intestinal tissues induced by Salmonella infection. Thus, P. goldsteinii administration may decrease the Salmonella burden, reducing inflammation, and improving intestinal pathology. Although subsequent experiments indicated that P. goldsteinii did not directly inhibit the growth of S. typhimurium SL1344 in vitro (Fig. 5A), previous studies have indicated that P. goldsteinii closely interacts with host intestinal mucosal cells, resulting in the reduction of various inflammation-related diseases28,35–38. We examined the possibility that P. goldsteinii may restrict Salmonella growth indirectly by activating innate immunity in the intestinal mucosal barrier, leading to decreased inflammation. Because Salmonella exploits inflammatory conditions to gain a competitive advantage in the gut, PG-mediated suppression of inflammation plausibly restricts Salmonella expansion by eliminating this niche rather than through direct antimicrobial activity.

Symbiotic bacteria may stimulate host intestinal epithelial cells to secrete secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and AMPs to neutralize pathogens, including Salmonella51. Among the AMPs, defensin family members, small cationic peptides produced by various cells including epithelial and immune cells, participate in host innate immunity and constitute an important defense mechanism against pathogenic bacteria51. Defensins also suppress the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby reducing inflammation. Some defensins inhibit the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which is a central regulator of inflammatory gene expression51. Furthermore, defensins shape the gut microbiota composition and function, which in turn, influence immune homeostasis and inflammatory responses in the gut52. Defensins may mitigate inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract by maintaining the abundance of commensal bacteria and preventing dysbiosis53. Defensins, recognized primarily for their antimicrobial functions, also have the potential to modulate inflammatory responses and contribute to the resolution of inflammation54. P. goldsteinii can enhance innate immunity activation within the intestinal mucosal barrier. This strengthens epithelial integrity and increases the production of AMPs, creating a hostile environment for invading pathogens. While antimicrobial peptides and secretory IgA are well-established components of mucosal defense, they were not directly quantified in the present study and only defensins were measured. Transcriptomic analyses revealed enrichment of immune- and barrier-associated pathways, and ZO-1 expression was increased at the epithelial level, suggesting improved mucosal integrity. Future studies incorporating direct measurement of antimicrobial peptides and sIgA will be necessary to validate their contribution to the observed phenotype.

In the mucosal barrier, in addition to defensins, Reg3 proteins, produced primarily by Paneth cells, possess bactericidal activity and protect against pathogens on the intestinal surface55. Reg3 proteins may directly target and kill certain bacteria, thus playing important roles in the physical segregation of the microbiota from the host as well as the immune response induced by enteric pathogens, including Salmonella50. Among the Reg3 proteins, Reg3g has a protective role in Salmonella-induced mucosal infection49. Reg3g is involved in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier and contributes to the formation and maintenance of the mucus layer49. Further, Reg3g promotes tissue repair and regeneration in the intestine, maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier and contributing to the formation and maintenance of the mucus layer56. This barrier function is important for preventing the entry of harmful substances and pathogens into the bloodstream. Reg3g also regulates the activation and migration of immune cells, such as neutrophils and macrophages, involved in the regulation of inflammation in the gastrointestinal system56.

Tight junctions in the mucosal barrier are specialized structures that connect adjacent cells and regulate the passage of ions, solutes, and immune cells across epithelial and endothelial tissues. ZO-1 is a tight junction protein that plays an important role in maintaining the integrity of epithelial and endothelial cell barriers, including those in the gastrointestinal tract. The disruption or dysfunction of tight junctions can lead to increased permeability, allowing harmful substances, pathogens, or allergens to pass through the barrier and trigger inflammatory responses or cause tissue damage57. ZO-1 staining revealed that P. goldsteinii restores the continuity of the intestinal barrier between the columnar cells of the upper epithelium of the mouse colon in the STM + PG group, and the expression of tight junction proteins was greater compared with that in the STM group of mice. Moreover, P. goldsteinii increases the transepithelial electrical resistance of TNFα-treated Caco2 cells36. These results suggest that pretreatment with P. goldsteinii increases or maintains intestinal barrier integrity, which may play an important role in preventing the translocation of Salmonella. P. goldsteinii may suppress the excessive inflammatory responses that are often exploited by Salmonella for colonization and expansion by promoting immune homeostasis. This dual role of boosting mucosal defenses while reducing inflammation may contribute to a microbiota-mediated mechanism of colonization resistance, in which beneficial commensals indirectly suppress pathogenic infections.

Using GF mice as a model, we examined the effects of P. goldsteinii treatment on intestinal gene expression profiles. Among the genes associated with combating Salmonella infection, a set of α-defensin genes (Defa5, 20, 21, 22, 24, 29, 30 and 34), Lyz1, Reg3g, and CARD9 were attenuated by P. goldsteinii treatment and contribute to the defense against Salmonella infection (Fig. 5C). Based on these results, we hypothesized that instead of killing or inhibiting the growth of S. Typhimurium, P. goldsteinii increases gut barrier integrity and reduces intestinal permeability. Moreover, P. goldsteinii activates mucosal immunity by stimulating the production of innate immune effectors that inhibit Salmonella growth in the intestine. These factors prevent the translocation of S. typhimurium from the intestine to the blood, thus its systemic spread is reduced.

P. goldsteinii treatment affects the microbiota composition of infected mice. Specifically, the abundance of L. johnsonii, L. reuteri, and L. reuteri subsp. reuteri were increased significantly (Fig. 4). L. johnsonii L531 pretreatment was indicated to alleviate the intestinal damage caused by Salmonella Infantis, which is associated with the inhibition of NOD activation, regulation of endoplasmic reticulum stress, and promotion of autophagic degradation58. Another strain, L. reuteri S5, also antagonizes S. enterica infection59. Some specific Lactobacilli strains may help protecting intestinal barriers during Salmonella infection60–63. Although the causal relationship for these effects remains unclear and requires further study, we state that Parabacteroides goldsteinii may exert protective effects through host-directed mechanisms that overlap with pathways reported for other commensal bacteria, without implying synergistic or cooperative interaction.

In this study, the effect of heat-killed P. goldsteinii on reducing Salmonella infection was evaluated. Although probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that are beneficial for the host when administered in adequate amounts22, several concerns regarding their use have been raised. In particular, the safety of consuming any live microorganisms must be examined, even for those with no previous reports of adverse effects. This is crucial when administering probiotics to vulnerable people, such as the elderly and immunodeficient individuals. Such drawbacks have increased the interest in new products based on nonviable probiotics, such as paraprobiotics64,65, which are defined as “inactivated microbial cells (nonviable) that confer a health benefit to the consumer,” that can regulate the adaptive and innate immune systems, exhibit anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, and antioxidant effects; and antagonize pathogens. Paraprobiotics may have increased safety, ensure technological and practical benefits, and can be used in products suitable for elderly individuals and people with weak immune systems. These features represent an important opportunity to develop safer and more stable functional foods and nutraceuticals.

We hypothesized that P. goldsteinii may indirectly limit Salmonella growth by modulating the host immune response, thereby exerting a protective role in the gut. Specifically, P. goldsteinii enhances innate immunity within the intestinal mucosal barrier. This leads to the production of cytokines, which increases the integrity of the epithelial barrier and stimulates the secretion of AMPs, such as Reg3g and defensins. P. goldsteinii creates a less favorable environment for Salmonella colonization by strengthening the epithelial layer and mucosal defense mechanisms. P. goldsteinii can reduce excessive inflammation and prevent tissue damage and dysbiosis, which generally favors the overgrowth of pathogens. These dual actions strengthen defenses, while maintaining immune homeostasis, which highlights the microbiota-mediated mechanism through which enteric pathogens resist colonization. Therefore, the immunomodulatory properties of P. goldsteinii may represent a promising strategy for controlling intestinal infections.

In conclusion, prophylactic administration of Parabacteroides goldsteinii reduced the systemic burden of S. typhimurium, preserved mucosal barrier integrity, attenuated intestinal inflammation, and modulated gut microbiota composition, thereby mitigating infection-associated enteritis. These findings indicate that P. goldsteinii enhances host intestinal resilience and limits S. typhimurium pathogenicity when administered prior to infection. Collectively, our results support the potential development of P. goldsteinii as a preventive dietary supplement to reduce the risk or severity of Salmonella-associated enteritis.

Methods

P. goldsteinii isolation

The daily administration of Hirsutella sinensis mycelium water extract or its high-molecular-weight polysaccharide (20 mg/kg) using intragastric gavage enriched the abundance of P. goldsteinii in mice28. P. goldsteinii MTS01 was isolated from the mouse feces36.

P. goldsteinii cultivation and genome sequencing

P. goldsteinii was grown at 37 °C on anaerobic blood agar (Creative, New Taipei City, Taiwan) and thioglycolate liquid media (BD, Lake Franklin, USA) in a Whitley DG250 anaerobic chamber (Don Whitley, Bingley, UK) in the presence of a gas mixture containing 5% carbon dioxide, 5% hydrogen, and 90% nitrogen. After culturing in liquid media, the bacteria were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and heat-killed by autoclaving (121 °C for 15 min). The mice were treated daily with 5 × 10⁸ colony-forming unit (CFU) of heat-killed P. goldsteinii by oral gavage for 12 days. This dose, which was the same as that used in our previous studies, ameliorates several inflammatory diseases36–38.

In our previous study, the Illumina and PacBio platforms were used to sequence the full genome of the P. goldsteinii MTS01 strain and for gene annotation (under the NCBI BioProject accession number PRJNA674720), in which the genes involved in the synthesis of the anti-inflammatory lipopolysaccharide Pg-LPS were identified.

Animal experiments and in vivo animal study design

Four- to six-week-old female C57BL/6 J mice were obtained from NARLab (Taiwan) and bred in the animal facility at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. The animals were housed at four to five per cage with free access to food and sterile drinking water (reverse osmosis grade) in a temperature-controlled room (21°C ± 2 °C under a 12-h light–dark cycle). The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chang Gung Hospital (approval number: CMRPG3J1441-3_2019032801) and performed based on the guidelines of the Animal Protection Act (Taiwan).

A total of three experimental groups were established (Fig. 1A). The normal control group was orally administered PBS for the entire experimental period and was not infected with S. typhimurium. The STM group was inoculated daily with PBS via oral gavage until day 12 before intragastric S. typhimurium infection. Finally, the STM + PG group received daily P. goldsteinii via oral gavage on day 12 before intragastric infection with S. typhimurium. We compared the results of the STM group with those from the STM + PG group to evaluate the therapeutic effect of P. goldsteinii. Body weight changes were measured on days 0, 1, 2, 5, 6, and 7. Feces were collected on days 2, 5, and 7 post-infection. All mice were sacrificed according to the schedule, seven days after S. sp. Typhimurium infection.

S. typhimurium cultivation

The wild-type strain of S. typhimurium SL1344 was grown in LB broth (10 g of peptone, 5 g of yeast extract, and 5 g of sodium chloride per liter). For the infection studies, the bacteria were grown in LB broth at 250 rpm for 16–18 h at 37 °C. Bacteria in the stationary phase were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, washed twice, and resuspended in sterile PBS. The OD600 was measured to estimate the bacterial density (CFU/ml; an OD600 of 1.0 is equivalent to approximately 1.3 × 109 CFU/ml), and serial plating was performed on LB agar plates to quantify the actual infection dose following 16 h of incubation at 37 °C. Each animal was infected with 1.6 × 105 CFU of S. Typhimurium SL1344.

To determine the colony forming unit (CFU) in Salmonella infection

To evaluate the Salmonella load, feces or tissue homogenates of the spleen, liver, or mesenteric lymph node (MLN) were serially diluted and plated in duplicate on Salmonella–Shigella agar (BDPS040, Dr. plate, Taipei, Taiwan). Salmonella–Shigella agar was selected for its ability to inhibit gram-positive bacteria growth, while differentiating into Salmonella colonies, which form black precipitates through the production of hydrogen sulfide. Salmonella CFUs were counted after incubation overnight at 37°C44,66.

Detection of cytokines and chemokines in the host

Whole blood was collected from mice using cardiac puncture and transferred to anticoagulant-free tubes (#365967, BD, Franklin Lakes, USA). The samples were allowed to clot for 30 min and were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C. The supernatants (serum) were collected and stored at − 80 °C.

ProcartaPlex multiplex assays were used to measure interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-33, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), RANKL, CXCL9, and CXCL10 serum levels based on the manufacturer’s instructions (PPX-14-MXT2A3M, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria). The ProcartaPlex multiplex assays were selected because the capture antibody is conjugated to a bead instead of being adsorbed to a microplate well as in traditional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)67.

The samples were analyzed in duplicates according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The net median fluorescence intensity was measured using xPONENT 4.3 software (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA). Sample concentrations were calculated from a standard curve using a logistic five-parameter (5 P) regression formula.

Histopathology of the colon and ileum

The mice were euthanized 7 days following Salmonella infection. Colon and ileum samples were collected and fixed for 24 h in 10% formalin at a volume ratio of 10:1. Next, the fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin wax and cut into 5-µm-thick sections. For histological analysis, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 5 min. Pathology was assessed using a modified histologic scoring system67, which consisted of three parts, as previously described66. Part 1 comprised the determination of inflammatory cell (IC) infiltration in the colon/ileum, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 (0: normal cell pattern; 1: scattered ICs in the lamina propria (LP); 2: increased numbers of ICs in the LP; 3: confluence of ICs extending into the submucosa; and 4: transmural extension of the infiltrative ICs). Part 2 included the evaluation of colon/ileum tissue damage, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 (0: normal tissue pattern; 1: discrete lymphoepithelial lesions; 2: mild colonic crypt hyperplasia with or without focal invasion of the epithelium; 3: distinct colonic crypt hyperplasia, invasion of the epithelium and goblet cell depletion; and 4: excessive mucosal damage extending through deeper structures of the bowel wall). Part 3: Determination of colon/ileum thickness. Bowel wall thickness was calculated as the sum of the crypt depth (from the bottom of the villus to the muscularis mucosa) and smooth muscle thickness (from the external longitudinal layer to the submucosa). The ileum wall thickness was scored on a scale of 0–4 (0: <150 µm, normal thickness; 1: 150–200 µm wide; 2: 200–250 µm wide; 3: 250–300 µm wide; and 4: severe thickening >300 µm). Colon wall thickness was also scored on a scale of 0–4 (0: <250 µm, normal thickness; 1: 250–300 µm wide; 2: 300–350 µm wide; 3: 350–400 µm wide; and 4: severe thickening >400 µm). The total colon/ileum pathology score was calculated as the sum of the ICs, tissue damage, and tissue thickness scores44,68–71.

Ileal epithelial damage was scored on a scale of 0–3 (0: no pathological changes detected in 10 high-power fields (400× magnification); 1: epithelial desquamation; 2: erosion of the epithelial surface (gaps of 1 to 10 epithelial cells/lesion); and 3: epithelial ulceration (gaps of >10 epithelial cells/lesion)40. The ileal crypt structure was evaluated by calculating the ratio of the villus height to the crypt depth72,73.

Immunohistochemistry

Colon samples from C57BL/6 mice were harvested and fixed for 24 h in 10% formalin at a volume ratio of 10:1. After fixation, the tissue was embedded in paraffin wax and cut into 5-µm-thick sections. The sections were then processed using the mouse/rabbit ImmunoDetector DAB HRP Brown Detection System (Bio SB, CA, USA) based on the manufacturer’s protocol. The sections were air-dried for 80 min at 65 °C, followed by deparaffinization in 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 6.0. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the sections at 115 °C for 30 min in a Tinto Retriever pressure cooker (Bio SB, CA, USA), followed by blocking endogenous peroxidase for 5 min. The primary antibody against zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (61-7300, Invitrogen, US) was diluted 100-fold in PBS and incubated with the sections overnight at 4 °C66. The tissue sections were then washed with PBS to remove unbound primary antibody, and the antibody‒antigen reaction was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 3 min, followed by counterstaining with Mayer’s hemalum for 20 s. A section that was not incubated with the primary antibody was used as a negative control.

Histological images from three fields of view for each section and two sections of each sample were captured using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic 3DHISTECH MIDI II) and randomly selected to quantify ZO-1 staining under constant magnification using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). A blinded researcher analyzed two sections of each sample to ensure reproducibility.

Determination of the inhibition of S. typhimurium growth

The ability of P. goldsteinii or its culture supernatant to inhibit S. typhimurium SL1344 growth was determined using a kinetic study. The supernatant of P. goldsteinii was collected from bacteria cultured overnight in a thioglycolate liquid medium by centrifugation and syringe-filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. S. typhimurium (5 ml at approximately 5 × 107 CFU/ml in thioglycolate liquid medium) was co-cultured with 5 ml of thioglycolate liquid medium, P. goldsteinii (approximately 5 × 107 CFU/ml in thioglycolate liquid medium), or P. goldsteinii supernatant at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber. The growth of S. typhimurium alone (STM) and that of S. typhimurium co-incubated with P. goldsteinii (STM + PG) or with the culture supernatant of P. goldsteinii (STM + PG sup) at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h was quantified by plating serial dilutions on Salmonella–Shigella agar (Dr. plate, Taipei, Taiwan). The fold-change in STM growth (determined by dividing by the original colony count) at 2, 4, 8, and 24 h was calculated.

Microbiota library construction and sequencing

Fecal samples were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at 80 °C. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Full-length 16S genes (V1-V9 regions) were amplified using barcoded 16S gene-specific primers74. Based on the amplification of the full-length 16S gene with Barcoded Primers for the Multiplexed SMRTbell Library Preparation and Sequencing Procedure (PacBio, CA, USA), each primer contained a 5ʹ buffer sequence (GCATC) with a 5ʹ phosphate modification, a 16-base barcode, and degenerate 16S gene-specific forward or reverse primer sequences as follows: (forward: 5ʹ Phos/GCATC-16-base barcode-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3ʹ and reverse: 5ʹ Phos/GCATC-16-base barcode-RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3ʹ). The degenerate bases were R = A, G; Y = C, T; and M = A, C. Briefly, 2 ng of DNA was used for PCR, which was conducted with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) under previously reported PCR conditions74. The PCR products were visualized on a 1% agarose gel. Samples with bright main bands of approximately 1500 bp were selected and purified using AMPure PB beads for subsequent library preparation.

The SMRTbell library was prepared by amplifying the full-length 16S gene using barcoded primers for multiplexed SMRTbell library preparation and sequencing (PacBio). Briefly, equal amounts of each barcoded PCR product were pooled. Then, 500–1000 ng of the pooled sample was used for DNA damage repair, followed by end-repair/A-tailing and ligation, to introduce the universal hairpin adapters onto the double-stranded DNA fragments using the SMRTbell prep Kit 3.0 (PacBio). Following purification using AMPure PB beads to remove the adapter dimer, the library was incubated with Sequel II primer 3.1 and Sequel II Binding Kit 3.1 for primer annealing and polymerase binding. Finally, sequencing was conducted in circular consensus sequence (CCS) mode on a PacBio Sequel IIe instrument to generate HiFi reads with a predicted accuracy (Phred Scale) of 3055,56.

Full-length 16S rRNA gene-based metagenomics analysis workflow

The CCS reads were determined with a minimum prediction accuracy of 0.9 and a minimum number of passes of 3 in the official PacBio workflow using SMRT Link software74,75. After demultiplexing, the HiFi reads were further processed using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) (version 1.20) to obtain amplicons at single-nucleotide resolution76,77. The DADA2 workflow included quality filtering, dereplication, dataset-specific error model learning, amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference, and chimera removal. Trimming and filtering were performed with a maximum of two expected errors per read (maxEE = 2). The DADA2 algorithm resolves exact ASVs from the full-length 16S rRNA gene with a near-zero error rate at single-nucleotide resolution. For each representative sequence, the feature-classifier78 and classify-consensus-search79 algorithms in QIIME2 (v2022.11)80 were used for taxonomy classification based on the information retrieved from the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA database. To analyze the sequence similarities among different ASVs, multiple sequence alignment was conducted with the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA database using the MAFFT tool within QIIME281,82. Alpha diversity analysis was done to analyze species diversity within a sample, whereas beta diversity analysis was performed to analyze differences in species complexity between samples. For statistical analysis, the significance of all species among the groups was analyzed using an unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction. In this study, taxa with p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Transcriptomic analysis of mouse colon tissue samples

Total RNA was isolated from mouse colon tissues. Transcriptomic analysis and the experimental procedures were performed as previously described83. Total RNA quality was assessed using a Qubit® RNA Assay Kit and a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). RNA integrity was examined using an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Subsequently, the RNA was subjected to cDNA library construction and Illumina sequencing. RNA-seq by expectation-maximization was used to quantify gene expression. The clean data were mapped back onto the assembled transcriptome, from which the read count for each gene was obtained. Volcano plots were used to visualize the magnitude and statistical significance of the fold-changes in gene expression and highlight biologically relevant genes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq R package, and the resulting p-values were adjusted with Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 and a |log2 (fold-change) | > 1 were considered as DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/)83. Pathways with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Quantitative RT‒PCR analysis

Colonic tissues were initially preserved in tubes containing 400 μl of RNAlater and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and 1-bromo-3-chloropropane was added for phase separation, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was combined with equal parts isopropanol, and RNA was purified using an RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a Quant II Fast RT Kit (TOOLS, New Taipei, Taiwan). Gene expression was measured using gene-specific primers (see Table S1) and 2× SYBR qPCR Mix (TOOLS, New Taipei, Taiwan) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Singapore). The expression of the target genes was normalized to those of β-actin and determined relative to those of the control group using the ΔCt or ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as means ± standard errors of the means. For statistical analysis of more than two groups, the data were analyzed using the Kruskal‒Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for comparing groups. For statistical analysis of the two groups, the data were analyzed using an unpaired t- test (see Figure legends). A probability value of <0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.
