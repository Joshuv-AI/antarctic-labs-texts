Genetic analyses of autoimmune mutants uncover the MPK3/6-CHR5-SNC1 module in Arabidopsis
Authors: Axelle E. Deroubaix, Mathieu Weibel, Farah Naciri, Jean Colcombet, Jean Bigeard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant defense largely relies on an immune system that is induced by the binding of microbe-derived molecules to receptors at the plasma membrane, initiating the pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), and by the detection of pathogen effectors by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors (NLRs), eliciting the effector-triggered immunity (ETI). While PTI and ETI were initially thought to be independent, recent studies have revealed some interplay. Our group notably showed that the activity of mitogen-activated protein kinase 3 (MPK3) and MPK6 could link PTI and ETI responses by positively regulating the salicylic acid (SA)-dependent defense, through the up-regulation of some NLR genes. Here, we gained a better understanding of the relationship between these MAPKs and the key NLR suppressor of npr1-1, constitutive 1 (SNC1). By genetic analyses, we demonstrate that chromatin remodeling 5 (CHR5), a positive regulator of SNC1 expression, is downstream of MPK3 activity. We also provide evidence that CHR5 itself does not regulate immune MAPKs. We then show that plants expressing both MPK3 and SNC1 gain-of-function proteins synergistically exhibit a strong autoimmune phenotype. Finally, we report that MPK3 and MPK6 function redundantly upstream of SNC1. Overall, our results identified the MPK3/6-CHR5-SNC1 module, further refining the connection between PTI and ETI components.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55601-x.

Introduction

Plants, as a rich source of carbohydrates, are constantly subjected to the assault of a large spectrum of organisms, including bacteria, fungi and oomycetes. Plants first rely on pre-formed defences, such as the cuticle and constitutively produced antimicrobial compounds, to repel pathogen attacks. If not sufficient, plant defense also relies on a sophisticated inducible immune system that detects the pathogens and triggers adapted responses. In this way, plants can recognize conserved microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) through plasma membrane-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) which initiates the pattern-triggered immunity (PTI)1. A typical example is the recognition of the 22-amino-acid-long MAMP flg22, which is found in the N-terminus of the flagellin of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, by the Arabidopsis receptor-like kinase flagellin-sensitive 2 (FLS2) and its co-receptor BRI1-associated receptor kinase 1 (BAK1)2,3. Some pathogens have evolved to mitigate plant defenses via the secretion of so-called effector molecules which usually target PTI components, leading to effector-triggered susceptibility (ETS). Plants have thus evolved intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptor (NLR) proteins that activate effector-triggered immunity (ETI) upon detection of these effectors4. There are three types of NLRs: the sensor Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domain-containing NLRs (TNLs), the sensor coiled-coil (CC) domain-containing NLRs (CNLs) and the helper NLRs5. PTI and ETI were initially thought to be consecutive in time and independent6. This paradigm was then challenged by the discovery that PTI and ETI signaling involve numerous common mechanisms, such as the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of defense hormone pathways -such as the salicylic acid (SA) pathway-, and lead to a similar transcriptional reprogramming7. Recent studies have revealed even more about their interplay, showing that PTI and ETI mutually potentiate each other8–11. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the interplay between PTI and ETI is thus currently one of the key questions in the field of plant immunity12,13.

Some members of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family are involved in immune responses. These protein kinases are present in all eukaryotic organisms and function as signaling modules where MAPK kinase kinases (MAPKKKs) activate MAPK kinases (MAPKKs) which in turn activate MAPKs14. Once activated, MAPKs phosphorylate a large number of protein substrates, mainly contributing to transcriptional reprograming15. Of the 20 MAPKs encoded in the Arabidopsis genome, the implication during PTI of clade A MPK3 and MPK6, and clade B MPK4 has been well documented16. More recently, the involvement of MPK3 and MPK6 during ETI was also reported17–20. Our group notably showed that the activity of MPK3 and MPK6 could link PTI and ETI responses by positively regulating the SA-dependent defense, through the up-regulation of several NLR genes, allowing a better understanding of the PTI-ETI interplay20. The TNL suppressor of npr1-1, constitutive 1 (SNC1) is one of these NLR genes downstream of MPK3 activity20,21. SNC1 was reported more than two decades ago through the identification of the snc1-1 gain-of-function mutation in a genetic screen for suppressors of npr1-1 mutant22,23. It has since been the object of intense research. Interestingly, SNC1 recently turned out to be a key NLR as it both enhances ETI responses24 and guards activated disease resistance 1 (ADR1)-like 1 (ADR1-L1) and ADR1-L2 helper NLRs25.

Among the signaling components involved in immune responses, some ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling factors were also reported26,27. These proteins, conserved in eukaryotic organisms, modify chromatin via regulating nucleosome positioning, occupancy and composition, and thereby contribute to gene regulation28. Several chromatin remodelling factors were notably shown to regulate NLR genes. For instance, SPLAYED, from the sucrose non-fermenting 2 (SNF2) subfamily, was identified as a negative regulator of SNC1 expression29, while on the contrary chromatin remodeling 5 (CHR5), only member of the chromodomain, helicase/ATPase and DNA-binding domain 1 (CHD1) subfamily, was shown to positively regulate the expression of SNC130,31. CHR5 is more broadly a positive regulator of both basal and NLR-mediated immunity, as chr5 mutants are more susceptible to virulent, avirulent, and non-virulent Pseudomonas syringae bacterial strains30. Besides, CHR5 was also reported to have a role in embryo development, by establishing active chromatin state of seed maturation genes32.

In this study, we therefore decided to build on our previous work in order to gain a better understanding of the relationship between immune MAPKs and SNC1. We first demonstrated through genetic analyses that CHR5 is downstream of MPK3 activity. We then showed that plants expressing both MPK3 and SNC1 gain-of-function proteins synergistically exhibit a strong autoimmune phenotype. We also reported that some immune MAPKs function redundantly upstream of SNC1. Altogether, our results identified the MPK3/6-CHR5-SNC1 module, further refining the connection between PTI and ETI components.

Results

chr5 knock-out mutants partially revert MPK3-CA dwarf phenotype

SNC1, as a key NLR, is known to be under tight expression control, and the ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling factor CHR5 was reported to positively regulate SNC1 expression30,31. Besides, a phosphoproteomic study reported that one of CHR5 phosphorylation sites was genetically dependent on MPK3/4/633. To investigate the genetic link between MPK3 and CHR5, we crossed the mpk3-1 MPK3-PC2 CA2 (hereafter MPK3-CA) line34 with the two independent chr5-1 and chr5-2 knock-out (ko) mutants32. The MPK3-CA line expresses a constitutively active (CA) MPK3 protein version under its own promoter, and exhibits autoimmune symptoms, such as dwarfism, leaf lesions and ROS accumulation34. By measuring the rosette fresh weight (FW) of control lines and the different generated lines, we observed that both chr5 single mutants displayed a macroscopic WT-like phenotype, but interestingly that both chr5 mutants partially reverted, and to the same extent, the dwarf phenotype of the MPK3-CA line (Fig. 1). Besides, we observed that the curlying and yellowing of leaves were still present in the MPK3-CA chr5-1 and MPK3-CA chr5-2 plants, and the fertility was slightly increased compared to MPK3-CA plants. Overall, these results suggest that CHR5 is genetically downstream of MPK3 activity.

Autoimmune molecular phenotypes are similar in MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 adult plants

To try to better understand the partial reversion of MPK3-CA macroscopic phenotype by the chr5 mutation in adult plants, we characterized some molecular events known to be associated with autoimmunity35. By trypan blue staining, we observed some cell death in leaves from MPK3-CA plants, as previously reported34 (Fig. 2A). The cell death followed the leaf venation and also localized in small cell patches. However, we observed the same cell death pattern and could not distinguish a clear reduction of trypan blue staining intensity in MPK3-CA chr5-1 leaves. By 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining, we investigated the accumulation of H2O2 (Fig. 2B). As reported earlier34, leaves from MPK3-CA plants accumulated H2O2, along the leaf venation and in large leaf areas, but the DAB staining pattern and intensity were similar in MPK3-CA chr5-1 leaves. We then measured the expression of several marker genes by qRT-PCR experiments (Fig. 2C). While the MPK3-dependent phytoalexin deficient 3 (PAD3) gene, the MPK3-dependent and CHR5-dependent SNC1 gene and the SA marker pathogenesis-related gene 1 (PR1) gene were upregulated, as expected34, in the MPK3-CA plants, the same transcript expression levels were observed in the MPK3-CA chr5-1 mutant. Finally, we realized some ROS burst assays using the MAMP flg22 as the triggering stimulus, at a concentration of 1 µM (Fig. 2D). This phenotype had not been assessed yet on MPK3-CA leaves. We observed that MPK3-CA plants displayed a very weaker ROS burst compared to control plants. The response of MPK3-CA chr5-1 plants was however similar, although there was a tendency towards an intermediate response. Overall, these results indicate that, although the dwarf phenotype of MPK3-CA line is partially reverted by the chr5 mutation, autoimmune molecular indicators are very similar in adult plants.

The higher expression of some defense marker genes in MPK3-CA seedlings is CHR5-dependent

The above results prompted us to investigate the phenotype of younger seedlings. Interestingly, we previously reported that PTI defense was primed in another autoimmune mutant36. We thus measured the expression of the previously analysed defense marker genes in 14-day-old seedlings treated with 100 nM flg22 for 0, 2 and 8 h (Fig. 3, Table S1). We first observed a similar PAD3 gene expression pattern in WT and chr5-1 seedlings during the flg22 kinetics, with a mean relative expression increasing at 2 h and decreasing at 8 h. Besides, PAD3 expression was at least 10 times higher in MPK3-CA seedlings at 2 h and 8 h flg22 treatment, compared to WT and chr5-1 seedlings. Interestingly, the chr5-1 mutation partially suppressed the MPK3-CA effect on PAD3 expression at all time points. The same global pattern was observed for SNC1 expression, but the amplitude of expression changes was far less important. Still, there was a significant difference in SNC1 expression between MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 seedlings at 2 h and 8 h flg22 treatment. Finally, despite a high variability between biological repeats in the MPK3-CA genotype, we observed an interesting trend for the PR1 transcript expression. It accumulated after 8 h flg22 treatment in WT but not in chr5-1 and it was strongly expressed in MPK3-CA seedlings at all time points, even without flg22 treatment. Interestingly, the chr5-1 mutation suppressed the MPK3-CA effect on PR1 expression. Overall, these results show that some immune molecular events exhibit differences in MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 seedlings, suggesting an important role for CHR5 in MPK3-mediated gene expression reprogramming.

CHR5 does not regulate the amount or activity of immune MAPKs

There are two hypotheses that could explain why the chr5 mutation partially suppresses some autoimmune phenotypes of the MPK3-CA line. The first one is that CHR5 would act downstream of MPK3 and its ko mutation would thus partially impair the MPK3-mediated signaling events leading to the different immune responses. Alternatively, CHR5 could be involved in the control of MPK3/MPK3-CA expression or activation, which we could assess as the MPK3-CA construct is based on MPK3 full locus, promoter included, retaining all physiological regulations. To test this second hypothesis, we directly measured the abundance and flg22-triggered activation of MPK3, as well as MPK4 and MPK6, in 14-day-old seedlings of the different genotypes (Figs. 4, S1). By immunoblotting assays using different anti-MAPK antibodies, we first did not observe any difference of accumulation of the three immune MAPKs in the different genetic backgrounds. As expected, the endogenous MPK3 protein was absent in the MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 lines. Then, using the anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) antibody, we found that the flg22-triggered MAPK activation pattern was similar in the different genotypes, with no signal in the absence of flg22 peptide, a strong activation at 15 min, and a decreasing signal at 45 min of flg22 treatment. Finally, we measured the abundance of the tagged MPK3-CA protein with an anti-Myc antibody, and we detected an identical amount of this protein in the MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 lines. Taken together, these results show that CHR5 does not regulate the amount or activity of immune MAPKs, favouring the hypothesis that CHR5 is indeed solely downstream of MPK3.

MPK3-CA and snc1-1 gain-of-function mutants synergistically exhibit strong autoimmune phenotype

From our results above, showing that MPK3 is upstream of CHR5, and literature data, reporting that CHR5 positively regulates SNC1 expression, a model emerges suggesting that MPK3, CHR5 and SNC1 would constitute a signaling module. To further investigate this possibility, we generated lines combining both the MPK3-CA and snc1-122 gain-of-function mutations, and the chr5-1 mutant. As the multiple mutants containing both the MPK3-CA and snc1-1 homozygous alleles were not able to get seeds, we had to handle snc1-1 segregating populations and genotype each plant individually for the SNC1 WT and snc1-1 alleles. Nonetheless, it allowed studying the dose effect of the presence of heterozygous or homozygous snc1-1 mutant. We measured the rosette FW of the generated lines as a readout to assess autoimmunity levels36 (Figs. 5, S2). We first observed that the MPK3-CA line exhibited more severe autoimmune phenotypes than the snc1-1 mutant line, with a more pronounced dwarfism, curlier and yellower leaves, and spontaneous leaf lesions (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, as reported previously (Fig. 1), the chr5-1 mutant clearly led to a partial suppression of MPK3-CA phenotype, but only displayed a tendency to the reversion of snc1-1 phenotype in our culture conditions, contradicting somehow a previous report showing that chr5 mutation suppresses snc1-1 phenotype30. Strikingly, the combination of both MPK3-CA and snc1-1 gain-of-function mutations led to an extreme autoimmune phenotype which was slightly attenuated when snc1-1 allele was heterozygous (Fig. 5A–C). Finally, the chr5-1 mutant very partially reverted both the MPK3-CA snc1-1 and MPK3-CA snc1-1 +/- phenotypes, here again with an attenuation of the autoimmune phenotype when snc1-1 allele was heterozygous. Altogether, these results demonstrate that the MPK3-CA and snc1-1 mutants exhibit a strong autoimmune phenotype in a synergistic manner, and that CHR5 partly contributes to its establishment. They also provide further evidence for the existence of the MPK3-CHR5-SNC1 module.

MPK3 and MPK6 function redundantly upstream of SNC1

The results obtained in our previous reports and above, by using the MPK3-CA mutant, showed that MPK3 functions upstream of SNC120,34. As MPK3 belongs to the large family of MAPKs, comprising 20 members in Arabidopsis14, we hypothesised that a functional redundancy could occur among MAPKs upstream of SNC1. MPK3 and its closest ortholog MPK6 are notably known to be involved in the same biological processes and to share most of their reported substrates37. Furthermore, MPK4 is another key MAPK that is often activated alongside MPK3 and MPK6 to control immune responses16. To genetically evaluate the putative functional redundancy of these immune MAPKs upstream of SNC1, we first generated the snc1-1 mpk3-1 and snc1-1 mpk6-2 double mutants. Besides, we also added the snc1-1 chr5-1 double mutant using a larger sample size than in Fig. 5, as we hypothesized that the high stringency of our statistical tests combined with an insufficient sampling could have led to underestimate the chr5-1 mutation effect. We observed that the chr5-1 mutant indeed partially reverted the snc1-1 phenotype (Fig. 6). In addition, we found that, while the mpk3-1 mutation had no effect on the snc1-1 phenotype, the mpk6-2 mutation led to a similar reversion as the chr5-1 mutation. The mpk4 mutants alone display strong autoimmune phenotypes38. To assess the effect of mpk4-3 mutation on the snc1-1 phenotype, we also had to combine a ko mutation in the suppressor of mkk1 mkk2 2 (SUMM2) CNL gene, as it is known indeed that summ2 ko mutations lead to an almost complete suppression of mpk4-3 symptoms39. We thus generated the snc1-1 mpk4-3 summ2-8 triple mutant. We did not observe any effect of mpk4-3 mutation on snc1-1 phenotype, but as the mpk4-3 summ2-8 double mutant itself is not WT-like, as previously reported39, we could not draw reliable conclusions (Fig. S3). Together with the above-mentioned MPK3-CA data, these results indicate that MPK3 and MPK6 function redundantly upstream of SNC1 and even suggest that MPK6 would have a preponderant role over MPK3.

Discussion

In a previous work from our group, we had reported that MPK3 and MPK6 act as key signaling components bridging PTI and ETI, by promoting SA-dependent defense through the induction of NLR gene expression, and thereby brought new insight into the PTI-ETI interplay20. We had showed also that the MPK3-CA phenotype was partially suppressed by snc1-11 ko mutant20. In the present study, we thus explored further the genetic link between the immune MAPKs and SNC1. Interestingly, we had contributed a collaborative work in which a phosphorylation site on CHR5 was identified as genetically dependent on MPK3/4/633, and CHR5 is known as a positive regulator of SNC1 expression, likely via regulation of nucleosome occupancy in its promoter region30,31. Here, we found that the MPK3-CA dwarf phenotype was partially and equally suppressed by two independent chr5 ko mutants (Fig. 1).

We previously demonstrated that the phenotype of MPK3-CA line was due to autoimmunity, and we reported that the mutation of some key immune components led to the partial reversion of its phenotype, for instance non-race specific disease resistance 1-1 (ndr1-1), SA induction-deficient 2-2 (sid2-2) and most notably enhanced disease susceptibility 1-2 (eds1-2)20,34. Of note, the sid2-2 and eds1-2 mutations both lead to a decrease of MPK3-CA protein abundance, suggesting that it at least partly contributes to the partial reversion of the phenotype20,34. Interestingly, however, the chr5-1 mutation does not lead to such a downregulation of MPK3-CA protein amount (Fig. 4). In addition, MPK3 protein kinase is known to have numerous substrates15,37. The partial reversion of MPK3-CA phenotype by the sole chr5 mutation thus underlies the important role played by CHR5 downstream of MPK3.

The reversion of MPK3-CA phenotype by chr5 mutations (Fig. 1) is seemingly more important than by snc1-11 mutation20. Knowing that, besides SNC1, several NLR genes are upregulated in MPK3-CA line21,34, it is likely that CHR5 would positively regulate other NLR genes as well, probably explaining the differential effects of chr5 and snc1-11 mutations on MPK3-CA phenotype.

In contrast to their macroscopic phenotype, when we investigated in MPK3-CA and MPK3-CA chr5-1 adult plants several molecular events associated with autoimmunity, i.e., cell death and accumulation of H2O2 and defense marker genes, we did not observe any significative difference between these genotypes (Fig. 2). It suggested that the major underlying molecular events occurred earlier in their development. We thus phenotyped 14-day-old seedlings for the expression of the above-mentioned defense marker genes upon flg22 treatment and found that the higher expression of some markers in MPK3-CA seedlings was at least partially suppressed by the chr5-1 mutation (Fig. 3). Besides the known role of CHR5 as a positive regulator of SNC1 expression, these results also indicate that CHR5 positively controls the expression of PAD3 and possibly PR1. We also assessed the flg22-induced ROS burst and observed a very weaker ROS induction in MPK3-CA plants compared to control plants, and a tendency towards an intermediate response in MPK3-CA chr5-1 plants (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that MPK3 activity negatively regulates flg22-induced ROS production. Interestingly, it was previously reported that the flg22-induced ROS production was also negatively regulated in MPK4-CA plants40. MPK3 and MPK4 are known to share partial functional redundancy, and although they are overall positive regulators of PTI, these results indicate that they both negatively regulate the MAMP-induced ROS burst. However, the precise mechanism behind this negative regulation is currently unknown. Is it a direct negative regulation of components involved in the ROS burst, or is it a more indirect genetic effect?

The snc1-1 gain-of-function mutation was identified in a genetic screen for suppressors of npr1-1 mutant22,23. Nonexpresser of PR genes 1 (NPR1) gene was initially known as the only SA-mediated signaling component involved in systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and was later on identified as an SA receptor41. The snc1-1 mutant displays autoimmune phenotypes and has been instrumental in deciphering NLR-mediated immune mechanisms42,43. Here, we generated several multiple mutants combining the MPK3-CA, snc1-1 and chr5-1 mutations (Fig. 5).

When comparing the MPK3-CA and snc1-1 plants, we observed that MPK3-CA exhibited more severe autoimmune symptoms, such as its more pronounced dwarfism and curlier leaves. As mentioned above, several NLR genes, besides SNC1, are upregulated in MPK3-CA line21,34, possibly explaining the differential intensity of autoimmunity in these two gain-of-function mutants. As reported previously22, we also did not observe macroscopic lesions on snc1-1 leaves.

Regarding the snc1-1 chr5-1 double mutant, we first only observed a tendency for the reversion of snc1-1 phenotype (Fig. 5A-B), but we then confirmed the suppressive effect of chr5-1 mutation when increasing the sampling of our experimental plan (Fig. 6). While it was previously reported that the chr5 mutation completely suppresses snc1-1 dwarfism and curly leaf phenotype30, we observed only a partial suppression of snc1-1 dwarfism and seemingly a complete suppression of the curly leaf phenotype. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the growth conditions and the age at which plants were phenotyped. It should be noted also that we used the SAIL_504_D01 T-DNA mutant for the crosses, i.e., the chr5-1 allele according to the first nomenclature by Shen et al.32, while Zou et al. used the SALK_020296 T-DNA mutant for their cross, i.e., the chr5-2 allele according to Shen et al. but named chr5-1 in Zou et al.30. According to Shen et al., both T-DNA insertions interrupted the production of the full-length CHR5 transcript32. The discrepancy observed in the suppression of snc1-1 phenotype should thus not be due to the use of these two different chr5 T-DNA mutants.

Concerning the cross between both MPK3-CA and snc1-1 gain-of-function mutants, the generated double mutant displayed a very severe autoimmune phenotype (Fig. 5A-C). Notably, no seed could be obtained in normal growth conditions from this line, as could be observed for other severe autoimmune mutants, for instance snap3336. Our results suggest that a synergistic effect occurs when combining both MPK3-CA and snc1-1 mutations. As SNC1 is known to enhance ETI responses24, it is probable that the MPK3-CA effects are exacerbated in the presence of the snc1-1 mutation, which could be a plausible explanation for the observation of this synergistic effect. In addition, we observed an attenuation of MPK3-CA snc1-1 autoimmune phenotype when the snc1-1 allele was heterozygous (Fig. 5A-C), suggesting a dose effect linked to the zygosity. Such zygosity-linked dose effect was also observed with pSNC1::SNC1 lines expressing SNC1 under the control of its own promoter region44.

Despite the severity of MPK3-CA snc1-1 phenotype, the chr5-1 mutant still led to a very partial reversion of both MPK3-CA snc1-1 and MPK3-CA snc1-1 +/– phenotypes, here also with an attenuation of the autoimmune phenotype linked to snc1-1 allele zygosity. Overall, we observed only a partial suppressive effect of chr5-1 mutation on the different genotypes tested. These results support the existence of the MPK3-CHR5-SNC1 module, but also suggest that other signaling components are involved.

As immune MAPKs are known to often function redundantly, we investigated the possibility that MPK3, MPK6 and MPK4 are together upstream of SNC1 by crossing snc1-1 plants with these mapk mutants. Our results showed that mpk3-1 mutation had no effect on the snc1-1 phenotype (Fig. 6). In light of the partial reversion of MPK3-CA phenotype by the snc1-11 mutation20, it suggests that functional redundancy indeed occurs at the MAPK level. In addition, we found that the mpk6-2 mutation led to a partial reversion of snc1-1 phenotype, similar to the one induced by the chr5-1 mutation. It shows that MPK6 is upstream of SNC1 and even suggests, in comparison to the absence of reversion by the mpk3-1 mutation, that MPK6 has a more important role than MPK3 upstream of SNC1. Regarding MPK4, there was no apparent effect of the mpk4-3 mutation on the snc1-1 phenotype. However, we had to use the mpk4-3 summ2-8 double mutant for this genetic approach, and this double mutant does not display itself a WT-like phenotype. Besides, we have to consider the fact that there is no reversion by mpk3-1, while we know that MPK3 is indeed upstream of SNC1. We thus cannot conclude with confidence that MPK4 is or is not upstream of SNC1.

Several protein phosphatases are known to deactivate MPK3 and MPK6 by dephosphorylating the two residues of their activation loop required for their activation by MAP2Ks, including the dual-specificity phosphatase MAP kinase phosphatase1 (MKP1), the phosphotyrosine-specific protein phosphatase protein tyrosine phosphatase1 (PTP1) and the Ser/Thr protein phosphatase of the type 2C (PP2C) AP2C145–50. Interestingly, the mkp1-1 mutant (in Col background) displays an autoimmune phenotype that is partially suppressed by mpk6-2, mpk3-1 and snc1-11 mutants47. Besides, the ptp1-1 mutation enhanced the mkp1-1 autoimmune phenotype, and the suppressive effects of mpk6-2 and snc1-11 were weaker on the mkp1-1 ptp1-1 double mutant47. The connection between these MAPK protein phosphatases and SNC1 further support our results. Likewise, the mkp1-1 ap2c1 double mutant exhibited a severe autoimmune phenotype that could be partially suppressed by mpk6-2 and to a lesser extent by mpk3-1 mutations45. The authors did not report any genetic link between the mkp1-1 ap2c1 double mutant and SNC1, but it is highly probable that AP2C1 would be genetically linked to SNC1 as well.

Overall, our results and literature data suggest the model depicted in Fig. 7. We propose that MPK3 and MPK6, possibly MPK4 as well, contribute to the positive regulation of SNC1 expression at least in part through CHR5 function. It is expected that other components are involved besides CHR5. Likewise, SNC1 is not the only NLR gene positively regulated downstream of MAPKs20,34. Moreover, the link between SNC1 and MKP1 and PTP1 protein phosphatases was clearly established47, but knowing that MPK3 and MPK6 are also the substrates of other protein phosphatases51, it is probable that these phosphatases, such as AP2C1, connect to this network. In addition, the key ETI signaling component EDS1 connects downstream of the MPK3/6-CHR5-SNC1 module. Indeed, besides the above-mentioned strong reversion of MPK3-CA phenotype by eds1-2 mutation20, mkp1-1 and snc1-1 autoimmune phenotypes were reported to be partly and strongly suppressed by eds1-22 and eds1-2 ko mutations, respectively22,47. For more clarity, the presented model does not include EDS1-associated signaling components or other ETI actors that are known to be downstream of NLRs. While it is proposed that CHR5 would regulate SNC1 expression through the regulation of nucleosome occupancy in its promoter region30,31, the precise link between immune MAPKs and CHR5 has not been established yet. Our previous work suggests CHR5 threonine 1250 (T1250) as an in vivo putative target site for phosphorylation by these immune MAPKs33, whose function could potentially be to regulate CHR5 activity or binding to chromatin, for instance, according to known phosphorylation-dependent regulation mechanisms52. Interestingly, a large-scale report showed that MPK6 (MPK3 and MPK4 were not tested) was able to phosphorylate in vitro a CHR5 peptide harboring T125053. The hypothesis of CHR5 being a substrate of immune MAPKs is thus highly probable, but direct robust biochemical evidences are still lacking to fully support this assumption.

Our results indicate that CHR5 would not be the only signaling component downstream of MPK3/6 and upstream of SNC1. Of note, the chromatin-associated protein modifier of snc1 1 (MOS1) is known, besides CHR5, to positively regulate SNC1 expression31. In addition, MOS1 was identified as a putative target of immune MAPKs as well33. It would thus be interesting to investigate further the link between all these signaling components.

Genetic analyses of autoimmune mutants have played a key role in deciphering new immune signaling pathways and notably suppressor screens, such as the identification of modifier of snc1 (mos) mutants that suppress snc1-1 phenotype54,55. In this study and in previous reports, we identified several mutants that are able to suppress, at least partially, the autoimmune phenotype of the MPK3-CA line20,34. The search for additional suppressors of MPK3-CA phenotype should help unveil the involvement of new signaling components in MPK3-mediated immune responses. Finally, the PTI-ETI interplay, that has been the object of a greater attention these very last years, should also benefit from the study of autoimmune mutants, as in most cases NLR deregulation is the underlying mechanism at the origin of their phenotypes35.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Colombia-0 (Col-0) was used as wild-type (WT) plant for all experiments performed. All mutants used in this work were generated in the Col-0 genetic background. Those previously described were: chr5-1 (SAIL_504_D01)32, chr5-2 (SALK_020296)32, mpk3-1 (SALK_151594)56, mpk4-3 summ2-8 (EMS mutant, SAIL_1152_A06)39, mpk6-2 (SALK_073907)57, and snc1-1 (EMS mutant)22. The mpk3-1 MPK3-CA2 line, hereafter MPK3-CA, was reported earlier34. The multiple mutants generated in this study were obtained by genetic crosses. Homozygous T-DNA insertion mutants were identified by PCR-based genotyping, and homozygous EMS-generated mutations were identified by sequencing and/or (d)CAPS using the primers listed in Table S2.

For cultures on Jiffy pots in Percival growth chambers (rosette fresh weight measurement, trypan blue and DAB staining, ROS burst, and some quantitative RT-PCR experiments), seeds were previously stratified at 4 °C in water for at least four days, sown directly in soil, then grown at 20 °C day/18 °C night, 60% humidity, in long day conditions, i.e. 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, with 100–120 µmol m-2 s-1 light intensity, for 35 days unless otherwise stated. Sample randomization was applied to all these experiments.

For in vitro cultures in Petri dishes placed in growth chambers (immunoblotting assays and some quantitative RT-PCR experiments), seeds were surface-sterilized and stratified at 4 °C on growth medium for at least four days. The growth medium was composed of ½ Murashige and Skoog basal salts with minimal organics (Sigma M6899), 0.05% MES hydrate (Sigma M8250) and 0.5% agar type E (Sigma A4675). The pH was adjusted to 5.7 with KOH. Seedlings were grown at 22 °C day/20 °C night in long day conditions, i.e. 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, with 170–190 µmol m-2 s-1 light intensity, for 14 days.

Rosette fresh weight measurements

Plants were cultivated in Jiffy pots in Percival growth chambers for 35 days, unless otherwise stated. Representative pictures of all plant genotypes were processed with Fiji/ImageJ software (version 1.54p, http://imagej.org). The plant rosettes were cut and then weighed individually on a precision scale (Sartorius, model 1712001). Multiple comparisons were tested with the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test followed by Dunn post-hoc tests using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for p-values adjustment. Different letters above boxplots indicate statistical difference (p-value < 0.05) between the corresponding genotypes.

Trypan blue and DAB staining

For trypan blue staining, leaves from 35-day-old plants were boiled at 95 °C for 2 min in a trypan blue solution containing 0.03% trypan blue powder (Sigma T6146), 16.6% lactic acid (Fluka 69785), 16.6% phenol (Sigma P4557), 16.6% glycerol (Euromedex EU3550), and 50% ethanol. The samples were then washed with a 2.5 g/mL solution of chloral hydrate (Sigma C8383) and stored in a 50% glycerol solution. Staining with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Sigma D8001) was carried out on leaves from 35-day-old plants as described by Daudi and O’Brien58. Leaves were mounted on glass slides and photographs were taken with the ZEISS Axio Zoom.V16 stereo microscope. Four independent biological repeats were performed, with four plants of each genotype per biological repeat.

ROS burst

Oxidative burst detection was carried out on 4-mm-diameter leaf discs from 35-day-old plants. Discs were equilibrated overnight in water. Then, water was replaced with a solution containing 200 µM luminol (Sigma A8511), 10 µg mL-1 horseradish peroxidase (Sigma P6782) and 1 µM flg22 (GeneCust synthesis). Total light emission from 2 to 40 min was quantified using a TECAN infinite M200 Pro spectrophotometer and values are indicated as relative light units (RLU). Three independent biological repeats were performed and are represented on the graph. Multiple (> 2) comparisons were tested with the non-parametric two-sided Tukey test with a confidence level set to 95% using the nparcomp package in R59.

qRT-PCR experiments

For quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses, 14-day-old seedlings, cultivated in vitro in the conditions described above, were treated with 100 nM flg22 (GeneCust synthesis) for 0, 2 and 8 h. cDNA were synthesized from 500–1000 ng of total RNA using SuperScript™ II reverse transcriptase and oligo(dT)18 primers (ThermoFisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara RR820A) was used to prepare qPCR mixes. Primers (Table S2) were used at 400 nM final concentration. The following PCR program was used: 95 °C for 30 s; 40 × [95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s]. qPCR reactions were carried out with the CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad) and analyzed with the CFX Manager Software (Bio-Rad). SAND (AT3G28390) and ACTIN2 (AT3G18780) transcripts were used as references to assess the relative expression of the transcripts of interest. Four independent biological repeats were performed and are represented on the graphs. Multiple (> 2) comparisons were tested with the non-parametric two-sided Tukey test with a confidence level set to 95% using the nparcomp package in R59. Two-sample differences were tested with two-sided unpaired Student’s t-tests and p-value < 0.05 indicated a statistical difference.

Immunoblotting assays

14-day-old seedlings, cultivated in vitro in the conditions described above, were treated with 100 nM flg22 (GeneCust synthesis) for the indicated times and were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Seedlings were ground in a mortar with liquid nitrogen and equal amounts of plant powder were resuspended in extraction buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 1% Nonidet-P40 (Sigma chemicals) and protease inhibitors (Roche, Complete cocktail). After vortexing and incubation on ice for 10 min, samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 16000 g at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and their protein concentrations were determined by Bradford method (Thermo Scientific #1856209) using BSA standard (Thermo Scientific #23209) and a microplate reader at 595 nm (TECAN Infinite M200 Pro spectrophotometer). Normalized protein amounts were denatured by heating at 70 °C with SDS-sample buffer for 5 min. Protein samples were separated on SDS-PAGE gels of appropriate concentrations at a constant amperage of 15 mA per gel, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad #1620177) for 75 min at a constant voltage of 100 V. Blots were blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma A9647) in Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for at least 1 h and probed with primary antibodies in 5% BSA in TBST overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies (Ab), all from rabbits, were used as follows: anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) monoclonal Ab (Cell Signalling #4370) at 1/10000 dilution, anti-MPK3 polyclonal Ab (Sigma M8318) at 1/10000 dilution, anti-MPK4 polyclonal Ab (Davids Biotechnologie, previously described56), at 1/10000 dilution, anti-MPK6 polyclonal Ab (Sigma A7104) at 1/10000 dilution, and anti-Myc polyclonal Ab (Sigma C3956) at 1/10000 dilution. The secondary antibody, conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP), was used as follows: goat anti-rabbit-HRP polyclonal Ab (Sigma A6154) at 1/15000 dilution. HRP activity was detected with a chemiluminescent reagent (Bio-Rad #1705061) using a CCD imaging system (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP). Blots were finally stained with Coomassie blue for protein visualization and loading control. Image processing (cropping and slight uniform contrast) was realized with Fiji/ImageJ software (version 1.54p, http://imagej.org). Three independent biological replicates were performed with similar results.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with the R software (version 4.2.1, https://www.r-project.org) in the RStudio environment (version 2025.09.1 + 401, https://posit.co/products/open-source/rstudio). The statistical tests used are indicated for each experiment, as well as the sample size (n).
