Treatment, evidence, imitation, and chat
samweisenthal, Samuel J. Weisenthal samweisenthal@gmail.com

Abstract




Abstract

Large language models are thought to have potential to aid in medical decision making. We investigate this here. We start with the treatment problem, the patient’s core medical decision-making task, which is solved in collaboration with a healthcare provider. We discuss approaches to solving the treatment problem, including—within evidence-based medicine—trials and observational data. We then discuss the chat problem, and how this differs from the treatment problem—in particular as it relates to imitation. We then discuss how a large language model might be used to solve the treatment problem and highlight some of the challenges that emerge. We finally discuss how these challenges relate to evidence-based medicine, and how this might inform next steps.

The treatment problem

The treatment problem in medicine can be formulated as a decision analysis
[
75
,
64
]
or reinforcement learning problem
[

]
.

2.1
Patient utility optimization

In particular, we have, for patient characteristics,
X
, treatment,
T
, and “policy”,
\pi^{*},
that

\displaystyle T\sim{\pi^{*}}(T|X),

(1)

where

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}U(T,X),

(2)

where
U
is the patient’s utility, and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}U
stands for the patient’s expected utility. In more detail,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}U

\displaystyle=\int udP(u)

(3)

\displaystyle=\int\int\int udP(u|t,x)d\Pi(t|x)dP(x)

(4)

with

\pi

\int zdP(z)
defined, for CDF

as
\int zp(z)dz,
if

\sum_{z}zp(z),
if

The optimization in (

For example, suppose we wish to determine whether to recommend a statin to a patient who has certain characteristics,
X
, where

X=(\text{age, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, smoking status, etc...})^{T}.

We hope to maximize the patient’s overall utility,
U,
which will be a function of whether or not they took the statin,
T,
and their post-treatment state, which we can define as
X^{\prime}
and will include events that might occur, such as cardiovascular events (strokes, myocardial infarctions, etc) and side effects of statins (myopathy, etc). Sometimes, one writes (
3

X^{\prime},
but we suppress

here, since

is a function of

The policy,

in (

to a treatment decision,

This policy will maximize some function,
U(X^{\prime}),
in expectation.

The utility,

often varies by patient, making solving (
2

[
54
]
). We can attempt to solve it, however, using tools from evidence-based medicine.

2.2
Evidence-based medicine

Evidence-based medicine allows one to use data to address the treatment problem (
2
). The evidence can be derived from trials or observational data, both of which will be discussed below.

2.2.1
Trials

A trial involves experimentation, which is also known as randomization
[
27
]
(for examples of trials, see, e.g.,
Stone et al. 2014
or
Chaudhuri et al. 2023
). For treatment
T,
patient information
X,
and utility
U,
a trial would ideally give an estimate of
P(U|T,X)
(this gives the probability of, for example, the patient being well given no treatment, the patient being well given treatment, etc). Then, one would be able to plug estimates of the probabilities,
P(u|t,x),
into (
2
) and solve for
\pi
. This would give a direct solution to (
2
).

However, a trial does not usually provide
P(U|T,X),
possibly because it is difficult---from a statistical power perspective---to estimate something so complex as the joint distribution of utility.

The following indirect, heuristic approach is usually performed instead. First, one consults a trial for an estimate of a treatment effect,

\tau=EY[A=1]-EY[A=0],

which might correspond to whether a medication improves some outcome,
Y
(the outcome,

is a component of the patient post-treatment state,

One would also have an estimate of what would happen without a medication,

One can also avoid referencing a trial’s treatment effect directly by using guidelines. For example, for statins, one can invoke the US preventive taskforce (USPTF) guidelines

Note that the procedures described above might not solve the true treatment problem, (

(in such a case, it might be important to consider an approach for multiple outcomes, such as

\displaystyle\tau_{x}=EY[A=1|X=x]-EY[A=0|X=x],

(5)

which—better aligned with the conditional nature of

2

2.2.2
Observational data

In the same way one can obtain an estimate for treatment effect,
\tau,
from a trial, one can, in theory, obtain an estimate of a treatment effect from an observational study, as long as there is, among other conditions, proper adjustment for confounders
[
88
,
76
]
. For example, a confounder-adjusted treatment effect would be

\tau_{o}=EY(1)-EY(0),

where the subscript
o
denotes “observational,” and
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
are potential outcomes
[
88
]
. In particular, under causal assumptions—including the assumption that some variable
Z
contains all confounders—we have that

EY(t)=E(E(Y|t,Z)),

where the latter can be estimated with the observed data. Note that the no unmeasured confounders assumption cannot be verified with data alone.

Overall, observational data gives us a way to—in theory—work around the need for randomization, which is sometimes necessary for ethical reasons (e.g., one cannot randomize tobacco use). It is also thought that
\tau_{o}
might complement
\tau
for a variety of other reasons
[
7
,
90
,
17
,
5
]
. The conditional treatment effect described in (
5

[
53
]
).

Analogously to estimating
P(U|T,Z)
in a randomized controlled trial and then directly solving the treatment problem, (
2
)—as opposed to heuristically solving it with
\tau

2
) using observational data—as opposed to heuristically solving it with
\tau_{o}
. Solving the treatment problem in this way involves

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}U

\displaystyle=\int\int\int udP(u|t,z)d\Pi(t|z)dP(z)

\displaystyle=\int\int\int udP(u|t,z)d\Pi(t|z)\frac{d\Pi_{0}(t|z)}{d\Pi_{0}(t|z)}dP(z)

\displaystyle=E_{\pi_{0}}\left\{\frac{\pi}{\pi_{0}}U\right\},

(6)

where
\Pi_{0}
is the CDF of
\pi_{0},
the policy that generated the observed data (i.e.,
T\sim\pi_{0}(T|Z)).
The expression in (
2.2.2
) can then be estimated. This framework encompasses trials, which are equivalent to (
2.2.2
) with a treatment policy (propensity score),
\pi_{0}=0.5.

Maximizing the objective (

) with respect to

, offline-reinforcement learning
[

,

Some variation of (

,

,

It is difficult to say whether solving the policy search objective in (
2

2

Possibly, to obtain patient utilities, an agent, provider or machine, might need to be interactive, which is difficult to automate in a rigorous way. Nevertheless, the objective (
2

potentially gives us a way to avoid randomization (while still directly solving the treatment problem).

2.2.3
Challenges of evidence-based medicine

Overall, although evidence-based medicine takes us in the right direction, it is not a panacea. For example, in terms of trials, we must take into account the ethics of randomization. 2.3
Experience

For many medical decision problems, however, evidence-based studies might not exist. So, one must use other means to solve the treatment problem, (
2
). For example, one might use clinical intuition
[
36
]

to determine if a patient will have a cardiovascular event,
P(Y=1|T=0,x),
or to estimate
P(U|T,X)
more generally. This intuition is based on experience. For example, a provider might have seen that patients who do not take statins tend to have more adverse events, such as strokes, and this might give the provider intuition for the component of
P(U|T,X)
related to
P(\text{stroke}|\text{no statin},\text{age},\cdots).

Note that estimating
P(U|T,X)
via experience requires an ability to tabulate events into degrees of belief, or frequencies. This might involve creating and populating a mental database (such structure would ostensibly need to be programmed into a machine, if its engineers would like it to learn from experience), either with trial-and-error or observation. The former is precluded for the most part by ethics, so observation is usually used.

As discussed in Section
2.2.2
, however, observation is subject to confounding (which would occur, e.g., if patients who forego statins are usually sicker than those who take them).
It is interesting that human providers can rely so much on observation in their own experiential practice, despite the strong assumptions, and still generally make reasonable treatment decisions—although this may be because observation is often augmented by mechanistic knowledge, as we will now discuss.

2.4
Knowledge

In practice, any treatment strategy likely depends on some mechanistic understanding of disease
2
2

2

For an interesting discussion of knowledge in the context of clinical decision making, see
Tonelli 2006
.
. For example, this might be that statins decrease cholesterol via enzyme inhibition, that cholesterol is associated with obstructions that lead to cardiovascular events, and, therefore, that one should give statins when a patient has high cholesterol. Fundamentally, mechanistic knowledge allows providers to make statements about the likelihood of disease progression (cholesterol increase the likelihood of blockages and cardiovascular events), or treatment effects (statins inhibit enzymes and thus decrease the likelihood of cholesterol), without relying on direct experience (or evidence, which is experience in the collective sense).

The results of a trial, i.e., treatment effects such as
\tau,
are also a form of knowledge. Hence, the boundary between knowledge and evidence may be blurred. One can, however, distinguish quantitative and qualitative knowledge, where the former, which includes treatment effects, can be used to calculate (
2
) directly. Qualitative facts, however, can be powerful, especially when mechanisms are well understood.

2.5
Imitation

We can also imagine a healthcare provider who attempts to solve the treatment problem, (
2
), by just imitating others. The imitated treatment decision,
T,
would be

T\sim{\hat{\pi}}(T|X)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}=\arg\min_{\pi}KL(\pi,\pi_{0}),

(7)

where KL is Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
56
]
and
\pi_{0}
is the policy that is being imitated. If (
7
) is estimated with data, it is known as imitation learning/behavioral cloning
[
55
,
104
]
, and, in such a case, it is imitation of
\pi_{0},
the policy that generated the dataset (i.e., the providers in the hospital that generated the dataset were acting according to
\pi_{0}
). Imitation learning is also known as maximum likelihood estimation, since minimizing KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing likelihood
[
108
]
.

To employ an imitation strategy, one might see a patient with high cholesterol and recommend a statin, because that is what other providers do. Although imitation might reflect evidence, if those being imitated use evidence, imitation is often not ideal for solving (
2
). For example, if most providers do not know how to treat a certain disease, imitation will be problematic. Even more, the imitation objective in (
7
) does not take into account utility,
U
, which is the essence of (
2
). Imitation therefore cannot solve the treatment problem directly. This said, in medicine, imitation—to some degree—is important, since diverging from standard practices can be problematic.

2.6
Combining imitation and patient utility optimization

One can interpolate between imitation and utility maximization using methods from
[
91
]
. In particular,

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}U(T,X)-\lambda d(\pi,\hat{\pi}),

(8)

where
d
is some norm. A variation of (
8
) is considered for treatment problems (e.g., in
Futoma et al. 2020
and
Weisenthal et al. 2023
) and non-treatment problems (e.g., in
Farahmand et al. 2016
and
Ueno et al. 2012
).

It is difficult to know how to choose
\lambda,
which informs the tradeoff between imitation and utility maximization. However, in practice, a healthcare provider weighs their own treatment recommendations against prevailing practice, and, in the process, sets
\lambda
.

Overall, medical decision making is a difficult task, and it makes sense that evidence-based medicine has emerged to help. Decision-aids that help in other ways would ostensibly be welcome. We will now discuss chatbots and then the potential for language models to help with solving the treatment problem, (
2
).

3
The chat problem

Although the details of specific chatbots are not always made public, systems that solve the chat problem are comprised of language models trained within reinforcement learning frameworks
[
73
]
. The reinforcement learning component is structurally similar to (
8
). Therefore, there will be many analogies between treatment and chat. However, an agent that solves the chat problem does not necessarily solve the treatment problem.

3.1
Imitating chat

We started our discussion of the treatment problem with utility optimization, but we start our discussion of the chat problem with imitation. We do so because one can think of a chatbot’s engineers as prioritizing the Turing test
[
105
]
, which can be thought of—at some level—as a test for whether a machine can imitate a human. A language model that imitates a human would solve the following problem, where
Q
is a question, or prompt, and
A
is an answer,

\displaystyle A\sim{\hat{\pi}}_{c}(A|Q),

(9)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{c}=\arg\min_{\pi_{c}}KL(\pi_{c},{\pi_{0}}_{c}),

(10)

where
{\pi_{0}}_{c}
is the mapping that generated the observed text data (e.g., a set of human-generated question-answer pairs from the internet), and the subscript
c
denotes “chat” to distinguish
\pi_{c}
from the aforementioned treatment policy,
\pi,
in (
2
).

A system that solves the chat problem (
10
) might appear to have human-like qualities, and humans appear to solve the treatment problem, (
2
), but (
10
) and (
2
) are very different.

3.2
User utility

We will now introduce the idea of maximizing user utility, or user preference, a problem which is analogous to
2
and might be considered the primary
commercial
chat problem.

In solving the user utility problem, a chatbot provides a distribution from which an answer,
A,
rather than—as in (
2
)—a treatment,
T,
is drawn, and it is done conditionally on a prompt,
Q,
rather than patient information,
X.
In particular, a chatbot’s engineers aim to provide a response that maximizes user utility, which is drawn as

\displaystyle A\sim\pi^{*}_{c}(A|Q),

(11)

where the subscript
c,
as above in (
10
), denotes chat (in order to distinguish this policy from the aforementioned treatment policy,
\pi,
in (
2
)).

The distribution
\pi^{*}_{c}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{c}=\arg\max_{\pi_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S(A,Q),

(12)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S
represents, similarly to (
3
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S

\displaystyle=\int sdP(s)

(13)

\displaystyle=\int\int\int sdP(s|a,q)d\Pi_{c}(a|q)dP(q),

(14)

with
\Pi_{c}
the CDF of
\pi_{c}
, and
S
denoting user utility with respect to the chatbot’s answer. Then
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S(A,Q)
is the expected human user’s utility under arbitrary policy,
\pi_{c}
.

As an aside, one can also imagine a sort of post-answer state,
Q^{\prime}
(the post-answer state of the user), analogous to post-treatment state,
X^{\prime},
discussed in (
3
), but, as we did there, we suppress
Q^{\prime}
from (
13
), since
S(Q^{\prime})
is a function of it.

Note that (
12
) and (
2
) are similar in that they are both optimization problems. However, they have very different inputs, outputs, and utilities. Maximizing user utility, therefore, does not imply the ability to maximize patient utility. For example, ChatGPT’s medical recommendations might change if a user is asking from different perspectives (which has been confirmed empirically with respect to healthcare providers vs insurance agents
[
117
]
). However, the treatment problem only has one optimal solution, which corresponds to the patient’s utility.

3.3
Evidence

To truly maximize user utility,
S,
a chatbot is programmed to experiment with respect to its response,
A
. This is called “exploration” in reinforcement learning, and it is a form of “randomization” in evidence-based medicine. In other words, chatbot engineers are constantly running some version of randomized-controlled trials and then updating the chatbots with the results. It is important to remember that chatbot engineers have greater flexibility in terms of experimentation, because the stakes in chat are (in theory) lower than in healthcare.

Chatbot engineers might also incorporate varying degrees of observational data, which is known as off-policy reinforcement learning, analogous to the observational data treatment problem in (
2.2.2
). In particular, in doing so, they estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S=E_{\pi_{0c}}\left\{\frac{\pi_{c}}{\pi_{0c}}S\right\},

(15)

which depends on
\pi_{0c}
instead of
\pi_{0}
, as did (
2.2.2
), where the subscript “c” is again used to designate “chat.” Estimating (
15
) depends on observational data, but the chat problem is lower stakes, so the violation of any observational data analysis assumptions may not be impactful.

Overall, collecting evidence for the chat problem, whether this evidence be from randomization or observation, is easier than collecting evidence for the treatment problem, because the chat problem is lower stakes (this is not to say that models like ChatGPT do not have massive impact, especially at their current scale).

3.4
Combining imitation and user utility optimization

Chatbots are trained to limit their divergence from the original imitation policy,
\hat{\pi}_{c},
using methods similar to those in trust region policy optimization
[
91
]
. Hence, there is a tradeoff between imitation and utility-maximization that is similar to the tradeoff discussed for the treatment problem in (
8
). In particular, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{*}_{c}=\arg\max_{\pi_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{c}}S(A,Q)-\lambda d(\pi_{c},\hat{\pi}_{c}),

(16)

where
d
is some norm. By solving the tradeoff optimization in (
16
), a system like ChatGPT can incorporate user preferences while still seeming human in its responses (in contrast, a recommender system
[
61
]
focuses on user preferences, but it is not conversational).

As an aside, besides allowing for the tradeoff between utility and imitation,
[
91
]
was developed to stabilize utility optimization (and it has other functions related to safety
[
1
]
). In practice, when training chatbots, the Lagrangian form in (
16
) is likely replaced by its extension in
Schulman et al. 2017
(or something similar), which has computational advantages.

Out of the box, a model that solves (
16
) is not going to solve the treatment problem (
2
) in any formal sense. The differences between
\pi^{*}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{c}^{*}
are profound, because
S
and
U
,
A
and
T
, and
Q
and
X
are all unrelated. Further, the chat problem appears to fundamentally prioritize imitation, as in (
10
), instead of user utility, as in (
12
), where optimizing for the latter is often described as a sort of extra“fine-tuning”
[
118
]
.

3.5
Knowledge

Within a chat, a user might request that a chatbot change in some way. For example, the user might request that the chatbot “be more mindful,” in which case the chatbot can search for a book on mindfulness, read it, and change its style accordingly. This capability for search and within-chat behavioral modification can be useful for many problems in medicine (e.g., for adapting to a patient’s conversational preferences). As discussed in Section
2.3
, however, a chatbot cannot use knowledge obtained in this way to permanently change its internal parameters, independent of its engineers (in contrast, a human can change their internal parameters at will based on new information). A chatbot also has an internal store of knowledge in its parameters. This knowledge is, by design, not necessarily factual
[
12
]
—parameters depend on the objective functions for which they are the solutions, and (
10
) and (
16
) do not optimize for factual accuracy. However, some of it can be (somewhat) factual.

3.6
Other uses

Chatbots might not need to directly solve (
2
) to be helpful in medicine.

3.6.1
Medical knowledge

One could use a chatbot trained on (
16
) to help with searching and critically interacting with the medical literature
[
32
,
3
,
119
]
. In fact, in doing so, a chatbot trained to solve (
16
) may appear to act autonomously as a healthcare provider—it might field a question, search the literature, and develop a treatment plan. This is similar to how a chatbot might search the literature for instruction on how to become “mindful,” as discussed in Section
3.5
. Although human providers likely solve some medical problems in this way (when taking a knowledge-based rather than experience-based or evidence-based approach), one should be cautious with using chatbots in this way, as shown in
[
79
]
. Ultimately, it is important to remember the difference between (
16
) and (
2
); a chatbot, even if it searches the medical literature, is not solving the treatment problem.

Further, using a chatbot trained for (
16
) to solve (
2
) is indirect. Doing so is similar to a human investigator who would like to know the average blood pressure of a room full of people asking a chatbot to provide it. The Chatbot, with the internet at its disposal, might give a good guess. However, the investigator should have instead recorded each person’s blood pressure and taken an average, which is analogous to solving the treatment problem in (
2
) directly (via, e.g., a well-designed trial or using (
2.2.2
), if possible).

In terms of post-test probability, there is a similar tension between an indirect, “fast-thinking”
[
49
]

Overall, the treatment problem, (
2

3.6.2
Support

Many problems—especially those related to prevention or chronic disease—benefit from gestalt knowledge, availability, and patience. These applications can be profound—for example, narrative medicine is a well-established, beneficial approach (see e.g.
[
20
]
). A chatbot might—via qualities like patience and availability—support a patient to develop a narrative. Note that a chatbot’s qualities, however, will always be a function of its reward, which is specified by human engineers.

It is difficult to say at what point a medical problem becomes a good candidate for a chatbot’s involvement (perhaps low-stakes, transparent problems, which allow for experimentation and user awareness of potential errors, respectively). Likely, patients will begin to use chatbots for medical purposes long before any official approval.

Either way, asking a model trained according to (
16
) to support a patient or help with searching or interpreting the literature is not the same as deploying a system that directly solves the treatment problem, (
2
).

4
Language models and the treatment problem

Below, we will discuss how an engineer might train a chatbot-like system specifically to solve the treatment problem, (
2
). However, first, we will briefly discuss the imitation of medical notes.

4.1

4.1.1
Disconnect between appearance and substance

Unfortunately, a system that solves (
18

), can be dangerous in a field such as medicine.

In general, such disconnects appear to emerge with respect to language models (e.g., see
[
40
]
) . Possibly these disconnects are due to terminology, since much of the terminology used to describe chatbots involves anthropomorphization of mathematical concepts, which make it difficult to see connections between approaches (e.g., consider how we have (hopefully) shown the connection between (

Losing these connections in a high-stakes field like medicine can be problematic. For example, one might argue that the public has a healthy skepticism for statistical modeling, but not necessarily, yet, for “artificial intelligence,” even though the essences of these concepts, in particular as related to potential drawbacks (bias, variance, etc), are very similar (in fact, language models can be thought of as a form of regression). Healthy skepticism is essential for funding agencies, healthcare providers, and—most importantly—patients, because it helps them orient themselves with respect to how these new technologies might impact care.

4.2

Ultimately, one could conceive of a system that truly solves the direct treatment problem, (
2

This signal could come, for example, from follow-up notes. Obtaining utility in this way might have issues, such as loss to follow up, difficulty assessing utility, etc. However, these problems are fundamental to medical decision making, not to language models. 4.2.1
Experimentation

Chatbots conceivably excel at maximizing user utility in the chat problem, (
12
), because they can experiment with their responses (which likely does not disgruntle the users too much) and learn from it. To solve (
19
) in this way, the system would need to experiment on patients. This is not possible from an ethical standpoint (this is the case for human providers too, and hence trials have strict ethical oversight).

This inability to experiment will hinder progress in terms of using reinforcement learning to train a system to solve the treatment problem, (
2
), because reinforcement learning has traditionally thrived in game-like settings
[
94
]
, where experimentation is easy and can be done at scale (e.g., in chess, because all the rules are known, it is easy to simulate a million opponents and train the system by letting it play against them). If the success of systems like ChatGPT depends mostly on experimentation (and it is likely they do), we are unlikely to be able to use the same techniques to create a system that treats disease as well as ChatGPT engages users.

4.2.2
Observation

Interestingly, though, even though chat is not really a game in the way Chess or Go are games, ChatGPT still excels at chat. This may imply that off-policy learning contributes to ChatGPT’s success. In such a case, it is solving (

\hat{\pi}_{tc}

4.3
Next steps for solving the treatment problem

Note that when discussing the treatment problem in Section
4.2
, we have found ourselves again discussing the evidence-based medicine topics (randomization and observation) from Section
2.2

in (

However, as discussed in Section
4.1.1
, there is a large amount of hype associated with chatbots, which is likely due in part to terminology (as well as the myriad techniques that can be conceptualized to fall under the umbrella of artificial intelligence (see, e.g.,

Interestingly, many of the problems with evidence-based medicine relate closely—via dependence on probability, optimization, etc—to chatbots. Further, the new chatbot-invigorated interest in reinforcement learning (i.e., decision analysis) might help focus our efforts on the patient’s expected utility, which is a good thing for evidence-based medicine.

Also, let us not discount the potential for chatbots to help in other ways (besides solving the treatment problem directly), such as by offering support to the patient or by acting as a co-pilot for the provider when searching the literature. However, a chatbot cannot search literature that does not exist.

5
Summary and conclusion

Overall, medical decision making must start with maximizing patient utility. This is a difficult problem. In practice, it often requires that a healthcare provider balance evidence, knowledge, experience, and imitation.

It is important to understand that the chat problem, which focuses mostly on the imitation of conversation, is different from the treatment problem, which focuses mostly on optimizing patient utility. A chatbot, by nature, does not solve the treatment problem. This is not to say that a chatbot cannot aid in other ways, such as by helping providers search the literature or by supporting patients. However, these things are not directly related to solving the treatment problem.

Designing a system to solve the treatment problem is complex (imitating medical notes will not work here). The barriers to doing so are largely related to the ethics of experimentation. One may be able to work around this using observational data, which is a known research area within evidence-based medicine. Language models might help, but this is a moonshot problem.

Overall, however, while language models are interesting, it is important that the hype associated with chatbots and artificial intelligence not overshadow other important problems in evidence-based medicine, many of which are quite pressing.
