Convergence and stability of Q-learning in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning
Massimiliano Manenti, Andrea Iannelli

Abstract
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning promises, among other benefits, to efficiently capture and utilize the temporal structure of a decision-making problem and to enhance continual learning capabilities, but theoretical guarantees lag behind practice. In this paper, we propose a Feudal Q-learning scheme and investigate under which conditions its coupled updates converge and are stable. By leveraging the theory of Stochastic Approximation and the ODE method, we present a theorem stating the convergence and stability properties of Feudal Q-learning. This provides a principled convergence and stability analysis tailored to Feudal RL. Moreover, we show that the updates converge to a point that can be interpreted as an equilibrium of a suitably defined game, opening the door to game-theoretic approaches to Hierarchical RL. Lastly, experiments based on the Feudal Q-learning algorithm support the outcomes anticipated by theory.



Keywords:
Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning, Feudal Q-learning, Two-Timescale Stochastic Approximation, ODE method.

The main goal of our work is to contribute to the Feudal RL theory by proposing a Feudal Q-learning algorithm with proven theoretical guarantees.

In a two-level Feudal RL algorithm, two policies are interacting with each other. The high-level policy learns which goals to set for the low-level policy, which, in turn, learns how to achieve them.
Broadly speaking, Feudal Q-learning consists of two interconnected Q-learning update rules that influence each other using data generated from a stochastic process.
To rigorously analyze such intertwined updates under stochastic data, we rely on the Stochastic Approximation (SA) framework
[
34
]
,
[
5

[
4
]
.
By leveraging the ODE method
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
, we study the update rules as coupled dynamical systems, which directly tackles the non-stationarity problem.
We consider infinite-horizon Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with finite state and action spaces and, by analyzing Feudal Q-learning through the lens of TTSA, we are able to prove convergence and stability of the updates.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, our work is the first to prove stability of an HRL algorithm and convergence for Feudal Q-learning updates that are interdependent.
The closest works in the literature are
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
.
In
[
6
]
, the authors define a high-level infinite-horizon MDP and a low-level episodic MDP that is independent from the high-level policy.
Assuming convergence in the low-level MDP, convergence in the high-level MDP is proven
[
6
, Theorem 2]
.
Our work differs from
[
6
]
as two interconnected infinite-horizon MDPs are considered, and a coupled two-timescale analysis is performed, showing both convergence and stability of the updates.
Our setup can bring some benefits, for example, it is easy to consider an extrinsic reward in the low-level infinite-horizon problem, which is shown to be beneficial in practice
[
41
]
.
Moreover, in our framework, we do not need to assume that the duration of the low-level episode is sampled from a geometric distribution, which can potentially result in arbitrary long episodes.
Another drawback of the setup in
[
6
]
is that the choice of the low-level discount factor is tied to the choice of the expected episode duration.
In
[
7
]
, the work in
[
6
]
is extended by considering the convergence of Q-learning deep RL methods with non-stationary features that are convergent.

Another benefit of the approach we propose is that, by directly linking Feudal RL to the theory of TTSA,
one could leverage results present in the vast literature of TTSA, e.g., finite-time guarantees
[
14
]
,
[
8
]
, or multi-timescales
[
11
]
that would enable deeper hierarchies.
Thus, TTSA theory is not only employed to establish convergence and stability, but also to introduce a new perspective on HRL, by demonstrating that a Feudal RL problem can be rigorously cast within the TTSA framework.
This is an important step towards closing the gap between theory and practice in HRL, as seeing the HRL problem as a TTSA one can help in designing new and efficient algorithms with theoretical guarantees.

An additional contribution of our work is to formalize a game theoretic interpretation of this HRL problem.
Game theory and RL are closely related fields, as both deal with optimal decision-making.
Probably the most popular connection is between game theory and multi-agent RL (MARL)
[
44
]
, as MARL involves multiple agents interacting in a shared environment.
It is interesting to note a more subtle connection between HRL and game theory:
even though the original problem setup involves finding only one agent, we are decomposing the original MDP into multiple levels, thus we can see each level as an agent in a multi-agent system.
We highlight that our analysis shows that the updates converge to a point that can be interpreted as a Nash and Stackelberg equilibrium,
opening the door to game-theoretic approaches to HRL.

2.1
High-level MDP

Let us now introduce the quantities composing the high-level MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{h}}=\langle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}},\Omega,P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}},r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}},\gamma^{\mathrm{h}}\rangle
.
We choose
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}=\mathcal{S}
, and, for ease of exposition and without loss of generality,
\Omega=\mathcal{S}
.
The variable
s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}
represents the state of the high-level MDP at time
t
and corresponds to the original MDP state
s_{tT}
,
whereas
\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t}\in\Omega
is the action of the high-level MDP at time
t
, i.e., the goal for the low-level MDP.
\pi^{\mathrm{h}}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}\times\Omega\to[0,1]
represents the high-level policy selecting goals.

P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1}|s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})
and
r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})
describe, respectively, the dynamic and the reward in the high-level MDP.
Before describing in more detail
P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
and
r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
, let us introduce some useful quantities.

A decision epoch is a time step in the low-level MDP where the high-level MDP has made a decision, i.e., all the low-level time steps belonging to the set
\{0,T,2T,\ldots\}
.
In order to take into account the time spent between decision epochs, we introduce the discrete variable
s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{de}}
, where
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{de}}:=\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
.
The variable
s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}
is recursively defined as:

s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k+1}=\begin{cases}s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}+1,\ &\mathrm{if}\ s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}\neq T-1\\
0,\ &\mathrm{if}\ s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}=T-1\end{cases},\quad\text{with }s^{\mathrm{de}}_{0}=0.

Given the deterministic evolution of
s^{\mathrm{de}}
, one can easily find the probability
P^{\mathrm{de}}(s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k+1}|s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k})
of transitioning to
s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k+1}
given
s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k}
.
At time step

\pi^{\mathrm{l}}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
selects an action
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
to apply to the real system.
We are now ready to present
P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
and
r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
.

The high-level dynamic is:

P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1}|s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{1},\ldots,s_{T-1}\in\mathcal{S}\\
a_{0},\ldots,a_{T-1}\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{T}P(s_{i}|s_{i-1},a_{i-1})\pi^{\mathrm{l}}(a_{i-1}|s_{i-1},\omega^{\mathrm{l}}_{i-1},s^{\mathrm{de}}_{i-1})

(2)

where
s_{0}=s_{t}^{\mathrm{h}}
,
s_{T}=s_{t+1}^{\mathrm{h}}
, and
\omega^{\mathrm{l}}_{i}=\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t}
,
s^{\mathrm{de}}_{i}=i
,
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
.

2
) can be found in Appendix
B

It is clear from Eq. (
2
) that the dynamic of the high-level MDP depends on the low-level policy, we highlight this dependence by placing the subscript

Since the high-level policy should learn how to solve the original MDP

The high-level reward is:

where
\gamma^{\mathrm{h}}\in(0,1)
.

The high-level Q-function

is defined as:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{N}(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{tT}r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})\ \big|\ s^{\mathrm{h}}_{0}=s^{\mathrm{h}},\ \omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{0}=\omega^{\mathrm{h}}\big]

(4)

With the definitions of

and

, and by setting

, if we consider a trajectory of the system,

In this way, it is possible to compare the performance of a flat RL algorithm and a Feudal one.

2.2
Low-level MDP

Recall that the low-level MDP is
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{l}}:=\langle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}},\mathcal{A},P^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}}},r^{\mathrm{l}},\gamma^{\mathrm{l}}\rangle

Here, the state space is

s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k}:=(s_{k},\omega^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},s^{\mathrm{de}}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}

, the goal to be tracked

The low-level dynamic is:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{l}},a)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}\big[\sum_{k=0}^{N}(\gamma^{\mathrm{l}})^{k}r^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})\ \big|\ s^{\mathrm{l}}_{0}=s^{\mathrm{l}},\ a_{0}=a\big]

where

2.3
Problem statement

Having defined the high- and low-level MDPs, we can now state the problem we want to solve.
Our goal is to find the optimal high- and low-level Q-functions,
Q^{\mathrm{h},*}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},*}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, that satisfy, for every state-action pair, the Bellman equations:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{h},*}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})=r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l},*}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})+(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l},*}}\big[\max_{\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1}}Q^{\mathrm{h},*}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1})\big]

(7a)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{l},*}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})=r^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})+\gamma^{\mathrm{l}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h},*}}\big[\max_{a_{k+1}}Q^{\mathrm{l},*}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k+1},a_{k+1})\big]

(7b)

\displaystyle\text{with }\pi^{\mathrm{h},*}\in\argmax_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}}}Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l},*}}\text{ and }\pi^{\mathrm{l},*}\in\argmax_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}Q^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h},*},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}

(7c)

It is interesting to note that the Bellman equation for one level, e.g., Eq. (
7a
), depends on the policy of the other level, e.g,
\pi^{\mathrm{l},*}
.
This is a non-stationarity issue typical of HRL algorithms that complicates their analysis
[
24
]
,
[
28
]
.

The goal that we want to reach is to jointly learn optimal Q-functions, while the other level is using the optimal policy.

3
Convergence and stability guarantees of Feudal Q-learning

In this Section, in order to solve the problem in Eq. (
7
), we will propose an update rule based on Q-learning.
Since the TTSA theory constitutes our primary theoretical framework, we will briefly recall its main elements and employ it to derive insights into the behaviour of the proposed Feudal Q-learning updates.
The Feudal Q-learning updates,
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}
, where
n>0
represents the iteration, will be shown to converge to the optimal high- and low-level Q-functions,
Q^{\mathrm{h},*}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},*}
in Eq. (
7
).
Moreover, we will prove that the learning process is stable, thus
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}
will always have finite values.

3.1
Feudal Q-learning updates

As the Feudal Q-learning updates will depend on trajectories collected through experiments or simulations, before presenting the update rule, we will make an assumption on how the data is collected.

Assumption 3

(On-policy updates and Lipschitz continuous policies)
.

The policies,
\pi^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
\pi^{\mathrm{l},n}
, that collect trajectories used to update
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}
depend respectively on
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}
, i.e.,
\pi^{\mathrm{h},n}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}(Q^{\mathrm{h},n})
and
\pi^{\mathrm{l},n}=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{l},n}(Q^{\mathrm{l},n})
, where the operators
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{l},n}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
denote the policy extraction methods.

Moreover, the high-level policy is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
,
i.e.,
\forall n,\ \exists L_{\pi^{\mathrm{h},n}}^{Q^{\mathrm{h}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\ :\forall(Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{1},Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}
:

|\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}(Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{1})-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}(Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{2})|\leq L_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}}}^{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}\left\lVert Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{1}-Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{2}\right\rVert_{\infty}

An analogous assumption holds for the low-level policy, with a constant
L_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}^{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}
.

Concerning Assumption
3
, a Boltzmann exploration policy satisfies the Lipschitz continuous assumption
[
18
]
.

We are now ready to present the Feudal Q-learning updates:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{h},n+1}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})=

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})+

\displaystyle+\beta(n)[\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{k}r(s_{tT+k},a_{tT+k})+(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{T}\max_{\omega}Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1},\omega)-Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})]

(8a)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{l},n+1}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})=

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{l},n}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})+

\displaystyle+\alpha(n)[r^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})+\gamma^{\mathrm{l}}\max_{a}Q^{\mathrm{l},n}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k+1},a)-Q^{\mathrm{l},n}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})]

(8b)

with step-sizes
\alpha(n),\ \beta(n)>0
.

There is no requirement on how to initialize the iterative updates,
e.g.,
Q^{\mathrm{h},0}
and
Q^{\mathrm{h},0}
can be set to zero or they can be optimistically initialized
[
17
]
.

3.2
TTSA and its connection to Feudal Q-learning

Pioneered by
[
34
]
, SA algorithms are a family of iterative methods to solve root finding problems, e.g., finding
z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(z^{*})=0
, where only noisy data is available, e.g., we only have access to
f(z_{n})+M_{n+1}
, where
M_{n+1}
is a martingale difference sequence playing the role of the noise. A classic Single Timescale SA scheme can be written as:

z_{n+1}=z_{n}+\alpha(n)[f(z_{n})+M_{n+1}]

(9)

where
z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the iterates of the algorithm. When referring to Two-Timescale SA schemes, the updates are:

\displaystyle x_{n+1}

\displaystyle=x_{n}+\alpha(n)[h(x_{n},y_{n})+M^{(1)}_{n+1}],

(10a)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\beta(n)[g(x_{n},y_{n})+M^{(2)}_{n+1}],

(10b)

where
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
can be thought of as the Two-Timescale counterpart of
z_{n}
.

Two fundamental properties of an SA scheme are convergence and stability of the algorithm.
Convergence refers to having the iterates converging almost surely (a.s.) to
z^{*}
, stability indicates that the iterates remain bounded (preferably without the need of projecting the iterates into a predefined set)
[
5
]
.
A notable method to analyze the convergence and stability of SA schemes is the so-called ODE method
[
3
]
. The central idea is to show that the iterate
z_{n}
”tracks” the continuous time ODE
\dot{z}(t)=f(z(t))
, whose solutions converge to
z^{*}
.
The ODE method is supported by a rich theoretical foundation both for the Single Timescale case and the Two-Timescale case
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
27
]
.

Concerning our Feudal Q-learning problem, we firstly cast the updates in Eq. (
8
) as Eq. (
10
), with
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
respectively representing
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
.
Secondly, similar to
[
5
, Chapter 8]
, we study the related singularly perturbed ODEs:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}h(x(t),y(t)),

(11a)

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=g(x(t),y(t))

(11b)

that the iterates
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
should track.
The vector-valued function
h
has as component
h_{s^{\mathrm{l}},a}(x_{n},y_{n})
, related to the low-level state and action pair
(s^{\mathrm{l}},a)
, the low-level Bellman error:

h_{s^{\mathrm{l}},a}(x_{n},y_{n}):=r^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}},a)+\gamma^{\mathrm{l}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{P^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}}}}[\max_{a^{+}}Q^{\mathrm{l},n}(s^{\mathrm{l},+},a^{+})]-Q^{\mathrm{l},n}(s^{\mathrm{l}},a)

(12)

while the vector-valued function
g
has as component
g_{s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}}(x_{n},y_{n})
, related to the high-level state and action pair
(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})
, the high-level Bellman error:

g_{s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}}(x_{n},y_{n}):=r^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})+(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{P^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}}[\max_{\omega^{+}}Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h},+},\omega^{+})]-Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})

(13)

For more details, see Appendix
A
.
The parameter
\epsilon>0
is the singular perturbation parameter and, by having
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, we impose a faster,
x(t)
, and a slower,
y(t)
, dynamic.
This timescale separation is obtained in the Feudal Q-learning algorithm by imposing the condition
\frac{\beta(n)}{\alpha(n)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
on the step-sizes, which motivates part of the following assumption;
the remaining parts are standard Robbins-Monro conditions
[
34
]
.

Assumption 4

(Step-sizes)
.

The step-sizes satisfy the following conditions:

1.

the step-sizes are not summable:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\alpha(n)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\beta(n)=\infty,

2.

the step-sizes are square-summable:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\big(\alpha(n)^{2}+\beta(n)^{2}\big)<\infty,

3.

in the limit, the ratio between the step-sizes satisfies:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\beta(n)}{\alpha(n)}=0.

Within the TTSA framework, the timescale separation ensures that the iterates of the algorithm in Eq. (
10
) ”track” the solutions of the following ODEs:

\derivative{x}{t}=h(x(t),y)

(14)

where
y
is held fixed as a constant parameter, and:

\derivative{y}{t}=g(\lambda(y(t)),y(t))

(15)

where
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
.

One can easily see from Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) that equilibria of the ODEs (
14
) and (
15
) correspond to solutions of the Bellman equations.
Thus, studying the asymptotic behaviour of the ODEs, as done in Lemmas
1
and
2
, is of great interest and sheds light on the behaviour of the algorithm.

Lemma 1

(Existence and property of the globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of
h
)
.

The ODE (
14
) has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
\lambda(y)
. Moreover,
\lambda
is a Lipschitz map.

Lemma 2

(Existence of the globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of
g
)
.

The ODE (
15
) has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
y^{*}
.

For a proof of Lemmas
1
and
2
, which will be used to prove Theorem
1
, see Appendix
C
.

An interpretation of Lemma
1
is that, for a fixed high-level Q-function,
y
, the low-level Q-function,
x
, converges to the solution of the low-level Bellman equation.
Thus, while the high-level Q-function is slowly varying, the low-level Q-function quickly converges to the optimal low-level Q-function.
Even though it is quasi-static from the low-level Q-function perspective, the high-level Q-function is not constant.
Lemma
2
is telling us that the high-level Q-function converges to the solution of the high-level Bellman equation, where the low-level Q-function is assumed to be always at its optimal value.

3.3
Theoretical guarantees

After establishing the remaining assumptions, this subsection presents the main theoretical result of the paper.

Since we are using an on-policy algorithm, we would like to be greedy in the limit, in order to converge to the optimal policies.
Furthermore, we would like to assure infinite state-action visitation, which is a standard requirement in Q-learning
[
42
]
.
For this reason, we require the following assumptions, which are stated for the sake of readability only for the high-level policy, but apply to the low-level one as well.

Assumption 5

(Greedy in the limit policies)
.

Assume, for the sake of simplicity, that for a given state
s^{\mathrm{h}}
,
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega)
has a unique maximizer
\omega^{\mathrm{h}}
.
The high-level policy is greedy in the limit, i.e., as
n\to\infty
:

\pi^{\mathrm{h},n}(\omega^{\mathrm{h}}|s^{\mathrm{h}})=\begin{cases}1,&\omega^{\mathrm{h}}=\argmax_{\omega\in\Omega}Q^{\mathrm{h},n}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega)\\
0,&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

(16)

Assumption 6

(Infinite exploration policies)
.

Consider a high-level trajectory
(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and denote with
n_{t}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})
the number of times action
\omega^{\mathrm{h}}
has been chosen in state
s^{\mathrm{h}}
during the first
t
time steps,
i.e.,
n_{t}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}):=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathbb{1}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{i}=s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{i}=\omega^{\mathrm{h}})
, where
\mathbb{1}(\cdot)
represents the indicator function.
We assume that,
\forall(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}\times\Omega
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}n_{t}(s^{\mathrm{h}},\omega^{\mathrm{h}})=\infty
a.s.

Policies satisfying Assumptions
5
and
6
are referred to as Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration (GLIE), see
[
38
]
for a Boltzmann exploration policy and an
\epsilon
-greedy policy that are GLIE.

An additional assumption is needed to prove the stability of the algorithm
[
22
]
.

Assumption 7

(Compact convergence)
.

The policies
\pi^{\mathrm{h}}_{c}:=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}(cQ^{\mathrm{h}}),c\geq 1,
satisfy
\pi^{\mathrm{h}}_{c}\to\pi^{\mathrm{h}}_{\infty}
as
c\to\infty
, uniformly on compacts for some
\pi^{\mathrm{h}}_{\infty}
.

See
[
22
, Proposition 11]
for a Boltzmann policy that satisfies Assumption
7
. Moreover, an analogous assumption applies for the low-level policy.

Having laid out the necessary assumptions, we are now in a position to state the main theorem.

Theorem 1

(Convergence and stability of Feudal Q-learning)
.

If Assumptions
1
-
7
hold,
then:

1.

Feudal Q-learning converges to the optimal values:

\lim_{n\to\infty}(Q^{\mathrm{h},n},Q^{\mathrm{l},n})=(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}),\text{ a.s.};

(17)

2.

the iterates
(Q^{\mathrm{h},n},Q^{\mathrm{l},n})
are stable:

\sup_{n}(||Q^{\mathrm{h},n}||+||Q^{\mathrm{l},n}||)<\infty,\text{ a.s.}

(18)

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

This result provides the first convergence and stability guarantees on Q-learning for Feudal RL.
While relying on classical techniques used for analysis of RL algorithms
[
3
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
25
]
,
this result represents an important addition to the existing literature.
Firstly, in order to leverage the existing theory on TTSA, the updates had to be cast as on-policy updates (Assumption
3
).
Otherwise, the terms
M^{(1)}_{n+1}
and
M^{(2)}_{n+1}
would not be martingale difference sequences, resulting in a nonvanishing bias.
Secondly, having cast the Feudal Q-learning updates within the TTSA framework and interpreted the algorithm as coupled slow and fast updates,
one can view the design of HRL algorithms as a problem of synthesizing the underlying ODEs.
In particular, modifying the mean fields
h
and
g
or introducing structured gains
[
12
]
offer a principled route to derive variants with improved transient behaviour or asymptotic properties.
Lastly, as reported in Section
3.2
, our result sheds light on the behaviour of HRL algorithms. Qualitatively, faster updates in the low-level, which translates in our setup in bigger step-sizes for the low-level, are beneficial for convergence.

4
Game-Theoretic Interpretation

In this section, we highlight the connection between HRL and game theory.
In particular, we focus on the proposed Feudal Q-learning scheme and discuss how it can be interpreted as a two-player game,
whose solution is, in this special case, both a Nash equilibrium and a Stackelberg equilibrium.

Learning in the high- and low-level MDP can be seen as a two-player game, where the high-level player is trying to maximize
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
and the low-level player is trying to maximize
Q^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
.
The agents are learning Q-functions
Q^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},n}

and, through mappings
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{h},n}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{l},n}
, use them to derive their policies, which will be greedy in the limit as in Assumption
5
.
For this reason, with a slight abuse of notation, we sometimes replace the policy symbol with a Q-function.

We then define the optimal Bellman operators
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}

and
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{l}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}}}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
as:

(\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}Q^{\mathrm{h}})(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t}):=r^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})+(\gamma^{\mathrm{h}})^{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}\big[\max_{\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1}}Q^{\mathrm{h}}(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t+1})\big],\quad\quad\forall(s^{\mathrm{h}}_{t},\omega^{\mathrm{h}}_{t})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}\times\Omega

(19)

(\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{l}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}}}Q^{\mathrm{l}})(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k}):=r^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})+\gamma^{\mathrm{l}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}}}\big[\max_{a_{k+1}}Q^{\mathrm{l}}(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k+1},a_{k+1})\big],\quad\quad\forall(s^{\mathrm{l}}_{k},a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}\times\mathcal{A}

(20)

A standard result in RL states that a greedy policy with respect to the optimal Q-function, i.e., the Q-function that is a fixed point of the optimal Bellman operator, represents an optimal policy
[
39
, Chapter 3]
.
This motivates the following definitions
[
36
]
. We call high-level rational reaction set,
D^{\mathrm{h}}
, and low-level rational reaction set,
D^{\mathrm{l}}
, the sets:

D^{\mathrm{h}}:=\{(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\ |\ Q^{\mathrm{h}}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}Q^{\mathrm{h}}\}

(21)

D^{\mathrm{l}}:=\{(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{h}}|\times|\Omega|}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{l}}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}\ |\ Q^{\mathrm{l}}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{l}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}}}Q^{\mathrm{l}}\}

(22)

For completeness, we introduce the definitions of Nash equilibrium and Stackelberg equilibrium
[
36
]
,
[
37
]
.

Definition 1

(Nash equilibrium)
.

A pair
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})
such that
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{h}}\cap D^{\mathrm{l}}
is a Nash equilibrium.

Definition 2

(Stackelberg equilibrium)
.

A pair
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
such that:

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}\geq Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}},\quad\forall(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}.

(23)

is a Stackelberg equilibrium with player
\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}
as a leader and player
\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}
as a follower.

Now, we are ready to state and prove Lemma
3
, connecting our HRL updates to game theory.

Lemma 3
.

The pair
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})
found by the Feudal Q-learning updates is both a Nash equilibrium and a Stackelberg equilibrium
where the high-level is the leader and the low-level is the follower.

Proof.

In order to show that
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*}
,
Q^{\mathrm{l},*})
is a Nash equilibrium, we need to show that
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})\in D^{\mathrm{h}}\cap D^{\mathrm{l}}
.
It is immediate to verify it noticing that
Q^{\mathrm{h},*}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},*}
are satisfying Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
), i.e., they are verifying the conditions

Q^{\mathrm{h},*}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\text{l,*}}}Q^{\mathrm{h},*}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l},*}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{l}}_{Q^{\text{h,*}}}Q^{\mathrm{l},*}
.

We now want to show that the solution found by the Feudal Q-learning updates is a Stackelberg equilibrium, where the high-level is the leader and the low-level is the follower. In order to show it, we need to show that:

1.

(Q^{\mathrm{h},*}
,
Q^{\mathrm{l},*})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
,

2.

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}}\geq Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}},\quad\forall(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
.

Point 1. is satisfied using the result stated in the paragraph on Nash equilibrium, since
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})\in D^{\mathrm{h}}\cap D^{\mathrm{l}}
implies that
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
.

Point 2. can be shown by contradiction.
Assume that there exists one or more pairs
(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
such that
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}>Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}}
and
(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\neq(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})
. Let us call
\mathcal{C}
the non-empty set of such pairs.

Let us consider the pair
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})
that is maximizing
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}
, i.e.,
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}

\geq

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}\ \forall(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in\mathcal{C}
.

The condition
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
implies satisfying Eq. (
7b
), i.e., finding the unique fixed point satisfying
\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{l}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}}\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}
.

For a given low-level Q-function,
Q^{\mathrm{l}}
, the optimal high-level Q-function is unique and it is the only fixed point of the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{l}}}
.
Thus,
\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}
.
Thus we have found the unique pair
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})
satisfying
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{h}}\cap D^{\mathrm{l}}
.
Being the unique pair satisfying
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{h}}\cap D^{\mathrm{l}}
and since
(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})
satisfies the condition, then
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}

=

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}}
and
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})=(Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*})
, reaching a contradiction.

Moreover, the other pairs
(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})
in
\mathcal{C}
either lead to
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}

=

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}
or
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}}}

>

Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}
.
On the one hand, the equality case can be ruled out with arguments similar to the ones used for the pair
(\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{h}},\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{l}})
. On the other hand, the inequality case would result in
(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})
pairs for which
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}<Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}}
.

Thus, there is no other pair
(Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}})\in D^{\mathrm{l}}
such that
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h}},Q^{\mathrm{l}}}>Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{Q^{\mathrm{h},*},Q^{\mathrm{l},*}}
.

∎

8a
and

, we test our HRL algorithm in the Four Rooms Minigrid environment

shown in Figure

6
Conclusions and Outlook

We presented an HRL scheme, termed Feudal Q-learning, and theoretical results stating its convergence and stability.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge this is the first work guaranteeing stability of the hierarchical algorithm.

The non-stationarity problems typical of HRL are directly tackled using a timescale separation stemming from the theory of singularly perturbed ODEs.
The dynamical system interpretation of the algorithm enabled a general analysis that delivered new theoretical insights on sufficient conditions for achieving desired properties of the algorithm.
Future work could include analyzing HRL algorithms with other system theoretic techniques, e.g., the small gain theorem, or designing alternative HRL algorithms by employing the ODE method as described in
[
27
, Chapter 8]
.

Additionally, the proposed scheme naturally connects to the field of game theory:
we have multiple agents interacting,
\pi^{\mathrm{h}}
and
\pi^{\mathrm{l}}
, while having potentially different goals, i.e., respectively maximizing
Q^{\mathrm{h}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
and
Q^{\mathrm{l}}_{\pi^{\mathrm{h}},\pi^{\mathrm{l}}}
. Future work should further explore the connections between HRL and game theory.
