Women’s postpartum adjustment questionnaire (WPPAQ): development and psychometric evaluation in an exploratory sequential mixed methods study
Authors: Mahboobeh Asadi, Mahnaz Noroozi, Mousa Alavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpartum women experience a range of physical, psychological, and social changes. Identifying and helping maladjusted women requires a comprehensive and efficient questionnaire. This mixed methods study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the women’s postpartum adjustment questionnaire (WPPAQ) across a sample of Iranian postnatal women.Materials and methodsThis study employed the impact item method and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to ascertain the psychometric properties and construct validity of the developed questionnaire. The primary questionnaire was developed based on a qualitative study and literature review. A principal components analysis (35 items) with Varimax rotation was undertaken in a cross-sectional study on 203 postnatal women.ResultsIn the qualitative study and literature review, of the 34 items extracted, those with an impact item index of over 1.5, a content validity ratio of over 0.49, and a content validity index of over 0.79 were considered as valid. EFA reduced the list of the items to 30 with five factors capable of forecasting 57.19% of the variance. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for WPPAQ was 0.912.ConclusionsBased on the results, WPPAQ designed in this study is valid and reliable for assessing women’s adjustment to postpartum changes and enabling healthcare providers to identify women who are not adequately adjusted to these changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09095-0.Keywords: Adjustment, Postpartum, Psychometrics, Mixed methods, Iran

Background

The postpartum period is considered a critical and challenging time for women, newborns, and families. Women undergo numerous physical, psychological, social, economic, and familial changes, which can be stressful and challenging for them, making them more susceptible to the onset of serious psychological problems [1, 2]. Postpartum depression and mood disorders are observed in 20% of women [3]. Moreover, other family members can be so affected that postpartum depression in women can predict depression and anxiety in their partners [4, 5]. Further, postpartum psychological disorders in the mother can weaken the mother-child bond and lead to disorders in cognitive and physical development in the child [6, 7]. Meanwhile, postpartum psychological problems inflict heavy costs on the healthcare system. A study on the subject in South America found that anxiety and depression linked to pregnancy and delivery cost society about 2.8 billion dollars a year [8]. However, women’s adjustment to postpartum changes can prevent or minimize these costs.

Adjustment refers to a deliberate and conscious change in behavior made through constant efforts to cope with a specific stressful situation [9]. Following the delivery, the adjusted women appraise the situation and try to manage it using the available resources. Meanwhile, if this does not take place, they can become overwhelmed and stressed [10]. Screening with appropriate tools is critical in addressing these problems [11], enabling healthcare professionals to identify women’s needs and address them promptly [1]. Different tools have been designed for this purpose, some assessing a specific adjustment. For instance, the Postpartum Adjustment Questionnaire (PPAQ) is a tool to ascertain postpartum social adjustment [12]. Further, the Barkin Index of Maternal Functioning is a suitable tool for evaluating an individual’s perception of their functioning, though it is not applicable for assessing problems such as anxiety [13]. Some questionnaires are designed for a specific culture, such as the Being a Mother Scale, which was developed for English women and may not apply to other cultures [14]. Similarly, social and cultural factors have not been considered in the design of the Postpartum Adjustment Self-Assessment Questionnaire [15]. The existence of several tools to evaluate women’s adjustment to postpartum changes highlights the importance of the subject and reflects the multifaceted nature of the problem along this period [16]. It seems necessary to develop a questionnaire to evaluate women’s adjustment to postpartum changes that can distinguish itself from existing scales in its capacity to evaluate both cultural relevance and psychological, social, and functional adjustment concurrently. In response to the identified gaps in existing tools, this study aimed to develop and evaluate the psychometric properties of a culturally sensitive and multidimensional instrument— women’s postpartum adjustment questionnaire (WPPAQ).

Materials and methods

In this mixed methods study applying an exploratory sequential approach, a questionnaire was designed for ascertaining women’s adjustment to postpartum changes and its psychometric properties were analyzed from December 2019 to June 2020 in Isfahan, Iran (Fig. 1). This study sought to answer the question of whether the designed WPPAQ has appropriate psychometric properties.

Study visual flowchart

Phase I: item generation along with face and content validation

In the first phase, in order to gain experiences related to women’s adjustment to postpartum changes, a qualitative study was performed using the content analysis. Deep and semi-structured interviews were conducted with 29 participants in Isfahan, including 17 postpartum women and 12 healthcare providers (three midwives, three gynecologists, three psychologists, and three psychiatrists). The participants were asked to describe experiences of adjusting to postpartum changes. The participating postpartum women were selected via purposive sampling with maximum variation in terms of age, level of education, occupation, number of deliveries, and delivery types. Healthcare providers were chosen through purposive sampling with maximum variation in terms of work experience. Inclusion criteria included willingness to participate in the study, providing informed consent, the ability to express one’s experiences, being an Iranian citizen, having given birth to a live and healthy baby within the past two years, no significant psychological health issues, and no chronic conditions. The participants were recruited from comprehensive health centers, hospitals, and private medical offices. They were approached via face-to-face meetings or phone calls. The time and place of the interviews were confirmed once the oral and written informed consent was acquired and their input was considered. The interviews continued until data saturation was reached, having interviewed 29 persons, with no new codes being formed, and all previously obtained codes being duplicated. Nevertheless, to avoid false data saturation, the researchers conducted five additional interviews after repletion of codes in interview No. 29, in order to ensure greater confidence in achieving accurate data saturation, with no new data collected in the next five interviews. At this point, the researchers concluded that they would stop data collection and analysis, as data saturation had been achieved. Data were analyzed via qualitative content analysis using Krippendorff’s approach [17]. The four main criteria —credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability were ascertained to ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative research findings [18]. Next, a literature review was conducted to complement the findings regarding women’s experiences of adjustment to postpartum changes. The literature was reviewed through searching databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Medline, Science Direct, Scopus, Google Scholar (as a search engine) as well as Iranian databases including Irandoc, Iran Medex, Magiran and SID from 1980 to 2020. The keywords for searching included postpartum, motherhood, adaptation, adjustment, parenthood, along with their Persian equivalents. Based on the qualitative research findings and the literature review, an initial questionnaire was designed using a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree, strongly disagree). In this regard, themes extracted from qualitative research and the literature review were translated into preliminary items. After two sessions with the research team, some items were merged, whereby the questionnaire was ready for the psychometrics phase. The validity of WPPAQ was evaluated through face validity, content validity, and construct validity. Face validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively. To ascertain the qualitative face validity, face-to-face interviews were conducted with 10 postnatal women to appraise the difficulty in comprehending items, the relevance of items to the purpose of the questionnaire, any possible ambiguities (misunderstandings about the items), as well as semantic insufficiencies. For evaluating the quantitative face validity, the impact item index was ascertained by administering the questionnaire to 20 health professionals (in the fields of reproductive health, gynecology, and midwifery), who rated the importance level of the items using a Likert scale ranging from one (not at all important) to five (completely essential). The impact item index for each item was calculated using the relative importance and frequency, whereby the items that scored under 1.5 were removed [19].

Content validity was evaluated using both qualitative and quantitative methods. The qualitative method involved asking 15 health professionals (in the fields of reproductive health, gynecology, and midwifery) to provide their input on content coverage, observing Persian language grammar, using proper vocabulary, and sentence placement. Their input was captured and changes were made accordingly. Content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were utilized to evaluate the quantitative content validity. The Lawshe formula was applied for CVR calculation [20]. Fifteen experts were asked to provide input on the questionnaire validity by rating the items using a three-point Likert scale, with the options “Item is essential,” “Item is beneficial but not essential,” and “Item is not essential”. A CVR of over 0.49 was considered as valid [21]. Further, the Waltz and Bausell method was utilized and CVI of over 0.79 were considered as valid [22]. A four-point Likert scale rating items in terms of relatedness, clarity, and simplicity was used for calculating the scores collected from the experts’ inputs.

Phase II: construct validation and reliability assessment

For calculating the sample size for construct validity, the number of subjects for each variable was determined as three to five [23]. Since the questionnaire developed in present study included 42 items, the sample size was determined to be 4.7 times, equivalent to 203 samples. The construct validity of the questionnaire was appraised through a cross-sectional study undertaken among 203 postnatal women recruited from comprehensive health centers, hospitals, and private medical offices in Isfahan city (Tables 1 and 2). Sampling was performed using convenience sampling method. The inclusion criteria were similar to those of the qualitative study. Eligible samples were invited and the designed questionnaire were completed after obtaining informed consent. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.00. The correlation between the items was calculated using the correlation coefficient, whereby an acceptable value was assumed to be greater than 0.4. Further, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were employed to assess sampling adequacy and the correlation among samples [24]. In the present study, the principal components analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation was utilized to extract the factors [25]. PCA with Varimax rotation was chosen to maximize interpretability and minimize factor overlap, consistent with scale development best practices. In addition, the number of extracted factors was determined by a scree plot, assuming the factor loading cut-off point to be 0.4 [26, 27]. Items with acceptable loading on more than one factor and conceptual similarities were considered to facilitate the organization of the factors. Subsequently, factors were identified based on the concepts represented by the items, while considering the results of the qualitative phase. The minimum and maximum scores were calculated to determine the compatibility score in each area and the total compatibility score in all areas. The mean score was calculated for each area, whereby the total, as well as the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, were determined. For assessing the reliability of the questionnaire, 31 postnatal women completed the questionnaire and repeated in two weeks (test retest), followed by evaluation of Cronbach’s s α coefficient and correlation coefficient for WPPAQ.

Results

In the qualitative phase, women’s experiences about adjustment to postpartum changes were categorized into six main categories: “feeling of decreased feminine attractiveness,” “feeling of frustration and desperation,” “beginning a new phase in life,” “formed reactions in new conditions,” and “factors and conditions affecting adjustment”. The literature reviews initially comprised 107 articles, of which 50 were selected. Next, based on the results of the qualitative study and literature review, an initial questionnaire with 69 items was prepared. After holding session with the research team and merging a number of items, the items was reduced to 64 whereby the questionnaire entered the psychometric phase. Along the evaluation of face validity, nine items were eliminated, and two items were edited based on input from postnatal women and experts. When assessing the qualitative content validity of the questionnaire, seven items were removed. The quantitative content validity resulted in the removal of 13 items as their CVR was ≤ 0.49 and their CVI was ≤ 0.70. Further, four items with CVI between 0.70 and 0.79 were modified, and a final questionnaire was obtained with 35 items. According to Table 3, the value of KMO was 0.86, indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a value of 3519.074 with a significance level of p ≤ 0.001, reflecting a significant correlation in the sample for exploratory factor analysis. EFA reduced the list of the items to 30 with five factors capable of predicting 57.19% of the variance (Table 4). The scree plot demonstrated that five factors could be extracted (Fig. 2). Their factor loadings were, 979.312%, 521.14%, 658.12%, 695.11%, and 8.9% respectively. The first factor (construction of maternal identity) consisted of 7 items with factor loadings ranging from 0.579 to 0.869. The second factor (perceived supportive resources) was comprised of 7 items with factor loadings ranging within 0.583–0.746. The third factor (self-care ability (consisted of seven items with factor loadings ranging from 0.453 to 0.770. The fourth factor (self-efficacy) included five items with factor loadings ranging from 0.489 to 0.792, and the fifth factor (perceived attractiveness and beauty) consisted of four items with factor loadings ranging from 0.489 to 0.817. In this phase, five items with factor loadings lower than 0.4 were omitted, leading to a 30-item questionnaire with a five-point Likert scale featuring five choices: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = no opinion, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree (the questionnaire is attached). In the case of inverted questions, the scoring was done in reverse. Based on the results, the questionnaire’s total score ranged from 30 to 150. The total score revealed “no adjustment” if it was lower than 103 (25th percentile), “weak adjustment” if it was between 103 and 114 (between the 25th and 50th percentiles), “average adjustment” if it was between 114 and 120 (between the 50th and 75th percentiles), and “strong adjustment” if it was higher than 120.

In the present study, the Cronbach’s s α coefficient for the entire questionnaire was 0.912, and it was 0.883 for factor one, 0.840 for factor two, 0.824 for factor three, 0.725 for factor four, and 0.812 for factor five. The correlation coefficient for the entire questionnaire was 0.992, and the it was 0.966 for factor one, 0.955 for factor two, 0.985 for factor three, 0.986 for factor four, and 0.981 for factor five. The correlation of factor one with the entire questionnaire was 0.695, while the correlation was 0.808 for factor two, 0.813 for factor three, 0.637 for factor four, and 0.620 for factor five.

Discussion

The current study aimed to design and ascertain the psychometric properties of WPPAQ. The results indicated that WPPAQ with 30 items, demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability across five dimensions: construction of maternal identity, perceived supportive resources, self-care ability, self-efficacy, as well as perceived attractiveness and beauty. According to the results, “construction of maternal identity” was the first dimension of the WPPAQ. Although the motherhood experience is exaggerated and shocking, in most cases, it is considered a positive experience [28]. In the Iranian culture, where the mother shoulders the responsibility of the care and upbringing of children, motherhood is the most important role of a woman and the cause of her perfection. It seems that following delivery, mothers often feel worthy, mature, and accomplished by accepting the new role of motherhood, which is associated with care and nurturing [29]. This positive feeling helps mothers adjust to the new conditions. In line with the results of the current study, Pinto et al. found that becoming a parent leads to maturity, as it is linked to several new and significant responsibilities [30]. Buta et al. found that satisfaction with new parenting roles is correlated with emotional well-being and a sense of competence in mothers [31]. Further, Chayo et al. noted that feeling joyful and satisfied in adopting new roles in caring for a baby is essential for adjusting to a new birth [32]. Tamsis et al. concluded that childbirth is a motivator and source of hope for mothers to continue [33].

Another dimension of WPPAQ was “perceived supportive resources”. Social support refers to the emotional, psychological, and physical assistance provided by others [34]. The massive changes and serious responsibilities of the motherhood period require enhanced social support. If women perceive their supportive resources to be fewer than the demand, they become stressed and agitated [9]. Support could be provided by family members, friends, neighbors, colleagues, relatives, and doctors; nevertheless, a spouse’s support is essential for women’s adjustment to the new conditions, and they expect support from their husbands in child care, house chores, in addition to their emotional support [35]. Eslahi et al. found that spouses’ support along the postpartum period enhances mothers’ perceived parenting skills and self-confidence while also strengthening the mother-child bond. It also boosts mothers’ ability to adapt and cope with the new postpartum stress factors [34]. Langeh et al. stated that support and care are among the essential needs of mothers in the postpartum period [36]. Nevertheless, in Iranian culture, motherhood and housework are considered women’s duties. Therefore, women are deprived of the participation and cooperation of their husbands along the postpartum period. In this regard, it is recommended that policymakers consider the participation of spouses in the postpartum care process in order to boost social support for mothers and plan to enhance spouses’ awareness and their role in promoting maternal and newborn health.

The third dimension of WAPPQ was the “self-care ability”. The physical, psychological, and social changes in postpartum can predispose women to physical disabilities, pain, and psychological disorders. Thus, they need to recover with sufficient care and attention. Meanwhile, after the new birth, most of the mothers’ energy and time are devoted to childcare, which can cause them to ignore their own needs; hence, these circumstances can negatively affect their quality of life [35–37]. Sometimes, a lack of self-care in mothers and an inability to address their personal needs result in behavioral reactions such as anger, irritability, seclusion, or even depression. Mothers who care about their needs are happy and satisfied [38]. In line with the results of the current study, Winderzimann et al. as well as Walka and Muri reported that mothers’ physical pain and unhealthy state are among the factors associated with depression [9, 39]. Also, Kizilirmak et al. observed that mothers’ lack of sufficient sleep, fatigue, and physiological problems (problems related to breasts, etc.) can cause maladjustment to and incompatibility with postpartum changes [35]. Lange et al. noted that mothers’ postpartum adjustment and positive experience require identifying and addressing their specific needs as well as providing them with appropriate care [36]. Thus, educational interventions to improve self-care ability in postpartum can facilitate women’s adjustment to postpartum changes.

The third dimension of WAPPQ was “self-efficacy”. It is regarded an essential concept in mother care given its emphasis on self-management and empowerment [40]. Following delivery, caring for the child and addressing their needs are the parents’ responsibility; thus, if the mother feels that she can easily identify the newborn’s needs and address them, she feels more satisfied and adjusts more easily to the new motherhood responsibilities. Concordant with the results of the current study, Botha et al. showed that mothers who felt capable of caring for their newborns were more compatible and enjoyed their parenting role; on the other hand, among those who felt incompetent in accomplishing their parental roles, there was more despair, anxiety, and a higher likelihood of weaning the infant [41]. Chua et al. reported that an effective bond with the child and the feeling of competence in new parenting roles are important predictors of satisfaction with parenting [32]. Saha found that feeling competent and satisfied with the bond with the newborn is positively linked to well-being and negatively associated with stress, depression, and anxiety [42]. Hence, designing educational interventions to enhance self-efficacy can be effective in increasing women’s adjustment to postpartum changes.

The fifth dimension of WAPPQ was “perceived attractiveness and beauty”. In the current study, one of the dissatisfactions and worries mentioned by mothers in Iranian culture was extensive changes in their bodies post-delivery (a negative body image). This negative body image had affected mothers’sexual function in some cases. In accordance with these results, Walka and Muri also confirmed the negative body image in mothers and its negative influence on sexual performance as a postpartum stressor [9]. Riesco-González et al. confirmed the relationship between perceived body image and postpartum depression [43]. In the same vein, Kapa et al. found that concerns about body image in postpartum women lead to feelings of incompetence and premature weaning [44]. In such situations, education and reassurance are very effective. Mothers need to be informed that the return of physical changes is a long process. Couples also need to be educated on how to interact properly along this period.

Based on what was mentioned, it seems that WAPPQ as a valid, reliable, and acceptable questionnaire with appropriate psychometric properties is capable of appraising women’s adjustment to postpartum changes, considering the extensive physical, psychological, social, and family changes that take place postpartum in cultural context of Iranian society. The important features of the present questionnaire are desirable validity and reliability, clarity and transparency, a small number of items, as well as the possibility of completing it within a short period of time. Thus, healthcare providers can use WAPPQ to measure various dimensions of women’s adjustment to the experience of childbirth and motherhood.

Limitations and future directions

Since the quantitative phase of this study was undertaken during the COVID-19 pandemic, there were some difficulties in ascertaining postnatal women owing to social distancing and reduced visits to medical centers, private gynecologists’ and midwives’ offices. In some cases, they were unwilling to have close contact with the researchers. As such, some postpartum women were approached via the Internet during their postpartum period, which can be regarded as one of the limitations of this study. Also, since this study reviewed the literature between 1980 and 2020, the lack of reviewing literature after 2020 can be considered a limitation of this research. It was also recommended that for a high-reliability estimate, at least one hundred subjects should be recruited in psychometric studies [45]. Thus, this can be considered as a limitation for the present study. It is expected that the questionnaire designed in this study could be employed as a basis for the future research in the field of women’s adjustment to postpartum changes and will result in identification of newer research areas.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that ascertaining women’s adjustment to postpartum changes through questionnaire entails examining five dimensions including “construction of maternal identity”, “perceived support resources”, “self-care ability”, “self-efficacy”, and “perceived attractiveness and beauty”. Healthcare providers can apply WAPPQ designed in this study as a valid, reliable, and suitable tool to evaluate women’s adjustment to postpartum changes, considering the extensive physical, psychological, social, and family changes that occur postpartum.
