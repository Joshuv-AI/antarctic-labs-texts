MeCP2 MBD-ID module: a unified DNA/RNA binding interface disrupted in Rett syndrome
Authors: Josiah A Peter, Thomas A Weiser, Lee A Niswander, Robert T Batey, Deborah S Wuttke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRett syndrome neurodevelopmental disorder is caused by mutations in the epigenetic regulator MeCP2. While the MeCP2 methyl-CpG binding domain (MBD) is well-characterized, the function of the adjacent intervening domain (ID) remains largely understudied. The ID has been described as a distinct RNA-binding region, yet evidence also suggests RNA competitively displaces MeCP2 from DNA. Here, we address these conflicting findings by demonstrating the MBD and ID do not function in isolation but as a synergistic functional unit. The ID significantly enhances affinity of the MBD for methylated DNA by ∼35-fold. Moreover, these two subdomains form a high-affinity RNA-binding module, with preference for structured RNAs increased over 1000-fold compared to the MBD or ID alone. We find binding to RNA precludes binding to DNA, such that the integrated MBD-ID unit explains the competition phenomenon. Analysis of Rett syndrome-associated ID mutations (R167W, K174Q, and R190H) and a therapeutic MiniGene reveals they do not disrupt methyl-DNA binding but instead selectively weaken RNA and non-methylated DNA binding, thereby disrupting the competitive balance between nucleic acids. Our work establishes the MBD-ID module as MeCP2’s central nucleic acid interaction hub, whose disruption provides a potential molecular etiology of Rett syndrome due to mutations in the ID.

Introduction

Methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2) is a multifunctional nuclear protein critical for neuronal development and function [1, 2] and mutation of MECP2 underlies the devastating neurodevelopmental disorder Rett syndrome [1]. MeCP2 is canonically recognized for binding cytosine methylated DNA [3–10] and interacting with chromatin modifying proteins [11–15]. As such, MeCP2 acts as a master regulator of chromatin architecture [2, 16]. MeCP2 has broad functional versatility: it influences transcription [17], modulates splicing [18] and miRNA processing [19, 20], and is implicated in the DNA damage response [21]. Emerging functions in protein synthesis [22] and primary cilium biology [23] suggest a multifaceted role in biology that extends beyond simple transcriptional regulation [17]. MeCP2 functional multiplicity is reflected in its largely disordered domain architecture [24, 25] (Fig. 1A), which includes a partially structured and well-studied methyl-CpG binding domain (MBD), a transcription repression domain (TRD) and NCor/Smrt interacting domain (NID), which together make up the major protein–protein interaction interface [14], N- and C-terminal domains (NTD and CTD), and an intervening domain (ID). The ID has been implicated in select protein interactions (e.g. Lamin B, DNMT1, and SIN3A) [11, 26, 27] but its specific contributions to the diverse functions of MeCP2 remain largely unknown [16]. Thisprevious broad multifunctionality, however, presents a fundamental challenge: how can a single protein achieve its affinity for many different partners, comprised of DNA and RNA and proteins, within the crowded nuclear environment?

A significant, yet poorly understood, function of MeCP2 is its direct interaction with RNA [16, 28]. This is underscored by our recent finding that MeCP2 directly binds the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) Rncr3 to control miR-124a processing during brain development [29]. The current model for the mechanism of RNA binding, based on qualitative assays, proposed a clear segregation of functions: the MBD binds methylated DNA, while the ID is responsible for RNA binding [28]. This model, however, conflicts with evidence that RNA can compete with and displace MeCP2 from DNA [28]. This contradiction implies an uncharacterized functional interplay between the MBD and ID that the segregation model cannot account for. To address this, we sought to determine whether the MBD and ID instead constitute an integrated functional unit governing interaction of MeCP2 with both DNA and RNA. Furthermore, we investigated the biophysical consequences of Rett syndrome patient mutations within the ID to assess whether disruption of this potential integrated nucleic acid function could represent a novel disease mechanism.

Materials and methods

MeCP2 amino acid multiple sequence alignment

Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Clustal Omega algorithm (v.1.2.2) through the Jalview interface (v2.11.5.0) with default parameters, using the full-length sequences from the following UniProt entries: P51608_1 (Homo sapiens); A0A2I3TPC7 (Pan troglodytes); A0A8C0N4P8 (Canis lupus familiaris); Q9Z2D6 (Mus musculus); F6TJ54 (Monodelphis domestica); F6WTL1 (Xenopus tropicalis); H3AT37 (Latimeria chalumnae); A8WIP2 (Danio rerio). The resulting alignment to the ID was visualized using R (v4.4.1) with the ggplot2(v4.0.1) package, with MeCP2 missense variants identified and downloaded from the ClinVar database [30] (Human MeCP2 protein NM_004992.4; NP_004 983 isoform 1, accessed December 2025).

Cloning, protein expression, and purification of MeCP2 and MeCP2-derived constructs

MBD, MBD-ID, MBD-ID with RTT-Patient ID Mutations [R167W, K174Q, R190H, and K(171–174)A control], and MeCP2 MiniGene

Human MECP2 cDNA (Isoform E2, UniProt: P51608_1) and h∆NIC (MiniGene) were obtained from AddGene (#48078 and #110191, respectively). The coding sequences for the MiniGene and the subdomains (MBD and MBD-ID) were amplified by PCR and cloned into a pET28a expression vector (EMD Biosciences) using Gibson Assembly (NEB E5510). All constructs feature an N-terminal hexahistidine tag followed by a TEV cleavage site (Supplementary Table S1). Rett patient ID mutations [R167W, K174Q, R190H, as well as K(171–174)A control] were introduced into the MBD-ID construct using the NEB site-directed mutagenesis kit (E0552) with oligonucleotides in Supplementary Table S3. All plasmid constructs were sequence verified and transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta 2 expression cells and plated on LB agar overnight. Protein expression and purification methods were adapted from established protocols [31, 32]. For each construct, a single colony was grown in LB media with 50 μg/ml kanamycin overnight at 37°C. The next day, 1:100 of the overnight culture was inoculated into 1 l of 2xYT rich media with 50 μg/ml kanamycin and cultured at 37°C to an OD600 0.4–0.7 and cold shocked on ice for 30 min. Isopropyl β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 0.5 mM final concentration) was added to induce protein expression and cultures were grown at 37°C for 3 h while shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 RCF for 10 min at 4°C (Avanti JXN-26 floor centrifuge) and pellets stored at −20°C.

Cell pellets from 1 l of culture were thawed on ice and resuspended using a Dounce homogenizer in 40 ml of lysis buffer [20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole pH 7.5, 5% glycerol and freshly added one EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche), and 1 mM final concentration of dithiothreitol (DTT)]. Cells were lysed on ice using a sonicator (QSonica Q500) (60% amplitude, 3 min total ON-time, pulse: 10 s ON/15 s OFF, three times using ½ inch tip) and the lysate cleared by centrifugation (27 000 RCF for 30 min at 4°C). Cleared lysate was filtered using 0.2 μm syringe filter and loaded into HisTrap column (Cytiva; attached to an AKTA pure FPLC) via a 50 ml superloop. After two washes with 20 and 50 mM imidazole in buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol, the desired protein was eluted with an imidazole gradient in the same buffer. SDS–PAGE-confirmed fractions were then pooled. The pooled eluate was transferred into a 6–8K MWCO dialysis tubing (Spectra/por – Spectrum Labs) with 10 U/mg of TEV protease (Promega V610) added to remove the hexahistidine tag. Eluate was dialyzed overnight at 4°C against 1 l of G75 Column Buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT). Dialyzed eluate was filtered (0.2 μm filter) and concentrated using 5K and 10K MWCO spin concentrators (Vivaspin Turbo), for MBD and MBD-ID, respectively. Concentrated sample was filtered again before being loaded onto a size-exclusion column (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75) and all constructs eluted as a monomer. Pooled fractions containing recombinant protein were assessed for purity using SDS–PAGE, quantified, aliquoted, flash-frozen, and stored at −70°C until use. The MeCP2 MiniGene protein is particularly prone to proteolysis; in this case, lysis and wash buffers contained 10 μM leupeptin, 1 μM pepstatin, 0.2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 1 μM aprotinin, in addition to EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche). The extinction coefficient used to quantify MBD, MBD-ID, MiniGene, and all MBD-ID-derived constructs is 11 460 M−1 cm−1. A typical yield ranged between 1 and 1.5 mg/l of culture. Rett patient ID mutant SDS–PAGE shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Full-length MeCP2

The human full-length MeCP2 (isoform E2, UniProt: P51608-1) expression vector, containing a TEV-cleavable N-terminal Strep-Tag II (Supplementary Table S1), was a gift from Dr. Vignesh Kasinath. The E1 isoform (Uniprot: P51608-2), which differs from E2 only at the N-terminus, was generated by cloning a gBlock encoding the unique E1 N-terminal sequence (Supplementary Table S1) into the E2 vector. The protein expression and purification protocol was adapted from the Kasinath lab with slight modifications. A single transformed colony of E. coli Rosetta 2 was grown, IPTG-induced, harvested, and stored as above. Cell pellets from 1 l cultures were thawed on ice and resuspended using a Dounce homogenizer in 40 ml of resuspension buffer [25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 10 μM leupeptin, 1 μM pepstatin, 1 mM PMSF, 1 μM aprotinin, protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche), DNase, and salt active nuclease]. Cells were lysed and cleared as above. Cleared lysate was filtered using a 0.2 μm syringe filter and added into a gravity column, containing a prepared affinity resin (IBA streptactinXT 2-5010-010). The column was sealed and incubated in cold room with gentle rocking for 2 h, followed by 2 washes with 30 ml of low and high salt buffers [0.5 and 1 M NaCl, respectively, in 25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, 10 μM leupeptin, 1 μM pepstatin, 1 mM PMSF, 1 μM aprotinin, protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche), DNase, and freshly added 1 mM DTT]. After the washes, 15 ml of elution buffer (IBA buffer BXT 2-1042-025) was added into the column. Resin was disturbed and allowed to sit for 10 min before eluate was collected and confirmed by SDS–PAGE. Freshly made ATP and MgCl2 (final concentration: 10 and 20 mM, respectively) was added to the 15 ml eluate. The entire solution was transferred into a 6–8K MWCO dialysis tubing (Spectra/por – Spectrum Labs) and dialyzed against 1.5 l dialysis buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT) overnight at 4°C. Dialyzed solution was filtered and loaded into a heparin column attached to an AKTA Pure FPLC, and eluted with a salt gradient elution buffer (100 mM to 1 M NaCl, in 25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT) to separate MeCP2 from co-purifying contaminants like HSP70. To further polish the protein, the fraction eluted from the heparin column was concentrated and loaded onto a size-exclusion column (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75) and eluted with sizing buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT), after which high-purity fractions were pooled, quantified, concentrated, aliquoted, and flash-frozen for storage at −70°C. The extinction coefficient used to quantify the full-length MeCP2 is 19 940 M−1 cm−1. A typical yield ranged between 1 and 1.5 mg/l of culture.

Oligonucleotide preparation

All nucleic acid ligand sequences used for binding experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S2. DNA oligonucleotides were ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) with standard desalting and HPLC purified. For fluorescently labeled ligands used in anisotropy measurements, the sense strand was synthesized with fluorescein (6-FAM) conjugated to the 5′-end. The labeled and unlabeled complementary strands were annealed by slow cooling (heating at 95°C for 3 min, then slow cooled to room temperature for 3 h) to promote intermolecular interaction, at 1 μM in annealing buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl).

Fluorophore-labeled RNA for anisotropy measurements was ordered from Horizon Discovery and IDT with standard desalting and HPLC purified. RNA ligands (predicted secondary structure shown in Supplementary Fig. S3) were refolded for binding assays by snap cooling (95°C for 3 min, ice for >5 min) to promote intramolecular interaction, at a 1 μM concentration in folding buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl). The labeled and unlabeled complimentary strands used to make double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) were annealed as described above. All oligonucleotides were verified on a native polyacrylamide gel.

UV A₂₆₀ nm melt assay for RNA structure determination

Computational structure prediction for the 23 nt RNA fragment derived from Rncr3 (Rncr3_1) was performed using UNAfold [33] and RNAfold [34], yielding a three base paired structure with ΔG ≈ −0.6 kcal/mol stability. To validate the potential for this RNA to form secondary structure, we performed a UV absorbance melt assay. RNA was diluted to 0.2 mg/ml in melting buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1× PBS pH 7.0), snap-cooled (95°C for 3 min, then ice for 5 min) to promote intramolecular folding, and transferred to a 0.5 mm path length quartz cuvette. Absorbance at 260 nm was monitored as the sample was heated from 25 to 95°C at a rate of 1°C/min using a Chirascan Plus spectrometer (Applied Photophysics) equipped with a CD 250 temperature controller (Quantum Northwest). Melting transitions were visualized by taking the first derivative of the absorbance versus temperature curve. The raw melt curve and first derivative plot are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Fluorescent anisotropy-binding assay for KD,app determination

The apparent binding affinities were determined using previously described methods [35–37]. The binding buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 0.025% NP-40) was used to dilute purified proteins and oligos to desired concentration. The full-length MeCP2 was stored in 500 mM NaCl and usually first diluted with nuclease free water to bring NaCl concentration to 150 mM prior to serial dilution. The 6-FAM labeled DNA and RNA ligand final binding experiment concentrations were 1 and 2 nM, respectively. Purified proteins were serially diluted (1:2 for each dilution) from 10 µM to 2.4 pM in binding buffer. Protein and ligand were mixed in a 20 µl binding reaction in a flat-bottom low-flange 384-well black NBS polystyrene plate (Corning) and allowed to incubate for at least 1 h at room temperature to reach equilibrium before measurements. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed as previously described [39–41] with modifications. Purified recombinant MBD-ID and MBD were 1:2 serially diluted from 1000 to 0.5 nM (12 concentration points) in binding buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 0.025% NP-40). 10 nM of labeled Rncr3_1 RNA was added to the protein (at a 1:1 volume ratio; final volume: 20 μl) and allowed to equilibrate for an hour at 4°C. While samples were equilibrating, a 15% acrylamide:bisacrylamide 1X TBE gel was pre-run at 175 V for 15 min at 4°C. The entire binding reaction was then run at 200 V for 30 min at 4°C. Gels were imaged using Cy2 settings (488 nm excitation) on Typhoon 5 Imager (Cytiva).

Mass photometry for determining FL MeCP2 molecularity

Mass photometry measurements were performed on a Refeyn TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd., Oxford, UK) at room temperature. All samples were prepared in buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 4% glycerol, and 0.025% NP-40, at final concentrations of 50 nM FL MeCP2 and 20 nM DNA (mBDNF or BDNF). Prior to each measurement, a clean coverslip (high-precision 24 × 50 mm No 1.5 coverslips, Thorlabs CG15KH) was washed with isopropanol and Milli-Q water, dried with bursts of nitrogen gas, and then coated with poly-L-lysine (P4707 Sigma–Aldrich). A six well silicone gasket (Grace BioLabs 103 250) was placed on the coverslip, and 18 µl of mass photometry dilution buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 4% glycerol) was added for focusing. Subsequently, 2 µl of sample was mixed with the buffer to achieve a final volume of 20 µl and final concentrations of 5 nM protein and 2 nM DNA. Sixty-second movies with a raw frame rate of 475.2 Hz (binned frame rate of 47.5 Hz) were acquired using the Refeyn AquireMP software (v. 2025-R1, Refeyn Ltd.). Mass calibration was performed using β-amylase, which exists as monomers (56 kDa), dimers (112 kDa), and tetramers (224 kDa) in solution. Data were analyzed using DiscoverMP (v. 2025 R1) software (Refeyn Ltd.). Mass histograms were generated from at least three independent movies, and peaks were fitted to Gaussian distributions to determine the molecular masses of observed species.

Results

Molecular dissection of the MeCP2 intervening domain

The MeCP2 ID is an intrinsically disordered [25], 46-amino-acid region (AAs 161–207) that is highly basic and contains motifs associated with nucleic acid binding [39], including arginine-glycine (RG)-rich, lysine-proline (KP)-rich, and extended AT-hook motifs [42]. Despite its association with Rett syndrome pathology [30, 39], the molecular function of the ID remains poorly defined. This functional gap is particularly pressing, as the ClinVar [30] database reports numerous missense mutations within this domain associated with MECP2-related neurodevelopmental disorders, many of which are classified as variants of unknown significance [30] (Fig. 1A). The strong evolutionary conservation of the ID from humans to zebrafish underscores its critical and non-redundant role (Fig. 1B).

To address this knowledge gap and determine the contribution of the ID to MeCP2 function, we adopted a reductionist biochemical approach. We purified recombinant full-length (FL) MeCP2 (isoform E1 and E2) alongside a panel of sub-domain constructs (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S1) designed to deconvolute the individual and potential integrated functions of the MBD and the ID. These included (i) the isolated MBD (AAs 78–165), representing the well-studied canonical methyl-CpG DNA-binding module [16]; (ii) the isolated ID (AAs 161–207), implicated in nucleic acid interactions [28, 39]; and (iii) a construct encompassing both domains (MBD-ID, AAs 78–207). To overcome the solubility challenges of the intrinsically disordered ID, the ID region was expressed and purified as a fusion with maltose-binding protein (MBP-ID). All proteins were expressed and purified to homogeneity (Fig. 2B).

Synergy between the ID and MBD drives high-affinity DNA binding by MeCP2

To directly test whether the MBD and ID constitute an integrated functional unit, we first established a quantitative baseline for their individual and combined contributions to DNA binding. We employed an in vitro fluorescent anisotropy (FA) assay to determine the affinity and specificity of our purified protein constructs for a 15 bp sequence derived from the BDNF promoter, which is bona fide target of MeCP2-mediated regulation, used in prior structural studies of the MeCP2 MBD [43]. We tested oligonucleotides containing either the high-affinity single symmetric CpG methylated BDNF sequence (mBDNF) or non-methylated (BDNF) version (Supplementary Table S2). All binding assays were performed in a buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5 at room temperature.

Consistent with prior studies [10, 32, 44–46]), FL MeCP2 bound mBDNF cooperatively with low nanomolar affinity (KD,app = 2.5 ± 0.1 nM, Hill n = 2.4, for E2; KD,app = 3.2 ± 0.2 nM, Hill n = 2.7, for E1) and displayed approximately 6-fold tighter binding compared to the non-methylated BDNF sequence (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S5), confirming MeCP2 canonical function as a methyl-CpG binding protein [3–5]. It has been previously reported that the MeCP2 E1 isoform is a weaker binder of DNA than the E2 isoform [47]. In contrast to the prior study, we observe no significant difference between the isoforms with respect to DNA binding when performed under identical FA conditions. Given the fact that these isoforms share >95% identity, with only 12 amino acid changes in the N-terminal region, which is not associated with nucleic acid binding activity, our result is not surprising. The prior study [47] used different experimental conditions and assay, which may account for the discrepancy.

(B) FA binding curve for MBD-ID, MBD, and ID bound to methylated and non-methylated BDNF. Shown are representative data points of n > 3 independent experiments. Standard error of the mean reported. The Hill coefficient > 1, observed by us and others [32, 44, 48], suggests MeCP2 can multimerize, and DNA methylation density dependent cooperativity has been reported before using atomic force microscopy [32]. To test this further, we performed mass photometry of FL MeCP2 in complex with methylated and unmethylated BDNF DNA. Supplementary Fig. S6 shows that FL MeCP2 is able to form a discrete dimer upon binding methylated DNA but remains monomeric with unmethylated DNA.

Next, we probed the specific functions of the MBD and ID regions by FA and found that both the MBD and MBD-ID constructs retained preferential binding for mBDNF over unmethylated BDNF (Fig. 3B), similar to FL MeCP2. However, the inclusion of the ID dramatically enhanced the DNA-binding affinity of the MBD by approximately 35-fold (Fig. 3B), consistent with prior observations [31, 49]. Moreover, the MBD-ID construct recapitulates the methylated DNA affinity and selectivity of the FL MeCP2 but lacks the positive cooperativity observed in the FL protein, suggesting that a region(s) outside the MBD-ID is responsible for conferring cooperative binding. In contrast, the ID alone showed weak micromolar affinity to both mBDNF and BDNF with little to no preference for the methylated sequence (Fig. 3B). These data indicate that the ID acts in concert with the MBD to constitute the core DNA-binding module of MeCP2. We acknowledge that Claveria-Gimeno et al. [49] previously reported a modest DNA binding profile of the isolated ID and suggested that the MBD-ID construct harbors two independent DNA-binding sites. We note that the weak second binding event observed in reference 49 under ITC conditions (constant protein concentration, titrating DNA, and low ionic strength) may reflect a different aspect of the binding interaction than our FA experiments (constant DNA concentration, titrating protein, and physiological ionic concentrations). While we interpret the second event in our FA data (not shown) as likely reflecting non-specific interactions of a second protein molecule on the initial 1:1 protein:DNA complex driven by high protein concentration, we acknowledge that the ITC data may report on a distinct binding mode that is not observed under our experimental conditions. Further studies will be required to fully reconcile these observations.

It is interesting to note that FL MeCP2 and MBD-ID exhibit modest selectivity towards methylated DNA (6- to 8-fold), while the MBD alone displays over 50-fold selective binding of methylated DNA. This would suggest that the highly positively charged ID domain provides non-sequence specific electrostatic interactions that diminish its selectivity for methylated DNA. Taken together, the MBD and ID function as an integrated functional unit, with the MBD providing specificity for methylated DNA [43] and the ID contributing substantially to tighter DNA-binding affinity.

The MeCP2 MBD-ID is a high-affinity, promiscuous RNA-binding module

While MeCP2 is well known as a methyl-DNA binding protein [4, 5, 16, 43, 50], several lines of evidence have suggested that MeCP2 can also interact with RNA [16]. A 2004 study first proposed that MeCP2 possesses distinct DNA- and RNA-binding domains [28], with the ID facilitating RNA interactions potentially due to the presence of basic amino acid patches and an extended AT-hook motif [39, 42] (Fig. 1B). This concept has been reinforced by more recent studies, including RIP-seq experiments that identified a range of MeCP2-bound lncRNAs in mouse brain [51] and our finding that MeCP2 directly interacts with the lncRNA Rncr3 to control miR124a processing during brain development [29]. Furthermore, a recent SELEX-based study confirmed that MeCP2 can bind a broad spectrum of structured and dsRNAs with high affinity [52], although the role of the ID in this RNA binding was not directly tested.

The quantitative biophysical parameters governing MeCP2–RNA interaction and their comparison to MeCP2–DNA interactions are not resolved. For example, it is not known if the ID alone is sufficient for high-affinity RNA binding, as initially suggested [28], or if the ID acts in concert with MBD, similar to the results above for DNA interaction. To bridge this gap, we systematically characterized the RNA-binding capacity of the FL MeCP2 and subdomain constructs. We selected a 23-nt segment of RNA (Rncr3_1) corresponding to a naturally occurring functional MeCP2-bound region within the Rncr3 lncRNA previously identified as both methylated and necessary for biological activity [29]. This Rncr3_1 RNA is computationally predicted to form three distinct hairpin structures with predicted melting temperatures ∼50°C (IDT tools). UV melt analysis of this RNA revealed a broad transition around 50–60°C, indicating that the RNA forms base-paired hairpin structures (Supplementary Fig. S4). We found that the FL MeCP2 bound this RNA segment with high affinity (KD,app = 8 ± 3 nM for E2; KD,app = 9 ± 2 nM for E1) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S7). Similarly, the MBD-ID construct bound the same RNA ligand with comparable affinity (KD,app = 5 ± 1 nM), as measured by both FA and EMSA (Fig. 4A and B). In stark contrast, the isolated ID or the isolated MBD bound this RNA very weakly (KD,app ∼ 2 µM and >6 µM, respectively; Fig. 4A and B). Hence, the ID alone cannot account for the tight affinity binding to RNA. Instead, the ID in conjunction with the MBD enhances RNA binding by almost three orders of magnitude in comparison to the individual domains, indicating that the MBD-ID is necessary and sufficient to recapitulate the RNA binding behavior of the full-length protein.

We next sought to characterize the RNA-binding landscape of the MBD-ID module. Based on our previous work demonstrating a biologically relevant interaction between MeCP2 and the lncRNA Rncr3 [29], we probed two additional Rncr3-derived RNA segments (Rncr3_2, Rncr3_3) predicted by UNAfold [33] to form stem-loop structures. The MBD-ID construct bound these two RNA ligands with low nanomolar affinity (Fig. 4C, KD,app ≈ 2–4 nM), a range comparable to its affinity for methylated DNA (Fig. 3B).

To determine if this high-affinity binding was specific to Rncr3, we tested structured RNA targets of the transcription factors glucocorticoid receptor (GR) [53] and GATA1 [54], including the GAS5 and 7SK-GAGA hairpins. The MBD-ID bound these RNAs with similarly tight affinity (KD,app ≈ 4–10 nM) (Fig. 4C, raw anisotropy binding curves in Supplementary Fig. S8). This broad recognition of diverse structured RNAs, akin to other proteins with arginine-rich motifs (ARMs) like the estrogen receptor (ERα) [36], GR [53], and GATA1 [54], suggests that the MBD-ID lacks intrinsic sequence specificity for RNA and instead exhibits a preference for structural features.

We probed further this specificity by testing a dsRNA mimicking the BDNF promoter, but this was bound with only moderate affinity by MBD-ID (KD,app 190 ± 30 nM) (Fig. 4C), indicating that dsRNA structure is not sufficient for high-affinity binding. We tested single-stranded RNAs (ssRNAs) of varying lengths that are unlikely to form stable secondary structures. Here, we observed a clear length-dependent affinity, with MBD-ID binding a 31-nt env8 sequence approximately 11-fold more tightly than a shorter 20-nt variant (Fig. 4C). Our findings are consistent with a recent SELEX-based study [52] and implies that electrostatic interactions, contributed by an extended interaction surface, are a key determinant in non-specific binding. Overall, the MBD-ID module of MeCP2 is a high-affinity but low-specificity RNA-binding unit, exhibiting a preference for RNAs that contain a combination of unpaired bases (bulges) and double-stranded regions, rather than a defined primary sequence.

MeCP2 does not discriminate between unmodified and m5C-methylated RNA

Given that MeCP2 functions as a selective reader of methylated DNA (Fig. 3) [3–5], we next asked whether it similarly discriminates between unmodified RNA and RNA containing 5-methylcytosine (m5C), a prevalent and functionally relevant RNA modification [55, 56]. To test this, we employed FA to measure the binding affinity of both FL MeCP2 and the MBD-ID construct to a panel of RNA ligands, with varying densities and patterns of m5C methylation.

We first generated mono-, di-, and tri-m5C methylated variants of Rncr3_1 RNA and compared their binding to FL MeCP2 and MBD-ID relative to the unmodified RNA. As shown in Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S9A, both MBD-ID and FL MeCP2, respectively, bound all m5C-modified variants with affinities comparable to the unmodified RNA (Fig. 4A), with no significant selectivity observed for any methylation density tested (KDapp values ranging from 4.5–5.5 nM for MBD-ID and 9–11 nM for FL MeCP2).

To extend this analysis, we next tested the Rncr3_2 RNA, a second Rncr3-derived sequence, with two distinct methylation patterns: a di-m5C variant and a pseudo-symmetrically methylated (Rncr3_2 PSM) variant. Consistent with our findings for Rncr3_1, neither MBD-ID nor FL MeCP2 exhibited preferential binding to the methylated versions over the unmodified RNA (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S9B).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that, unlike MeCP2’s well-established role as a reader of m5C in DNA, it does not function as a general reader of m5C in RNA. While we cannot exclude the possibility that MeCP2 recognizes m5C in specific sequence contexts or in complex with other protein partners (given that MeCP2 has over 40 known protein interactors [2]), our results suggest that the MBD-ID module does not intrinsically discriminate RNA methylation status. These findings further delineate the nucleic acid binding specificity of MeCP2 and highlight fundamental differences in how the MBD-ID module engages DNA versus RNA ligands.

RNA-mediated displacement of MeCP2 from DNA requires the intervening domain

Another outstanding question is whether DNA- and RNA-binding through the MBD-ID can occur simultaneously or whether this is a competitive interaction [28]. To test this, we employed a FA competition assay by pre-forming complexes of MeCP2 constructs with fluorescently labeled mBDNF and challenging the complex with a competitor nucleic acid. As a control, we first tested unlabeled mBDNF DNA and found this could efficiently compete for binding, leading to displacement of all constructs (FL MeCP2, MBD-ID, and MBD) from the labeled DNA (Fig. 6A–C). When unlabeled Rncr3_2 RNA was used as a competitor, it potently displaced both FL MeCP2 and the MBD-ID from the DNA (Fig. 6A and B), but the isolated MBD was completely resistant to RNA competition (Fig. 6C), consistent with its weak affinity for RNA.

To quantitatively assess the competition data, we fit the displacement curves using the equation described by Wang (1995) [38], which accounts for ligand depletion and allows for the calculation of the apparent dissociation constants (KD,app) for the competitor. The KD,app values derived from the competition assays for both DNA and RNA competitors were in agreement with those obtained from direct binding measurement (Supplementary Table S4), confirming that the competition assay faithfully reports on the same nucleic acid binding event measured in our direct titrations.

These data reinforce our finding that the MBD and ID do not function as independent modules but as an integrated functional unit. The binding of MBD-ID to one nucleic acid, be it DNA or RNA, sterically or allosterically precludes its simultaneous binding to the other nucleic acid, providing biochemical limits on suggested models for RNA-binding functions.

Rett-associated mutations in the intervening domain affect nucleic acid interactions

The profound neurodevelopmental deficits observed in Rett syndrome [1, 16, 39] due to MECP2 mutations underscores the critical importance of MeCP2 molecular functions. Pathogenic mutations that map to the MBD and TRD are well studied for their impact on MeCP2 biology [16]. However, missense mutations within the ID are also associated with Rett syndrome and other MECP2-related neurodevelopmental disorders [30, 39] (Fig. 1A), although they are largely of unknown pathogenicity as little attention to date has been paid to possible ID function or its biophysical activities [16]. To bridge this gap between genetics and mechanism, we quantitatively profiled the nucleic acid binding properties of three patient-derived ID variants (R167W, K174Q, and R190H) within the context of the MBD-ID module. These mutations were selected for their established clinical significance: R167W is associated with familial mental dysfunction [57], K174Q with autism spectrum disorder [30], and R190H with atypical Rett phenotype and defects in DNA binding and chromatin compaction [39]. These were compared to a positive control mutant, K(171–174)A, previously shown by our lab to disrupt MeCP2–Rncr3 interaction [29]. We also characterized a therapeutic candidate, the MiniGene (ΔNIC) [58], a miniaturized MeCP2 variant which retains a small segment of the NTD, all of the MBD, the first 13 amino acids of the ID, an SV40 nuclear localization signal (NLS), and the NID (Fig. 1A and B). This construct is currently in clinical trials for Rett syndrome gene therapy (FDA: TSHA 102).

All three patient variants and the K(171–174)A control bound methylated DNA (mBDNF) with an affinity nearly identical to that of wild-type MBD-ID and FL MeCP2 (Table 1). This indicates that the core methyl-DNA binding function, governed primarily by the MBD, remains largely intact. However, a clear defect emerged when binding was probed to non-methylated DNA (BDNF) and, notably, to Rncr3_1 RNA. Here, all pathogenic mutants exhibited a weakened affinity, typically showing a 2- to 4-fold reduction in BDNF DNA and RNA binding compared to the FL MeCP2 and MBD-ID (Table 1). These data reveal a common molecular phenotype for at least some Rett syndrome-associated ID mutations: the preservation of methyl-DNA binding, and a reproducible impairment in the recognition of non-methylated DNA and RNA. This establishes that pathogenic dysfunction could potentially arise not only from a loss of the canonical DNA-binding activity, but from a more subtle decoupling of the dual nucleic acid binding capabilities of the MBD-ID module, pointing to an essential role of the ID in fine-tuning MeCP2 genomic and epitranscriptomic interactions.

Having established the role of the ID in a unified DNA/RNA-binding interface, we next asked how this function is represented in the therapeutic MiniGene. The profile of the MiniGene was particularly informative. It recapitulated wild-type-level binding affinity to both methylated and non-methylated DNA. However, it lacks the FL MeCP2-mBDNF positive cooperativity and its affinity for RNA was reduced >5-fold relative to FL MeCP2 and MBD-ID, a defect more compromised than the patient variants. This biochemical signature suggests that while the MiniGene retains robust DNA-binding capacity, its interaction with the RNA interactome is altered, underscoring the need to consider the engineering of optimized therapeutic protein constructs in the context of their biochemical properties.

Discussion

Our study redefines the mechanistic paradigm of MeCP2 by demonstrating that the methyl-CpG- and RNA-binding activities are not segregated but are integrated functions of a single, synergistic MBD-ID module contained within the full-length protein. This model resolves the conflicting evidence [28] of how RNA can displace MeCP2 from DNA: it is not a competition between independent domains, but a competition for a functionally overlapping interface. The synergy we observe, with the ID enhancing the MBD’s DNA affinity by 35-fold and RNA affinity by over three orders of magnitude, underscores that the MBD and ID are co-dependent elements necessary and sufficient for the nucleic acid binding activities of MeCP2. While the MeCP2 NID has recently been shown to facilitate RNA-dependent protein interaction, deletion of the NID/RBD had only a marginal effect on direct RNA interaction [59], indicating that the MBD-ID module is a major determinant of MeCP2–RNA binding. This has direct and critical implications for understanding the effect of mutations that lie within the highly conserved ID (Fig. 1), many of which are classified as variants of unknown significance [30]. We found that pathogenic mutations within the ID lead to a molecular decoupling, that is, they preserve high-affinity methyl-DNA binding while modestly but selectively impairing interactions with non-methylated DNA and RNA. The MeCP2 MiniGene, which is being tested in the clinic (FDA: TSHA 102), preserves DNA-binding functions but exhibits diminished RNA binding and lacks DNA methylation-dependent positive cooperativity observed in the FL MeCP2. Although in vitro reconstitution cannot capture the complexity of the nuclear environment, biochemical studies are necessary to define intrinsic biochemical parameters, such as binding affinities, stoichiometries, and cooperativity, which provide a quantitative foundation for interpreting more complex cell-based systems. Our biochemical studies suggest that normalizing the balance of nucleic acid binding activities may have better therapeutic outcomes.

We speculate that the physiological function of this dynamic, competitive interplay between DNA- and RNA-binding is to modulate MeCP2 chromatin occupancy. Given the exceptionally high levels of MeCP2 in mature neurons [60] and the abundance of its methyl-CpG/CpH recognition sites [6–10], a mechanism must exist to prevent irreversible chromatin condensation and allow for dynamic gene regulation. Our data point to a model where RNA binding could serve as a critical rheostat for MeCP2 chromatin occupancy. Nuclear RNAs, including nascent transcripts and lncRNAs, can have a local concentration that exceeds that of the DNA template [61], which we propose could act as molecular competitors to actively displace a subset of MeCP2 molecules from chromatin to fine-tune gene regulation. Future investigation into the range of nuclear RNAs that are physiological targets of MeCP2, especially in the brain, which likely include structured RNAs and perhaps modified or sequence-specific RNAs, and how these RNAs may impact MeCP2 chromatin occupancy is an important future direction.

Our data also lead us to speculate about the relevance of MeCP2 binding to unmethylated DNA. It was recently shown that when MeCP2 acts as a transcriptional activator, it co-occupies hypomethylated transcription start sites with RNA polymerase II [62]. Given the low selectivity of FL MeCP2 for methylated versus unmethylated DNA (approximately 6-fold), non-specific interaction with unmethylated DNA may serve to reduce the effective search time for MeCP2 to its canonical recognition regions on chromatin. This “reduced dimensionality” mechanism, where non-specific binding facilitates faster target location, is well-established for other DNA-binding proteins (e.g. transcription factors such as p53 and LacI) [63, 64].

A critical future step to test our model will be to determine the structural basis of this integrated DNA–RNA binding MeCP2 interface. We have evaluated structural modeling using AlphaFold3 [65] but found current limitations (scarcity of protein–RNA training data, hallucinations with intrinsically disordered regions) preclude reliable predictions [66–68]. High-resolution structures of the MBD-ID module bound to methylated DNA and to RNA are needed to reveal the precise molecular contacts that define the competitive interface and address whether MeCP2 recognizes RNA shape, sequence, or hybrid features. This structural knowledge would facilitate the rational design of separation-of-function mutations that selectively disrupt RNA binding while preserving DNA binding. These engineered variants would then serve as powerful tools to dissect the potential contributions of MeCP2–RNA interactome to chromatin organization and transcriptional regulation in neurons.

This RNA rheostat model positions MeCP2 at a nexus: it is a chromatin organizer whose own placement is regulated by the epitranscriptome. The pathology of MECP2-related neurological disorders may therefore arise not only from disrupted DNA binding and chromatin architecture but also from dysfunctional RNA interactions. Future studies should help to reveal whether ID mutations and disrupted RNA interaction cause hyperstability of MeCP2 on chromatin, leading to reduced ability to dynamically modulate gene expression during neural cell fate transitions and establishment of functional neural circuits. Together our studies suggest that the MBD-ID module acts as a molecular processor of nucleic acid cues, and its balanced output is essential for neurodevelopment.
