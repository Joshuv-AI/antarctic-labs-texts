Signal peptide peptidase-like proteases OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 facilitate ER-associated protein degradation in rice
Authors: Hai-Ping Lu, Jian-Hang Xu, Jia-Xin Chang, Hui-Dan Luo, Jian-Xiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Signal peptide peptidases (SPPs) play a critical role in intramembrane proteolysis of signal peptides in mammals. However, their function in plants remains poorly understood. Here, we uncover the critical role of two rice SPP-like proteins, OsSPPL1/ 2, in ER-associated degradation (ERAD). Their expression is directly upregulated by OsbZIP50 under ER stress conditions. Mutations in OsSPPL1/2 result in increased ER stress sensitivity, whereas their overexpression enhance ER stress tolerance. We further demonstrate that OsSPPL1/2 localize in ER, and physically interact with the ERAD components OsDER1/2, indicating their involvement in ERAD. Using a GFP protein fused with a segment of maize floury-2 protein defective in signal peptide cleavage (ZmFL2m-GFP), we show that OsSPPL1/2 interact with ZmFL2m-GFP in ER and facilitate its degradation in tobacco leaves and rice plants. Additionally, OsSPPL1/2 double mutants exhibit exaggerated thermal sensitivity, while OsSPPL1/2-overexpressing plants display improved thermotolerance. Together, our findings identify OsSPPL1/2 as components of ERAD and highlight the importance of ERAD in plant thermotolerance.

Introduction

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) quality control (ERQC) system plays a crucial role in ensuring the folding fidelity of proteins entering the secretory pathway, particularly when plants are exposed to environmental stresses, such as pathogen attack, heat, drought, and salinity1,2. As a central component of ERQC, the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR), a highly conserved stress response mechanism in eukaryotic organisms. The UPR upregulates genes involved in enhancing protein folding capacity and eliminating misfolded proteins3,4. In plants, the UPR is mediated by at least two distinct pathways: the site-2 protease (S2P)-basic leucine zipper 28 (bZIP28) and inositol-requiring protein 1 (IRE1)-bZIP60 pathways, each involving the activation of specific ER stress transducers5. In Arabidopsis, bZIP28, a key UPR regulator, is an ER-localized type-II membrane-associated transcription factor that transduces stress signals from the ER to the nucleus6,7. Under normal conditions, bZIP28 resides in the ER membrane. However, upon ER stress and the accumulation of misfolded proteins, it relocates to the Golgi apparatus, where it is processed by the metalloprotease S2P before entering the nucleus to regulate downstream gene expression8,9. In contrast, bZIP60, another ER membrane-associated transcription factor, is activated through a distinct mechanism involving the ER-localized transmembrane protein kinase/ribonuclease IRE110,11. Upon activation, IRE1 recognizes a double stem-loop structure in bZIP60 mRNA, splicing out a 23-base intron. This splicing event induces a frameshift, eliminating the transmembrane domain of bZIP60 and enabling its nuclear translocation10,12,13. These two UPR pathways converge to regulate downstream genes critical for plant adaptation to adverse environmental conditions, such as heat stress14,15.

Another critical aspect of the ERQC system is ER-associated degradation (ERAD), which identifies, retrotranslocates, ubiquitinates, and degrades misfolded proteins in the ER16,17. Our knowledge of ERAD mechanism is still very limited in plants. The most extensively studied ERAD pathways in plants are the 3-HYDROXY-3-METHYLGLUTARYL (HMG)-CoA REDUCTASE DEGRADATION (HRD1) and DEGRADATION OF ALPHA2-10 (DOA10) pathways. These pathways involve conserved components shared with yeast and mammals, including the E3 ubiquitin ligases HRD1 and DOA10, as well as associated proteins, such as Arabidopsis OSTEOSARCOMA9 (AtOS9)/EMS bri1 SUPPRESSOR6 (EBS6), AtHRD3/EBS5, and DEGRADATION IN ER 1 (DER1)18–20. In yeast, HRD1 forms a retrotranslocation channel for the movement of misfolded polypeptides through the ER membrane21. Der1, a yeast integral membrane protein is recruited to HRD1 to function in substrate recognition, ubiquitination, and retrotranslocation of ERAD substrates22. Der1-like proteins (Derlins) in maize are capable of functionally complementing a yeast Der1 deletion mutant and function in ERAD in plants23 Similarly, the DOA10 complex includes key components, such as the E3 ubiquitin ligases RING MEMBRANE ANCHOR1 (RMA1) and RING finger protein (EMR), which interact with UBIQUITIN-CONJUGATING ENZYME 32 (UBC32)24–26. These two ERAD machineries mutually inhibit each other to fine-tune ERAD activity under basal conditions27.

Signal peptide peptidases (SPPs) are aspartic proteases characterized by seven transmembrane domains. They catalyze the intramembrane cleavage of peptide segments following initial processing by signal peptidase (SP)28. SPPs share structural similarities with presenilins, another class of intramembrane-cleaving proteases29. Despite the presumed importance, the functions of SPP and SPPL proteins in plants remain largely unexplored.

The rice (Oryza sativa L.) genome harbors two SPPs and five SPPLs. In this study, we investigate the roles of two rice SPPL proteins, OsSPPL1 (LOC_Os02g57710) and OsSPPL2 (LOC_Os06g51430), in ER stress response and thermotolerance. We demonstrate that OsSPPL1/2 are critical for ER stress response and are involved in ERAD by regulating the degradation of a substrate protein defective in signal peptide cleavage. We also provide evidence that OsSPPL1/2-mediated ERAD process operates in granules in plants, and OsSPPL1/2 are critical for heat stress tolerance. Our findings provide insights into the mechanisms of ERAD in plants and highlight the importance of SPPL proteins in plant stress adaptation.

Results

OsSPPL1/2 is induced by ER Stress depending on OsbZIP50

Our RNA-seq analysis of rice plants treated with the ER stress inducer tunicamycin (TM) revealed a significant upregulation of two rice SPPL genes, OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2, which was further validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1a). In the rice UPR system, OsbZIP50 (also known as OsbZIP74) is the ortholog of AtbZIP60, while OsbZIP60 corresponds to AtbZIP2811,30–32. To check whether these UPR regulators directly regulate OsSPPL1/2 induction under ER stress conditions, we firstly examined their expression in bzip50 and bzip60 mutants, respectively33. The results demonstrated that OsSPPL1/2 upregulation was significantly attenuated in the bzip50 mutant but remained unaffected in the bzip60 mutant (Fig. 1a), indicating that OsbZIP50 acts as an upstream transcriptional regulator of OsSPPL1/2 under ER stress conditions. We subsequently performed effector-reporter assays using constitutively expressed OsbZIP50 (the activated form without the C-terminal transmembrane domain) as an effector, and firefly luciferase driven by different fragment of promoter sequences of OsSPPL1/2 as the reporter, respectively. The results showed that OsbZIP50 could activate the promoter sequences (region A, -2.5 kb to -2.0 kb relative to TSS) of both OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 (Fig. 1b). Further EMSA and ChIP-qPCR experiments demonstrated that OsbZIP50 binds to the P-UPRE-like cis-elements3 presented in the OsSPPL1/2 promoters (Fig. 1c, d). OsSPPL1/2 are predicted to be multi-spanning membrane proteins in the secretory pathway with a signal peptide and nine transmembrane domains according to ARAMEMNON34 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The N-terminus of OsSPPL1/2 contains a signal peptide sequence which directs the protein to the secretory pathway; therefore, we fused GFP sequences to the C-terminus of OsSPPL1/2, and checked subcellular localization of OsSPPL1/2 in rice protoplasts. The results revealed that both OsSPPL1-GFP and OsSPPL2-GFP were colocalized with an ER marker (SP-mCherry-HDEL) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Insertion of GFP to the N-terminus of OsSPPL1/2 after the signal peptide sequence also showed an ER localization pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Together, these results suggest that OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 are involved in ER stress responses.

OsSPPL1/2 are critical for ER stress tolerance

To elucidate the functional roles of OsSPPL1/2 in ER stress responses, we generated sppl1 and sppl2 single mutants, as well as sppl1 sppl2 double mutants with the CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Under normal growth conditions, these mutants exhibited no significant differences in shoot length, root length, or plant weight compared to the wildtype ZH11 (Fig. 2a). However, under TM-induced ER stress, the sppl1 sppl2 double mutants displayed heightened sensitivity, as evidenced by reduced shoot length, root length, and plant weight (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3). We also generated OsSPPL1/2 overexpression plants (Supplementary Fig. 2e) and checked their ER stress sensitivity. Overexpression of either OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 enhanced ER stress tolerance without affecting growth under normal conditions (Figs. 2c and 2d; Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings collectively demonstrate that OsSPPL1/2 are critical for ER stress tolerance in rice.

OsSPPL1/2 are involved in ERAD

To explore the mechanistic basis of OsSPPL1/2 function, we conducted a split-ubiquitin yeast two-hybrid screen using OsSPPL2 as a bait. Totally 155 candidate interacting proteins were obtained, and 40% of them were predicted to be localized in the secretory pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5). Among the identified interactors (Supplementary Data 2), OsDER1 emerged as a top candidate due to its role in the ERAD pathway35. We included OsDER2, a paralog of OsDER1 in the validation experiment and further analysis confirmed interactions between OsSPPL1/2 and both OsDER1 and OsDER2 in split-ubiquitin yeast two-hybrid assays (Fig. 4a, b). Pull-down assays subsequently confirmed the direct physical interactions between OsSPPL1/2 and OsDER1/2, while another multi-spanning membrane protein OsNRT2.436 did not interacted with OsSPPL1/2 (Fig. 4c, d). We further performed split-YFP assays in rice protoplasts. The full-length of OsSPPL1/2 interacted with OsDER1/2, while the C-terminal truncated form (deletion of the last transmembrane segment) of OsSPPL1/2 did not interact with OsDER1/2 (Fig. 4e–h). The YFP fluorescence was colocalized with an ER marker (Fig. 4g, h). OsSPPL1/2 interact with ZmFL2m-GFP and promote its degradation

Many ERAD factors in plants were initially isolated based on Arabidopsis thaliana bri1-5 and bri1-9 suppressor screening, or by investigation of yeast homologous components of ERAD machinery using mutated misfolded proteins, such as BRI1-5, BRI1-9, MLO, SUBEX-C57Y2. Understanding of the importance of ERAD function under physiological conditions is currently very limited because of the lack of physiologically relevant endogenous substrates in plants and even in mammalians37. To investigate the role of OsSPPL1/2 in ERAD, we utilized a maize-derived synthetic substrate, ZmFL2m-GFP, which contains the first 35 amino acids of ZmFL2, harboring an amino acid mutation (A21V) in the N-terminus preventing signal peptide cleavage38–40. We then carried out split-YFP assays in both rice protoplasts and N. benthamiana epidermal cells using the full-length and C-terminal truncated form of OsSPPL1/2. We further stably expressed ZmFL2m-GFP or ZmFL2-GFP in ZH11, sppl1/2-1 double mutants, OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 overexpression rice plants. Comparing to other genetic background, overexpression of ZmFL2m-GFP in sppl1/2-1 double mutants led to growth retardation (Fig. 6a, b). Overexpression ZmFL2m-GFP but not ZmFL2-GFP activated UPR (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that ZmFL2m-GFP is misfolded when overexpressing in plants. Green foci were observed in root cells of ZmFL2m-GFP overexpression plants in ZH11 background, and the signals were enhanced in sppl1/2-1 double mutant background (Fig. 6c), while co-overexpression of OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 suppressed these effects (Fig. 6c). The endogenous substrates of plant ERAD are largely unknown. Recently, the turnover of auxin transport facilitators PIN-LIKES (PILS) was reported to be controlled by the ERAD machinery in Arabidopsis42. Interestingly, one rice PILS protein (LOC_Os08g09190) was identified as the candidate interactor of OsSPPL2 (Supplementary Data 2). We selected four stress-associated OsSPPL2 candidate interactors from the list43–46 based on the antibody availability and performed western-blotting analysis. The results showed that the accumulation of a receptor-like kinase (LOC_Os04g56430), an aquaporin protein (LOC_Os02g44630), and a monosaccharide transporter (LOC_Os09g15330) was increased in OsSPPL1/2 double mutant while decreased in OsSPPL1 overexpression and OsSPPL2 overexpression plants (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating that these membrane proteins are endogenous substrates of OsSPPL12. The detailed mechanism awaits further investigation in future.

OsSPPL1/2 are required for thermotolerance

Both OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 were induced by heat stress (Supplementary Fig. 12), therefore, we assessed the role of OsSPPL1/2 in thermotolerance at both seedling and reproductive stages. At the seedling stage, sppl1/2-1 and sppl1/2-2 double mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to heat stress, while OsSPPL1/2 overexpression lines showed enhanced tolerance, as measured by survival rates (Fig. 7a, b). We extended our analysis to the reproductive stage. Mutant and overexpression plants initially grown at normal growth temperature were subjected to heat stress beginning at the heading stage, followed by a recovery period until maturity. The results showed that heat stress significantly reduced seed setting rate, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield in the double mutants, whereas overexpression lines displayed improved performance (Fig. 7c–h). In agreement with these phenotypes, the expression of heat stress responsive UPR genes was higher in the OsSPPL1/2 double mutants while lower in the OsSPPL1/2 overexpression plants compared to the ZH11 control plants (Supplementary Fig. 13). To identify natural alleles of OsSPPL1/2, we conducted a haplotype analysis using the RiceVarMap v.2.0 database (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) on 4,726 rice accessions, including Indica, Aus, Japonica, and Intermediate varieties. Our analysis revealed only five single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the coding sequence (CDS) region of OsSPPL1. In contrast, the 2.5-kb promoter sequence contained 42 SNPs and 9 insertions/deletions (InDels), the 5’ untranslated region (UTR) had 2 InDels, and the 3’ UTR had 2 SNPs and 2 InDels (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Sequence analysis revealed complete conservation of the bZIP50 transcription factor binding site in the OsSPPL1 promoter (positions 35339589-35339598, MSU7.0 reference genome), with no variation detected. Excluding synonymous mutations, six major haplotypes (Hap1–Hap6) were classified by 5 SNPs presented in CDS. Hap1 and Hap2 accessions accounted for 93.20% of the 4,706 rice accessions analyzed. Furthermore, 85.48% of Hap1 accessions belonged to Japonica, while 88.53% of Hap2 accessions were Indica varieties. (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Similarly, in OsSPPL2, we identified 2 SNPs in the CDS, 18 SNPs and 13 InDels in the promoter region, 1 SNP and 4 InDels in the 5’-UTR, and 5 polymorphisms in the 3’-UTR (Supplementary Fig. 14c). After excluding synonymous mutations, only one major haplotype was identified, defined by a single non-synonymous SNP (Supplementary Fig. 14d). The bZIP50 binding site in the promoter of OsSPPL2 (31149017-31149026) also showed complete sequence conservation. To determine whether OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 has undergone domestication selection, we performed nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis of the 500-kb region spanning the CDS region of OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2. Both Japonica and Indica exhibit lower Pi values compared to wild rice (O. rufipogon), but show no notable difference between themselves (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). Furthermore, population differentiation analysis (Fst) further reveals that the genetic divergence between Indica and Japonica is smaller than that between either cultivated subspecies and wild rice (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b), a pattern consistently observed in both OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2. These findings suggest that OsSPPL1/2 underwent similar selective pressures during rice domestication, resulting in reduced genetic diversity in cultivated varieties relative to their wild rice, with no detectable difference in selection effects between Indica and Japonica subspecies.

To evaluate the potential of OsSPPL1/2 overexpression plants for biological breeding, we conducted preliminary field trials in Zhejiang University Experimental Station (Changxing, Huzhou, Zhejiang). Our findings revealed that the seed setting rate, 1000-grain weight, and grain yield per plot were increased in OsSPPL1/2 overexpression plants compared to the ZH11 control plants (Supplementary Fig. 16), However, higher chalkiness, an undesirable trait that reduces grain quality, was observed in OsSPPL1/2 overexpression plants (Supplementary Fig. 17). Therefore, achieving an optimal balance between thermotolerance and grain yield quality remains a critical challenge for future utilization of the OsSPPL1/2 loci in rice breeding programs.

Discussion

Global warming poses significant challenges to sustainable agriculture, with mathematical models predicting a 6-7% reduction in cereal production for every 1 °C increase in environmental temperature47. Recent advances have begun to elucidate the mechanisms underlying plant heat stress tolerance48–52. One of the most detrimental effects of heat stress on cellular function is the denaturation of proteins and the formation of protein aggregates in both the cytosol and ER53. For instance, Thermotolerance 1 (OgTT1), a major quantitative trait locus (QTL) identified in African rice (O. glaberrima), encodes the α2 subunit of the 26S proteasome, which plays a critical role in degrading cytotoxic denatured proteins under heat stress54,55. Similarly, OsNTL3, a plasma membrane-associated transcription factor, translocates to the nucleus in response to heat stress and regulates downstream genes involved in ER protein folding56. These mechanisms underscore the importance of intracellular protein homeostasis in maintaining proper protein conformation and function during heat stress17. Previous studies have highlighted the role of the UPR in plant heat stress tolerance15,56,57. In this study, we demonstrate that OsSPPL1/2, components of the ERAD pathway, are also crucial for heat stress tolerance in plants, emphasizing their role in maintaining ER homeostasis through the efficient clearance of misfolded proteins. In addition, OsSPPL1/2 are up-regulated by ER stress and heat stress, which is dependent on the UPR regulator OsbZIP50, connecting these two pathways under heat-induced ER stress conditions.

The signal peptide peptidase (SPP) family is highly conserved across multicellular eukaryotes, yet its functions in plants remain poorly understood58. Rice possesses two SPP proteins, OsSPP1 and OsSPP2, which are strongly expressed in vegetative shoot apices, young panicles, developing panicles, and early-stage florets59. Our attempts to generate OsSPP1 or OsSPP2 mutants were unsuccessful, likely due to the lethality of null mutations. This is consistent with findings in Arabidopsis, where homozygous AtSPP T-DNA insertion mutants could not be recovered, and pollen viability was severely compromised29,60. Medicago BID1, a nodule-specific SPP, plays a central role in ER-symbiosome association, host protein secretion, and symbiosome differentiation61. In the current study, we generated single and double mutants of two rice SPP-like genes and observed that they exhibited normal growth under standard conditions but displayed heightened sensitivity to environmental stresses, such as heat stress. These findings significantly expand our understanding of the roles of SPP-like proteins in plants.

ERAD has been extensively studied in yeast and is categorized into three pathways: ERAD-L, which degrades misfolded soluble proteins in the ER lumen and membrane proteins with misfolded luminal domains; ERAD-M, which targets misfolded domains within the membrane; and ERAD-C, which degrades membrane proteins with misfolded cytoplasmic domains62. In plants, mutated forms of single transmembrane proteins, such as Arabidopsis brassinosteroid receptor Brassinosteroid Insensitive 1 (BRI1) and immune receptor Elongation Factor Thermo-Unstable Receptor (EFR), have been used as ERAD-L substrates, while the mutated form of multiple transmembrane protein Barley Mildew Resistance Locus O (MLO) serves as an ERAD-M substrate17. In the current study, the accumulation of mutated MLO but not BRI1-5/-9 was affected by co-expression of OsSPP1/2, indicating that OsSPPL1/2 are involved in ERAD-M. Given that SPPs are known to cleave signal peptides of proteins in the ER membrane58, we utilized the segment of maize floury-2 protein (ZmFL2m), derived from a mutated α-zein, a storage protein normally processed during synthesis38. A pivotal aspect of ERAD involves the retrotranslocation of misfolded proteins from the ER lumen or membrane into the cytosol, where they undergo ubiquitination at the cytoplasmic site and are subsequently degraded by the 26S proteasome17,63. In our investigation, the inhibition of the 26S proteasome by MG132 attenuated the degradation of ZmFL2m-GFP mediated by OsSPPL1/2, underscoring the significance of ZmFL2m-GFP retrotranslocation and ubiquitination in the OsSPPL1/2-mediated ERAD pathway (Fig. 8). Conventionally, SPP is believed to cleave only type II transmembrane proteins in mammals, with the large portion of N-terminal domain oriented towards the cytoplasm64. Contrary to this paradigm, our study reveals that ZmFL2m-GFP, a type I protein anchored to the ER membrane due to a mutation in signal peptide cleavage site with its long C-terminus residing in the ER lumen, can also serve as a substrate for OsSPPL1/2. This suggests that such misfolded proteins may undergo retrotranslocation from the ER to the cytosol for further processing (Fig. 8). OsSPPL1/2 interacts with OsDER1/2, members of the Derlin family within the rhomboid superfamily. The cryo-EM structure of human Derlin-1, which forms a channel for the translocation of misfolded proteins35, supports the hypothesis that ZmFL2m-GFP could be retrotranslocated by OsDER1/2 and further processed by OsSPPL1/2 in the ER membrane during ERAD. Alternatively, OsSPPL1/2 cleavage within the ER membrane may enhance the retrotranslocation of ZmFL2m-GFP by OsDER1/2.

The HRD1 complex in rice comprises essential components, including OsHRD1, OsHRD3, OsCDC48-1, and OsCDC48-265. Here, we reveal that OsSPPL1/2 associates with OsDER1/2, suggesting a potential functional linkage between OsSPPL1/2 and the HRD1 complex. E3 ubiquitin ligases play a critical role in both the HRD1 and DOA10 pathways27. Consistent with this, our data indicate that OsSPPL1/2-mediated protein degradation is suppressed by a 26S proteasome inhibitor, implicating the ubiquitin-proteasome system in this process. Future studies should focus on identifying the specific E3 ubiquitin ligase(s) involved in OsSPPL1/2-dependent ERAD.

The UPR is a well-established cytoprotective mechanism that mitigates ER stress by upregulating genes involved in protein folding, trafficking, and ERAD at the transcriptional level during ER stress responses15. In parallel, ERAD functions independently at the post-translational level as the terminal effector, mediating the clearance of misfolded proteins from the ER17. In the present study, we observe a pronounced activation of UPR marker genes involved in protein folding in the OsSPPL1/2 double mutants; however, these mutants still exhibit heightened sensitivity to ER stress. These results implicated that in the OsSPPL1/2 mutant, although the UPR is induced, its effector arm, ERAD, is compromised. The resulting accumulation of misfolded proteins due to ERAD dysfunction likely creates a chronic stress state that outweighs the protective effects of UPR activation. Consequently, despite the UPR-mediated enhancement of protein folding capacity, this adaptive response is insufficient to compensate for ERAD deficiency, ultimately leading to the observed ER stress sensitivity. This interpretation aligns with previous findings in rice; for instance, the ERAD-defective hrd3 mutant also exhibits strong UPR activation yet remains stress-sensitive and accumulates aberrant protein aggregates in seeds65. Future studies are warranted to dissect the relative contributions of ERAD and UPR-remediated protein folding in determining the heat-sensitive phenotype.

Biomolecular condensates are membraneless organelle-like structures that can concentrate molecules and often form through liquid-liquid phase separation66. Nucleolus, stress granules, and nuclear speckles have been designated as biomolecular condensates to describe their shared property of spatially concentrating biomolecules67. Intriguingly, ZmFL2m-GFP forms distinct green foci in rice protoplasts and root cells, and the interaction between ZmFL2m and OsSPPL1/2 was also localized to these foci in both rice protoplasts and tobacco epidermal cells. These green fluorescent foci resemble protein bodies, which are membrane-bound organelles containing storage proteins in the starchy endosperm of cereal grains41. However, since only a 35-amino acid fragment of FL2/FL2m was fused to GFP and no similar foci were observed for ZmFL2-GFP, these structures in non-storage organ are unlikely to represent classical protein bodies. The nature of these green foci—whether they represent protein aggregates or specialized organelles dedicated to ERAD—remains an open question warranting further exploration.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the critical role of OsSPPL1/2 in ERAD and their contribution to heat stress tolerance in plants. These insights not only advance our understanding of ERAD in plants but also provide potential targets for improving crop resilience in the face of global warming.

Methods

Plant materials and genetic constructs

The wild-type rice (Oryza sativa L. ssp. japonica cv. Zhonghua 11, ZH11) was used in this study. To generate transgene-free gene-edited mutants, gene-specific single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting OsSPPL1 (LOC_Os02g57710) and OsSPPL2 (LOC_Os06g51430) were designed and cloned into a CRISPR-Cas9 binary vector. For OsSPPL1/2 overexpression lines, the coding sequences (CDS) of OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 were amplified and independently inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-FLAG vector. For protein accumulation assay, ZmFLm-GFP or ZmFL-GFP was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector in ZH11, sppl1/2-1, SPPL1OE-1, or SPPL2OE-1 background. To verify the expression of transgenes, protein extracts were subjected to immunoblotting with the following primary antibodies: Mouse anti-FLAG monoclonal antibody (1:5,000 dilution; Abmart, Shanghai, China); Mouse anti-GFP antibody (1:5,000, Abmart); Mouse anti-β-tubulin monoclonal antibody (1:5,000 dilution; Abmart). Secondary antibody: Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000 dilution; Abmart). All primers used in the study are listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Phenotypic analysis of ER stress sensitivity

Rice seedlings were hydroponically cultivated in Kimura B nutrient solution under controlled conditions: 20,000 lux light intensity (12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod) in growth chambers (Model PGR15, Conviron, Canada). To induce ER stress, tunicamycin (TM) was supplemented into the medium at varying concentrations for two weeks. Shoot length, root length, and fresh weight were then measured to assess stress sensitivity.

Thermotolerance assays

At seedling stage, seven-day-old seedlings grown at 30 °C were subjected to heat stress at 45 °C for 2 days in growth chambers, followed by recovery at 30 °C for 7 days. Survival rates were quantified. At reproductive stage, plants were maintained at 30 °C until heading, and uniformly three developed tillers/panicles from each plant were tagged and exposed to 38 °C for 3 days (12 h of heat treatment in the light for each day). After stress treatment, plants were returned to 30 °C until maturity. Seed-setting rate, 1000-grain weight, and total grain yield per plant were evaluated.

RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analysis

Seven-day-old seedlings of ZH11 and sppl1/2 double mutants were treated with 10 μg/mL TM for 4 hours. Root tissues were harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s procedure and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) with paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw data were processed using HISAT2 software for alignment and DESeq2 for differential expression analysis, with significance thresholds set at fold change (FC) ≥ 2 and P ≤ 0.05. For RT-qPCR validation, total RNA was isolated from frozen root tissues using the RNA Prep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China), followed by DNase I treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg total RNA using 5× PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA) with SuperReal PreMix Plus (Tiangen, China) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Reactions were run in triplicate, and gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene OsActin using the ∆∆Ct method.

Protein subcellular localization study

To determine the subcellular localization of OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2, their coding sequences (CDS) were PCR-amplified and cloned into the pTMZ28-GFP vector, generating C-terminal GFP-tagged fusion constructs. The ninth transmembrane domain and C-terminal sequence of OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 were removed (∆TM9) and were also used in the study. To generate ZmFL2m-GFP and ZmFL2-GFP, the first 35 amino acids of ZmFL2 (MATKILALLALLALLVSATNAFIIPQCSLAPSASI) and its mutant form ZmFL2m (MATKILALLALLALLVSATNVFIIPQCSLAPSASI) derived from maize were fused to GFP, respectively. These constructs were transiently co-expressed with an ER marker (HDEL-mCherry) in rice protoplasts using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation. After 16–24 h of incubation at 25 °C in darkness, fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 710, Zeiss, Germany). Images were processed using ZEN software (Zeiss) to assess co-localization.

Proteinase K protection assay

Rice sample was ground and extracted with pre-chilled extraction buffer (0.4 M, sucrose, 50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH7.5; 5 mM EDTA; 5 mM DTT; 1 mM PMSF; 0.6% PVP-40; 1% BSA) on ice for 15 min, then filtered through cheesecloth. After centrifuging at 4 °C using the following centrifugal forces: 1500 g for 10 minutes, collect supernatant; 12,000 × g for 20 minutes, collect supernatant; 100,000 × g for 60 min, the pellet constitutes the ER extract. The pellet was resuspended using the reaction buffer (0.25 M sucrose; 10 mM HEPES-KOH, pH7.5; 1 mM DTT) by pipetting, then treated with protease K and or Triton X-100. After incubation at 25 °C for 30 min, SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added to stop the reaction for Western blot analysis. The ER protein BiP was used as the internal control.

Yeast two-hybrid analysis

Full-length coding sequences of OsSPPL1/2 and OsDER1/2 were cloned into pBT3-SUC and pPR3-N vectors, respectively. All bait-prey combinations were co-transformed into yeast strain NMY51 using the Coolaber transformation kit and selected on SD/-Leu/-Trp or SD/-Leu/-Trp-His-Ade plates at 30 °C for 2-4 days. Positive interactions were verified through X-Gal filter lift assay: Colonies were transferred to sterile filter papers by gentle pressing (10 sec contact). Filters were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen (10 sec) and thawed (2 min, RT). Filters were incubated on Z-buffer-saturated paper (60 mM Na2HPO4, 40 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4, 0.5 mg/mL X-gal, pH 7.0). Color development was monitored at 30 °C in the dark.

Split-YFP assays

To investigate protein-protein interactions in planta, we employed a split-YFP system with the following construct combinations: OsSPPL1/2 were fused to the C-terminal fragment of YFP (cYFP) in vector pXY104. OsDER1/2 were fused to the N-terminal fragment of YFP (nYFP) in vector pXY103. The first 35 amino acids of ZmFL2 and its mutant form ZmFL2m derived from maize were fused to nYFP (pXY103). The ninth transmembrane domain and C-terminal sequence of OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 were deleted (∆TM9) to make sure that the c-YFP portion is in the ER luminal site where the n-YFP portion resides during the interaction assays with ZmFL2m/ZmFL2. For each interaction test, appropriate plasmid pairs were co-transformed into rice protoplasts via polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation or tobacco epidermal cells via agrobacterial infiltration. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 710, Zeiss, Germany).

In vitro pull-down assays

The coding sequences of OsSPPL1/2 and OsDER1/2 were cloned into pGEX-4T-1 (for GST-tagged proteins) and pETMALc-H (for MBP-tagged proteins), respectively. These constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells for protein expression. Protein production was induced with 300 μM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C for 16–18 h to promote proper protein folding. The recombinant fusion proteins were purified using Glutathione-Superflow resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Amylose agarose beads (New England Biolabs). For interaction assays: Equal amounts of purified proteins were incubated in pull-down buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed five times with ice-cold pull-down buffer to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were detected by Western blotting with anti-GST antibody (Abmart, Shanghai, China) or anti-MBP antibody (GenScript, Piscataway, USA).

Cell-free degradation assay

To analyze the stability of ZmFL2m-GFP and its potential regulation by OsSPPL1/2, we performed a cell-free degradation assay in tobacco (N. benthamiana) leaves: Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 strains carrying FL2m-GFP, OsSPPL1, or OsSPPL2 constructs were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 72 hours, leaf tissues were harvested and ground in extraction buffer: 50 mM Tris-MES (pH 8.0), 0.5 M sucrose, 1 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, and 1× cOmplete Mini protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Shanghai, China). Total proteins were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). After that, the protein mixtures were incubated with 10 mM ATP (energy source) and 50 μM cycloheximide (CHX) (to block new protein synthesis) at room temperature for 0 to120 min in the presence or absence of 50 μM MG132 (proteasome inhibitor) and terminated by adding SDS loading buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with anti-GFP antibody (1:5,000, Abmart) to monitor FL2m-GFP degradation. Anti-tubulin (1:10,000) was used as a loading control. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Ubiquitination assay

The plants were treated with 100 μM MG132 for 2 hours, and total proteins were extracted using buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, 1X protease inhibitor. After incubation on ice for 15 min, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, supernatant was collected. Immunoprecipitation was performed using GFP antibody conjugated to protein G beads at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times and subjected to SDS-PAGE. The immunoprecipitated proteins were blotted with anti-GFP and anti-ubiquitin antibodies.

Effector–reporter assays

For effector–reporter assays, the promoter sequence of OsSPPL1 or OsSPPL2 were inserted into the pGreen0800-II vector together with the 35S minimal promoter to generate the firefly luciferase reporter, and the Renilla luciferase driven by the 35S promoter served as an internal control. The CDS of spliced form of OsbZIP50 (OsbZIP50A) was inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-FLAG vector to generate the effector vector bZIP50. Different combinations of constructs were transiently expressed in tobacco leaves, and the luciferase activity was measured with a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega). Relative luciferase activity is the firefly luciferase activity normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity (internal control), which was then normalized to the pCAMBIA1300-FLAG empty vector control.

EMSA

The CDS of spliced form of OsbZIP50 was cloned into pETMALc-H vector. The fusion activated (OsbZIP50A) proteins, MBP-bZIP50, were expressed in BL21 cells and then extracted and purified for EMSA. The 3′ ends of probes used for EMSA were labeled with biotin according to the Biotin 3′ End DNA Labeling Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). EMSA was performed using a Light Shift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, each binding reaction (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 80 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 2.5 mM DTT, 0.07 mg mL-1 BSA and 8 ng mL-1 poly dI-dC) was set at room temperature for 20 min and run on a 5% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel. After transferring to a nylon membrane (GE), the blot was UV light cross-linked and detected with the chemiluminescent nucleic acid detection module (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

ChIP-qPCR

The MYC-tagged full-length form of OsbZIP50 (OsbZIP50P) was expressed driven by its native promoter (2 kb) in rice protoplasts. Before immunoprecipitation, protoplasts were treated with TM (5 μg/mL) for 8 h to induce ER stresses. DMSO was used as a negative control. After 12 h of incubation, formaldehyde was added for cross-linking at room temperature for 10 min, with a final concentration of 1%. Glycine was then added to terminate the reaction, followed by incubation for 5 min, with a final concentration of 125 mM. Subsequently, the protoplasts were collected by centrifugation at 600 g for 2 min at 4 °C. At room temperature, 120 μL of lysis buffer (50 Mm Tris-HCl, pH8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1%SDS, 1 mM PMSF, 1Xprotease inhibitor) was added, followed by vertexing and incubation on ice for 5 min. The DNA was sheared by sonication using a cycle of 30 s on and 30 s off for a total of 15 min. After sonication, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 100 μL supernatant was collected. 900 μL of dilution buffer was added, then incubated with either MYC antibody or IgG (negative control) for 4 h, followed by incubation with Protein G beads for an additional 4 h. The sample was centrifuged to collect the beads, and washed sequentially with low-salt buffer, high-salt buffer, LiCl buffer, and TE buffer. Finally, 250 μL of elution buffer was added and incubated in a 65 °C water bath for 15 min. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes to collect the supernatant. NaCl was added for overnight de-crosslinking. RNase A and proteinase K was added, and incubated at 45 °C for 1 h to remove proteins and RNA. DNA was precipitated using sodium acetate method, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to obtain the pellet, and stored for later use. The following qPCR analysis was performed to examine DNA enrichment. We included 1% of the input chromatin as a negative control. For each of the three biological replicates, qPCR was carried out and the DNA enrichment was calculated using the percent input method based on ΔCt values.

Natural variation analysis

Natural variation and haplotype analysis were conducted on the RiceVarMap v.2.0 (https://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn/) to determine the SNP sites and haplotype classifications of OsSPPL1/2, with the promoter and coding sequences selected as the gene regions for analysis. The VCF format file used for the population selection analysis, sourced from the 10548 rice accessions, was processed using vcftools (v0.1.16) to calculate nucleotide diversity (Pi) and population differentiation index (FST) for the 500-Kb regions flanking OsSPPL1/2, employing a 1 kb window size with a 100 bp overlapping step size across the genome.
