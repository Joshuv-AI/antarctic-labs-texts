The association of self-rated health with physical activity and demographic factors: a study in primary care from Northwest of Iran
Authors: Hanieh Moradi, Abouzar Raeisvandi, Mahdie Bahrami, Mohammad Amerzadeh, Mozhgan Abbasi, Zahra Hosseinkhani, Mehdi Osooli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-rated health (SRH) is an international measure and indicator of mortality and morbidity that can be used as a mortality predictor in adulthood. This study aimed to examine the status of SRH among adults in Qazvin Province and its relationship with physical activity levels and demographic factors in primary care centers.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study. We included data from 1223 individuals 18–64 years residing in Qazvin Province, located in northwest Iran. Participants were selected using the electronic health records in primary care and through a multi-stage cluster sampling method. Demographic and self-reported health information were collected using a self-administered questionnaire. SRH has been categorized ranging from very good to very poor. We estimated the Odds Ratio (OR) of suboptimal SRH in association with level of physical activity and adjusting for age, sex, marital status, and employment.ResultsIn this study, 407 (33.2%) of the participants, including 37% of female and 26% of male, exhibited sub-optimal SRH, while overall 34% reported no physical activity per week. The percentage of individuals with sub-optimal SRH was higher among those with no physical activity, older age groups, female sex and retirees. SRH was associated with having no physical activity (OR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.13, 2.14), older age, female sex (OR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.23, 2.48), and being retired (OR: 1.94; 95% CI: 1.05, 3.96).ConclusionsAbout one-third of the individuals demonstrated suboptimal SRH. Health policymakers must promote physical activity programs, especially among older age groups, retirees, and females to improve SRH in the community.Keywords: Self-rated health, Physical activity, Primary care, Iran

Introduction

Self-rated health (SRH) is a single-item measure of current health that involves asking individuals to evaluate their health on a Likert scale or in comparison to their peers [1]. SRH is an international measure of general health and an indicator of mortality and morbidity [2], as it reflects not only diagnosed diseases but also undiagnosed conditions, the state of mental health, and coping abilities that together predict future health outcomes and survival [3]. SRH is widely used in medical and epidemiological research [4] and is associated with healthy lifestyle and behaviors such as regular physical activity, low sedentary time, controlled healthy diet, and abstinence from substance abuse [5].

Physical activity is an important public health issue that can influence various aspects of health and SRH. It is a significant protective factor against the development of chronic diseases, and enhancing physical activity is expected to improve individuals’ overall health status [6]. On the other hand, physical inactivity is one of the leading causes of preventable disease, disability, and death [7], contributing to 7.2% of all-cause mortality worldwide and 69.0% of deaths in middle-income countries [8, 9].

Despite the well-known health benefits of physical activity, about 23% of the world’s population is inactive [6, 8]. A recent meta-analysis found that 49.2% of adults and 74.4% of adolescents in North Africa and the Middle East do not meet activity guidelines [10]. In Iran, physical inactivity was notably widespread, as the 2021 STEPS survey found that 51.3% of adults were inactive, including 42.0% of males and 58.0% of females [11]. Neighboring Arab countries show varied rates, from 5.2% in Jordan to 67.6% in Saudi Arabia [12].

Based on this, many international and national health organizations, including primary health care centers, have prioritized promoting physical activity to improve health [6]. Consequently, physical activity counseling is an effective approach to increasing physical activity levels within primary care settings [13]. Additionally, healthcare providers working in these centers should encourage patients with chronic diseases to engage in physical activity [14].

Identification of SRH changes and determinants of these can enable health professionals to target prevention and health promotion interventions efficiently [15]. To date, no studies have examined the interconnection between physical activity and SRH among adults attending primary health care centers in Iran. Due to the specific cultural, social, and healthcare context in Iran, findings from elsewhere cannot be entirely applied.

According to this, the purpose of this study is to assess the SRH status among adult residents of Qazvin province and to identify its correlation with physical activity and demographic factors. The results gathered would be valuable to develop evidence-based targeted health promotion programs and policies to improve health of adults in Iran.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study. The data included demographic information and SRH of the individuals residing in Qazvin Province, Iran. A multi-stage cluster sampling method was used to select the participants. Since Urban and Rural Health Centers, and Health Houses cover the entire region’s population, the questionnaires were self-administered by routine visitors to these centers, as well as by individuals who had been informed to participate.

Initially, 366 Urban and Rural Health Centers and Health Houses were listed, and each of them was considered as a cluster in each city and rural setting. The number of Health Centers or clusters included: Urban 96 units, and Rural 270 units. Based on the study sample size and the population covered by each health unit, the health centers were randomly and systematically selected. Subsequently, considering the population covered by the area of each health center, the number of individuals was selected proportional to the size of each unit and finally the samples randomly selected.

After coordinating with the health authorities, we held a coordination meeting (virtual) with the interviewers (health experts and staff in health units). After explaining the project objectives, we provided instructions for completing the questionnaire.

Routine visitors to healthcare centers and individuals who were informed to visit these centers completed the questionnaires in a self-administered manner after receiving explanations from the interviewer. If an individual was unable to complete the questionnaire independently, it was filled out through face-to-face interviews. It is worth noting that in a few instances where individuals could not visit the health unit, the interviewer would visit their homes to complete the questionnaire. The study data were gathered over a four-month period, from December 2021 to March 2022. In the present study, 1,285 individuals were invited the study and finally 1,223 participated which showed the response rate of 95.2%.

Using an odds ratio of 1.2, a statistical power of 80%, and a significance level (α) of 0.05, a minimum sample size of 1,168 was required. To compensate for an estimated 5% attrition rate, the final sample size was increased to 1,227 participants.

The eligible participants were 18–64 years, residing in Qazvin Province, willing to participate and complete the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria were unwillingness to participate in the study.

Data were collected using the World Health Organization (WHO) version of self-rated health questionnaire [16]. The questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part pertained to the participant’s demographic information, including age, gender, marital status, and occupation, and also physical activity (hours per week) information. The second part of the questionnaire evaluated the individual’s SRH with a single question that has been recommended by WHO: “How do you evaluate your health status”? on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very good, good, moderate, poor, to very poor. We classified “good” and “very good” to the optimal group and others as a suboptimal group [17, 18]. The validity of the self-reported health questionnaire has been confirmed in various studies and communities, including the Iranian population [18, 19]. The reliability of this single question instrument was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which was 0.83 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.72–0.90 [20, 21].

Descriptive-analytical statistics were used for data analysis. Mean and standard deviation were used for description of quantitative data, and for categorical variables frequency, and percentage were reported. The association between SRH and physical activity, and demographic variables were examined using univariate and multivariate regression modelling. We reported estimated Odds Ratio (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Data were cleaned and analyzed using STATA version 14.0.

Overall, 407 participants (33.2%) had sub-optimal SRH. Regarding age group categorization, the prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was higher in older age groups; specifically, in the oldest age group studied (individuals aged 55–64 years), the percentage of individuals with sub-optimal SRH (50.5%) exceeded that of individuals with optimal SRH (49.5%). The lowest percentage of individuals with sub-optimal SRH was observed among those aged 18–24, at 20.06%. Additionally, the prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was higher among female participants (36.8%) than males (25.5%).

In terms of marital status, the highest prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was reported among divorced/widowed individuals, where the percentage of those with sub-optimal SRH (56.0%) was greater than that of those with optimal SRH (44.0%). Among married individuals, 34.6% had sub-optimal SRH compared to 24.4% of single participants.

Regarding employment status, the highest prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was found among retirees (49.0%), which was even higher than that of the unemployed (35.6%), while the lowest prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was observed among employed individuals (27.7%). Based on physical activity status, the prevalence of sub-optimal SRH was highest among individuals with no physical activity (40.7%), while those engaging in physical activity for five hours or more per week had the lowest prevalence (29.80%).

Table 2 shows the status of SRH and physical activity of participants by gender groups. The results indicated that 816 individuals (66.7%) rated their health as good and very good. SRH was good and very good for 530 female participants (63.2%) and 286 male (74.4%). A higher percentage of males compared to females reported good and very good SRH. Overall, 420 (34.3%) of participants reported not having any physical activity, of which 312 (37.2%) and 108 (28.1%) were female and male, respectively. Finally, 319 (26.1%) participants including 212 female (25.3%) and 107 (27.9%) male participants reported having had ≥ 5 h of physical activity per week.

The multiple regression results (Table 3) indicated some higher odds of suboptimal SRH among participants without physical activity compared to those with ≥ 5 h per week (OR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.13, 2.14). On the other hand, the odds of sub-optimal SRH in individuals who engaged in physical activity for 1–4 h per week did not show a significant difference compared to those who engaged in physical activity for ≥ 5 h per week (OR: 1.11; 95% CI: 0.80, 1.53).

In assessing the association of age on suboptimal SRH, we considered 18–24 as the reference group. According to the results, all age groups had higher odds of suboptimal SRH compared with the reference group with stronger association in older groups. For example, among those aged 25–34 and 55–64 years the odds of suboptimal SRH was 1.57 (95% CI: 1.02, 2.40) and 3.63 (95% CI: 1.97, 6.70), respectively.

The analysis revealed association between SRH and no physical activity (OR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.13, 2.14). Female participants had a higher odds of suboptimal SRH compared with males (OR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.23, 2.48). However, the odds of suboptimal SRH among participants who were unmarried (OR: 1.20; 95% CI: 0.80, 1.78) and those divorced/widowed 2.15 (95% CI: 0.93, 4.97) did not have a statistically-significant difference than those who were married. Finally, retired participants had nearly two-fold higher odds (OR: 1.94; 95% CI: 1.05, 3,96) of suboptimal SRH compared to those still working. There was no significant difference in the odds of sub-optimal SRH between unemployed individuals (OR: 1.16; 95% CI: 0.84, 1.60) and employed individuals.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe SRH and its association with demographic factors and physical activity among the people of Qazvin Province. SRH is a valuable indicator for predicting health outcomes in the general population and various subgroups. Overall, the results of this study showed that 63.2% female and 74.5% male reported their SRH as optimal. SRH was also associated with physical activity, sex, older age, and employment status.

The prevalence of optimal SRH in the current study was 66.7%, consistent with reports showing 60% to 80% depending on demographics characteristics and study settings. For example, Baziyar et al.‘s population-based study from Ilam province of Iran reported 59.38% optimal SRH [22], and the same studies in Khorramabad [23] and Shiraz [24] reported 81% and 78%, respectively. In addition, a survey of Turkey and 11 European countries showed the optimal SRH levels of 70% to 75% [25].

Based on age categorization, our results showed that SRH decreases with increasing age. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Asia and Europe, which have shown a strong association between decreasing SRH and increasing age. In line with our findings, most studies have reported better SRH among adolescents and younger individuals compared to older age groups [26, 27]. Individuals’ SRH reflect their psychosocial conditions, lifestyle, performance, and chronic disease especially among elderly [28, 29]. A study in USA and China has shown that elderly people with reduced mobility, living with chronic disease, or mental health issues report lower SRH [30]. Other studies from India have shown physical activity and performance are strong predictor of SRH among elderly [31, 32].

Given that the elderly population in Iran is rapidly increasing and that recent years have witnessed significant changes in family lifestyles, as well as a weakening of the supportive structures for elderly individuals [33], it is essential to pay greater attention to the needs and challenges faced by this demographic, as these factors can significantly impact their SRH.

Another important factor to consider in analyzing SRH in cross-sectional studies is the cohort effect. This is fluctuation in self-rated health that is caused not only by age, but also by the social, economic, and cultural conditions of the birth cohort. Declines with age in SRH therefore may be capturing partly differences between generations in experience and resource availability, instead of the aging process itself [34, 35].

Male participants in this study scored their self-rated health higher compared to females, consistent with previous studies [15, 22, 26, 36]. This may reflect Iran’s cultural context, where traditional female roles involve greater family caregiving and restrict women’s physical activity and independent healthcare access. Naqavi’s study showed a positive link between women’s physical activity and social health, yet many women had low activity due to traditional beliefs and limited support [37]. Social expectations likely contribute to women’s poorer reports of social and mental health. Vafaei et al. noted that Iranian women face structural barriers including family roles and limited service access [38]. Psychosocial stressors tied to social norms also likely lower women’s health ratings.

In this study, although the proportion of individuals with sub-optimal SRH was higher among those who were divorced or widowed compared to other groups, similar to another study among female in reproductive age SRH did not show a statistically significant relationship with marital status [39]. The relationship between marital status and SRH varies across different cultures. In some studies, being in a relationship has positive impact on SRH [15, 40, 41]. On the other hand, a study among female those who were married had poorer SRH [42]. Also, results from the study by D’Souza et al. showed that female in India prioritized their duties as a partner or mother over their own health [43].

In this study, a statistically significant association was found between SRH and individuals’ employment status, with the proportion of individuals with sub-optimal SRH being higher among retirees than employed and unemployed individuals. Cheraghi et al. also showed a significant association between employment and SRH among the elderly [15]. Having a job is associated with increased social interactions, independence, and a sense of empowerment, which can affect individuals’ health. However, contrary to our results, Bakouei et al.‘s study did not find an association between employment status and SRH [39].

Retirement is a major life transition that involves work cessation, role change, reduction in earnings, and greater amounts of leisure time [44]. It can influence habits, mental health, and pastimes, with impacts on SRH [45]. Health effects are variably evidenced: improvements in some [46, 47], reductions [48, 49] or no change in others [50, 51], attributing to variation in methodology, populations, and social context. On the other hands, psychosocial determinants have strong impacts on retirement in Iran. Alavi et al. identified social isolation and loss of work identity as affecting health perceptions [52]. Rouhi et al. highlighted loneliness and negative age perceptions, using social support as the reason for its significance in quality of life [53]. The findings suggest retirement’s health impact in Iran is complex and culturally built.

Our results indicate that SRH is associated with physical activity. In this study, 41% of individuals who reported no physical activity indicated sub-optimal SRH, which was significantly higher than the proportion observed among those who engaged in physical activity. These results are consistent with Sharma’s study, which showed that physical activity and an active lifestyle are associated with a tendency to report positive health [54]. Some other studies have also reported results consistent with our study [55, 56]. In our study, approximately 74% of female and male engaged in less than five hours of physical activity per week, associated with poor SRH. This finding is consistent with the study by Erginoz [57]. In another study conducted among elderly Swedes, physically inactive individuals were more likely to report poorer SRH [36].

It should also be noted that individuals’ poor health may prevent them from engaging in sufficient and regular physical activity, which cannot be thoroughly examined due to the cross-sectional nature of these studies. Nevertheless, our results indicate that physical activity plays a significant role in individuals’ SRH.

The study is not free of limitations. First, the cross-sectional design did not allow the identification of causal effects. Second, the self-reported participants’ responses may suffer from recall bias, and low accuracy particularly among elderly participants.

Third, the role of certain confounding variables, such as physical, social and cultural environmental conditions and socioeconomic status, was not examined in this study. However, the strengths of the study include relatively large sample size, the use of multivariate regression modeling to control for potential confounding effects, and the implementation of a multistage sampling approach utilizing the capabilities of the electronic health records of households in primary care centers.

Conclusion

Although two-thirds of participants rated their health as optimal, sub-optimal SRH was most common among older, female, and retired adults—groups showing the greatest vulnerability. Since sub-optimal SRH correlated with no/low physical activity, targeted interventions are needed. For Iran’s primary health care, strategies should include accessible facilities for women and the elderly, disability inclusion, and routine screening and counselling on physical activity. Community centers, caregiver education, and the involvement of health workers and rehabilitation councils can promote sustained activity. Continuous monitoring of program quality is also essential.
