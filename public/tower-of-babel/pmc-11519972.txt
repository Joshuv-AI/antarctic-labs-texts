Light inducible gene expression system for Streptomyces
Authors: Ryuta Noya, Kyohei Murakoshi, Madoka Fukuda, Tetsuya Yushina, Kaichi Kitamura, Manami Kobayashi, Hideaki Takano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe LitR/CarH family comprises adenosyl B12-based photosensory transcriptional regulators that control light-inducible carotenoid production in nonphototrophic bacteria. In this study, we established a blue–green light-inducible hyperexpression system using LitR and its partner ECF-type sigma factor LitS in streptomycin-producing Streptomyces griseus NBRC 13350. The constructed multiple-copy number plasmid, pLit19, carried five genetic elements: pIJ101rep, the thiostrepton resistance gene, litR, litS, and σLitS-recognized light-inducible crtE promoter. Streptomyces griseus transformants harboring pLit19 exhibited a light-dependent hyper-production of intracellular reporter enzymes including catechol-2,3-dioxygenase and β-glucuronidase, extracellular secreted enzymes including laccase and transglutaminase, and secondary metabolites including melanin, flaviolin, and indigoidine. Cephamycin-producing Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304, carrying an entire actinorhodin gene cluster, exhibited light-dependent actinorhodin production after the introduction of the pLit19 shuttle-type plasmid with the pathway-specific activator actII-ORF4. Insertion of sti fragment derived from Streptomyces phaeochromogenes pJV1 plasmid into pLit19 increased its light sensitivity, allowing gene expression under weak light irradiation. The two constructed Escherichia coli–Streptomyces shuttle-type pLit19 plasmids were found to have abilities similar to those of pLit19. We successfully established an optogenetically controlled hyperproduction system for S. griseus NBRC 13350 and Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304.Keywords: Streptomyces, LitR, Light-inducible, Recombinant protein, OptogeneticsSubject terms: Microbiology, Applied microbiology

Introduction

Streptomyces genus consists of gram-positive soil-dwelling bacteria that exhibit complex morphological differentiation similar to that of fungi1. Streptomyces spp. produce various bioactive substances of commercial and research value including antibiotics, anticancer agents, and immunosuppressive agents2. Genomic information has revealed the diversity of secondary metabolite biosynthesis gene clusters, suggesting the presence of unknown biosynthetic pathways and novel enzymes that catalyze these reactions.

Streptomyces are also excellent natural producers for industrial enzymes, such as xylanase3, endoglucanase4, transglutaminase5, phospholipase D6, and chitinase7. Furthermore, Streptomyces are excellent hosts for producing functional recombinant proteins using molecular biology tools7–9. There are many advantages as a production host; Streptomyces is non-pathogenic and does not produce inclusion bodies and toxic impurities such as lipopolysaccharides7–9. It can be rapidly and cost-effectively grown in inexpensive media, and extensive fermentation expertise has been accumulated from industrial antibiotic production. Thus, Streptomyces is a robust and scalable industrial strain.

Constitutive and chemically inducible gene expression systems in Streptomyces have been used for molecular genetic analysis and heterologous protein production. Two types of promoters are used: (i) constitutive promoters stably expressed during each growth phase and (ii) chemically inducible promoters regulated by chemical sensor-type transcriptional regulators. The constitutive type includes strong promoters such as ermE*p10, SF14p11, and KasOp*12. The chemically inducible promoters are controlled by antibiotic thiostrepton13, tetracycline14, and ε-caprolactam-inducible15 regulatory systems that are widely used. Recently, oxytetracycline-16, cellobiose-17, cumate-18, theophylline-19, and xylose-20inducible systems have been developed.

Optogenetic techniques control the function of cells artificially engineered with photosensor genes using externally irradiated light stimuli. It was initially developed as a temporally precise non-invasive control technique for the activity of mammalian neurons with algal opsins21,22. It is now widely used in animal and plant cells, eukaryotic microorganisms, and bacteria with various photosensors23–30. Bacterial gene expression switching using light stimuli has been reported27–30; however, tools for the large-scale production of recombinant proteins have not been established, except for pDawn, a plasmid constructed for light-induced protein production in Escherichia coli that was built on the blue light-specific sensor LOV and can increase gene expression by approximately 460-fold31.

The LitR/CarH family serves as a MerR-type transcriptional regulator with photosensory function32. This family of proteins is encoded by various bacteria, including gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, and its functions have been widely studied in Streptomyces33, Bacillus34, Myxococcus35, and Thermus36. The main role of LitR/CarH is the light-dependent regulation of carotenoid biosynthesis genes, whose products serve as antioxidants against harmful agents, such as singlet and triplet molecular species generated in illuminated environments37,38. LitR/CarH requires adenosyl B12 (adoB12) as a chromophore to absorb blue and green light39. The LitR–adoB12 complex specifically binds to its target promoter and represses transcription in the dark. Under light conditions, the conversion of adoB12 to hydroxyl-B12 inactivates the repressor function of LitR, thereby allowing gene expression from the target promoter39. Recently, a B12-independent LitR family and other types of LitRs have been reported40–42, and diverse photosensing mechanisms in non-photosynthetic bacteria have been suggested.

We previously revealed that Streptomyces coelicolor A3(2) produces carotenoids in response to illumination and identified LitR and LitS as key transcriptional regulators of light-inducible expression33,43. Optogenetic tools have been developed for various bacteria; however, such tools are yet to be established for Streptomyces spp. This technology can aid in precise and complex gene regulation in Streptomyces, leading to improved yields and production of valuable compounds, even if the compound is toxic to the host. In this study, we developed a light-inducible, high-protein production system based on the litR–litS regulatory switch using Streptomyces spp. as a host.

Results

Development of a light-inducible expression system

To develop a light-inducible expression (LiEX) system in Streptomyces griseus NBRC13350 as a host, we used a photoswitch module consisting of a photosensor LitR, an RNA polymerase σ factor LitS (σLitS), and a crtE promoter (crtEp) recognized by σLitS (Fig. 1A). Under dark conditions, LitR is probably complexed with adenosyl B12 (adoB12), which represses two bidirectional promoters, litR and litS. In contrast, upon illumination with blue-green light, the converted LitR-hydroxyl-B12 activates the transcription from the litS promoter, and then RNA polymerase containing σLitS directs the transcription from crtEp. The gene of interest cloned into a multiple cloning site (MCS) was highly expressed under light conditions.

Light-inducible transcription via LitR and LitS (A) and its application to a hyper-production system (B). (A) Schematic model of the litR–litS regulatory system. Under dark conditions, LitR protein in complex with adenosyl B12 (B12) represses the bidirectional promoters of litR and litS. Upon sensing the blue-green light, LitR-B12 activates the transcription of the litS promoter, and RNA polymerase containing σLitS directs the transcription from the crtE promoter (crtEp). A gene of interest cloned into the multiple cloning site (MCS) is specifically and highly expressed in response to light stimuli. (B) Schematic representation of pLit19, a light-inducible hyper-expression vector. pLit19 is a self-replicative plasmid in Streptomyces carrying the pIJ101 replication region (pIJ101ori) and pIJ101rep gene, thiostrepton resistance gene (tsr), litR, litS, crtEp, and unique cloning sites as shown in its restriction enzyme name.

We constructed a self-replicating light-inducible expression plasmid pLit19 (plasmid: Light-inducible transcription no. 19) (Fig. 1B). The backbone of pLit19 is based on the multiple-copy number plasmid pIJ702 (estimated copy number of 50–300 copies per chromosome44), which is generally used in genetic studies of Streptomyces spp. and industrial applications. pLit19 comprises four modules: (i) a photoswitch module that converts light signals to transcriptional signals (litR and litS), (ii) a replication module containing the replication region (ori and rep genes of pIJ702), (iii) a drug resistance marker module as a selectable marker containing the thiostrepton resistance gene45 (tsr), and (iv) a cloning module containing MCSs.

Light-inducible hyper-production of intracellular recombinant proteins

To evaluate the function of pLit19, S. griseus NBRC13350, a streptomycin producer46, was used as the expression host. The reason for its use is (i) the high yield of the S. griseus cells can be stably obtained compared to other model strains, such as S. coelicolor and S. lividans based on our experience, (ii) genetic manipulation methods for S. griseus are well-established47, and the regulatory mechanism underlying the A-factor (2-isocapryloyl-3R-hydroxymethyl-γ-butyrolactone), a switch for secondary metabolite production and morphological development, has been revealed by precise genetic and biochemical studies48–50. To evaluate the transcript levels of crtEp in pLit19, catechol 2,3-dioxygenase (XylE) from Pseudomonas putida was used as the reporter enzyme51. We previously reported that the prototype light-inducible expression vector pQRS carrying the melanin biosynthesis genes successfully led to brown melanin pigment production in response to light stimuli43. The components of pQRS are similar to those of pLit19; however, this vector does not carry crtEp, and the expression of a gene cloned into the MCS is directed only by read-through transcription from the litS gene. S. griseus transformant containing pQRS–XylE and pLit19–XylE were cultivated under dark and blue light (λmax = 450 nm, Photosynthetic Photon Flex Density (PPFD): 3 µmol s–1 m–2) conditions. XylE activity in transformants carrying pQRS–XylE was low under both dark (non-induced) and blue light (induced) conditions (Fig. 2A). In contrast, transformants carrying pLit19–XylE exhibited remarkably high XylE activity in response to blue light. Consistent with this result, the recombinant protein corresponding to XylE was the most highly expressed among the cytoplasmic proteins, as determined by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (CBB) staining (Fig. 2B). We also examined a culture medium that enabled the LiEX system to generate high expression levels (Fig. 2C). XylE production was confirmed in all the transformants cultured in five types of culture media: YMP-glucose, tryptic soy broth (TSB), ISP2, Bennett’s glucose, and Bennett’s maltose. Therefore, we successfully established a light-inducible and highly expressed system in S. griseus.

LiEX system is induced by blue-green light

LitR family proteins sense light between blue and green wavelengths (450–530 nm) because adoB12 absorbs this wavelengths32. Although the biochemical function of LitR in S. coelicolor remains unknown33, we previously found that disruption of the B12 synthesis gene in this bacterium prevents light-induced transcription by crtEp52. Therefore, LitR expressed in pLit19 may use adoB12 as the chromophore. Here, we tested whether blue-green light irradiation actually activates the LiEX system by culturing the S. griseus carrying pLit19–XylE under blue (λmax = 450 nm), green (λmax = 530 nm), and red (λmax = 660 nm) light conditions. XylE activity was strongly induced when the transformant was irradiated with blue (3 µmol s–1 m–2) or green (1 µmol s–1 m–2) light. When the transformants were cultured under green light at 3 µmol s–1 m–2, the gene expression was inhibited. Thus, weak green light (1 µmol s–1 m–2) was irradiated. In contrast, no induction was observed under red-light irradiation (Fig. 3A). This result suggests that blue and green lights induce the LiEX system.

To determine the optimal light intensity, the transformants were cultivated under different intensity conditions (2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 μmol s–1 m–2), and the effect was evaluated based on XylE activity. Among the three conditions, the highest activity was observed when transformants were incubated at the lowest light intensity (2.5 μmol s–1 m–2; Fig. 3B). On the other hand, irradiation with intense light (5.0 or 10.0 μmol s–1 m–2) interfered with the LiEX system. This indicates that the LiEX system is induced by weak light, which may not cause oxidative stress to the cells because the light intensity of direct sunlight on sunny and cloudy days is 1500–2000 and 50–100 μmol s–1 m–2, respectively.

High production of extracellular secreted enzymes

Streptomyces spp. are excellent producers of various industrial enzymes, such as xylanase3, endoglucanase4, transglutaminase5, and phospholipase D6. Proteases known as Actinase E and chitinase7 are commercially produced by S. griseus. We then analyzed whether pLit19 has a high ability to produce secreted enzymes, including small laccases (SLAC and EpoA), transglutaminase (TGase), xylose isomerase (XylA), endo-1,4-beta-xylanase C (XlnC), alkaline phosphatase D (PhoD1), and alpha-l-arabinofuranosidase (AbfA) (Supplementary Table S2). In gram-positive bacteria, the two main secretory pathways for proteins are the general secretion (Sec) and twin-arginine translocation (Tat) pathways53. The predicted secretory pathways of the analyzed enzymes are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Transformants carrying pLit19 with the gene-encoding enzymes were cultured under dark and blue light conditions. The cell-free culture supernatants were 50-fold concentrated by ethanol precipitation and the samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and CBB staining. Under dark conditions, the protein band pattern was almost identical to that of the empty vector pLit19, which was used as a control (Fig. 4). In contrast, under light conditions, large protein bands for SLAC, EpoA, TGase, XylA, PhoD1, and AbfA were observed at positions consistent with the estimated molecular weight of each enzyme.

We also confirmed the effect of the light wavelength on the LiEX system carrying pLit19–SLAC. The overproduction of SLAC was observed under blue (3 μmol s–1 m–2) and green (3 μmol s–1 m–2) light conditions, whereas red-light irradiation had no such effect (Fig. 5A). This result is consistent with the XylE expression analysis (Fig. 3A), except for the difference in green light intensity. Green light irradiation of 3 μmol s–1 m–2 induced massive production of SLAC while inhibiting XylE expression as described above. This difference may be due to differences in their localization. XylE, expressed in large amounts intracellularly, is expected to have inhibited the LiEX system, although the mechanism is unknown. In contrast, SLAC did not inhibit this system, probably due to its secreted protein. We also examined five different culture media for SLAC production and observed high SLAC production with YMP-glucose, TSB, and ISP2 media, but not with Bennett’s glucose or Bennett’s maltose media (Fig. 5A).

As the amount of enzyme secreted into the culture supernatant was extremely high as described above, the following enzyme production was monitored every 24 h for 7 days using 10 μL of culture supernatant; SLAC was predicted to have Tat-dependent signal peptide, and TGases of S. netropsis NBRC 12880 and S. mobaraensis NBRC 13819 were predicted to have Sec-dependent signal peptide. Unconcentrated cell-free supernatant (10 µL) was subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis, and extracellular proteins were detected using CBB staining. SLAC requires copper as a cofactor, but the effect of exogenous copper addition to the culture media on production has not been fully studied. Thus, we analyzed the production under copper-free and 10 μM copper-supplemented conditions. As shown in Fig. 5B, a large protein band equivalent to the molecular weight of SLAC (33.8 kDa) was observed under copper-free conditions. SLAC production was detected as early as 48 h after the start of cultivation and increased as the cultivation time increased. In the copper-supplemented medium, high production of SLAC was observed after 48 h, and this high production was constant throughout the seven-day cultivation period. This result indicated that the exogenous addition of copper increased SALC production level.

We also successfully produced TGases from S. netropsis NBRC 12880 and S. mobaraenesis NBRC 13819 (Fig. 5C). High production was observed as early as 24 h after cultivation. The highest production was achieved when TSB was used as the culture medium. The reason for lower production in mid-culture is unknown. These results indicate that pLit19 is suitable for the hyperproduction of intracellular proteins and secreted enzymes.

Light-dependent secondary metabolite pigment production

To evaluate whether the LiEX system is suitable for secondary metabolite production, we analyzed enzymes catalyzing pigment biosynthesis by one or two reactions, including MelC1–MelC254 of pIJ702 (originally found in S. antibioticus IMRU 3720) for melanin with a light brown color, a type III polyketide synthase RppA55 of S. griseus NBRC 13350 for flaviolin with a brownish brown color, and a nonribosomal peptide synthetase IndC56 of S. albus J1074 for indigoidine with indigo blue color. Transformants carrying pLit19 and the above genes showed high light-dependent production of the corresponding pigments (Fig. 6A). We also examined the expression of red fluorescent protein57 (mCherry) using pLit19. The transformants exhibited red fluorescence even without LED illumination. This indicated that pLit19 is suitable for secondary metabolite production and fluorescent protein expression.

Light-dependent actinorhodin (act) production

The Streptomyces antibiotic regulatory protein (SARP) family of transcriptional regulators acts as a pathway-specific regulatory protein that induces the expression of secondary metabolite biosynthesis gene clusters in Streptomyces and its related genera58,59. The ActII-ORF4 protein is a master switch for the biosynthetic gene cluster of act60, a blue-pigmented benzoisochromanoquinone polyketide antibiotic produced by S. coelicolor A3(2). To assess whether pLit19 allows the light-induced expression of secondary metabolite gene clusters, a SuperCos1 cosmid vector containing the entire act biosynthesis gene cluster (23 kbp) was integrated into the genome of cephamycin-producing Streptomyces sp. strain NBRC 1330461 using the function of phiC31 integrase. This bacterial strain was used instead of S. griseus owing to its high cosmid transformation efficiency. Subsequently, pUWLit19 (empty vector as a control) or pUWLit19-actII-ORF4 (described below, with pUWLit19 as E. coli–Streptomyces shuttle vector) was introduced into Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304/act clusters via conjugative transfer. The transformants were cultivated under dark and light conditions on a YMP-glucose solid medium. The Streptomyces sp. The NBRC 13304/act cluster/pUWLit19 (empty as a control) transformant did not produce acts under either dark or blue light conditions and formed light-brown colonies, similar to that of the parental strain (Fig. 6B). In contrast, Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304/act cluster/pUWLit19-actII-ORF4 transformant produced act under blue light conditions, whereas low-level production of act was observed under dark conditions. This indicates that the production of secondary metabolites synthesized from large biosynthetic gene clusters can be controlled in a light-dependent manner by expressing the SARP-type regulator from pUWLit19.

A-factor-deficient S. griseus HH1 strain serves as a host for the LiEX system

A-factor, a microbial hormone-like substance synthesized by S. griseus NBRC 13350, serves as a master switch for aerial mycelial formation, secondary metabolite production, and secretory protease production50. The S. griseus HH1 strain lacks the chromosomal terminal region containing the A-factor biosynthesis gene afsA47,62. The low protease activity of the HH1 strain suggests that it may be an excellent expression host for secreted recombinant proteins. We examined whether the LiEX system functions in HH1 strain using a reporter enzyme β-glucuronidase (GUS). The HH1 strain carrying pLit19–GUS showed a high ratio of light induction and hyperproduction of GUS (Fig. 7), which was similar to that of the wild-type strain carrying pLit19–GUS. Thus, the A-factor is not involved in LitR-controlled light induction, and the HH1 strain is also a suitable host.

Optimization of the ribosome-binding site (RBS)

Gene expression is primarily affected by the nucleotide sequence of RBS (also known as the Shine–Dalgarno sequence). We examined three RBSs (RBSnitA, RBSw52, and RBSsav2794) reported to be suitable for high gene expression in Streptomyces15,63,64 (Fig. 8A). Transformants of pLit19 carrying the above RBSs in the preceding xylE gene were grown under light and dark conditions and evaluated based on the activity and production level of XylE (Fig. 8B). RBSnitA, originally used in the PnitA-nitR high-expression system in Streptomyces spp.15, was the most suitable for high production. Therefore, RBSnitA was used in the LiEX system.

Optimization of crtEp

The nucleotide sequence of crtEp was optimized to increase recombinant protein expression in the LiEX system. Repressor-type proteins may bind to the crtEp region at operator sites to inhibit transcription initiation. We hypothesized that excluding such operator sequences would increase the promoter activity. First, we constructed an evaluation system for crtEp activity using mCherry as a visible marker reflecting the transcriptional level of crtEp (Fig. 9A). To eliminate the possibility of read-through transcription generated by the litS promoter, the transcriptional terminator ttsbiB, which has been reported to functions in S. lividans63 and Mycobacterium spp.65, was inserted between litS and crtEp (Supplementary Fig. S1). Transformants carrying pLit19–mCherry-A (control) or pLit19–mCherry-B exhibited light-dependent mCherry expression (Fig. 9A). In contrast, the transformant carrying pLit19–mCherry-C did not express mCherry. This indicated that ttsbiB terminates read-through transcription from the litS promoter. Therefore, we used pLit19–mCherry-B to optimize crtEp.

Consensus sequence of light-inducible crtE promoter. (A) Evaluation system of light-inducible crtEp. Expression of mCherry in pLit19–mCherry-A (positive control) is directed via transcription of crtEp and litSp. The expression in pLit19–mCherry-B (screening vector) is directed only by crtEp as ttsbiB is located between litS and crtEp. mCherry in pLit19–mCherry-C is not expressed as (i) pLit19–mCherry-C does not have crtEp, and (ii) ttsbiB is present downstream of litS. Transformants of S. griseus carrying the above plasmids were cultured on YMP-glucose solid medium for two days in the dark and under white light (WL, 1.3 μmol s–1 m–2), and mCherry expression was observed as a red color. (B) Consensus sequences of three-type crtEp, including the wild-type (upper), light-inducible type (middle), and non-expressing type (bottom). Logos obtained by MEME analysis were shown. Original nucleotide sequences are shown in Supplementary Figs. S2, S4, and S5.

We analyzed the –10 and –35 sequences of crtEp by comparing the nucleotide sequences based on the 18 genome sequences of Streptomyces spp. (Supplementary Fig. S2). The consensus sequences of residues 35 and 10 were predicted to be GCATCCR-N15-CVGAAD (R:G or A, V:C, A, or G, and D:G, A, or T) using MEME analysis66, indicating that the σLitS-recognized 35 and 10 regions were highly conserved (Fig. 9B). Thus, we mainly examined the following three regions: (i) 15 bp spacer region between −10 and −35, (ii) 5 bp downstream region of −10, and (iii) 10 bp upstream region of −35.

We prepared a DNA fragment pool with the random upstream, spacer, and downstream sequences of crtEp via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the oligonucleotide, 5’-N10-GCATCCR-N15-CGGAAGSA-N5-3,’ and the PCR amplicon was inserted into pLit19–mCherry-B (Supplementary Fig. S1; see Sect. “Methods”). Promoter activity was evaluated based on the degree of redness of the transformants owing to mCherry expression (Supplementary Fig. S3). This experiment was performed under white conditions instead of blue LED to observe many solid cultures simultaneously because the square LED light source is small in size. White light, containing blue, green, and red light, can also activate LiEX system to a similar level as blue light. We analyzed 105 transformants, including 32.4% and 22.2% of the light-induced and non-expressing types, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). The 35 strains (red letters in Supplementary Fig. S3) showed that light-inducible mCherry expression was as high as that in wild-type crtEp. mCherry expression in the 24 strains (blue letters in Supplementary Fig. S3) were not observed under dark or light conditions. The 49 strains (white letters in Supplementary Fig. S3) showed weak mCherry expression under light conditions compared to wild-type crtEp. Alignment analyses of light-induced (Supplementary Fig. S4) and non-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. S5) types were independently performed using MEME analysis, and the consensus sequences are shown in Fig. 9B. No consensus sequences without -10 and -35 regions could be found for each type. However, this information can aid in improving LiEX systems.

Insertion of the pJV1 sti fragment increases light sensitivity

Rolling circle replication (RCR)-type plasmids require two-type replication origin, double-strand origin (dso) and single-strand origin (sso, also called sti in Streptomyces). Dso is essential for RCR-type plasmids, whereas sso is required to increase copy number and enhance stable maintenance67–69. In Streptomyces, insertion of sti increases the copy number of pIJ101 derivatives such as pIJ702 and pUWL67,68, and pSG5 derivatives such as pGM series57.

To improve the copy number of pLit19, sti fragments obtained from three Streptomyces multi-copy number plasmids, pIJ10144, pSG557, and pJV170, were inserted into pLit20, a pLit19 derivative with modified cloning sites. sti was inserted into pLit20 in two directions: natural indicates the same direction to the rep gene in the sti-carrying natural plasmid, and opposite indicates vice versa. The constructed pLit20 derivatives carrying sti fragments were purified from each transformant, and the approximate DNA yields were estimated via agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 10A). pLit20-stipJV1ΔBamHI (designated pLit20-stipJV1Δ, natural direction) was expected to have the highest copy number among the analyzed sti-carrying plasmids. We analyzed the plasmid yield and wet cell weight of pLit20 and pLit20-stipJV1Δ-carrying transformants to examine their reproducibility. We obtained a high yield of pLit20-stipJV1Δ without affecting cell growth, suggesting that the copy number of pLit20-stipJV1ΔBamHI was consistently high (Table 1).

Discussion

In Streptomyces, chemically inducible gene expression systems are widely used for molecular genetics studies and heterologous protein production. Here, we successfully developed a blue-green light-inducible expression system that can externally switch gene expression without opening the culture vessel or changing the composition of the culture medium. This system is also characterized by the repeated use of LED light sources without any environmentally hazardous compounds, such as antibiotics and non-natural artificial compounds. Here, the LiEX system based on pLit19 enabled S. griseus to produce large amounts of intracellular and extracellular enzymes in response to blue and green light. Therefore, the LiEX system is a powerful tool for basic and applied research. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of an optogenetic tool in Streptomyces and the only successful example of light-induced hyperproduction of recombinant proteins in gram-positive bacteria.

The LiEX system is characterized by its ability to be induced by green and blue light (Fig. 3A). In nonphototrophic bacteria, photosensory proteins sense UV, blue, red, and far-red light via their chromophore-binding domains27–30. In contrast, green light-specific photosensors have been reported in photosynthetic bacteria71 but not in non-photosynthetic bacteria, except for the LitR/CarH family. Therefore, green light photoreceptors are referred to as blind spots, and green light is a wavelength band that is not sensed by general blue light-specific photosensors, such as LOV72 and BLUF73. Blue light generates singlet oxygen, a toxic reactive oxygen species, when a photosensitizer such as heme absorbs blue light74,75. Therefore, irradiation with high-intensity blue light is a source of oxidative stress. This suggests that gene expression using green light does not activate endogenous photosensors and does not turn on unwanted genes related to the oxidative stress response. Therefore, the LiEX system is suitable for the specific transcriptional activation of target genes.

The Magnet system is the most widely used optogenetic tool, developed based on the vivid gene of Neurospora crassa, which encodes a blue light-specific LOV-type sensor that uses flavin mononucleotide as a chromophore76,77. The blue light-dependent heterodimerization of enzymes or proteins fused to the Vivid domain is applied to light-responsive protein-protein interactions to activate enzymes such as split Cre recombinase77. In the Magnet system, the intensity of blue light required for the heterodimerization is approximately 15 µmol s–1 m–2 PPFD. In contrast, our LiEX system is induced by a weak light irradiation of approximately 1.0–2.5 µmol s–1 m–2 PPFD. Therefore, the LiEX system can be induced by 10-fold weaker light than the magnet system. The light intensity required for the LiEX system is less likely to activate the oxidative stress response.

Many studies have investigated light-inducible expression systems in various microorganisms, including bacteria, yeast, and fungi; however, only a few have focused on high production of recombinant proteins. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first light-induced high-yield production system that uses gram-positive bacteria as hosts. The protein production ability of the LiEX system was similar to that of the PnitA-nitR amide-inducible system reported by Herai et al.15 based on the production level of XylE. In gram-negative bacteria, the pDawn system has been developed as a light-regulated E. coli system with a high yield comparable to that of the pET system31. However, this system is not widely used because of the drawbacks of the complex circuits consisting of four genes. Further research is required to apply the LiEX system to phylogenetically diverse bacterial species.

We also succeeded in light-dependent actinorhodin production by the strong expression of a SARP-type regulator using the LiEX system (Fig. 6B). Many dormant secondary metabolite biosynthetic clusters have been reported in Streptomyces spp78. Thus, the LiEX system may be effective as an awakening technology for such gene clusters by overexpressing SARP-type regulators and may lead to the discovery of novel bioactive substances. Thiostrepton-inducible expression plasmids (e.g., pIJ602113), which have been frequently used for many years, require the sensory regulator tipA gene on the host genome. However, many Streptomyces species do not contain tipA. In contrast, the LiEX system is expected to have a wide range of applications because the light sensor is included in pLit19. In addition, this study demonstrated that cephamycin-producing Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 was excellent in terms of both the efficiency of cosmid transformation by conjugative transfer and secondary metabolite productivity and is expected to be used as a new production host.

Streptomycin-producing S. griseus NBRC13350 is a suitable host for pLit19. Notably, the LiEX system did not function in S. lividans, in which neither light induction nor high production was observed. The difference between LiEX-working and non-working strains remains unknown. We speculate that there may be differences in the regulatory mechanisms of carotenoid production between S. griseus and S. lividans. We confirmed that S. lividans exhibits light-dependent carotenoid production and genomic information suggests that S. lividans has a light-regulated system similar to that of S. coelicolor A3(2). Two sets of carotenoid biosynthesis gene clusters were found in the S. griseus genome, but no environmental or chemical signals inducing their production were detected; therefore, they were considered dormant. However, the constitutive overexpression of crtS, a stress-responsive sigB family member, induced carotenoid production in S. griseus79. This strain harbors the gene SGR_817, which encodes a putative LitR protein that exhibits 70% similarity with S. lividans. In contrast, genes similar to litS were not detected in S. griseus. Another possible explanation is the high B12 synthesis capacity of S. griseus. This bacterium has been used as an industrial strain for B12 commercial production (patent number: US2595499A) and possibly supports the function of LitR proteins that are highly expressed in the multiple-copy plasmid pLit19.

Insertion of the pJV1-derived sti fragment into LiEX system increased its light sensitivity; weak light irradiation (1.0 µmol s–1 m–2) induced the high production of XylE (Fig. 10B). This result implies that the sti insertion increases the copy number of pLit20 and subsequently increases the number of LitR proteins that serve as antenna molecules, thus increasing the light sensitivity. Many studies have reported the increase in plasmid stability and copy number via inserting own sti fragments57,67,68. To our knowledge, this study may be the first to use a combination of pIJ101rep and pJV1sti for high protein production. pJV1 is an 11-kbp multiple-copy-number conjugative plasmid originally isolated from S. phaeochromogenes DSM 388680,81. Our findings may aid in improving the stability and copy number of Streptomyces RCR plasmids.

Escherichia coli–Streptomyces shuttle-type pUWLit19 and pGMLit19 were also constructed in this study. The instability of pIJ101-based shuttle vectors in E. coli K-12 derivatives has been reported82; however, deletions in parts of pUWLit19 and pGMLit19 were not observed in the general cloning host E. coli HTS08 used in this study. We showed that these two shuttle plasmids functioned in cephamycin-producing Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304. In S. albus J1074, high GUS production was observed; however, the rate of light induction was low. These results suggest that Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 is a suitable host for LiEX systems. To determine the differences between the LiEX-working and non-working strains, genome sequencing of Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 is necessary.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, chemicals, and culture media

Streptomyces griseus NBRC 13350 (previously known as IFO 13350), Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304, S. netropsis NBRC 12880, S. mobaraensis NBRC 13819, S. violaceoruber NBRC 13385 (natural pIJ101-carrying strain), S. avermitilis MA-4680 (NBRC 14893), and S. phaeochromogenes DSM 3886 (natural pJV1-carrying strain) were obtained from the Institute of Fermentation (Osaka, Japan), NITE Biological Resource Center (Chiba, Japan), and Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen GmbH (DSMZ). S. coelicolor A3(2) M145 and S. albus J1074 were obtained from the John Innes Center. E. coli K-12 strain HST08 (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) was used for routine subcloning and plasmid propagation. HST04 (Takara Bio) carrying pUB307-aphA::Tn783 was used as a helper strain for intergeneric conjugation between E. coli and Streptomyces. pIJ702, a Streptomyces multiple-copy plasmid harboring tsr, was used as the backbone of pLit19 and its derivatives. pUWL201PW-creDEM84 (Plasmid #112242; Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA) and pGM119257 (Plasmid #115683; Addgene) were used to construct E. coli–Streptomyces shuttle plasmid vectors. pXE4,51 provided by J. Westpheling, was used as the PCR template to amplify the xylE gene. pIJ787, kindly provided by the John Innes Center, was used as a PCR template to prepare a cassette fragment containing attPphiC31, phiC31 integrase, oriT, and tetracycline resistance (tet) gene when the SuperCos1 cosmid was modified into the PhiC31 integrase-based genome integrative vector. For λ-Red-mediated recombination, E. coli SW10685 and E. coli BW25141/pKD7886 were obtained from National Cancer Institute (MD, USA) and the Coli Genetic Stock Center (Yale University, USA), respectively.

The following growth media for Streptomyces spp. were used in this study: YMP/sugar medium (per liter: 2 g yeast extract, 2 g meat extract, 4 g Bacto-peptone [Becton, Dickinson and Co., Sparks, MD], 5 g NaCl, 2 g MgSO4.7H2O, and 10 g Glucose or Maltose [Kokusan, Tokyo, Japan], [pH 7.2]), Bennett’s/sugar medium (per liter: 1 g yeast extract [Becton, Dickinson and Co.], 1 g fish extract [Kyokuto, Tokyo, Japan], 2 g NZ amine [Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan], and 10 g Glucose or Maltose [Kokusan], pH 7.2), ISP No.2 medium (ISP2) (per liter: 4 g yeast extract, 10 g Malt extract [Becton, Dickinson and Co.], 4 g Glucose, [pH 7.3]), and TSB (per liter: 30 g TSB; Nissui). Agar (1.5%; Kokusan) was added to the above mixtures to prepare the solid media. To select transformants and maintain the plasmids in Streptomyces and E. coli, antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: ampicillin (Wako Pure Chemical) and kanamycin (Wako) at 50 μg/mL, chloramphenicol at 20 μg/mL, tetracycline at 10 μg/mL, spectinomycin (Wako) at 60 μg/mL, and apramycin at 50 μg/mL for E. coli; thiostrepton (Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA) at 20 μg/mL, and kanamycin at 20 μg/mL for Streptomyces.

Standard DNA manipulations

DNA manipulation of E. coli and Streptomyces spp. was performed as described by Sambrook et al.87 and Kieser et al.88, respectively. All synthetic oligonucleotides used for vector construction and cloning experiments were purchased from Eurofins Genomics K.K. (Tokyo, Japan) and listed in Supplementary Table S4. All enzymes used for DNA manipulation were purchased from Takara Bio and New England BioLabs (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Standard PCR was performed using PrimeSTAR GXL Premix (Takara Bio), PrimeSTAR Max Premix (Takara Bio), Ex Premier DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio), and Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB). Plasmid DNA was isolated using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and PCR amplicon DNA was purified using 0.8% agarose gels with the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen). All the experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA sequencing was performed by Eurofins Genomics.

Plasmid purification

In this study, we established a new plasmid purification method for Streptomyces that combines alkaline lysis and spin-column purification. Streptomyces carrying natural plasmids or transformants harboring pLit19 or its derivatives were shaken cultured for two days at 28 °C in a test tube containing 10 mL YMP-glucose medium for S. griseus or TSB medium for other Streptomyces spp. Solution I (2 mL; 50 mM glucose, 10 mM EDTA, and 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) was added to the cells obtained by centrifugation and completely resuspended. Lysozyme at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with stirring. Then, solution II (2 mL; 0.2 N NaOH and 1% SDS) was added and mixed, and the samples were incubated in ice water for 5 min. Solution III (2 mL; 3 M potassium acetate and 2 M acetic acid) was then added and stirred quickly and vigorously, and the samples were incubated in ice water for 30 min. After centrifugation at 6255 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to a 15-mL tube, and 6 mL of 2-propanol was added, mixed, and incubated for 2 h in ice water. After centrifuging at 6255 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was completely dissolved with 250 μL of 1×TE (pH 8.0), transferred to a 1.5-mL tube containing 1250 μL PB solution (QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit), and mixed well. Then, 750 μL out of the total 1500 μL sample was transferred to the QIAprep 2.0 spin column and centrifuged at 9603 × g for 1 min. The flow-through was discarded, and the remaining 750 μL sample was added to the same spin column and centrifuged at 9603 × g for 1 min. The flow-through was discarded, 750 μL PE solution was added, and centrifuged at 9603 × g for 1 min. The flow-through was discarded and the sample was centrifuged at 16,230 × g for 1 min. Then, 135 μL of EB solution was added and centrifuged at 9603 × g for 1 min to obtain the crude DNA purified solution. To remove the chromosomes and RNA, the crude DNA solution was treated with Plasmid-Safe ATP-Dependent DNase (Air Brown) and RNase (NIPPON GENE).

Construction of pLit19 derivatives

To construct pLit19, a DNA fragment containing the litR–litS regulatory system of S. coelicolor A3(2) was amplified by PCR using the primer pair P01/P02, and a DNA fragment containing crtEp and MCS was amplified using the primer pair P03/P04. Two DNA fragments were fused via overlap-extension PCR with the primer pair P01/P04, based on annealing between identical sequences within the P2 and P3 primers. A selectively amplified litR–litS–crtEp–MCS fusion cassette (nucleotide sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S5) was inserted between the KpnI and BglII sites of pIJ702 to form pLit19.

Fourteen genes were amplified by PCR using the primer pairs listed in Supplementary Table S4 and cloned into pLit19. Nucleotide sequences of the genes and amplicons are listed in Supplementary Table S5. The genes included those encoding XylE of pXE4 with primer pair P05/P06, SLAC (SCO6712) of S. coelicolor A3(2) with primer pair P07/P08, and EpoA of Streptomyces sp. AGR T-94 with primer pair P09/P10, transglutaminase (TGase) of S. netropsis NBRC 12880, S. mobaraensis NBRC 13819, and Streptomyces sp. No. 201 with primer pairs P09/P10, P13/P14, and P15/P16, respectively; xylose isomerases (XylA:SGR_1070 and SAVERM_7182) of S. griseus NBRC 13350 and S. avermitilis MA-4680 with primer pairs P17/P18 and P19/P20, respectively; and endo-1,4-beta-xylanase C (XlnC) of Streptomyces sp. AGR T-94 with primer pair P21/P22, alkaline phosphatase D (PhoD1:SAVERM_6139) of S. avermitilis with primer pair P23/P24, alpha-L-arabinofuranosidase (AbfA:SCO2431) of S. coelicolor A3(2) with primer pair P25/P26, mCherry of pGM1202 (Addgene Plasmid #69615) with primer pair P27/P28, MelC1-MelC2 of pIJ702 with primer pair P29/P30, RppA (SGR_6620) of S. griseus NBRC 13350 with primer pair P31/P32, and IndC (XNR_5634) of S. albus J1074 with primer pair P33/P34. These amplified DNA fragments were cloned between the HindIII and BamHI sites for xylE, HindIII–XbaI sites for SLAC, and two TGases, mCherry, rppA, indC, and HindIII–EcoRI sites for melC1-melC2 of pLit19. The nucleotide sequence (5’-AAGCTTAGCAACGGGAGGTACGGACATG-3’) was inserted between the HindIII site and the start codon of the above genes (HindIII site, RBSnitA, and ATG codon are underlined).

To construct pIJ702QRS–XylE, a primer pair (P39/P40) was used for the PCR amplification of xylE, and the amplicon was inserted between the SphI and BglII sites of pIJ702-QRS with the appropriate restriction enzymes and T4 Ligase.

For the construction of RBSW52 and RBSSAV2794-retaining pLit19–XylE plasmids, DNA fragments containing RBSw52–xylE or RBSsav2794–xylE were obtained via PCR with primer pairs P41/P43 and P42/P43, respectively, and the resulting amplicons were inserted between the HindIII and XbaI sites of pLit19 using the appropriate enzymes.

Construction of a genome-integrative cosmid vector with the entire act biosynthesis gene cluster

We previously constructed the SCO2G5 SuperCos1 cosmid as our original library containing the entire act biosynthesis gene cluster of S. coelicolor A3(2). A DNA fragment containing attPphiC31, phiC31 integrase, oriT, and tet was amplified by PCR using the primer pair P44/P45 and pIJ787 as templates. The amplified PCR fragment was introduced into E. coli BW25141/pKD78 or SW106 containing the SCO2G5 cosmid via electroporation, and the bla gene region of SCO2G5 cosmid was replaced with the above gene cassette via λ-red-mediated recombination technology to generate tet-resistance SCO2G5-int cosmid vector. The resulting SCO2G5-int cosmid was introduced into Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 was integrated into the genome via conjugative transfer. Transformants were selected for kanamycin resistance and proper integration was confirmed by PCR using the appropriate primer pairs.

Construction of sti-carrying pLit19

An improved pLit19 vector, pLit19ΔBamHI, was constructed by removing frequently used restriction enzyme site of pLit19. To remove the BamHI (5’-GGATCC-3’) site located in the pIJ101rep gene, a DNA fragment containing the partial rep gene, litR, litS, crtEp, and MCS was amplified via PCR using primer pair P46/P47. The amplicon containing the 5’-GGACCC-3’ sequence replaced with the parental BamHI site was introduced between the XhoI and EcoRI sites of pLit19 and was designated as pLit19ΔBamHI. To modify the MCS and add the NsiI site between pIJ101rep and litR, a DNA fragment containing litR–litS–crtEp–MCS was amplified by PCR using the primer pair P48/P49. The amplicon was inserted between the KpnI and EcoRI sites of pLit19ΔBamHI to generate pLit20.

The pIJ101sti fragment was PCR-amplified with a primer pair (P50/51) or (P52/53) using pIJ101 purified from the natural carrier S. violaceoruber NBRC 13385 as the template. The fragment was cleaved with KpnI and NsiI and inserted between the same sites in pLit20. The former and latter amplicons were inserted in the same and opposite directions, respectively, against the rep gene in pIJ101. A similar strategy was used to construct the pSG5sti-retaining pLit20. The pSG5sti fragment of S. ghanaensis DSM 2932 (NCBI No.X80774) was PCR-amplified with primer pairs P54/P55 and P56/P57 using the sti-containing pGM1202 (Addgene Plasmid #69615) as a template and cloned into pLit20.

The pJV1sti fragment was obtained from S. phaeochromogenes DSM 3886 (NCBI No. NC_001759). A similar strategy was used to construct pJV1sti-retaining pLit20. The pJV1sti was PCR-amplified with primer pairs P58/P59 and P60/P61 using pJV1 as a template and cloned into pLit20 to generate pLit20stipJV1. We also substituted the nucleotide at the BamHI site (5′-GGATCC-3′) within pJV1sti to 5′-CGATCC-3′. The upstream and downstream regions were amplified using the primer pairs P62/P63 and P64/P65, respectively. Two fragments were assembled via overlap extension PCR with a primer pair (P58/P59) based on a 20 bp-overlapping sequence within primers P63 and P64. The fragment of pJV1sti without the BamHI site was cut with KpnI and PstI and cloned into pLit20 to generate pLit20stipJV1Δ.

To introduce xylE into pLit20 and pLit20-stipJV1Δ, xylE was PCR-amplified using the primer pair P05/P06, and the resulting amplicon was digested with HindIII and BamHI. This DNA fragment was inserted between the same sites in pLit20 and pLit20-stipJV1Δ to form pLit20–XylE and pLit20-stipJV1Δ–XylE, respectively.

Construction of mCherry reporter vectors

To construct pLit19–mCherry-C, a DNA fragment containing litR–litS and ttsbiB was PCR amplified using the primer pair (P70/P71), and the amplicon was inserted between the KpnI and HindIII sites of pLit19–mCherry.

Assessment of the crtE promoter strength based on mCherry expression

The pLit19–mCherry-B reporter vector was used to screen for nucleotide sequence-optimized crtEp from a random library. A DNA fragment containing ttsbiB and crtEp was amplified using the primer pair (P72/P73) and pLit19–mCherry-B as templates. The amplicon was digested with SphI and HindIII, and then inserted into the same site of pLit19–mCherry-B, yielding a crtEp random library. The library was introduced into S. griseus using the protoplast-PEG method, and the resulting transformants were cultured on YMPD solid agar medium under dark and light conditions. The activity of crtEp was evaluated using the red fluorescence exhibited by mCherry expression as an indicator, as colonies with high mCherry expression showed red color under visible light conditions.

Construction of pUWLit19 and pGMLit19

To construct the E. coli–Streptomyces shuttle multiple-copy number plasmid pUWLit19, a DNA fragment containing litR–litS–crtEp–MCS was amplified by PCR with a primer pair (P74/P75) and inserted between the KpnI and XbaI sites of pUWL201PW-creDE using an In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara Bio). A reporter gus gene, amplified by PCR with a primer pair (P76/P77), was inserted between the HindIII and XbaI sites of pUWLit19 using In-Fusion and designated as pUWLit19–GUS.

To construct another E. coli–Streptomyces shuttle multiple-copy number plasmid, pGMLit19, a DNA fragment containing litR–litS–crtEp–MCS was PCR using a primer pair (P78/P79) with pLit20 as a template and inserted between the SnaBI and BglII sites of pGM1192 using In-Fusion. The gus gene was PCR-amplified using a primer pair (P80/P81) inserted between the HindIII and EcoRI sites of pGMLit19 using In-Fusion and designated as pGMLit19–GUS.

Light irradiation experiments

Incubators equipped with blue (LC-LED470B; Taitech, Saitama, Japan or TH2-300X300BL; CCS Inc, Kyoto, Japan; λmax = 450 nm), green (TH2-300X300GR-SP2; CCS; λmax = 530 nm), and red (LC-LED660R; Taitech; λmax = 660 nm) LED light irradiation units were used for shaking the liquid and solid cultures. An illuminating incubator (BR-180LF; Taitech; Saitama, Japan) equipped with white fluorescent lamps (20 W; Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe many solid cultures simultaneously. Light intensity was measured as the photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) using a Model LI-250 Light Meter (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA).

Analysis of the enzymatic activities and protein expression levels of XylE and GUS

The enzymatic activities of XylE and GUS were measured as previously described51,89. Transformants grown on the agar medium were cut into 1 cm2 squares and inoculated into test tubes containing 10 mL of YMP-glucose medium for S. griseus or TSB medium for S. albus and Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 was supplemented with 20 μg/mL thiostrepton and incubated at 28 °C for 2 d with reciprocal shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, the seed culture was inoculated at 1% into 500-mL baffled Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL YMP-glucose medium for S. griseus or TSB medium for S. albus and Streptomyces sp. NBRC 13304 and incubated at 28 °C for two days on a rotary shaker (135 rpm) under dark and blue light conditions. To prepare cell-free extracts, the transformants were collected from 1 mL of culture broth by centrifugation (16,230 × g for 3 min) at room temperature, and the resulting pellets were suspended in 1 mL of pre-chilled 50 mM phosphate buffer and disrupted with a sonicator (Astrason XL2020, Misonix, NY, USA) under the following conditions: ON for 1 s and OFF for 1 s × 30 times; amplitude: 30. After cell debris was removed by centrifugation (16,230 × g for 3 min) at 4 °C, the supernatant was used to measure the activities of XylE and GUS.

For the XylE assay, the formation of 2-hydroxymuconic semialdehyde was measured in a reaction mixture (200 µL) comprising 100 µL of 2 x assay buffer (20 mM phosphate buffer [pH7.5] and 0.2 M catechol solution as a chromogenic substrate) and 2–100 µL of cell lysate. The absorbance change at 375 nm was measured every 30 s for 11 min at 28 °C using Multiskan GO or Multiskan SkyHigh (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). XylE activity was calculated as the change in absorbance at 375 nm/min per milligram (mg) of total protein. The protein concentrations in the cell lysates were measured using a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For the GUS assay, the formation of p-nitrophenol was measured in a reaction mixture (200 µL) comprising 100 µL of Z buffer (50 mM phosphate buffer [pH7.0] containing 50 mM KCl, 10 mM MgSO4・7H2O, 0.27% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.8 mg/mL p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucuronide as a chromogenic substrate) and 2–100 µL of cell lysate. Changes in absorbance at 420 nm were measured every 30 s for 11 min at 37 °C using Multiskan GO or Multiskan SkyHigh. GUS activity was calculated as the change in absorbance at 420 nm/min per mg of total protein.

To detect XylE and GUS expression in the cytoplasm, cell-free extracts were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE and visualized by CBB staining. XL-Ladder Broad (Apro Science Group, Pharma Foods International Co. Ltd., Tokushima, Japan) was used as the molecular weight marker.

Expression analysis of secreted enzymes

The transformants were grown in a shaking culture for 48 h under dark and light conditions. After centrifugation at 3700 × g for 10 min, 15 mL filtrated culture supernatant was mixed with 30 mL of 99.5% ethanol and incubated at –80 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 6600 × g for 10 min, the resultant precipitate was dissolved in 300 μL of 10 mM Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 7.0), and the heat-denatured 10 μL solution was subjected to 12.5% SDS-PAGE, and visualized by CBB staining.

To analyze the culture supernatants, the transformants were cultured at 28 °C on a rotary shaker (135 rpm) in 500-mL flasks under dark and blue light conditions. After cultivation, 1 mL of culture was withdrawn at 24 h intervals. After the cells were removed via centrifugation (16,230 × g for 3 min at 4 °C), heat-denatured 10 μL of the cell-free supernatant was subjected to 12.5% SDS-PAGE and CBB staining.

Analysis of wet cell weight

Wet cell weight was used to measure the biomass of Streptomyces. A 1.0-mL culture broth was harvested by centrifugation (16,230 × g for 5 min), and the pellets were washed with 40% glycerol solution. The washed pellets were harvested via centrifugation, and the wet cell weight was calculated by subtracting the previously measured weight of the 1.5-mL tube using a precision electronic balance.

Prediction of the secretory pathway

Prediction of signal peptides and their cleavage sites was performed using SignalP 6.0, as described previously90.
