Language, Race, Ethnicity, and Insurance-related Disparities in Blepharoptosis Surgery
Authors: Ayden Case, Jake E. Radell, Alice Dalo, Albert Y. Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Given that patients’ primary language, race, ethnicity, and insurance may affect their healthcare, this study assessed differences in blepharoptosis surgical rates, waiting times, and outcomes in patients across different demographic groups and insurance types.Methods:This retrospective cohort study followed up patients diagnosed with blepharoptosis, with primary outcome measures including blepharoptosis surgical procedure, diagnosis-to-procedure interval, procedure type, and procedure complications.Results:Among 5045 patients diagnosed with blepharoptosis, age at diagnosis differed significantly among ethnic groups, with Hispanic patients presenting younger than the overall cohort (60 versus 64 y). Once diagnosed with blepharoptosis, Hispanic patients underwent surgery at the highest rate (20.8%), followed by Asian (18.5%), White (13.0%), and Black (12.8%) patients. English speakers were least likely to undergo surgery after a blepharoptosis diagnosis (14.3%), whereas Vietnamese (22.8%), Mandarin (21.4%), and Spanish (21.3%) speakers underwent surgery more frequently. Patients needing an interpreter were also more likely to undergo surgery (21.5% versus 14.3%).Conclusions:Hispanic and Asian blepharoptosis patients undergo surgery at a significantly higher rate than White and Black patients. In non-English speakers and for patients requiring an interpreter, surgery also occurs more frequently.

Takeaways

Question: Do racial, ethnic, and language-based outcome disparities exist in blepharoptosis surgery?

Findings: This study identified that Hispanic and Asian patients undergo blepharoptosis surgery at a higher rate than White and Black patients. Furthermore, non-English speakers and patients requiring an interpreter undergo surgery more often.

Meaning: The disparities identified can inform oculoplastic surgery practice by highlighting specific identity-based factors that may impact blepharoptosis treatment, mitigating providers’ potential biases.

INTRODUCTION

Blepharoptosis, also commonly referred to as ptosis, is clinically defined as upper eyelid drooping, which can impair vision by blocking light entering the eye. Ptosis severity may be measured by marginal reflex distance 1 (MRD1): the distance between the upper lid margin and the corneal light reflex. Ptosis surgery is indicated when the MRD1 is 2 mm or less, the eyelid droop measures 12 degrees or more, or there is a 24% superior visual field loss; when the upper eyelid position interferes with central vision; when down-gaze ptosis impairs reading; when the chin-up or backward head tilt due to visual axis obscuration; when discomfort or eye strain is caused by droopy lids; or when the patient reports functional impairment.1 Ptosis surgery is typically performed via 1 of 4 approaches: conjunctivo-tarso-Muller muscle-levator resection, frontalis sling, levator palpebrae superioris resection/advancement by either internal or external approach, and rarely the superior rectus method.

Ptosis surgery that impairs vision is often covered by medical insurance. However, when surgery is covered by insurance, socioeconomic factors may still contribute to disparities, shaping care delivery.2,3 Furthermore, even when controlling for economic factors, there are known racial and ethnic disparities that affect patients’ access to care in the United States.4 These disparities exist across medical specialties and can impact prognosis and quality of care.

Within ophthalmology, there are limited studies exploring how socioeconomic disparities impact patient outcomes and access to care. One study examining anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatment for patients with diabetic macular edema found significantly worse visual outcomes in Black patients compared with their White counterparts, who received more anti-VEGF injections per year.5 Upon initiation of anti-VEGF treatment, Black and Hispanic patients also tended to present with considerably more severe diabetic eye disease than White patients.6 Importantly, socioeconomic disparities in ptosis surgery have primarily been studied outside the United States, in populations with different demographic compositions and less ethnic diversity.7,8 For instance, 1 South Korean study found that blepharoptosis prevalence was heavily associated with lower socioeconomic status, but the study did not compare ethnic groups.8 Although the aforementioned studies are helpful for hypothesizing socioeconomic disparities in access to ophthalmic care and consequent visual outcomes, the heterogeneous demographic makeup of the United States and its substantially different health insurance system make these findings hard to generalize and underpin the urgency of further investigation.

Disparities in medical care continue to remain a particular concern in surgical specialties, as it has been reported that several minority groups experience worse surgical outcomes, with surgery’s time-sensitive nature making its delivery particularly susceptible to access issues.9–12 These disparities’ origins are multifactorial and have recently been the subject of considerable research. Racial disparities in care received are compounded by language-based disparities.13 As the US healthcare system works toward greater equity and better health outcomes for all groups, it is crucial for surgical subspecialties within ophthalmology to recognize and understand these potential disparities and their roots so that the community can address them. Here, we studied ptosis patients accessing oculoplastic care to assess whether patients of different primary languages, races, ethnicities, and insurance statuses underwent ptosis surgery at different rates, had differing wait times, or had differing surgical outcomes.

METHODS

An institutional review board approved this study, and all study components adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient data were obtained from the Stanford Research Repository Database under institutional review board protocol 61488. All analysis of patient-identifiable data was conducted in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, and no confidential patient data are presented in this study.

Patients aged older than 18 years with a ptosis diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-10 code H02.4) were included in the study. Patients were included if they had a ptosis-related encounter between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2020. Collected variables included race, ethnicity, age at first ptosis diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), sex, preferred language, interpreter requirement, Elixhauser comorbidity sum, patient ICD-10 codes, encounter current procedural terminology (CPT) codes, and encounter dates. The Elixhauser sum was calculated using collected ICD-10 codes and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Software from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project. Racial and ethnic groups were defined using self-reported race and ethnicity values. White was defined as non-Hispanic White, Black as non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic/Latino as Hispanic ethnicity or Latino race. Asian patients were defined as anyone with Asian race listed. Other, smaller racial, ethnic, insurance, and language groups included in the overall cohort were not specifically analyzed.

All data analysis was performed using R and Python. Statistical analyses were performed using analysis of variance, a χ2 test, or Kruskal–Wallis test, where appropriate. Additionally, a backward stepwise logistic regression was calculated from a starting model including race, ethnicity, age, BMI, sex, preferred language, interpreter requirement, and Elixhauser sum. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated based on the stepwise regression results.

Patient and Public Involvement

Given the retrospective cohort design of the study, no patients or members of the public were directly involved in the research process. However, the results of this study have implications for patient care, so the research team intends to disseminate their findings to the public in an approachable, clinically relevant manner.

RESULTS

Age at initial ptosis diagnosis differed significantly among ethnic groups (P < 0.001, Table 1), with Hispanic patients presenting younger (median 60 versus 64 y overall). Primary language spoken differed significantly among racial and ethnic groups (P < 0.001), with English being less common among Hispanic (62.8%) and Asian (74.5%) patients compared with White (97.9%) and Black (99.2%) patients. Need for an interpreter also differed significantly (P < 0.001), with Hispanic (34.9%) and Asian (21.8%) patients requiring interpreters more frequently compared with White (1.9%) and Black (0.8%) patients.

Cohort Demographics

Ethnic groups had significantly different insurance statuses (P < 0.001), with Medicaid managed care being more common among Hispanic patients (22.5% of Hispanic patients versus 8.2% overall), Medicare being less common among Hispanic patients (27.2% versus 40.7% overall), and Medicare being most common among White patients (48.1% versus 40.7% overall). BMI also differed significantly among ethnic groups (P < 0.001). BMI for Hispanic (28.15 kg/m2) and Black (28.14 kg/m2) patients was higher than for Asian (24.04 kg/m2) or White (25.96 kg/m2) patients.

The rate at which ptosis patients received ptosis surgery differed significantly by ethnicity (P < 0.001, Table 2). Hispanic patients underwent ptosis surgery at the highest rate (20.8%), followed by Asian (18.5%), White (13.0%), and Black (12.8%) patients. There was a nonsignificant trend toward differing intervals between ptosis diagnosis and surgery (P = 0.092). Hispanic (16 wk) patients had shorter median intervals between initial diagnosis and surgery when compared with the overall cohort (18 wk). Ptosis surgery technique differed significantly between ethnic groups (P = 0.026). Levator resection or advancement with an internal approach (CPT 67903), was more common in non-Hispanic White (61.3%) and Asian (60.4%) than in Hispanic (49.7%) and Black (43.8%) patients. Levator resection or advancement with an external approach (CPT 67904) was more common in Hispanic (38.3%) and Black (37.5%) patients than in Asian (31.5%) and White (28.4%) patients.

Surgical Frequency and Outcomes by Need for Interpreter

Surgical frequency in ptosis patients differed significantly by insurance status (P < 0.001). (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays surgical frequency and outcomes by insurance type, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E487.) Notably, the Medicaid patient sample size was the smallest of all groups (n = 78 out of a total of 5045 diagnoses, and 5 underwent surgery out of 765 total surgical procedures). Medicaid patients were least likely to undergo surgery after ptosis diagnosis (6.4%), followed by uninsured (9.5%), privately insured (13.1%), Medicare (17.3%), Medicare managed care (17.6%), and Medicaid managed care (20.1%). Ptosis surgery techniques varied significantly among insurance groups (P < 0.001). Levator resection with an internal approach was most common in uninsured patients (71.7%), followed by privately insured (67.1%), Medicare (57.1%), Medicaid managed care (48.8%), Medicare managed care (44.3%), and Medicaid (20%). The external approach was most common in Medicare managed care patients (47.1%), followed by Medicaid (40.0%), Medicaid managed care (35.4%), Medicare (32.0%), uninsured (26.1%), and privately insured (24.2%). Emergency department visits within 90 days of surgery differed significantly by insurance status (P < 0.001), with Medicaid patients most frequently presenting to the ED (40.0%), followed by Medicaid managed care (4.9%), Medicare (3.7%), Medicare managed care (2.9%), uninsured (2.2%), and privately insured (1.9%).

Backward stepwise regression confirmed that both Asian (OR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.18–1.80) and Hispanic (OR = 1.93, 95% CI = 1.52–2.44) patients were more likely than non-Hispanic White patients to undergo ptosis surgery. Furthermore, increased Elixhauser score was associated with decreased likelihood of ptosis surgery (OR = 0.85, 95% CI = 0.81–0.89), whereas increased age augmented the likelihood of surgery (OR = 1.01, 95% CI = 1.00–1.01).

DISCUSSION

Our data indicate that Hispanic-identifying and primarily non-English-speaking patients undergo ptosis surgery at a significantly higher rate than White and English-speaking patients. Additionally, Hispanic patients present with ptosis at a younger age and higher BMI than non-Hispanic White patients. Logistic regression indicates that Hispanic patients are about twice as likely to undergo ptosis surgery after receiving a ptosis diagnosis, whereas Asian patients are about 1.5 times as likely. Although these disparities’ causes cannot be determined from our analysis, there are several factors that may contribute to these findings.

The Hispanic/Latino health paradox (HP) has been minimally explored within surgical fields, let alone oculoplastics. The HP refers to the phenomenon that Hispanic patients demonstrate better-than-expected health outcomes despite elevated risk factors, as studies have documented minimal disparities between Hispanic patients and non-Hispanic White counterparts.14–16 However, a study by Eguia et al17 concluded with results contrary to published literature regarding the HP, showing that Hispanic patients were at an increased risk of worse health outcomes and mortality rates after surgery, regardless of comorbidities. Although the mortality rate was not measured in our analysis, differences in surgical rates and wait times potentially indicate a disparity in care received. The disparities observed in Hispanic populations can be attributed to several factors that can hinder these individuals from receiving proper care, such as higher poverty rates and language barriers between patients and providers.

Our data show that both Hispanic and Asian patients were more likely to undergo ptosis surgery than White patients. Hispanic eyelid anatomy has been only moderately characterized with regard to reconstructive surgery.18–20 Notably, similarities have been observed between Latino and Asian eyelids, including the presence of epicanthal folds and statistically different MRD1 differences between Latinos and White patients.20 Although anatomical differences may partially account for disparate surgical rates, this is unlikely to fully explain the observed surgical disparity, as greater ptosis prevalence in these populations should not necessarily correspond with a higher surgical rate among ptosis-diagnosed patients. Greater ptosis severity in Hispanic and Asian individuals could potentially explain higher surgical rates in these populations, but the literature review did not uncover evidence of greater ptosis severity in these patients.

Interestingly, non-English-speaking patients diagnosed with ptosis were significantly more likely to receive surgery than English speakers. Patients primarily speaking another language and requiring an interpreter may have less dialogue with their physicians regarding surgical planning and decision-making. Research has demonstrated that online plastic surgery resources do not offer adequate patient-facing Spanish-language educational materials.21 Although this has not been studied in oculoplastics specifically, it is known in other areas of medicine, including plastic surgery, that Spanish-language resources have inferior understandability, actionability, and cultural sensitivity compared with English counterparts.21–24 US websites may not offer appropriately leveled Spanish materials for patients seeking information on cosmetic surgery, despite Hispanic patients being the second-largest demographic group to undergo cosmetic surgery in 2017.21 Although it is known that there are language barriers that can impact an individual’s prognosis, we posit that patients who are unable to adequately educate themselves on their surgical options due to inadequate resources or translation may be more likely to accept the initial recommendation for surgery, whereas patients speaking their provider’s language may be more likely to further discuss surgical options.

When undergoing ptosis surgery via levator resection, Hispanic and Black patients were also more likely to undergo surgery with an external approach, whereas non-Hispanic White and Asian patients more frequently underwent levator resection via the internal approach. The external approach is typically preferred for more severe ptosis cases.25,26 This difference in surgical technique for Hispanic and Black patients may indicate that these patients are not operated on until their condition is more severe, which aligns with the finding that Hispanic patients may experience shorter wait times from diagnosis to surgery.

When analyzing patients by insurance status, our data showed that uninsured patients were less likely to have surgery following a ptosis diagnosis. Although this finding is not surprising, it emphasizes the known hardships that uninsured patients in the US face. In this case, they were far less likely to receive medically indicated surgery.

When analyzing postoperative outcomes, Medicaid-using patients were more likely to return to the ED within 90 days following ptosis surgery. However, it should be noted that this insurance-stratified group was substantially smaller than the others and, thus, may not be a reliable representation of the larger population. Although Medicaid patients may use the ED more frequently due to less consistent primary care, this is likely an insufficient explanation for these patients’ higher ED usage following surgery. Medicaid patients are known to have a harder time making follow-up appointments,27 and this may lead them to follow-up in the ED rather than with their oculoplastics provider. To prevent costly increases in ED visit frequency, it is important to increase surgery follow-up accessibility for Medicaid patients. Doing so would both improve patient care quality and decrease cost to the healthcare system. Future studies should examine how to accomplish this goal in a cost-effective and equitable manner.

Although this study provided useful insight into the racial, ethnic, language, and insurance-related disparities that shape oculoplastic care, there are several study limitations. First, this observational study presented no causative evidence to indicate the sources of disparity in who receives ptosis surgery, how long they wait for it, and what surgical method they receive. Second, variations in ptosis prevalence and severity by race and ethnicity have not been reported but may exist. More severe ptosis in Hispanic and Asian patients could explain the higher surgical rates in those groups, including Hispanic patients' potentially shorter wait time between initial diagnosis and surgery. There were also no available ptosis severity data at the time of diagnosis or surgery, as measured by MRD1. Third, it was only possible to analyze surgical cases that were coded with CPT and ICD codes for insurance billing. Thus, patients who paid entirely out-of-pocket may not have been included if their visits were not coded, potentially also leading to instances where the initial diagnosis but not the subsequent surgery were accounted for. This limitation may also have led to selection bias, as patients with greater financial resources may have paid out-of-pocket at a higher rate. Despite these limitations, this study provided useful insight into the higher surgery likelihood that Hispanic, Asian, and non-English-speaking patients experience with oculoplastic providers.

These insights are important for providers and national organizations to consider when treating patients with and drafting guidelines for blepharoptosis. Moving forward, we aim to apply standardized, objective clinical benchmarks—such as MRD1 measurements and documented superior visual field loss—as primary determinants for surgical candidacy. We plan to implement regular audits of ptosis cases to monitor consistency across demographic groups and to expand interpreter services and standardized patient education to address language-related barriers. These steps are intended to minimize the impact of nonclinical factors on surgical decision-making and to promote more equitable access to care. National oculoplastic surgery organizations can further support access to care by specifying standardized diagnosis-to-surgery interval targets and clearly outlining factors that may unintentionally impact intervention frequency, such as insurance type or race.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, Hispanic and Asian patients were more likely to receive ptosis surgery compared with non-Hispanic White patients in this insurance-based, observational study. Non-English-speaking patients and those requiring an interpreter more frequently received surgery after ptosis diagnosis compared with English-speaking patients. Future studies should analyze why Hispanic, Asian, and non-English-speaking patients are undergoing surgery more frequently or more rapidly. They should also analyze whether ptosis severity is greater in Asian and Hispanic populations to determine whether more severe ptosis contributes to higher surgery rates in these patients, or whether it may be attributable to language- or ethnicity-based disparities in healthcare delivery.
