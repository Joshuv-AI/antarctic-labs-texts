Balancing two defects: surgical decision-making in low-birth-weight neonates with esophageal atresia and tetralogy of Fallot—a case series
Authors: Ming-tang Ye, Yu-zhong Yang, Ji-rong Qi, Xu-ming Mo
Published: 2026

Background

Esophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula (EA/TEF) is a life-threatening congenital anomaly in neonates that requires prompt surgical intervention, and affected infants rarely survive without timely treatment. Previous studies have shown that the incidence of concomitant congenital heart disease (CHD) is relatively high in patients with EA/TEF [1]. Among these, complex CHD has been identified as one of the major risk factors adversely affecting prognosis in this population [2, 3]. Complex CHD may cause persistent, severe, or even fatal hemodynamic instability, thereby negatively influencing both the therapeutic outcomes and survival of infants with EA/TEF.

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is one of the most common complex congenital heart diseases requiring surgical correction in infancy [4]. When it coexists with EA/TEF, treatment planning becomes exceptionally challenging. On the one hand, infants with EA/TEF require prompt restoration of esophageal continuity or ligation of the fistula to prevent pulmonary complications such as salivary aspiration and aspiration pneumonia [5]. On the other hand, TOF is characterized by right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, which may result in hypercyanotic spells and even life-threatening hypoxemia [6]. The pathophysiological interplay between these two malformations further complicates management: unrepaired TOF may exacerbate systemic hypoxemia because of reduced pulmonary blood flow, whereas retention of airway secretions associated with EA/TEF may predispose patients to pulmonary infection, making perioperative respiratory and hemodynamic management particularly difficult.

Low birth weight has long been regarded as a major risk factor for complex neonatal surgery. Previous studies have demonstrated that low birth weight is an independent predictor of postoperative mortality and morbidity in neonates [7, 8]. Even in specific high-complexity procedures, such as repair of esophageal atresia [9] or corrective surgery for congenital heart disease [10], low-birth-weight infants have significantly poorer outcomes than those with normal birth weight. In infants with concomitant EA/TEF and TOF, low birth weight further increases the complexity of determining the optimal timing of surgery and selecting the appropriate operative strategy [11]. If cardiac surgery is performed first, cardiopulmonary bypass may aggravate pulmonary injury and impair healing of the esophageal anastomosis, while postoperative anticoagulation may increase the risk of anastomotic bleeding. Conversely, if esophageal surgery is prioritized, TOF-related hyper cyanotic spells and hemodynamic instability may become life-threatening. At present, no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal management of such patients. Reported cases of EA/TEF associated with TOF remain rare, and in a case series of nine patients reported by O’Shea et al. [12], the overall survival rate was only 56%, highlighting the substantial therapeutic difficulty and prognostic uncertainty associated with this combination of malformations.

In this report, we describe the surgical management of two low-birth-weight neonates (1.7 kg and 2.2 kg) with concomitant EA/TEF and TOF. Two different surgical pathways were adopted: in one patient, a staged approach was chosen because intraoperative exploration revealed an excessively long gap between the proximal and distal esophageal segments; in the other, primary esophageal anastomosis was successfully completed during the initial operation, followed by delayed cardiac repair. Both patients ultimately underwent successful definitive correction and achieved favorable outcomes. By reviewing their diagnostic and therapeutic courses, we aim to explore the rationale underlying surgical decision-making in this complex clinical setting and to provide a reference for the management of similar cases.

Case presentation

Case 1

A preterm male neonate with a birth weight of 1.7 kg and a gestational age of 33 weeks was admitted because of tachypnea and excessive salivation shortly after birth. After admission, an upper gastrointestinal contrast study showed retention of contrast medium in the proximal esophagus with no passage distally. Marked gastric insufflation was observed, whereas no gas was seen in the intestinal tract, suggesting esophageal atresia possibly associated with duodenal atresia (Fig. 1A). Echocardiography revealed TOF, an atrial septal defect (ASD), and a complete vascular ring. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography further confirmed the diagnosis of EA/TEF, TOF, ASD, and a complete vascular ring (Fig. 2).

On day 2 after admission, corrective surgery for esophageal atresia, diamond-shaped duodenoduodenostomy, and a Ladd procedure were initially planned. During the first operation, a right lateral chest wall incision was made via the fourth intercostal space. Intraoperative exploration revealed an approximately 3-cm gap, precluding primary anastomosis, and the tracheoesophageal fistula was ligated. Subsequently, a transverse right upper abdominal incision was made to perform an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative findings did not reveal duodenal obstruction; therefore, duodeno-duodenostomy was not performed. The Ladd procedure was completed to address possible malrotation, and a gastrostomy tube was placed for enteral access. The operative approach thus consisted of an open thoracotomy combined with a laparotomy. Total operative time was 4 h and 25 min. The infant was extubated on postoperative day 3 and transitioned to nasal cannula oxygen. Enteral feeding via gastrostomy was initiated after extubation and gradually advanced, which was well tolerated. During the two-month interval, the infant remained hospitalized. A 6 F tube was placed orally into the proximal pouch for continuous aspiration of secretions. After more than 2 months of comprehensive management, including nutritional support and anti-infective therapy, the patient’s condition gradually stabilized.

At 2.5 months of age, when the infant weighed 2.8 kg, a second-stage operation was performed, including complete repair of TOF, correction of the complete vascular ring, and closure of the ASD under cardiopulmonary bypass. Because of postoperative myocardial edema and poor cardiac function, delayed sternal closure was required, and the chest was successfully closed on postoperative day 3. Seventeen days later, a third-stage operation, namely definitive repair of the esophageal atresia, was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful. Following delayed esophageal anastomosis, oral feeding was initiated 4 months after the esophageal repair. The infant underwent a 16-day gradual transition and eventually achieved full oral feeding. Repeat upper gastrointestinal contrast study demonstrated good esophageal patency (Fig. 1B). He was eventually discharged in good condition. The patient has been followed for 16 months post-discharge, with favorable growth and development.

Case 2

A preterm male neonate with a birth weight of 2.2 kg and a gestational age of 34 weeks was admitted because of tachypnea shortly after birth. Following admission, an upper gastrointestinal contrast study showed retention of contrast medium in the proximal esophagus with no passage into the distal esophagus (Fig. 3A). Echocardiography revealed TOF and an ASD. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography further confirmed the diagnosis of EA/TEF, TOF, and ASD (Fig. 3B).

A Preoperative upper gastrointestinal contrast study showed retention of contrast medium in the proximal esophagus with no distal passage. B Preoperative chest computed tomography showed the locations of the esophagus, trachea, and tracheoesophageal fistula

On day 3 after admission, the patient underwent primary repair of the esophageal atresia and tracheoesophageal fistula. The infant was placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and a right posterolateral thoracotomy was performed via the third intercostal space. Total operative time was 2 h and 15 min. The infant was extubated on postoperative day 2 and transitioned to nasal cannula oxygen. The postoperative course was uneventful, and contrast study performed on postoperative day 7 revealed no anastomotic leakage; therefore, the infant was started on oral water intake on postoperative day 8, followed by gradual transition to formula milk feeding. Full oral feeding was achieved by postoperative day 14, and he was discharged.

Three months after discharge, he was readmitted with a 3-day history of cough and more than 1 day of cyanosis. Chest computed tomography showed patchy bilateral pulmonary opacities, as well as stenosis of the distal trachea and left main bronchus (Fig. 4A). The patient was found to have developed anastomotic esophageal stenosis, which manifested as feeding difficulties and was confirmed on contrast study. After readmission, comprehensive treatment, including anti-infective therapy, was initiated, and endoscopic esophageal dilatation was performed on hospital day 2 to relieve the stenosis. Anti-infective treatment and nutritional support were continued thereafter. On hospital day 22, he underwent complete repair of TOF and ASD, together with placement of a bioresorbable external tracheal splint. During the cardiac procedure, a median sternotomy was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass. Intraoperative exploration revealed tracheomalacia extending 1 cm above the carina, which was managed by placement of a 1.8-cm external tracheal splint with suspension. Intraoperative bronchoscopy confirmed significant improvement of the tracheomalacia. Cardiopulmonary bypass time was 90 min, with an aortic cross-clamp time of 48 min. The infant was extubated on postoperative day 3 and recovered uneventfully. Upper gastrointestinal contrast study demonstrated good esophageal patency (Fig. 4B), and he was discharged on postoperative day 18. The patient has been followed for 2 years post-discharge, and currently demonstrates favorable growth and development.

Discussion

The concomitant management of EA/TEF and TOF in low-birth-weight infants represents one of the most challenging clinical scenarios in pediatric surgery. The two cases presented here suggest that favorable outcomes can be achieved in selected low-birth-weight infants through individualized surgical strategies. Based on our experience, several issues merit particular attention.

Choice and timing of surgical strategy

Reports of EA/TEF associated with TOF remain scarce. O’Shea et al. [12] described the largest case series reported to date, comprising nine patients with a median birth weight of 1,790 g and an overall survival rate of 56% (5/9). With respect to surgical management, primary esophageal anastomosis was performed in six patients, whereas the remaining three underwent ligation of the tracheoesophageal fistula with or without gastrostomy and/or esophagostomy. To date, no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal sequence of treatment when EA/TEF and TOF coexist. Traditionally, three main strategies have been considered: (1) single-stage simultaneous correction of both anomalies; (2) initial cardiac repair followed by esophageal surgery; and (3) initial repair of the esophageal lesion followed by cardiac surgery. Each of these approaches has its own theoretical rationale and potential risks.

The two infants in the present series underwent markedly different surgical pathways, which reflects the central principle of individualized decision-making. In Case 1, intraoperative exploration revealed an approximately 3-cm gap between the proximal and distal esophageal segments, and the excessive anastomotic tension precluded primary repair. A staged approach was therefore adopted, consisting of initial ligation of the tracheoesophageal fistula and establishment of enteral access via gastrostomy, followed by complete TOF repair once the patient’s condition had stabilized and body weight had increased to 2.8 kg, and finally delayed esophageal anastomosis. In contrast, in Case 2, primary esophageal anastomosis was successfully achieved during the initial operation, followed 3 months later by complete TOF repair and placement of a bioresorbable external tracheal splint. Both patients achieved favorable outcomes, suggesting that the choice of surgical strategy should be determined on the basis of anatomical features, overall clinical status, and intraoperative findings.

Based on our experience, the management strategy for low-birth-weight neonates with concomitant EA/TEF and TOF should be individualized according to anatomical features and clinical status. For patients with favorable esophageal anatomy and stable hemodynamics, primary esophageal repair followed by elective TOF repair after recovery is a reasonable approach. For those with a long-gap esophagus or significant hemodynamic instability, a staged strategy is recommended: initial fistula ligation and gastrostomy, followed by complete TOF repair after weight gain and stabilization, and finally delayed esophageal anastomosis. In cases with complex cardiovascular anomalies such as vascular ring or tracheomalacia, comprehensive repair during cardiac surgery may be considered. Regardless of the chosen approach, aggressive nutritional support, meticulous perioperative management, and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential to achieving favorable outcomes.

Particularly noteworthy is the three-stage strategy adopted in Case 1—ligation of the tracheoesophageal fistula without primary esophageal anastomosis, followed by complete repair of TOF, and subsequently delayed esophageal anastomosis—which has rarely been reported in the literature. The rationale for this approach is threefold. First, initial treatment limited to fistula ligation and establishment of enteral access addresses pulmonary inflammation and infection related to aspiration and reflux while avoiding the risks of anastomotic leakage and esophageal stricture associated with forced anastomosis under conditions of low body weight and tissue edema. At the same time, gastrostomy provides a nutritional route that helps prepare the infant for subsequent cardiac surgery. Second, complete repair of TOF can be performed after weight gain and stabilization of cardiac function, when the infant’s tolerance to major surgery is substantially improved. Third, delayed esophageal anastomosis can be undertaken after postoperative tissue edema has resolved and tissue perfusion has improved following cardiac surgery, thereby maximizing the likelihood of anastomotic healing. This strategy may provide a reproducible decision-making framework for selected patients with similar clinical characteristics.

Recently, a case of long-gap esophageal atresia associated with TOF and a right aortic arch was reported [13]. In that case, initial repair of the esophageal atresia via a right thoracic approach failed, and the patient subsequently underwent staged repair through a left thoracic approach; however, the infant ultimately died of airway obstruction at 93 days of age. This case suggests that even with a staged strategy, the prognosis of such complex combined malformations remains guarded. The authors emphasized that individualized surgical planning and thorough preoperative vascular assessment are crucial, particularly in patients with complex vascular anomalies or rare forms of esophageal atresia. In addition, clinical case discussions included in The Hendren Project have also addressed the management of EA/TEF associated with TOF. Key considerations include prioritizing control of the tracheoesophageal fistula to stabilize respiratory status and postponing definitive TOF repair until cardiac conditions permit, underscoring the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and the principle that survival during the first postoperative week should be regarded as the primary goal.

In our series, Case 1 was managed with a three-stage strategy consisting of ligation of the tracheoesophageal fistula, complete repair of TOF, and delayed esophageal anastomosis. This approach is conceptually consistent with the staged strategies described in the literature and further supports the rationale for adopting a staged approach in patients with unfavorable anatomical conditions or poor overall clinical status. By contrast, Case 2 underwent primary esophageal anastomosis followed by delayed cardiac surgery and likewise achieved a favorable outcome. This finding suggests that, in patients with suitable anatomy, primary esophageal repair may avoid prolonged dependence on gastrostomy and thereby improve quality of life.

Whether low birth weight should be considered a contraindication to surgery

Traditionally, low birth weight has been regarded as a major risk factor for surgery in infants with complex congenital heart disease and EA/TEF [7–10]. However, Case 1 in our series, with a birth weight of only 1.7 kg, successfully tolerated three operations, suggesting that under the support of modern anesthesia, cardiopulmonary bypass, and intensive care, low birth weight alone should not be considered an absolute contraindication to surgical intervention. The key lies in several aspects. First, the urgency of each malformation must be accurately assessed. In Case 1, initial management of the tracheoesophageal fistula was mandatory because aspiration, gastroesophageal reflux, and related pulmonary inflammation required prompt intervention. Second, favorable conditions for subsequent surgery should be actively created. With enteral nutritional support through gastrostomy, the infant’s body weight increased from 1.7 kg to 2.8 kg within 2 months, thereby providing a more favorable window for second-stage cardiac repair. Third, meticulous perioperative management is essential, including postoperative respiratory and hemodynamic support.

Concomitant correction of complex cardiac anomalies

The cardiac malformations in Case 1 were considerably more complex than isolated tetralogy of Fallot, as the patient also had an atrial septal defect and a complete vascular ring. A vascular ring may compress the trachea and esophagus, thereby exacerbating respiratory distress. During the second-stage operation, the surgical team simultaneously performed definitive repair of TOF, closure of the atrial septal defect, and correction of the vascular ring, thereby addressing all major cardiovascular anomalies in a single procedure and avoiding the additional risks and financial burden associated with multiple staged cardiac operations. Although this approach requires a high level of anatomical understanding and surgical expertise, it may reduce the cumulative risk of repeated operations in appropriately selected patients.

Identification and management of postoperative complications

Both patients in our series developed postoperative complications requiring intervention, underscoring the complex and often protracted clinical course of these challenging cases.

In Case 1, poor cardiac function and marked edema after cardiac surgery necessitated delayed sternal closure. Once the patient’s condition had stabilized, the chest was successfully closed. Delayed sternal closure is a commonly used strategy in the postoperative management of complex congenital heart disease, as it may relieve cardiac compression, improve cardiac output, and provide favorable conditions for myocardial recovery.

In Case 2, the patient was readmitted 3 months after primary esophageal anastomosis because of cough and cyanosis. Imaging studies revealed pneumonia as well as stenosis of the distal trachea and left main bronchus. This complication may have been related to several factors: first, external airway compression associated with right ventricular outflow tract obstruction caused by TOF itself; and second, traction on the adjacent airway resulting from local tissue edema or scar formation after esophageal anastomosis. During complete repair of TOF, a bioresorbable external tracheal splint was placed simultaneously, which relieved the airway compression and was followed by an uneventful recovery. Clinical experience with bioresorbable external tracheal splints in pediatric airway stenosis remains limited, and this case may provide a useful reference for the management of this rare complication.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a case series, the sample size was small, and the generalizability of our conclusions requires validation in multicenter studies with larger cohorts. Second, because the two patients underwent different treatment strategies, direct comparison of the relative advantages and disadvantages of these approaches is difficult. Third, the duration of follow-up was limited, and long-term outcomes, including visceral function such as esophageal motility, cardiac function, airway development, and neurodevelopment, still require continued observation. Finally, our experience was derived from a single center, and the surgical techniques and perioperative management may be center-specific; therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to other institutions.

Conclusions

The successful management of these two low-birth-weight infants with concomitant EA/TEF and TOF suggests several important clinical implications. First, the choice of surgical strategy should be individualized according to each patient’s specific circumstances. Both primary and staged approaches have their respective indications, and the final decision should be based on anatomical features, overall clinical condition, and intraoperative findings. Second, low birth weight alone should not be regarded as an absolute contraindication to surgery. With aggressive nutritional support and meticulous perioperative management, some infants may tolerate complex surgical interventions. Third, delayed sternal closure is an effective postoperative management strategy in complex congenital heart disease and may facilitate recovery of cardiac function. Fourth, In patients with tracheal stenosis or tracheomalacia, a bioresorbable external tracheal splint may represent a viable therapeutic option. Finally, timely recognition and appropriate management of postoperative complications are critical to achieving favorable outcomes.
