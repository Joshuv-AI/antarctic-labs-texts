Decoding the human milk virome: a comparative analysis of composition, colonization, and health impacts
Authors: Samar Yahia, Selvasankar Murugesan, Souhaila Al Khodor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast milk (BM) is a complex biological system that not only delivers essential nutrition to the baby, but is also a rich collection of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses and fungi. Dominated by bacteriophages and eukaryotic viruses, the BM virome plays a role in shaping the infant gut microbiome composition, influencing immune development and long-term health outcomes. Emerging research confirms the vertical transmission of bacteriophages from the mother to her infant via breastfeeding, and the shared viral taxa suggest functional roles in modulating bacterial populations and maintaining gut homeostasis. Disruptions in this early life virome, driven by factors such as maternal health, mode of delivery, and geographic location, have been linked to dysbiosis and disease susceptibility for conditions ranging from inflammatory bowel disease to metabolic disorders.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in NCBI/PUBMED and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) using the keywords “Milk virome,” “Milk phage,” “Milk phageome,” and “Milk metagenome”. After applying our inclusion and exclusion criteria, two studies remained. This comparative analysis explores the composition and diversity of the BM virome across two geographically distinct cohorts-Turkey and the United States-revealing significant differences in viral diversity and composition.ResultsThe Turkish cohort exhibited a significantly higher viral richness and unique taxonomic distributions compared to the USA group. Notably, bacteriophage families like Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, and Podoviridae were shown to be abundant in both populations. The Turkish BM virome featured a higher prevalence of plant- and insect-derived viruses (e.g. Partitiviridae, Tospoviridae), likely tied to dietary or regional agricultural practices.ConclusionsDespite limitations related to sample size and methodological variability, this study emphasizes how dietary habits, environmental exposures, and geographic location may shape the breast milk virome. Further research integrating longitudinal data from a multi-ethnic population, cohorts is essential to fully harness the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of the human milk virome.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08599-9.Keywords: Maternal and Child Health, Breastfeeding, Infant gut microbiome, Bacteriophages, Ethnic and Geographic Variation, Vertical Transmission

Introduction

Breast milk (BM) serves not only as a critical nutritional source for infants but also as a key contributor to microbial colonization during early development. This complex biological fluid harbors a diverse ecosystem comprising bacterial communities (including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus species) alongside a dynamic viral component known as the virome [1–5]. Recent studies indicated that viral sequences constitute approximately 85.7% of DNA contigs in healthy BM compared to just 14.3% of bacterial origin [6]. This viral predominance underscores their potential functional significance, particularly in gut homeostasis and infant health [7]. The BM virome is predominantly composed of bacteriophages-viruses that infect specific bacterial hosts-as well as eukaryotic viruses, which collectively play a pivotal role in shaping the infant gut microbiome and modulating the immune system maturation [8, 9]. The BM virome undergoes marked shifts during lactation: transitional milk is dominated by bacteriophages (87.6% of viral sequences), primarily Podoviridae, Myoviridae, and Siphoviridae, alongside trace Herpesviridae. On the other hand, the prevalence of bacteriophages declines to 67% in mature, with Siphoviridae becoming more dominant [8]. Delivery mode was also shown to affect the BM virome and its compsotion during milk maturation: after vaginal delivery, Podoviridae dominates transitional milk but drops to 8.5% in mature milk, whereas Cesarean section results in lower initial Podoviridae (15.8%) and a subsequent increase in Myoviridae and Herpesviridae [8]. Studies suggest that these bacteriophages selectively modulate bacterial populations in both the mammary gland and infant gut, maintaining microbial balance and function, [9, 10] underscoring the BM’s role in establishing a resilient neonatal microbiome, enhancing infection resistance, and potentially reducing risks of allergic and autoimmune disorders later in life.

The high similarity between BM bacteriophages and those in the infant gut supports maternal-neonatal transmission, suggesting that perturbations to the maternal virome-whether due to diet, antibiotic use, delivery mode, or gestational age—may have lasting consequences for infant microbial colonization [8, 9]. Comparative analyses of BM and infant fecal viromes reveal distinct yet overlapping profiles, with vertical transmission implicated in seeding the infant gut [7]. The vertical transmission of viruses from mother to infant is represented by bifidophages-temperate bacteriophages that co-migrate with their Bifidobacterium hosts-into BM [11]. This co-transfer depends on the presence of both phage and host bacteria, with metagenomic studies confirming greater virome similarity between breastfed infants and their mothers’ gut microbiomes compared to formula-fed infants [9]. Specific bacteriophages targeting Staphylococcus (Siphoviridae, Podoviridae), Escherichia, Enterobacter, and beneficial genera like Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus are consistently shared postpartum, demonstrating breast milk’s role as a conduit for maternal virome transmission [6, 12]. Beyond seeding the infant gut, breast milk actively modulates viral dynamics through bioactive components (e.g., oligosaccharides, lactoferrin) that suppress pathogenic eukaryotic viruses while enriching beneficial phages [12]. This dual function highlights breast milk’s central role in establishing a balanced infant virome [7, 13, 14]. During the first year of life, the infant gut virome undergoes dynamic shifts, initially dominated by Caudovirales (Siphoviridae, Myoviridae, and Podoviridae) [15] including CrAssphage, which targets Bacteroidetes and may originate from BM [13, 15]. Bacteriophage diversity peaks in the first six months, coinciding with initial microbial colonization, while eukaryotic virus richness increases later, likely reflecting dietary and environmental exposures. [15]. Interestingly, the infant gut harbors a greater phage diversity than the maternal gut, emphasizing their developmental importance [15]. In addition to bacteriophages, the infant gut virome includes a variety of eukaryotic viruses including the disease-associated mammal-infecting viruses (DaMiVs) (e.g., Adenoviridae, Picornaviridae), plant-derived viruses (e.g., Virgaviridae), and fungus-infecting viruses (e.g., Totiviridae), though their functional roles remain poorly understood [13]. Temperate phages dominate early infancy but decline by 12 months, suggesting phased adaptation to maturing bacterial communities, whereas eukaryotic viruses like enteroviruses and parechoviruses become increasingly prevalent [13, 16].

Together, these findings establish the breast milk (BM) virome as a foundational architect of infant gut microbial ecology, immune maturation, and long-term health-solidifying its role as a critical mediator of the protective benefits of breastfeeding. In this study, we aim to characterize differences in BM virome composition across geographically distinct populations and compare patterns of viral diversity, bacteriophage abundance, and the potential for vertical transmission to the infant gut.

Methods

Literature screening

A systematic literature search was conducted in NCBI/PUBMED and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) using the keywords “Milk virome,” “Milk phage,” “Milk phageome,” and “Milk metagenome”. Our systematic search of PubMed and the ENA initially identified 407 studies (Fig. 1). After applying our inclusion criteria, we excluded 327 studies involving animal milk samples and 65 studies limited to 16S rRNA sequencing without virome data. Of the remaining 15 studies, 13 were excluded due to their focus on maternal health conditions or pregnancy complications.

Study selection workflow for the comparative analysis of the human milk virome. Records were identified through searches of PubMed and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) (n = 407). Studies involving animal milk (n = 327) were excluded during the identification stage. The remaining records (n = 80) underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of studies using 16S sequencing data (n = 65). Eligibility assessment based on study methods and reported outcomes identified studies involving mothers with health issues or pregnancy complications, which were excluded (n = 13). Ultimately, two studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the comparative analysis

Two studies met all inclusion criteria for healthy populations including Dinleyici et al. (Turkey) [8] who published a prospective study of 32 breast milk samples (vaginal/cesarean deliveries; transitional/mature stages), and Pannaraj et al. (USA) [7], a study that included 10 transitional milk samples collected from healthy Hispanic mothers (vaginal/cesarean deliveries). These studies provided standardized virome sequencing data from healthy mothers, enabling robust comparative analysis of transitional milk viromes across diverse populations.

Data analysis

Studies were screened to include only those analyzing human milk samples from healthy controls. For eligible studies, raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were retrieved from the European Nucleotide Archive. Viral community profiling was performed using FastViromeExplorer [17] to generate comprehensive virome abundance profiles for each sample. Downstream bioinformatic analysis was conducted in R using the “MicrobiotaProcess” and “MicroEco” packages for data normalization, diversity analysis, and statistical comparisons [18, 19].

Results

Study selection and characteristics of included studies

Our systematic search of PubMed and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) as described in methods section initially identified 407 records (Fig. 1). Following application of our inclusion criteria, we excluded 327 studies that analyzed animal milk samples and 65 studies that relied solely on 16S rRNA sequencing without virome characterization. Of the remaining 15 studies, 13 were further excluded due to their focus on maternal pathologies, pregnancy complications, or non-healthy cohorts.

Only two studies met all predefined criteria for inclusion:

PRJEB26810 [8]: A prospective study of 32 breast milk samples from healthy mothers, stratified by delivery mode (vaginal vs. cesarean) and lactation stage (transitional and mature milk).

PRJNA427187 [7]: A study of 10 transitional milk samples collected from healthy Hispanic mothers, with documented delivery modes and standardized metagenomic virome sequencing.

These two studies provided high-quality, comparable virome data from healthy, term mothers, enabling a robust comparative analysis of transitional milk virome composition across geographically and demographically distinct populations.

Geographic variation in breast milk virome diversity

To assess differences in viral diversity across populations, we compared alpha and beta diversity metrics between the Turkish and USA cohorts. Alpha diversity parameters–Observed, Shannon, and Simpson indices-were used to evaluate viral richness and evenness. All parameters demonstrated significantly higher alpha diversity ((****, p < 0.0001) in the Turkish cohort compared to the USA cohort (Fig. 2A), indicating a more diverse and evenly distributed breast milk virome among Turkish mothers. We then evaluated the beta diversity between the two cohorts using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index to assess differences in breast milk virome composition across populations. As illustrated in Fig. 2B, Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity plot revealed a clear separation between the Turkish and USA cohorts, indicating distinct viral community structures (PERMANOVA: p = 0.001) in each group. Furthermore, Between-group Bray–Curtis distances (Fig. 2C), exposed that this observed divergence was statistically significant (****, p < 0.0001), suggesting distinct viral community structures between the analyzed cohorts. These findings highlight substantial geographic variation in both the diversity and composition of the breast milk virome.

Taxonomic profiling of the breast milk virome across cohorts

Analysis of the BM viral taxa revealed distinct patterns across taxonomic levels. At the family level (Fig. 3A), the breast milk virome exhibited differences in taxonomic composition between the Turkey and USA cohorts. Turkish cohort showed a relatively consistent viral family composition dominated by Partitiviridae, Picornaviridae, and Bromoviridae, with comparatively stable abundance patterns across individuals. In contrast, USA samples displayed greater inter-individual variability, with pronounced enrichment of several families including Myoviridae and Podoviridae, suggesting increased heterogeneity in virome composition across samples.

At the genus level (Fig. 3B), the compositional shifts were observed between geographic cohorts. The Turkey cohort was characterized by a predominance of Betapartitivirus, Alphapartitivirus, Cardiovirus, and Bromovirus, which remained relatively consistent across samples. By contrast, USA samples exhibited a more heterogeneous viral genus profile, with variable enrichment of taxa such as Pbunavirus,and Lambdavirus. These findings demonstrate substantial differences in the taxonomic organization of the breast milk virome between the geographically distinct cohorts. However, the contribution of geographic factors cannot be distinguished from other cohort-specific variables.

Heatmap analysis of the top viral genera further highlighted distinct abundance patterns between cohorts (Fig. 3C). Samples from Turkey clustered with relatively homogeneous viral signatures, whereas USA samples demonstrated more variable genus-level abundance profiles with selective enrichment of specific taxa across individuals. Overall, the observed patterns support geographic stratification in the breast milk virome at multiple taxonomic levels.

Geography shapes the breast milk virome

Differential abundance analysis revealed marked geographic differences in the breast milk virome at both the genus and species levels. At the genus level, several viral genera were significantly enriched in the Turkey cohort (Fig. 4A–C), including Orthobunyavirus, Cypovirus, Tospovirus, Chrysovirus, Circovirus, Phlebovirus, Pomovirus, and Muromegalovirus. In contrast, the USA cohort showed enrichment of Pbunavirus, Bpp1virus, and Phi29virus, suggesting distinct phage-associated virome signatures between geographic groups (Fig. 4A). Volcano plot analysis further highlighted distinct differential virome signatures between cohorts, with multiple significantly enriched genera distributed across both Turkey and USA groups (Fig. 4B). Several taxa displayed strong effect sizes and statistical significance, supporting cohort-specific viral enrichment patterns and reinforcing the presence of pronounced geographic stratification within the breast milk virome.

At the species level (Fig. 4C), Turkish cohort demonstrated significantly higher abundance of species such as Human endogenous retrovirus K (HERV-K), Iris yellow spot orthotospovirus, Shamonda orthobunyavirus, BeAn 58,058 virus and Dendrolimus punctatus cypovirus 22, whereas USA samples were enriched for bacteriophage species including Burkholderia virus BcepF1, Burkholderia phage KS9, and Staphylococcus phage Ipla7, further emphasizing geographic variation in viral composition. Only 118 taxa were shared between cohorts, with 416 Turkey-exclusive and 124 USA-exclusive taxa (Fig. 4D). These shared taxa represent potential biomarkers, while population-specific signatures suggest geographic or lifestyle influences on virome composition.

Discussion

In this comparative analysis we described the BM virome by identifying two studies that had explored the BM virome on two geographically distant cohorts. We show that the BM virome is a dynamic ecosystem that varies according to population-specific factors, with profound implications for infant health. The striking divergence in viral diversity between the Turkish and USA cohorts-evident through both alpha and beta diversity metrics-paints a compelling picture of how human populations evolve distinct viral signatures. These differences likely arise from a complex interplay of genetic, dietary, and environmental factors, mirroring patterns observed in microbiome studies [20]. Moreover, ethnicity has been shown to have a greater effect on microbial composition than other variables like BMI, age, and sex, reinforcing the importance of considering population-specific factors in virome research [20]. Additionally, certain bacterial taxa are known to be genetically inherited and vary with human genetic diversity [20], suggesting that similar patterns may apply to the virome and contribute to the observed differences between ethnic groups.

The conserved presence of Caudovirales phages (Myoviridae, Siphoviridae, Podoviridae) across both cohorts suggests their fundamental role in infant health. Importantly, Myoviridae and Podoviridae exhibit lytic activity against Staphylococcus aureus, a multidrug-resistant pathogen. Their effectiveness in phage therapy applications [21] highlights their therapeutic potential. In the USA cohort, the most abundant genus within the Myoviridae family was Pbunavirus, and the species Burkholderia virus BcepF1 was highly prevalent. This phage targets Burkholderia cenocepacia, an opportunistic pathogen associated with infections in cystic fibrosis patients [22], suggesting a possible protective or therapeutic role in early life. In addition, Siphoviridae has been associated with enhanced gut-brain axis function, including improvements in executive cognitive performance and verbal memory [23], indicating broader implications beyond microbial regulation. Other prominent viral families we observed in this comparative analysis were Partitiviridae and Picornaviridae. The Picornaviridae family belongs to the Picornavirales order and includes important human pathogens transmitted via the oral-fecal route, causing conditions such as the common cold, meningitis, hepatitis, and paralysis [24]. A key subfamily, human enteroviruses, are acid-stable and replicate in the gastrointestinal tract, raising questions about their effects when transferred from BM to the infant gut. The dominant genus identified in this family was Cardiovirus A, which has been linked to myocarditis and encephalitis in animal models [25]. Partitiviridae, on the other hand, primarily infect fungi, plants, protozoans, and insects [26]. The most abundant genera in our study were Betapartitivirus and Alphapartitivirus, with species such as Rosellinia necatrix partitivirus 2, R. necatrix partitivirus 6, and Red clover cryptic virus 2 being particularly prevalent. Given that these are not human viruses, they could potentially be acquired through dietary intake or environmental exposure.

Beyond the dominant orders Caudovirales and Picornavirales, the Bunyavirales order was notably abundant in the Turkish cohort. Members of this group are known to infect a wide range of hosts, including humans, mammals, insects, and plants, and are often associated with hemorrhagic fevers and encephalitis, typically transmitted by arthropods like mosquitoes and ticks [27]. However, their presence in BM is unusual and may reflect environmental contamination rather than active replication. Differences in eukaryotic virus abundance were also noted between the two cohorts. Fewer eukaryotic viruses were detected in samples from Hispanic mothers in the USA, while higher levels were found in the Turkish cohort. This discrepancy may be attributed to sample processing methods, such as the use of filters with small pore sizes that could exclude larger viruses like herpesviruses [7]. Notably, some viruses, such as those from the Poxviridae family, have been identified in the human oropharyngeal virome and may represent retrograde transfer from infant saliva to maternal breast milk [28]. The Turkish cohort showed the significant presence of HERV-K. Previous studies which reported the presence of HERV-K in human fluids including mother’s milk, highlighted its contribution to immune modulation and potential signaling pathways, as well as its link to the maternal–fetal immune environment rather than functioning solely in nutrition [29, 30]. While bacteriophages predominantly influence microbial balance, eukaryotic viruses may directly affect infant health through infections and immune responses.

Environmental and occupational exposures

As previously discussed, previous studies have suggested that geographic location and lifestyle significantly contribute to shaping the healthy virome [20]. For instance, Partitiviridae and Tospoviridae -both plant-infecting viral families - are more prevalent in the Turkish cohort [13, 31]. These viruses are possibly introduced into BM through dietary sources or environmental exposure. Within these families, Betapartitivirus, Alphapartitivirus, and Tospovirus showed the most significant differences between the two cohorts. Specifically, Rosellinia necatrix partitivirus 6 and Vicia cryptic virus were more abundant in the Turkish cohort, possibly reflecting local agricultural practices. Additionally, Poxviridae viruses are more commonly reported among farmers, butchers, and veterinarians in Turkey, where they are known to cause zoonotic infections [32]. The species BeAn.58058 was the most differentially abundant in the Turkish cohort, further supporting the influence of occupational exposure. Conversely, the Bpp1virus genus was more abundant in the USA cohort and infects Bordetella bacteria, which are commonly found in environmental reservoirs [33]. Similarly, Burkholderia virus BcepC6B was more prevalent in the USA cohort and targets Burkholderia species-opportunistic pathogens associated with worsened outcomes in cystic fibrosis patients [22]. However, the prevalence of these phages in the USA population remains understudied.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with larger, more diverse populations are necessary to validate these results. Additionally, differences in virome extraction and sequencing protocols between the two studies may have influenced the detection sensitivity and taxonomic resolution, highlighting the need for standardized methodologies in future comparative virome studies. More longitudinal studies enrolling mothers and babies’ pairs are needed to understand the role of the BM virome in modulating the infant gut microbiome, immunity and growth.
