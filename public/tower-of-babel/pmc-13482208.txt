Periconceptional GLP-1 Receptor Agonist Exposure
Authors: Ritu Singh, Akriti Singh, Awadhesh Kumar Singh
Published: 2026

We read with interest the brief report by Kodali et al1 evaluating exposure to glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) during or before pregnancy. The authors address an important and timely question, particularly as GLP-1RAs are increasingly used in women of reproductive age. However, we believe the reported “improved maternal and preterm birth outcomes” require more cautious interpretation.

First, there appears to be an internal inconsistency in the principal fetal outcome. In the text, the authors state that overall adverse fetal outcomes were not associated with GLP-1RA exposure (OR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.72-1.16; P = 0.44). In contrast, Figure 1 reports a statistically significant reduction in adverse fetal outcomes (OR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.36-0.96; P = 0.03), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 87%). These estimates imply materially different conclusions: no association vs apparent benefit. Clarification is needed as to whether this discrepancy reflects a typographical error, different data sets, adjusted vs unadjusted estimates, or different composite definitions.

Second, the evidence base is sparse. The meta-analysis included 4 observational studies, with as few as 2 for adverse maternal outcomes and as many as 4 cohorts for adverse fetal outcomes contributing to individual outcomes. The apparent precision of some pooled estimates may therefore be misleading. Moreover, Figure 1 suggests that a single study dominated several pooled results: Imbroane et al2 contributed 96.3% of the weight for adverse maternal outcomes and 87.8% for preterm birth. Such dominance limits generalizability and makes the findings sensitive to study-specific design, population selection, and outcome definitions.

Third, exposed and comparator groups were not clinically exchangeable. The GLP-1RAs group had more overweight/obesity, whereas comparator pregnancies had much higher pre-existing type 2 diabetes. These differences are directly related to congenital anomalies, hypertensive disorders, pregnancy loss, cesarean delivery, fetal growth, and preterm birth. The reported lower maternal and preterm-birth risks may therefore represent confounding by indication, diabetes severity, treatment access, or health system selection rather than drug-associated benefit.

Fourth, the exposure window weakens pregnancy-specific inference. Only 21% of exposed women used GLP-1RAs periconceptionally or until the first trimester; most exposure was preconceptional. Therefore, conclusions about pregnancy exposure should be separated from preconception metabolic optimization, drug discontinuation effects, and residual pharmacokinetic exposure. The broad composite outcomes are also clinically heterogeneous, combining endpoints with different mechanisms, severities, frequencies, and susceptibility to ascertainment bias.

Finally, although Kodali et al call for randomized clinical trials to determine efficacy and safety, deliberate randomization to GLP-1RA during pregnancy is unlikely to be ethically acceptable at present. Nevertheless, this analysis is concordant with 2 recent meta-analyses by Armstrong et al3 and Hakim et al,4 which also provide reassuring evidence for inadvertent early exposure, but the certainty remains low (Table 1). We feel that the appropriate message is not therapeutic benefit or established pregnancy safety, but the need for prospective exposure registries, active-comparator pharmacoepidemiology, agent-specific analyses, standardized exposure windows, and long-term offspring follow-up.
