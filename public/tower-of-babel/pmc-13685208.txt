Association between normal-weight central obesity and cardiometabolic disorders: a longitudinal analysis from health check-up data
Authors: Yang Cheng, Hui Zheng, Hongli Yin, Donghua Yin, Hui Wang, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Obesity, typically defined by body mass index (BMI) above 30 kg/m2 [1], has emerged as a major public health issue worldwide. In China, the prevalence of general obesity has reached approximately 16–17% in adults and is projected to increase further, representing a substantial public health burden [2]. Prior studies have demonstrated that obesity is a strong forerunner of cardiometabolic disorders (CMD), including diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), and hyperlipidemia (HLP) [3–5], suggesting its devasting threat to health.

Notably, in the past few decades, the prevalence of these traditionally obesity-related cardiometabolic diseases has also increased among individuals without general obesity, and even in those considered “healthy” by BMI standards [6–8]. This phenomenon highlights the limitation of BMI, which, although widely used, cannot reflect regional body fat distribution or ectopic fat accumulation in the human body [9]. As a complementary anthropometric indicator, waist circumference (WC) has been recognized as a reliable surrogate of abdominal and visceral adiposity and is now recommended as a key component of cardiometabolic risk assessment in both international and Chinese guidelines [10, 11].

Given the limitations of BMI or WC as a single measure for obesity, we recommended combining these two parameters to comprehensively explore obesity-related risks. Thus, a new obesity phenotype, termed normal-weight central obesity (NWCO), has been introduced in clinical and epidemiological research [12–14]. Individuals with NWCO have a normal BMI but excess central adiposity, often reflected in elevated WC or unfavorable fat percentage. Because their BMI values remain within the “normal” range, they are easily overlooked in routine health screening, leading to missed opportunities for early prevention and delayed intervention [15]. With the rising living standards and prevailing sedentary lifestyle, the prevalence of NWCO is increasing in a worrisome manner [16], ranging from 5 to 45% [17, 18]. This trend underscores the critical need to investigate the health risks associated with this condition.

In recent years, emerging literature demonstrates significant associations between NWCO and multiple cardiometabolic dysregulation, including insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, subclinical atherosclerosis, and increased cardiovascular risk in Western populations [19, 20]. In addition, growing evidence from China has also confirmed the detrimental impact of NWCO. A large cohort study among older Chinese adults reported that NWCO and its transition from normal-weight and non-central obesity (NWNO) were significantly associated with higher incident cardiovascular disease risk [21]. Another population-based cross-sectional study found that NWCO was independently related to elevated HTN risk among Chinese adults [22]. Nevertheless, the majority of available investigations have been cross-sectional or limited to specific age groups, with scarce evidence from large-scale cohort studies examining the longitudinal association between NWCO and the incidence of CMD in the general Chinese population.

Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically investigate the relationship between NWCO and the development of CMD, including HTN, DM and HLP, using a large-scale real-world health check-up dataset in China.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective cohort study constructed from a prospectively collected health examination database from the health management institution of Jiangsu Province Geriatric Hospital (Nanjing, China) was performed in our study. In 2022, the baseline cohort (n = 16,002) comprised all individuals who underwent routine health check-ups at our center during the year and met the following criteria: normal BMI (18.0–24.0 kg/m2) and an available WC measurement at the same visit. Participants were excluded if they had: (i) a prior history of HTN, DM, or HLP in 2022 (n = 7,326); (ii) no follow-up physical examination in 2024 (n = 2,931); and (iii) missing data on WC at follow-up (n = 139). Besides, baseline age was obtained from the health examination recodes at the time of the initial visit in 2022. Ultimately, we analyzed 5,606 participants in this study for the final analysis of the association between NWCO phenotype and incident CMD (Supplementary Fig. 1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jiangsu Province Geriatric Hospital (Approval No. 004 − 1, 2025).

Anthropometric and physiological measurements

According to the standard procedures, parameters were retrieved from our physical examination system. It includes (1) demographic characteristics: age, sex, smoking and drinking status; (2) physical examination indicators: weight, height, WC, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (3) laboratory examination indicators: total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG); and (4) medical history: HTN, DM and HLP.

Definition of essential parameters

CO was defined as WC at a level ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 80 cm in women based on the International Diabetes Federation defined metabolic syndrome for Asians [23]. NWCO was defined as normal-weight (BMI: 18.0–24.0 kg/m2) but with CO, whereas NWNO as defined as normal-weight and without CO.

Temporal changes in waist circumference

Temporal changes in WC were calculated by subtracting the baseline (2022) values from those obtained at follow-up (2024). The relative change (%) was defined as (follow-up WC - baseline WC) / baseline WC × 100. For interpretability, this variable was further standardized per 10% increment (WC_change_per10).

Outcomes

Incident outcomes were ascertained at follow-up check-ups in 2024. The primary outcome was CMD including HTN, DM and HLP. HTN was defined as SBP of ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP of ≥ 90 mmHg, and or self-reported diagnosis of HTN. DM was defined as a FBG of ≥ 7.0 mg/dL, and or self-reported DM. HLP was defined as TC of ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, LDL-C of ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, HDL-C of ≤ 1.0 mmol/L, triglyceride level of ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, and or self-reported HLP [24]. CMD were diagnosed as DM, HTN, and/or HLP.

Statistical analyses

We performed all statistical analyses by using R 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Categorical data are presented as percentages (n, %), and were compared between the NWCO and NWNO groups using Chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate). Continuous variables including age, were assessed for normality; as all were non-normally distributed, they are presented as median with interquartile range and compared between groups by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The start time for follow-up was defined as the date of the baseline health examination in 2022. The follow-up period was calculated from this date until the occurrence of incident CMD or the follow-up health examination in 2024, whichever came first. Crude incidence rates were calculated as the number of incident events per 100 person-years at risk. We conducted Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for CMD events including its components. Different models were constructed as follows: Model 1 included NWCO alone without adjustment; Model 2 included NWCO, age and sex, and Model 3 included NWCO, age, sex, cigarette smoking, and drinking status. The proportional-hazards (PH) assumption was evaluated for each model and each covariate using Schoenfeld residual tests (cox.zph in R) as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Besides, we also included WC_change_per10 in Cox regression models to evaluate its association with incident CMD and its subtypes with adjustments of age, sex, cigarette smoking, and drinking status. HRs represent the relative hazard of CMD associated with each 10% increase in WC over the two-year follow-up.

We further conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our primary findings. First, we conducted subgroup and interaction analyses according to age and sex. To formally test for effect modification, we included multiplicative interaction terms (NWCO * age group and NWCO * sex) in the Cox models, and the P-values for interaction were derived using likelihood ratio tests. Second, to address the violation of the proportional hazards assumption for the sex variable identified in our primary models, we refitted the Cox models treating sex as a stratification variable (strata(sex)), which relaxes the PH assumption for sex. Third, body fat percentage was calculated by the CUN-BAE formula [25]: %BF=-44.988+(0.503*age)+(10.689*sex)+(3.172*BMI)-(0.026*BMI2)+(0.181*BMI*sex)-(0.02*BMI*age)-(0.005*BMI2*sex)+(0.00021*BMI2*age), where sex is codified as men = 0 and women = 1. Further, the association between body fat percentage and the development of CMD was examined using Cox regression analysis. Following previous studies, we classified the participants into three groups: normal body fatness, overweight, and obesity [26]. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

First, to address the violation of the proportional hazards assumption for sex, we refitted Cox models treating sex as a stratification variable. The results showed that the association between NWCO and CMD remained significant and materially unchanged across both unstratified and stratified models (Supplementary Table 3). Second, we performed a subgroup analysis of the fully adjusted model stratified by age and sex (Fig. 3). It was found that sex significantly influenced the association between the NWCO and the incidence of HTN (interaction P = 0.036). No significant difference was found for the interaction of CMD, DM and HLP. Third, we evaluated the association between BF% calculated by the CUN-BAE formula and CMD. After adjusting for age, gender, smoking and drinking, we identified that BF% tertiles were positively increased the risk of developing CMD, including HTN, and HLP, but not DM (see Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our study, a large cohort of health check-up participants, demonstrated that in individuals with NWCO defined by BMI and WC had a significantly increased risk of CMD. Additionally, our analysis revealed that temporal changes in WC were significantly associated with an elevated risk of CMD and its components excluding DM among normal-weight adults. These findings underscore that evaluating cardiometabolic risk in normal-weight individuals should go beyond BMI, and further that combining BMI with central obesity measures may improve risk stratification between high- and low- risk populations.

A previous meta-analysis by Mohammadian et al. [27], together with several cross-sectional studies [22, 28] demonstrated a positive association of normal-weight obesity with cardiometabolic risk factors. Consistent with these findings, our study further confirms this association in a longitudinal health examination cohort. Additionally, we observed that per 10% increase in WC from baseline to follow-up was associated with a higher risk of developing CMD even in normal-weight individuals, suggesting the potential value of monitoring changes in central adiposity for risk identification. Notably, these analyses are observational and therefore do not support causal inference, and further validation in studies with longer follow-up periods is warranted.

Several possible mechanisms could be suggested to explain the results of the present study. First, individuals with NWCO have higher inflammatory and pro-thrombotic biomarkers such as plasma homocysteine, interleukins, and C-reactive protein when compared to the non-obese individuals [29–32]. Second, it has been found that mitochondria in hypoxic adipocytes, commonly observed in individuals with excess fat, leads to an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thereby activating kinases inducing over-expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [33]. Third, increased visceral adiposity in NWCO also predisposes to adverse cardiometabolic effects like insulin resistance and atherogenic lipid changes as suggested by previous research [34].

The key strength of this study lies in that it is one of the few large-scale cohort studies investigating the association between NWCO and a wide range of CMD outcomes, supported by extensive sensitivity analyses. Further, limitations of this study were also presented. First, our study was based on a single-center health check-up cohort that primarily targeted working-age individuals; and therefore, selection bias such as a “healthy worker” effect cannot be ruled out, and the external validity of our findings may be limited, especially for individuals from rural residents or those who do not participate in regular health check-ups. Second, evidence suggested that Asians exhibit a greater propensity for visceral fat than Europeans for a given BMI [35], while most studies on NWCO have been widely conducted in Asia [14, 16, 36]. Thus, further investigations are needed in other populations of races or ethnicity. Third, the short follow-up period of approximately two years, may not fully capture long-term disease trajectories or late-onset outcomes in chronic diseases, particularly diabetes. In our study, the small number of incident DM cases resulted in wide confidence intervals, and thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Longer-term cohort studies are therefore necessary to confirm our findings and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the temporal relationship between NWCO phenotype and incident CMD. Fourth, information on physical activity, dietary patterns, socioeconomic status, family history of CMD, and medication use was unavailable in our dataset and could not be adjusted for. Thus, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out and these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with more comprehensive baseline data are needed to confirm the association. Last, body fatness in our study was estimated by this CUN-BAE equation rather than direct methods such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which is considered more accurate for assessing body fat distribution [37, 38]. In addition, this equation was originally developed in Spain based on a European population, and large-scale validation in Chinese adults is still lacking. Thus, our results should be interpreted with caution, and further validation in Asian populations is warranted.

Conclusion

NWCO was significantly associated with unfavorable cardiometabolic markers, including blood pressure, glycemic status, and lipid measures, highlighting the added value of assessing central adiposity in addition to BMI for cardiometabolic risk identification in health screening settings. Reliance on BMI alone may lead to under-recognition of risk among a subset of normal-weight individuals. Further studies are warranted to determine whether more detailed assessments of body fat distribution can refine risk stratification in broader populations.
