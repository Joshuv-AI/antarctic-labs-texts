SOME Q Q -HYPERGEOMETRIC IDENTITIES ASSOCIATED WITH PARTITION THEOREMS OF LEBESGUE, SCHUR AND CAPPARELLI
Yazan Alamoudi, Krishnaswami Alladi

Abstract
. Here, we establish a polynomial identity in three variables a , b , c a,b,c , and with the degree of the polynomial given in terms of two integers L , M L,M . By letting L L and M M tend to infinity, we get the 1993 Alladi-Gordon q q -hypergeometric key-identity for the generalized Schur Theorem as well as the fundamental Lebesgue identity by two different choices of the variables. This polynomial identity provides a generalization and a unified approach to the Schur and Lebesgue theorems. We discuss other analytic identities for the Lebesgue and Schur theorems and also provide a key identity ( q q -hypergeometric) for Andrews’ deep refinement of the Alladi-Schur theorem. Finally, we discuss a new infinite hierarchy of identities, the first three of which relate to the partition theorems of Euler, Lebesgue, and Capparelli, and provide their polynomial versions as well.

1.
Introduction

One of the fundamental
q
-hypergeometric identities is Lebesgue’s identity:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{q^{T_{i}}(-cq)_{i}}{(q)_{i}}=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}(1+q^{m})(1+cq^{2m})=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}\frac{(1+cq^{2m})}{(1-q^{2m-1})}.

The importance of (
1.1
) is because when
c=0
, it yields Euler’s series and product generating functions for partitions into distinct parts, and with the
dilations and translations given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{dilation}\,q\mapsto q^{2},\quad\text{translations}\,c\mapsto cq^{-1}\,\text{or}\,c\mapsto cq,

it yields the
q
-hypergeometric identities for the Little Göllnitz partition theorems (see Theorem G below). In
[
6
]
, Alladi-Gordon gave the following combinatorial interpretation of Lebesgue’s identity as a weighted partition theorem and gave a bijective proof of it:

Theorem L:

Let
D(n;j)
denote the number of partitions of
\pi:b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{\nu}=n
into distinct parts
b_{i}
, such that there are
j
gaps
b_{i}-b_{i+1}\geq 2
among the parts for
i=1,2,\cdots\nu
, with the convention
b_{\nu+1}=0
.

Let
C(n;k)
denote the number of partitions of
n
with even parts non-repeating, such that there are precisely
k
even parts. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j}D(n,j)(1+c)^{j}=\sum_{k}C(n;k)c^{k}.

Under the transformations in (
1.2
), the two Göllnitz identities that emerge are:

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{q^{i^{2}+i}(-cq^{-1};q^{2})_{i}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}}=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}(1+q^{4m})(1+q^{4m-2})(1+cq^{4m-3}),

and

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{q^{i^{2}+i}(-cq;q^{2})_{i}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}}=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}(1+q^{4m})(1+q^{4m-2})(1+cq^{4m-1}).

The Little Göllnitz theorem(s)
[
22
]
, which are the partition interpretations of (
1.2a
) and (
1.2b
), are:

Theorem G:

For
i=1,2
, let
g_{i}(n;k)
denote the number of partitions of
n
into parts that differ by
\geq 2
, with strict inequality if a part is odd, and with
k
odd parts, and with smallest part
\geq i
.

For
i=1,2
, let
G_{i}(n;k)
denote the number of partitions into distinct parts, which are of the form
2,4,\,or\,2i-1(mod\,4)
, and with
k
parts
\equiv 2i-1(mod\,4)
. Then

\displaystyle g_{i}(n;k)=G_{i}(n;k),\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,2.

Remark:

It is only at the undilated level, that is for Lebesgue’s identity, the partition theorem (Theorem L) is a weighted partition theorem. Once we have the dilation as in (
1.2
), Theorem G is a regular partition theorem (not weighted).

The celebrated 1926 partition theorem of Schur is:

Theorem S:

Let
B(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
into distinct parts
\equiv\pm 1(mod\,3)
.

Let
S(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
into parts that differ by at least 3, with strict inequality if a part is a multiple of 3. Then

\displaystyle S(n)=B(n).

Comment:

Note one similarity in the difference conditions in Theorems G and S, namely in Theorem G, the gap between parts is
\geq 2
with strict inequality if a part is odd, while in Theorem S, the gap between parts is
\geq 3
, with strict inequality if a part is a multiple of 3. We shall call the partitions enumerated by
S(n)
as
Schur partitions
.

Gleissburg
[
21
]
showed that Theorem S can be refined to

\displaystyle B(n;k)=S(n;k)

where
B(n;k)
and
S(n;k)
denote the number of partitions enumerated by
A(n)
and
B(n)
with the condition that the number of parts is
k
, and with the convention that parts which are multiples of 3 are counted twice by
S(n;k)
.

In 1993, Alladi and Gordon
[
7
]
proved a two-parameter refinement and generalization of Theorem S, and in doing so, for the first time, cast Theorem S in the form of a
q
-hypergeometric identity, which they dubbed as a
key-identity
:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\frac{a^{\alpha+\gamma}b^{\beta+\gamma}q^{T_{s}+T_{\gamma}}}{(q)_{\alpha}(q)_{\beta}(q)_{\gamma}}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{a^{i}b^{j}q^{T_{i}+T_{j}}}{(q)_{i}(q)_{j}}=(-aq)_{\infty}(-bq)_{\infty}.

where
s=\alpha+\beta+\gamma
.

By using the transformations

(1.4)

\displaystyle(\text{dilation})\,q\mapsto q^{3},\quad\text{and}\quad(\text{translations})\,a\mapsto aq^{-2},\,b\mapsto bq^{-1}

in (
1.3
), the following strong refinement of Theorem S falls out:

Theorem A-G:

Let
B(n;i,j)
denote the number of partitions of
n
into
i
distinct parts
\equiv 1(mod\,3)
, and
j
distinct parts
\equiv 2(mod\,3)
.

Let
S(n;\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
denote the number of partitions of the type enumerated by
S(n)
, with the condition that the the number of parts
\equiv 1,2,3(mod\,3)
are
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
respectively. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha+\gamma=i,\beta+\gamma=j}S(n;\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=B(n;i,j).

Notice that in Theorem A-G, the total number of parts is

\displaystyle i+j=\alpha+\gamma+\beta+\gamma=\alpha+\beta+2\gamma,

and so the parts which are multiples of 3 are counted twice. In
[
7
]
, the combinatorial interpretation of the key-identity was given in terms of partitions into parts occurring in three possible colors - two primary colors
a,b
, and one secondary color
ab
, with gap conditions on the colored parts, and with the condition that the parts in the secondary color are counted twice. Under the dilation and translations given in (1.4), the parts in primary colors
a,b
correspond to parts
\equiv 1,2(mod\,3)
, and parts in secondary color are then the multiples of 3.

The colored partition version (generalization) of Theorem A-G is proved combinatorially (bijectively) in
[
7
]
. The combinatorial proofs of the weighted partition Theorem L given in
[
6
]
, and of the colored partition version of Theorem A-G in
[
7
]
and similar, with the main difference being in the final step, where in the case of Theorem L, a certain choice could be made; this is why
D(n;j)
has a weight
(1+c)^{j}
attached to it. Since the combinatorial proofs of Theorem L and the colored generalization of Theorem A-G are so similar, it is natural to ask if there is a unified
q
-hypergeometric approach to Lebesgue’s identity (
1.1
) and the key-identity (
1.3
) for the generalized Schur theorem? After establishing a finite analog of Lebesgue’s identity in Section
3
, we provide in Section
6
a new polynomial identity, from which, under two different specializations, the finite Schur and Lebesgue identities fall out. Following this, in Section
6
, we provide a
q
-hypergeometric
key-identity
for Andrews’ deep refinement of the Alladi-Schur Theorem. Finally, in Section
7
, we introduce a new infinite hierarchy of identities of which the first three correspond to the partition theorems of Euler, Lebesgue, and Capparelli; we provide a polynomial version of this infinite hierarchy as well.

Notation:

Throughout, we have adopted the following standard notation:

For complex numbers
a,q
, we use the
q
-Pochhammer symbols

\displaystyle(a)_{n}=(a;q)_{n}:=\prod^{n-1}_{j=0}(1-aq^{j}),

and

\displaystyle(a;q)_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(a;q)_{j}=\prod^{\infty}_{j=0}(1-aq^{j}),\quad\text{if}\quad|q|<1.

The variable
q
is called the base. We often write
(a)_{n}
in place of

(a;q)_{n}
suppressing
q
, but when the base is anything other than
q
,
it will be displayed.

We also make use of the
q
-binomial coefficients given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle{n\brack m}={n\brack m}_{q}:=\frac{(q)_{n}}{(q)_{m}(q)_{n-m}},\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq n

which are polynomials in
q
of degree
m(n-m)
. The
q
-binomial coefficients have value 0, when
m<0
or when
m>n
. This is because
1/(q)_{j}=0
when
j<0
.

We shall often use the following identity involving the
q
-binomial coefficients:

(1.6)

\displaystyle(-cq)_{n}=\sum^{n}_{k=0}c^{k}q^{T_{k}}{n\brack k},

where here and throughout,
T_{k}=k(k+1)/2
, is the
k
-th Triangular number.

2.
A very short proof of the key identity for Schur’s theorem

The proof of the key identity (
1.3
) given in
[
7
]
utilizes the
q
-Chu-Vandermonde summation. A second proof of (
1.3
) was given in
[
7
]
by rewriting it suitably and then using a Durfee rectangles argument. In October 2022, the second author communicated
[
3
]
to George Andrews a very short proof of the key identity (
1.3
), which we give here because this proof and the underlying combinatorics motivate the construction of the polynomial identity that provides the unification:

Proof:
Begin by expanding
(-aq)_{\infty}
and by splitting
(-bq)_{\infty}
as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle(-aq)_{\infty}(-bq)_{\infty}=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}(-bq)_{i}(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty}.

Next, expand
(-bq)_{i}
and
(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty}
, and substitute these expansions in (
2.1
) to get

\displaystyle(-aq)_{\infty}(-bq)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}\left(\sum^{i}_{j=0}b^{j}q^{T_{j}}{i\brack j}\right)\left(\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}\frac{b^{\ell}q^{T_{\ell}+i\ell}}{(q)_{\ell}}\right)

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}a^{i}q^{T_{i}}\left(\sum^{i}_{j=0}\frac{b^{j}q^{T_{j}}}{(q)_{j}(q)_{i-j}}\right)\left(\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}\frac{b^{\ell}q^{T_{\ell}+i\ell}}{(q)_{\ell}}\right).

At this stage, consider the following replacements in (
2.2
)

(2.3)

\displaystyle j\mapsto\gamma,\quad i-j=i-\gamma\mapsto\alpha,\quad\text{and}\quad\ell\mapsto\beta,

to rewrite the expression on the right in (
2.2
) as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\frac{a^{\alpha+\gamma}b^{\beta+\gamma}q^{T_{s}+T_{\gamma}}}{(q)_{\alpha}(q)_{\beta}(q)_{\gamma}},

where we have used the identity

\displaystyle T_{n}+T_{m}+nm=T_{n+m}

for Triangular numbers, and
s=\alpha+\beta+\gamma
. The key identity follows from (
2.1
) and (
2.4
).

Remark:

In
[
7
]
, the first two steps in the combinatorial proof of the Generalized Schur Theorem were as follows: Start with a vector partition
<\pi_{a},\pi_{b}>
in which
\pi_{a}
is a partition into
i
parts in color
a
, all distinct, and
\pi_{b}
is a partition into
j
parts in color
b
, all distinct. Then separate the parts of
\pi_{b}
into those that are
\leq i
in size and those that are
>(i+1)
in size. There are a total of six steps in that combinatorial proof, but these first two steps correspond to

\displaystyle\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}(-bq)_{i}(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty},

and this motivated the starting point of the short proof of the key identity.

3.
A finite version of Lebesgue’s identity

The
q
-binomial coefficients
{n\brack m}
have the property that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{n\brack m}=\frac{1}{(q)_{m}}.

So, a natural way to construct polynomial analogs of
q
-hypergeometric identities is to bring in
q
-binomial coefficients in place of terms like
1/(q)_{m}
. We now establish a polynomial version of Lebesgue’s identity:
1
1

1

An equivalent identity appears in
[
26
]
in a different form. However, our approach is different.

Theorem 1:

For all positive integers
M
, we have

\displaystyle\sum^{M}_{i=0}q^{T_{i}}(-cq)_{i}{M\brack i}=(-q)_{M}\left(\sum^{M}_{k=0}\frac{c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k}}\right)(q^{M-k+1})_{k}.

Proof:

Begin by expanding
(-cq)_{i}
and rewrite the left-hand side of the expression in Theorem 1 as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum^{M}_{i=0}q^{T_{i}}(-cq)_{i}{M\brack i}=\sum^{M}_{i=0}q^{T_{i}}\left(\sum^{i}_{k=0}c^{k}q^{T_{k}}{i\brack k}\right){M\brack i}=\sum_{i,k}c^{k}q^{T_{i}+T_{k}}\frac{(q)_{M}}{(q)_{k}(q)_{i-k}(q)_{M-i}},

by using (
1.5
). If we set
I=i-k
, we may rewrite the right hand side of (
3.2
) as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{I,k}c^{k}q^{T_{I+k}+T_{k}}\frac{(q)_{M}}{(q)_{k}(q)_{I}(q)_{M-I-k}}=\sum_{I,k}c^{k}q^{T_{I+k}+T_{k}}{{M-I}\brack k}{M\brack I}

once again by (
1.5
). At this stage, we replace
T_{I+k}
in (
3.3
) with
T_{I}+T_{k}+Ik
to rewrite (
3.3
) as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{I,k}c^{k}q^{2T_{k}+T_{I}+Ik}{{M-I}\brack k}{M\brack I}=\sum_{I,k}c^{k}q^{2T_{k}+T_{I}+Ik}{M\brack k}{{M-k}\brack I},

by (
1.5
). Finally, we write the right-hand side of (
3.4
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{k}c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}{M\brack k}\left(\sum_{I}q^{T_{I}+Ik}{{M-k}\brack I}\right)=\sum_{k}c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}{M\brack k}(-q^{k+1})_{M-k}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=(-q)_{M}\sum^{M}_{k=0}\frac{c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k}}\frac{(q)_{M}}{(q)_{M-k}}=(-q)_{M}\sum^{M}_{k=0}\frac{c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k}}(q^{M-k+1})_{k},

and this proves Theorem 1.

Lebesgue’s identity as a limiting case of Theorem 1:

Let
M\to\infty
in Theorem 1. Then, in view of (
3.1
), the left hand side of Theorem 1 is

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{q^{T_{i}}(-cq)_{i}}{(q)_{i}},

which is the left-hand side of (
1.1
). When
M\to\infty
, the right-hand side of Theorem 1 becomes

(3.7)

\displaystyle(-q)_{\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\frac{c^{k}q^{2T_{k}}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k}}=(-q)_{\infty}(-cq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty},

because
(q^{M-k+1})_{k}\to 1
as
M\to\infty
. This yields Lebesgue’s identity.

Another finite version of Lebesgue’s identity:

There are several possible finite versions of Lebesgue’s identity, such as

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sum^{m+n}_{N=0}q^{(N^{2}+N)/2}\sum^{N}_{k=0}b^{k}q^{(k^{2}+k)/2}{m\brack k}{n\brack{N-k}}=(-q)_{n}(q)_{m}\sum^{m}_{i=0}\frac{b^{i}q^{i^{2}+i}(-q^{n+1})_{i}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}(q)_{m-i}}

which is due to Alladi (1994 - unpublished), but we have emphasized the finite version in Theorem 1 because this is connected to the unified approach to the Schur and Lebesgue identities that will be given below.

To realize that (
3.8
) is a finite version of Lebesgue’s identity, let
m,n\to\infty
in (
3.8
). Then the right hand side (
3.8
) becomes

(3.9)

\displaystyle(-q)_{\infty}(q)_{\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{b^{i}q^{i^{2}+i}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}(q)_{\infty}}=(-q)_{\infty}(-bq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty},

which is the right-hand side of (
1.1
). Under these limits, the left side of (
3.8
) becomes

(3.10)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{N=0}q^{T_{N}}\sum^{N}_{k=0}\frac{b^{k}q^{T_{k}}}{(q)_{k}(q)_{N-k}}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{N=0}\frac{q^{T_{N}}}{(q)_{N}}\sum^{N}_{k=0}b^{k}q^{T_{k}}{N\brack k}=\sum^{\infty}_{N=0}\frac{q^{T_{N}}(-bq)_{N}}{(q)_{N}},

which is the left-hand side of (
1.1
).

We now sketch the proof of (
3.8
) which is non-trivial.

Proof of (3.8):

In Alladi
[
2
]
, the following was established both hypergeometrically and combinatorially:

LEMMA 1 (Transformation Formula)

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{a^{n}q^{n^{2}}(-bq;q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{(ab)^{n}q^{2n^{2}}(-aq^{2n+1};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}.

To prove the Lemma, expand
(-bq;q^{2})_{n}
on the left-hand side using (
1.6
) and reverse the order of summation to get the right-hand side. The combinatorial proof of Lemma 1 uses a redistribution idea of Bressoud (see
[
18
]
).

The finite version of Lemma 1 is

LEMMA 2:

\displaystyle\sum^{m+n}_{N=0}a^{N}q^{N^{2}}\sum^{n}_{k=0}b^{k}q^{k^{2}}{m\brack k}_{q^{2}}{n\brack{N-k}}_{q^{2}}=\sum^{m}_{i=0}(ab)^{i}q^{2i^{2}}{m\brack i}_{q^{2}}(-aq^{2i+1};q^{2})_{n}.

To prove Lemma 2, expand
(-aq^{2i+1};q^{2})_{n}
to rewrite the right hand side as

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sum^{m}_{i=0}(ab)^{i}q^{2i^{2}}{m\brack i}_{q^{2}}\sum^{n}_{j=0}a^{j}q^{j^{2}+2ij}{n\brack j}_{q^{2}}.

If we rearrange the right hand side of (
3.11
) by putting
i+j=N
, we get the left hand side of Lemma 2, thereby proving it.

Next in Lemma 2, replace
a
by
aq
and
b
by
bq
to get

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum^{m+n}_{N=0}a^{N}q^{N^{2}+N}\sum^{N}_{k=0}b^{k}q^{k^{2}+k}{m\brack k}_{q^{2}}{N\brack{N-k}}_{q^{2}}=\sum^{m}_{i=0}(ab)^{i}q^{2i^{2}+2i}{m\brack i}_{q^{2}}(-aq^{2i+2};q^{2})_{n}.

In (3.12) replace
q^{2}
by
q
to get

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sum^{m+n}_{N=0}a^{N}q^{T_{N}}\sum^{n}_{k=0}b^{k}q^{T_{k}}{m\brack k}{n\brack{N-k}}=\sum^{m}_{i=0}(ab)^{i}q^{i^{2}+i}{m\brack i}(-aq^{i+1})_{n}.

If we set
a=1
in (
3.13
), the right hand side becomes

(3.14)

\displaystyle(q)_{m}\sum^{m}_{i=0}\frac{b^{i}q^{i^{2}+i}(-q^{i+1})_{n}}{(q)_{i}(q)_{m-i}}=(-q)_{n}(q)_{m}\sum^{m}_{i=0}\frac{b^{i}q^{i^{2}+i}(-q^{n+1})_{i}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}(q)_{m-i}},

and so (

4.
A power series identity unifying Schur and Lebesgue

Here we prove the following power series identity due to Alamoudi from which (
1.1
) and (
1.3
) emerge as special cases:

Theorem 2:

With free parameters
a,b,c
, we have

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}\left(-\frac{c}{a}q\right)_{i}(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty}=\sum_{t,j,\ell}\frac{a^{t}b^{\ell}c^{j}q^{T_{t+\ell+j}+T_{j}}}{(q)_{t}(q)_{\ell}(q)_{j}}.

Proof:

We expand
(-\frac{c}{a}q)_{i}
and
(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty}
to get

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}\left(-\frac{c}{a}q\right)_{i}(-bq^{i+1})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}\left(\sum^{i}_{j=0}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)^{j}q^{T_{j}}{i\brack j}\right)\left(\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}\frac{b^{\ell}q^{T_{\ell}+i{\ell}}}{(q)_{\ell}}\right)

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}a^{i}q^{T_{i}}\left(\sum^{i}_{j=0}\frac{(\frac{c}{a})^{j}q^{T_{j}}}{(q)_{j}(q)_{i-j}}\right)\left(\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}\frac{b^{\ell}q^{T_{\ell}+i{\ell}}}{(q)_{\ell}}\right).

Now for the sum on the right in (
4.1
), put
t=i-j
to rewrite it as

\displaystyle\sum_{t,j,\ell}\frac{a^{t}b^{\ell}c^{j}q^{T_{t+j}+T_{j}+T_{\ell}+(t+j){\ell}}}{(q)_{t}(q)_{j}(q)_{\ell}}=\sum_{t,j,\ell}\frac{a^{t}b^{\ell}c^{j}q^{T_{t+j+\ell}+T_{j}}}{(q)_{t}(q)_{j}(q)_{\ell}},

and this proves Theorem 2.

We record two corollaries to Theorem 2:

Corollary 1:

The key-identity (
1.3
) for the generalized Schur’s theorem holds
.

Proof:

In Theorem 2, take
c=ab
. Then, the left-hand side of Theorem 3 is

(4.2)

\displaystyle(-bq)_{\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{a^{i}q^{T_{i}}}{(q)_{i}}=(-aq)_{\infty}(-bq)_{\infty}.

The right-hand side of Theorem 2 is

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{t,j,\ell}\frac{a^{t+j}b^{\ell+j}q^{T_{t+j+\ell}+T_{j}}}{(q)_{t}(q)_{j}(q)_{\ell}}.

Now (
1.3
) follows from (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) with the replacements

\displaystyle t\mapsto\alpha,\quad\ell\mapsto\beta,\quad\text{and}\quad j\mapsto\gamma.

Hence Corollary 1.

Corollary 2:

The Lebesgue identity (
1.1
) holds
.

Proof:

Take
b=0,a=1
in Theorem 2. Then, the left-hand side of Theorem 2 is the left-hand side of (
1.1
). Since
b=0
, the only contribution to the right-hand side of Theorem 2 is from
\ell=0
, interpreting
b^{0}=1
always. So the right-hand side replacing
t\mapsto i
is

\displaystyle\sum_{i.j}\frac{c^{j}q^{T_{i+j}+T_{j}}}{(q)_{i}(q)_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{c^{j}q^{T_{i}+2T_{j}+ij}}{(q)_{i}(q)_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\frac{c^{j}q^{2T_{j}}}{(q)_{j}}\left(\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{q^{T_{i}+ij}}{(q)_{j}}\right)=\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\frac{c^{j}q^{2T_{j}}}{(q)_{j}}(-q^{j+1})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(-q)_{\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\frac{c^{j}q^{2T_{j}}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{j}}=(-q)_{\infty}(-cq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty},

and this yields (
1.1
). Hence Corollary 2.

Remark 1:

6.
A key identity for the Alladi-Schur Theorem

Schur’s partition theorem has always been associated with the modulus 3 or the
modulus 6, the latter because

(6.1)

\displaystyle\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}(1+q^{3m-2})(1+q^{3m-1})=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}\frac{1}{(1-q^{6m-5})(1-q^{6m-1})}.

Sometime during the 90’s, the second author noted that

\displaystyle\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}\frac{1}{(1-q^{6m-5})(1-q^{6m-1})}=\prod^{\infty}_{m=1}(1+q^{2m-1}+q^{4m-2}),

Theorem A:
(Andrews’ refinement of the Alladi-Schur theorem)

\geq 3

k

(6.4)

\displaystyle A(n;k)=s(n;k).

Remark:
What is surprising is that in Theorem A, partitions of the Schur type are classified according to their parity. Thus, the equality (

12

was

For positive integers

\displaystyle\sum^{m}_{j=0}\sum^{n}_{i=0}(bc)^{i}a^{j}q^{2T_{i}+2T_{j}}{m\brack i}_{q}{n\brack j}_{q^{2}}(-cq^{i+1})_{\ell+j}

(7.7)

\displaystyle=\sum^{m+n+\ell}_{N=0}c^{N}q^{T_{N}}\left(\sum^{n}_{j=0}\sum^{m}_{i=0}b^{i}a^{j}q^{T_{i}+2T_{j}}{m\brack i}_{q}{n\brack j}_{q^{2}}{{\ell+j}\brack{N-i}}_{q}\right).

If we let

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\frac{(bc)^{i}ia^{j}q^{2T_{i}+2T_{j}}(-q)_{i}(-cq^{i+1})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{i}(q^{2};q^{2})_{j}}

Identities (

\displaystyle(-aq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(-bq^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(-q)_{\infty}.

But there is a finite version of (

For non-negative integers

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq M_{1},\,j\leq M_{2},\,i+j+k\leq L}a^{i}b^{j}c^{k}q^{2T_{i}+2T_{j}+T_{k}+(i+j)k}{{M_{1}}\brack i}_{q^{2}}{{M_{2}}\brack j}_{q^{2}}{{L-i-j}\brack k}(-q)_{i+j}.

If we sum the inner sum over

\displaystyle\sum_{k}c^{k}q^{T_{k}+(i+j)k}{{L-i-j}\brack k}=(-cq^{i+j+1})_{L-i-j},

REMARK: We have given the intermediate identity (

In closing this section, we note that when we set

in (

We present this in the next section along with a new polynomial version of this infinite hierarchy due to Alamoudi.

Concluding thought:
Two things have been accomplished in this paper: (i) a unification of Schur’s theorem and Lebesgue identity, and (ii) an infinite hierarchy of
q
-hypergeometric identities of which the initial ones are the identities for the partition theorems of Lebesgue and Capparelli. The unification of the Schur and Lebesgue partition theorems was motivated by the fact that the basic idea behind both of their combinatorial proofs was the same - namely, a method of Bressoud suitably adapted to each of the two. Since the combiatorial proof of Capparelli’s theorem in
[
4
]
also uses the technique of Bressoud, it is our desire and hope to produce a unification of all three theorems of Schur, Lebesgue and Capparelli by a common
q
