Tarlov Cyst with Pelvic Extension Mimicking an Adnexal Mass: A Case Report
Authors: Mahsa Karbasi, Alireza Motamedi, Armin Bakhshipour Roudsari, Esmaeil Gharepapagh, Mahsa Malekian, Sahar Rezaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPelvic extension of sacral Tarlov cysts can closely resemble adnexal masses on ultrasound. When cystic pelvic lesions coexist with radicular pain or sitting intolerance, MRI is mandatory prior to gynecologic surgery. Conservative treatment is preferred unless neurological deterioration occurs.Keywords: adnexal mass mimic, magnetic resonance imaging, pelvic extension, perineural cyst, radiculopathy, Tarlov cyst

Introduction

Perineural (Tarlov) cysts are dilatations of the nerve root sheath that typically involve the dorsal root ganglion and contain cerebrospinal fluid [1]. MRI‐based studies report sacral perineural cysts in a substantial minority of imaged populations [2]. A meta‐analysis of imaging studies found a pooled incidence of approximately 4.18% for sacral perineural cysts. Individual studies in that analysis reported prevalences ranging from 1.3% to 13.2% depending on methodology and scanner protocols [2].

Only a minority of sacral Tarlov cysts become symptomatic [3]. Estimates of the symptomatic proportion vary between studies and range from about 1% to over 16% [4]. These differences reflect heterogeneous definitions of symptoms and variability in patient selection [2].

Tarlov cysts arise in structurally vulnerable segments of the nerve root sheath near the dorsal root ganglion. The perineurium in this region is thinner and more distensible [5]. Chronically elevated pulsatile CSF pressure can progressively dilate this segment [4]. Transient CSF pressure increases induced by activities that raise intra‐abdominal pressure, such as prolonged sitting or straining, may further promote cyst enlargement [6]. A ball‐valve mechanism, in which CSF enters the cyst but exits less efficiently, has been described in some cases [1]. Other cysts remain in open communication with the subarachnoid space and can still enlarge under repeated pressure fluctuations [4]. The nerve root traversing the cyst lumen is particularly vulnerable to compression and stretching [7]. Symptomatic Tarlov cysts can present with low back or sacrococcygeal pain [4]. They may also produce radicular leg pain and perineal or pelvic pain [8]. Bladder, bowel, and sexual dysfunction are frequent in symptomatic cohorts [9]. Under‐recognition of these symptoms and a tendency to consider Tarlov cysts incidental contribute to delayed diagnosis [4].

Most Tarlov cysts remain confined to the sacral canal. A subset extends through the anterior sacral foramina into the presacral space or pelvis. In one French MRI cohort, 4.9% of sacral perineural cysts showed presacral or pelvic extension [10]. Such cysts can appear as adnexal masses on ultrasound [8]. Misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary gynecologic surgery when the lesion is neurogenic rather than gynecologic [11]. We present a case of a sacral Tarlov cyst with anterior pelvic extension that initially mimicked an adnexal mass on ultrasound. We discuss the imaging features, differential diagnosis, and management considerations in light of current evidence.

Case History/Examination

A 32‐year‐old woman presented with a 6‐month history of chronic pain involving the lower abdomen, right flank, low back, and right leg. The pain radiated from the lumbosacral region to the posterior aspect of the right thigh and leg, consistent with S2–S3 dermatomal distribution. Symptoms were exacerbated by prolonged sitting and physical activity.

Detailed history‐taking explored associated urinary, bowel, and pelvic symptoms. The patient reported mild urinary urgency and occasional frequency. She denied urinary incontinence, hesitancy, weak stream, or incomplete bladder emptying. There were no bowel disturbances, including constipation, fecal urgency, or incontinence. She denied dyspareunia, vulvar pain, vaginal pain, persistent genital arousal symptoms, or saddle anesthesia.

Her past medical history was significant for a cesarean section performed three years earlier. No prior spinal surgery was reported. She had no chronic systemic diseases and no known drug allergies.

On physical examination, there was localized tenderness over the lower lumbar spinous processes (L4–L5) and the right sacroiliac region. Straight‐leg raise testing was positive on the right at 60°, reproducing radicular pain in the S2–S3 distribution and negative on the left. Motor strength was 5/5 in all lower‐extremity muscle groups. Sensory examination to light touch and pinprick was intact bilaterally. Deep tendon reflexes were symmetric, and gait was normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Given the initial sonographic impression of a complex multiloculated cystic lesion in the right hemipelvis, the differential diagnosis included an adnexal cystic mass (e.g., ovarian cystic neoplasm), hydrosalpinx, peritoneal inclusion cyst, and other non‐gynecologic cystic lesions such as lymphocele or presacral/retroperitoneal cysts. However, the posterolateral location relative to the uterus, the inability to confidently assign the lesion to either ovary, and the coexistence of radicular symptoms raised concern for a non‐adnexal etiology.

Pelvic ultrasound demonstrated a complex multiloculated cystic lesion with internal septations and low‐level echoes, measuring approximately 6 cm in maximal diameter. The lesion was located posterolateral to the uterus on the right and medial to the iliac vessels. Both ovaries were visualized and appeared normal, and the lesion showed no solid components or internal vascularity on color Doppler imaging.

MRI of the abdomen and pelvis was performed for further characterization. Imaging was acquired on a 1.5‐T system and included axial and sagittal T2‐weighted sequences, axial and coronal T1‐weighted sequences, and post‐contrast fat‐suppressed T1‐weighted sequences. Additional lumbar spine sequences (sagittal T1‐weighted, sagittal and axial T2‐weighted, and sagittal STIR) were obtained to assess for alternative spinal causes of radicular symptoms.

Pelvic MRI demonstrated a loculated cystic lesion measuring 20 × 43 × 60 mm in the right hemipelvis, overlying the right piriformis muscle. The lesion showed cerebrospinal fluid–equivalent signal intensity (T1 hypointense and T2/STIR hyperintense) without mural nodularity, restricted diffusion, or internal enhancement after gadolinium administration. Mild scalloping of the adjacent sacral bone was present without cortical destruction (Figures 1, 2). Importantly, the lesion was continuous with the right S2–S3 neural foramen, confirming origin from the nerve root sheath and establishing the diagnosis of a Tarlov (perineural) cyst with anterior pelvic extension (Figures 1, 2). A smaller contralateral perineural cyst measuring 21 × 15 mm was also identified at the left S1–S2 level. The uterus and ovaries were otherwise unremarkable apart from a small functional cyst in the right ovary.

Lumbar spine MRI demonstrated mild levoscoliosis and a small asymmetric L4–L5 disc bulge without significant canal or foraminal stenosis. In the context of the patient's S2–S3 dermatomal symptoms and the demonstrated foraminal continuity of the pelvic lesion, the L4–L5 disc bulge was considered incidental.

Given the absence of progressive neurological deficits and considering the potential morbidity of invasive interventions, conservative management was selected. Treatment consisted of non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs for analgesia, gabapentin for neuropathic pain, and activity modification aimed at avoiding maneuvers associated with transient increases in cerebrospinal fluid pressure (e.g., prolonged sitting and strenuous physical activity). The patient was counseled regarding red‐flag symptoms (worsening radicular pain, new weakness, saddle anesthesia, or bladder/bowel dysfunction) that would warrant urgent reassessment and consideration of surgical or minimally invasive options.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

At 4‐month follow‐up, the patient reported approximately 50% improvement in radicular and pelvic pain with conservative management. No new neurological deficits developed, and urinary symptoms remained stable. She continued treatment with non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs and gabapentin, along with activity modification aimed at reducing transient cerebrospinal fluid pressure increases. Ongoing clinical monitoring was recommended, with instructions to seek immediate reassessment in the event of worsening radicular pain, new motor weakness, saddle anesthesia, or bladder or bowel dysfunction.

This case highlights that sacral Tarlov cysts with anterior pelvic extension can closely mimic adnexal masses on ultrasound. Recognition of associated radicular symptoms and confirmation of neural foraminal continuity on MRI are essential to avoid unnecessary gynecologic intervention. In the absence of progressive neurological compromise, conservative management can provide meaningful symptomatic relief.

Discussion

This case demonstrates that sacral Tarlov cysts with pelvic extension can convincingly mimic adnexal masses on ultrasound. In our patient, the pelvic cyst was initially interpreted as a potential adnexal lesion [8]. The presence of normal ovaries and a lesion located posterolateral to the uterus should prompt consideration of non‐gynecologic etiologies [11].

Kim et al. reported a series in which Tarlov cysts were misdiagnosed as adnexal masses, hydrosalpinx, or ovarian cysts on pelvic sonography and only recognized as perineural cysts after CT or MRI [8]. H'ng et al. described three women whose perineural cysts were initially classified as adnexal lesions on ultrasound [11]. Raza et al. documented that Tarlov cysts may present as complex bilateral adnexal masses on sonography [12]. Zhu et al. detailed three cases of giant presacral Tarlov cysts with pelvic extension that mimicked gynecologic masses [13]. These reports highlight that cross‐sectional imaging is essential when ultrasound findings are atypical for gynecologic lesions. Symptomatic Tarlov cysts frequently produce a combination of axial sacral pain, radicular pain, and pelvic or perineal discomfort [4]. Urogenital and bowel dysfunction are also common. Comprehensive history‐taking is required to identify these manifestations. Hulens et al. showed that detailed questioning can distinguish symptomatic Tarlov cyst patients from those with specific low back pain based on characteristic symptom clusters [9].

The straight‐leg raise test is not a sensitive or specific sign for Tarlov cyst‐related radiculopathy. Serratrice et al. described a postpartum patient with a hemorrhagic S1 Tarlov cyst causing sciatica in whom straight‐leg raise and several other tension signs were negative [14]. Diagnosis should therefore rely more heavily on imaging and detailed symptom analysis than on straight‐leg raise testing alone [5].

The pathophysiology of Tarlov cysts has evolved beyond the original ball‐valve concept. Acosta et al. described a valve‐like communication that allowed CSF inflow but limited outflow [1]. However, more recent work indicates that many cysts are in open communication with the subarachnoid space and that chronic elevations in CSF pulsatile pressure and transient pressure spikes from sitting and straining contribute to cyst behavior [5]. Hulens et al. proposed that idiopathic CSF pressure dysregulation may play a role in Tarlov cyst symptomatology. Large‐ and small‐fiber neuropathy has been documented in a substantial proportion of patients with Tarlov cysts [7].

Kuhn et al. quantified the prevalence of sacral perineural cysts and demonstrated that a minority of these lesions extend into the presacral or pelvic space [10]. Zhu et al. showed that pelvic extension reduces the constraining effect of bone and allows cysts to enlarge into the pelvis. Such cysts may reach large sizes before they become symptomatic [13].

Cyst size is an imperfect predictor of symptoms. Devkota et al. reported a small S1–S2 Tarlov cyst causing significant radiculopathy in an elderly woman [15]. Hulens et al. found that symptom severity does not necessarily increase with cyst diameter [9]. Location relative to the nerve root and intracystic pressure trends may be more important than size alone [6].

Pregnancy has been implicated as a trigger for symptom onset in some Tarlov cyst patients. Serratrice et al. reported delivery‐induced hemorrhage within a Tarlov cyst that produced acute postpartum sciatica [14]. There is no evidence that cesarean section itself directly causes Tarlov cyst formation. It is more likely that increased intra‐abdominal and CSF pressure during pregnancy and delivery can unmask or worsen symptoms in pre‐existing cysts [5].

Management of symptomatic Tarlov cysts remains controversial. Burke et al. reported that microsurgical treatment can improve symptoms in a majority of carefully selected patients but is associated with substantial rates of CSF leak and other complications [16]. Fletcher‐Sandersjöo et al. developed an algorithm that reserves surgery for patients with severe, refractory pain or progressive neurological deficits [3]. Murphy et al. emphasized that many patients in a large referral series benefitted from optimized conservative management without surgery [5]. Conservative management in our patient emphasized avoidance of activities that increase CSF pressure [5]. This approach is supported by observational data showing that sitting and Valsalva‐type maneuvers frequently exacerbate symptoms in Tarlov cyst patients [9]. Muthu and Chandrasekaran reported a case of sacral Tarlov cyst managed successfully with conservative therapy that included tailored activity modification [17]. Minimally invasive procedures such as CT‐guided fibrin glue injection have also shown promise in selected cases. Symptomatic resolution of a lumbar Tarlov cyst followed CT‐guided trans‐sacral fibrin glue injection [18].

The differential diagnosis for pelvic cystic lesions is broad and includes both gynecologic and non‐gynecologic etiologies [19]. Yacoub et al. reviewed cystic lesions in the abdomen and pelvis and stressed the importance of carefully analyzing lesion location, signal characteristics, and enhancement patterns [19]. In our case, the foraminal origin and CSF‐equivalent signal were key clues that the lesion was neurogenic.

This report has several limitations. The diagnosis is based on characteristic MRI features without histopathologic confirmation. The follow‐up period is relatively short. No follow‐up MRI was available at the time of writing, and long‐term cyst behavior in this patient remains unknown. Nonetheless, the case adds to the literature on Tarlov cysts presenting as pelvic masses and provides practical guidance on conservative management.
