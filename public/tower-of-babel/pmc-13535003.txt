Current Advances of Treatments, Therapeutic Targets, Biomarkers, Novel Drugs, and Machine Learning for Hypopharyngeal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Ce Li, Rui Guan, Rui‐Jie Jiao, Zhi‐Jie Bi, Yang Fu, Yin Wang, Chen‐Yang Xu, Ye Qian, Ran Wei, Sheng‐Da Cao, Wen‐Ming Li, Dong‐Min Wei, Da‐Peng Lei

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HSCC) is an aggressive subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma with insidious onset, early metastasis, and dismal prognosis. Conventional multimodal therapy achieves limited survival benefit, highlighting an urgent need for refined precision strategies. This review systematically summarizes the latest progress in HSCC, covering epidemiological characteristics, imaging diagnosis, prognostic prediction, surgical and non‐surgical treatment advances, as well as coding and non‐coding therapeutic targets, biomarkers, and candidate drugs. It further introduces the application of multi‐omics and machine learning in HSCC research, analyzes current challenges and future directions, and provides a theoretical reference for precise diagnosis, individualized treatment, and prognostic evaluation of HSCC.

Epidemiology and Research Significance of Hypopharyngeal Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HSCC)

Epidemiology of HSCC

Hypopharyngeal cancer is a malignancy that originates in the region between the hyoid bone and the lower end of the cricoid cartilage in the head and neck. The vast majority (95%) of hypopharyngeal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas, and this type of cancer accounts for approximately 3% of all head and neck malignancies [1]. HSCC, the most prevalent histological subtype of hypopharyngeal cancer, has one of the poorest prognoses among head and neck malignancies [1]. Most patients with HSCC are diagnosed at an advanced stage upon initial presentation. Recent epidemiological data show an alarming case fatality rate, with a 5‐year survival rate ranging from 25% to 45% [2]. The disease is relatively rare in developed countries and predominantly affects males with a history of heavy smoking and alcohol consumption, while its incidence in females remains low. However, recent epidemiological reports from developed countries indicate a gradual increase in the proportion of women diagnosed with this malignancy. In contrast, developing countries have documented a rising incidence of hypopharyngeal cancer in both sexes, largely due to the increasing prevalence of behaviors such as early initiation of tobacco use, cigarette smoking, excessive alcohol intake, and betel nut chewing [3]. Other documented risk factors include Plummer–Vinson syndrome and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Bile reflux, as a component of GERD, may act as an independent risk factor for HSCC (Grade II clinical evidence) [4]. Unlike oropharyngeal cancer, human papillomavirus (HPV) is not a major etiological factor in HSCC, as demonstrated by its relatively low infection rate. However, in the small subset of HPV‐positive HSCC cases, the virus may be associated with more favorable prognostic features [5]. The hypopharynx lies close to a dense network of lymphatic and vascular structures, which predisposes tumors in this region to early cervical lymph node involvement and distant metastasis. Lymphatic drainage from the hypopharynx primarily involves the jugular lymphatic chain (Levels II–IV) and retropharyngeal lymph nodes [6]. At present, multimodal treatment that combines surgical resection with concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) is considered the standard of care for locally advanced hypopharyngeal cancer [7]. Nevertheless, despite the adoption of this aggressive therapeutic approach, overall survival (OS) rates have remained largely unchanged. Due to its concealed anatomical position, nonspecific early clinical symptoms, and strong tendency for early regional and systemic dissemination, the disease is frequently diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages. This delayed detection contributes to high postoperative recurrence rates, an increased likelihood of metastatic progression, and an overall unfavorable prognosis [8] (Figure 1).

Research Significance of HSCC

HSCC is characterized by the core features of early‐stage concealment, late‐stage high incidence, strong tendency to metastasize, and poor prognosis. The geographical differences in risk factors and population changes influence its epidemiological distribution. Lymphatic metastasis and local recurrence are the key bottlenecks restricting the therapeutic efficacy. There is an urgent need to develop precise diagnostic techniques, optimize treatment strategies, and explore effective molecular targets based on the specific biological characteristics of HSCC, which represent the core directions and clinical requirements of current HSCC research. We systematically retrieved English literature published over the past two decades and included a total of 140 eligible articles in this review. For the first time, it comprehensively delineates HSCC‐related coding gene targets, non‐coding gene targets, clinical biomarkers, candidate drugs, as well as the applications of multi‐omics and machine learning, thereby addressing a critical gap in this research area (Figure 2).

Imaging Diagnosis of HSCC

Terahertz (THz) radiation refers to electromagnetic waves with frequencies in the range between infrared and millimeter waves. Over the past three decades, THz technology has demonstrated considerable promise in medical diagnosis and biomedical applications. Based on substantial evidence supporting its utility, THz radiation detection methods can serve as an important tool for the early identification of HSCC, providing improved precision in delineating the boundaries and infiltration depth of malignant tissues [10]. This technique has emerged as a pivotal potential tool for the early diagnosis of HSCC, leveraging its non‐invasive nature and high resolution to address the limitations of conventional endoscopy and imaging modalities in identifying early‐stage lesions. Positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) can provide valuable information for predicting patient prognosis in HSCC. The combination of SUVmean, ADCmean, and Ktrans derived from 18F‐FDG PET/MRI accurately predicted the preoperative histological grade of HSCC [11]. Contrast‐enhanced CT (CECT) of intratumoral/peritumoral radiomic features enables prediction of lymphovascular invasion (LVI), a key poor‐prognosis marker in HSCC [12].

Prognostic Prediction Models of HSCC

Yu et al. conducted a multivariate analysis of 462 patients with primary HSCC and ipsilateral lymph node metastasis (iLNM), identifying age, tumor subregion, pathological T (pT) stage, ipsilateral maximum lymph node size (MLS), number of metastatic lymph nodes (MLN), and the presence of lymph node necrosis as independent risk factors for central lymph node metastasis (cLNM). These results were used to develop two predictive nomograms that accurately estimate the likelihood of contralateral neck involvement in patients with iLNM‐positive HSCC [13]. The model has pioneered individualized prediction of HSCC lymph node metastasis, yet its clinical generalizability requires validation through large‐sample, multicenter studies. The SEER database was retrospectively queried to extract data from eligible patients diagnosed with HSCC between 2004 and 2015. Among the 391 patients included, the 5‐year cancer‐specific survival (CSS) and OS rates were 44% and 33.7%, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with a lymph node ratio (LNR) ≥ 0.23 had significantly worse CSS and OS compared with those with LNR < 0.23. In addition, postoperative adjuvant therapy (POAT) was identified as an independent protective factor for patients in the LNR ≥ 0.23 group, but it did not confer a significant benefit in the LNR < 0.23 group. These findings highlight a strong association between LNR and prognosis in patients with lymph node‐metastatic HSCC [14]. This finding delineates the pivotal role of LNR in prognostic stratification of HSCC and provides a rationale for the precise selection of postoperative adjuvant therapies. A nomogram model based on lymph node parameters effectively stratifies the prognosis of HSCC patients, identifies individuals suitable for chemotherapy, and provides references for clinical decision‐making while reducing the risk of overtreatment [15]. The current HSCC/LSCC grading system based on WHO classification has limited prognostic accuracy. A novel three‐level scoring system based on tumor budding activity (BA) and cell nest size (CNS) sum scores (highly differentiated [nG1], moderately differentiated [nG2], poorly differentiated [nG3]) demonstrates superior independent prognostic performance for LSCC/HSCC patients compared with the current WHO grading system, reflecting underlying biological differences in SCCS that influence survival [16]. A retrospective analysis of 89 patients was performed, and the platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), derived neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (dNLR), and neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) were calculated based on complete blood count data. Elevated PLR is significantly associated with an increased risk of recurrence and reduced survival in patients with progressive HSCC undergoing radical resection. Furthermore, a high preoperative PLR may serve as an independent predictor of recurrence‐free survival (RFS) [17]. Due to their ease of detection and high reproducibility, inflammatory biomarkers present themselves as promising candidate indicators for prognostic evaluation in HSCC.

Summary

HSCC diagnosis has advanced from single‐modal TNM staging to multi‐omics/imaging integration, with THz radiation a promising tool for early detection and PET/MRI/CECT radiomics enhancing advanced‐stage prognostic stratification. Nomograms and inflammatory biomarkers enable individualized metastasis and survival prediction, but most models lack multicenter validation—limiting clinical generalizability. Future research should focus on cross‐center validation and integration of molecular/imaging/clinical data for unified prognostic models.

Advances in Surgical Treatment for HSCC

Over the past two decades, the role of primary surgery for hypopharyngeal cancer has shifted toward primary nonsurgical treatment using induction or concurrent CRT. The current treatment options for untreated, newly diagnosed hypopharyngeal cancer include surgical resection of the primary tumor and metastatic lymph nodes, radiotherapy, and systemic medical therapies such as conventional chemotherapy and immunotherapy. A multimodal treatment strategy that combines surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy is the preferred curative approach. Best supportive care is offered to patients who are not candidates for curative treatment or who present with distant metastases. A multidisciplinary approach is essential for comprehensive management to achieve optimal oncologic outcomes while preserving or improving function [18]. The primary objective of this transition is to maximize the preservation of swallowing and vocal functions while ensuring tumor control, thereby improving patients’ quality of life (QoL). Surgical intervention serves as a critical remedial or alternative approach for patients who are either non‐responsive to or unsuitable for non‐surgical treatments. Below is a stage‐stratified approach to surgical advances, with clear differentiation of oncologic versus functional goals.

Early‐Stage HSCC (T1–T2N0)

Management of T1–T2 hypopharyngeal cancer has progressed from traditional open surgical techniques, including radical or partial organ‐preserving procedures, to non‐surgical organ preservation strategies such as radiotherapy (RT) or CRT, as well as minimally invasive procedures including transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) and transoral robotic surgery (TORS). Comparable oncological outcomes have been consistently reported across treatment modalities. However, patients receiving RT/CRT or TLM/TORS have exhibited superior functional outcomes [19]. The primary goals are radical tumor resection (oncologic) and maximal functional preservation (functional), with minimal morbidity and improved QoL as key endpoints.

Locally Advanced HSCC (T3–T4/N+)

A retrospective analysis of 137 newly diagnosed HSCC patients at the University of Pittsburgh between 1994 and 2014 showed that most surgical cases presented with advanced tumor stages. Primary surgical treatment was associated with improved survival outcomes compared with non‐surgical approaches [20]. Locally advanced HSCC (cartilage invasion, vocal cord paralysis) was historically considered inoperable for organ preservation; modern open‐neck surgery with precise resection now enables successful tumor removal, with reconstruction critical for functional recovery [21]. By carefully preserving laryngeal function, the paraglottic space approach enables direct visualization and mucosal preservation during surgery, which improves postoperative swallowing function and reduces complication rates. In a large cohort study involving 426 HSCC patients treated with this technique, the OS rate was 52.3%, accompanied by a low incidence of postoperative complications [22]. Primary goals are radical resection and regional control (oncologic) and functional recovery via personalized reconstruction (functional).

Salvage Surgery for HSCC

Salvage surgery is a critical remedial option for HSCC patients with non‐surgical treatment (CRT/immunotherapy) failure or postoperative recurrence. It is tailored to the site of recurrence/residual disease, with the dual goals of salvage oncologic control (oncologic) and preservation of remaining organ function (functional)—though functional outcomes are often limited by prior treatment‐related tissue damage. The most suitable candidates for open‐neck surgery are patients with small‐volume T‐stage tumors and small to medium‐volume neck metastases, although such cases are relatively uncommon. In contrast, more advanced tumors, including those with cartilage invasion, vocal cord paralysis, or those located in the piriform apex and posterior ring area, which were previously considered unsuitable for open‐neck organ‐preserving surgery, can now be successfully resected and reconstructed. This approach reduces morbidity and improves QoL. At present, most reports on the surgical management of hypopharyngeal cancer originate from Asia, particularly South Korea, Japan, and China. For certain patients who are either unsuitable for or unwilling to undergo current nonsurgical treatments, laryngeal‐preserving open‐neck surgery may serve as a more appropriate alternative to less intensive or modified chemotherapy or bioradiotherapy regimens, potentially improving both survival and QoL [21]. Historically, treatment for operable hypopharyngeal cancer has involved total laryngectomy, and occasionally partial laryngectomy, combined with partial or circumferential pharyngeal resection, often followed by reconstruction and postoperative radiotherapy. Efforts to preserve speech and swallowing function in hypopharyngeal and laryngeal cancer surgery have reduced reliance on total laryngopharyngectomy and contributed to significant advances in reconstructive techniques, including microvascular free tissue transfer [23].

Surgical Reconstruction for HSCC

The clinical value of function‐preserving surgical techniques in the treatment of HSCC has been confirmed. The pectoralis major myocutaneous flap is commonly used in pharyngeal reconstruction. Aslan successfully performed tumor resection through partial pharyngectomy and reconstructed the hypopharyngeal defect using a rolling supraclavicular flap with an end‐to‐side technique. Selected cases involving the piriform sinus achieved favorable oncologic and functional outcomes following partial laryngectomy and supraclavicular pharyngeal reconstruction with a supraclavicular flap [24]. Microvascular free tissue transfer has further advanced reconstruction, expanding the scope of organ‐preserving surgery for advanced HSCC and reducing postoperative fistula/stenosis rates [23]. All reconstruction strategies prioritize functional recovery (swallowing/vocal/airway) and complication reduction, with QoL as a primary goal. Innovations in reconstruction techniques have further expanded the scope of application for open surgeries and improved the success rate of organ‐preserving surgeries.

Summary

The surgical treatment of HSCC has established a core framework characterized by staging and stratification, prioritizing minimally invasive procedures, and emphasizing both radical resection and functional preservation. For early‐stage cases, minimally invasive surgery and organ‐preserving strategies are the preferred options. For locally advanced cases, functional preservation is achieved through precise resection combined with personalized reconstruction. Salvage surgery provides a remedial solution for patients with treatment failure. The innovation of reconstruction techniques and the improvement of postoperative rehabilitation have further enhanced the functional value of the surgery. In the future, attention should be focused on long‐term verification of the oncological efficacy of minimally invasive surgery and standardized procedures for complex defect repair.

Early‐Stage HSCC (T1–T2N0): Radical Radiotherapy

A research cohort from 2008 to 2023 showed that the 5‐year survival rate for early‐stage hypopharyngeal cancer patients who received radical radiotherapy can reach 80% [25]. According to the Chinese Guidelines for Radiation Therapy of Hypopharyngeal Cancer (2021 edition), radiation therapy is recommended as the preferred treatment for T1 and T2N0 lesions, particularly for exophytic tumors. A retrospective analysis using the national cancer database identified HSCC patients who received definitive radiotherapy, and outcomes were compared between intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and other radiotherapy techniques. The influence of clinicopathological variables, radiotherapy modality, and dose on OS was assessed using the log‐rank test and Cox proportional hazards model. IMRT demonstrated significantly improved OS (p = 0.013). For HSCC patients treated with definitive radiotherapy, IMRT may provide substantial survival benefits compared with non‐IMRT modalities [26].

Locally Advanced HSCC (T3–T4/N+): Surgery ± Adjuvant Therapy versus Definitive CRT

According to the Chinese Guidelines for Radiation Therapy of Hypopharyngeal Cancer (2021 edition), preoperative radiation therapy may be considered for patients with T3 or T4N0‐1 lesions who undergo surgery. Postoperative radiation therapy is essential for patients with positive surgical margins, residual lymph nodes larger than 6 cm in diameter, extensive lymph node metastasis, extranodal extension, or peripheral perineural invasion (PNI) following neck dissection. For lymph nodes larger than 3 cm with a hard and fixed texture or skin invasion, the regional control rate achieved by radiotherapy alone is suboptimal. Therefore, preoperative radiotherapy followed by surgery should be the primary treatment approach. Inoperable patients may receive palliative radiotherapy. A small proportion of patients whose tumors shrink after radiotherapy may subsequently undergo surgical resection. Patients with postoperative recurrence are typically treated with palliative radiotherapy. For poorly differentiated or undifferentiated cancers, regardless of disease stage, radiotherapy should be the first‐line treatment. If residual disease persists after radiotherapy, surgical resection may be considered. To evaluate OS following surgical intervention versus CRT in locally advanced hypopharyngeal cancer, a study used data from the SEER database (2010–2015) for patients diagnosed with hypopharyngeal cancer (T2–T4aM0). These patients either underwent total pharyngectomy combined with lymph node dissection or received definitive CRT. Subgroup analysis based on T classification (T2–3 or T4a) was included in multivariate analyses. The findings show that the OS rate of CRT in patients with locally advanced HSCC is comparable to that of surgery. CRT may also serve as a viable alternative for patients with T4a tumors, as it may preserve pharyngeal function [27]. From 2004 to 2018, data were collected from 2574 patients in the SEER database, along with 66 patients treated at their center between 2013 and 2022 with T3–T4 or N + HNSCC. The results showed that patients receiving surgery combined with POAT had significantly higher 3‐year OS (52.9% vs. 44.4%, p < 0.01) and 3‐year CSS (58.7% vs. 51.5%, p < 0.01) compared with those receiving concurrent CRT [28]. Another study evaluated 218 stage IVa‐b HNSCC patients who underwent initial surgical treatment with negative margins. Independent risk factors for recurrence were identified, and survival outcomes were compared according to recurrence risk and the use of POAT. Significant improvements in RFS and OS were observed only in high‐risk patients receiving POAT (p = 0.003 and p = 0.018, respectively). Postoperative CRT demonstrated significantly better RFS (p = 0.035) and OS (p = 0.048) than postoperative radiotherapy alone. Thus, high‐risk stage IVa‐b HNSCC patients should receive POAT, with postoperative CRT providing superior outcomes. For low‐risk patients, regular follow‐up after tumor resection is sufficient [29]. Chemotherapy has been used as a larynx‐preserving strategy for more than two decades. A retrospective study evaluated the efficacy of paclitaxel, cisplatin, and 5‐fluorouracil (TPF regimen) in Chinese patients with hypopharyngeal cancer who chose organ‐preserving approaches. Induction chemotherapy was administered to 170 patients with locally advanced hypopharyngeal carcinoma. Among them, 107 patients (63%) who achieved complete or partial responses underwent laryngeal preservation, while radical surgery was performed in the remaining 63 non‐responsive patients (37%). The findings suggest that induction chemotherapy with the TPF regimen followed by radiotherapy may serve as an effective larynx‐preserving therapy for patients who achieve complete remission. However, it may be less effective for those achieving only partial remission [30]. Adjuvant chemotherapy did not provide survival benefits in patients with HPV‐positive tumors undergoing primary radiation therapy [5].

Immunotherapy/Targeted Therapy for HSCC

Immunotherapy and targeted therapy have brought new treatment opportunities for advanced/recurrent metastatic HSCC and have also become the current core research hotspot. With the development of immunotherapy and targeted therapies, several clinical trials of human anti‐programmed death receptor 1 (PD‐1) antibodies, including Pembrolizumab [31, 32], Nivolumab [33], Avelumab [34], and EGFR antibody Cetuximab [35], have demonstrated the efficacy of PD‐1 and EGFR inhibitors in recurrent or metastatic HNSCC. However, when combined with concurrent radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the addition of immunotherapy or targeted drugs has not significantly improved treatment outcomes. Further research is therefore required to identify the optimal patient population for immunotherapy and targeted therapy in advanced HNSCC. The induced anti‐PD‐1 antibody Toripalimab combined with chemotherapy has shown encouraging activity, promising laryngeal preservation rates, and acceptable toxicity in patients with locally advanced laryngeal and hypopharyngeal cancers [36]. Camrelizumab combined with the TPF regimen as induction chemotherapy and immunotherapy demonstrates a high objective response rate in locally advanced HNSCC, along with an acceptable safety profile [37].

Although standardized treatment protocols recommended by current guidelines enable long‐term survival in some patients with HSCC, the overall 5‐year survival rate has remained unchanged for decades. This stagnation is largely attributed to the inadequate control of local recurrence and distant metastasis. Perioperative immunotherapy represents a crucial development direction in the treatment of locally advanced HSCC. Large‐scale randomized Phase III clinical trials evaluating the clinical benefits of perioperative immunotherapy in locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (LA‐HNSCC) are expected to drive the transition of perioperative immunotherapy for HSCC from exploratory strategies to established standard‐of‐care regimens. KEYNOTE‐689 implemented a comprehensive perioperative treatment strategy by administering pembrolizumab both before and after surgery, along with chemotherapy and radiotherapy tailored to pathological risk factors. In contrast, NIVOPOSTOP employed a focused adjuvant approach, delivering nivolumab combined with postoperative CRT exclusively to high‐risk patients. Despite differences in design, both trials demonstrated significant improvements in event‐free survival (KEYNOTE‐689: hazard ratio 0.73; 95% confidence interval [0.58–0.92]) and disease‐free survival (NIVOPOSTOP: hazard ratio 0.76; 95% confidence interval [0.60–0.98]). KEYNOTE‐689 showed a reduction in distant recurrence, while NIVOPOSTOP improved local‐regional control. Although OS data remain immature, both trials revealed favorable trends [38]. Summary

We have summarized HSCC treatment framework by stage (Table 4). In summary, the radiotherapy and drug treatment of HSCC have entered an era of precision and combination. The core trend is to select individualized treatment plans based on tumor staging, pathological characteristics, and molecular markers (such as HPV status). The optimized combination of immunotherapy with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, as well as the screening of the advantageous population, is the core breakthrough points for improving the efficacy of advanced HSCC in the future. However, it should be carefully pointed out that most of the Phase III clinical trials supporting the efficacy of immunotherapy in HNSCC (such as KEYNOTE‐048 and CheckMate‐141) mainly included patients with oral cancer and oropharyngeal cancer, with a relatively low proportion of HSCC patients. The unique biological behavior of HSCC (such as stronger local invasiveness and lymphatic metastasis tendency) may affect its response to immunotherapy.

Advances in the Regulation of Proliferation and Metastasis of Coding Gene Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

The regulation of proliferation and metastasis in HSCC primarily focuses on core signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, NF‐κB, and Wnt/β‐catenin. PCDH20 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HSCC cells by suppressing the downstream Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. Low PCDH20 expression served as an independent prognostic factor for OS in HSCC patients [39]. Decreased CDH1 expression was strongly associated with HSCC metastasis and was accompanied by STAT3 phosphorylation and MMP‐9 upregulation. Silencing CDH1 enhances MMP‐9 levels and promotes the invasion and metastasis of hypopharyngeal cancer [40]. S100A11 expression was markedly upregulated in HSCC tissues and contributes to HSCC proliferation and metastasis through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [41]. Mcl‐1 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in HSCC tissues, and this elevation correlated with tumor size and clinical stage [42]. GRK6 mRNA and protein levels were notably reduced in HSCC compared with adjacent normal tissues due to abnormal promoter methylation. Aberrant methylation of the GRK6 promoter may underlie its downregulation and play a critical role in promoting metastasis [43]. UBE2L3 has been identified as a negative regulator of miR‐378a‐5p, leading to increased IκBα degradation and subsequent activation of the NF‐κB signaling pathway, thereby contributing to the progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and HSCC [44]. Myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) expression was upregulated in hypopharyngeal tumor tissues compared with healthy tissues. A significant positive correlation was observed between high MLCK expression and poor 5‐year survival rates. MLCK knockdown reduced the migration and invasion capabilities of FaDu cells [45]. Ye et al. demonstrated that ALKBH5 suppresses cell proliferation in HSCC cells through m6A‐dependent regulation of human Toll‐like receptor 2 (TLR2). ALKBH5 decreases the m6A modification level of TLR2 mRNA, which was subsequently recognized by the m6A reader proteins IGF2BP2 and YTHDF1. IGF2BP2 enhances TLR2 mRNA stability, whereas YTHDF1 promotes its translation [46].

Advances in Lymph Node Metastasis Regulation of Coding Gene Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

Lymph node metastasis is a core biological characteristic of HSCC and a crucial factor influencing patients’ prognosis. These types of targets mainly exert their effects by regulating signaling pathways such as Notch1, HIF‐1α, and ATM, and are closely associated with the malignant phenotype of tumors. Annexin A1 (ANXA1) expression was significantly downregulated in HSCC with lymph node metastasis. The mRNA expression of ANXA1 showed a positive correlation with Yap1 expression. Lymph node metastasis in HSCC may be suppressed by regulating Yap1 expression [47]. O‐GlcNAcylation was markedly upregulated in HSCC and demonstrated a positive correlation with tumor size and lymph node metastasis. In vitro and in vivo experiments showed a positive correlation between Nrf2 protein levels and O‐GlcNAcylation [48]. Adenosine 3‐phosphate‐dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1) was overexpressed in HSCC, and its expression was positively associated with poor prognosis and distant metastasis. The PDK1/Notch1 axis plays a critical role in HSCC proliferation and migration [49]. Increased BTF3 expression correlated positively with lymph node metastasis in HSCC. In vivo and mechanistic studies indicate that BTF3 can significantly enhance cell proliferation, promote cell cycle progression, and inhibit apoptosis through the ATM signaling pathway, thereby facilitating HSCC development and progression [50]. Notch1 and EGFR expression were upregulated in HSCC tissues compared with adjacent non‐cancerous tissues. Notch1 knockdown inhibits HSCC cell migration and invasion in vitro by reducing EGFR expression at the transcriptional level. Notch1 expression was positively correlated with tumor differentiation and distant metastasis, and negatively correlated with patient survival [51]. Fascin‐1 (FSCN1) was overexpressed in HSCC and closely associated with lymph node metastasis. Fascin‐1 enhances invasion and migration by upregulating HIF‐1α in HSCC cells [52]. PAFAH1B3 was overexpressed in HSCC tissues, and high PAFAH1B3 expression levels were strongly associated with cervical lymph node metastasis. PAFAH1B3 promotes cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis by accelerating cell cycle progression [53].

Advances in the Regulation of Cell Cycle and Apoptosis by Coding Gene Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

Cell cycle dysregulation and apoptosis inhibition constitute the core molecular basis for the proliferation of HSCC cells. These types of targets primarily regulate the cell cycle and apoptosis through pathways such as the P38 MAPK, Akt, and Caspase families. HSCC tissues display elevated expression levels of Ras‐related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1). Silencing Rac1 induces cell cycle arrest and suppresses HSCC growth and migration through the P38 MAPK signaling pathway [54]. Livin, a member of the human inhibitor of apoptosis protein family, was overexpressed in HSCC tissues compared with adjacent normal mucosa. Downregulation of Livin markedly reduces the proliferation of human HSCC cells and induces G1 phase arrest. Livin may enhance the tumorigenicity of HSCC by regulating mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation and Akt signaling pathways [55]. S100A4 expression was also elevated in HSCC and positively correlated with cervical lymph node metastasis. Increased nuclear expression of S100A4 serves as an independent prognostic indicator for patients with HSCC. S100A4 knockdown significantly suppresses the migration and invasion of FaDu cells and increases the proportion of G0/G1 phase cells, while the proliferation and apoptosis of FaDu cells remain unaffected [56]. STK33 was found to be upregulated in HSCC. Downregulation of STK33 may inhibit proliferation, induce apoptosis, and reduce clonogenic capacity, migration, and invasion. STK33 downregulation increases the expression of Caspase‐3, Nm‐23‐H1, and E‐Cadherin, and decreases the expression of Bcl‐2, Ki‐67, and Vimentin. These results indicate that STK33 plays an important role in promoting the occurrence and development of HSC [57]. Furthermore, downregulation of STK33 has been shown to inhibit the proliferation and metastasis of FaDu cells in nude mice. An interaction between STK33 and intracellular Ca(2+) signaling also exists in FaDu cells [58].

Advances in EMT Process Regulation of Coding Genes Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

EMT is the core biological process by which HSCC cells acquire the ability for invasion and metastasis. These targets mainly regulate the expression of EMT‐related proteins through pathways such as AKT/mTOR, YAP, and WNT. The inflammatory state triggers overexpression of LGR5 in HSCC, and LGR5 enhances the expression of EMT‐related proteins by inhibiting YAP phosphorylation [59]. As a tumor suppressor gene, RBM24 regulates Twist1, thereby suppressing lymph node metastasis and inhibiting EMT in HSCC [60]. TIPE2 was significantly downregulated in HSCC tissues, and low TIPE2 expression may be associated with poor prognosis in HSCC patients. TIPE2 inhibits tumor growth by modulating the EMT process and activating exogenous apoptotic pathways [61]. PD‐L1 (CD274) serves as an important biomarker for predicting poor prognosis in HSCC patients. PD‐L1 promotes HSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, thereby increasing HSCC malignancy. PD‐L1 may also induce EMT through the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [62]. Elevated ADAM10 expression promotes HSCC proliferation, migration, and EMT progression [63]. Stathmin1 (STMN1), a microtubule‐destabilizing phosphoprotein, plays a crucial role in regulating cellular microtubule dynamics and controlling mitotic progression. High STMN1 expression correlated with low survival rates in HSCC patients, suggesting STMN1 plays a critical role in HSCC proliferation, migration, and EMT processes [64].

Summary

In summary, HSCC‐encoding gene targets are predominantly associated with the PI3K/Akt, NF‐κB, Notch1, and AKT/mTOR core signaling pathways. Among these, STK33, Notch1, and PD‐L1 demonstrate dual significance in both prognostic evaluation and targeted therapy, representing priority directions for clinical translation. However, notable limitations persist in this field: nearly all mechanistic studies have been performed only in FaDu cells, lacking validation in clinically representative cell lines and primary cell models. Consequently, the reliability of translating in vitro findings to clinical applications remains uncertain. Future research should focus on combination therapies involving pathway inhibitors and conduct HSCC‐specific clinical trials.

Advances in lncRNA Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

LncRNA primarily regulates the expression of downstream miRNA/mRNA through the ceRNA sponge mechanism, thereby participating in biological processes such as proliferation, metastasis, and EMT in HSCC. Qian et al. demonstrated that the upregulation of UCA1 promotes proliferation, invasion, and metastasis in HSCC potentially by inhibiting apoptosis and accelerating cell cycle progression. These findings indicate that UCA1 may serve as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HSCC [65]. The expression level of HOXA11‐AS (also named HOXA11‐AS1) was significantly elevated in HSCC tumors and was positively correlated with lymph node metastasis. HOXA11‐AS may function as a molecular sponge for miR‐155 and thereby facilitate the proliferation and metastasis of HSCC [66]. Knockout of RP11‐156L14.1 suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HSCC cells, suggesting that the RP11‐156L14.1/miR‐548‐SSR1 axis could be a potential therapeutic target and prognostic marker for HSCC [67]. Silencing of LncSNHG1 inhibited the malignant progression of HSCC by upregulating PARP6, and the XRCC6/β‐catenin axis may represent the downstream pathway of the SNHG1/PARP6 axis in HSCC [68]. LEF1‐AS1 acts as a competitive endogenous RNA by binding to miR‐221‐5p, regulating GJA1 expression, and promoting tumor progression and the EMT process [69]. Knockdown of MALAT1 inhibited HSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by targeting miR‐194 and induced cell apoptosis [70]. MALAT1 was upregulated in HSCC, whereas miR‐429 expression was downregulated. Inhibition of MALAT1 reduces ZEB1 expression by acting as a miR‐429 sponge and thereby suppresses HSCC progression [71]. Upregulation of lncRNA PEG10 in HSCC tissues enhanced tumorigenic activity, proliferation, and migration [72]. The expression level of AB209630 in HSCC tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues. AB209630 inhibits the growth, metastasis, and invasion of HSCC cells and induces apoptosis, demonstrating a tumor‐suppressive effect in HSCC. These findings suggest that AB209630 may serve as a promising prognostic marker for HSCC [73].

Advances in circRNA Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

Due to its circular structure, high stability, and ceRNA‐mediated function, circRNA has emerged as a potential novel target for HSCC. Its advantages lie in high stability and tissue specificity, making it more suitable as a clinical diagnostic marker and a target for targeted therapy. In recent studies, circRNA expression profiles were generated using circRNA microarrays from four pairs of HSCC tissues and adjacent normal tissue samples. A total of 2392 circRNAs, including 1304 upregulated and 1088 downregulated circRNAs, exhibited significant differences in HSCC tissues compared with normal tissues. These circRNAs were able to act as miRNA sponges within the predicted circRNA–miRNA–mRNA network, as confirmed by real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) [74]. Li et al. demonstrated that circ_0058106 promotes tumor proliferation, metastasis, and invasion in HSCC cells during the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition by positively regulating the Wnt2b/β‐catenin/c‐Myc pathway through sponging of miR‐185‐3p [75]. Wang et al. found that silencing circMATR3 significantly inhibits the proliferation and cell cycle progression of FaDu cells and induces apoptosis [76]. Additionally, metformin has been shown to block tumor growth in vivo through the circ_0003214–miR‐489‐3p–ADAM10 axis [77]. Lin et al. demonstrated that RAB3B in extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) serves as a unique encoding protein that promotes cisplatin resistance in HSCC by inducing autophagy. They further proposed that eccDNA may significantly contribute to cisplatin resistance through amplification of functionally relevant genes [78].

Advances in microRNA Therapeutic Targets in HSCC

As endogenous small non‐coding RNAs, miRNAs primarily function by targeting and degrading mRNAs or inhibiting their translation. In HSCC, miRNAs exhibit both oncogenic and tumor‐suppressive modes of action. MiR‐107 was shown to suppress HSCC cell proliferation and migration and to enhance apoptosis by targeting NOB1 through regulation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, indicating that the miR‐107/NOB1 axis plays an important role in HSCC progression [79]. In contrast, miR‐211‐3p enhanced HSCC progression, elevated CSF2/CCL20/TNF signaling, and increased resistance to induction chemotherapy [80]. MiR‐204 was downregulated in HSCC tissues, and its low expression correlated with significantly worse OS times. Overexpression of miR‐204 markedly inhibits cell proliferation and reduces the number of S‐phase cells, while downregulating CyclinD1, CDK4, and CDK6 expression and upregulating p21 in FaDu cells [81]. MiR‐15a acted as a tumor suppressor in HPV‐positive HSCC, and its downregulation impedes apoptosis [82]. MiR‐140‐5p was significantly downregulated in HSCC tissues and correlated with T classification and lymph node metastasis in HSCC. It inhibited HSCC migration and invasion by disrupting the Adam10‐mediated Notch1 signaling pathway [83]. MiR‐451a was also significantly downregulated in HSCC, and its overexpression markedly suppresses cancer cell migration and invasion. ESDN/DCBD2, which encodes endothelial and smooth muscle cell‐derived neuropeptide‐like molecules, was identified as a direct target of miR‐451a regulation [84]. MiR‐504 was significantly downregulated in HSCC tissues, and its downregulation promotes HSCC cell proliferation by targeting CDK6 [85]. Additionally, miR‐489 suppressed tumor growth in HSCC cell lines, with PTPN11, encoding a cytoplasmic protein tyrosine phosphatase, identified as a potential target gene of miR‐489 [86].

Non‐coding RNAs form a regulatory network centered around the PI3K/Akt and Notch1 pathways through the ceRNA mechanism, and they coordinately regulate the progression of HSCC with coding genes. Due to their high stability and strong tissue specificity, circRNAs are the preferred targets for non‐invasive diagnosis and targeted therapy; the miRNA regulatory axis has both diagnostic and therapeutic value. In the future, it is necessary to develop targeted drugs and combination therapy regimens based on non‐coding RNAs.

Summary

Non‐coding RNAs establish a regulatory network centered on PI3K/Akt and Notch1, coordinating with coding genes to modulate the progression of HSCC. The stability and specificity advantages of circular RNAs (circRNAs) render them a promising option for non‐invasive diagnosis and targeted therapy, while miRNA regulatory axes hold significant potential in both diagnostic and therapeutic applications. However, all existing studies have been confined to the FaDu cell line, lacking validation across multiple cell lines, primary cells, and clinical samples. Future research should prioritize the development of targeted non‐coding RNA pharmaceuticals and combinatorial treatment strategies.

Advances in Biomarkers in HSCC

Advances in biomarkers in HSCC are summarized as follows (Figure 3). We divide them into two categories [1]: prognosis biomarkers in HSCC and [2] lymph node metastasis biomarkers in HSCC.

Advances in biomarkers of HSCC.

Advances in Prognosis Biomarkers in HSCC

The prognostic biomarkers of HSCC encompass multiple categories, such as metabolism‐related genes, autophagy‐related molecules, inflammation‐related indicators, non‐coding RNAs, and epigenetic markers. Tumor molecular markers play an important role in evaluating the prognosis of HSCC. Recent research, both domestic and international, has highlighted increasing interest in these markers. Analysis using the NCounter Metabolic Pathways Panel showed that CD276, LDHB, SLC3A2, EGFR, SLC7A5, and HPRT1 were upregulated and may serve as unfavorable prognostic markers in HSCC. In contrast, EEA1, IDO1, NCOA2, REST, CCL19, and ALDH2 were downregulated and may act as favorable prognostic markers in HSCC [87]. The expression of LC3B in HSCC correlated with immune cell infiltration, suggesting that changes in autophagy activity might influence cellular immunity in HSCC. Ratios such as tCD8+/CD39+ and tCD8+/CD163+ may serve as prognostic indicators for HSCC [88]. Survival analysis showed that high SERPINE1 and SPP1 expression levels were associated with worse prognosis in HSCC patients [89]. Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that low AIM2 expression correlated with poorer prognosis in HSCC patients. Cox proportional hazard regression analysis confirmed that low AIM2 expression significantly predicts poor prognosis in HSCC. Multivariate analysis further suggested that AIM2 expression levels could serve as an independent prognostic factor. In addition, AIM2 and p‐STAT3 expression detected by IHC could predict tumor progression and independence in HSCC [90]. Multivariate analysis also demonstrated that low GRP78/BiP expression was an independent prognostic factor for poor OS and progression‐free survival in HSCC patients [91]. Plasma samples from HSCC patients and healthy controls were analyzed using two‐dimensional differential gel electrophoresis (2D‐DIGE). Differentially expressed proteins were identified with MALDI‐TOF/TOF MS, yielding 26 proteins with altered expression. Alpha‐2‐Heremans‐Schmid glycoprotein (AHSG) protein levels were validated by Western blotting and ELISA, indicating that AHSG may be a potential biomarker for HSCC [92]. The methylation rate of DAPK1 in HSCC tissues was significantly higher than in adjacent normal tissues, and its expression was markedly downregulated. These changes correlated with advanced TNM stage and poor survival outcomes in HSCC [93]. Furthermore, Beclin‐1 and LC3‐II were downregulated in HSCC, and their abnormal expression was associated with poor prognosis. Multivariate analysis indicated that Beclin‐1 functions as an independent prognostic factor for OS in HSCC [94]. CircMORC3 showed significant downregulation in both HSCC tissues and plasma, suggesting that it may serve as a potential biomarker for HSCC [95]. Moreover, elevated expression of miR‐200a‐3p, miR‐4451, and miR‐30b‐5p was significantly associated with reduced OS and disease‐specific survival in HSCC patients compared with those showing lower expression levels [96].

Advances in Lymph Node Metastasis Biomarkers in HSCC

Previous literature has reported lymphatic metastasis markers in HNSCC [97], but there is no relevant review and summary for HSCC. Lymph node metastasis is a key adverse prognostic factor for HSCC, and its biomarkers encompass multiple categories, including membrane proteins, transcription factors, non‐coding RNAs, and kinases. Caveolin‐1 (CAV1) expression was significantly higher in HSCC tissues than in adjacent non‐cancerous tissues, and its expression was closely associated with tumor differentiation and lymph node metastasis [98]. Claudin‐1 and cyclin B1 were overexpressed in HSCC tissues. Claudin‐1 expression positively correlated with tumor differentiation and lymph node metastasis, while cyclin B1 expression was associated with cancer differentiation [99]. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that SIRT1 protein expression was downregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal mucosa, and its expression was inversely correlated with clinical stage and lymph node metastasis [100]. Programmed death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) was overexpressed in HSCC tissues, accompanied by extensive infiltration of PD‐1 + T lymphocytes. Combined detection of PD‐1, PD‐L1, and APE1 expression levels may provide predictive value for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in HSCC [101]. The mRNA transcription levels of PHLPP1 and PHLPP2 were significantly reduced in tumor samples compared with adjacent non‐tumor mucosa. Their expression levels were associated with clinical stage, degree of differentiation, and cervical lymph node metastasis, and higher expression correlated positively with better OS in HSCC patients [102]. RP11‐169D4.1‐001 expression was markedly lower in HSCC tissues than in adjacent normal tissues, and it correlated positively with T‐classification and lymph node metastasis. Higher RP11‐169D4.1‐001 expression levels were associated with shorter OS [103]. MiR‐21 expression was significantly upregulated in HSCC tissues, and this increase was closely associated with T‐classification and lymph node metastasis. MiR‐21 levels also showed a negative correlation with PTEN expression [104].

Biomarkers for HSCC have covered three major scenarios: prognosis assessment, metastasis prediction, and treatment response prediction. Plasma biomarkers and non‐coding RNA biomarkers hold promising clinical application prospects due to their non‐invasiveness and high specificity. In the future, multi‐center and large‐sample studies are required to construct a “multi‐dimensional biomarker combination model” to achieve precise assessment across the entire process from diagnosis to prognosis.

Summary

The biomarkers of HSCC have covered prognosis assessment, metastasis prediction, and treatment response prediction. The plasma/liquid biopsy markers (AHSG, circMORC3, miRNA signature) have the highest clinical translation potential, while the tissue markers (CAV1, AIM2, Beclin‐1) can serve as practical companion diagnostic tools. The cross‐study consistency is relatively high among the core biomarkers, but multi‐center external validation is the main obstacle for clinical adoption. Current biomarker research of HSCC predominantly relies on single‐center studies, small sample sizes, and unimodal biomarker detection, lacking cross‐validation and clinical translational feasibility assessment. Future efforts should focus on conducting large‐scale, multicenter studies to develop integrated multidimensional biomarker models, enabling precise end‐to‐end evaluation from diagnosis to prognosis. In the future, multi‐center studies should be conducted to build a multi‐dimensional biomarker combination model of “plasma + tissue + clinical data” to achieve precise stratification of HSCC from diagnosis to prognosis.

Advances in Therapeutic Drugs of HSCC

The therapeutic drugs for HSCC fall into multiple categories, including natural products, chemically synthesized drugs, and repurposed old drugs. Their mechanisms of action mainly involve inducing apoptosis, inhibiting proliferation, and suppressing metastasis. Solamargine downregulated HOXA11‐AS expression and induced apoptosis by suppressing c‐Myc in HSCC cells, providing evidence for its anti‐cancer effects on HSCC [105]. Costunolide induced apoptosis in HSCC cells and enhanced cisplatin‐induced cytotoxicity through increased production of reactive oxygen species [106]. Patients with diabetic hypopharyngeal cancer who received metformin showed significantly higher OS rates compared with untreated individuals. Metformin therapy modulated the p38/JNK pathway, leading to downregulation of Cyclin D1 and Bcl‐2 expression. It also activated the AMPKα/MEK/ERK pathways, which reduced phosphorylation at the p27 (Thr198) site and decreased mTOR phosphorylation in HSCC cells. These effects contributed to G1 cell cycle arrest, induction of autophagy, and subsequent apoptosis [107]. Nobiletin (NOB) (5,6,7,8,3’,4′‐Hexamethoxyflavone), a polymethoxyflavone, inhibited the proliferation and migration of several cancer cell types. At concentrations of 15 and 40 µM, NOB reduced cell proliferation by approximately 40%. At 50 µM, NOB induced apoptosis and suppressed migration by downregulating extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK), protein kinase B (Akt), and phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K). These findings indicated that its effects on FaDu cells were primarily due to inhibition of these protein kinases [108]. A novel platinum (IV) complex, veratricplatin, was synthesized as a potential COX‐2 inhibitor. Veratricplatin demonstrated significantly greater cytotoxicity than platinum (II), veratric acid monotherapy, or their combination. The synthesized prodrug exhibited reduced toxicity toward normal cells (MRC‐5) while inducing apoptosis through markedly increased DNA damage in FaDu cells. In vivo studies confirmed strong anti‐tumor activity of veratricplatin without evident toxicity in BALB/c nude mice bearing FaDu tumors. Tissue immunofluorescence analysis also showed that veratricplatin significantly inhibited tumor blood vessel formation [109]. The alkaloid colchicine has been widely used for treating inflammatory conditions such as gout, primarily by inhibiting the chemotaxis and phagocytic activity of neutrophils. By downregulating MMP9, the uPA system, and the FAK/SRC complex, colchicine effectively impaired the proliferation, invasion, migration, and adhesion abilities of FaDu cells [110].

Advances in Chemosensitizers and Radiosensitizers of HSCC

Radiochemotherapy resistance represents a central bottleneck in the clinical treatment of HSCC. Radiochemotherapy sensitizers exert their effects by reversing drug resistance and enhancing the cytotoxicity of radiochemotherapy. Their mechanisms of action primarily involve inhibiting autophagy, regulating apoptotic pathways, and targeting core oncogenic pathways. Moreover, most of them are small‐molecule inhibitors, which possess high specificity and significant clinical translation value. Down‐regulation of GPR81 combined with cisplatin treatment increased the expression of the mitochondrial outer membrane translocation enzyme 20 (TOMM20) and caspase‐3, while decreasing Bcl‐2 expression. These findings suggest that GPR81 plays an important role in modulating cisplatin sensitivity in HSCC [111]. Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor γ (PPARG) has been shown to correlate with chemotherapy sensitivity in HSCC tumor cells. Up‐regulation of PPARG may enhance the sensitivity of HSCC cells to chemotherapy for HSCC [112]. Enhanced autophagy was a critical determinant of cisplatin resistance in HSCC cells. Treatment with 3‐methyladenine significantly reduced autophagy levels, mediated cell cycle arrest, promoted apoptosis, and reversed cisplatin resistance by inhibiting Beclin‐1 expression [113]. Inhibition of PHF20 promoted apoptosis and increased cisplatin sensitivity through the OCT4/pSTAT3/MCL1 signaling pathway in HSCC [114]. Both GDC‐0980 and Refametinib showed substantial antitumor activity in HSCC cells. Treatment with these agents arrested cell cycle progression at the G1 phase by increasing p27 expression and decreasing cyclin D1 and phospho‐Rb (p‐Rb) levels. GDC‐0980 effectively inhibited HSCC cell migration and downregulated migration‐related proteins, including phospho‐protein kinase C zeta (p‐PKCζ), phospho‐integrin β1 (p‐Integrinβ1), and urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA). Combination therapy with GDC‐0980 and Refametinib produced potent synergistic antitumor effects in HSCC [115]. NVP‐BEZ235, a dual inhibitor of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) and phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K), suppressed HSCC growth by phosphorylating p70S6K and showed a synergistic effect when combined with cisplatin for HSCC treatment [116]. In xenografted mouse models, chloroquine (CQ) inhibited autophagy and enhanced cisplatin‐induced apoptosis. Combining cisplatin‐based chemotherapy with CQ may therefore represent a promising therapeutic strategy for HSCC [117].

A total of 673 differentially expressed proteins were identified in HSCC samples compared with adjacent normal tissues using liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS). This included 172 up‐regulated and 501 down‐regulated proteins. Comparison between chemotherapy‐sensitive and chemotherapy‐resistant cancers revealed 183 differentially expressed proteins, consisting of 102 up‐regulated and 81 down‐regulated proteins. FADD and RIPK1 may act through necroptosis and pattern recognition receptor (PRR) signaling pathways, serving as potential molecular markers for multidrug resistance (MDR) chemotherapy in HSCC patients [118]. CCL4L1 may also serve as a potential molecular marker for HSCC chemotherapy. Excessive CCL4L1 led to up‐regulation of PRAME and down‐regulation of miR‐375. This reduced HSPB8 expression and enhanced chemotherapy sensitivity [119]. Microarray analysis identified 1579 differentially expressed genes in HSCC tissues responding to docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5‐fluorouracil (TPF) chemotherapy, including 815 up‐regulated and 764 down‐regulated genes. The MAPK and JAK/STAT signaling pathways may play critical roles in TPF chemotherapy sensitivity. Furthermore, qRT‐PCR detected changes in IL‐6, MAPK14, JUN, CDK5, and CAMK2A expression in vitro, providing insight into potential mechanisms underlying the TPF chemotherapy response in HSCC [120]. Through microarray analysis, Zhou et al. identified significant differences in the expression profiles of long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and messenger RNAs (mRNAs) between radioresistant (RS) FaDu cells and parental FaDu cells treated with 4 Gy radiation. Certain lncRNAs, such as TCON_00018436, may play regulatory roles in modulating radiation resistance in HSCC cells [121]. Additionally, UVRAG has been shown to display oncogenic properties in HSCC, and its inhibition enhances radiosensitivity by inducing lysosome membrane permeabilization. Therefore, targeting UVRAG may represent a promising treatment strategy for HSCC [122]. Furthermore, BAY‐87‐2243, a novel small‐molecule inhibitor of mitochondrial complex I and hypoxia‐induced HIF‐1 activity, combined with radiotherapy effectively alleviated tumor hypoxia and improved local tumor control. Inhibitors targeting the HIF‐1 pathway also show potential for enhancing radiosensitivity when used with radiotherapy [123]. Moreover, combining celecoxib with radiotherapy suppressed tumor proliferation and promoted apoptosis in FaDu cells [124].

Summary

The drug development of HSCC is still in the preclinical stage. The repurposing of old drugs has the highest feasibility for clinical translation due to FDA approval, low cost, and clear safety. Although natural products and novel synthetic compounds have strong anti‐tumor/sensitizing activities, they require dosage form optimization or extensive safety studies. All candidate drugs target the core oncogenic pathways of HSCC (PI3K/Akt, autophagy, EMT), and autophagy inhibitors are the most promising direction for radiotherapy and chemotherapy sensitization. However, the research on candidate drugs is limited to the FaDu cell line and nude mouse models, and no pharmacokinetic, toxicological, or clinical trials have been conducted, with an unclear clinical translation path; the markers of radiotherapy and chemotherapy resistance are only preliminary screening and have not been verified for clinical value. In the future, cross‐study comparisons need to be carried out to evaluate the feasibility of clinical translation of drugs and promote specific clinical trials for HSCC.

Advances in Multi‐Omics Research on HSCC

In recent years, the emergence of novel omics technologies, such as single‐cell sequencing, metabolomics, spatial omics, and imaging omics, has greatly accelerated quantitative and high‐throughput research. These technological advances provide powerful tools to explore the fundamental principles of life sciences and to gain deeper insight into molecular mechanisms. Although these cutting‐edge omics approaches have begun to aid in the discovery and diagnosis of molecular markers in several cancers, including esophageal, prostate, breast cancer, and so on [125], studies specifically focusing on HSCC remain relatively limited. Chen et al. constructed the first single‐cell transcriptome map of HSCC and revealed intercellular crosstalk. Among the differentially expressed genes between tumor cells and epithelial cells, BMPR2 was identified as promoting proliferation and migration in HSCC [126]. Li et al. used single‐cell sequencing to characterize the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and lymphatic metastasis in HSCC. Their findings showed that IGHA1 and IGHG1‐specific plasma cells were significantly overexpressed in HSCC tissues compared with normal hypopharyngeal and lymphatic tissues [127]. Guan et al. analyzed the single‐cell transcriptomic landscape and microenvironment of normal adjacent tissues in HSCC [128]. Cai et al. conducted single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) on six primary tumor tissues and two adjacent normal mucosal tissues from six untreated HSCC patients. Myofibroblasts (mCAFs) were shown to function as communication hubs in the HSCC tumor microenvironment (TME). They enhanced the invasive capacity of HSCC cells and mobilized surrounding cells to build a microenvironment that supports tumor growth [129]. Ren et al. performed a comprehensive analysis of mutations, transcriptomes, and proteomes from tumor samples of 103 Chinese HSCC patients. The study found that male patients had a higher incidence and a poorer prognosis. Upregulation of the estrogen response pathway and aberrant phosphorylation of protein kinase C (PKC) and cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) were also observed in HSCC tissues. In addition, the researchers developed a predictive model that integrates clinical variables with the expression levels of six genes [130]. Li et al. applied spatial transcriptomics to characterize the tumor microenvironment and demonstrated the correlation between SLCO2A1 and tumor suppression in HSCC [131].

Currently, multi‐omics research on HSCC remains relatively limited, with most studies being single‐omics and small‐sample research. There is a lack of multi‐omics integrated analysis with large samples and multiple centers. However, existing studies have confirmed that multi‐omics technology can reveal the core biological mechanisms of HSCC from multiple dimensions such as single cells, space, and molecules, providing new evidence for the precise diagnosis and targeted treatment of HSCC. An increasing number of researchers are now combining genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single‐cell sequencing, and spatial omics analyses to comprehensively investigate different tumor types [132]. In the future, the integrated analysis of technologies such as genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, single‐cell sequencing, and spatial omics will become the core direction of HSCC research. This approach can not only more comprehensively explain the occurrence and development mechanisms of HSCC but also construct molecular typing and prognosis prediction models based on multi‐omics data, promoting the development of precision medicine for HSCC.

The Application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning in HSCC Research

The research on AI and machine learning in HSCC has shifted from simple technical modeling to exploration of clinical applications. Given the highly aggressive and metastatic nature of hypopharyngeal cancer, there is an urgent clinical need to develop a precise prognostic model that can optimize treatment strategies and improve prognostic evaluation. The integration of tumor molecular markers with patient‐specific prognostic data, together with the use of advanced prognostic models, has been widely applied across various cancer types [133]. Deep learning techniques have shown considerable potential for improving prediction accuracy and can play an important role in developing robust prognostic models [134, 135, 136]. Therefore, constructing an accurate and reliable prognostic model for hypopharyngeal cancer is essential for both diagnosis and treatment.

Patients with HSCC who exhibit LVI generally have a poor prognosis. Xu et al. obtained intratumoral and peritumoral radiomic features through contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT). A total of 1648 radiomic features were extracted from the gross tumor volume (GTV) and from tumor margins ranging from 1 to 5 mm in width, designated as Peri1V to Peri5V. A deep learning model was also constructed based on the GTV. A radiomics nomogram was created by integrating the deep learning model features with clinical characteristics. The study found that the combined intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics model based on CECT provided a more effective prediction of LVI in patients with HNSCC. This finding highlights its potential value for future clinical application [12]. Zhong et al. collected data from patients with laryngeal and HSCC who underwent PET‐CT scans before radical treatment between 2008 and 2017. Tumor segmentation and radiomics analysis were performed using LIFEx software. The data were divided into training and validation sets following the TRIPOD guidelines at a ratio of 80 to 20. Features were selected through recursive feature elimination, and random forest classifiers were constructed for four predictive models based on PET, CT, clinical, and combined PET‐CT parameters. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with the area under the curve. Radiomics features derived from FDG PET‐CT may serve as potential predictors of early disease progression in patients with locally advanced laryngeal and hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LHSCC) [137]. Li et al. performed a retrospective analysis of data from 295 patients with HSCC, incorporating 22 clinical parameters as training features. Twelve different machine learning algorithms were used to construct the models, and the XGBoost algorithm demonstrated the best performance. The study developed a machine learning model for predicting the 3‐year survival status of patients with HSCC, providing a new prognostic assessment approach for clinical management [138]. Liu et al. developed and evaluated a radiomics‐based predictive model to distinguish T2 from T3 staging in LHSCC. The retrospective study included 118 pathologically confirmed LHSCC patients. Multiple radiomics models were generated through feature processing based on 851 radiomics features extracted from contrast‐enhanced CT images, using three feature selection methods and seven machine learning classifiers. Among all peri‐tumoral models, the Peri1V‐Radscore showed the best predictive performance in the validation dataset [139].

Currently, the application of AI and machine learning in HSCC still has numerous limitations. First, most existing models are based on single‐center and small‐ sample studies, lacking multi‐center external validation, which results in insufficient model generality. Second, the majority of models only integrate a single type of data, lacking the integration of multi‐dimensional data, and there is still room for improvement in prediction accuracy. Third, the interpretability of the models is poor, and the “black‐box” problem restricts their wide application in clinical practice. Fourth, there is a lack of prospective studies to verify the clinical value of the models, and most models remain at the retrospective research stage. To enhance the clinical applicability and feasibility of AI and machine learning in HSCC, future research should focus on the following aspects. First, conduct large‐sample and multi‐center studies to externally validate the models and improve their generality. Second, integrate multi‐dimensional data such as clinical, imaging, molecular, pathological, and hematological data to construct multi‐modal fusion models and enhance prediction accuracy. Third, carry out prospective clinical studies to verify the practical value of the models in treatment plan selection and prognosis assessment, and promote the clinical translation of the models. Fourth, construct machine learning models by combining multi‐omics data to achieve the combination of molecular mechanisms and clinical applications. Overall, AI and machine learning have great application potential in the precise diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and treatment plan selection of HSCC. Breaking through the existing limitations and promoting the clinical translation of the models are the core directions of future research.

Conclusions and Prospects

Core Conclusions

In this review, we systematically and critically reviewed the research progress of HSCC over the past two decades in areas such as novel therapies, therapeutic targets, biomarkers, and candidate drugs, with a focus on core biological mechanisms and key signaling pathways. HSCC is a distinct, aggressive HNSCC subtype with unique biological and clinical features: HSCC has a low HPV prevalence and high metastatic potential. It is characterized by late diagnosis, poor survival, and limited treatment options relative to other HNSCC subtypes. Broader HNSCC data have significant extrapolation limitations for HSCC, highlighting the need for HSCC‐specific research and clinical trials.

We summarize the current research progress of HSCC into the following five aspects [1]: Transformation of diagnosis and prediction models: The diagnosis and prognosis of HSCC have shifted from relying on a single indicator to a multi‐dimensional and multi‐technology integrated model based on imaging, pathology, molecular, and blood markers [2]. Maturity of Treatment Framework: The treatment of HSCC has established a surgical framework based on the principles of staging stratification, minimally invasive priority, and functional preservation, as well as a radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and drug treatment system characterized by precision and combination [3]. Key Molecular Targets and Pathways: PI3K/Akt, NF‐κB, Notch1, and AKT/mTOR are the core signaling pathways of HSCC. STK33, Notch1, PD‐L1, and key molecules in the non‐coding RNA regulatory network are important potential therapeutic targets [4]. Establishment of Biomarker Spectrum: Multiple biomarkers covering prognosis assessment, metastasis prediction, and treatment response have been identified, among which liquid biopsy markers have extremely high clinical translation potential [5]. Technology‐driven Precision: Multi‐omics and AI/machine learning technologies are driving HSCC research from molecular mechanism exploration to clinical precision decision‐making. This systematic review identifies several critical limitations in current HSCC research [1]: Prevalent low evidence quality. majority of studies are based on the FaDu cell line, and are further limited by the absence of systematic functional validation in vivo [2]; Small validation cohort sizes. Most prognostic biomarker studies are constrained by limited sample sizes in validation cohorts, thereby restricting statistical power and generalizability [3]. Large‐scale clinical trials specifically targeting HSCC patients remain insufficient, and the majority of existing clinical trials are predominantly conducted on HNSCC patients with primary oral cancer.

Future Prospects

Based on our summary of the research on HSCC and the analysis of its limitations, we put forward the following prospects for the research on HSCC.

Develop clinically representative HSCC models and validate molecular targets: Address the FaDu cell line limitation by generating HSCC patient‐derived xenografts (PDXs) and organoids (PDOs); validate top molecular targets (STK33, Notch1, miR‐21) in these models to improve clinical translatability. Prioritize targets with dual prognostic/therapeutic value (PD‐L1, S100A11, miR‐107) for drug development.

Construct a multicenter, multidimensional precision medicine framework for HSCC: Validate a combination biomarker model (plasma + tissue + clinical/radiomic data) via multicenter studies for early diagnosis, prognosis stratification, and treatment selection; integrate multi‐omics data to develop HSCC molecular subtypes and personalized treatment algorithms. Leverage AI/machine learning to create interpretable, multi‐modal models for clinical decision‐making.

Accelerate HSCC‐specific clinical trials of repurposed drugs and immunotherapy combinations: Repurposed drugs have high clinical feasibility and preclinical anti‐tumor/sensitizer effects—Phase 2 trials of repurposed drug + chemotherapy/CRT are the highest priority for near‐term clinical translation. For immunotherapy, combination strategies (PD‐1 inhibitor + MDSC‐targeting agent/Notch1 inhibitor) to reverse HPV‐negative resistance are exploratory but promising—HSCC‐specific Phase 1/2 trials are needed.

HSCC is a challenging disease with poor outcomes, but recent advances in stage‐stratified treatment, molecular target identification, and multi‐omics/AI have created new opportunities for precision medicine. The key to improving HSCC survival and QoL is HSCC‐specific research and clinical trials—avoiding over‐reliance on broader HNSCC data—and multidisciplinary collaboration between clinicians, basic scientists, and AI experts to bridge the gap between preclinical research and clinical translation.
