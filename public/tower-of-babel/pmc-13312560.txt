Investigating causal associations among inflammatory proteins, blood metabolites, and Alzheimer’s disease risk
Authors: Shuai Liu, Jingjing Zhu, Hua Zhong, Dan Zhou, Quan Long, Zichen Zhang, Xiaochen Yang, Qing Wu, Chao Cheng, Jianyong Wu, Hung N Luu, Jing Wang, Bingxin Zhao, Chong Wu, Youping Deng, Lang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a prevalent degenerative neurological disorder with limited treatment options. Prior studies reported specific metabolites and inflammatory proteins to be related to AD risk. However, the intricate relationship between inflammatory proteins, blood metabolites, and AD risk in European population remains unclear. Genetic instruments for 1,091 metabolites and 736 inflammatory proteins were derived from two recent comprehensive genome-wide association studies. Univariable Mendelian Randomization was employed to assess potential causal effects of metabolites on AD risk, potential effects of inflammatory proteins on metabolites, and effects of inflammatory proteins on AD risk. Multivariable MR (MVMR) was further applied to disentangle direct effects of proteins and metabolites on AD. Twelve metabolites were identified to be associated with AD risk, and 226 inflammatory proteins demonstrated likely to be causal effects on these 12 metabolites. Further examining the associations between such inflammatory proteins and AD risk revealed 22 associations for which the effect directions from inflammatory proteins to metabolites, from metabolites to AD risk, and from inflammatory proteins to AD risk were aligned, suggesting inflammatory protein - metabolite - AD risk pathway. MVMR further highlighted four trios in which the effect directions were consistent with the UVMR results, supporting a metabolite‑mediated pattern. This large‑scale genetic analysis highlights specific metabolites as direct contributors to AD risk and suggests that certain inflammatory proteins may influence AD primarily through downstream metabolic pathways. Our findings offer potential novel therapeutic targets for AD intervention. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects older adults, causing a gradual decline in cognitive function, memory loss, and behavioral changes [1–3]. According to recent estimates, the healthcare costs associated with AD treatment were approximately $321 billion in 2022, with costs projected to surpass $1 trillion by 2050 [4]. Metabolism refers to the collection of all biochemical reactions that take place in an organism. Metabolites are intermediate products of metabolism and play a variety of roles, including serving as fuel, providing structural support, transmitting signals, altering the activity of enzymes, and playing a role in defense mechanisms and interactions with other organisms [5, 6]. In recent years, there has been growing interest in the role of metabolites and inflammation in AD, as abnormal levels of specific blood metabolites and inflammatory proteins have been implicated in the development and progression of this condition [7–12]. For instance, an earlier study reported that elevated glutamine levels were associated with a lower risk of AD [13], while others revealed that decreased ceramide levels were linked to AD progression [14, 15]. Several studies have reported significant alterations in the levels of kynurenine pathway (KP) metabolites in the serum or plasma of patients with AD compared with age-matched normal controls [8, 16, 17]. The kynurenine pathway of tryptophan metabolism is directly induced by inflammatory cytokines and generates metabolites with both immunoregulatory and neurotoxic properties [18, 19], making it a biologically plausible link between systemic inflammation and AD. Beyond KP, circulating lipid metabolism also appears to modulate inflammation in AD, with altered phospholipids, sphingolipids, fatty acids, and glycerolipid metabolites observed in plasma or brain tissue of AD patients and implicated in neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes [20, 21]. Sun et al. [22] identified four metabolites (i.e., epiandrosterone sulfate, 5alpha-androstan-3beta-17beta-diol disulfate, sphingomyelin, and glutamine) had potential causal effects on AD risk using genetic instruments. Furthermore, additional metabolites had been reported to have likely causal effects on AD risk, including N-lactoyl tyrosine, argininate, ergothioneine, piperine, 1,7-dimethyluric acid, 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate sulfate, leucylalanine, and alpha-glutamyltyrosine, etc [23, 24]. These findings highlight the potential importance of metabolites in the development and progression of AD and the need for further research in this area.

Inflammation is also reported to play a central role in the pathogenesis of AD, with multiple inflammatory proteins showing altered levels in both peripheral blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of AD patients compared with cognitively normal controls. Several studies have reported elevated peripheral levels of high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hs‑CRP), interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), soluble tumour necrosis factor receptors 1 and 2 (sTNFR1, sTNFR2), and α1‑antichymotrypsin (α1‑ACT) in AD, supporting systemic inflammatory involvement in disease progression [25, 26]. In the CSF, concentrations of pro‑ and anti‑inflammatory proteins such as IL‑10, monocyte chemoattractant protein‑1 (MCP‑1), transforming growth factor‑β1 (TGF‑β1), soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (sTREM2), and YKL‑40 have been associated with cognitive decline and AD pathology [27–29]. Additional biomarkers, including chemokines (e.g., fractalkine/CX3CL1), adhesion molecules (e.g., soluble intercellular adhesion molecule‑1 [sICAM‑1] and soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule‑1 [sVCAM‑1]), interferon‑γ‑induced protein 10 (IP‑10), and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), have also been implicated in AD through cross‑sectional and longitudinal studies [26, 28]. Clinically, circulating cytokines including interleukin (IL)‑1β, IL‑6, IL‑8, tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), as well as acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein (CRP), are frequently altered in patients with AD or mild cognitive impairment [30–32]. Complement proteins, particularly complement component C3, play a central role in systemic inflammatory regulation, as all complement activation pathways converge at this node. Notably, lower plasma C3 levels have been observed in individuals with AD [33–35], and validated using Mendelian Randomization (MR) approach, suggesting a causal effect between reduced C3 and increased disease risk.

Conventional epidemiological studies face potential challenges in adequately controlling for confounding variables, potentially introducing bias into observed results [36]. Additionally, issues of selection bias may arise, as the chosen sample might not represent broader population. To overcome confounding and reverse causation in observational studies, we apply MR using genetic instruments for metabolites and proteins. [37–41]. The MR approach leverages naturally occurring genetic variations as instrumental variables (IVs), mimicking a randomized controlled trial (RCT) setting [42]. By utilizing genetic variants associated with exposures of interest as instruments, MR can mitigate confounding and reverse causation, offering a more robust and relatively unbiased estimation of causal effects compared with traditional observational studies [6]. Two-sample MR allows the use of large-scale genetic association summary data, in which variant-exposure and variant-outcome associations are characterized from two independent genome-wide association studies (GWASs); thus, enhancing the statistical power to identify a broader spectrum of causal effects between exposures and outcomes [37, 43].

In this study, we aimed to: (1) identify blood metabolites causally associated with AD risk; (2) determine whether inflammatory proteins regulate the levels of these AD-related metabolites; (3) evaluate the direct effects of these inflammatory proteins on AD risk; and (4) uncover integrated inflammatory protein–metabolite–AD pathways with consistent directional associations suggesting mediation. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale MR study that systematically evaluates the protein–metabolite–AD pathway by integrating protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) and metabolite quantitative trait loci (mQTL) instruments with the most comprehensive AD GWAS available to date. Findings from this work may offer novel insights into AD pathogenesis by highlighting upstream inflammatory drivers mediated through blood metabolites, thereby providing potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design

The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1. Details regarding the data sources, selection of instrumental variables (IVs), and statistical analyses are described below.

Study design

Data source for blood metabolome, inflammatory proteins, and AD

To identify likely causal effects of blood metabolites on AD risk, we used mQTL of 1,091 metabolites (Supplementary Table 1) as IVs [44]. GWAS summary statistics of 21,101,114 variants generated in a recent comprehensive AD meta-analysis were used for the association analyses. This AD study comprised several consortia/datasets, including European Alzheimer & Dementia Biobank (EADB), Genomic Research at Ace (GR@ACE), European Alzheimer’s Disease Initiative (EADI) Consortium, Genetic and Environmental Risk in AD/Defining Genetic, Polygenic and Environmental Risk for Alzheimer’s Disease Consortium (GERAD/PERADES), The Norwegian Dementia Genetics Network (DemGene), Bonn, the Rotterdam study, the Copenhagen City Heart Study (CCHS), The Neocodex–Murcia study (NxC), and the UK Biobank (UKBB). This meta-analysis included 39,106 clinically diagnosed AD cases, 46,828 proxy-ADD cases, and 401,577 controls [45].

To assess putative causal effects of inflammatory proteins on AD-associated blood metabolites, we leveraged GWAS summary statistics of inflammatory proteins in the UKBB involving 34,557 individuals of European ancestry [46] to determine the instrumental variables. Among the 2,923 proteins measured, 736 proteins classified under inflammation-related panels were selected for this study. For the association analysis between protein levels and metabolites, we used GWAS summary statistics from the study by Chen etal [44].

We further evaluated the direct effects of inflammatory proteins on AD risk. For this analysis, the same IVs of proteins based on summary statistics from the UKBB study [46] were used. We leveraged summary statistics of the AD GWAS from the study by Kunkle et al. [47], which included 21,982 cases and 41,944 cognitively normal controls. Notably, this particular GWAS did not include samples from the UKBB, ensuring the validity of assumptions required by the MR analyses-specifically, the independence of instruments from confounders and the avoidance of bias due to sample overlap.

Genetic instruments for blood metabolites and inflammatory proteins

The steps to select instrumental variables (IVs) for both metabolites and inflammatory proteins were identical, as shown in Fig. 1: (i) For each blood metabolite or protein of interest, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that were associated at the genome-wide significance level (p-value < 5 × 10−8) were used as potential genetic instruments [39, 40, 48]; (ii) SNPs were filtered with linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 < 0.1 within a window of 500kb); (iii) The alleles should be the same between exposure and outcome datasets; (iv) The IVs for exposure should not be directly associated with outcome (p-value > 1 × 10−5); and (v) Only strong IVs were retained (F-statistic ≥ 10). Only metabolites or inflammatory proteins with at least three SNPs as IVs and R2 ≥ 0.5% (proportion of variation of metabolite or protein levels that can be explained by the IVs) were included for further analyses.

Univariable MR analyses

Two-sample MR was utilized to estimate the associations between (a) genetically predicted metabolite levels and AD risk, (b) genetically predicted inflammatory protein levels and levels of AD-associated metabolites, and (c) genetically predicted inflammatory protein levels from (b) and AD risk. Such MR analysis only requires the GWAS summary statistics and does not require individual-level data. If well conducted, two-sample MR could reduce biases due to unmeasured confounding or reverse causality [49].

After extracting the association estimates between variants and exposure of interest or outcome, the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was used to conduct MR analysis [38, 39, 50, 51]. Heterogeneity among genetic instruments was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. If significant heterogeneity was present (p-value < 0.05), a random-effect IVW model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effect IVW model was used. The significance of the MR results was determined using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05 to adjust for multiple testing.

To evaluate the robustness of our results in the situation of possible violations of MR assumptions, we conducted two sensitivity analyses: (i) MR-Egger’s intercept was used to estimate the possibility of horizontal pleiotropy [52], and (ii) MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) was used to identify potential horizontal pleiotropy influenced by outlier IVs [53, 54].

Associations showing consistent direction of effect for the inflammatory protein - metabolite - AD risk pathway

We evaluated the associations of metabolites on AD risk, inflammatory proteins on metabolites, and inflammatory proteins on AD risk to assess whether there were associations showing consistent direction of effect for the inflammatory protein - metabolite - AD risk pathway. Such findings may suggest that specific inflammatory proteins may influence AD risk by regulating levels of target metabolites.

Multivariable MR, genetic correlation analyses, and colocalization analyses

To investigate whether the identified inflammatory proteins exert direct effects on AD independently of their associated metabolites, we performed MVMR. MVMR estimates the direct causal effect of each exposure on the outcome while conditioning on other correlated exposures, thereby allowing disentangling of independent pathways and evaluating whether the protein - AD association is consistent with mediation through the metabolite of interest. For instrument selection, we applied the same parameters as our univariable MR framework. We then constructed multi-exposure genetic association matrices for each pair and performed IVW-MVMR using the MVMR R package [55]. This model simultaneously estimates (i) protein - AD direct effect conditional on the metabolite, and (ii) metabolite - AD direct effect conditional on the protein. The nominal p-value was used to determine the significance of the effects.

We applied LD Score regression (LDSC) [56] to quantify genome‑wide genetic overlap among the protein-metabolite-AD trios that passed MVMR analysis. LDSC estimates genetic covariance by regressing the cross‑trait products of GWAS test statistics on the corresponding LD scores, with the resulting slope representing the genetic correlation. Analyses were performed using the LDSC software package with European‑ancestry LD score reference panels derived from the 1000 Genomes Project. All analyses were restricted to high‑quality HapMap3 SNPs [57] to reduce potential biases from poor imputation or low allele frequency. Prior to regression, GWAS summary statistics were standardized using the “munge_sumstats.py”, and genetic correlation analyses were conducted using “ldsc.py” within the LDSC framework.

Colocalization analysis was performed to assess whether inflammatory protein levels or circulating metabolites and AD share the same single causal genetic variants. For each locus, summary statistics for the molecular trait (protein or metabolite) and AD were extracted within 250 kb windows centered on lead genome‑wide significant molecular QTL variants. Colocalization was evaluated using the Bayesian framework implemented in the coloc package [58], which estimates posterior probabilities for five competing hypotheses. Default prior probabilities were used (p1 = p2 = 1 × 10−4; p12 = 1 × 10−5). A posterior colocalization probability (PP.H4) of 80% was used to denote a shared causal signal, whereas regions with high posterior probability for distinct causal variants (PP.H3) were interpreted as genetically independent signals.

Results

Associations of blood metabolites with AD risk

After selecting valid genetic instruments for blood metabolites, a total of 484 metabolites with at least three independent IVs were included in the main MR analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Twelve of them were significantly associated with AD risk (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Among these 12 metabolites, tryptophan betaine showed an inverse association with AD risk (β = −0.09), consistent with previous findings in females [59]. Similarly, epiandrosterone sulfate exhibited an inverse association (β = −0.05), in line with earlier studies reporting z-score (Z) = −4.24 and odds ratio (OR) = 0.60 [22, 24]. Five metabolites exhibited positive associations with AD risk, whereas seven showed inverse associations. Two instances of potential horizontal pleiotropy were identified by the MR-Egger intercept for metabolites tauro-beta-muricholate and epiandrosterone sulfate, and one global pleiotropy instance was identified by MR-PRESSO on decadienedioic acid (C10:2-DC)**. After removing outlier SNPs, all metabolites still showed similar associations compared with those from the IVW analyses. The genetic instruments used for these 12 metabolites are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Manhattan plot of MR results for all metabolites on AD risk. The red dashed lines indicate a significant association at FDR < 0.05

Associations between predicted levels of inflammatory proteins and AD-associated metabolites

After determining genetic instruments for inflammatory proteins, a total of 704 inflammatory proteins were included in the main MR analysis for assessing associations of their predicted levels with the 12 AD-associated metabolites. Of them, a total of 265 unique inflammatory proteins (506 pairs) showed likely causal effects on 12 metabolites (Supplementary Table 4).

Associations between predicted levels of inflammatory proteins and AD risk

We further assessed the associations of predicted levels of inflammatory proteins that are associated with AD-related metabolites and AD risk. A total of 264 inflammatory proteins were evaluated, excluding the RALB protein, which could not be tested due to a lack of IVs. Nineteen inflammatory proteins were significantly associated with AD risk after FDR correction (FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Table 5).

Associations showing consistent effect for the inflammatory proteins - metabolites - AD risk pathway

After aggregating results from the above mentioned analyses, a total of 13 unique inflammatory proteins and eight unique metabolites exhibited consistent associations (22 pairs) with AD risk (Table 1), suggesting that these inflammatory proteins may influence AD risk through their regulatory effects on the corresponding metabolites. For example, epiandrosterone sulfate was inversely associated with AD risk (p-value = 1.82 × 10−5), aligning with previous evidence [22, 24]. Our analysis also revealed that predicted levels of four inflammatory proteins (i.e., BTN3A2, LILRB4, CD22, and CSNK1D) were inversely associated with epiandrosterone sulfate levels, while predicted levels of one inflammatory protein (BTN2A1) exhibited a positive association with epiandrosterone sulfate levels. We further observed positive associations of BTN3A2, LILRB4, CD22, and CSNK1D, and inverse association of BTN2A1 with AD risk. Notably, Bu et al. [60]reported elevated levels of plasma soluble CD22 (sCD22) in individuals with preclinical and dementia stages of AD. This alignment underscores the robustness of our findings with existing literature.

Associations showing suggested consistent direction of effect for the inflammatory proteins - metabolites - AD risk pathway

MVMR, LDSC, and colocalization analyses of protein–metabolite–AD relationships

MVMR analyses across inflammatory protein – metabolite - AD trios revealed different patterns of direct effects (Supplementary Table 6). Seven pairs demonstrated metabolite‑only direct effects, in which the metabolite - but not the protein - remained significantly associated with AD risk after adjustment. Four of them showed consistent effect directions on AD compared with univariable MR results, including CD22 - tauro-beta-muricholate – AD, APOE − 1-stearoyl-2-linoleoyl-GPI (18:0/18:2) – AD, JCHAIN − 1-stearoyl-2-linoleoyl-GPI (18:0/18:2) – AD, and ANGPTL2 - 1-methyl-4-imidazoleacetate - AD pathways. In contrast, the remaining protein - metabolite combinations showed no supported evidence for protein ‑ mediated contributions to AD risk. This group included pairs where both exposures reached nominal significance, only the protein was significant, or neither exposure exhibited a direct effect in the multivariable model. Complementing the causal inference analyses, LDSC genetic‑correlation tests across all protein - AD and protein - metabolite pairs revealed no statistically significant genome‑wide correlations. Most estimates were small in magnitude with wide confidence intervals, consistent with the modest or near‑zero SNP‑heritability and low mean χ2 values characteristic of many protein traits. Overall, the LDSC results indicate no detectable genome‑wide genetic correlation among the profiled inflammatory proteins, metabolites, and AD, and underscore the challenges of estimating genetic correlation for low‑heritability molecular traits (Supplementary Table 7).

Moreover, colocalization analysis was performed to assess whether inflammatory proteins or metabolites share causal genetic variants with AD. Among the proteins examined, strong evidence for colocalization with AD was observed for JCHAIN (PP.H4 = 1.00), IL17RB (PP.H4 = 0.99), CD22 (PP.H4 = 0.90), and GC (PP.H4 = 0.84), indicating that genetic variants influencing these protein levels are likely to be directly involved in AD susceptibility (Supplementary Table 8). For metabolites examined, colocalization analysis did not support shared causal variants with AD (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

In this study, we identified 12 metabolites associated with AD risk, among which tryptophan betaine and epiandrosterone sulfate were notable, which were reported by previous studies [22, 24, 59]. A total of 13 inflammatory proteins showed a consistent effect for the inflammatory proteins - metabolites - AD risk pathway using univariable MR. Among them, five (i.e., BTN2A1, BTN3A2, LILRB4, CD22, and CSNK1D) were associated with both epiandrosterone sulfate and AD risk. BTN2A1 [61], in conjunction with BTN3A2, participates in capturing phosphoantigens that activate Vγ9 Vδ2 T cells. These T cells play a significant role in influencing inflammation [62]. LILRB4 is expressed in various immune cells, including T cells and plasma cells, and plays a critical role in immune system function [63]. The CD22 surface molecule is predominantly expressed in mature B cells and plays an important role in regulating B cell signaling and immune responses. During inflammation, B cells contribute to the immune response by producing antibodies, and CD22 is thought to potentially influence B cell function, thereby impacting the inflammatory response [64]. CSNK1D is a kinase involved in various cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation and signal transduction. CSNK1D has been identified to potentially modulate the activity of immune cells by affecting signal transduction pathways [65]. All five proteins thus potentially function in the immune process. Epiandrosterone sulfate is a steroid hormone characterized by its androgenic activity. Epiandrosterone sulfate is an androgenic steroid hormone, formed from dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) via 5α-reductase to epiandrosterone, followed by sulfation [24]. The exact roles of such identified inflammatory proteins and metabolites in AD etiology warrant further investigations.

Using a combined MVMR and LDSC framework, we evaluated the causal and genetic relationships among inflammatory proteins, circulating metabolites, and AD risk. MVMR analyses revealed several distinct causal patterns. Notably, four protein–metabolite–AD trios exhibited metabolite‑only direct effects with the same effect directions to univariable MR, in which each metabolite of interest retained a significant association with AD risk after adjustment for the corresponding protein of interest. Examples include the CD22 - tauro-beta-muricholate – AD, APOE − 1-stearoyl-2-linoleoyl-GPI (18:0/18:2) – AD, JCHAIN − 1-stearoyl-2-linoleoyl-GPI (18:0/18:2) – AD, and ANGPTL2 - 1-methyl-4-imidazoleacetate – AD pathways. These findings suggest that, for several inflammatory pathways, metabolic alterations may represent more proximal determinants of AD risk than the upstream protein of interest. Such patterns are consistent with metabolite‑mediated mechanisms wherein proteins exert their influence through downstream biochemical changes rather than through direct inflammatory signaling.

In contrast, for several protein–metabolite pairs, MVMR did not support direct causal effects of the inflammatory protein on AD risk after accounting for the paired metabolite of interest, even when univariable MR suggested protein–AD or metabolite–AD associations. This divergence is not surprising, as univariable MR estimates the total effect—including direct, mediated, and correlated pathways—whereas MVMR isolates direct effects conditional on correlated exposures. When substantial biological or genetic correlation exists between a protein and a metabolite, conditioning them in MVMR can attenuate or reverse the observed effects. Thus, the lack of protein‑specific direct effects does not diminish the biological relevance of inflammatory proteins but instead suggests that much of their apparent influence on AD risk may operate through metabolic intermediates or shared molecular pathways.

To complement the MR findings, we conducted LDSC analyses to examine genome‑wide shared genetic architecture. Across all protein–AD and protein–metabolite pairs, we observed no statistically significant genetic correlations, with small genetic correlation estimates and wide standard errors. This pattern is not unexpected given the molecular specificity and modest polygenicity of many protein traits of interest. While LDSC does not indicate causality, these results provide useful context regarding the degree of shared heritability across molecular layers.

Colocalization analysis identified a shared causal variant between CD22 protein levels and AD (PP.H4 = 0.90), supporting CD22 as a genetically anchored upstream driver of AD risk. MVMR further suggested that this effect is transmitted primarily through tauro‑β‑muricholate, as the metabolite retained a significant association (p-value = 0.006) with AD risk after adjustment for CD22. Although tauro‑β‑muricholate did not colocalize with AD, this pattern is consistent with a mediated causal architecture in which genetic perturbation of CD22 influences disease risk through downstream alterations in bile‑acid metabolism rather than through shared disease‑causal variants. Similarly, JCHAIN exhibited strong evidence of colocalization with AD risk (PP.H4 = 1.00), indicating a shared causal genetic signal at the locus. In MVMR analyses jointly modeling JCHAIN and 1stearoyl2linoleoylGPI (18:0/18:2), the metabolite retained a statistically significant direct effect on AD risk, whereas the conditional effect estimate for JCHAIN was markedly attenuated toward the null and no longer significant (Supplementary Table 6). This pattern is consistent with a mediation framework in which genetically predicted variation in JCHAIN influences AD risk predominantly through downstream regulation of this lipid metabolite rather than via an independent proteinlevel pathway. Despite the absence of metabolite - AD colocalization in these pathways, this pattern is biologically plausible given that circulating lipid metabolites are downstream biochemical traits influenced by genetically regulated proteins and broader metabolic networks, rather than traits expected to share a single disease‑causal variants with AD.

For proteins involved in the identified protein - metabolite - AD pathways, we compared our findings with prior protein - AD MR studies [66–71]. Several proteins showing evidence of causal effect on AD in our analysis have also been reported in previous studies with consistent effect directions, including TREM2 [68, 70], ACE [67, 70], CR1 [66, 69, 70], BTN2A1 [71], SERPIND1 [70], APOE [66, 67, 69], JCHAIN [66], GC [70], BTN3A2 [71], and ANXA11 [70], supporting the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Table 9). By contrast, CD22 and ANGPTL2 were not identified in previous protein - AD MR studies although both proteins have been reported in observational studies [60, 72]. Our multivariable MR analyses further suggested that the effect of CD22 on AD is primarily mediated through tauro‑β‑muricholate, whereas the association between ANGPTL2 and AD appears to be mediated through 1‑methyl‑4‑imidazoleacetate (Supplementary Table 6). Notably, neither the associations of CD22–AD nor ANGPTL2 were supported by colocalization evidence, indicating that these findings should be interpreted with caution and further experimental validation is warranted to further understand the relationship.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which specific circulating metabolites, rather than upstream inflammatory proteins, demonstrate more direct and robust relationships with AD risk. The protein–metabolite–AD trios showing metabolite‑only direct effects highlight biologically meaningful metabolic axes that may bridge peripheral inflammation with neurodegenerative processes.

The current study offers several notable strengths. First, we utilized information from the latest comprehensive metabolite GWAS to determine genetic instruments for studying metabolites, which substantially enhanced the robustness of our analyses. Second, by employing the latest and largest publicly available AD GWAS summary statistics, our study provides substantially increased statistical power to identify metabolites associated with AD risk. Third, we leveraged information from UKBB to examine the associations between inflammatory proteins and metabolites, and further determined associations supporting the inflammatory proteins - metabolites -AD risk pathway through univariable MR and MVMR, thereby providing novel insights into the etiology of AD.

However, specific limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings. It is well known that dietary and lifestyle patterns vary considerably among diverse racial and ethnic groups, thereby exerting a noticeable influence on the composition of blood metabolites. In the present study, we focused on studying individuals of European ancestry, which may constrain the generalizability of our findings to populations of non-European descent. Future works focusing on other populations beyond Europeans are thus warranted. [73]

Conclusions

The present systematic MR analysis unveiled 12 metabolites potentially causally linked to AD risk, with 226 inflammatory proteins identified to potentially regulate such metabolites. By integrating data from inflammatory proteins, metabolites, and AD datasets, we further pinpointed four protein – metabolite - AD pathways consistently supported by both univariable MR and MVMR. Together, these findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the etiology of AD and highlight specific metabolic axes that may mediate the influence of inflammatory signaling on AD risk.
