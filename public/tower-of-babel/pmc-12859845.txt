Senescence of bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells: a narrative review of mechanisms, functional consequences, and rejuvenation strategies for age-related disorders
Authors: Wenting Zhang, Yuanyuan Xie, Bin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the intensification of global population aging, research on aging-related diseases has become increasingly critical. Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs), residing in bone marrow microvironment, serve as precursors for osteoblasts and adipocytes while playing unique roles in regulating hematopoietic and immune systems. However, BMSCs undergo progressive senescence during aging, characterized by diminished proliferative capacity, skewed differentiation potential, compromised immunomodulatory functions, and the development of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). These age-related alterations exacerbate inflammatory responses within the bone marrow microenvironment, contributing to the pathogenesis of degenerative diseases such as osteoporosis and osteoarthritis, while potentially inducing hematopoietic dysfunction and oncogenic transformation. Notably, senescent BMSCs secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that establish a chronic inflammatory milieu, which not only impairs hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) functionality but also promotes bone marrow adipogenesis. Despite these insights, the intricate interplay between BMSC senescence and microenvironmental alterations remains incompletely understood. This narrative review Comprehensively synthesizes current knowledge on the molecular mechanisms underlying BMSCs senescence, with particular emphasis on telomere attrition, DNA damage accumulation, oxidative stress, epigenetic dysregulation, and bidirectional microenvironmental crosstalk. Furthermore, we critically evaluate emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating BMSCs senescence and optimizing their clinical applications in age-related disorders.Keywords: Bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells, Cellular senescence, Telomere shortening, Oxidative stress, Senescence-associated secretory phenotype, Anti-Aging therapeutics

Introduction

The remarkable progress in medical technology and sustained improvements in living standards have led to a substantial extension of human lifespan, consequently accelerating global population aging and its associated health challenges. Aging is characterized by progressive physiological deterioration occurring at both organismal and cellular levels, ultimately compromising tissue and organ function [1]. This age-related functional deterioration significantly diminishes quality of life in elderly populations, elevating risks of falls, physical disability, and mortality while creating considerable socioeconomic burdens [2]. Contemporary gerontological research has identified several fundamental pathological mechanisms underlying age-related disorders: (1) chronic low-grade inflammation, (2) dysfunction of biomacromolecule and organelle, (3) accumulation of senescent cells, and (4) progressive decline in stem cell functionality [3]. The current scientific consensus maintains that human aging primarily originates from the senescence of tissue-resident stem cells, particularly through the depletion of stem cell reservoirs [4].

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), first identified and characterized by Caplan in 1991, represent a population of multipotent stromal cells with self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation capacities. These cells serve as crucial precursors for non-hematopoietic tissues and play pivotal roles in tissue homeostasis, repair, and regeneration processes [5]. MSCs can be isolated from the connective tissue compartments across various anatomical sites, including bone marrow, skeletal muscle, hair follicles, umbilical cord blood, adipose tissue, and placenta tissue, with documented presence in humans, non-human primates, and rodents models. Among MSC subtypes, bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs), occupy a unique niche as osteogenic and adipogenic progenitors while critically maintaining bone marrow microenvironment homeostasis through sophisticated paracrine signaling networks. Distinct from other MSC populations, BMSCs exhibit specialized regulatory functions in both hematopoietic and immune systems. During aging, BMSCs progressively accumulate DNA damage and experience chronic oxidative stress, resulting in: (1) skewed differentiation potential, (2) impaired homing capacity, (3) dysregulated immunomodulatory functions, and (4) development of a SASP [6]. These senescence-associated alterations collectively contribute to the establishment of chronic low-grade inflammation, progressive bone loss, and pathological marrow adiposity - hallmark features of age-related degenerative disorders and hematological malignancies [7].

The rapid evolution of molecular biology and genetic engineering has established cell therapy as a transformative modality for treating age-related degenerative disorders. With the growing elderly population emerging as primary recipients of such therapies, BMSCs have gained significant clinical traction. Similar to other MSC populations, BMSCs demonstrate remarkable therapeutic potential through their ability to: (1) differentiate into multiple lineages (osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic, myogenic, tendogenic, stromal, and neural tissues) for cellular replacement, (2) recruit endogenous progenitor cells to facilitate tissue regeneration, and (3) modulate immune responses [8]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of BMSCs is substantially compromised by cellular senescence - whether induced by physiological aging or prolonged in vitro expansion. This impairment manifests as diminished proliferative capacity, restricted differentiation potential, and detrimental paracrine effects mediated by the SASP [9]. Consequently, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying BMSCs senescence and developing targeted anti-aging interventions have become paramount for both improving management of age-related pathologies and enhancing the clinical performance of BMSCs-based therapies. This article presents a comprehensive narrative review that aims to summarize and critically evaluate the current understanding of BMSC senescence. To ensure a broad and representative coverage of the literature, we conducted extensive searches in the PubMed database. The search strategy utilized a combination of relevant keywords, including but not limited to: “bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells,” “cellular senescence,” “aging,” “SASP,” “telomere,” “oxidative stress,” “epigenetics,” “autophagy,” and “rejuvenation.” While this review adheres to a narrative format and does not constitute a systematic review with strictly predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria, we have endeavored to adopt a systematic approach in our literature analysis and synthesis. Our goal is to provide a structured and balanced overview of the key molecular mechanisms driving BMSCs senescence and the emerging therapeutic strategies designed to counteract it.This narrative review comprehensively examines current understanding of BMSCs senescence mechanisms, with particular emphasis on: telomere attrition dynamics, DNA damage accumulation, oxidative stress pathways, epigenetic modifications, microenvironmental crosstalk. We further critically evaluate novel therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating BMSCs senescence and optimizing their clinical translation for regenerative medicine applications.

Literature search strategy

To ensure a comprehensive and unbiased overview of the current knowledge, the literature for this narrative review was identified through systematic searches of the PubMed/MEDLINE and Web of Science electronic databases, covering publications up to March 2025. The search strategy utilized a combination of relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms, including but not limited to: “bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells”, “BMSCs”, “MSC senescence”, “cellular aging”, “replicative senescence”, “senescence-associated secretory phenotype”, “telomere shortening”, “oxidative stress”, “DNA damage”, “autophagy”, and “rejuvenation”. The inclusion criteria focused on original research articles and authoritative reviews published in the English language. Additionally, the reference lists of key publications were manually screened to identify further relevant studies that may not have been captured by the initial database search. This multi-pronged approach was designed to encompass the fundamental mechanisms, functional consequences, and emerging anti-aging strategies pertaining to BMSCs senescence.

General characteristics of BMSCs

Stem cells constitute a unique class of undifferentiated, multipotent cells that possess two fundamental biological properties: capacity for self-renewal and ability to differentiate into specialized cell types under appropriate physiological or experimental conditions [10]. Based on their ontogenic origin, stem cells are broadly classified into two major categories: embryonic stem cells (derived from early developmental stages) and adult stem cells (resident in mature tissues). Among adult stem cell populations, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) were the first to be characterized and subsequently translated into clinical applications. BMSCs were initially identified as a distinct population of non-hematopoietic, plastic-adherent cells exhibiting characteristic fibroblastoid morphology and demonstrating multilineage differentiation capacity. These cells were first described in the scientific literature under various nomenclature, including “colony-forming unit fibroblasts” (CFU-Fs) and “bone marrow stromal fibroblasts,” before being formally designated as BMSCs in 1991 by the scientific community [11]. While their prototypical niche resides within the bone marrow stroma, subsequent research has identified phenotypically and functionally similar cell populations in diverse anatomical locations, including adipose tissue, umbilical cord blood, skeletal muscle, dental pulp, and other connective tissue compartments. BMSCs exhibit remarkable plasticity in vitro, demonstrating the capacity to undergo expansion while maintaining their differentiation potential. Under appropriate induction conditions, these cells can commit to multiple mesenchymal lineages, including: osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic, tenogenic, myogenic, neural lineages, stromal supporting cells. Beyond their well-characterized regenerative capabilities, BMSCs play an essential physiological role in regulating the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells through complex paracrine mechanisms and direct cell-cell interactions [11]. This unique combination of properties - multipotency, expandability, and hematopoietic regulatory functions - has established BMSCs as invaluable tools in diverse biomedical applications, particularly in the fields of tissue engineering, cellular therapeutics, and regenerative medicine [12].

Anatomical localization of BMSCs

BMSCs primarily reside within the bone marrow stroma, where they form a critical structural and functional component of the hematopoietic niche alongside hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). Their spatial distribution demonstrates significant heterogeneity, with a predominant perivascular localization that facilitates direct cellular crosstalk with endothelial cells and pericytes (Fig. 1). This strategic positioning enables BMSCs to actively participate in maintaining vascular integrity and regulating marrow perfusion [13]. BMSCs also prominently localize along trabecular bone surfaces, where they establish functional interactions with osteoblasts and osteocytes to coordinate bone remodeling and repair mechanisms [14]. Within this complex microenvironment, BMSCs maintain close physical proximity to HSCs and regulate hematopoietic stem cell dynamics through direct cell-cell interactions, paracrine signaling molecules, and extracellular matrix-mediated communication. The bone marrow niche represents a highly organized ecosystem where multiple cellular components reciprocally support BMSC and hematopoietic cell functions. The key cellular interactions include: (1) Endothelial cells provide vascular support and nutrient exchange, (2) Sympathetic nerves and Schwann cells mediate neural regulation, (3) IL-7⁺ stromal cells participate in lymphoid development, and (4) LEPR⁺ cells contribute to niche maintenance. Key molecular mediators including VCAM-1 facilitate cellular adhesion and signal exchange within this highly organized ecosystem, which plays essential roles in preserving normal bone marrow physiology, coordinating skeletal homeostasis, and supporting hematopoietic functions. The dynamic interplay between BMSCs and their microenvironment underscores their fundamental importance in both physiological and pathological contexts, particularly in hematopoiesis and skeletal maintenance.

The immunomodulatory effects of BMSCs

BMSCs exhibit inherently low immunogenicity due to their unique molecular expression profile. These cells lack MHC-II antigens, Fas ligands, and costimulatory molecules such as B7-1/B7-2 and CD40/CD40L, while maintaining relatively low expression levels of MHC-I. This distinctive molecular signature enables BMSCs to evade recognition by allogeneic T cells [15]. In vitro studies have demonstrated the potent immunosuppressive capacity of BMSCs. When introduced during the early stages of mixed lymphocyte reactions (MLR), BMSCs can suppress lymphocyte proliferation by over 70% and effectively inhibit activation induced by either MLR or phytohemagglutinin (PHA) stimulation [16]. This immunosuppressive effect displays dose-dependency—pre-culture with BMSCs three days prior to MLR reduces allogeneic lymphocyte proliferation by more than 50%. Notably, suboptimal BMSC concentrations may paradoxically enhance lymphocyte proliferation [17]. Remarkably, the immunosuppressive function of BMSCs remains consistent regardless of donor origin or HLA matching, demonstrating equivalent efficacy in autologous, allogeneic, and third-party contexts [18]. The underlying mechanisms involve both cell-contact-dependent inhibition and paracrine signaling through immunomodulatory factors including TGF-β1, HGF, IDO, PGE2, and IL-6. Additionally, BMSCs promote immune tolerance by upregulating populations of CD4 + CD25 + natural regulatory T cells (nTregs) and CD8 + Tregs in coculture systems [19]. The temporal aspect of BMSC administration proves particularly crucial in modulating cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity: when added during MLR initiation, BMSCs significantly attenuate CTL cytolytic function, whereas delayed addition preserves baseline cytotoxicity [19, 20]. Beyond T cell modulation, BMSCs impair dendritic cell (DC) differentiation and maturation by suppressing IL-1 and CD40 ligand expression. This intervention weakens DC endocytic capacity, reduces IL-12 and TNF-α secretion, and downregulates costimulatory markers CD83 and CD86. Regarding NK cells, BMSCs engage inhibitory receptors through their low MHC-I expression to suppress IL-2-driven proliferation, though IL-2-preactivated NK cells maintain cytotoxic potential against BMSCs. Furthermore, BMSCs exhibit dose-dependent inhibition of B cell proliferation and immunoglobulin (IgM, IgG, IgA) production while attenuating B cell chemotaxis via downregulation of CXCR4/CXCR5/CCR7 [21].

These multimodal immunoregulatory properties endow BMSCs with broad clinical potential, including applications in inflammatory disorders (e.g., multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis), transplantation tolerance enhancement through Treg expansion, and post-hematopoietic reconstitution immune rebalancing. The HLA-independent nature and temporal sensitivity of BMSC-mediated immunosuppression allow for flexible clinical protocol design.

Regulatory effects of BMSCs on HSCs

As core components of the bone marrow microenvironment, BMSCs orchestrate HSC homeostasis through multidimensional regulatory networks that govern self-renewal, differentiation, and stress responses. At the physical interaction level, adhesion molecules on BMSCs anchor HSCs to specific bone marrow niches via integrin α4β1 (VLA-4) binding, maintaining their quiescence and anti-apoptotic state [22]. Simultaneously, extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as fibrin and laminin secreted by BMSCs activate the FAK/Src signaling pathway in HSCs through integrin α5β1, suppressing excessive proliferation while enhancing DNA damage repair [23]. BMSCs modulate critical cellular functions through distinct signaling pathways. The Notch pathway, mediated by Jagged-1/Notch interaction, activates HES1/HEY1 to promote self-renewal while inhibiting myeloid differentiation [24]. The SCF/c-Kit axis induces PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, enhancing proliferation and oxidative stress resistance [25]. Additionally, CXCL12/CXCR4 chemotactic signaling facilitates HSC homing and sustains stemness through Wnt/β-catenin activation [26]. These cascades work in concert to regulate stem cell behavior and maintain microenvironmental homeostasis.

BMSC-derived exosomes epigenetically regulate hematopoietic stem cell homeostasis through a dual miRNA-mediated mechanism. They deliver miR-21-5p to suppress PTEN expression, thereby enhancing DNA repair capacity, while miR-29a-3p inhibits DNMT3A activity to delay cellular senescence [27]. BMSCs coordinate a synergistic regulatory network where gap junction-transferred mitochondria and NAD + maintain hypoxic metabolism to counteract oxidative stress, effectively coupling epigenetic regulation with metabolic modulation [28]. Pathologically, BMSCs demonstrate context-dependent dual effects. They suppress HSC overactivation through PGE2/TGF-β1 secretion during acute inflammation [29], while in chronic inflammatory conditions such as myelofibrosis, they promote NF-κB-mediated pathological myelopoiesis via excessive IL-1β/TNF-α production [30, 31]. BMSCs exhibit functional heterogeneity in HSC regulation, with distinct subpopulations employing specialized mechanisms to support hematopoiesis. Clinically, BMSCs co-infusion has demonstrated significant efficacy, reducing post-transplantation platelet recovery time by 30% through senescence modulation pathways [32]. These findings collectively advance our understanding of BMSC-mediated HSC regulation and inform therapeutic development for hematopoietic disorders.

BMSCs-mediated regulation of skeletal system homeostasis

BMSCs play a pivotal role in maintaining skeletal integrity through their multifunctional capacities in osteogenic differentiation, fracture repair, bone remodeling, and microenvironment modulation [33]. As progenitor cells of osteoblasts, BMSCs directly contribute to bone matrix synthesis by producing organic components enriched with collagen and critical non-collagenous proteins including osteocalcin and osteopontin. These protein constituents undergo subsequent mineralization to form structural bone tissue, representing fundamental biological processes in fracture callus formation and bone defect regeneration [34]. This regulatory capacity is significantly enhanced through the secretion of various paracrine trophic factors, particularly bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) that induce osteoblast differentiation [34], transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) that regulates matrix deposition [34], and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) that promotes osteoblast survival [35]. These factors function synergistically to coordinate cellular interactions among endothelial cells and immune cells within the bone niche. Recent advances highlight the emerging role of BMSC-derived exosomal miRNAs as novel modulators of bone metabolism. A notable example is miR-29b-3p, which stimulates osteogenic processes through DKK1 inhibition, offering promising therapeutic potential for targeted intervention in metabolic bone disorders [36].

Homing capacity of BMSCs

The homing capacity of BMSCs represents their intrinsic ability to migrate from circulation to sites of injury or inflammation, which is essential for targeted tissue repair and regeneration [37]. This multistep process begins with chemotactic recruitment mediated by damage-associated molecular patterns, including SDF-1/CXCL12 chemokines and IL-6/TNF-α cytokines, which bind to BMSC surface receptors and activate intracellular PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways to initiate migration [38]. Following mobilization from bone marrow niches into circulation, BMSCs undergo selectin-mediated tethering and integrin-dependent rolling interactions with vascular endothelium at target sites [39]. Subsequent transendothelial migration relies on matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9) to degrade basement membrane components, facilitated by adhesion molecules (ICAM-1 and VCAM-1) and chemokine gradient sensing. After extravasation, BMSCs promote tissue repair through trilineage differentiation and paracrine secretion of regenerative factors that enhance angiogenesis, mitogenesis, and immunomodulation [40]. Homing efficiency is influenced by donor-specific factors [41], and lesion microenvironment characteristics (e.g., inflammatory intensity, hypoxia, and ECM composition), with BMSCs from younger sources exhibiting superior migratory capacity [42]. Clinically, enhancing BMSC homing has therapeutic potential in myocardial infarction [43], osteoporotic fractures [44], and diabetic ulcers [45]. However, challenges remain, including low in vivo homing rates, prompting strategies such as genetic engineering [46], and nanoparticle-mediated chemokine delivery to amplify chemotactic gradients, offering promising avenues for improving regenerative therapies.

Senescent phenotypes of BMSCs

During in vitro expansion, BMSCs frequently undergo replicative senescence, characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest following prolonged passaging - a phenomenon independent of tissue origin. This process manifests through distinct cellular alterations, including cytoplasmic flattening and nuclear enlargement [47]. Concurrently, functional decline occurs alongside dysregulation of critical senescence markers, most notably upregulation of the cell cycle inhibitor p16INK4a and loss of telomerase activity [48], collectively diminishing therapeutic potential. The age-associated functional deterioration presents as: (1) compromised stress response capacity, particularly reduced resistance to oxidative stress, UV radiation, and chemical insults [49]; (2) skewed lineage differentiation potential featuring diminished osteogenic/chondrogenic capacity with concomitant adipogenic predisposition; and (3) aberrant secretion of pro-inflammatory SASP factors [50]. These changes culminate in proliferative arrest and accumulation of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) [51]. Mechanistically, replicative senescence impairs BMSC microenvironment maintenance via: (1) mitochondrial dysfunction-induced ROS overproduction and (2) osteogenic promoter hypermethylation-mediated epigenetic drift [52]. Comprehensive evaluation requires multiparametric assessment including: population doubling time, senescence markers (p21CIP1/γH2AX), differentiation assays, and secretory factor profiling (IL-6/TGF-β) (Table 1). Elucidating these senescence hallmarks enables precise BMSC biological age determination, facilitating: (1) optimized donor selection criteria and (2) therapeutic efficacy enhancement through regenerative approaches like sirtuin activation and mitochondrial transplantation.

Morphodynamic alterations during BMSCs senescence

BMSCs undergo progressive morphological transformations during replicative senescence, characterized by cytoskeletal reorganization and substantial cellular enlargement. Previous studies have established that early-passage BMSCs (P1-P3) maintain a characteristic spindle-shaped, compact morphology that correlates with their robust proliferative capacity. In contrast, late-passage cells (P5+) adopt a flattened morphology with up to 150% increase in surface area, concomitant with markedly diminished mitotic activity-collectively representing hallmark morphological indicators of in vitro growth arrest [53]. Advanced single-cell morphometric analyses have quantitatively characterized this senescence-associated cellular hypertrophy. Clinical-grade BMSCs demonstrate approximately 4.8-fold volumetric expansion from P1 to P9 passages, with P5 representing a statistically significant inflection point (p < 0.05) for this transformation [54]. These morphodynamic changes correlate strongly with two critical senescence markers: RhoA/ROCK pathway activation induces actin stress fiber condensation [55]; Significant elevation of SA-β-gal actosidase activity compared to P1 cells, as quantitatively verified by X-gal assays [56]. Quantitative morphological profiling enables high-specificity discrimination of senescent BMSCs, with low circularity and high nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio serving as reliable identifiers [57]. This non-invasive approach provides robust quality control for therapeutic-grade BMSC production, effectively minimizing batch-to-batch variability in regenerative medicine applications.

Mechanisms of BMSCs senescence: integrated metabolic, genetic, and epigenetic dysregulation

BMSCs senescence arises from the complex interplay of metabolic reprogramming, genomic instability, and epigenetic dysregulation, collectively impairing cellular function through defective transcriptional and translational control [58]. During prolonged in vitro culture, replicative senescence manifests as proliferative arrest, cytoskeletal enlargement, and telomere attrition, exacerbated by persistent DNA damage that triggers p53/p21 and p16INK4A-RB pathways, enforcing irreversible G1/S cell cycle arrest [59]. Simultaneously, mitochondrial dysfunction—characterized by membrane depolarization, excessive ROS production, and bioenergetic failure—synergizes with impaired autophagic flux to disrupt proteostasis and organelle quality control, establishing a self-amplifying cycle of macromolecular damage [60]. Notably, senescent BMSCs display sexually dimorphic SASP, where sex hormones modulate the secretion of inflammatory mediators and matrix-remodeling factors, exacerbating microenvironmental inflammation and impairing hematopoietic niche function. These multilayered mechanisms—encompassing metabolic collapse, genomic erosion, and paracrine signaling—collectively create a pro-senescence environment that accelerates age-related functional decline in BMSCs (Fig. 2).

Telomere attrition-driven senescence

Telomere shortening represents a fundamental hallmark of cellular aging, with diminished telomerase activity recognized as a primary mechanistic driver of BMSCs senescence [61]. Telomeres—specialized nucleoprotein complexes composed of 5’-TTAGGG-3’ repetitive sequences at chromosomal termini—serve as critical mitotic counters in BMSCs, progressively eroding with each cellular division [62]. During population doubling, BMSCs lose approximately 100 bp of telomeric DNA, ultimately reaching the Hayflick limit at a critical threshold of ~ 7.1 kilobase pairs, which triggers replicative senescence [63].

This telomeric crisis activates the p53/p21 and p16INK4A-RB tumor suppressor pathways, inducing irreversible G1/S cell cycle arrest and functional deterioration in both proliferative and differentiation capacities. Notably, experimental overexpression of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) has been shown to extend telomere length, restore replicative potential, and delay senescence onset [64]. These findings establish telomerase modulation as a scientifically validated anti-aging strategy, offering potential therapeutic avenues for preserving BMSCs function.

Epigenetic deregulation in BMSCs senescence

BMSCs senescence involves coordinated epigenetic dysregulation across multiple layers, including DNA methylation disorders, histone modification abnormalities, and non-coding RNA network imbalances. Genome-wide analysis reveals progressive DNA hypomethylation coupled with localized hypermethylation at promoter regions of key osteogenic genes (Runx2 and SP7), which suppresses transcription factor binding and significantly impairs osteogenic differentiation potential [65]. Concurrent histone modification changes feature increased repressive H3K27me3 marks and decreased activating H4K16ac levels at osteogenic loci, driving chromatin condensation and transcriptional silencing [66]. These epigenetic alterations are further amplified by dysregulated non-coding RNAs: miR-195 directly suppresses telomerase activity by targeting TERT mRNA, while exosome-derived miR-183-5p inhibits BMSCs proliferation through negative regulation of the MAPK/ERK pathway [67]. Together, these interconnected epigenetic disturbances create a self-reinforcing cycle that accelerates BMSCs functional decline during aging.

Oxidative stress cascades in BMSCs senescence

Oxidative stress serves as a critical mediator of BMSCs functional impairment through multifaceted pathological mechanisms, primarily by inducing cellular senescence and apoptosis via activation of PI3K/AKT and p53 signaling pathways [68]. In conditions like femoral head necrosis, ischemia-hypoxia-induced mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction generates excessive ROS, creating a localized oxidative stress microenvironment - experimentally modeled using high-concentration H2O2 exposure [69]. Under such oxidative stress, BMSCs exhibit characteristic mitochondrial dysfunction, including membrane potential collapse, impaired bioenergetics, and accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which further exacerbates intracellular ROS accumulation. This self-perpetuating cycle leads to extensive DNA damage, culminating in either apoptotic cell death or stress-induced premature senescence - evidenced by elevated β-galactosidase activity and upregulated expression of senescence markers. The surviving BMSCs population demonstrates permanent cell cycle arrest and typical SASP, establishing oxidative stress as a key driver of both acute cytotoxicity and chronic functional decline in BMSCs.

Genomic instability in BMSCs senescence

As the fundamental carrier of genetic information, DNA plays a critical role in maintaining genomic stability. In BMSCs, DNA damage accumulation triggers either cellular senescence or apoptosis, with unrepaired lesions significantly increasing risks of malignant transformation and age-related functional decline [70]. During in vitro expansion, persistent DNA damage activates the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway, leading to irreversible cell cycle arrest and markedly reduced proliferative capacity. The phosphorylation of γH2AX - a histone H2A variant - serves as a sensitive biomarker for detecting double-strand breaks and evaluating DNA repair efficiency [71]. BMSCs face DNA damage threats from both exogenous sources and endogenous processes. Chronic oxidative stress, resulting from disrupted redox homeostasis between ROS production and antioxidant defenses, emerges as a major contributor to genomic instability. Sustained high ROS levels compromise DNA replication accuracy, inhibit proliferation-related signaling pathways, and accelerate the development of SASP in BMSCs [72]. Hyperoxia-based in vitro studies confirm that excessive ROS activates key pro-senescence pathways including PI3K/AKT/mTOR/FOXO3 and p38/MAPK cascades, which play central roles in mediating cellular senescence. The therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways is evidenced by reduced MSC apoptosis following their pharmacological inhibition [73]. Quantitative analyses demonstrate significantly higher ROS levels in senescent BMSCs compared to normal cells, establishing oxidative stress as both an indicator and driver of cellular aging [74]. Notably, ROS-induced damage extends beyond genomic instability to impair BMSCs’ differentiation capacity through mitochondrial dysfunction and epigenetic alterations. By disrupting redox-sensitive transcription factors, oxidative stress creates a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that perpetuates the senescent state. Therefore, maintaining redox balance through antioxidant interventions or enhancing endogenous ROS-scavenging systems represents a crucial strategy for preserving BMSCs function and extending their therapeutic lifespan.

Autophagy dysregulation in BMSCs senescence

Autophagy, a highly conserved lysosomal degradation pathway essential for cellular homeostasis, exhibits a dual role in regulating BMSCs senescence, functioning as both an anti-senescence mechanism through basal selective autophagy and a pro-senescence factor when generally activated. The TP53/p53 pathway mediates this balance by transcriptionally activating autophagy-related genes ULK1, ATG5, ATG7, where sustained activation promotes general autophagy and senescence progression [75].

In BMSCs, autophagy deficiency accelerates senescence, as demonstrated by ATG7 knockdown studies showing suppressed autophagy accompanied by increased cell death and senescence markers, while pharmacological inhibitors (3MA, bafilomycin) exacerbate oxidative stress-induced senescence, confirming autophagy’s protective role against stress [76]. This phenomenon extends to the BMSC microenvironment, where aged macrophages with impaired autophagy exhibit reduced autophagic flux, p62 accumulation, and elevated β-galactosidase activity - all reversible through ATG5 overexpression [77]. These findings collectively position autophagy modulation as a promising therapeutic strategy to counteract BMSCs senescence and enhance their regenerative potential.

Small molecule compounds and inhibitors to inhibit the aging of BMSCs

Small molecule compounds and inhibitors offer promising targeted strategies to inhibit BMSCs senescence by intervening in specific core aging pathways. Antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC) effectively counteract the oxidative stress cascade by reducing the elevated ROS levels characteristic of senescent BMSCs, thereby preventing oxidative stress-induced DNA damage and functional decline. Specifically, NAC enhances intracellular glutathione production, which directly addresses the redox imbalance in aged BMSCs, leading to decreased apoptosis and senescence markers—SA-β-gal, and promoting osteogenic differentiation capacity [78]. Vitamin E derivatives maintain BMSCs proliferative capacity by mimicking hypoxic conditions and reducing mitochondrial oxygen consumption [79]. The mTOR inhibitor rapamycin suppresses SASP expression in senescent BMSCs while restoring their differentiation and proliferation potential. Epigenetic modulators like the DNMT inhibitor RG108 can reactivate silenced tumor suppressor genes, improving stem cell function and reducing senescence markers [80]. These compounds provide versatile pharmacological approaches to counteract BMSCs aging and enhance their therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine.

Growth factors and hormone regulation inhibit the senescence of BMSCs

Growth factors and hormones play crucial roles in inhibiting BMSCs senescence through multiple mechanisms. Overexpression of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) significantly enhances the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs and improves the function of senescent cells [81]. Essential growth factors like fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2) are particularly effective in maintaining BMSCs’ self-renewal and proliferative potential, thereby delaying cellular senescence and preserving their therapeutic value [82]. Specifically, FGF-2 has been shown to restore BMSCs’ differentiation and proliferation capabilities by modulating ROS levels and inhibiting the SASP. Additionally, melatonin, a potent antioxidant hormone, enhances BMSCs’ antioxidant defenses and immunomodulatory functions by regulating ROS production, effectively suppressing cellular senescence [81]. These findings demonstrate that growth factors and hormonal regulation are vital for counteracting BMSCs aging, offering promising new approaches for anti-aging therapies.

Genetic engineering methods inhibit the senescence of BMSCs

Genetic engineering methods provide the most precise strategies to inhibit BMSCs senescence by directly correcting defective genetic and metabolic programs. Overexpression of ATG7, a key regulator of autophagosome formation, directly targets autophagy dysregulation, a core senescence mechanism. This intervention has been demonstrated to restore autophagic flux, prevent the accumulation of damaged cellular components, and consequently prevent stem cell senescenc [83]. Additionally, knockdown of LRRc17 activates mitophagy and enhances the colocalization between LC3B and mitochondria, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial impairment and cell senescence. This approach has been shown to maintain the regenerative abilities of BMSCs by removing damaged mitochondria and improving mitochondrial function [84]. These genetic modifications provide targeted strategies to alleviate oxidative stress-induced stem cell aging while preserving their therapeutic potential.

Summary and prospects

In conclusion, BMSCs hold significant promise for regenerative medicine and the treatment of age-related diseases. However, their therapeutic potential is limited by senescence-related functional decline. This narrative review has Comprehensively examined the key mechanisms driving BMSCs senescence, including telomere attrition, DNA damage, oxidative stress, epigenetic alterations, and autophagy dysregulation. Looking forward, the field should transition towards the concept of “precision anti-senescence therapy” for BMSCs. Future interventions ought to be tailored based on the predominant senescence phenotype identified in a patient’s BMSCs or the specific pathological context of the age-related disorder, rather than applying a uniform approach. For instance, BMSCs from an osteoporotic individual might benefit most from strategies that reverse the epigenetic repression of osteogenic genes using DNMT inhibitors or enhance osteo-anabolic signaling with growth factors like FGF-2 and IGF-1. In contrast, BMSCs contributing to chronic inflammation could be targeted with SASP-neutralizing agents such as mTOR inhibitors. The strategies summarized in this review provide a foundational toolkit for such phenotype-directed interventions. Therefore, future research must prioritize several critical areas: first, elucidating the complex crosstalk between different aging pathways to identify novel therapeutic targets; second, developing robust biomarkers to accurately diagnose specific BMSCs senescence subtypes in patients; and third, rigorously evaluating the long-term safety and efficacy of these targeted anti-aging strategies in clinical settings. With a deepening understanding of BMSCs biology and continuous technological advancements, the development of these precise anti-aging strategies is expected to accelerate, ultimately improving the quality of life for the aging population and advancing the field of regenerative medicine. The successful translation of these discoveries into clinical benefits will necessitate close collaboration among researchers, clinicians, and industry partners.
