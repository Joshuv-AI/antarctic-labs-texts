Translation, psychometric testing and implementation of the perineal assessment tool for assessing incontinence-associated dermatitis risk in semi-intensive care patients
Authors: Supin Simim, Samoraphop Banharak, Ladawan Panpanit, Sutin Chanaboon, Chakkarin Sommana, Chantra Promnoi, Wipa Sae-Sia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin injury is a growing issue among older adults, especially when admitted to an intensive care unit or long-term care facility. Skin prevention is a key point in dealing with this issue, and prompt and effective screening instruments should be used to identify risks and start preventive interventions and care. This descriptive study aimed to translate and examine the psychometric property and implement the Perineal Assessment Tool for incontinence associated dermatitis risk assessment. The English version was translated into Thai following the international guidelines from the World Health Organization by the bilinguals. From May to June 2023, 119 patients from the semi-intensive care unit were included using a consecutive sampling method. The research instruments included the demographic questionnaires, the original English version, and the translated Thai version. The Thai Version had a content validity index, an index of item-objective congruence, a Pearson correlation coefficient of percent agreement, the Pearson correlation coefficient of the interrater reliability, the Kappa coefficient of interrater reliability, the Pearson correlation coefficient of the intra-rater reliability, and the Kappa coefficient of intra-rater reliability ranked from 0.75 to 1.00. The 21.95% had a high risk of developing incontinence-associated dermatitis, which was associated with females, the length of stay in hospital and semi-intensive care unit longer than or equal to four days, receiving antibiotics, disease severity greater than or equal to 20 points, the albumin level less than 3.8 g/dl, and neuropathy and brain disorders. In conclusion, this instrument has high validity and reliability and can assess patients at risk of incontinence-associated dermatitis. This tool will help with early risk detection and guide for planning to manage various risk conditions to prevent dermatitis instead of taking care of them after the skin has already been damaged.Keywords: Incontinence Associated Dermatitis, Perineal Assessment Tool, Psychometric test, TranslationSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD) is a condition of skin damage associated with moisture and irritation1. This damage is a typical skin lesion among critically ill patients and patients in long-term care facilities, especially older patients with incontinence1,2. It is caused by the skin coming into contact with urine, containing urea and ammonia or stool, containing enzymes, chemicals, and bacteria that cause alkalinity (increased pH) or both feces and urine, along with frequent skin cleaning after bowel movements using soap or cleaning agents that cause chemical irritation and abrasion2. This irritation allows substances to penetrate the cell, reducing the skin’s protective function and allowing Bacteria to grow easily3. When it occurs with friction from materials that wrap around the skin, such as clothing, mattresses, or chairs, it causes an inflammatory skin condition called incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD)3. It was found that the patients admitted to the intensive care unit had health conditions and treatments that affected the strength of the skin, such as decreased blood flow, hypoxia, restriction of movement, and the use of inotropes4, then easily experience IAD.

A meta-analysis of the prevalence of IAD in China that collected data from 10 studies, with a total sample of 40,039 patients, revealed that the prevalence of IAD was 48.02% among patients with fecal incontinence, 11.35% among patients with urinary incontinence and 25.78% among patients with dual incontinence5. A study in the United States among 5,342 patients also found that the prevalence of IAD was 21.3%, which was various levels of severity, including mild (52.3%), moderate (27.9%), severe (9.2%), and secondary infection 11.6%6. In a specific setting study, an Intensive Care Unit (ICU), 60% of critically ill patients were at a high risk of IAD7, and 19%8 and 33.33%9 was found as IAD which about 17.10% of patients developed IAD as 4–12 days after admission10. The statistics and its impact make this skin injury a concern.

Background

When having an IAD, it causes discomfort, pain, burning, and itching in the area where it occurs10. It also affects daily activities and sleep, reducing quality of life10. If severe, it can cause recurrent urinary tract infections and pressure sores up to six times higher risk, resulting in additional expenses, care, and extended hospital stays10. A study of prevention and care for IAD among critically ill patients receiving treatment in the ICU9 formulated a set of nursing care indices, including (1) IAD risk assessment and (2) dermatological care to prevent and treat IAD. A systematic review by Banharak et al. (2021) also highlights comprehensive approaches to IAD prevention and care among older adults, including assessment, incontinence and causative factor management, skin cleansing, application of moisturizing and barrier products, body positioning, nutrition promotion, health education and training, and outcome evaluation11. These two studies indicate that the first element of IAD prevention is risk assessment, which is early detection using risk assessment screening demonstrates the nurse’s role in caring for IAD12. Therefore, proactive risk assessment by nurses is essential to deliver effective care and prevent IAD, ensuring optimal patient outcomes.

The literature review on risk assessment tools for IAD highlights the Perineal Assessment Tool (PAT) developed by Nix (2002) as a reliable tool for evaluating IAD risk13. Its psychometric properties have been validated, and it is straightforward to use, making it suitable as a standard tool for risk assessment. PAT can help identify at-risk patients, encourage proactive nursing interventions for IAD prevention, and generate valuable data on IAD incidence13. Sharma et al. (2021) also utilized this tool to develop care interventions for incontinent patients7. Additionally, PAT can be used to educate patients and their families during hospitalization and after discharge, promoting awareness and understanding of IAD risk, which may reduce incidence rates7. Despite its potential, there is currently no standardized IAD risk assessment tool or IAD data in the study ward, limiting the medical team’s ability to develop effective interventions for IAD prevention.

Currently, the risk and incidence of IAD among patients in semi-intensive medical care units in Thailand remain unreported. Although extensive IAD research exists internationally, studies on this topic are limited in Thailand. Most available literature focuses on the roles of enterostomal therapy nurses in wound care, ostomy, and incontinence3,12, as well as factors related to the competence of registered nurses in IAD prevention among critically ill patients14. These limited studies highlight the need for instrument translation, risk assessment, and statistical data on IAD in Thailand.

To address IAD prevention, a risk assessment tool that meets international standards is essential. Nix’s (2002) Perineal Assessment Tool (PAT) has been translated into several languages, with studies establishing cutoff points for IAD risk levels13. However, an accurate Thai translation of PAT following international translation guidelines has yet to be developed. This study aimed to fill this gap by translating PAT into Thai using the World Health Organization’s translation guidelines15, testing its psychometric properties, and implementing the tool for risk screening among patients in semi-intensive care. The tool’s translation, testing, and application were conducted to ensure its validity and reliability for future use, establish IAD risk data among critically ill Thai patients, and inform the development of targeted IAD prevention interventions.

AIMS

To translate and examine the validity and reliability of the Perineal Assessment Tool Thai Version (PAT-T) and investigate the risk of IAD among patients in the semi-intensive medical care unit.

Methods

Design

This descriptive study aimed to translate and examine the validity and reliability of PAT-T and investigate the risk of IAD among patients in the semi-intensive medical care unit.

Setting and sampling

There were two sample groups: a group of patients and a group of nurses. The first group was male and female patients aged 18 years and over who were admitted to the semi-intensive medical care unit of a large university hospital in the Northeast of Thailand, and the second group was the registered nurses in the same hospital. Validity, reliability and rigor

The tool was translated according to WHO guidelines concerning the context and language of the target population. In addition, the criteria for selecting translators for both forward (Nurse professional using bilingual) and back translation (unrelated health professional using bilingual) were set to reduce bias from the translators during the translation process. Finally, interrater and intra-rater reliability was assessed to reflect and confirm the research instrument’s reliability, which was translated correctly according to the meaning of the original version and suitable for the language and context of the target population being studied to reflect the instrument’s reliability comprehensively. All translation processes strictly followed the WHO’s guidelines to maintain the meaning and quality of the original instrument, which was expected to be suitable for our target population.

Results

Sample’s demographic characteristics

Of 119 samples whom PAT-T assessed, most of them were male (56.30%), aged 60 years and over (68.07%), with an average age of 64.80 years (S.D. =15.54). The BMI was considered overweight (42.02%). The average BMI was 23.10 kg/m2 (S.D. =7.36). The length of hospital stay was four days or more (55.46%), with the mean length of stay of 5.46 days (S.D. =6.44). The length of stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit was less than four days (74.79%), with a mean length of stay of 3.30 days (S.D. =2.90). Most of them did not receive sleeping pills (91.60%) and laxatives (85.71%) but received antibiotics (80.67%). The majority received one type of antibiotics (67.71%). The disease severity scores assessed by the APACHE II score ranged from 0 to 19 points (63.87%). The albumin level was less than 3.8 g/dl (73.95%). The score of the ability to perform activities of daily living assessed by the Barthel Activities of Daily Living (ADL) was less than 12 points (91.60%). Most were at risk of malnutrition, assessed by SPENT (77.31%), and at risk of severe malnutrition, assessed by NAF (69.57%). The causes of the admission to the semi-intensive medical care unit were health problems related to the respiratory system, the cardiovascular system, the gastrointestinal and liver systems, the nervous system, the urinary system, and other diseases (50.00%, 12.67%, 10.67%, 7.33%, 4.67%, and 14.67%, respectively). The underlying diseases included high blood pressure, respiratory system diseases, cancer, and diabetes (15.45%, 14.23%, 13.82%, and 12.69%, respectively) (Table 1).

Reliability

Interrater reliability and intra-rater reliability were verified using the following steps: (1) Pearson correlation coefficient was analyzed to find out the relationship of ratings between the first rater and the second rater (interrater reliability) and the relationship of ratings within one rater (intra-rater reliability) with bilingual expertise who assessed the original English version and the Thai version of the tool. (2) The Kappa coefficient was analyzed to compare the consistency between the first rater and the second rater (interrater reliability) and compare the consistency within one rater (intra-rater reliability) with bilingual expertise who assessed the original English version and the Thai version of the tool.

Interrater reliability

Based on the assessment among 119 samples by two raters, the Pearson correlation coefficient was determined to analyze the total score, and the Kappa coefficient was used to analyze scores by item. It was found that PAT-T had interrater reliability with the Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.99 (r = 0.99) and the Kappa coefficient of 0.92 (k = 0.92) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to translate and examine the validity and reliability of PAT-T and investigate the risk of IAD among patients in the semi-intensive medical care unit. The results can be discussed as follows.

Quality of PAT-T

Based on the translation and validation of the PAT-T, the Content Validity Index (CVI) was 0.90, and the Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) was 0.75, indicating acceptable reliability, as both values meet or exceed the threshold of 0.7518. This result aligns with findings from Brandao et al. (2018), who translated the PAT into German and reported a CVI of 0.91 or higher19. Experts suggested refining the interpretation of the “Duration of irritant,” which could cause confusion. The original version indicates that patients with linen/pad changes at least every two hours frequently urinate or defecate, increasing their exposure to irritants13. However, experts noted that more frequent linen/pad changes reduce exposure duration, implying a lower risk than for patients with changes every four to eight hours. Nevertheless, the tool was not modified. It still adhered to the original content because this item focuses on how often the patient has uncontrolled feces and urination, which are the main factors causing IAD. However, explanation/clarification was noted regarding the use of the tool so the research team could have a common understanding of the meaning of the original version before using the tool to collect data. Suppose anyone is interested in using this tool in nursing practice and research studies. In that case, they must understand the origins and meaning of each risk assessment and the main objectives of each risk assessment according to the recommendations of this research.

Percent agreement was assessed by surveying the opinions of 30 registered nurses by rating 1–10 points. It was found that the percent agreement of PAT-T was at the highest level with a mean score of 9.45 and the mean item scores of 9.37, 9.60, 9.53, and 9.30, respectively. These scores were higher than those of a Nix (2002) study among 102 nurses, which found that the mean item scores were 8.27, 7.66, 7.85, and 8.40, respectively13. This difference in result may be because the period for conducting this study is more than 20 years compared to the study of Nix (2002)13. IAD has been widely studied, and knowledge has been disseminated in the country and abroad. Therefore, nurses have more knowledge and understanding in this matter, which may be an essential factor, causing this study to get higher scores in assessing the percent agreement of nurses than in the previous study.

Regarding interrater reliability, the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.99, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.99, indicating that the tool has very high interrater reliability. This result is consistent with a study by Nix (2002), which found that interrater reliability was assessed, and the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.97 (95%CI 0.92–0.99 p < 0.001)13. In the present study, intra-rater reliability was also checked. The Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.97, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.97, indicating the tool has high intra-rater reliability. This result is because the translation process was strict at every step. Specific qualifications of the translators were specified. They must have appropriate knowledge and abilities of the topic being translated and expertise outside the topic being translated to reduce bias in translating the tool. In addition, the target context and cultural differences were of concern during the translation process. The words clearly conveying the meaning of communication were chosen, making it easy to understand. As a result, the tool can be used in assessment following the original version.

Although the tool’s validity is high, some cautions in using PAT-T are provided. The raters or users should have an understanding of the risk score in the second subscale, which is “Duration of irritant” (length of time the skin is exposed to an irritant)” in the following two issues13. 1) If the linen/pad is changed frequently, at least every two hours, as mentioned earlier, it indicates an increased frequency of skin contact with an irritant or a greater number of bowel movements, which results in skin irritation. As a result, there is a higher chance of being exposed to irritants than the patient with fewer linen/pad changes or a small number of bowel movements at least every four or eight hours. 2) In the case where the patient has an abnormal level of consciousness and is unable to express his or her needs and the caregiver detects that the patient has defecated or urinated every time he or she is moved or turned over and the linen/sheet is changed at least every two hours, this patient will have a higher risk level for the second subscale than the patient who urinates and defecates frequently but can express his or her need to clean the skin after defecation or can defecate into the device or be able to go to the bathroom. This situation includes patients wearing urinary support devices such as urinary catheters and condom catheters for males. In the second subscale, the ability to communicate the need for cleaning after a bowel movement, using medical equipment for excretion, and the ability to defecate in the bathroom should be assessed.

The risk of IAD according to the participant’s sociodemographic and health characteristics

Of 119 samples, 21.95% of them were at a high risk of developing IAD. This result is similar to the study of Li et al. (2019) that assessed the risk of patients in intensive care units and an affiliated home care service using PAT, which found that 21.30% of patients were at high risk of IAD17. However, it was lower than the study by Sharma et al. (2021), which assessed the risk of IAD using PAT among critically ill patients7. It was found that 60% of critically ill patients were at high risk of IAD. These different results may be due to differences in the sample groups, which comprised more older and critically ill patients with more severe illnesses than semi-critically ill patients. As a result, the number of patients at risk was three times higher than in this study.

In this study, it was found that the sociodemographic and health characteristics of the participants were associated with a high risk of developing IAD. These characteristics were female, the length of hospital stay longer than or equal to four days, the length of stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit greater than or equal to four days, receiving antibiotics, the disease severity (APACHE II score) greater than or equal to 20 points, the albumin level less than 3.8 g/dl, and neuropathy and brain disorders. It can be explained that the skin around the perineal area of females is more exposed to urine than males due to the physical structure of women with a shorter urinary tract, causing more frequent urination, and the structure of the perineum area easy to irritate and contact with the urine and feces20. From the observation, the male samples used urinary support devices such as condom catheters, which can reduce contact with urine. The length of time the skin is exposed to irritants is also a factor that causes the skin’s strength to decrease, resulting in the risk of developing IAD13. Therefore, a high risk was found less in males than in females. This finding is consistent with the study of Kayser et al. (2019), which found that females were at risk of IAD (p = 0.034)8.

Studies have shown that an APACHE II score of 20 or higher indicates a high-risk group. In this study, the mean APACHE II score was 20.46, suggesting that this patient group was at elevated risk for IAD. This finding aligns with Van Damme et al. (2018), who found a significant association between high APACHE II scores (Mean = 24.90, SD = 7.70) and IAD development21. Additionally, Chianca et al. (2017) reported that patients with trauma diagnoses were significantly more likely to develop IAD (p = 0.017)22. Consistently, our study found that underlying neurological and brain conditions were linked to a higher risk of IAD. This elevated risk may be due to hygiene challenges in maintaining skin integrity around the perineal area, as patients with decreased consciousness or mobility limitations may experience reduced quality in hygiene care and prolonged exposure to irritants. This explanation is supported by Kottner et al. (2014), who found that patients dependent on others for daily activities were significantly associated with IAD (p < 0.001)23. Furthermore, most participants in this study were older adults whose skin is more prone to tearing and deterioration due to reduced tissue strength24.

In terms of the length of hospital stay and the length of stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit, the findings revealed that the length of hospital stay and the length of stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit greater than or equal to four days were associated with a high-risk group. In this study, the patient’s average length of hospital stay was 9.00 days (2–49 days), and the average stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit was 4.61 days (2–20 days). The length of hospital stay increases the risk of complications from hospital admission, such as hospital-acquired infections, skin lesions, and IAD25. From the observation, the majority of the samples were put on a ventilator and special equipment and slept in bed all the time, resulting in being restricted in movement, having limitations in communication, decreased ability to do daily activities, and needing help for cleaning up after a bowel movement. This physical and communication restriction affects the duration the skin is exposed to irritants. In addition, patients on ventilators must be fed through a tube instead of eating by mouth. Tube feeding is another factor associated with loose bowel movements, causing IAD26.

Receiving antibiotics was also a factor related to the development of IAD. Using certain drugs can cause the risk of developing IAD. Although the duration of the drug’s effect on IAD is unknown, research has shown that the use of antibiotics, steroids, immunosuppressants, and drugs that affect blood flow increases the chance of developing IAD27,28. It can be further explained that these drugs may affect the skin’s protective function and alter the skin’s microbiome or gut. Antibiotics are another risk factor that can cause diarrhea13.

It was also found that the albumin level below 3.8 g/dl was associated with a high-risk group. It is because albumin plays a role in regulating blood pressure in blood vessels. It permeates between the cells outside the blood vessels, causing them to swell and press against blood vessels. As a result, blood flow to the tissues in that area decreases, affecting the skin’s strength29. Moreover, albumin levels affect tissue strength. The skin of patients with low albumin is weak and quickly has inflammation or skin wounds25. Weak skin occurs when exposed to other stimulating factors such as sleeping for a long time, high temperatures, inactivity, and being moved to a bed subject to shearing and friction forces, making patients more vulnerable to damage and injury.

Limitations

The tool translation and risk assessment study for IAD involved experts, nurses, and patients in a semi-intensive medical care unit, which may limit the generalizability of the risk percentage to other patient groups. Therefore, additional studies should evaluate the tool’s quality in diverse professional and patient groups, such as critically ill patients and those in general medical wards, to provide a more comprehensive view across various settings. Additionally, as this study included a small sample size, future research should be conducted with a larger population to strengthen the findings.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings revealed that PAT-T had validity with a CVI of 0.90, an IOC of 0.75, and a Pearson correlation coefficient of percent agreement of 0.94. In terms of reliability, the Pearson correlation coefficient of the interrater reliability was 0.99, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.99. In addition, the Pearson correlation coefficient of intra-rater reliability was 0.97, and the Kappa coefficient was 0.97. Also, 21.95% of the samples had a high risk of IAD. The participants’ sociodemographic and health characteristics associated with a high risk of developing IAD were female (p = 0.012), the length of hospital stay longer than or equal to four days (p = < 0.001), the length of stay in the semi-intensive medical care unit longer than or equal to four days (p = 0.023), receiving antibiotics (p = 0.024), the disease severity (APACHE II score) greater than or equal to 20 points (p = 0.033), the albumin level less than 3.8 g/dl (p = 0.016), and neuropathy and brain disorders (p = 0.023). This study indicated that the PAT-T has the potential to assess the risk of IAD among hospitalized adults and older adults, pending further research on its implementation and effectiveness in clinical settings.
