On the optimal regret of collaborative personalized linear bandits
Bruce Huang, Ruida Zhou, Lin F. Yang, Suhas Diggavi

Abstract
Stochastic linear bandits are a fundamental model for sequential decision making, where an agent selects a vector-valued action and receives a noisy reward with expected value given by an unknown linear function. Although well studied in the single-agent setting, many real-world scenarios involve multiple agents solving heterogeneous bandit problems, each with a different unknown parameter. Applying single agent algorithms independently ignores cross-agent similarity and learning opportunities. This paper investigates the optimal regret achievable in collaborative personalized linear bandits. We provide an information-theoretic lower bound that characterizes how the number of agents, the interaction rounds, and the degree of heterogeneity jointly affect regret. We then propose a new two-stage collaborative algorithm that achieves the optimal regret. Our analysis models heterogeneity via a hierarchical Bayesian framework and introduces a novel information-theoretic technique for bounding regret. Introduction

Stochastic linear bandits are a fundamental model in sequential decision-making, where in each round, an agent selects a vector-valued action and receives a stochastic reward with expectation equal to the inner product between the action and an unknown parameter vector
[
14
]
. This setting has been extensively studied in the single-agent case, where the goal is to maximize cumulative reward over time through efficient exploration and exploitation
[
19
,
12
,
1
]
.

In this paper, we consider a generalized multi-agent setting, motivated by many practical scenarios such as distributed decision systems
[
13
,
24
]
, personalized adaptive interfaces
[
16
,
11
]
, and multi-device learning
[
4
]
—where in each round, a set of
m
agents must each select an action and receive feedback from a stochastic, potentially heterogeneous reward function.
A natural baseline approach is to run a standard single-agent linear bandit algorithm independently for each agent. While straightforward, this approach entirely ignores potential similarities across agents. When the agents’ environments are similar, such independent learning leads to redundant exploration and a suboptimal use of data. In contrast, our goal is to develop collaborative learning algorithms that adaptively share information across agents—without knowing in advance how similar or different they are. This raises a fundamental question:

How can collaboration reduce regret under unknown heterogeneity?

To address the question, we model heterogeneity using a hierarchical/empirical Bayesian framework, where each agent’s unknown parameter vector is drawn from an
unknown
population distribution. Then the variance of this population captures the degree of heterogeneity. Although the population distribution can be arbitrary in the general framework, in this work we specifically study the Gaussian population case. The Gaussian population distribution captures the heterogeneity through the population variance, around the
unknown
arbitrary mean vector without any prior on it. Besides being a useful theoretical framework, effectively this population distribution has been extensively used in algorithms for personalized FL, in supervised frameworks
[
18
]
and references therein. We consider an unknown population distribution and do not assume further prior distribution on the population parameters since the agents will not have such information in practice.

We then propose a novel two-stage algorithm to leverage collaboration: in the first
collaborative learning stage
, agents jointly perform phased elimination to identify a promising subset of actions by pooling their information. In the second
personalized learning stage
, agents refine their individual models based on local feedback. This structure allows the algorithm to smoothly interpolate between joint learning (when agents are similar) and local adaptation (when they are not). We carefully design a threshold to guide the transition between the two stages, so that collaboration is activated only when it benefits learning. In the analysis, we show that the algorithm achieves a regret bound that can be significantly smaller than the independent-learning baseline when the number of rounds is small—highlighting the benefit of collaboration in the data-scarce regime.

To show the optimality of our algorithm, we derive an
information-theoretic lower bound
on the regret across different levels of heterogeneity. A central challenge in this analysis stems from the nature of heterogeneity: unlike the classical single-agent setting, where the worst-case parameter governs the lower bound, we must now consider the
worst-case population distribution
over agents’ parameters. To overcome this, we introduce novel techniques involving
rotation and coupling
to construct hard instances and analyze the regret. Our lower bound precisely characterizes how the population variance
\sigma^{2}
affects collaborative learning. Furthermore, we show that our proposed algorithm matches the lower bound, achieving regret that is
both globally and individually optimal
.

Our Contributions

•

Related Work

There is extensive work in the bandit and linear bandit literature (see, e.g., the comprehensive review in

. In most of the works, agents in the same cluster share a common unknown parameter. However, variations in users’ parameters within the same cluster can significantly change the problem. Small variations between parameters in the same cluster are allowed in
[
2

O(1/m\sqrt{n})
, and applying single-agent methods to users with such variation gives optimal regret bound. This does not answer how heterogeneous data can help through collaboration.
[

also propose a two-stage algorithm, while the algorithm does not adapt to heterogeneity. Other works characterize similarity between the agents with different structures like networks
[
3
,
23
]
, or consider agents sharing a global component in their unknown parameters
[
15
]
. Our work considers an unknown population model characterizing heterogeneity instead. We discuss the two most related works next.

After completing our work, we recently became aware of the work
[
5
]
. They consider heterogeneous linear contextual bandits, and they measure heterogeneity as the maximum difference,
\varepsilon
, between any pair of individual parameters. They propose an algorithm and provide upper and lower bounds that depend on
\varepsilon
for the problem. In our work, we model heterogeneity differently through unbounded population distribution, and thus the value of
\varepsilon
can go to infinity even when the level of heterogeneity is low in our case. Furthermore, their upper and lower bounds do not match in all regimes with up to a
\sqrt{d}
gap for their model, whereas our results show matching bounds in all regimes. Additionally, due to the different modeling for heterogeneity, their lower bound techniques cannot be applied to our setup. We develop novel techniques in Section
4
to resolve challenges emerging in our minimax lower bound.

Another related work is
[
9
]
, which proposes a hierarchical Bayesian framework to model heterogeneity in multi-armed bandits,
and introduces a hierarchical Thompson Sampling algorithm for the setting. A major limitation of their approach is the assumption of a known prior distribution over the population model. The case of fixed unknown population parameters is important since agents do not have information on such prior distribution in practice. However, the case is not captured in their setting and their result does not apply. In contrast to their pure Bayesian setting, we adopt an empirical Bayes approach without assuming any prior distribution on the unknown population parameters, which can make the analysis significantly more challenging. Furthermore, their work does not provide a regret lower bound, whereas we establish a matching lower bound that demonstrates the optimality of our proposed algorithm.

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{C}),

Main Results & Technical Overview

3.1
Algorithm Overview: Collaborative Personalized Phased elimination

In this section, we briefly introduce our proposed algorithm in preparation for our main results in Section
3.2
. The regret analysis and algorithm details can be found in Section
6
and Section
7
.

We propose Collaborative Personalized Phased Elimination (CP-PE) by adapting the method of phased elimination with
G
-optimal design exploration in
[
14
]
(Chapter 21) to our heterogeneous setting. A phased elimination algorithm shrinks the action set in each phase by eliminating the actions that are too far from the estimated parameter with a decreasing target error. Additionally, CP-PE consists of two stages: the
collaborative learning stage
and the
personalized learning stage
. A threshold is carefully designed to guide the transition between the two stages so that collaboration is activated only
when it benefits learning.

In the collaborative stage, all users perform the phased elimination algorithm together as if they have the same unknown parameter. This benefits learning when the target error is larger than the degree of heterogeneity since estimating
{\bm{\mu}}
already gives a relatively good estimation to all
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s. However, as target error drops below the degree of heterogeneity, collaboratively learning an estimation on
{\bm{\mu}}
does not give good enough estimations on
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s anymore. Thus, all users transit to the local stage, where they perform phased elimination on their own to refine their results.

Regret Characterization

In Theorem
3.1
, we identify three distinct regimes for the number of rounds

data-scarce regime:

n=o(dm^{-1}\sigma^{-2})

n=\Theta(dm^{-2\gamma}\sigma^{-2})

n=\omega(d\sigma^{-2})

Equivalently, by fixing

•

Data-scarce regime
corresponds to
low heterogeneity

\sigma=\omega(\sqrt{dm^{-1}n^{-1}})

•

Intermediate regime

\sigma=\Theta(\sqrt{cdm^{-2\gamma}n^{-1}})

•

Data-abundant regime
corresponds to

\sigma=\omega(\sqrt{dn^{-1}})

The theorem shows that the regret behavior varies across these regimes, indicating whether collaboration among users is beneficial. Below, we elaborate on each regime.

In the data-scarce regime
, where
n
is small or
\sigma
is low, we prove that no policy can achieve a regret smaller than
\Omega(d\sqrt{mn})
. This matches the minimax regret lower bound for the standard stochastic linear bandit with
mn
total samples. This result can be interpreted in two ways. First, it shows that collaboration is highly beneficial when heterogeneity is sufficiently low: users can collectively estimate the shared population parameter
{\bm{\mu}}
, achieving performance comparable to the homogeneous case. Second, when
n
is small, individual users lack sufficient data to accurately estimate their own parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
, and hence benefit from jointly estimating
{\bm{\mu}}
using all
mn
samples.

In the data-abundant regime
, where
n
is large or
\sigma
is high (i.e., the highly heterogeneous case), we show that any policy incurs regret at least
\Omega(dm\sqrt{n})
. This matches the minimax lower bound for the joint regret when each user solves their own linear bandit problem independently using
n
samples, and the regrets are summed across all
m
users. Again, this can be seen from two perspectives. First, it implies that collaboration offers little to no benefit in this regime due to the high heterogeneity. Alternatively, when
n
is large, each user has enough data to accurately estimate their own parameter
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
, making collaboration unnecessary.

The most interesting behavior arises in the intermediate regime
, where both the number of rounds and the heterogeneity level are moderate. We capture this regime using a smooth interpolation parameter
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
, which governs the transition between the two extremes. Our results show that regret interpolates smoothly between the bounds in the data-scarce and data-abundant regimes, depending on
\gamma
. This reveals how the benefit of collaboration varies with both the level of heterogeneity
\sigma
and the number of samples
n
. As
\gamma
increases from
0
to
1/2
, the problem gradually shifts from a collaboration-friendly regime to one where users are better off acting independently.

Information-Theoretic Lower Bound

In this section, we provide details on our minimax lower bound result and a sketch proof highlighting the challenges and novel techniques.

Theorem 4.1

(Minimax lower bound on the Bayesian regret)
.

Consider the unit-ball action set
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2}
. Assume that
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2}
and
\sigma\leq 1
. Recall the minimax regret
\overline{R}_{m,n}
in Definition
3.1
. Then, we have the following results:

•

For
n=o\left(\frac{d}{m\sigma^{2}}\right)
and
mn\geq d
, we have
\overline{R}_{m,n}=\Omega\left(d\sqrt{mn}\right)
;

•

For
n=\Theta\left(\max\left[\frac{d}{m^{2\gamma}\sigma^{2}},d\right]\right)
, we have
\overline{R}_{m,n}=\Omega\left(dm^{1-\gamma}\sqrt{n}\right)
;

•

For
n=\omega\left(\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
, we have
\overline{R}_{m,n}=\Omega\left(dm\sqrt{n}\right)
.

The interpretation of the results on the regret is discussed in Remark
3.2
, and we discuss the assumptions we made in the theorem in Remark
5.2
.

We provide a proof sketch of Theorem
4.1
while keeping the detailed proof in Section
5
. In the data-scarce regime where
n=o(d/m\sigma^{2})
, we will show that the heterogeneity is low such that the regret lower bound can not be far from the lower bound on the regret for the classic stochastic linear bandits with
mn
samples. Thus, we get our result with the classic lower bound
\Omega(d\sqrt{mn})
plus an additional term due to the heterogeneity, where the term is small enough such that the final bound stays in the order of
\Omega(d\sqrt{mn})
.

A technical challenge emerges in the data-rich regime. In the classic stochastic linear bandit problem, the Bayesian regret is defined as
R_{n}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle{\bm{x}}^{*}-{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}\rangle\right]
for any given
{\bm{\theta}}
. A set of “bad”
{\bm{\theta}}
’s is chosen to be the vertices of a
d
-dimensional hypercube. With the probabilistic method, it is shown that any policy must get a large regret on one of the
{\bm{\theta}}
’s being the true parameter, and thus the minimax lower bound is shown to be
\Omega(d\sqrt{n})
for any policy
\pi
(
[
14
]
, Section 24.2). The proof strongly depends on the fact that the chosen
{\bm{\theta}}
has the same absolute value in each coordinate and has a length in the order of
d/\sqrt{n}
.

However, in our heterogeneous setting, the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s are not arbitrary but follow the population distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bm{\mu}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
. Thus, our minimax bound considers the worst-case
{\bm{\mu}}
instead of a worst-case
{\bm{\theta}}
. Especially in the data-rich regime when the heterogeneity is high, the norm of
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s will be larger than the order of
d/\sqrt{n}
since
\sigma=\omega(\sqrt{d/n})
. Additionally, each
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
can be in any direction following the population model. To address the challenges, we develop novel techniques to deal with
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s with large norms and pointing into arbitrary directions.

Due to symmetry, we can focus on analyzing the regret from one user, say, user
m
in (
3
). To address the issue of
{\bm{\theta}}_{m}
having a large norm, we choose the set of “bad”
{\bm{\theta}}_{m}
’s to be the vertices of a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypercube in (
8
) instead, which we denote them as
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\}
in the proof. Although
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
’s has a large norm, we sacrifice one of the
d
coordinates to absorb most part of the norm and carefully construct them such that the distance between them is bad for the learner, meaning that the
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
’s are hard to distinguish so the regret is large, yet separated enough such that an agent cannot get a small regret by simply keep selecting the same action.

Now we are able to construct a set of “bad”
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
’s, we need them to marginally follow the population model as we mentioned. This is achieved by first sampling an arbitrary
{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
as in (
5
) following the population model. Then, we form a set
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\}
around
{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}
as in (
6
) and Figure
2
.
Following the construction, each
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
marginally follows the population model due to symmetry, yet they are artificially coupled so that they form the vertices of a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypercube. Since we consider the unit ball action set, we can rotate
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
to align with the coordinates in (
9
).

Dealing with the large
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
’s and choosing the appropriate set of “bad”
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
’s are the most challenging parts in the proof. We achieve this by carefully choosing both the correct order of
n
and the suitable coefficients to form the suitable angle between the
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
’s in (

{\bm{\theta}}
’s in the regret lower bound for classic stochastic linear bandit problem
[
14
]
(Section 24.2), our proof also provides a way to show the lower bound when given the knowledge that the unknown parameter
{\bm{\theta}}
’s are large. In other words, given the information that the norm of
{\bm{\theta}}
is large does not make the problem easier as larger
{\bm{\theta}}
might seem to be easier to distinguish. In contrast, our construction shows that the order of the regret remains the same.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}_{d}^{2}
be the unit ball action set. We prove the lower bound in the three regimes separately.

5.0.1
Lower bound for large
\sigma

We start from the third regime, the data-rich regime, in Theorem
4.1
. Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle\sigma=\Theta\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n^{1-\alpha}}}\right)

for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
By the definition of the regret, for any given
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{m,n}({\bm{\mu}})=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\langle{\bm{x}}_{i}^{*}-{\bm{x}}_{i,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{i,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;m\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{m,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rangle\right].

(3)

The second equality comes from the fact that
\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}^{2}}\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rangle=\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\rVert_{2}
and the last equality is due to symmetry. Thus, let us focus on the term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{m,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rangle\right].

Despite the expression, note that the actions
\{{\bm{x}}_{m,t}\}_{t=1:n}
still depend on the parameters of all users,
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1:m}
.
Consider the case when

\displaystyle\sigma=c_{1}d^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}

(4)

for some constant
c_{1}>0
and
\alpha\geq 0
. Notice that the results can be easily extended to the case where
c_{1}d^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\leq\sigma\leq c_{1}^{\prime}d^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}
.

Suppose that we have a
d
-dimensional random vector
{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\in\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
. We have the following lemma:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}
be
d
independent random variables that follows the normal distribution

\displaystyle z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\quad\forall i\in[d]

for some
\sigma>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\in\left[\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2},\frac{3d\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]\right)\geq c_{2}

for some constant
c_{2}\in(0,1)
.

The lemma holds since
\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}/\sigma^{2}
follows the chi-square distribution.

Let us consider the case that
{\bm{\mu}}=0
. Since
\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}(\theta^{(0)}_{\ell})^{2}
and
\theta^{(0)}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, by the lemma, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert^{2}\in\left[\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2},\frac{3d\sigma^{2}}{2}\right]\right)\geq c_{2}

with some constant
c_{2}\in(0,1)
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert\in\left[\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}},\sigma\sqrt{\frac{3d}{2}}\,\right]\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert\in\left[\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{2}}{2}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha},\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{6}}{2}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\right]\right)\geq c_{2}.

(5)

We denote the event by
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\left\{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert\in\left[\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{2}}{2}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha},\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{6}}{2}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\right]\right\}

and thus
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq c_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert)
be the sphere centered at
\boldsymbol{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with radius
\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert
in the
d
dimensional space. Let us define a distribution on a set of
d
-dimensional random vectors
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}:{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
where the index set
\mathcal{Z}=\{\pm 1\}^{d-1}
. Given the event
\mathcal{E}
, let us write

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert=c_{3}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}

(6)

where
c_{3}\in\left[\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{2}}{2},\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{6}}{2}\right]
. Let us denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{d-2}_{n}({\bm{\theta}}^{(0)})=\left\{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0})\rVert):\frac{{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}}{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}\rVert\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert}=\cos\eta\right\}

where

\displaystyle\eta=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{c_{4}\sqrt{d(d-1)}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert}\right)=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{c_{4}}{c_{3}}\sqrt{\frac{d-1}{dn^{\alpha}}}\right)\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right),

(7)

\displaystyle c_{4}=\min\left\{\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{2}}{2},\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{6\sqrt{2}}}\right\}\leq c_{3}.

that align with the basis. Due to the symmetry of sampling

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})\quad\forall{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}.

(8)

Now, with some rotation matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we can rotate

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}=W{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}^{({\bm{z}})}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert\cos\eta\quad&\ell=1,\\

and thus

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}}_{1})}-\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}}_{2})}\rVert=2c_{4}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\quad\forall{\bm{z}}_{1},{\bm{z}}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z},\lVert{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}\rVert_{1}=2.

Thus,
\{{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\}
are the vertices of a
(d-1)

Now we want to show a lower bound for the average regret with respect to
\{\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}:{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
. For simplicity, let us denote
\Theta=\{{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}:{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\}
. Suppose that
{\bm{z}}
is uniformly randomly sampled from
\{\pm 1\}^{d-1}
and we set
{\bm{\theta}}_{m}={\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
. Since
{\bm{\theta}}_{m}
and
{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
follow the same distribution, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{m,t},{\bm{\theta}}_{m}\rangle\right]=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert_{2}-\langle{\bm{x}}_{m,t},{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle\bigg|{\bm{z}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{Z}\rvert}\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert-\langle{\bm{x}}_{m,t},{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle\bigg|{\bm{z}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{Z}\rvert}\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert-\langle W^{-1}{\bm{x}}_{m,t},W{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle\bigg|{\bm{z}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{Z}\rvert}\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert-\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{m,t},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle\bigg|{\bm{z}}\right],

(10)

where
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{m,t}
still falls in the unit ball due to the rotation.
For any
t\in[n]
,we have

\displaystyle\;\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert-\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{m,t},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta-\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}\tilde{\theta}^{({\bm{z}})}_{1}\right)+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\frac{\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\tilde{\theta}^{({\bm{z}})}_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta-\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert(\cos\eta)\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cdot\frac{\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cdot\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}\right)z_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta}{2}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos\eta+\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}^{2}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta}{2}-\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2(d-1)}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\left(\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cdot\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}\right)z_{\ell}+\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2(d-1)}-\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta}{2}\left(1-2\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}}{\cos\eta}+\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}^{2}}{\cos^{2}\eta}\right)+\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta}{2}-\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{d-1}-\frac{2\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}z_{\ell}}{\sqrt{d-1}\sin\eta}+\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\eta}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2(d-1)}-\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\cos^{2}\eta}{2}\left(1-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,1}}{\cos\eta}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\;+\left(\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert}{2}-\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert-\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{i,t},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rangle\bigg|{\bm{z}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;c_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}\rVert\sin^{2}\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;c_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{c_{4}^{2}d(d-1)n^{-1+\alpha}}{2\lVert{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\rVert}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;c_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\frac{c_{4}^{2}d(d-1)n^{-1+\alpha}}{2c_{3}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{c_{2}(d-1)}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\frac{\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}}{\sin\eta}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{c_{2}(d-1)}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}\sin^{2}\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{c_{2}(d-1)}{2\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{c_{3}dn^{\alpha}}{d-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\frac{c_{2}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{1}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;c_{5}dn^{-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}{\bm{z}},\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|\mathcal{E}\right]

(11)

where
c_{5}=(\sqrt{2}c_{1}c_{2})/4
and

\displaystyle n^{\prime}=\min\left\{n,\min\left\{\tau:\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}\geq\frac{n\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}\right\}\right\}.

Now, we want to show that give a
({\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime})
pair such that
{\bm{z}}
and
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}
only differ in the
\ell
-th coordinate, the regret

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}},\mathcal{E}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot z^{\prime}_{\ell}\right)^{2}\bigg|{\bm{z}}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\right]

is bounded by some lower bound.
For any
w\in\{1,-1\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\cdot w\right)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\;2\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}+2\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{2n\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}+2\left(\frac{n\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}+1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{4n}{d-1}\cdot\frac{c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}\cdot\frac{d-1}{dn^{\alpha}}+2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{4c_{4}^{2}n^{1-\alpha}}{c_{3}^{2}d}+2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{4(c_{4}^{2}/c_{3}^{2})n^{1-\alpha}+2d}{d}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{6(c_{4}^{2}/c_{3}^{2})n^{1-\alpha}}{d}.

Following the proof in Section 24.2 of
[
14
]
, with Pinsker’s inequality and Exercise 14.4 in
[
14
]
, we have

\displaystyle\;\left\lvert\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\Bigg|{\bm{z}}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\Bigg|{\bm{z}}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\left(\frac{8(c_{4}^{2}/c_{3}^{2})n^{1-\alpha}}{d}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{z}}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\right)},

where
\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{z}}}
is the joint distribution of
\{({\bm{x}}_{i,t},y_{i,t})\}_{i=1:m,t=1:n}
given that
{\bm{\theta}}_{m}={\bm{\theta}}^{({\bm{z}})}
and event
\mathcal{E}
holds.
With Section 24.2 and Exercise 15.7 in
[
14
]
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{z}}}\|\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\right)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left\langle\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{m,t},\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}})}-\tilde{{\bm{\theta}}}^{({\bm{z}}^{\prime})}\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(x_{m,t,\ell}\cdot\left(\theta^{({\bm{z}})}_{\ell}-\theta^{({\bm{z}}^{\prime})}_{\ell}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\left(2c_{4}\sqrt{d}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}x_{m,t,\ell}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\left(2c_{4}\sqrt{d}n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{n\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}+1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;2c_{4}^{2}dn^{-1+\alpha}\left(\frac{n}{d-1}\frac{c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}\frac{d-1}{dn^{\alpha}}+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{2c_{4}^{4}}{c_{3}^{2}}+2c_{4}^{2}dn^{-1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{2c_{4}^{4}}{c_{3}^{2}}+\frac{2c_{4}^{4}}{c_{3}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{4c_{4}^{4}}{c_{3}^{2}}.

With the bound on the KL-divergence term, we have

\displaystyle\;\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\left(\frac{6(c_{4}^{2}/c_{3}^{2})n^{1-\alpha}}{d}\right)\sqrt{\frac{4c_{4}^{4}}{c_{3}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{12(c_{4}^{4}/c_{3}^{3})n^{1-\alpha}}{d}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]\geq

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{12(c_{4}^{4}/c_{3}^{3})n^{1-\alpha}}{d}.

Adding an extra term on the both side gives us

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}+\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}-\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\sin\eta}{\sqrt{d-1}}+\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{12n^{1-\alpha}}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;2\sum_{t=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}+2\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t-1}^{n^{\prime}}\tilde{x}_{m,t,\ell}\right]-\frac{12n^{1-\alpha}}{d}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\frac{2n\sin^{2}\eta}{d-1}-\frac{12n^{1-\alpha}}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{2n}{d-1}\frac{c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}^{2}}\frac{d-1}{dn^{\alpha}}-\frac{12n^{1-\alpha}}{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{2c_{4}^{2}n^{1-\alpha}}{c_{3}^{2}d}\left(1-\frac{6c_{4}^{2}}{c_{3}}\right).

Since we have

Theorem 7.1
.

Consider any finite action set
\mathcal{A}
with
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert=k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and assume that
\lVert{\bm{\mu}}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
,
\sigma\leq 1
, and
n\geq\min\{k,d^{2}\}
. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, by running Algorithm
1
, we have the following results:

•

When
n=o\left(d/m\sigma^{2}\right)
, we have

.

•

When

with
\gamma\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]

\hat{R}_{m,n}({\bm{\mu}})=\tilde{O}\left(m^{1-\gamma}\sqrt{dn}+md^{2}\right)
.

•

When
n=\omega\left(d/\sigma^{2}\right)
, we have
\hat{R}_{m,n}({\bm{\mu}})=\tilde{O}\left(m\sqrt{dn}+md^{2}\right)
.

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

7.2
Proof of Theorem
7.1

For notational convenience, denote by
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{1,\ell}=\dots=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{1,\ell}=\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}
during the collaborative learning stage, i.e., when
h_{\sigma,\delta}\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\ell}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Following Algorithm
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N},\;\forall i\in[m],\;\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}:\lvert\langle\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i,\ell}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle\rvert\leq\varepsilon_{\ell}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

Suppose that the algorithm has
L_{c}\in\mathbb{N}
collaboration phases. That is,

\displaystyle L_{c}=\max\left\{0,\left\lfloor\log_{2}\frac{1}{2h_{\sigma,\delta}}\right\rfloor\right\}.

In the collaboration phases, for any
\ell\in[L_{c}]
and action
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A_{\ell}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle=\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle.

(18)

Let
\mathbf{X}_{i}
be a
\bar{n}_{i,\ell}
by
d
matrix such that each row of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
is the actions chosen by user
i
at phase
\ell
, and
{\bm{y}}_{i}
is the vector with corresponding rewards. For simplicity, we omit the index
\ell
in the notation. For the first term in (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\;\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{y}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}({\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{u}}_{i})+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\right)-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle

(19)

\displaystyle\;+\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle

(20)

For the first term in (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{\mu}},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}{\bm{x}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle

and thus

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle=0.

For the second term in (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}{\bm{x}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle.

Since each

, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{m}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}\right),

(21)

and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle\geq s\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{ms^{2}}{2{\bm{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}}\right\}

(22)

for any
s\geq 0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\ell\in[L_{c}],\;\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle\right|\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}\left(\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}\right)\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{4}

(23)

for any
\delta>0
. For the third term in (
20
), since
\mathbf{X}_{i}
and
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\right).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle\right|\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{m^{2}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}}^{2}\log\frac{2k\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2\ell(\ell+1)}

for any
\delta>0
.
From the algorithm, we choose the actions
\mathbf{X}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\;\frac{2}{m^{2}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}}^{2}\log\frac{2k\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{2}{m^{2}}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left\lceil\frac{8g(\pi_{\ell})}{m\varepsilon_{\ell}^{2}}\log\frac{2k\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}\right\rceil\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}}\pi_{\ell}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}){\bm{x}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\right)^{\dagger}{\bm{x}}\log\frac{2k\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{\varepsilon_{\ell}^{2}}{4}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\forall\ell\in[L_{c}]:\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\right\rangle\right|\geq\frac{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\ell}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2\ell(\ell+1)}.

(24)

For the second term in (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}).

Thus, for any
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\langle{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle\geq s\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{s^{2}}{2{\bm{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}{\bm{x}}}\right\}.

(25)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\ell\in[L_{c}],\;\exists i\in[m],\;\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:\left|\langle{\bm{\mu}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle\right|\geq\sqrt{2\left(\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}\right)\log\frac{4km}{\delta}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{4}

(26)

for any
\delta>0
. In the collaborations, we know that

\displaystyle h_{\sigma,\delta}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}\left(\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}\right)\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}+\sqrt{2\left(\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}\right)\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}\leq\frac{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\ell}}{2}.

Plug (
23
), (
24
) and (
26
) back to (
20

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\ell\in[L_{c}],\;\exists i\in[m],\;\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:\langle\hat{{\bm{\mu}}}_{\ell}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle\geq{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\ell}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{L_{c}+1}\right)\delta.

Now, for the local training phases, we know that for any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i,\ell}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}\right)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[m],\;\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}:\left|\langle\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{i,\ell}-{\bm{\theta}}_{i},{\bm{x}}\rangle\right|\geq\sqrt{2\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}}^{2}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2\ell(\ell+1)}

for any
\delta>0
. From the algorithm, we choose
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s such that

\displaystyle\;2\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{\dagger}}^{2}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;2{\bm{x}}^{\top}\left(\left\lceil\frac{2g(\pi_{i,\ell})}{\varepsilon_{\ell}^{2}}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}\right\rceil\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}}\pi_{\ell}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}){\bm{x}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}{\bm{x}}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\;{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\ell}^{2}.

.

\ell_{i,{\bm{x}}}=\min\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:4\varepsilon_{\ell}<\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}\}

Proof of Lemma

The proof is straightforward from Algorithm

.

7.2
,
7.3
, and

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}\leq\eta}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,{\bm{x}}}+\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,{\bm{x}}}\right)

For any

\displaystyle\;mn\eta+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,\ell}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}\left(2\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{x}}\rangle+2\sqrt{2\left(\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert{\bm{x}}\rVert_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}\right)\log\frac{4km}{\delta}}\,\right)\left\lceil\frac{8\pi_{\ell}({\bm{x}})g(\pi_{\ell})}{m(1/2)^{2}}\log\frac{2k\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}\sum_{\ell=L^{\prime}_{c}+1}^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,\ell}({\bm{x}})\leq

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=L^{\prime}_{c}+1}^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil}\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}2\varepsilon_{\ell}\left\lceil\frac{2\pi_{i,\ell}({\bm{x}})g_{i}(\pi_{i,\ell})}{\varepsilon_{\ell}^{2}}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;m\sum_{\ell=L^{\prime}_{c}+1}^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil}2\cdot 2^{-\ell}\left(\frac{d(d+1)}{2}+\frac{2d}{2^{-2\ell}}\log\frac{2km\ell(\ell+1)}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;md(d+1)2^{-L^{\prime}_{c}-1}+\left(2^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil+1}-2^{L^{\prime}_{c}+1}\right)4dm\log\frac{4km\left(L^{\prime}_{c}\right)^{2}}{\delta}.

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{m,n}({\bm{\mu}})\leq

\displaystyle\;+\left(2^{\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\eta}\right\rceil+1}-2^{L^{\prime}_{c}+1}\right)\left(4dm\log\frac{4km\left(L^{\prime}_{c}\right)^{2}}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;r_{1}+mn\eta+\frac{1}{2}md(d+1)+\min\left\{\frac{1}{h_{\sigma,\delta}},\frac{16}{\eta}\right\}\left(16d\log\frac{4k\left(\log_{2}\frac{8}{\eta}\right)^{2}}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\left(\frac{16}{\eta}-\min\left\{\frac{1}{h_{\sigma,\delta}},\frac{16}{\eta}\right\}\right)\left(4dm\log\frac{4km\left(\log_{2}\frac{8}{\eta}\right)^{2}}{\delta}\right)

for any
\eta>0
.

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{{\bm{x}}:\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}>\eta}\Delta_{i,{\bm{x}}}n_{i,1}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\leq

7.4
The additional term in the upper bound

The extra
md^{2}
term in the regret upper bound comes from (
15
) in our collaborative phased elimination algorithm.
As we will see in the details of Algorithm
1
in Section
6
, in each phase of the collaborative learning stage, we first decide how many times each action
{\bm{x}}
will be selected to reach the target error in the phase. Let us denote it by
n_{\ell}({\bm{x}})
for now. Then, we split the workload so that each user is responsible to select each action
{\bm{x}}
for
n_{i,\ell}({\bm{x}})=\left\lceil n_{\ell}({\bm{x}})/m\right\rceil
times. Since the ceiling function can increase the value by almost 1 for each user and action in the worst-case, and the number of users is
m
and the number of the actions to be pulled (the cardinality of support of the design) can be upper bounded by
d(d+1)/2
(
[
14
]
, Theorem 21.1), we will have at most extra
md^{2}
pulls in each phase. By the way we choose the exponentially decreasing target error, this leads to an
md^{2}
term in the regret. A more efficient way to split the workload might be first calculating the total rounds needed for all action combined,
n_{\ell}=\sum_{{\bm{x}}}n_{\ell}({\bm{x}})
, then split it among the users. In this case, the users might be selecting different actions in the phase, and our proof need to be modified since we assume that each user selects the same actions during the collaborative learning stage. After the change, we will have an extra
(m+d^{2})
pull instead. The
m
term remains due to the fact that our problem formulation is synchronized. For instance, if the number of users
m
is much larger than the workload
n_{\ell}
in some phase
\ell
, our current formulation does not allow users to remain idle while a each of the
n_{\ell}
selected user perform a pull. And thus since all users are forced to select some action in the phase, there are at least
m
pulls instead of
n_{\ell}
pulls in each stage. This cause the
m
term in the regret. To resolve this, we have to remove the synchronized assumption on the formulation. For example, if the users are sequentially selecting actions, there can be only a part of the users that have to select actions in every phase. This prevent from the extra
m
term in the regret. However, the regret analysis will be different from our current one.

7.5
Sub-Gaussian population distributions

Our results on the regret analysis for CP-PE can be translated to the case where
({\bm{\theta}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}})
’s are sub-Gaussian random vectors. This is because sub-Gaussian random variables have similar concentration results to Gaussian random variables. Let us formally define the term first:

Definition 7.1

(Sub-Gaussian random variables)
.

A random variable
\boldsymbol{X}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\boldsymbol{X}}\right]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}}

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

With the definition of a sub-Gaussian random variable, we can extend the idea to a random vector.

Definition 7.2

(Sub-Gaussian random vectors)
.

A random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
in the
d
-dimension space is said to be sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
if it is centered and for any
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lVert{\bm{u}}\rVert_{2}=1
, the real random variable
{\bm{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
. We write
\boldsymbol{X}\sim\mathrm{subG}(\sigma^{2})
.

Let
{\bm{u}}_{i}={\bm{\theta}}_{i}-{\bm{\mu}}
follow a sub-Gaussian distribution with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
for each
i\in[m]
. Then, for any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}^{2}
,
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}
is
\mathrm{subG}(\sigma^{2})
and thus

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}\right\rangle\sim\mathrm{subG}(\sigma^{2}/m).

(32)

From the concentration property of sub-Gaussian random variables (
[
21
]
, Chapter 2.1), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\langle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{i}\right\rangle\geq s\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{ms^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(33)

Recall that in Section
7.2
, the regret analysis of CP-PE, the Gaussianity of
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
is only used for the additivity of Gaussianity and its concentration properties. Since similar properties are also established for sub-Gaussian random variables in (
33
), we can simply replace (
21
) with sub-Gaussian
{\bm{u}}_{i}
’s, the replace (
22
) and (
25
) with the concentration inequality of sub-Gaussian random variables. The rest of the proof is essentially the same. Additionally, CP-PE is not using the fact that
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
follows a Gaussian distribution. Thus, our algorithm and the regret analysis also work for any sub-Gaussian population distribution.

Experiments

In our experiments, we compare CP-PE with Federated Phased Elimination (Fed-PE, all agents perform phased elimination as if they have the same unknown parameter) and Individual Phased Elimination (Ind-PE, each agent performs phased elimination on it own and evaluate the joint regret). We pick the population parameter
\sigma
such that the heterogeneity falls in the second regime, and CP-PE usually performs one to two phases in the collaborative stage depending on the instance of
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1:m}
in each run.

We compare CP-PE, Fed-PE, and Ind-PE on synthetic datasets. In the first experiment, we set the number of users

The head start of Fed-PE is due to the benefit of collaboration, which significantly increases the effective sample size and eliminates the bad actions rapidly. However, without personalization, agents are forced to find a global best action that can be distinct from each agent’s individual best action, and thus a linear regret is inevitable. On the other hand, CP-PE benefits from the collaborative learning stage and switch to the personalized learning stage before each agent’s best action is eliminated. The transition happens in a few of the early rounds since the first few phases in phased elimination are composed of much fewer pulls of actions compared to the later phases, and the pulls are even equally divided among each user in the collaborative learning stage. By monitoring each run, we can see that agents under CP-PE rapidly shrink the action set in the collaborative learning stage and start with a much smaller action set when starting the personalized learning stage. Thus, CP-PE obtains a better regret than Ind-PE.

9
Conclusion

We provide the first complete characterization of the benefit of collaboration under our hierarchical/empirical Bayesian framework for heterogeneous linear bandits. Overcoming technical challenges, we establish a minimax lower bound and propose CP-PE, an algorithm that achieves minimax-optimal regret up to polylogarithmic factors. Despite this complete characterization, several important directions remain open for future work. First, one can consider alternative heterogeneity models beyond the Gaussian/sub-Gaussian population studied in this work. Second, our proposed algorithm CP-PE depends on the known level of heterogeneity.
How agents can collaborate without this knowledge, and whether there is a fundamental gap in regret from the known heterogeneity case, remains an open and important question of ongoing investigations.
