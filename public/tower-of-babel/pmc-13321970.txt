Deep learning for automated diagnosis and differentiation of otitis media on temporal bone CT
Authors: Yong Tang, Weixuan Fan, Zhitao Cheng, Yanlin Leng, Yuhang Liu, Tingfang Wu, Song Su, Jing Fei, Leiji Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe accurate diagnosis of otitis media (OM) has long been a challenge for clinicians (especially for less experienced clinicians) due to the variety of types and the complex anatomical structures of the middle ear. Although deep learning (DL) based on different examination methods (mostly otoscopy) has been applied to the diagnosis of single species OM in previous studies, DL using temporal bone computed tomography (TBCT) images to diagnose OM and simultaneously differentiate between chronic otitis media (COM) and otitis media with effusion (OME) has not been investigated in depth. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a DL framework for the automated diagnosis of OM and identifying OME and COM with or without cholesteatoma using TBCT images.MethodsOur team created a unique large dataset of 2011 TBCT images from 1200 patients who were diagnosed with OM, which was determined the regions of interest (ROI) for middle ear (ME) by experienced experts. Then, a DL model was trained to detect the MEs in TBCT images and determine the OM status with this dataset of pre-processed images. Five-fold cross-validation was utilized for training and selecting the models. Finally, we evaluated the model using 406 images and verified the effectiveness of model-assisted diagnosis for different levels of clinicians in a comparative study.ResultsIn the detection of the ME, the DL model achieved a detection ratio of 98.53%. The model showed satisfying performance in the classification of normal middle ear (NME), OME, and COM with an accuracy of 0.9238. With the assistance of the DL, the diagnostic accuracies were significantly improved from 81.53% to 93.60% (junior clinician) and from 87.93% to 95.57% (senior clinician), respectively.ConclusionsThe findings suggested that the DL model could accurately identify MEs in TBCT images and classify NME, OME, and COM with satisfying accuracy. DL could also effectively assist clinicians in TBCT interpretation for OM diagnosis.Keywords: Middle ear, Otitis media with effusion, Chronic otitis media, Temporal bone computed tomography, Deep learning

Background

Otitis media (OM) refers to a group of inflammatory diseases in middle ears (ME), including acute otitis media (AOM), chronic otitis media (COM), and otitis media with effusion (OME) [1]. As one of the most common global health problems, OM affects up to 0.7 billion individuals worldwide [2]. The OM complications are the primary causes of preventable hearing loss, especially in developing countries [2–5]. This highlights the importance of accurate early diagnosis and timely intervention to prevent OM progression, especially for COM and OME.

Currently, temporal bone computed tomography (TBCT) is the first choice for the diagnosis of OM [6]. However, its sensitivity and accuracy in the subjective human-based diagnosis of OM is suboptimal so far. In a previous comparison of the preoperative TBCT assessment based on the intra-operative findings in patients with COM, the sensitivities were far from sufficient for several ME structures [7]. The reported sensitivities of the TBCT for the detection of attic (48.88%), malleus–incus complex (62.8%), and oval window (62.8%) did not meet the clinical needs. These limitations might lead to the underestimation of the actual lesion and misdiagnosis of OM. Therefore, enhancing the accuracy of TBCT readings is crucial for the diagnosis of OM, refining surgical plans, and improving patient outcomes.

Recent groundbreaking advances in deep learning (DL) have attracted significant enthusiasm in applications of various domains [8]. Technically, DL models are novel artificial intelligence (AI) models based on multilayer neural networks and convolutional structures [9]. Through a series of connected layers, trained DL models can extract abstract representations of the inputs for tasks like image processing [10]. Particularly, DL has demonstrated encouraging performance in medical image analysis on par with human experts, like segmentation, classification, registration, and prediction [11–14]. DL has also been successfully applied to medical images, including CT, MRI, X-ray, endoscopy, and ultrasound [15, 16]. In the field of otorhinolaryngology, DL facilitates the automated diagnosis of various types of ear diseases based on medical images. However, existing studies have primarily focused on otoendoscopic images, while the application of DL for diagnosing OM using TBCT remains insufficiently explored [17–20].

To the best of our knowledge, only a handful studies have applied AI to diagnose OM in the analysis of TBCT images [20–23]. 1However, these four studies focused on cases of COM or OME independently. Indeed, TBCT has an important role in the preoperative diagnosis of COM and OME [6, 24]. To date, there are no reports of the application of automated DL frameworks to identify ME regions and NME, COM, and OME diagnosis. To fulfill the gap, we enrolled NME, COM, and OME cases in this study. Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop and evaluate the DL framework in the automated diagnosis of OM in TBCT images, including the ME region detection and the classification of NME, COM, and OME.

Methods

Data collection

In this retrospective study, we collected TBCT images of patients treated in the Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University. Figure 1 illustrated the flowchart of this study. A total of 1200 patients who underwent TBCT scan examinations in the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University between September 2018 and June 2025 were included. Those patients were excluded if (1) clinical information was incomplete; (2) underwent ME surgeries before TBCT examinations. Each collected ME was treated as an independent case. As a result, we obtained 2011 images from 1200 patients with 2019 MEs. Among these MEs, 998 MEs were diagnosed as OME through surgical findings and clinical features, 619 MEs were diagnosed as COM through surgical findings, pathological-histological examination, and clinical features, and the other 402 MEs were diagnosed as normal middle ear (NME) in TBCT scans. All data were anonymized, and ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (No.KY2022226). Informed consent from patients was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study.

Flowchart of this study

CT imaging

All patients underwent TBCT scanning examination using a 40-channel multidetector CT scanner (Shanghai United Imaging Healthcare Co., Ltd., Shanghai, P.R. China). The scanning range extended from the lower margin of the external auditory meatus to the top margin of the petrous bone. The scan parameters varied among patients with a tube current of 111–497 mA, slice thicknesses of 0.6–3 mm, and a tube voltage of 120 or 140 kV. To ensure a diverse dataset for subsequent DL development, we varied tube current, slice thickness, and tube voltage. All images were downloaded from the Network Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University and saved as JPEG files in 512 × 512 pixels.

Image annotation

The TBCT images of MEs were manually annotated using the Labelme (v5.0.1) software by two experienced otolaryngologists who had access to the clinical diagnosis records. Initially, in all images, the two otolaryngologists discussed and reached a consensus on the locations of ME regions based on the necessary surgical field and anatomical structures, including the ossicular chain, mastoid process, tympanic chamber, and sinus tympani. Subsequently, the region of interest (ROI) for each ME was manually delineated and all visible ROIs for left and right MEs in each TBCT image were identified and assigned annotations as NME, OME, or COM. The status of a given ME was determined from the clinical diagnosis records by the otolaryngologists and experienced radiologists. The NME cases were determined by reviewing TBCT scan results. The OM status evaluations were divided into two groups, the operated group and unoperated group. For operated ears, intra-operative findings and histopathological examinations were utilized to determine the diagnosis. For unoperated ears, the OM status was evaluated based on a combination of medical history, clinical features, ear exams, audiograms, and preoperative images. For convenience, the ROIs were delineated using bounding boxes in the TBCT images. Representative samples of NME, OME, and COM cases with their ROIs are presented in Fig. 2. The annotations were subsequently extracted to serve as ground truths for training and evaluating the DL algorithm.

Samples of (a) NME cases, (b) OME cases, and (c) COM cases with annotated MEs in TBCT images. NME, normal middle ear; OME, otitis media of effusion; COM, chronic otitis media

Deep learning model

In preparing datasets for training and testing, we divided the TBCT images based on patients to ensure no images from the same patient appeared in both the training validation set and testing set. As a result, we obtained one training-validation set (75%, patient = 890, n = 1612) and one independent testing set (25%, patient = 310, n = 407), as shown in Fig. 3. The testing set was balanced by including the same number of three type of ME regions. In training, we utilized the five-fold cross-validation approach, namely, the training-validation set was further divided into five subsets, and the model was trained iteratively in four subsets and validated in the rest one. The best model parameters were selected according to the averaged performance in the cross-validation and finally evaluated in the testing set.

The details of dataset preparation and dataset splitting, including the numbers of each class patients, images, and middle ears

The DL framework was mainly based on the architecture of the ‘You only look once’ (YOLO) model, specifically YOLOv8n. The YOLO model was utilized to detect the ROIs of MEs in the TBCT images. YOLO is a popular object detection DL model with state-of-the-art performances and real-time processing capability [25]. In YOLO, pixels are assigned to bounding boxes and the class probabilities. In this way, we could obtain candidate boxes for target objects. The ROIs thus could be detected in images as bounding boxes. Based on the results, YOLO will conduct a three-category classification of NME, OME, and COM. We exhibited the detailed structures of YOLO in Fig. 4, which illustrated the schematic for the architecture of YOLO for detections and classification of MEs in TBCT images. The YOLO was first trained independently using the training-validation data. Later, the best performing model weights of YOLO were selected and finally evaluated in the testing set.

The main structure of YOLOv8n

The DL framework was developed in Python programming language (3.11.5) using publicly available libraries, including NumPy (1.24.3) for data handling, PyTorch (2.1.2) for deep neural network implementations, and matplotlib (3.7.2) for visualizations. The experiment was performed in a DL server equipped with one NVIDIA RTX3070 laptop graphic processing unit (8GB memory), one 11th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-11900 H @ 2.50 GHz, and 32 GB main memory. The learning rate is 0.0005, the batch size is 16, and the epoch number of each fold is 50. The trained DL framework could run efficiently with a conventional desktop computer.

Deep learning-assisted diagnosis

To further evaluate the value of the DL framework in assisting clinicians during the diagnosis of OM, we conducted comparison experiments. We invited one senior clinician with over 20 years of experience and one junior clinician with five years of experience to participate. From the testing set, one image per patient was randomly selected. In total, 406 TBCT images were chosen for the two clinicians to judge and record ME status independently. In the first experiment, two clinicians read the raw TBCT images without any annotations and determined ME statuses solely based on their judgments. In the second experiment, TBCT images were provided to the clinicians with annotations predicted by the DL framework for reference. With the assistance of DL, two clinicians judged and recorded the ME status again. After the two experiments, the ground truth of the MEs was used to calculate the accuracies of senior and junior clinicians’ readings with and without the DL references.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the performance of the detection of the MEs in TBCT images, we calculated the values of ROI@0.5 and mean average precision (mAP)@0.5:0.95. The mAP values were obtained over different interval of union (IOU) thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 with a step of 0.05. To determine the accuracy of the three classifications of NME, COM, and OME. We adopted the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), accuracy (ACC), F1-score, specificity, and sensitivity. The results were also visualized as the confusion matrices and the diagrams of receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC).

Performance of diagnosis using deep learning

In our DL framework, the YOLO model was trained in the training-validation set and evaluated in the independent testing set. The examples of detecting MEs were illustrated in Fig. 5. We saw that YOLO could accurately locate the MEs in TBCT images. The mAP@0.5 was 0.9136 and the mAP@0.5:0.95 was 0.7286, respectively, indicating excellent accuracies in identifying MEs in TBCT images. Meanwhile, YOLO outputs the classifications of NME, OME, and COM. In summary, the classification of the YOLOv8n model achieved promising performance with a detection ratio of 98.53% (1.47% MEs were not detected). Among the MEs that were detected, our model achieved an AUC of 0.9951 (95% CI, 0.9903–0.9982), an ACC of 0.9238, an F1-score of 0.9392, a sensitivity of 0.9395, and a specificity of 0.9700 in the test set of classification task. In Fig. 6, we visualized the results as a confusion matrix and plotted the diagram of ROC for the result of the best model on the test dataset. The results showed that the DL framework could accurately diagnose the ME status as NME, COM, or OME in TBCT images.

Performance of AI model assisted diagnosis

In the comparative study, A total of 406 images were shown to a junior and a senior clinician. The results of both clinicians were compared, with and without the assistance of the results predicted by the DL model. The correct and incorrect ratio of judgments made by junior and senior clinicians before and after AI assistance were shown in Fig. 7. Without the assistance of AI, 331(81.53%) images were correctly diagnosed by the junior clinician while 75(18.47%) images were incorrectly diagnosed, and the senior clinician correctly diagnosed 357(87.93%) images while 49(12.07%) were incorrectly diagnosed. With the assistance of AI, 380(93.60%) images were correctly diagnosed by the junior clinician while only 26(6.40%) were incorrectly diagnosed, and the senior clinician correctly diagnosed 388(95.57%) images while 18(4.43%) images were incorrectly diagnosed. The diagnostic accuracy of the junior clinician was significantly improved by 12.07% with the aid of AI. The senior clinician’s accuracy was also improved by 7.64% with the assistance of AI. The results demonstrated that both junior and senior clinicians could benefit from the assistance of AI with significantly improved diagnostic accuracies.

Discussion

To achieve an automated diagnosis of OM by the DL method, we developed and validated a DL framework that could accurately detect MEs in TBCT images and determine the OM status. The model showed satisfying performance in detecting Mes with a detection ratio of 98.53% and classifying OM status with an accuracy of 0.9238. Furthermore, we performed comparison experiments to further evaluate the performance of the DL framework. Incorporation of AI significantly improved the diagnostic accuracy of the junior clinician from 81.53% to 93.60%, while that of the senior clinician increased from 87.93% to 95.57%. The results indicated that the DL framework enhanced the diagnostic accuracy of both clinicians. Based on these results, the DL application in OM diagnosis showed promising results.

In the field of otorhinolaryngology, the OM contributes to major medical visits, antibiotic prescriptions, and mortality, with more than 21,000 people dying due to the complications each year, leading to a hearing impairment of over two million (disability-adjusted life-years) each year [2]. According to the effusion in the ME, clinical symptoms, tympanic membrane appearance, and perforation, OM can be divided into three types, including acute otitis media (AOM), otitis media with effusion (OME), and chronic otitis media (COM) [1]. AOM is typically a self-limiting infection that does not require TBCT scans or surgical intervention. In addition, the cholesteatoma has been considered as an aggressive subtype of COM in recent studies due to its inflammatory properties [26, 27]. Therefore, we focused our study on cases of NME, OME, and COM with or without cholesteatoma.

In particular, COM (characterized by purulent fluid buildup and discharge) and OME (characterized by the presence of a middle ear effusion in the absence of infection) represent two common, yet distinct types of OM. While both are classified as OM, it is important to note that the treatment options for these conditions differ significantly. OME, for instance, can be treated with less invasive methods, such as watchful waiting, medical therapy and grommet insertion, which can yield favourable outcomes. In contrast, COM requires more complex surgical interventions, such as mastoidectomy, which involves the removal of part of the temporal bone. Therefore, differential diagnosis between COM and OME is crucial for effective disease management and surgical planning.

With the superiority of displaying osseous erosion, TBCT remains the golden standard of evaluating disease progression and potential complications of OM [28, 29]. However, limitations in disease detection still exist because the fluid in the MEs of COM and OME is similar in density on TBCT images and anatomical structures of MEs are complex and tiny [30]. Blurs may occur due to limitations associated with TBCT scans resolutions [31]. Therefore, missed diagnoses may occur, especially for less experienced clinicians [7]. Overcoming these limitations in clinical diagnosis remains a significant challenge. In recent years, with the rapid advancement of deep learning (DL), AI has demonstrated substantial potential in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, addressing the increasing demands of modern healthcare. Therefore, in this study, we proposed and developed a DL model to automatically detect the MEs and diagnose OM in TBCT images. We also carried out comparison experiments to evaluate its assistive role in the diagnostic process. The DL model could be further extended to future AI-based classification of OM subtypes, which facilitate conservative management or surgical planning for clinicians.

Previous studies on the use of AI in OM identification primarily focused on traditional otoscopic images [18, 26]. A recent study investigated AI application in smartphone-based otoscopic images, and reported promising results [18]. However, traditional otoscopic images are potentially limited by a narrow field of view and insufficient diagnostic information. Compared with otoscopy-based studies, those investigating TBCT of OM, which offer greater accessibility, richer amount of anatomical information and adequate sensitivity in revealing pathological changes, are relatively fewer. In this study, we performed a systematic search of literature database and identified only four studies concerning the use of AI in TBCT images for the diagnosis of OM [20, 23]. Among them, one study utilized DL to extract ROIs to diagnose COM [20] and another study proposed a hybrid model to determine whether COM was linked to cholesteatoma [22]. Neural network models were investigated in differentiating OME and primary ciliary dyskinesia-related OM [21]. 3D convolutional neural network-based model was used in evaluating COM and enhance interpretability through heatmaps [23]. However, these studies only explored the imaging characteristics of COM or OME alone, and enrolled relatively few cases.

Compared to the above works, our study is the first to propose a DL framework to identify the MEs and diagnose OM diseases, including both OME and COM in TBCT images. In addition, we significantly enlarged data volume with 2011 TBCT images from 1200 patients, which would significantly improve the performance of the DL framework with reliable accuracy and reliability. Compared to the moderate dataset in previous studies, the larger dataset in this study also improved the confidence of our results. Furthermore, we did not limit the window width, window level, or thickness of TBCT images. Images with different parameters could ensure stability and generalization of research results. The results of our study prove the value of AI in diagnosing OM, which could support rapid and accurate clinical decision-making, thus reducing the rate of early misdiagnosis and avoiding severe progression.

This study also has several limitations worth further effort. First of all, this study is a single-center retrospective study with inherent risk of institutional bias. Even though, the ROI was manually annotated by two otolaryngologists at different professional levels, we intentionally did not limit the window width, window level, or slice thickness of CT images. The training set included images with different scanning parameters, which to some extent enhanced the model’s robustness to variations in technical parameters. Nevertheless, multi-center validation remains a necessary next step. In the future, we plan to conduct prospective multi-center studies at two to three hospitals of varying levels to comprehensively assess the model’s generalizability in diverse clinical settings. Secondly, as a first-stage proof-of-concept, the current dataset only includes axial-plane CT images, excluding coronal planes. Although, our model has demonstrated good performance on axial images, the most routinely acquired and widely available imaging plane in routine clinical practice. Nevertheless, the coronal plane remains essential for providing intuitive visualization of key surgical structures such as the facial nerve canal. To construct a more generalizable model for junior clinicians with entry-level interpretation experience and even non-medical backgrounds as a “second reader,” we plan to systematically collect multi-planar CT data in the next-stage research. We will also, explore multi-planar fusion deep learning models (such as 3D Convolutional Neural Network or attention mechanisms), and specifically focus on evaluating their incremental value in intraoperative navigation and surgical planning. Finally, due to its inflammatory properties, the cholesteatoma has been regarded as an aggressive subtype of COM in recent studies. Given the similar surgical management of cholesteatoma and COM, this study treated COM with and without cholesteatoma as a unified entity. Nevertheless, cholesteatoma can be classified into different subtypes based on the anatomical sites involved and the extent of invasion. Preoperative assessment of these subtypes can significantly influence surgical decision-making, including the choice of approach, the extent of resection, and technical considerations, thereby enabling individualized surgical strategies and precision medicine. As a next step, we plan to conduct fine-grained recognition research of cholesteatoma subtypes by learning imaging features characteristic of different subtypes (such as patterns of destruction and bone resorption).

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically trained, validated, and tested a DL framework for the fully automated detection of MEs and classification of NME, COM, and OME cases in TBCT images. The DL framework achieved promising accuracies in ME detection and ME status classification. This study also demonstrated that DL could assist clinicians in TBCT interpretation and improve the diagnostic accuracy of OM. The results indicate an attractive potential for DL application in diagnosing OM and other ear diseases.
