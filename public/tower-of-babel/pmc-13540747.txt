A narrative review of qualitative studies exploring the normalization of gambling for youth and younger adults
Authors: Monique Murray, Hannah Pitt, Simone McCarthy, Grace Arnot, Bram Constandt, Steffi De Jans, Samantha Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGambling is a highly visible and socially embedded activity among young people, with marketing, social practices, and digital platforms shaping perceptions and behaviours. This article presents a narrative review of qualitative studies (2015–25) exploring how gambling is normalized among youth and younger adults (up to 35 years), and how they perceive and respond to these processes. Four themes were constructed from n = 22 articles using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis. First, gambling was socially constructed as a normative activity through early family exposure and peer group practices, where participation could both encourage and discourage engagement. Second, exposure to gambling advertising, particularly through sport, was a pervasive driver of normalization, framing gambling as entertaining, glamorous, and overwhelmingly positive. Third, digital access via smartphones, apps, and online platforms further embedded gambling into daily life, enabling engagement in previously restricted spaces and facilitating peer-to-peer participation. Finally, youth and younger adults articulated strategies to counter the normalization of gambling, emphasizing structural and regulatory approaches, including stricter controls on advertising, counter-marketing campaigns, education, and youth participation in policy development. Policy action is needed to tackle the influence of a range of determinants of gambling normalization, with a particular focus on how commercial drivers and political responses impact youth and younger adults in specific sociocultural settings and contexts.

Introduction

The normalization of contemporary forms of gambling

Technology has enabled the gambling industry to rapidly transform its products and marketing practices, contributing to unprecedented health and social harms across the globe (Glozah et al. 2023, Thomas et al. 2023a, García-Pérez et al. 2024). New gambling environments have revolutionized gambling from a largely land-based activity, to one that is instantaneously available through a range of online digital platforms (Kairouz et al. 2025). These platforms enable gambling companies to transcend geographical boundaries with innovative products and promotions, embedding gambling within valued social institutions, such as sport (McGee and Bunn 2024). Much like the tobacco (Watts et al. 2022), alcohol (Miller et al. 2021), and ultra-processed food industries (Moodie et al. 2021), the gambling industry employs a range of sophisticated tactics (sometimes described as the ‘corporate playbook’) to promote the consumption of its products, maximize profit, and prevent regulatory reform at the expense of public health (Lacy-Nichols et al. 2022, McCarthy et al. 2024, Thomas et al. 2024). Researchers argue that these strategies seek to create environments and infrastructure that foster consumption by integrating gambling into everyday social practices and routines (Thomas et al. 2022).

Normalization theory describes the processes through which behaviours become routine, accepted, and embedded in everyday life (Parker et al. 2002). Thomas et al. (2018) developed on this original work to define the normalization of gambling as:

The interplay of socio-cultural, environmental, commercial, and political processes which influence how different gambling activities and products are made available and accessible, encourage recent and regular use, and become an accepted part of everyday life for individuals, their families, and communities. (p. 6)

Gambling is embedded into cultural traditions, rituals, and routines in everyday lives through a range of social, commercial, and political processes (Egerer and Marionneau 2019, Thomas et al. 2026). For example, studies have demonstrated that gambling is perceived as a socially and culturally accommodated activity in countries such as Australia (Marko et al. 2022, Johnson et al. 2023). Researchers have explored how gambling is increasingly normalized for some specific population groups, including migrant women (Cox et al. 2022), women (Casey 2008, McCarthy et al. 2023), and young men (Deans et al. 2017, Mancini et al. 2025). They have also investigated how gambling occurs in various community settings—particularly associated with sports (Constandt et al. 2022, Kasemi et al. 2026).

Public health approaches to the normalization of gambling for youth

In recent years, there has been a particular focus from researchers, health organizations, community groups, and politicians on the mechanisms that may contribute to the normalization of gambling for youth (up to the age of 24 years), and the threats gambling poses to their short- and long-term health and social equity (Standing Committee on Social Policy and Legal Affairs 2023, Woodhouse and Lalic 2023, Kerr et al. 2024, Charlebois and Kelly 2025). Despite legislated age restrictions for gambling, which range from 16 to 21 years in various parts of the world (Thomas et al. 2023b), studies have demonstrated that many youth engage in either informal or formal gambling well before the legal gambling age (Kristiansen et al. 2018, Oksanen et al. 2021, Constandt et al. 2022, Freund et al. 2022). Until recently, gambling researchers have largely focused on quantitative addiction and psychological approaches to measuring and treating youth gambling (Livazović and Bojčić 2019, Riley et al. 2021). These studies demonstrate that gambling during youth and younger adulthood is associated with the heightened risk of financial loss, poor mental health, relationship strain, and reduced educational engagement, with early initiation a strong predictor of gambling harm in adulthood (Richard and King 2023). However, these studies mostly focus on the individual determinants of gambling in youth and younger adulthood. Some researchers have demonstrated that social norms developed within family and peer networks can provide early exposure to gambling and reinforce gambling behaviours over time (Pérez et al. 2025). However, public health approaches to youth gambling have described the need to examine the range of determinants that may create a pathway to different gambling products and activities (Thomas et al. 2023b).

Youth exposure to the tactics employed by the gambling industry and those with vested interests in gambling (such as sporting codes, community clubs, broadcasters, and other media) are central to how youth form attitudes towards gambling (Pitt et al. 2017a, Thomas et al. 2023b, Royce et al. 2025). A range of tactics are used by the industry (and others with vested interests) to legitimize gambling as a part of everyday life (Thomas et al. 2023b). These commercial tactics are situated within the social contexts of different groups of youth, meaning that some groups, e.g. those that are fans of sport (Deans et al. 2017) or those who live in regional areas in which gambling venues are central to community life (Bestman et al. 2019), may be more vulnerable to tactics that normalize gambling (Thomas et al. 2023b). Changes in accessibility, particularly to online forms of gambling such as sports wagering, and the widespread prevalence of gambling sports sponsorship not only has increased opportunities to gamble but also framed gambling as a socially accepted and culturally valued activity for youth (Constandt et al. 2022). Similarly, the embedding of gambling establishments in community settings creates another environment where gambling products such as electronic gambling machines become normalized through co-location alongside family-friendly facilities (Bestman et al. 2020) or as key leisure spaces (Pérez et al. 2025). Research with parents shows an increased awareness and concern about the extent to which gambling is becoming normalized for youth in a range of settings. This includes concern about the saturation of gambling promotion in sport, with parents often likening industry strategies to those historically used by tobacco and alcohol companies (Pitt et al. 2016, 2024a, David et al. 2020).

While the evidence is clear about the need to develop robust regulatory strategies that protect youth, those with vested interests have powerful positions of influence over gambling policy, and governments have been reluctant to implement policies that protect youth well-being over the interests of the gambling industry and its allies (Thomas et al. 2023b, 2023c, 2026). A recent Australian parliamentary inquiry commented that those who have vested interests in gambling are often ‘lockstep’ in their approaches, including efforts to prevent gambling policies aimed at protecting youth (Standing Committee on Social Policy and Legal Affairs 2023, p. iv). Normalization processes are further shaped by political and regulatory environments, which can either constrain or enable commercial practices that promote gambling (Thomas et al. 2018), and government willingness to prevent gambling marketing to protect youth has varied across the world. Government responses have ranged from comprehensive gambling advertising restrictions aimed at protecting youth in countries such as Spain (Aonso-Diego et al. 2025) and Belgium (Constandt and De Jans 2024) through to significant resistance to implementing such restrictions in Australia (Thomas et al. 2026) and limited consideration of regulation in parts of sub-Saharan Africa (Sichali et al. 2023, Badu et al. 2025). This means the extent to which youth are exposed to pro-gambling messages may vary considerably according to the different environments in which they live, work, and play.

The importance of a conceptual focus on normalization in gambling policy reform

Recently, public health attention to the normalization of gambling for youth has sought to identify the range of comprehensive policy and regulatory strategies (beyond education and treatment) that can be used to disrupt pathways to gambling for youth. This includes understanding how youth conceptualize and respond to new gambling products and marketing tactics; how gambling environments may positively or negatively shape their gambling attitudes and behaviours; and the extent to which social groups may impact gambling practices (Thomas et al. 2023b). However, while this growing body of work has helped to shift public health responses beyond a narrow focus on individual determinants, there remains a need to more clearly conceptualize how these different influences work together. A deeper conceptual engagement with the normalization of gambling is needed to guide and strengthen both research and policy reform. Without a clear conceptual framework, studies risk documenting discrete influences—such as marketing or peer influences—without fully explaining how a range of determinants interact to produce broader sociocultural acceptance of gambling for youth. This in turn is important in strengthening the evidence base for policy, helping to identify leverage points for interventions that address not only individual behaviours but also the wider systems that normalize gambling.

The following narrative review aimed to critically examine how gambling normalization has been applied to qualitative studies related to youth (including those studies which also include younger adults up to 35 years). The review sought to identify gaps in the scientific literature and advance a stronger conceptual foundation to guide both future research and policy reform efforts. This review was guided by three research questions:

Materials and methods

Approach

Narrative reviews are grounded in subjective and interpretivist paradigms and aim to synthesize knowledge about a specific topic, providing critical interpretation rather than descriptive summary, allowing researchers to offer a unique, contextual perspective on the literature (Sukhera 2022, Theile and Beall 2024). Unlike systematic reviews that seek to aggregate findings and emphasize neutrality, narrative reviews provide an authoritative interpretation of the underpinning evidence and the processes used to draw conclusions (Greenhalgh et al. 2018). The review used a ‘Big Q’ approach to qualitative data interpretation, recognizing that knowledge is partial, situated, and co-constructed through researcher reflexivity (Braun and Clarke 2025). Our positionality was as a multidisciplinary group of researchers from public health, marketing, and sports management who take a public health approach to gambling.

Search strategy

The search strategy was informed by a preliminary scan of the scientific literature, which was refined to capture studies related to the normalization of gambling among youth and younger adults. The search terms were developed to align with the Thomas et al. (2018) definition of gambling normalization, while also including broader terms to capture articles that included concepts related to normalization. The search terms combined three core concepts: normalization, gambling, and youth (including initially children and young people). For each concept, a set of keywords and subject terms was used. For normalization, terms included ‘norm*’, ‘social acceptance’, ‘social practices’, ‘social identity’, ‘peer group’, ‘advertising’, ‘marketing’, and ‘culture*’. For gambling, terms included ‘gambling’, ‘betting’, ‘wagering’, ‘pokies’, ‘slots’, ‘casino’, and ‘lotteries’. For youth, terms included ‘teen*’, ‘adolescen*’, ‘youth*’, ‘minor*’, ‘child*’, ‘kid*’, ‘student*’, and ‘young’. These sets were combined using Boolean operators. Using EBSCOHost, a range of databases were included to identify articles in the health and social sciences covering the period from 2015 to 2025. A 10-year time period was chosen as it captured the increasing focus on the normalization of gambling in the academic literature and also included the rapid expansion of online gambling and digital marketing over the last decade. The initial search strategy was guided by the World Health Organization’s definition of youth and young people, with a focus on individuals aged up to 24 years (World Health Organisation n.d.). However, during the screening process it became apparent that many studies did not report findings for this age group separately. Instead, participants were frequently grouped into broader ‘younger adult’ categories, such as 18–29 or 18–35 years. To avoid excluding studies that included substantial youth populations, we broadened the eligibility criteria to include studies with participant age ranges up to 35 years.

We included empirical and peer-reviewed journal articles which used any qualitative methodology. We chose to focus on qualitative studies given the importance of centring youth voices in public health and health promotion research (Thompson and Lindo 2024) and experiences in understanding the processes that may contribute to the normalization of gambling (McCarthy et al. 2024, Pitt et al. 2024b, Thomas et al. 2025). To ensure the review remained focused on gambling as a distinct social and cultural practice, we included studies in which gambling was the primary focus (e.g. gambling marketing, environments, or behaviours). Studies were not required to explicitly use the term ‘normalisation’ or provide a definition of normalization (although we documented how many studies did this). Rather, studies were included if they addressed concepts or processes relevant to the normalization of gambling, such as factors that may contribute to increased social or cultural acceptance of gambling, may shape social practices or norms, or may shape attitudes towards gambling. We also included studies that consulted with youth about strategies to respond to the normalization of gambling. Studies were excluded if they were mixed methods without primarily reporting qualitative findings, lacked clear participant age ranges, or considered gambling solely as a secondary feature of broader behavioural or health issues.

Screening and search outcomes

Study selection was guided by the Preferred Reporting of Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (Moher et al. 2009; Fig. 1). The Covidence systematic review software was used to manage the data. Initial search results (n = 930) were imported into Covidence, and duplicates (n = 537) were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by M.M. and S.M. Discrepancies were resolved through discussions with the research team, with H.P. and G.A. acting as adjudicators. Members of the research team were co-authors on a number of the studies included in this review. To ensure transparency and reduce potential bias in decisions about study inclusion, the eligibility of these articles was confirmed with members of the research team who were not involved in the authorship of papers. This process helped to maintain consistency in the application of the inclusion criteria and strengthen the credibility of the review. Full texts were reviewed to ensure eligibility, and again any discrepancies were discussed and resolved with the research team. This resulted in the inclusion of n = 22 articles.

Data analysis

M.M. documented the key methodological characteristics of each study, identifying the overall qualitative methodological approach taken, country of origin, the type of sampling used, inclusion of research questions, how the data were collected, whether the study used a theoretical framework, whether any limitations were outlined, the funding source, and whether any conflicts of interest were declared. Reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) was used to interpret the data, through an iterative, non-linear interpretive process (Braun and Clarke 2022). RTA involved a non-linear process of developing, analysing, and interpreting patterns of meaning across the dataset using Braun and Clarke’s (2022) six-phase approach to analysis. Quotations from the included articles were selected to provide examples of the themes identified through the synthesis and to support the narrative interpretation of the findings. Reflexivity occurred through team meetings and Microsoft Teams chats where ideas were discussed, drawing on different perspectives according to the team members’ disciplinary backgrounds.

Overview of the articles and methodological characteristics.

Age ranges

There was little consistency in age ranges employed in the studies, with only n = 7 articles providing a justification for why certain age ranges were chosen. Ten articles included participants only under the age of 18 (Royce et al. 2025, Hing et al. 2024a, 2024b, Pitt et al. 2017a, 2017b, 2022, 2024b, 2024c, Kitchin et al. 2024, Nyemcsok et al. 2021), with three articles involving participants both under and over the age of 18 (Adebisi et al. 2021, Kirkeby 2025, Pérez et al. 2025).

Recruitment according to specific sample criteria

The majority of articles (n = 17) recruited both male and female participants. Some articles recruited specific types of youth—including youth who were fans of sport (n = 4) or only those who had gambled (n = 9)—although there was limited consistency in the required frequency of gambling (Gordon et al. 2015, Nekich and Ohtsuka 2016, Lamont and Hing 2019, McCarthy et al. 2020, McGee 2020, Adebisi et al. 2021, Torrance et al. 2021, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Di Censo et al. 2025). Additionally, two studies screened for ‘problem gambling’ behaviours (Gordon et al. 2015, Hing et al. 2024b).

Funding source and declaration of interests

Eighteen articles included a funding statement. Over half of these reported funding via government sources (n = 10), including from state government statutory funding (Gordon et al. 2015, McCarthy et al. 2020, Hing et al. 2024a, 2024b, Di Censo et al. 2025), and independent national research council funding (Pitt et al. 2017a, 2022, 2024b, 2024c, Kitchin et al. 2024), two from university funding sources (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, Kirkeby 2025), two from charities (McGee 2020, Torrance et al. 2021)—including a PhD bursary from GambleAware, an organization funded via voluntary donations from various gambling companies (Torrance et al. 2021), one from a research organization (Pérez et al. 2025), one from a health promotion agency (Royce et al. 2025), and one article reported that it received no funding (Nyemcsok et al. 2022). One article listed multiple sources of author funding but did not specify any support directly for the study (Pitt et al. 2017b). The remaining four articles had no funding statement (Nekich and Ohtsuka 2016, Lamont and Hing 2019, Adebisi et al. 2021, Jones et al. 2022).

Nineteen articles had a formal conflict of interest statement, although over half of them stated that there were no interests to declare (n = 12). In seven articles, researchers declared a range of interests including non-industry funding, advisory roles, editorial roles, and government affiliations. The remaining three articles did not have a conflict of interest statement (Gordon et al. 2015, Adebisi et al. 2021, Jones et al. 2022, Di Censo et al. 2025).

Methodological approaches

Qualitative approach and definitions

The majority of articles (n = 19) specified the type of qualitative approach utilized, with nine using a version of (reflexive) thematic analysis (either Braun and Clarke 2006 or Braun and Clarke 2021). Only two articles included a definition for gambling normalization (McCarthy et al. 2020, Nyemcsok et al. 2021).

Sampling, recruitment, and data collection approaches

Seven articles did not provide information about a specific approach to sampling. Those that did, mostly used a combination of convenience, purposive, and snowball approaches. Participants were recruited through a range of settings including sporting and community groups, social media sites, schools, gambling venues, online forums, and online panel companies. More than half of the articles used individual interviewing techniques to collect data (n = 12) (Nekich and Ohtsuka 2016, Pitt et al. 2017a, 2017b, 2022, Lamont and Hing 2019, McCarthy et al. 2020, McGee 2020, Adebisi et al. 2021, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Hing et al. 2024a, 2024b, Kirkeby 2025). While all articles in the final sample included youth and younger adults as participants, none reported that they involved youth in the design or conceptualization of the research.

Reported limitations

All but one of the articles reported study limitations (n = 21), although these were largely positivist in nature. This included lack of generalizability (n = 11), or that samples were skewed by age (Pitt et al. 2017a, Nyemcsok et al. 2021), gender (Pitt et al. 2017a, 2017b, 2024b, 2024c, Pérez et al. 2025), or higher socioeconomic backgrounds (McCarthy et al. 2020, Pitt et al. 2024c)

Thematic analysis

Four themes were constructed from the qualitative data presented in the articles. Figure 2 summarizes the key themes and subthemes constructed from the data.

Themes and subthemes. Exploring the normalization of gambling for youth and younger adults: a narrative review of qualitative studies.

Theme 1: the social construction of gambling as a normal part of everyday life

Several articles found that gambling was embedded in young people’s everyday lives through social contexts. Many participants described being exposed to gambling during childhood through shared family activities, such as playing card games, visiting gambling venues for meals, or celebrating birthdays in these spaces (Pitt et al. 2017a, McCarthy et al. 2020, Pérez et al. 2025):

My parents always play the Christmas lottery and the Euromillions, other relatives play the typical pools quite a lot and, for example, my grandmother, when she has nothing to do, goes with her friends to the bingo hall to hang out. (age not specified, male) (Pérez et al. 2025, p. 7)

Observing family members engaging in sports betting during childhood played a role in participants’ interest in (Di Censo et al. 2025), their knowledge of, and confidence with gambling (McCarthy et al. 2020). For some participants, these early experiences continued into adulthood, with gambling becoming an independent activity once they reached the legal gambling age (McCarthy et al. 2020, p. 378).

Only one article found that youth reported that their parents discouraged and tried to role model non-gambling behaviours:

My parents always despised gambling as my uncle wasted all his money on it and went off the rails. So that early instilling of the bad rep of gambling has stuck with me. (15–17 years old, male) (Hing et al. 2024a, p. 6)

Social familiarization extended beyond the family. Peer groups played a central role in normalizing gambling through shared behaviours, group expectations, and social reinforcement (Lamont and Hing 2019, Jones et al. 2022, Hing et al. 2024b, Kirkeby 2025, Pérez et al. 2025). Multiple articles reported that gambling was seen by participants as an enjoyable group activity, that was embedded in school, sport, and leisure settings (Jones et al. 2022, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Pérez et al. 2025, Royce et al. 2025). Social conformity and norms also played a role in encouraging younger adult participation in gambling:

No, it’s not peer pressure, but social conformity. If everyone in that room was doing it … I wasn’t going to be the odd one out sort of thing. (20 years old, female) (McCarthy et al. 2020, p. 378)

However, three articles indicated that peer expectations and behaviours could deter gambling (Hing et al. 2024a, Di Censo et al. 2025, Pérez et al. 2025). This included seeing peers gambling in a ‘disordered fashion’, which encouraged youth to reflect on their own betting decisions (Di Censo et al. 2025, p. 12), as well as having peer groups where gambling was not regarded as a positive or normal cultural activity:

My friends come from backgrounds where gambling is highly discouraged and they have carried that out through our friendship, we don’t talk about gambling often and so I tend not to associate with it, this has also discouraged me from gambling. (15–17 years old, female) (Hing et al. 2024a, p. 7)

Gambling practices in social groups were considered ‘highly gendered’, including elements of peer pressure (McCarthy et al. 2020, p. 378). One study found young men felt compelled by ‘persuasive peer pressure’ to bet, driven by a desire to ‘shore up their masculine credentials and peer group status’ (Lamont and Hing 2019, p. 255). Sports betting was strongly associated with being a sports fan, something that intensified enjoyment and deepened connection with peers (Pitt et al. 2017b, McGee 2020). This normalization of gambling within sport was sometimes so powerful that youth and younger adults felt the need to gamble to be part of sports cultures:

It wouldn't be interesting if there wasn't a few quid and a bit of a sweat on! I don't think I could go through a Saturday and not gamble on the football anymore. (31 years old, male) (McGee 2020, p. 90)

Theme 2: the marketing and media ecosystem that normalizes gambling

Gambling advertising was constructed as a key mechanism of normalization (Pitt et al. 2017a, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Royce et al. 2025). Participants, regardless of age, expressed concern about the saturation of gambling ads, which was described as ‘excessive’ (Pitt et al. 2024c, p. 9), ‘inescapable’ (Royce et al. 2025, p. 4), and ‘immoderate and unwarranted’ (Torrance et al. 2021, p. 980). Articles noted that frequent exposure, particularly in sport, reinforced the view of gambling as a normal part of everyday life and increased interest in betting (Pitt et al. 2017b, McGee 2020, Torrance et al. 2021, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Di Censo et al. 2025).

Especially if I’m out for dinner and I see an ad on TV come up, it makes me feel the urge to bet. (23 years old, female) (Di Censo et al. 2025, p. 11)

Participants both under and over 18 years consistently identified sporting events as a key site of exposure to positive gambling messages (Pitt et al. 2017a, Nyemcsok et al. 2022, Royce et al. 2025). These frequent messages led youth to interpret betting as a regular part of watching sport, and something that most people participated in:

When there are ads…or the commentators say the odds, it makes you feel like there are a lot more people doing it. (15 years old, female) (Pitt et al. 2017b, p. 607)

In three articles specifically involving participants under 18, youth reported exposure to gambling marketing on online platforms, including YouTube, Snapchat, Instagram, and TikTok (Kitchin et al. 2024, Pitt et al. 2024b, Royce et al. 2025). In an Irish study with adolescents aged 14–17 years, Kitchin et al. (2024) found that youth recalled frequent exposure to gambling ads while engaging with digital content, often without the ability to skip or avoid them:

Like you always see them when you’re watching YouTube, like every two videos…especially if the team is sponsored by one of them. (no identifier) (Kitchin et al. 2024, p. 6)

Some articles found that the way advertisements framed gambling impacted youth perceptions and attitudes. In the UK, youth perceived gambling advertisements as one-sided, only showing ‘the fantastic points of gambling’ in a ‘glamorous’ or ‘glorified’ way rather than also including more realistic representations such as loss, frustration or addiction (Torrance et al. 2021, p. 982). Australian studies also found that gambling advertisements were framed to emphasize fun, glamour, and success, while minimizing or omitting the risks of harm (McCarthy et al. 2020, Di Censo et al. 2025). In McCarthy et al.’s (2020) study of young Australian women (aged 18–34) who had gambled, participants reported being encouraged to gamble because of the advertising they had seen for gambling:

That’s how I even started the sports betting because it was on TV. Bonus bet, sign up today. Okay, that sounds good. So that’s what got me in. (25 years old) (McCarthy et al. 2020, p. 379)

In Australian articles, the use of celebrities, athletes, and social media influencers in gambling promotions was a powerful strategy that increased the visibility, appeal, and perceived legitimacy of gambling (Pitt et al. 2017b, 2024c, Di Censo et al. 2025, Royce et al. 2025). Royce et al. (2025) reported that participants could readily identify the presence of celebrities, influencers, and musicians in gambling advertisements. Pitt et al. (2024c) found that social media influencers were seen as ‘particularly appealing’ to youth as they were ‘more relatable than celebrities’ (Pitt et al. 2024c, p. 6). Among younger adults aged 18–24 years, Di Censo et al. (2025) described how the inclusion of celebrities and influencers in gambling advertisements enhanced the perceived attractiveness of betting:

When [celebrities] endorse a betting company, it can lend credibility… create an association between sports betting and success, wealth, and glamour. (18 years old, female) (Di Censo et al. 2025, p. 8)

Theme 3: digital access and platforms that normalize gambling

Digital technologies expanded the accessibility of gambling through smartphones (Hing et al. 2024a, 2024b), apps (Pitt et al. 2017b, McGee 2020), and computers (Kirkeby 2025), embedding gambling into familiar digital spaces and routines (McGee 2020, Kirkeby 2025). Pitt and colleagues (2017b) found that youth under the legal gambling age were familiar with how to engage in gambling through mobile phones and apps through the marketing that they had seen:

Then he’s got his phone and then he like says ‘You should bet now it’s really easy’ and then he switches his phone a bit and then he gets it up and pretends to click the team. (13 years old, male) (Pitt et al. 2017b, p. 607)

Digital spaces also facilitated participation in online betting communities, including ‘chatrooms’, ‘forums’, and ‘tipping-related websites’ where youth sought tips and validation for their gambling (Di Censo et al. 2025, p. 12). This technological shift also enabled gambling to enter physical spaces previously considered off-limits including workplaces (McGee 2020), and school (Kitchin et al. 2024, Kirkeby 2025):

Well like during the world cup, there was a lot of people in my school who would have been betting… like a lot of the boys. They almost have bets within each other, and then they bet on the football league through the apps and stuff to see who could get the most money out of it. (17 years old, female) (Kitchin et al. 2024, pp. 6–7)

In addition to repeated gambling exposure across digital platforms, McGee (2020) found online gambling was perceived by young adult male participants as more socially acceptable than betting in physical venues and even described it as ‘more addictive’ due to the ‘facelessness’ of the technology (McGee 2020, p. 91).

Theme 4: Youth perspectives on tackling gambling normalization and harm

Seven of the 22 articles explored participants’ views on strategies for reducing the normalization of gambling, minimizing harm, and shaping responses to industry marketing and regulation (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, Torrance et al. 2021, Pitt et al. 2022, Kitchin et al. 2024, Pitt et al. 2024b, 2024c, Royce et al. 2025). Regardless of age, participants placed a strong focus on the regulation of advertising, with youth and younger adults expressing frustration at their constant exposure to promotions across media platforms (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, Torrance et al. 2021, Pitt et al. 2022). Youth under 18 recommended banning gambling advertising on television or at certain times when young people might be watching (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, p. 168). Participants recommended that gambling advertising ‘should be banned all together’ and that ‘there’s absolutely no reason gambling adverts should be tolerated, while smoking and other addictive products are not allowed to be advertised’ (Torrance et al. 2021, p. 983). Youth in two articles also called for greater government accountability in this area (Pitt et al. 2024b, Royce et al. 2025). They urged political leaders to take stronger action against the influence of gambling companies:

[The Prime Minister] needs to put more pressure on social media and media networks … to make sure that children aren’t exposed to gambling ads. (16 years old, female) (Pitt et al. 2024b, p. 9).

Youth also linked their concerns about advertising to gambling sponsorships in sport. One participant described these sponsorships as ‘hypocritical’, suggesting that sporting codes publicly promoted ‘Be Gamble Aware’ messaging while continuing to profit from gambling sponsorships (Kitchin et al. 2024, p. 9). Youth suggested ‘there should be less of a presence of gambling companies’ sponsorship in sports’ (Pitt et al. 2022, p. 6). Some recommended that teams should partner with sponsors that reflect sporting or health-related values:

….if it’s a sports team, it should probably be sponsored by something associated with the sports brand… like Adidas or Nike… or sports drinks. (16 years old, male) (Kitchin et al. 2024, p. 7).

Counter-marketing and awareness campaigns were recommended to ensure that youth were aware of the risks and harms of gambling and the gambling industry. In articles with participants under 18 years old, youth suggested the need for evidence-based, visible, and hard-hitting warnings (Pitt et al. 2022, 2024b, Royce et al. 2025). Some recognized gambling as being a similar health issue like smoking and thought that some of the effective public health strategies implemented by governments such as images of harm, could be used for gambling:

I think like when the government put … photos of how bad it can be on cigarette packets … they should probably do something about that for gambling … maybe … on the screen of the pokies machine. (12 years old, male) (Pitt et al. 2022, pp. 8–9)

School-based education was described by youth as another important strategy. Youth proposed that schools could include evidence-based gambling education in the curriculum. For example, ‘notify more kids to tell them what betting and gambling does and the consequences… by having people come into schools’ (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, p. 168), or run programmes from an early age so that ‘if the school did a program that teaches kids about the risk of [gambling] … they’ll be less likely to actually gamble’ (Pitt et al. 2022, p. 9). Involving people with lived experience in the development and delivery of gambling education was also recommended, with participants noting that ‘if they have somebody who has probably felt the effects of gambling right there, they will probably not gamble’ (Pitt et al. 2024b, p. 7). Within education initiatives, youth also recommended peer-led approaches, noting that messages might be more impactful when delivered by other youth:

If I saw on TV [someone] telling me not to gamble … I would most likely pay more attention to the one with my age telling me. (15 years old, female) (Pitt et al. 2024b, p. 8).

Additionally, in Australia, youth recommended that governments ban or reduce the availability of gambling products such as electronic gambling machines, which were located in community settings (Pitt et al. 2022). One article stressed the importance of youth involvement in decision-making, emphasizing that youth opinions should be sought by governments to guide formal policy responses (Pitt et al. 2024b).

Finally, there were some alternative views about strategies to reduce harm from the normalization of gambling. These included individual responsibility framings of gambling harm, suggesting that people just ‘need to think before they gamble’ (Royce et al. 2025, p. 7) and were capable of ‘mak[ing] their own decisions’ about gambling (Torrance et al. 2021, p. 981). One article reported that a small minority of participants believed gambling companies had ‘every right to advertise’ (Royce et al. 2025, p. 8). Similarly, a small number of participants in another article stated ‘it [gambling sponsorship] could give more of a boost to that sport’ (Pitt et al. 2022, p. 7).

Discussion

This narrative review examined how qualitative studies have investigated factors contributing to the normalization of gambling for youth and younger adults. The review sought to identify the characteristics of existing studies, the range of determinants that were identified as contributing to normalization, and youth recommendations to respond to the normalization of gambling. There were three key areas for discussion related to the research questions.

First, there were a range of strengths and limitations related to current qualitative research approaches. While the normalization of gambling for youth is recognized as a growing public health issue internationally (Armitage 2021, Bitanihirwe and Ssewanyana 2021, Savolainen et al. 2021, Schweitzer 2025), the qualitative evidence base remains geographically narrow, with most qualitative research identified in this review involving youth in Australia. The lack of qualitative research outside of Australia makes it difficult to understand whether the processes and drivers of gambling normalization for youth and younger adults operate in similar ways in other countries and contexts. There was a particular gap in research from the United States of America, which was not represented in this review, and there were very few studies conducted in low and middle-income countries (Adebisi et al. 2021). This limited evidence base contrasts the rapid commercial expansion of the online gambling industry in both of these contexts (Glozah et al. 2023, Galmes-Cerezo et al. 2025, Gramlich 2025, Lee and Lee 2025, Petrotta 2025). Future qualitative research outside of Australia, as well as cross-cultural research, is needed to better understand which aspects of gambling normalization for youth and younger adults are common across settings and which are context specific. Australian researchers should be open to sharing their methodological approaches and survey tools with researchers in these contexts. Noting that technology has enabled the gambling industry to operate across borders, such research will also help to develop a more comprehensive and comparative understanding of the global practices of the gambling industry and their strategies to appeal to youth and younger adults. This will enhance global efforts, including those led by the World Health Organisation (2024), to prevent future generations from experiencing gambling industry harm.

There is also an opportunity to strengthen methodological approaches to qualitative research in this area. Over half of the articles did not include a justification of the participant age ranges, and these were inconsistently defined, with studies combining youth and younger adults up to 35 years within the same sample. While this approach provided insight into transitional life stages, it risks conflating distinct developmental and social experiences (Richard and King 2023). The lack of transparency around age in these included studies meant that it was difficult to identify differences in the factors that might be contributing to the normalization of gambling among youth and younger adults, including in some articles which did not include a specific age in participant identifiers aligned with quotes. It is also important to note that this review only focused on studies that included participants up to 35 years, other studies that included young adults as part of a wider adult age range may have provided further insights into gambling normalization.

Social influences and norms, marketing exposure, and digital environments were identified as important determinants across all age groups. Where differences did emerge, these reflected age-specific contexts, such as gambling within school settings among younger participants. Importantly, youth and younger adults are not a homogeneous group, and it is essential that future qualitative studies engage diverse youth populations to capture the range of experiences that shape gambling normalization. This includes greater attention to those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, rural and remote communities, Indigenous and First Nations youth, and sexual and gender diverse groups, as studies focused on diverse youth populations remain largely absent from the existing qualitative literature. Addressing these limitations through broader geographic coverage, more inclusive study designs, and greater theoretical coherence will be essential to strengthening the global evidence base on the normalization of gambling and its implications for youth. While it is encouraging that few of the studies had been funded directly or indirectly by the gambling industry, this may be because the gambling industry (and its allies) are unlikely to fund research that investigates the broader sociocultural, commercial, and political determinants of gambling normalization.

Second, there were ongoing inconsistencies with how gambling normalization was theoretically defined and operationalized. Although a working definition of gambling normalization was published by Thomas and colleagues (2018), few studies were found to have adopted it. Conceptual and theoretical definitions are important because they provide a shared foundation for understanding, enabling consistent interpretation, comparison, and advancement of knowledge across studies (Solinger et al. 2024). This review and its search terms were informed by the definition of gambling normalization (Thomas et al. 2018). Consequently, the review may not have captured studies examining factors relevant to gambling normalization that were conceptualized or described using different terminology or theoretical frameworks. Despite this limitation, the studies included in this review identified a range of determinants that contribute to the normalization of gambling among youth and younger adults. Across studies, these determinants were socially constructed and situated within the everyday contexts of young people’s lives, where digital environments intersect with social, commercial, and political processes to amplify exposure to gambling marketing and reshape peer and leisure interactions (Kickbusch et al. 2021, Holly et al. 2025). Future research should examine the digital determinants of health in relation to gambling normalization (Kickbusch and Holly 2023, van Kessel et al. 2025). As gambling environments continue to evolve, it is important that conceptual definitions of gambling normalization are revisited and refined to reflect these developments (Solinger et al. 2024).

Moving forward, we propose a slightly modified version of the Thomas et al. (2018) (p.6) definition, to highlight the importance of social and political contexts in shaping the normalization of gambling:

The normalisation of gambling is a socially, culturally, commercially and politically shaped process. It occurs when interconnected determinants increase the availability, accessibility and visibility of gambling across physical and online environments, encourage recent and regular use of gambling products, and position gambling as an accepted part of everyday life for individuals, families and communities.

Finally, few studies sought the direct views from youth participants about the policy and regulatory factors that could prevent the normalization of gambling (Pitt et al. 2022, 2024b, Royce et al. 2025). Youth recommended a range of public health responses, including increased marketing restrictions, reducing the accessibility and availability of gambling opportunities, and evidence-based education initiatives (Nyemcsok et al. 2021, Torrance et al. 2021, Kitchin et al. 2024). However, while there was some inclusion of youth perspectives, there were no studies that included youth in the design or conceptualization of the research. This is despite the UN Convention of the Rights of the Child Article 12, which recognizes that youth should be provided the opportunity to have a say on matters that are relevant to them and be listened to (United Nations 1989). Including youth throughout the research process can improve the relevance of the research (McCabe et al. 2023), build youth participants capacity and skills, and create a sense of ownership over the research (Warraitch et al. 2024). Diverse youth should be engaged as active contributors in both research design and policy decision-making, recognizing their unique insights into how gambling operates. Meaningful youth participation can help reframe policy and public health responses towards collective responsibility and more equitable approaches to gambling industry regulation and harm prevention initiatives.

Conclusion

This review shows that the normalization of gambling for youth and younger adults is a socially constructed and situated process shaped by social, commercial, and political determinants. These determinants combine to present gambling as routine, acceptable, and desirable, embedding it into the lives of youth and younger adults. While previous reviews have emphasized the influence of gambling marketing and sport on youth gambling attitudes and behaviours, this review extends the evidence by synthesizing qualitative research on the broader social, commercial, political and digital determinants that contribute to gambling normalization. The findings also highlight the role of commercial influences in shaping the normalization of gambling among youth and younger adults, embedding gambling within everyday environments and framing gambling as entertainment. Addressing these issues requires expanding the global evidence base to examine gambling normalization as a public health threat. Future research should be co-designed with youth and younger adults, ensuring their perspectives and experiences inform the evidence and subsequent policy and public health responses.
