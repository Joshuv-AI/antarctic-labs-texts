The role of a transitional care program in supporting family caregivers to prevent relapse in severe mental illness: a qualitative study from Iran
Authors: Ali Asgharzadeh, Somayeh Janmohammadi, Maryam Pourabbas, Maryam Vahidi, Babak Rashti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDischarge from acute psychiatric care is a high-risk transition often associated with relapse, suicide, and poor treatment adherence. Transitional care programs. aim to improve care continuity through psychoeducation, case management, and structured follow-up. Despite mixed evidence on their impact on reducing readmissions, these programs are widely accepted by patients and caregivers. This study explored family caregivers’ experiences of a transitional care program in Iran, with a focus on its role in preventing relapse.MethodsThis qualitative study utilized a conventional content analysis approach. Participants were 15 family caregivers of patients with severe mental disorders attending the Post-Discharge Education and Care Unit at Razi Educational Therapeutic Center in Tabriz, Iran. Purposeful sampling was employed to ensure diversity, and data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews until saturation was achieved. Data analysis followed Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis framework, with trustworthiness ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.FindingsAnalysis revealed a transformative process comprising four interconnected themes: “Improved Psychological Well-being” (enhanced security and positive emotions),“Educational–Attitudinal Transformation” (shifting from blame to understanding), “Knowledge Sharing and Diligence in Practice” (active dissemination and improved caregiving), and “Enhanced Patient Engagement and Adherence” (managed patient anxiety and strengthened motivation). These themes converged into the overarching pattern of “Life Transformation”.ConclusionTransitional care programs can empower family caregivers by enhancing their knowledge, attitudes, and psychological well-being, which helps reduce caregiver burden and supports relapse prevention in patients with severe mental illness. The findings highlight the importance of integrating caregiver education, professional support, and continuity of care into psychiatric services to improve long-term outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Transitional care; Caregivers, Family; Patient compliance; Relapse prevention

Introduction

Discharging a patient from an acute psychiatric ward to home is considered a critical transition [1]. The initial days and weeks following discharge represent a period of high vulnerability for patients. During this time, individuals often face numerous challenges in daily life, including an increased risk of suicide, anxiety, loneliness, diminished self-esteem, stigma, non-adherence to treatment, and difficulties coping with recurring symptoms [2–4].

These challenges frequently lead to symptom relapse or hospital readmission, a key indicator of mental healthcare quality [5]. The readmission risk peaks within the first 30 days post-discharge [6], as evidenced by international rates: 30-day rates range from 8% in the U.S [7]. to 15.1% in Norway and 16.69% in China, with longer-term rates rising to 47.7% and 33.79% in Norway and China, respectively, at one year [8, 9]. A six-month rate of 21.4% was also reported in England [10].

Furthermore, disruptions in care continuity are common. One study found that nearly 40% of patients with schizophrenia did not attend any outpatient follow-up appointments within 30 days of discharge [11]. Unplanned discharge has been identified as a significant risk factor for readmission [9, 12]. Poor continuity of care is consistently associated with worse patient outcomes and higher healthcare costs [13]. Therefore, preventive measures and comprehensive transitional support are essential to ensure a successful transition from inpatient care to the community.

Interventions initiated prior to discharge and continued after inpatient care are termed transitional interventions with bridging components [3, 14]. These are structured discharge management strategies designed to maintain the therapeutic relationship established during hospitalization. By providing support both before and after the inpatient stay, healthcare professionals can identify pre-discharge needs and address or follow up on them in the community setting. This combination of pre- and post-discharge elements meets patients’ needs for continuity of care and is considered a beneficial and promising approach for supporting an effective transition from hospital to community [3]. A prominent example of this approach is Naylor et al.‘s Transitional Care Model (TCM). This nurse-led, multidisciplinary model is specifically designed for vulnerable populations. Its effectiveness is derived from core components that work in concert to maintain continuity, foster collaboration among the care team, patients, and relatives, deliver essential education, and establish the nurse as the primary coordinator of care [15]. A systematic review categorized 15 distinct transitional interventions into pre- and post-discharge components. Pre-discharge strategies encompassed case management (needs assessment, discharge/crisis planning, appointment preparation, family involvement), psychoeducation (individual counseling, medication integration), and CBT-based skills training and peer support. Post-discharge support was delivered via phone calls, home visits, or letters, focusing on ensuring outpatient follow-up, coordinating treatment, facilitating provider communication, monitoring health, implementing aftercare plans, and activating social resources. CBT sessions and further psychoeducation and peer support were also provided [3].

Evidence on the effectiveness of transitional interventions is mixed. An Iranian study of 43 patients receiving home-based care found a significant increase in readmission rates after the program ended [16]. Similarly, Marcus et al. linked prompt outpatient care to reduced readmission risk [17]. However, a systematic review found bridging interventions were not significantly more effective than usual care in reducing readmissions, though it noted limited evidence and called for more robust research [3]. Despite uncertain efficacy on readmissions, these interventions show high user acceptability, with majority of caregivers and patients reporting high satisfaction with services [3, 16].

The Post-Discharge Education, Care, and Follow-up Unit at Razi Educational Therapeutic Center was established in April 2022. Services initially focused on patients with bipolar disorder and supportive families before expanding after six months to include all neurotic and psychotic patients, excluding those with substance use or personality disorders. The service protocol begins with inpatient referral by a case manager, followed by a psychoeducational session for primary caregivers conducted by a psychiatric nurse, with psychological intervention available as needed. Simultaneously, a unit nurse meets with the patient pre-discharge to establish rapport and explain the follow-up process. On discharge day, patients and families visit the unit for treatment alliance formation and complementary education. Patients then receive stratified follow-up via telephone or in-person visits. The final planned step—referral to the Provincial Mental Health Center after stability assessment and checklist completion—is currently in the planning phase. A pre-implementation qualitative study at the center revealed caregivers’ significant distress and role overload due to inadequate support, highlighting the critical need for structured training and resources [18].

Preliminary hospital data and field evaluations suggest that the Transitional Care Program has contributed to a reduction in patient readmission rates and an increase in client and family satisfaction. Consequently, a qualitative study is warranted to explore the specific mechanisms and facilitators underlying these positive outcomes from the caregivers’ perspectives. Given their continuous presence and 24-hour care role, family caregivers are the most valuable source of information regarding the unit’s functioning. Qualitative methods are well-suited to deeply explore complex, context-specific phenomena such as this, often revealing aspects that quantitative studies may overlook [19–21]. Therefore, due to the importance of understanding caregivers’ experiences and the limited existing research, this qualitative study was conducted to elucidate not only the specific outcomes of the program but, more importantly, the underlying process and mechanisms through which it shaped caregivers’ experiences, empowered them in their role, and ultimately influenced patient relapse prevention.

Research Question: What is the process by which a transitional care program shapes the experiences of family caregivers and influences relapse prevention in patients with severe mental illness?

Methods

This qualitative study employed a conventional content analysis approach. The participants consisted of family caregivers of patients with severe mental disorders utilizing the Post-Discharge Education and Care Unit at Razi Educational Therapeutic Center in Tabriz, Iran. Purposeful sampling was used to identify information-rich participants. Eligible participants were identified through patient record reviews and assessed for cooperation, communication skills, and willingness to share experiences. After obtaining ethical approvals from Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (code: IR.TBZMED.REC.1403.609), the researcher contacted potential participants, explained study objectives, emphasized voluntary participation and the right to withdraw, and assured them that their care would not be affected. Written informed consent was obtained; for illiterate participants, verbal explanations were provided and consent was confirmed via thumbprint. Sampling aimed for diversity in gender, age, and patient diagnosis. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved [22], with cultural and religious considerations respected throughout. The study was conducted in the Post-Discharge Education and Care Unit. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 18 or older with no self-reported psychiatric disorders, at least 30 days of service utilization from the unit, cohabitation with the patient, and primary caregiving responsibility.

Interviews commenced with a broad, open-ended question: caregivers were asked, “Please describe your experience attending at the unit.” Subsequent questions were guided by the participants’ responses and aligned with the study’s goals. As the study progressed, the interviews were increasingly directed based on emerging categories, with the researcher formulating questions according to the salient and significant themes that developed. Throughout the interviews, probing questions—such as “Could you elaborate further?“, “What did you mean by that?“, and “Could you describe a specific situation you encountered and provide an example?“—were used to elicit deeper clarification and additional detail. All interviews were audio-recorded using a MP3 player and continued until data saturation was achieved [22]. Field notes served as an additional data collection method. These notes documented observations and auditory experiences in the research setting [22]. Additionally, the researcher utilized memoing: from the outset of data collection, ideas and relationships that arose during data gathering and analysis were promptly documented. These memos, like the field notes, were subsequently coded and integrated into the analysis.

Data analysis was conducted using qualitative content analysis following the approach described by Graneheim and Lundman [23]. The interview transcripts were read multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding of the content. The text was then divided into meaning units, which were subsequently condensed. These condensed units were abstracted and labeled with codes. The codes were compared based on their differences and similarities and organized into subcategories and main categories, representing the manifest content. The preliminary categories were reviewed and refined through discussions between two researchers. The process of reflection and discussion led to a consensus on the organization of the codes. Finally, the latent meaning of the categories, representing the underlying content, was formulated into themes.

Trustworthiness was ensured through Lincoln and Guba’s criteria [24] : Credibility was ensured through multiple strategies: prolonged engagement with participants to build trust, immersive data analysis through repeated reading of transcripts, purposeful diversity in participant selection (age, gender, relationship to patient), and regular team debriefings to critically review and refine interpretations. Confirmability was secured through an external audit where two qualitative experts independently verified the data analysis, combined with maintaining a detailed audit trail that systematically documented all analytical decisions and research steps to ensure the findings directly stem from the data. Dependability was ensured through an external inquiry audit where two independent qualitative researchers systematically examined the entire research process, including data collection methods, analytical decisions, and interpretation procedures, to verify that the findings were consistently derived from the data and the research process was logically structured and well-documented.

As a research team, we actively practiced reflexivity throughout the study—a process of critical self-reflection on our own backgrounds, assumptions, and potential influences on the research—to heighten the study’s rigor. Acknowledging that our professional backgrounds in nursing and psychology could inherently shape our interpretations, we systematically maintained reflexive journals and conducted regular debriefing sessions. In these sessions, we critically challenged our assumptions and explicitly sought alternative explanations for the data to ensure no single perspective dominated the analysis.

Results

This study involved a sample of 15 family caregivers. Participants ranged in age from 27 to 74 years, with 20% in the 27–42 age group, 40% in the 43–58 group, and 40% in the 59–74 group. The sample consisted of 7 females and 8 males. In terms of their relationship to the care recipient, seven were parents, five were spouses, two were children, and one was a sister. The care recipients had been diagnosed with Schizophrenia, Bipolar Mood Disorder, or Major Depressive Disorder.

Data analysis began with 400 codes that were progressively refined through an iterative process of comparison and consolidation. Our findings reveal a sequential and self-reinforcing process through which the transitional care program influenced caregiver experiences and improved patient outcomes. The process originated with the development of “Improved Psychological Well-being among caregivers”, characterized by a foundational sense of psychological safety, support, and the emergence of positive emotions such as hope, reassurance, and happiness. This psychological foundation served as a crucial prerequisite, enabling caregivers to engage effectively as receptive learners in the program’s educational components.

Feeling psychologically secure, caregivers participated actively in educational sessions, which prompted a profound Educational–Attitudinal Transformation. This shift—from misunderstanding and blame toward empathy and informed understanding—functioned as the core mechanism for building caregiver confidence. Equipped with this new knowledge and a more empathetic outlook, caregivers subsequently translated their learning into practical action, demonstrating Knowledge Sharing and Diligence in Practice. The combined effect of psychological safety and growing confidence empowered them to become proactive agents in care, generating positive ripple effects that extended beyond their immediate households.

Finally, this progression of caregiver development directly supported improved patient outcomes, resulting in Enhanced Patient Engagement and Adherence. This was achieved through two key program-mediated mechanisms: transforming patient anxiety into motivation for treatment adherence, and fostering a sense of importance and value in patients.

So, the analysis identified four core categories—“Improved Psychological Well-being,” “Educational–Attitudinal Transformation,” “Knowledge Sharing and Diligence in Practice,” and “Enhanced Patient Engagement and Adherence”—that collectively form a sequential process of change. Together, these interconnected categories converge into what participants powerfully characterized as a profound “Life Transformation.” The following sections elaborate on this process and describe each category in detail.

Improved psychological well-being

The process was initialized by a significant improvement in caregivers’ psychological well-being, rooted in a newfound “sense of safety and support” that generated “positive emotions” such as hopeful, reassured, and happy outlooks.

Sense of safety and support

Participants consistently emphasized that the existence of the center and its structured follow-up services fostered a strong sense of security. They described feeling less isolated in their caregiving role, reassured by the knowledge that professional support was readily available. Regular calls from the unit’s nurse were perceived as a form of ongoing supervision and encouragement, signaling to caregivers that both they and their patients were not abandoned.

As one caregiver reflected:

After discharge, Ms. … called me several times. At first, I wondered why they called so often, but my sister reminded me of the difficult times and how alone I had felt. She said if any problem arises, they will help. The calls are a source of encouragement—I feel my patient hasn’t been abandoned and that they are still following up. (Participant 13)

Others described the calls as a tangible source of strength and reassurance:

When the unit calls and asks how I am, I feel I’m not alone. It’s as if ten others are standing behind me, ready to help. I am very satisfied. (Participant 2)

The unit was also viewed as a reliable resource in times of conflict or crisis, providing both mediation and practical solutions:

Their support has been invaluable—I am an elderly woman living alone, and my patient doesn’t listen to me but cooperates with the unit. Whenever we disagree, the unit’s mediation helps us resolve the issue. (Participant 5)

Several caregivers highlighted instances where timely intervention from the center prevented escalation and promoted relational healing:

Initially, my patient was resentful toward his father for hospitalizing him, fearing it would affect his marriage prospects… After a 30-minute phone session with the follow-up unit, my patient’s perspective changed significantly. She spoke kindly to his father and me, and the unit provided solutions that greatly improved their relationship. This left a lasting impression on me, and I believe such efforts deserve recognition for preventing a crisis. (Participant 9)

Practical support in navigating the health system was another dimension of this sense of security:

Several times, we had difficulties booking appointments, and they guided us through the process—once even booking it for us. Another time, we couldn’t find a specific medication and had no access to the doctor. They informed the doctor, who prescribed an alternative. (Participant 11)

Many participants described voluntarily visiting the unit during hospital appointments to seek guidance, indicating their trust and sense of belonging to the service:

Every time we come for a medical appointment, we also visit the nurse to discuss my patient’s issues and receive guidance. We are very satisfied—may God be pleased with you. (Participant 2)

Experience of positive emotions

In addition to providing security, the center’s services generated positive emotions among caregivers, including happiness, satisfaction, hope, and reassurance. Caregivers repeatedly highlighted that the follow-up calls brought them peace of mind and a sense of being valued.

The follow-up unit’s calls brought us peace and were very helpful. Knowing there’s a place that cares about us and provides support gave positive energy to me, my family, and my child. We are very satisfied. (Participant 10)

The support also brought emotional relief to exhausted family members:

When they call, my family becomes very happy that someone is following up on my child. If we hadn’t come here, my child would have been lost… Previously, his mother was so exhausted she said her dignity was gone and wished for death, but now she’s doing well. (Participant 3)

Caregivers described the staff as consistently welcoming and responsive, which deepened their appreciation and trust:

Fortunately, they always welcome us with open arms. Despite having demanding jobs that require considerable mental effort, they always respond with patience and follow up diligently. We are very satisfied with them – may God reward them. (Participant 7)

This consistent support instilled hope and strengthened caregivers’ resilience:

Every time they call me, it creates hope that we are being cared for and gives us a sense of being supported. (Participant 6)

Educational–attitudinal transformation

With this new psychological foundation in place, caregivers were prepared to learn and became receptive to the program’s education. Their participation in the sessions then prompted a deep shift in their attitudes—from a place of blame to one of understanding and empathy. Caregivers stressed that the knowledge they acquired did more than teach skills; it fundamentally reshaped how they saw their situation.

Knowledge acquisition

Participants consistently described the unit as a pivotal source of structured and evidence-based knowledge. Engagement with psychoeducational sessions and specialist guidance expanded their understanding of psychiatric disorders, pharmacological treatment, the necessity of lifelong adherence, risks of discontinuation, and strategies for effective communication with patients.

One caregiver reflected:

The in-person classes were very effective. My spouse and I attended together. We received extensive information about the illness, medications, and how to administer them, which was extremely helpful. This changed our perspective on drug therapy. Your counseling was excellent; now he takes his medication regularly. (Participant 6)

Others described the limitations of their prior knowledge and the value of the educational sessions:

After joining this unit, I realized how incomplete my knowledge was. So, I participated in all the classes to improve my understanding. (Participant 4)

I didn’t know how to interact with my patient, and these guidelines have greatly improved our relationship. (Participant 15)

Attitudinal change

Beyond information, participants reported a profound shift in attitudes toward their relative, the illness, and their own caregiving role. They described moving from misunderstanding and conflict to empathy and acceptance, emphasizing that the illness was involuntary and required compassion rather than blame.

I realized my expectations of my spouse were unrealistic. Previously, we unintentionally experienced verbal conflicts and tension in our marital relationship because I didn’t understand him. I learned that the emergence of the illness is not the patient’s choice and is involuntary. (Participant 4)

Participants highlighted their evolving awareness of the family’s central role in sustaining recovery and the risks of rejection or stigmatization.

We came to understand that the family’s role is crucial at this stage, and we shouldn’t reject the patient but rather provide support. Fortunately, my work situation allows me to keep him with me while working. (Participant 2)

Whilewe all have high educational levels, our behavior toward the patient remainsvery poor, particularly in social settings. The perspective of both society and family toward this hospital is negative—it’s known among people as a‘madhouse.’ Unfortunately, this shames the patient. If the family’s approach iscorrect, half the treatment is accomplished. Even if doctors provide the besttreatments, one day of poor interaction with my patient can undo all thoseefforts.” (Participant 8)

Knowledge sharing and diligence in practice

This transformation in their education and attitudes was central to building their confidence. Equipped with new understanding and a more empathetic outlook, caregivers were driven to put their learning into practice through diligent care and by sharing knowledge with others. The powerful combination of emotional security and growing self-assurance enabled them to take initiative in their caregiving roles, creating positive effects that reached beyond their own homes.

Diligence in caregiving

Caregivers described becoming more conscientious and committed in their caregiving practices. The knowledge gained through the program fostered heightened vigilance regarding medication adherence, closer monitoring of symptoms, and more deliberate, thoughtful interactions with patients. Importantly, despite ongoing challenges, many participants expressed determination to attend educational sessions—sometimes even without their patient—highlighting their dedication to sustaining and expanding their caregiving capacity.

One caregiver reflected

My patient has been struggling with this illness for about ten years, and we endured considerable hardship. But this time, after discharge, his condition is stable. Every follow-up call from the unit reinforces our sense of duty—prompting us to monitor medications closely and maintain regular doctor visits. (Participant 12)

Another emphasized the role of the program inAnother relative described assuming greater responsibility for patient care

Based on what I learned here, I took responsibility for my patient’s care and advised his family, ‘You must stay vigilant and ensure a peaceful home environment. (Participant 3).

Knowledge sharing with others

Beyond transforming their own understanding, many caregivers described a strong motivation to disseminate the knowledge they had acquired. They reported explaining key concepts to relatives, correcting misconceptions when observing inappropriate behaviors, and encouraging others to engage with the unit. This outward flow of information extended caregiving support beyond the immediate family, creating ripple effects within the broader social network.

As one caregiver explained

After learning so much here, I repeatedly contacted relatives who have patients at home and reminded them of their inappropriate behaviors.(Participant 4).

Another emphasized the role of the program in enabling advocacy

The follow-up enabled me to recommendthis place to anyone in our city encountering problems. (Participant 13)

Enhanced patient engagement and adherence

This comprehensive growth in caregivers, combined with the program’s direct interventions with patients, worked in tandem to improve patient outcomes. This led to Enhanced Patient Engagement and Adherence through a dual pathway: the caregivers’ newfound empathy and skills created a more supportive home environment, while the program itself instilled a crucial sense of self-worth in patients and used follow-up calls to create a constructive “controlled recall” of hospitalization, thereby channeling anxiety into a powerful driver for adherence.

Controlled recall of hospitalization and managed anxiety

Caregivers consistently reported that the unit’s follow-up calls reminded patients of their hospitalization experiences, which often involved involuntary admission. While such reminders could have been distressing, participants described them instead as generating a constructive and manageable level of anxiety. This “controlled recall” of hospitalization functioned as a motivational driver, reinforcing the importance of treatment adherence and heightening patients’ receptiveness to the unit staff’s guidance.

As one caregiver described:

Whenever they call and ask about medication adherence, my patient takes it more seriously because they’ve been warned that stopping medication could lead to symptom worsening and rehospitalization. (Participant 12)

Another reflected:

Since our patient often refuses medication, the follow-up calls have created enough concern about rehospitalization that he now takes his medication and listens to our advice. (Participant 10)

Sense of importance

Participants also emphasized that patients felt valued and significant when contacted by the unit. The respectful and professional manner of the staff—including responsiveness, accessibility, and sustained attention—contributed to patients’ sense of dignity. This experience of being cared for not only improved emotional well-being but also strengthened adherence behaviors.

My spouse becomes very happy with the unit’s calls. During a medical visit, he even mentioned how diligently the nurse follows up with him. (Participant 13)

One participant recounted how oversight by the unit directly influenced patient behavior:

Sometimes when my patient hesitated to take medication, the unit would call and say, ‘You must take your medicines; we don’t want you readmitted. We care about you—but at home, not in the hospital.’ Immediately, my patient, who usually resisted, would take it willingly. (Participant 15)

Others observed that this sense of importance fostered gratitude and sustained motivation:

When I tell my son that the unit has called to follow up, he feels valued and important. This sense of significance has motivated him to continue his medication. (Participant 14)

Sustained Self-Care

Finally, participants noted that the structured, consistent follow-up reinforced self-care routines and created continuity in treatment management. The repeated reminders acted as external prompts, helping patients remain attentive to medication schedules, appointments, and other aspects of recovery.

When they called to ask if … had any problems or how she was doing, I would relay this to my patient. She said it felt like a reminder of things she might forget—like regular check-ups or medication. When these calls repeat every month, it’s like a train getting back on track; everything falls into routine. (Participant 3)

Another caregiver explained:

Every time they call, I inform my patient, and this helps her stay focused and attentive. (Participant 9)

Discussion

This study explored the role of a transitional care program in supporting relapse prevention among family caregivers of patients with severe mental disorders in Iran. Analysis of caregiver interviews revealed that the program catalyzed a transformative process, manifested through enhanced psychological well-being, educational and attitudinal transformation, active knowledge sharing, and increased diligence in caregiving. These interconnected changes collectively strengthened caregiver empowerment and resilience while producing multidimensional benefits for patients through improved engagement and treatment adherence, ultimately contributing to reduced caregiver burden and lower relapse rates.

The identified themes demonstrate strong alignment with key components of the Transitional Care Model (TCM) [15, 25], while the program’s profound impact can be further elucidated through Walsh’s (2003) family resilience framework [26]. This theoretical lens reveals how the intervention systematically strengthened three core resilience processes: it facilitated meaning-making and positive outlook within belief systems by transforming caregivers’ perspectives from blame to understanding; enhanced organizational patterns through creating professional support systems and extending kinship networks via knowledge sharing; and fostered communication processes through conflict mediation and promotion of emotional regulation. Functioning as a catalytic intervention, the program effectively activated these essential resilience mechanisms, thereby empowering families to fundamentally transform their experience of severe mental illness challenges. These findings also align with recent meta-analytic evidence confirming that transitional care interventions effectively reduce relapse and emergency service utilization while improving patients’ quality of life [27].

The study demonstrated that the unit’s services contributed to improvements in caregivers’ mental health by fostering a sense of security and professional support. Caregivers reported that they did not feel left to manage their patients alone, as they could rely on the unit for assistance during crises, challenges with medication access, or barriers to clinical services. Prior qualitative research in Iran identified the lack of systemic health support as a significant barrier to caregiver adaptation [18], while a systematic review highlighted unmet support needs as a central challenge for caregivers [1]. Evidence further indicates that receipt of adequate support reduces caregivers’ psychological distress [25, 28]. Consistent with these findings, most participants in our study emphasized that respectful, compassionate, and supportive interactions from nursing staff were critical to sustaining their engagement with the unit. This aligns with observations by Heru et al. (2004), who underscored the role of respectful and empathic communication in strengthening collaborative alliances with both patients and families. For optimal effectiveness, inpatient psychiatrists must therefore demonstrate competence not only in facilitating family understanding and adaptation to illness, but also in managing emotionally charged family dynamics and identifying as well as intervening in family-related problems [29].

The present results further indicated that the unit’s services significantly enhanced caregivers’ knowledge and awareness regarding psychiatric illnesses, pharmacological treatments, patient interaction strategies, and potential medication side effects. Patient and caregiver education is one of the components of the TCCM model [25]. Previous studies have identified insufficient information and lack of training as critical challenges faced by family caregivers of individuals with severe mental illness [30–32]. Indeed, inadequate preparation for the caregiving role often precipitates heightened psychological distress and impaired coping capacity [28]. Accordingly, psychoeducation and structured information provision play a central role in empowering caregivers, reducing distress, and enabling effective adaptation to the caregiving role.

A notable finding was that caregivers actively disseminated the knowledge they had acquired to other family members, relatives, and, in some cases, the broader community. This stands in contrast to earlier studies, in which caregivers frequently concealed their relative’s illness due to fear of public stigma and negative judgment, often resulting in social isolation [18]. The present findings suggest a marked attitudinal transformation: caregivers not only demonstrated reduced stigmatizing attitudes toward their own relatives but also engaged in efforts to influence broader social perceptions, thereby contributing to the reduction of discrimination against individuals with mental illness.

Another important finding of the study was that participants reported improvements in caregiving diligence as a result of the knowledge and attitudes they had acquired. This was reflected in greater sensitivity during patient interactions, closer monitoring of medication adherence, and more consistent attendance at follow-up visits. Consistent with these observations, previous studies have demonstrated that enhanced caregiver knowledge and more positive attitudes are closely associated with improved caregiving performance [33, 34].

Patient engagement is an important component of the TCM model [25], but the important point in this study is to identify the mechanism of TCM in patient engagement. An important finding of this study was that the unit’s follow-up calls served as reminders of patients’ previous involuntary hospitalization, thereby reinforcing motivation to maintain treatment adherence as a strategy to prevent rehospitalization. The stress induced by this reminder did not appear to cause psychological harm; instead, it operated as a therapeutic driver. In acute psychiatric settings, patients are frequently vulnerable to re-traumatization, and preventive interventions remain limited [35]. These findings suggest that the re-emergence of traumatic memories, rather than undermining recovery, may paradoxically facilitate stronger adherence to prescribed therapeutic regimens.

Follow-up calls were reported to strengthen patients’ perceived self-worth and reduce feelings of abandonment, thereby mirroring the supportive role of caregivers. Importantly, even among patients with limited insight into their psychiatric condition, adherence to treatment was often sustained as a means of preserving dignity and maintaining positive relational ties with nursing staff. These findings highlight the therapeutic significance of perceived validation and professional regard in fostering engagement with care, suggesting that such external reinforcement may serve as a powerful adjunct to intrinsic motivation in sustaining long-term treatment adherence.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings of this study highlight the need to strengthen transitional care programs through the integration of technology-based health solutions such as telepsychiatry and mobile applications, which can extend follow-up and support beyond hospital discharge. Developing structured caregiver education and support models is also essential for enhancing caregiver competence, reducing distress, and improving patient outcomes. Furthermore, greater investment in mental health services is required to ensure adequate staffing, training, and sustainability of transitional care programs. Finally, public education initiatives aimed at reducing stigma and promoting mental health literacy may foster stronger community support for patients and their families, ultimately contributing to lower relapse rates and improved quality of care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. In addition, cultural differences and variations in national health policies across countries may further limit the transferability of the results. Second, despite assurances provided to participants that their responses would not influence the quality of care they or their relatives received, the possibility of response bias cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusion

This study highlights the pivotal role of transitional care programs in strengthening caregiver empowerment and supporting relapse prevention among patients with severe mental illness. By enhancing caregiver knowledge, fostering attitudinal change, reducing psychological distress, and facilitating supportive interactions with health professionals, the program contributed to reduced caregiver burden and improved patient outcomes. Importantly, the findings underscore the value of psychoeducation, continuity of care, and respectful communication in shaping positive caregiving experiences and sustaining treatment adherence.
