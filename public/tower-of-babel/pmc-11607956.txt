Relationship between IgM deposition in the mesangial region and the prognosis of IgA nephropathy: a single-centre retrospective study
Authors: Li Gao, Xuan Zhang, Dongrong Yu, Mengjie Jiang, Can Yu, Xiaohong Li, Qin Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the prognostic value of mesangial IgM deposition in a long-term follow-up cohort of patients with immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN).MethodsA retrospective analysis of clinicopathological data from 774 patients with primary IgAN at Hangzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2018, was conducted. Patients were categorized into end-event and non-end-event groups according to whether they reached the renal composite endpoint, defined as a ≥ 50% decline in eGFR or progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Risk factors for adverse renal outcomes were evaluated via univariate and multivariate Cox regression models. Patients were further divided into three groups on the basis of IgM deposition levels in the glomerular mesangial area: IgM-negative, low (IF < 2+), and high (IF ≥ 2+). Comparative analyses of clinical and histopathological characteristics, along with treatment regimens, were performed across these groups.ResultsCompared with the IgM-negative and low-deposition groups, the high-IgM deposition group exhibited significantly lower serum albumin and eGFR levels and higher cholesterol, 24-hour urine protein, and blood immunoglobulin M levels. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified immunosuppressant use as an independent protective factor for IgAN prognosis, whereas low serum albumin, T2 lesions, and nephropathological IgM deposits were recognized as independent risk factors for the 5-year prognosis of patients with IgAN. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves revealed that patients with high IgM deposition had markedly poorer prognoses than those with negative or low IgM deposition.ConclusionIn addition to low serum albumin and T2 lesions, IgM deposition in the mesangial region has emerged as an independent risk factor for the 5-year prognosis of patients with IgAN.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) represents the most prevalent primary glomerular disease and is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Typically, immunofluorescence reveals IgA deposition in the mesangial region, occasionally extending to capillary loops, often accompanied by C3 deposition but rarely C1q deposition. IgG and IgM deposits may also be present, mirroring the distribution of IgA but with reduced intensity [1]. Managing IgAN is challenging because of its diverse clinical and pathological manifestations and the variability in therapeutic responses and prognoses [2]. Research has identified several risk factors for poor renal outcomes in patients with IgAN [3, 4], such as hypertension, a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and proteinuria. The Oxford typing score (MEST-C) has recently been validated as a tool for assessing pathological lesions in patients with IgAN [5] and is strongly correlated with clinical presentation and prognosis. Moreover, emerging evidence highlights the significant role of humoral immune activation in the inflammatory cascade and tissue damage associated with IgAN [6, 7]. IgM deposition, IgG deposition, and C3 deposition in renal tissue are associated with poor prognosis in patients with IgAN [8, 9]. For example, IgM deposition can lead to more severe segmental glomerulosclerosis and higher urinary protein levels [7]. As the intensity of C3 deposition increases, patients develop more haematuria, crescents, more severe interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration and higher segmental sclerotic lesion scores [10]. This study retrospectively examined clinical and renal tissue humoral immune deposition data from patients with IgAN at Hangzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2016–2018), with the aim of exploring the relationships between new pathological factors, such as IgM deposition, and the prognosis of IgAN patients, as well as providing clinical guidance.

Object and method

Research subjects

Patients who were diagnosed with IgAN at Hangzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2018, were included in this study (n = 894). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) complete case data; (2) a follow-up duration of at least 6 months; and (3) between 18 and 75 years of age. The exclusion criteria included (1) lupus nephritis, diabetic nephropathy or other kidney diseases; (2) secondary IgAN, such as IgA vasculitis or hepatitis B virus-associated nephritis; (3) acute kidney injury; (4) ESRD at the time of biopsy; (5) follow-up of less than 6 months or no composite endpoint event within 6 months; and (6) missing baseline data. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine and exempted participants from signing informed consent (ethics number: 2021KY045).

Clinical and laboratory data

Clinical and laboratory data at the time of renal biopsy were extracted from the hospital’s medical record system and included biopsy date, sex, age, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), body mass index (BMI), haemoglobin (HB), serum albumin (Alb), cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), blood uric acid (UA), serum creatinine (Scr), 24-hour urine total protein (24 h-UTP), microscopic examination of red blood cells, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and serum humoral immune indicators (IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, and C4). Hypertension was defined as three measurements of SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg on different dates and/or a history of hypertension [11]. The eGFR was calculated via the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation, with all indicators assessed according to traditional cut-off values per the KDIGO guidelines [12].

Histopathological data

Histopathological data were obtained from the Department of Nephrology at Hangzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Kidney biopsy tissues were subjected to routine staining procedures, including HE, PAS, silver hexamide, and Masson’s staining. Direct immunofluorescence was employed to detect the expression intensity and localization of IgA, IgG, IgM, C3, C4, and C1q. Pathological indicators, including the use of the Oxford IgA scale (MEST-C score) to assess tissue lesions, were scored for each patient [4]. All biopsy samples were reviewed by two renal pathologists who were blinded to the clinical data. The anti-IgM antibody used was a polyclonal rabbit anti-human secondary antibody (F0203) from Agjlent Dako (Copenhagen, Denmark) diluted 1:50. Immunofluorescence sections were scored from 0 to 4+: (-) not visible at any magnification; (±) faintly visible under high power; (+) visible under high power and faintly visible under low power; (++) clearly visible under high power and visible under low power; (+++) dazzling under high power and clearly visible under low power; and (++++) dazzling at both high and low power. Patients were categorized into three groups on the basis of IgM deposition in the glomerular mesangial area: IgM-negative, low (IF < 2+), and high (IF ≥ 2+). Clinical and histopathological characteristics, as well as treatments, were compared among these groups [8].

Treatment, endpoints, and follow-up

The study documented the use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and renin‒angiotensin system (RAS) blockers throughout the disease course. A composite endpoint was defined as either end-stage renal disease (eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or dialysis) or a permanent eGFR decline of ≥ 50% [13]. At each follow-up visit within 5 years of renal biopsy, data on serum creatinine (SCr), eGFR, total follow-up duration, and the occurrence of the renal composite endpoint were recorded. In cases where this information was missing, telephone inquiries were made.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), and graphs were created with GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the measurement data, whereas the Levene test was used to evaluate variance homogeneity. Normally distributed data are reported as the means ± standard _xdeviations (± s) and were compared via t tests. Nonnormally distributed data are presented as M (P25, P75), with comparisons made via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages), with group comparisons performed via the X2 test or Fisher’s exact test and rank data comparisons performed via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified risk factors for complex outcomes in patients with IgAN. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to analyse cumulative renal survival. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all two-sided tests.

Flowchart of research subject grouping

Clinical and pathological indices and treatment status among patients with IgAN over 5 years

The enrolment process is detailed in Fig. 1. A total of 774 patients with primary IgAN were ultimately included, comprising 306 males (39.5%) and 468 females (60.5%). Among these patients, 56 patients (7.2%) reached the renal composite endpoint. The patients were categorized into endpoint and non-endpoint groups on the basis of the occurrence of endpoint events (Table 1). The median follow-up duration was 48 months for the non-endpoint group and 32 months for the endpoint group. The endpoint group was older, had a greater proportion of males, and presented with significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure; cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein, blood uric acid, serum creatinine, 24-hour urine protein, and complement C4 levels; and lower haemoglobin, serum albumin, and eGFR levels. The non-endpoint group exhibited more severe capillary cell proliferation, whereas the prevalence of segmental sclerosis, renal tubular atrophy or interstitial fibrosis, and crescentic lesions was higher in the endpoint group. In terms of renal pathological immunofluorescence, the endpoint group demonstrated more pronounced IgM and C1q deposition, whereas the non-endpoint group had more severe IgA and C3 deposition. The comparison of therapeutic use in Table 1 shows that more glucocorticoids were used in the endpoint group.

Clinical and pathological indicators and treatment of different deposition intensities of nephropathological IgM in patients with IgAN

Patients were classified into three categories on the basis of the extent of IgM deposition in the glomerular mesangial area (refer to Fig. 2): the IgM-negative group, the low-deposition group (IF < 2+), and the high-deposition group (IF ≥ 2+). The clinical and histopathological characteristics and treatment details of these groups were compared (see Table 2). Compared with the IgM-negative and low-deposition groups, the high-IgM deposition group exhibited significantly lower serum ALB and eGFR levels and higher cholesterol, 24-hour urine protein, and blood immunoglobulin M levels. In terms of histopathology, the high deposition group exhibited more pronounced S-lesions and C3 and C1q deposition than the other groups did.

Patient survival curve analysis

Among the 774 patients, 718 had a favourable prognosis, whereas 56 reached the composite renal endpoint. The Kaplan‒Meier survival curves revealed an overall adverse prognostic rate of 7.2% for IgAN patients at 5 years (Fig. 3A). Among the 774 patients, the renal survival rates were 98.2% at 10 months, 97.4% at 20 months, 96.6% at 30 months, 95.6% at 40 months, 94.7% at 50 months, and 92.8% at 60 months. The Kaplan‒Meier curves further revealed that patients with high IgM deposition had a significantly worse prognosis than did those with negative and low IgM deposition (log-rank test P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B).

Cox regression analysis results

Univariate Cox analysis revealed several renal factors that influenced the composite endpoint, including age; blood pressure (systolic and diastolic); haemoglobin, serum albumin, cholesterol, triglyceride, LDL, blood uric acid, and serum creatinine levels; 24 h UTP; eGFR; blood IgG levels; renal pathology; complement C3, C4, and C1q deposition; E and T lesions; IgM deposition; and the use of glucocorticosteroids and immunosuppressants (Table 3). Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis, incorporating significant variables from the univariate analysis via stepwise selection, demonstrated that immunosuppressant use (HR = 0.436, 95% CI 0.222–0.856, P = 0.016) served as an independent protective factor for IgAN prognosis. Conversely, low serum ALB levels (HR = 0.918, 95% CI 0.846–0.996, P = 0.039), T2 lesions (HR = 12.853, 95% CI 3.185–51.864, P < 0.001), and nephropathological IgM deposits (HR = 1.442, 95% CI 1.011–2.057, P = 0.043) were identified as independent risk factors for the 5-year prognosis of IgAN patients.

Discussion

We analysed the clinical and pathological parameters of 774 patients with primary IgAN and found that the use of immunosuppressive agents improved the 5-year prognosis. In addition to low serum ALB levels and T2 lesions, which are known independent risk factors, glomerular mesangial IgM deposition was identified as an independent risk factor for the 5-year prognosis of IgAN patients.

In this study, approximately 7.2% of patients progressed to ESRD within 5 years, which aligns with the long-term follow-up results reported by Chang et al. [14], who reported a composite endpoint occurrence in 27 out of 330 patients (8.2%) over a median follow-up period of 47.4 months. Given that 30–40% of patients with IgAN may progress to ESRD within 10–25 years, the 5-year observation period may seem limited. However, the current international IgAN prognostic assessment tools aim to identify high-risk patients as early as possible, with a 5-year prediction window and an AUC of 0.9. Consequently, this study adopted a 5-year follow-up for composite endpoint events to promptly identify prognostic risk factors and facilitate early intervention to improve patient outcomes. In the present study, the proportion of male patients was approximately 39.5%, which is inconsistent with previous findings on the higher prevalence of males among IgAN patients [1]. However, in two other studies on the prognostic factors of IgAN in China [1, 3], the proportions of male patients were 42.2% and 43.0%, which is consistent with our findings and may be due to regional differences.

Recent intensive studies on IgAN have highlighted several emerging risk factors. Saleem et al. [5] conducted a retrospective analysis of 93 biopsy-confirmed primary IgAN cases, identifying T lesions as an independent risk factor for reaching the composite endpoint, both in univariate and multivariate analyses. Previous clinical research has consistently linked low serum ALB levels with poor renal prognosis [15–17]. Hypoalbuminaemia may indicate malnutrition, liver disease, chronic inflammation, or severe proteinuria [16], although the precise mechanism underlying its correlation with IgAN prognosis requires further investigation. Moriyama et al. [4] demonstrated that patients with E1, S1, or C1 lesions who received immunosuppressive therapy had markedly improved prognoses compared with those who did not. In this study, the Cox proportional hazards regression model identified T2 lesions as an independent risk factor for IgAN prognosis, whereas immunosuppressant use emerged as a protective factor. Lower serum ALB levels were also independently associated with poorer outcomes, which aligns with findings from Song et al. [16, 17]. The study also revealed that hormone therapy did not offer significant prognostic benefits, a finding that is consistent with the findings of the STOP-IgAN study [18] but contrasts with those of the TESTING study [19]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in patient risk profiles; for example, this study’s cohort had a mean baseline proteinuria of 0.9 g/d and an eGFR of 88.9 mL/(min· 1.73 m2), whereas patients with STOP-IgAN had a mean baseline proteinuria of 1.8 g/d and an eGFR of 57.4 mL/(min· 1.73 m2), and the TESTING study cohort had a mean proteinuria of 2.46 g/d and an eGFR of 61.5 mL/(min· 1.73 m2). This suggests that the mild disease severity in this study may have limited the observed efficacy of hormone treatments, but such therapies may significantly mitigate renal failure risk in high-risk IgAN individuals with substantial proteinuria and rapid progression. Notably, C1q deposition is usually not measured in IgAN renal biopsies, although C1q has also been reported to be an independent risk factor for IgAN prognosis [8]. In the present study, only approximately 19.6% of patients had trace-positive C1q deposits (IF ≤++), and the Cox regression model suggested that C1q was not an independent risk factor for IgAN prognosis.

In IgAN renal pathology, IgA staining typically correlates with C3 deposition and often occurs alongside IgG and/or IgM deposits. While extensive research has established a correlation between C3 and IgG deposits and patient prognosis, studies linking mesangial IgM deposits with morphological and clinical features of the Oxford classification remain limited [20, 21]. In this study, 87.9% of patients exhibited positive IgM deposits, whereas 12.1% exhibited no deposits, indicating a greater prevalence of IgM deposition in IgAN patients. A study by Stefan et al. [22] reported that the percentage of IgM deposition in the IgAN population was 70%. This finding is similar to our findings. On the basis of the prevalence of IgM in IgA nephropathy, it is necessary to further investigate whether it is pathogenic in IgAN and whether it has an impact on the final prognosis of the disease. Some previous reports have suggested that IgM deposition is not significantly correlated with the prognosis of IgAN [9, 22, 23]. However, other reports have suggested that it has an impact on prognosis, with higher levels of IgM deposition leading to poorer outcomes [24]. In our study, we found that patients with high levels of IgM deposition had lower serum ALB, higher 24-hour urine protein quantification, and high-grade S lesions. All of these factors have been shown to be detrimental to IgAN prognosis [15, 16, 25]. Therefore, there is strong interest in determining whether IgM deposition and the intensity of its deposition are more important factors associated with IgAN prognosis. Next, the KM survival curves in our study suggested that high IgM deposition resulted in lower 5-year renal survival than low IgM deposition and no IgM deposition. Although the proportion of endpoint events was slightly greater in the low IgM deposition group than in the IgM deposition-negative group over a short period of time, there was no statistically significant difference in prognosis between the two groups. The proportion of people with low IgM deposition who reached the endpoint event began to increase with time, and at 55 months, the proportion of people with low IgM deposition who reached the endpoint event exceeded that of the IgM deposition-negative group. These findings further suggest that IgM deposition, especially increased IgM deposition, leads to worse outcomes in patients with IgAN. This finding is in agreement with the study by Hao et al. [13]. However, only 21.8% of the IgAN patients in that study had IgM deposition, which differs from the results of the present study, and this difference may be caused by the large difference in sample size between the two studies.

To further explore the relationship between the intensity of IgM deposition and the prognosis of IgAN patients, Cox regression analysis was performed. The results demonstrated that IgM deposition is an independent risk factor for IgAN prognosis. The HR value of IgM deposition suggested that greater IgM deposition leads to poorer IgAN outcomes. A previous study [26] revealed that IgAN patients with IgM deposition exhibit more pronounced chronic lesions, such as cluster adhesions, old glomerular sclerosis, and segmental sclerosis. Some researchers suggest that this association may arise from the nonspecial accumulation of IgM in sclerotic areas due to its size and role in tissue repair [22]. Nevertheless, other findings suggest that IgM deposits may play a pathogenetic role in driving disease progression but are not simply associated or secondary phenomena caused by glomerulosclerosis. A study involving 30 patients with primary IgAN revealed a notable increase in IgM deposition at the second renal biopsy (from 11/30 to 21/30 patients), whereas complement deposits such as IgA and IgG remained unchanged [27]. This secondary IgM ‘deep deposition’, rather than mere ‘co-deposition’ with C3 or IgG, might drive IgAN progression. These findings, combined with the results of the present study, strongly demonstrate that IgM deposition in the glomerular mesangial region is associated with more severe chronic pathological changes in the kidney. However, these relationships do not necessarily reflect a complete causal relationship. It is not clear whether IgM deposition is the cause of injury to the kidneys or whether fluctuations in secondary IgM deposition levels occur because of renal pathology. However, one point is unequivocal in our study: glomerular mesangial IgM deposition was associated with the prognosis of IgAN patients. When the causal relationship is clarified, novel drugs for targeted treatment of IgAN may be developed.

On the basis of the above results, several studies have investigated the specific mechanisms by which IgM deposits affect IgAN progression. IgM is known to activate the lectin pathway during ischaemia/reperfusion injuries in various organs, including the kidney, by binding to antigens and exposing carbohydrate patterns that activate mannose-binding lectin (MBL), leading to further complement activation [28]. Recent findings by Heybeli et al. [26, 29] highlight that activation of C4d in the lectin pathway is an independent risk factor for progression to ESKD in patients with IgAN. The study also revealed a correlation between renal IgM deposition and C4d staining, suggesting that IgM might activate both the classical and lectin complement pathways, influencing IgAN progression. Lloyd et al. [30] proposed that the deposition of immune complexes, such as IgM, in the glomerular mesangial area might be linked to subclinical infections and that reducing IgM deposits could slow IgAN progression. However, the current understanding of the mechanisms by which IgM deposits affect IgAN progression is not yet conclusive.

Previous studies have shown that the phenomenon of IgM deposition is not unique to IgAN but also occurs in chronic kidney diseases such as focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and lupus nephritis (LN). Zhang et al. [31] reported that colocalization of IgM and C3 in the glomeruli of patients with primary FSGS was independently correlated with poorer therapeutic response and renal outcome, and Wang et al. [24] reported that glomerular deposition of IgM is involved in complement activation and renal injury in patients with LN. Glomerular IgM deposition was also found to be an independent risk factor for reduced renal function in patients with idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN) [32]. These findings highlight the important role of IgM deposition in a number of glomerular diseases and suggest that there may be common mechanistic pathways between IgAN and FSGS and between LN and IMN that warrant further investigation.

However, this study has several limitations. (1) As it is retrospective and observational, our study may not fully account for all residual confounders. (2) The single-centre nature of the study may restrict the generalizability of the findings to the broader IgAN population. (3) The follow-up period was relatively short, potentially affecting the assessment of long-term prognosis for patients with IgAN. (4) This study assessed only the correlation between IgM deposition in the mesangial area and IgAN prognosis without exploring the underlying mechanisms in depth. Future studies will address these limitations by extending the follow-up period, incorporating more factors and including multiple centers. Additionally, further studies should explore the pathological significance of IgM deposition in IgAN.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study suggests that the use of immunosuppressive agents may improve the 5-year prognosis of IgAN patients. Low serum ALB and T2 lesions could be used as independent risk factors for IgAN. Importantly, we found that glomerular mesangial IgM deposition was associated with the prognosis of IgAN patients. The prognosis of the high IgM deposition group was significantly worse than that of the IgM-negative and low IgM deposition groups. Glomerular mesangial IgM deposition could be a significant independent risk factor for IgAN prognosis. These findings may provide evidence for establishing a reasonable and targeted treatment plan, which is highly important for improving the prognosis of patients with IgAN.
