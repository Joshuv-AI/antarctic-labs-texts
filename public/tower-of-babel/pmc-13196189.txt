TrichoBase: a standardized genomic resource for comparative genomics of Trichoderma species
Authors: Alsayed Alfiky, Said A. Dora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ecological versatility of the fungal genus Trichoderma spans roles from mycoparasite to saprotroph, presenting a compelling model to investigate how genomic variation underpins niche adaptation. However, comparative genomic studies of Trichoderma are hampered by inconsistent annotation methods across independently sequenced genomes, complicating direct cross-species analysis. To address this, we developed and applied a unified, evidence-based annotation pipeline integrating RNA-seq and homology data to six key species spanning the genus’s ecological range (T. asperellum, T. harzianum, T. virens, T. reesei, T. longibrachiatum, and T. citrinoviride). This work presents ‘TrichoBase’, a consistently annotated genomic resource comprising standardized gene models, functional annotations, and comparative profiles. Using this comparable dataset, we confirm and quantitatively refine the correlation between transposable element (TE) accumulation and genome size expansion. We further demonstrate that TE-rich genomes of mycoparasitic strains are enriched in carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) and biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), while saprotrophic opportunistic lineages show streamlined profiles. Notably, the resource reveals patchy phylogenetic distributions of specific BGC families, such as sorbicillinoid and brefeldin A-like clusters. We provide the complete resource including annotation files, analysis scripts, and a reproducible pipeline to serve as a foundational dataset for future functional genomics and ecological studies in Trichoderma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12863-026-01429-3.

Introduction

The fungal genus Trichoderma (Hypocreales, Ascomycota) includes species of significant ecological and economic importance. Members of this genus perform a wide spectrum of ecological functions such as effective biocontrol agents against plant pathogens, prolific producers of high-value industrial enzymes, plant growth promoters, and, occasionally, opportunistic pathogens in humans, cereal crops and mushrooms [1–4]. Their ecological versatility is reflected in a diverse metabolome, including a rich arsenal of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) for biomass degradation, and biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) for secondary metabolites [5–7]. Consequently, Trichoderma has become a model system for studying the genomic basis of fungal ecological diversification.

Comparative genomic studies over the past decade have established foundational principles linking genome content to lifestyle in this genus. Landmark analyses have identified a stable core genome, and revealed that key adaptive traits such as mycoparasitism are associated with expansions in gene families encoding CAZymes, peptidases, and secondary metabolism pathways [8–11]. Furthermore, the dynamic roles of horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and chromosomal rearrangements in shaping genomic diversity has been highlighted as key influencing factors enabling ecological adaptation [12, 13]. While previous genomic studies have provided extensive insights into Trichoderma evolution [8, 11, 14–17], the field now faces a significant challenge common to broader comparative fungal genomics: the rapid accumulation of sequence data often outpaces our ability to ensure high-quality, uniform functional annotation [18, 19].

Despite the wealth of data, species comparisons are often complicated by inconsistent annotation methods, varying data types, and uneven genome quality. While numerous Trichoderma genomes have been deposited in public repositories (NCBI, JGI/MycoCosm), they were annotated using diverse pipelines, software versions, and evidence sources. This heterogeneity confounds direct cross-species comparisons, as observed differences may reflect methodological artifacts rather than true biological variation. Such discrepancies among analysis pipelines are a recognized hurdle in fungal ecology, necessitating the adoption of methodological standards to ensure that biological insights are not obscured by technical noise [20]. Furthermore, as recently reviewed, the integration of high-quality genomic data is essential to bridge the gap between sequence information and the biochemical mechanisms of plant-fungal symbiosis [21]. Consequently, the lack of a standardized framework introduces a significant constraint, as software versions and pipeline choices can confound the distinction between genuine biological innovation and methodological artifacts [22], limiting the resolution at which we can trace the genomic mechanisms of niche adaptation. Recently, large-scale pan-genome and phenogenomic studies have further underscored the genomic plasticity of Trichoderma, revealing an open pan-genome and linking accessory gene content to ecological adaptation [23, 24]. Our work complements these efforts by providing a consistently annotated genomic resource that enables direct, high-resolution comparative analyses and enhances the reproducibility of future investigations into Trichoderma ecology and evolution.

TrichoBase, as a unified framework, leverages the growing body of public genomic data to overcome previous limitations by implementing a unified, evidence-based annotation pipeline. We applied this consistent methodology to six Trichoderma species spanning the genus’s phylogenetic and ecological diversity: the mycoparasitic biocontrol agents T. asperellum, T. harzianum, and T. virens; the industrial cellulase producer T. reesei; and the saprotrophic opportunists T. longibrachiatum and T. citrinoviride. While acknowledging that ecological roles in Trichoderma are context-dependent and often overlap, we selected species representative of predominant lifestyles described in the literature. The primary output of this work is a consistently annotated genomic resource for Trichoderma that enables high-resolution comparative analysis. Using this resource, we sought to demonstrate its utility by addressing three key questions: (1) To what extent does a standardized analysis refine the quantitative relationship between TE-mediated genome plasticity and gene content? (2) What patterns of gene family evolution are revealed by phylogenomic reconstruction of this comparable dataset? (3) Can consistent annotation clarify the phylogenetic distribution of specific metabolic pathways? By providing this foundational dataset and addressing these questions, our study aims to establish a robust framework for comparative analysis and facilitate future research in Trichoderma genomics and ecology.

Methods

Genome acquisition and quality assessment

Genomes for six Trichoderma species (T. asperellum, T. citrinoviride, T. harzianum, T. longibrachiatum, T. reesei, and T. virens) were downloaded from NCBI (Table S1). Selected assemblies were verified for genome completeness and contiguity using BUSCO v5.5.0 with the fungi_odb10 dataset (758 orthologs) in genome mode.

Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) calculation

Whole-genome Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was calculated to provide a quantitative measure of genomic similarity and confirm species-level identity of the selected strains, complementing gene-tree-based phylogeny. Pairwise ANI values were computed using FastANI v1.33 with default parameters. The resulting similarity matrix was used to generate a heatmap with hierarchical clustering (average linkage) for visualization.

Identification and masking of repetitive elements

To identify and characterize repetitive sequences, we employed a two-stage pipeline of de novo repeat discovery; species-specific repeat libraries were generated for each genome using RepeatModeler v2.0.7 [25], and individual libraries were concatenated and processed with CD-HIT-EST v4.8.1 [26] at a 90% sequence identity threshold to create a unified, non-redundant repeat library. Genome-wide annotation and soft-masking of repetitive elements were performed using RepeatMasker v4.1.9 and the resulting soft-masked genomes were used for all subsequent gene prediction analyses.

Transcriptomic evidence for annotation

Publicly available RNA-seq datasets were downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA, accessed August 2025) to support gene model prediction. For each species, datasets representing specific physiological conditions (e.g., carbon source utilization, fungal interaction) were selected (Table S2). Reads were quality-checked with FastQC v0.12.1 and aligned to their respective soft-masked genomes using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [27] to generate transcript models. Alignment rates exceeded 90% for most species; however, substantially lower rates were observed for species with strain-unmatched genome assemblies and RNA-Seq data (T. harzianum, ~ 46%; T. citrinoviride, ~ 82%), which correlated with known strain-level divergence (Table S3). Importantly, downstream validation indicated that predicted gene models were not negatively impacted by these lower alignment rates. Alignments were processed with SAMtools v1.13, and condition-specific transcript assemblies were generated and merged using StringTie v2.2.1. The merged transcriptomes were validated using gffread v0.12.7 and gffcompare v0.11.2 [28]. The merged transcriptomes were provided as evidence for genome annotation.

Genome annotation

Genome annotations were generated using Funannotate v1.8.17. Each soft-masked genome was aligned to its species-specific RNA-seq reads using minimap2 v2.28-r1209. Merged multi-sample transcriptome assemblies (GTF files) were converted to nucleotide sequences (FASTA format) and used to improve gene predictions accuracy. For homology evidence, a high-confidence, non-redundant pan-Trichoderma protein set was constructed (see below) and searched against the genomes using DIAMOND v2.1.10, with alignments refined using Exonerate v2.4.0. For ab initio prediction, we employed AUGUSTUS v3.5.0 (using the Fusarium graminearum model), GeneMark-ES v4.71 (self-trained), and GlimmerHMM v3.0.4 (trained on BUSCO-predicted models). EvidenceModeler v2.1.0 integrated these sources, assigning double weight to the high-quality AUGUSTUS models, and the resulting consensus models were filtered to remove short proteins (< 50 amino acids) and TE-related genes. Transfer RNAs were identified using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.12.

Curated protein evidence set

Protein sequences for five species were retrieved from NCBI RefSeq (accessed August 5, 2025). For T. longibrachiatum, not available; therefore, sequences were sourced from the NCBI general Protein database. All datasets were filtered using SeqKit v2.3.1 and CD-HIT v4.8.1 [29] to remove fragments, proteins < 100 amino acids, and redundancies (99% identity) to retain unique representatives (Table S4). The T. longibrachiatum set was further validated via BLASTP against a custom database constructed from the NCBI filtered RefSeq proteins (E-value ≤1e − 5), retaining only 37 high-confidence sequences, which was deemed insufficient for species-specific protein evidence strategy, necessitating the pooled evidence approach to ensure consistent annotation quality across species. Filtered sequences from all six species were pooled and deduplicated at 99% identity, producing a final pan-Trichoderma evidence set of 53,542 sequences (Table S5), which was used as homology-based evidence in the Funannotate pipeline.

Validation of predicted gene models

Predicted gene models were validated through a multi-tiered approach. First, structural metrics such as mean CDS length, mean protein length, and the proportion of single- versus multi-exon genes, were calculated from the final species-specific GFF3 and protein FASTA files using gffread v0.12.7 and seqkit v2.3.0. Predicted models’ integrity was examined by verifying the presence of canonical start (ATG) and stop codons (TAA, TAG, TGA). Functional support was assessed from two sources: (1) Transcriptomic evidence, determined by intersecting exon coordinates with RNA-seq alignments using BEDTools v2.31.1 [30]; and (2) Protein homology, assessed by querying predicted proteins per species against the curated pan-Trichoderma dataset using DIAMOND and BLASTp (E-value < 1e − 10), where proteins with ≥ 1 significant match were considered supported. Finally, annotation completeness was benchmarked with BUSCO v5.5.0 (protein mode) against the sordariomycetes_odb10 dataset.

Functional annotation of CAZymes

CAZyme annotation was performed using a high-stringency consensus approach based on the dbCAN2 standalone pipeline with locally downloaded databases [31, 32]. Proteomes were searched against the local CAZyDB using DIAMOND (E-value < 1e-11, identity > 30%, query coverage > 70%). Resulting hits were further validated against the dbCAN HMM database v13 (released August 2024, based on CAZyDB July 9, 2024) [31] using HMMER v3.4 (domain E-value < 1e-15). Only proteins supported by both homology and HMM-based methods were retained as high-confidence CAZymes. Functional assignments were further confirmed via InterProScan v5.75-106.0, where a CAZyme was considered validated if it contained at least one carbohydrate metabolism-related Pfam or CDD domain [33]. The validation rate was calculated as the proportion of CAZymes with InterProScan support.

Identification and analysis of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)

Clusters were predicted using antiSMASH v8.0.2 with the --taxon fungi, --cb-general, and --cc-mibig flags [34]. Protein sequences from predicted BGCs were extracted and queried against the MIBiG database v3.1 using BLASTP (E-value cutoff, 1e-5). Results were filtered to retain only high-confidence homologs, defined by stringent criteria: ≥30% amino acid identity, E-value ≤ 1e − 10, and alignment length ≥ 100 aa.

BGC protein sets were further validated by analyzing their domain architecture against the Pfam-A v36.0 database predictions as they were analyzed against the Pfam-A (v36.0) database of hidden Markov models (HMMs) [35] using hmmscan from HMMER v3.4 (E-value < 1e − 5).

Hybrid clusters were defined as clusters containing both PKS and NRPS domains, either within a single protein or across multiple proteins in the genomic region.

Functional annotation with COG categories

General protein functional annotation was performed on the predicted proteome of each strain using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.13 and DIAMOND-based search against the eggNOG DB v5.0.2 [36], applying a seed ortholog e-value threshold of 1e-05. Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories were assigned based on the resulting orthology predictions.

Gene family identification with OrthoFinder

Orthologous groups were identified from the predicted proteomes of the six focal Trichoderma species, six additional NCBI-sourced Trichoderma proteomes, and two outgroups (Neurospora crassa and Fusarium graminearum) (Table S14) using OrthoFinder v3.1.0 [37]. The species tree was constructed from orthogroups inferred from the 14 fungal proteomes, using the Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA) concatenation method based on the STAG algorithm and rooted using STRIDE. The analysis was run with -M msa option and -S diamond for sequence search. This expanded phylogenetic context enabled robust inference of Trichoderma-specific gene family evolution. The resulting phylogeny was visualized and edited using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v7 [38].

Analysis of gene family dynamics with CAFÉ

Gene families exhibiting significant expansion or contraction were identified using CAFE5 (Computational Analysis of Gene Family Evolution) [39] which used OrthoFinder-derived orthogroup counts and the rooted species tree as input, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Genes within these significant families were functionally annotated, and the enrichment of COG categories within these gene sets was evaluated to identify biological functions associated with recent evolutionary dynamics.

Phylogenetic relationships

We established the evolutionary and genomic context for the six species in our resource. A phylogeny based on orthologous genes placed them within established Trichoderma clades, with the Viride and Harzianum clades containing predominantly mycoparasitic species (T. asperellum, T. harzianum, T. virens), and the Longibrachiatum clade encompassing saprotrophic and opportunistic lineages (T. longibrachiatum, T. citrinoviride, T. reesei) (Fig. 1A). Whole-genome Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis provided a complementary, quantitative view of genome-scale similarity, with clustering patterns consistent with the phylogenetic groups (Fig. 1B). Within-clade ANI exceeded 94%, while between-clade values ranged from 79 to 86%, confirming robust phylogenetic boundaries.

Genome annotations

Consensus gene sets integrated ab initio predictions, RNA-seq alignments, and homology evidence, yielding 9,193–11,918 predicted genes per genome (Table 2). Most transcripts were multi-exonic (74–78%) with average CDS lengths of ~ 1.5 kb, producing proteins of ~ 500 amino acids. BUSCO analysis of proteomes confirmed > 96% completeness. Transcriptome comparisons showed < 5% variation in gene counts across most species, underscoring annotation reliability.

Despite similarities in gene structure features, our analysis revealed notable variations in total gene content, with protein-coding genes ranging from 8,976 (T. longibrachiatum) to 11,699 (T. virens), a difference of ~ 30%. The high levels of transcript support (73.9–87.9%) confirm that the majority of annotations were transcriptionally validated, which, together with the high protein homology support (96.5–98.0%) and structural integrity evidenced by > 97% canonical start/stop codon presence, provides evidence for the accuracy and completeness of the predicted gene models.

As an independent line of validation, RNA-seq–derived transcript models were obtained and compared to the consensus predictions where gene counts differed by < 5% in most species between the two models (e.g., T. asperellum 10,880 genes vs. 11,004 loci; T. reesei 9,462 vs. 10,260 loci). Two exceptions stood out. In T. harzianum, the transcript-derived set predicted 12,899 loci compared to 11,847 from consensus, likely reflecting strain mismatch (assembly T11_w vs. RNA-seq TR274) that reduced alignment fidelity and proportion of multi-exon transcripts (59.7% vs. >81% in other species) (Table S6). In contrast, T. virens showed fewer transcript-derived loci (9,778) vs. (11,918 protein-coding genes), despite matched datasets, which might be due to incomplete transcriptome sampling.

Transposable element dynamics and genomic divergence

Repeat element content varied among species, from 3.9 to 8.2% of genome size, with T. harzianum showing the highest burden (Fig. 2 and Table S7).

In this study, genome size correlated with repeat burden (r = 0.915, R² = 0.837, p = 0.0106), suggesting TE expansion as a major driver of genome size differences [40]. Retroelements, particularly LTR (long terminal repeat) dominated, accounting for 1.3–2.9% of genome space (Table 3). Lineage-specific repeat patterns, such as enrichment of small RNA-associated repeats in T. harzianum, highlight diverse TE dynamics, while rolling-circle transposons (Helitrons), satellites and simple repeats were not detected under the threshold and parameters selected in this study.

The distribution of proteins across COG categories (especially when normalized to proteome size) revealed a strong core functional conservation but also highlighted specific expansions linked to ecological lifestyle (Fig. 3; Table 5 and, Table S8).

Comparative architecture analysis revealed a conserved syntenic core alongside lineage-specific adaptations (Fig. S1 and Table S11). High-confidence homologs of the sorbicillinoid BGC were identified exclusively in the streamlined genomes of T. reesei, T. longibrachiatum, and T. citrinoviride, and were absent from the mycoparasitic species. Furthermore, a lucilactaene-like BGC showed a patchy phylogenetic distribution, being highly conserved in T. reesei, T. citrinoviride, and T. virens but absent from other species (Table S12).

Screening against the MIBiG database identified homologs of the verticillin BGC, an epipolythiodioxopiperazine (ETP) class mycotoxin, in five of the six Trichoderma species. The Longibrachiatum clade members (T. longibrachiatum, T. citrinoviride, and T. reesei) each possessed five related genes, while T. harzianum and T. virens contained three and one, respectively. While automated annotation failed to identify the core NRPS gene verP, manual tblastn validation using a Gliocladium sp. query confirmed its presence (Table S13). The analysis revealed significant conservation (46.8–49.3% identity) across four species, a more divergent homolog in T. virens (33.4% identity), and low identity in T. asperellum (27.1%), suggesting the latter lacks a true ortholog.

The study further uncovered unexpected biosynthetic potential, notably an NRPS–PKS hybrid in T. harzianum with 50.8% identity to SwnK, the swainsonine biosynthetic enzyme. This discovery, along with numerous hypothetical proteins showing over 90% similarity to uncharacterized MIBiG entries, highlights Trichoderma as a vast, unexplored reservoir for novel secondary metabolite discovery.

Phylogenomic reconstruction of gene family gain and loss in Trichoderma

OrthoFinder analysis of 14 proteomes delineated 10,989 orthogroups (OGs) comprising 146,687 genes (94.2% of all predicted genes), including a conserved Trichoderma core genome of 6,529 OGs. CAFE analysis identified 917 orthogroups with statistically significant (p < 0.05) expansion or contraction, with 916 of these OG families shared across all six focal Trichoderma species in this study encompassing 22,434 genes. Functional annotation of the 916 significantly changing families revealed enrichment in several categories central to ecological performance, such as genes linked to carbohydrate metabolism, secondary metabolism, and membrane transport (Fig. 4). Interestingly, 8,538 genes lacked functional annotation (COG S), representing a reservoir of uncharacterized genetic potential. To further investigate the functional repertoires of expanded families in the biocontrol species T. harzianum and T. virens, we compiled detailed annotations for the 470 orthogroups with significant expansion (p < 0.05) in either species. These annotations including gene counts, p-values, Pfam domains, signal peptide predictions, and CAZyme families, are provided in (Table S15).

Discussion

The six Trichoderma species analyzed represent the major phylogenetic clades and ecological diversity of the genus, providing a high-resolution, comparative framework that ensures methodological consistency, enabling robust cross-species comparisons and minimizing artifacts. By implementing a synchronized annotation architecture across the genus, TrichoBase effectively mitigates the ‘versioning noise’ and cross-studies inconsistencies often found across different studies, providing a standardized baseline for the high-confidence comparison of gene count and structure features, CAZyme and BGC evolution in Trichoderma. This dataset establishes a validated foundation for expanding comparative analyses across broader taxonomic sets in future work. The ecological categories suggested here (biocontrol, saprotrophic, opportunistic) provide a useful and simple classification, however, boundaries often overlap due to ecological plasticity: biocontrol species such as T. harzianum have been linked to opportunistic infections, while industrial strains like T. reesei retain moderate antagonistic activity. Opportunists such as T. longibrachiatum and T. citrinoviride can exhibit biocontrol traits under certain conditions [42]. This plasticity reflects the difficulty of assigning rigid ecological roles, as natural populations inhabit complex niches and cryptic speciation may conceal specialized functions [10]. Thus, our classifications should be interpreted as predominant tendencies rather than absolute categories.

Despite shared capabilities such as mycoparasitism, cellulolytic saprotrophy, and rhizosphere colonization, Trichoderma species diverge markedly in morphology and ecological specialization. This divergence may reflect an evolutionary trajectory from a versatile ancestor, with subsequent lineage-specific adaptations shaping distinct ecological niches. All six genomes exhibited high completeness (BUSCO ≥ 96%), providing a strong basis for comparative analyses. Whole-genome similarity analysis using ANI was highly congruent with the phylogenetic relationships inferred by OrthoFinder and confirmed taxonomic placements and clustered species into established clades [43]. For example, T. asperellum grouped with Viride clade members (T. hamatum and T. atroviride), validating whole-genome similarity as a reliable phylogenetic tool in Trichoderma.

Transposable element (TE) dynamics emerged as a major driver of genomic variation as repeat burden showed a positive relationship with genome size, with biocontrol specialists showing the highest burdens consistent with previous reports for the genus [44] yet through potentially distinct strategies. While T. harzianum accumulated LTR retrotransposons, T. virens exhibited high-density compact LTRs and the largest DNA transposon complement. These TE-rich architectures parallel patterns in other fungi where TEs drive adaptive evolution, including virulence and stress response diversification [45]. In contrast, Longibrachiatum clade species (T. longibrachiatum, T. citrinoviride) underwent extreme streamlining with suppressed TE activity, suggesting a shift toward saprotrophy and opportunism. T. reesei represents an intermediate, retaining specialized LTR repeats despite overall reductive evolution for efficient cellulose degradation [8, 46, 47]. Collectively, these patterns indicate lineage-specific repeatome diversification contributing to both the stability of conserved traits and the emergence of novel ecological strategies. Notable exception such as T. virens showing a lower repeat burden than expected for its genome size highlight the role of lineage-specific factors such as differential TE silencing or recent activity bursts in shaping the repeatome evolution [48].

Our consistent annotation pipeline revealed conservation of gene structure features such as CDS length, protein size, and exon count, reflecting evolutionary constraints (potentially also due to applying the same consistent algorithm) which is aligned with previous reports [9, 44]. Yet total gene content varied by ~ 30%, driven by quantitative shifts in gene families, a pattern previously noted across the genus [14, 17, 49]. This variation of genome architecture and how different lineages allocate metabolic resources seems closely tied to ecological diversification: A spectrum of strategies has been observed, generalist mycoparasites maintain broad gene arsenals, while streamlined saprotrophs and opportunists retain compact repertoires.

Biocontrol-associated species exhibited a powerful toolkit for microbial competition with expanded CAZyme repertoires, particularly Glycoside Hydrolases and Auxiliary Activities, supporting cell wall degradation during mycoparasitism [14]. Our standardized annotations further strengthen the comparability of such functional inferences, providing a resource that can be directly integrated with the pan-genomic framework which characterized Trichoderma’s genome as “open” indicative of high genetic plasticity and adaptive potential [23]. This is strongly supported by experimental evidence of dynamic cell wall remodeling during mycoparasitism, a process facilitated by an ‘evolutionarily expanded group of specialized enzymes’ [46]. These specialists are also enriched for BGCs producing antimicrobials and siderophores, constituting a broad-spectrum chemical arsenal [14, 49], and finally, their COG profiles confirm enrichment in carbohydrate metabolism, consistent with rhizosphere colonization and antagonism [2, 50, 51]. Consistent with this, the expanded gene families in T. harzianum and T. virens identified by CAFE5 are significantly enriched for chitinases (GH18), secreted proteases, and other carbohydrate-active enzymes involved in cell wall degradation (Table S15), reinforcing the functional importance of these gene classes in mycoparasitism. This antagonism is further supported by the significant expansion of COG Category Q (Secondary metabolites biosynthesis) in these biocontrol specialists, ranging from 6.2% to 6.9% of the proteome (Table 5). This expanded metabolic capacity likely serves as a chemical shield, providing the redox-active compounds necessary to navigate the oxidative stress encountered during aggressive mycoparasitic interactions [52]. Conversely, Longibrachiatum clade members, previously characterized as saprophytes and opportunists [53], exemplify genomic streamlining. T. longibrachiatum has the smallest genome and gene count, with extensive loss of nutrition-related families such as chitinases and glucanases, while retaining key hydrolases under purifying selection [14, 54]. Similarly, T. reesei genome represents streamlining strategy for saprotrophic efficiency [55], its CAZome was the second smallest, reflecting evolution toward a narrow, highly efficient enzyme set rather than broad diversity, a pattern arguably interpreted as limited gene-family expansion rather than extensive loss [56]. T. reesei’s reduced BGC repertoire further underscores reliance on primary decomposition rather than chemical antagonism.

This minimalist strategy, potentially facilitated by suppressed TE activity, is reflected in elevated investment in protein turnover and chaperones (COG Category O; 7.1–7.3% in streamlined species) which represents a prioritized genomic allocation for proteostasis. This machinery is essential for the rapid repair and recycling of proteins damaged by environmental oxidative pressure, allowing these species to maintain cellular homeostasis despite their reduced gene repertoires. This emphasis on protein quality control and homeostasis and aligns with principles of genome streamlining in other microbes [57]. Reduced secondary-metabolite capacity and selective retention of certain BGCs, such as the sorbicillinoid-like cluster which is linked to core biological functions such as signaling, sensing and reproductive development suggest ecological trade-offs, deprioritizing chemical warfare in favor of alternative survival strategies [58, 59]. Occasional pathogenicity in immunocompromised hosts highlights the opportunistic potential of this clade although the mechanisms enabling this remain unclear [60].

These contrasting investments in primary decomposition (CAZymes) and specialized metabolism (BGCs) reveal a clear ecological spectrum (Fig. 5). Biocontrol specialists seem to follow a trend where they occupy the high-investment end, with large genomes, expanded CAZyme and BGC repertoires, and high TE content. Meanwhile, the Longibrachiatum clade members form a low-investment cluster, consistent with genome streamlining as an adaptive mechanism to lower metabolic burden and enable rapid growth. This coordinated shift in metabolic investment, shaped by TE dynamics and gene family turnover, explains the fluid boundaries discussed earlier between species and suggests ecological divergence arises primarily from differential expansion of conserved gene families as primary mechanism, supported by acquisition of novel functions through other mechanisms, e.g., HGT.

Analysis of high-confidence MIBiG homologs provided further insight into the ecological strategies of Trichoderma. The conserved core metabolome underpins the genus’s renowned biocontrol capabilities, while the species-specific adaptations delineate distinct trajectories. For example, unique features include enniatin synthetase in T. asperellum, suggesting cytotoxic potential, and a putative gibberellin pathway indicating specialized plant interactions. Experimental validation is required to confirm these functions [61, 62]. Brefeldin A-like clusters in T. citrinoviride and T. virens represent competitive tools or virulence factors, while lucilactaene-like clusters, patchily distributed, likely confer antibacterial advantages [63]. The presence of these clusters in opportunists such as T. citrinoviride warrants investigation into their roles in virulence.

Manual validation of the VerP NRPS gene confirmed its patchy distribution across species, which correlated with verticillin-related gene counts. This is strongly supported by the recent characterization of a homologous BGC in the biocontrol fungus T. afroharzianum T22, where the VerP homolog was shown to be essential and is proposed to produce 11′-deoxyverticillin A, a potent cytotoxic epipolythiodioxopiperazine (ETP) [64]. Verticillin-like clusters produce cytotoxic epipolythiodioxopiperazines with antibiotic and nematicidal properties, supporting roles in defense and competition. The presence of a functional ETP cluster in the opportunists T. citrinoviride warrants investigation into its potential role as a virulence factor, as ETP toxins can be deleterious to eukaryotes. Similarly, the detection of a SwnK homolog in T. harzianum raises intriguing possibilities, as swainsonine is a toxic alkaloid more commonly associated with plants and entomopathogenic fungi. Its potential presence in a biocontrol agent expands the known chemical space and warrants functional validation.

CAFE analysis revealed 916 orthogroups with significant expansion or contraction, with the most pronounced shifts inferred deep in the evolutionary history of the genus. Functional enrichments in carbohydrate metabolism, secondary metabolism, and signal transduction mirror core genomic priorities. Ancestral expansions provided raw material for diversification, followed by lineage-specific contractions, particularly in the Longibrachiatum clade. These findings align with previous reports, supporting gene family turnover as a central driver of ecological diversification [8, 9, 65, 66]. This pattern of a stable core genome complemented by lineage-specific expansions and contractions aligns with the open pan-genome architecture recently described for Trichoderma [23] and supports the view that accessory gene families are primary drivers of ecological diversification. Moreover, the functional enrichments we observe, particularly in carbohydrate metabolism and secondary metabolism, are consistent with the trait associations identified in a recent phenogenomics study, which linked these gene classes to antagonistic phenotypes across the genus [24]. Secondary metabolism evolution is highly dynamic, involving gains, losses, and complex remodeling. The tri5 gene exemplifies this, showing divergence, partial cluster loss, and positive selection [67].

While our work highlights gene family turnover as a major contributor, other drivers of genome evolution have likely played significant roles. For example, HGT has introduced new metabolic capabilities, particularly for genes involved in plant–fungal interactions and secondary metabolism, while structural variation and chromosomal rearrangements have been reported to shape the genus’s genomic architecture and evolution [12, 13, 67]. Collectively, these processes highlight the interplay of different drivers in shaping Trichoderma’s ecological versatility.

Study limitations and future perspectives

We acknowledge several limitations that contextualize our findings and define opportunities for future research. While the Trichoderma genus comprises over 400 species, our analysis focused on six key representatives to ensure high-resolution, evidence-based annotation. With a sample of six genomes, the statistical power to detect genus-wide correlations is inherently limited; therefore, the identified patterns should be interpreted as lineage-specific trends that warrant further investigation through expanded sampling.

However, the value of TrichoBase lies in its scalability, as it provides a standardized methodological framework that can be systematically applied to additional genomes as they become available. Furthermore, all functional annotations (e.g., CAZymes and BGCs) are computational and require experimental validation. The biological roles of gene clusters with patchy phylogenetic distributions, such as the sorbicillinoid and brefeldin A-like pathways, remain to be confirmed through targeted biochemical and genetic studies. Other promising directions include investigating specific drivers of genome evolution, such as Repeat-Induced Point (RIP) mutations or intraspecific polymorphism (SNPs/InDels), these represent logical next steps for understanding fine-scale adaptation within the genus.

Conclusions

This study set out to answer three questions: (i) How does a consistent annotation framework refine the quantitative relationship between transposable element dynamics, genome size, and ecological strategy? (ii) What patterns of gene family expansion and contraction emerge from phylogenomic analysis of a standardized dataset? (iii) Does uniform annotation clarify the phylogenetic distribution of specific biosynthetic gene clusters?

By applying a unified, evidence-based annotation pipeline to six key Trichoderma species, we established TrichoBase, which is a standardized genomic resource that enables direct, high-resolution comparison across the genus. Our analyses demonstrate that TE content correlates with genome size, with mycoparasitic specialists exhibiting expanded, TE-rich genomes enriched in CAZymes and BGCs. Phylogenomic reconstruction revealed ancestral expansions in carbohydrate metabolism and secondary metabolism followed by lineage-specific contractions, particularly in the Longibrachiatum clade. Standardized annotation further uncovered patchy distributions of specific BGCs, such as the sorbicillinoid-like cluster retained exclusively in streamlined genomes, suggesting functional repurposing during niche specialization.

Together, these findings refine our understanding of how genome architecture underpins ecological diversification in Trichoderma and provide a robust foundation for future comparative and functional studies. By releasing TrichoBase as a fully reproducible pipeline and dataset, we move the field toward more precise hypothesis testing and informed bioprospecting of these economically vital fungi.
