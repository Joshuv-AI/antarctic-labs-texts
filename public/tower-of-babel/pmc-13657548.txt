Impact of body mass index on operative efficiency and outcomes in parathyroid surgery
Authors: Nicholas A. O’Keeffe, Freya Timon, Niall McInerney, Andreea Nae, Conrad Timon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The relationship between obesity and surgical outcomes is well described in major operations, but its influence on contemporary parathyroidectomy remains uncertain. This study evaluated the impact of raised body mass index (BMI) on operative efficiency, preoperative biochemistry, localization imaging, and perioperative outcomes in a single-surgeon cohort.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed all parathyroidectomies performed by a single surgeon between September 2019 and September 2023. Patient demographics, BMI, operative duration, preoperative localization imaging, serum calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH), length of stay, and complications were analyzed. Patients were classified as normal weight, overweight, or obese according to World Health Organization definitions. Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA and categorical variables using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Multivariate linear regression including age, sex, BMI category, operative extent and localization status identified independent predictors of operative time (p<0.05).Results:Among 225 patients (81.3% female; mean age 58 years), 39.6% were normal weight, 36.0% overweight, and 24.4% obese. Mean operative time was longer in obese patients (50.9 minutes) compared with normal weight (35.8 minutes) and overweight (35.5 minutes) groups (P < 0.001). Obese patients had higher preoperative PTH (P = 0.022), with no significant differences in serum calcium, localization imaging success, length of stay, cure rates, or complication rates.Conclusions:Obesity was independently associated with prolonged operative time and higher preoperative PTH in patients undergoing parathyroidectomy, without an increase in complications or length of stay.

Introduction

Obesity is a growing health concern, driven by lifestyle, environmental, and behavioral factors, such as poor diet and physical inactivity. In the United States, it ranks as the second-leading cause of preventable death after smoking[1,2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), overweight is defined as a body mass index (BMI) greater than 25 kg/m2 and obesity as a BMI greater than 30 kg/m2. The WHO estimates that there are more than 1.9 billion adults who are overweight globally, with 650 million classified as obese[3,4].

Surgeons often consider obesity a complicating factor due to its association with increased perioperative risk and worse perioperative outcomes[5]. Multiple studies have demonstrated higher morbidity and mortality following major procedures, particularly abdominal surgeries, in obese patients[6,7]. However, the specific impact of obesity on parathyroid surgery outcomes remains less clear. A study by Canu et al found no difference in postoperative complications or hospital stay among obese patients undergoing thyroidectomy[8].

The aim of this review of 225 consecutive patients with primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) was to evaluate the impact of BMI on parathyroid surgery, particularly focusing on operative efficiency, biochemical profile, and imaging localization, as performed by a single high-volume surgeon. Few studies have examined the independent impact of BMI on operative efficiency within a controlled single-surgeon setting, where operator variability is minimized. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the effect of BMI on operative duration, biochemical profile, and perioperative outcomes in a contemporary parathyroid cohort.

Methods

Patient population

We retrospectively reviewed data from 225 patients who underwent parathyroid surgery at a tertiary referral center between September 2019 and September 2023. Patient data were extracted from a prospectively maintained surgical database of a single high-volume surgeon (senior author).

Clinical data were obtained through a detailed chart and operative record review.

Data collected included demographic information, BMI, operative approach, preoperative imaging, serum calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels, and total operative time.

Surgical method

The senior author routinely obtains at least two imaging modalities prior to surgery, usually ultrasound, single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT/CT), or technetium-99m (99mTc) sestamibi scan for gland localization. Standard intraoperative nerve monitoring is used where appropriate. Patients are generally positioned with a shoulder roll and head ring. To improve exposure in obese patients, we consider partial strap muscle division in selected cases to enhance the operative field. Biochemical markers, including PTH and calcium, are generally measured in the recovery room, followed by repeat blood samples the following morning. At present, the senior author does not use intraoperative PTH sampling or other surgical adjuncts to identify parathyroid glands.

Definitions

Patients were stratified into three BMI groups according to WHO criteria: normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (BMI >30.0 kg/m2).

A small number of underweight patients (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2) were included in the normal-weight group; subgroup analysis demonstrated no significant differences in outcomes, and thus, they were retained in this category.

Operative time was defined as the interval from skin incision to skin closure, as recorded in operating theater records.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they underwent primary parathyroidectomy for PHPT and had complete demographic, imaging, and operative records. Exclusion criteria included re-operative surgery, parathyroid carcinoma, or concurrent thyroidectomy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA, and categorical data were compared with chi-squared tests. Multivariate linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of surgical time. Variables included in the model were age, sex, BMI category, operative extent (number of glands explored), and preoperative localization status. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 225 patients underwent parathyroidectomy for PHPT. Patients’ height and weight were collected at the time of the study and were included in the analysis. The mean age of the patients was 58 ± 1 years, and 81.3% were female. The mean BMI was 28.2 kg/m2 ± 0.92 kg/m2.

Of the 225 patients, 89 (39.6%) were classified as normal weight, 81 (36.0%) as overweight, and 55 (24.4%) as obese. Underweight patients were included in the normal-weight group because there were no statistically significant differences in measured outcomes.

Postoperative complications were uncommon and included transient hoarseness (3), transient hypocalcemia (6), wound infection (4), seroma formation (2), and 1 lower respiratory tract infection. There was no statistically significant difference in complication rates among normal-weight, overweight, and obese patients (P = 0.727).

With a minimum of 6 month follow-up after parathyroidectomy, eucalcaemia, or cure, was achieved in 100% of normal-weight patients, 98.8% of overweight patients, and 98.2% of obese patients (P = 0.485).

Discussion

Parathyroidectomy is the definitive treatment for PHPT, most commonly involving removal of a single hyperfunctioning gland. Approximately 10% of cases involve multiple adenomas or four-gland hyperplasia[9,10]. In 2017, an estimated 17 000 parathyroidectomies were performed in the United States[11].

This study reaffirms that obesity remains associated with longer operative times and higher preoperative PTH levels among patients undergoing parathyroidectomy. However, these differences did not translate into an increased length of stay or adverse surgical outcomes, underscoring the safety and efficiency of parathyroid surgery in obese patients. While prior studies have demonstrated comparable safety outcomes in obese patients undergoing endocrine surgery, our study uniquely evaluates operative efficiency within a controlled single-surgeon environment. This allows isolation of BMI as an independent determinant of operative duration, reducing confounding from inter-surgeon variability and providing a more precise assessment of its impact.

Although the mortality associated with parathyroid surgery is low, notable complications include cervical hematoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and persistent or recurrent disease[9]. Surgeon experience plays a critical role in minimizing these risks. Stavrakis et al demonstrated that surgeons performing more than 100 endocrine procedures annually had significantly lower complication rates[12].

Obesity is traditionally perceived as a complicating factor in surgery due to associations with increased morbidity, technical difficulty, and perioperative management challenges. While this association is well-documented in abdominal surgery[6,7], the evidence is less clear for thyroid and parathyroid surgery. Certain studies suggest that obesity may not significantly increase complication rates in endocrine surgery[8].

In this study, obese patients exhibited higher preoperative PTH levels compared to individuals with normal weight, which is consistent with existing literature[13,14]. The increased PTH may reflect delayed diagnoses in obese patients due to overlapping symptoms with metabolic syndrome or altered vitamin D metabolism. Pitt et al, in their analysis of 776 patients, identified a significantly higher preoperative PTH in obese patients compared with non-obese individuals, as well as larger glands and longer hospital stays, without increased complication rates[14].

The longer surgical time in our study is likely multifactorial, including reduced neck extension due to body habitus, which may hinder surgical access, increase difficulty, and prolong dissection[8,14]. Although the absolute increase in operative time is modest (approximately 15 minutes), this may have cumulative implications in high-volume endocrine units. Anticipating longer operative durations in patients with obesity may facilitate improved theater scheduling, resource allocation, and overall operating room efficiency.

While operative time was significantly increased, complication rates remained consistent across BMI groups, underscoring that longer procedures do not necessarily translate to poorer outcomes.

These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating preserved safety profiles in obese patients undergoing thyroid and parathyroid surgery, while adding further insight into workflow and efficiency metrics[14,15]. Emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence and large language models, may further support preoperative planning, imaging interpretation, and workflow optimization in endocrine surgical practice[16].

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and a single-surgeon cohort. While the latter strengthens internal validity by minimizing variability in operative technique and decision-making, it may limit generalizability to other settings where surgeon experience and institutional workflows differ. Operative extent was inferred from pathology and a broad classification of unilateral versus bilateral neck exploration, rather than direct intraoperative documentation of the dissection field. Additionally, several factors that may influence operative complexity were not available for analysis, including gland weight, adenoma location, neck anatomy, and vitamin D status. These variables may act as confounders and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

Obesity is associated with modestly prolonged operative duration and higher preoperative PTH levels in patients undergoing parathyroidectomy and does not adversely affect complication rates or surgical success. These findings suggest that while BMI influences operative efficiency, it should not be considered a barrier to surgery and may instead inform operative planning and theater room scheduling in high-volume endocrine practices.
