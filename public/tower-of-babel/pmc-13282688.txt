Sinuhe Classification for Pretibial Injuries: Categorising Dermatoporotic Wounds
Authors: Toni Seppälä, Vahur Grünthal, Virve Koljonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPretibial injuries manifest as lacerations or haematomas. Despite differences in pathogenesis and treatment, these entities have been mixed together in previous classifications. No classification for pretibial haematomas exists, and the Modified Dunkin Classification includes a Type V category that conflates lacerations with haematomas. To present the Sinuhe Classification—a comprehensive system categorising pretibial lacerations and haematomas with evidence‐based treatment recommendations—and propose the umbrella term ‘dermatoporotic wounds’ to encompass the full spectrum of cutaneous injuries arising from chronic skin fragility. A narrative literature review of PubMed, MEDLINE and EMBASE was conducted. Treatment recommendations and clinical findings were synthesised from the available literature, including a doctoral thesis on pretibial injuries. The Sinuhe Classification categorises pretibial lacerations into linear lacerations; flap lacerations (vital, i.e., with adequate perfusion and non‐vital, i.e., with flap necrosis); and total skin loss. Pretibial haematomas are classified into open (ruptured), closed and necrotic haematomas. Each subtype is paired with specific treatment guidance. The classification eliminates the Modified Dunkin Type V category and provides a classification framework for pretibial haematomas. The Sinuhe Classification offers a unified yet distinct framework for diagnosing and treating pretibial injuries. Adoption into clinical practice and medical education is recommended.Keywords: chronic leg ulcers, classification, dermatoporosis, dermatoporotic wounds, pretibial haematomas, pretibial injuries, pretibial lacerations, wound management

Introduction

Multimorbid elderly patients with polypharmacy frequently present to hospitals and emergency rooms due to a pretibial injury—a unique category of traumatic wounds occurring between the knee joint and the ankle [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. These patients often suffer from a chronic cutaneous fragility syndrome, dermatoporosis—characterised by skin atrophy, loss of skin elasticity, superficial excoriations, stellate pseudoscars, senile purpura, and thus susceptibility to traumatic wounds. Dermatoporosis, along with other comorbidities, contributes to the genesis and complications of these injuries [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Pretibial injuries result from low‐energy trauma, such as falling on flat ground or bumping into a household object and manifest as two clinically distinct entities: pretibial lacerations (PLs) and pretibial haematomas (PHs) [2, 3, 4, 6]. It remains unclear why some patients develop a PL and others a PH; however, anticoagulant medication predisposes patients to PHs [14, 15, 16].

Despite the uniform patient demographics and trauma aetiology, PLs and PHs differ fundamentally in their clinical presentation, pathogenesis and treatment [3, 4, 6, 15, 16]. PLs are full‐thickness skin wounds—extending at least into the subcutaneous tissue or fascia—that can be categorised into linear lacerations, flap lacerations with varying degrees of flap viability and complete skin loss [3]. PHs, on the other hand, are closed or open subcutaneous haemorrhages in which blood accumulates in the subcutaneous tissue between the skin and the muscle fascia, generating pressure that may cause skin ischaemia and subsequent necrosis [15, 16]. The distinction between these entities is critical for appropriate management, as treatment strategies differ considerably.

PLs and PHs have been frequently mixed together in scientific publications, and previous classification systems have not addressed them as separate pathologies [3, 5, 17]. The Modified Dunkin Classification, proposed by Lo et al. in 2012, remains the most widely referenced system for PLs [3]. It categorises PLs into five types, where Type V constitutes a laceration accompanied by a subcutaneous haematoma. This Type V injury clinically resembles a PH and requires haematoma evacuation rather than laceration management, creating confusion in the distinction between the two entities [4]. No classification for PHs exists. Additionally, a recent systematic review encompassing 29 studies and 2893 patients confirmed that considerable variability exists in the assessment and management of pretibial injuries, with a lack of high‐quality evidence to guide clinical practice [18].

Pretibial injuries cause substantial morbidity, mortality and healthcare expenditure [2, 4, 6, 15, 19]. PLs have a median healing time of 53 days, become chronic wounds in 32% of cases and carry a 30% infection rate [4]. PHs cause skin necrosis in 42% of patients, with delayed diagnosis being a recognised problem [15]. The economic burden is significant, with PHs generating higher total treatment costs than PLs [19]. Despite this burden, pretibial injuries remain under‐recognised and are not included in standard chronic leg ulcer classifications or medical textbooks [4, 15, 20].

Furthermore, the terminology in the literature is inconsistent: PHs are described using terms such as deep dissecting haematomas, tension subcutaneous haematomas and traumatic haematomas, while PLs are referred to as skin tears, excoriations or abrasions [7, 14, 15, 16, 21, 22]. This inconsistency hampers communication, research and timely delivery of appropriate care.

The aim of this narrative review is to present a new, comprehensive classification system that addresses both PLs and PHs as distinct entities within a unified framework—the Sinuhe Classification—and propose the umbrella term ‘dermatoporotic wounds’ to encompass the full spectrum of cutaneous injuries arising from chronic skin fragility.

The proposed classification is accompanied by evidence‐based treatment recommendations synthesised from existing literature and clinical experience derived from, to the best of our knowledge, the first doctoral thesis on pretibial injuries [20].

Materials and Methods

A PubMed MEDLINE and EMBASE search was conducted in January 2026 using a combination of thesaurus terms (lacerations OR laceration), (haematomas OR haematoma) and keywords (pretibial, pre‐tibial). No date restrictions were applied. Only publications in English were included. A total of 87 publications were identified, including one doctoral thesis on pretibial injuries. The thesis is included because it constitutes the only comprehensive synthesis of evidence on pretibial injuries, comprising four original peer‐reviewed studies that are also cited individually [4, 15, 19, 23]. The thesis is referenced for its overarching synthesis and classification rationale, not as a primary data source [20]. Criteria for application of treatment guidelines to the new classification included meta‐analyses, randomised controlled trials, prospective comparative studies, case–control studies and retrospective studies. Case reports and non‐English publications were excluded from the synthesis of treatment recommendations but were considered for context.

The Sinuhe Classification was developed through an iterative process comprising (1) a critical appraisal of existing classification systems for pretibial injuries and skin tears, identifying their limitations and gaps; (2) a synthesis of current literature, including the doctoral thesis, with particular emphasis on PHs to establish a unified framework for both injury types and (3) expert consensus among the authors. Subtypes were defined based on clinically recognisable presentations that carry distinct therapeutic implications.

Results

Review of Previous Classifications

Skin Tear Classifications

The first classification for skin tears was described by Payne and Martin in 1990 and updated in 1993 [24, 25, 26]. This system focused on the extent of epidermal loss from the dermis and categorised injuries into three categories based on tissue loss. While skin tears share some features with PLs, the Payne and Martin classification does not include full‐thickness lacerations, which are characteristic of pretibial injuries. The classification was primarily adopted in nursing medicine [25, 26].

The Australian Skin Tear Audit Research (STAR) classification, introduced in 2007, is similar to the Payne and Martin system but emphasises the quality of the skin flap and its edges [13]. Like its predecessor, STAR was developed primarily for use in nursing care settings and does not account for the full clinical spectrum of pretibial injuries [13].

The International Skin Tear Advisory Panel (ISTAP) classification, published in 2013, was developed using a Delphi process and validated by 839 healthcare professionals [27]. It categorises skin tears into three types: Type 1 (no skin loss, flap can be repositioned), Type 2 (partial flap loss) and Type 3 (total flap loss). ISTAP is the most widely adopted skin tear classification internationally and provides a decision algorithm for management [27, 28]. However, the ISTAP definition spans a wide range of depth, encompassing both partial‐thickness injuries (separation of the epidermis from the dermis) and full‐thickness injuries and applies to any anatomical site [28]. Notably, ISTAP revised its definition in 2018 specifically to help differentiate skin tears from other injuries, including PLs, clarifying that while subcutaneous tissue may be involved, a skin tear does not extend through to the muscle fascia [29]. A PL, by contrast, is by definition a full‐thickness wound extending into the subcutaneous tissue or fascia and is anatomically confined to the pretibial region. The defining distinction therefore lies in depth and site specificity. Furthermore, ISTAP includes no category for PHs and no surgical treatment recommendations, such as skin grafting. Thus, while ISTAP serves an important role in nursing and primary care, it does not capture the clinical spectrum of pretibial injuries that require surgical assessment.

In summary, while skin tears and PLs share a common pathogenesis—mechanical force applied to fragile, dermatoporotic skin—the term ‘skin tear’ encompasses a wider spectrum of injuries, from superficial excoriations to full‐thickness wounds, at any anatomical location. Moreover, superficial excoriations are themselves a recognised morphological marker of dermatoporotic skin, reinforcing that the superficial end of this spectrum reflects the underlying skin condition rather than a discrete PL, which is by definition a full‐thickness wound [30]. ‘Pretibial laceration’ is the established and more precise term in the peer‐reviewed literature on pretibial injuries and is preferred for clinical and research communication.

Dunkin Classification (2003)

In 2003, Dunkin et al. proposed a more systematic classification for PLs based on an extensive literature review [5]. The classification aimed to unify communication between healthcare professionals and provide management guidance. Types ranged from linear lacerations (Type I) to major degloving injuries (Type IV). A notable limitation was the inclusion of a degloving injury category, which results from high‐energy trauma and may not be relevant for the predominantly low‐energy pretibial injuries encountered in elderly patients. Furthermore, Type III included a subcutaneous haematoma component, creating early confusion between laceration and haematoma pathologies.

Modified Dunkin Classification (2012)

In 2012, Lo et al. modified the Dunkin Classification after another comprehensive literature review [3]. The Modified Dunkin Classification separates linear lacerations from flap lacerations and considers flap viability. Degloving injuries were excluded. However, a Type V PL was introduced, defined as a laceration with an accompanying subcutaneous haematoma.

The Type V category is not accurate for several reasons. First, this injury clinically resembles a PH with skin rupture rather than a primary laceration, as the underlying pathology is subcutaneous haemorrhage [4, 15]. Second, PHs can also rupture spontaneously due to pressure, meaning that a PH can precede the formation of what is classified as a Type V PL. Third, the treatment of a Type V injury (haematoma evacuation and wound management) aligns with PH management, not laceration management. In the doctoral thesis on pretibial injuries, it was observed that the Type V category creates diagnostic confusion and should be excluded from laceration classifications [20]. Patients presenting with a Type V injury should instead be classified and managed as having a PH.

Classification of PHs: An Unmet Need

No formal classification for PHs has been published. In a study of 60 PH patients, two distinct treatment trajectories were identified: conservative management (58%) for patients without skin necrosis and surgical evacuation (42%) for patients developing skin ischaemia and necrosis [15]. The clinical presentation of PHs can be categorised into three recognisable patterns based on previously published data and clinical experience: (1) open or ruptured haematomas, where pressure or trauma has caused the skin to rip; (2) closed haematomas with intact skin with or without ischaemiaand (3) necrotic haematomas, where skin necrosis has already developed [14, 15, 16]. These categories carry distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications.

The dermatoporosis classification proposed by Kaya and Saurat includes stages for superficial haematomas (Stage IIIa) and deep dissecting haematomas (Stages IIIb and IV) [7, 8]. While this staging system importantly acknowledges haematomas as a complication of dermatoporosis, it does not function as a treatment‐oriented classification specific to PHs and does not distinguish between the clinically relevant PH subtypes described above.

In summary, existing classification systems either address superficial excoriations and lacerations without including haematomas, conflate the two injury types or lack treatment recommendations. None provides a unified framework that categorises both PLs and PHs into clinically actionable subtypes. To address these limitations, Sinuhe Classification was developed based on the available evidence and clinical experience on pretibial injuries [4, 15, 19, 20, 23] (Table 1).

Sinuhe Classification

The Sinuhe Classification is a classification system for dermatoporotic wounds addressing pretibial injuries (both PLs and PHs) as distinct entities within a unified framework. The classification was designed to guide clinicians in making the correct diagnosis and choosing appropriate treatment.

The core principle of Sinuhe Classification is the clear separation of PLs from PHs, as these two injury types differ in pathogenesis, clinical presentation and treatment requirements despite sharing patient demographics and trauma mechanisms. Within each category, subtypes are defined based on clinically recognisable features that carry direct therapeutic implications (Table 2).

Classification of PLs

PLs are full‐thickness traumatic wounds caused by low‐energy shearing forces on dermatoporotic skin. They are categorised into three subtypes:

Linear lacerations (Image 1) are straight or irregularly linear full‐thickness wounds without a raised skin flap. Treatment consists of tension‐free wound edge opposition using adhesive tape strips. Sutures should be placed through adhesive tape to prevent suture‐induced skin edge necrosis [31, 32, 33]. Alternatively, adhesive tape alone without sutures is adequate. Tight closure must be avoided.

Linear lacerations (Image 1) are straight or irregularly linear full‐thickness wounds without a raised skin flap. Treatment consists of tension‐free wound edge opposition using adhesive tape strips. Sutures should be placed through adhesive tape to prevent suture‐induced skin edge necrosis [31, 32, 33]. Alternatively, adhesive tape alone without sutures is adequate. Tight closure must be avoided.

Flap lacerations (Images 2 and 3) present with a raised skin flap, the viability of which determines treatment. Vital flap lacerations should be managed identically to linear lacerations with tension‐free wound edge opposition using adhesive tape [3, 33]. Non‐vital flap lacerations present with partial or complete flap necrosis (absent capillary refill, dusky discolouration) and require sharp debridement of the necrotic tissue. If the resulting skin defect exceeds 0.5%–1% TBSA (total body surface area), the patient should be referred for split‐thickness skin graft (STSG) evaluation [3, 4, 34, 35]. Peripheral arterial disease must be excluded, and other meaningful comorbidities treated prior to STSG referral.

If the resulting skin defect exceeds 0.5%–1% TBSA (total body surface area), the patient should be referred for split‐thickness skin graft (STSG) evaluation [3, 4, 34, 35]. Peripheral arterial disease must be excluded, and other meaningful comorbidities treated prior to STSG referral.

Total skin loss (Image 4) constitutes a complete avulsion of the skin without a salvageable flap. Non‐vital tissue is debrided, and a referral for STSG evaluation is made when the defect exceeds 0.5%–1% TBSA [3, 4, 34]. Exclusion or adequate treatment of peripheral arterial disease is mandatory before skin grafting.

Classification of PHs

PHs form when low‐energy shearing forces rupture subcutaneous blood vessels in the pretibial region, causing haemorrhage between the skin and the muscle fascia. Anticoagulant medication and dermatoporosis are the key predisposing factors [7, 14, 15, 16]. PHs are categorised into three subtypes:

Open (ruptured) haematomas (Image 5) occur when a full‐thickness laceration accompanies a haematoma or the expanding haematoma causes spontaneous rupture of the overlying skin. The injury presents as an open wound with active or recent haemorrhage from the subcutaneous space. Treatment consists of haematoma evacuation, haemostasis, wound revision and primary wound closure or STSG in a single operative session [15, 16].

The injury presents as an open wound with active or recent haemorrhage from the subcutaneous space. Treatment consists of haematoma evacuation, haemostasis, wound revision and primary wound closure or STSG in a single operative session [15, 16].

Closed haematomas (Image 6) present with intact overlying skin. The clinical assessment must determine whether skin ischaemia is present. Signs of ischaemia include severe pain (often requiring opioid analgesia), epidermal blistering and dermatoporotic skin quality (Image 7) [15]. When ischaemia or dermatoporosis is present, prompt haematoma evacuation within 24 h is recommended to prevent skin necrosis [15, 16] (Image 8). Needle aspiration is generally ineffective, as coagulated blood does not pass through standard needles [15, 36]. When the skin is healthy and non‐dermatoporotic, without signs of ischaemia, conservative management with compression bandaging from the toes to the knee is appropriate [15]. A follow‐up visit within 1 week is advised, and the anticoagulant medication should be reviewed.

Necrotic haematomas (Image 7) represent the most advanced stage, where prolonged ischaemia has resulted in full‐thickness skin necrosis forming a necrotic eschar. Dermatoporosis is the single most important risk factor for necrosis (p < 0.0001), followed by a higher Charlson Comorbidity Index and compromised patient independence [15]. Treatment consists of escharectomy, haematoma evacuation, wound debridement and STSG in a single operative session (if there is no clinical infection), ideally within 24–48 h [15, 16].

Evidence Base for Treatment Recommendations

Evidence for PL Management

The management of PLs has been studied since the 1970s, although the total number of studies remains small and all have significant limitations [3, 34, 35]. The earliest publications by Tandon et al. and Ramnani et al. advocated early debridement and STSG, achieving healing times of 23 and 15 days, respectively [34, 37]. Shankar et al. confirmed these results with STSG under local anaesthesia, achieving healing in 21 days [38]. The randomised controlled trial by Haiart et al. comparing STSG with defatting the flap demonstrated superior healing with STSG (13 days vs. 41 days) [39].

Silk et al. introduced the technique of suturing through adhesive tape, which distributes tension across the fragile wound edges. This prospective study demonstrated healing in 26 and 16 days for flap and linear lacerations, respectively, without the need for skin grafting [33]. The consensus since the 1980s has been to avoid conventional suturing due to suture‐induced ischaemia of fragile wound edges, causing skin edge necrosis [31, 32]. Despite this, conventional suturing remains common in clinical practice, as demonstrated in a Finnish cohort where 57% of PLs were sutured against recommendations [4].

Conservative treatment is appropriate for linear and vital flap lacerations, which constitute the majority (72% in the Finnish cohort) [4]. For larger skin defects (> 0.5%–1% TBSA), early STSG has been shown to be safe and to reduce chronic wound formation [23, 40]. Donor site healing among elderly dermatoporotic patients, while sometimes prolonged, does not result in major complications [23].

Evidence for PH Management

The evidence for PH management is more limited but growing. The largest study to date by Salmerón‐González et al. retrospectively reviewed 180 patients with tension haematomas treated at a plastic surgery department [41]. Patients who underwent debridement and coverage in a single operative session had significantly lower length of hospital stay, complication rates and mortality compared to those treated in two separate procedures (p < 0.05). Furthermore, patients who only required haematoma drainage were treated within 24 h of injury (p < 0.03), highlighting the importance of early intervention. The authors concluded that early drainage should be performed as soon as possible and that, when debridement and coverage are required, they should be performed in a single stage. These findings strongly support the treatment principles of the Sinuhe Classification. A Finnish study of 60 PH patients identified two distinct treatment paths [15]. Conservative management was successful in patients without skin ischaemia or dermatoporosis. Surgical evacuation was required in 42% of patients, predominantly those with dermatoporotic skin.

Dermatoporosis was the most significant risk factor for skin necrosis (p < 0.0001) [15]. Critically, 72% of the evacuated PHs were diagnosed only after skin necrosis had already occurred, indicating delayed recognition [15]. Misdiagnosis as erysipelas or deep vein thrombosis was documented in 10% of cases [15].

Clinical criteria for prompt PH evacuation have been suggested: ischemic pain, dermatoporosis and epidermal blistering [15]. Early evacuation can prevent the cascade to skin necrosis, reducing the need for subsequent revision surgery, STSG and prolonged wound care. When a revision and STSG are needed, performing these in a single operative session has been recommended to reduce costs and suffering [19, 41].

PHs do not cause compartment syndrome, as the haemorrhage is situated in the pre‐fascial subcutaneous plane [15]. However, the ischemic pain and threat to the skin advocate that PHs should be treated within a tolerable time frame [15].

Discussion

Sinuhe Classification is the first comprehensive classification system to address both PLs and PHs within a single framework while maintaining a clear distinction between the two entities. It was developed to address three fundamental problems in the current approach to pretibial injuries: (1) the lack of a classification for PHs, (2) the confusion generated by the Modified Dunkin Type V category and (3) the absence of standardised treatment guidance that integrates both injury types.

Origin of the Eponym: The classification is named after Sinuhe, the fictional Egyptian physician in Mika Waltari's internationally acclaimed Finnish novel Sinuhe the Egyptian (1945), who travels the ancient world treating the wounds of commoners and kings with equal dedication. The name reflects both the Finnish origin of this classification and the spirit of its eponym: bringing order and compassion to the care of the frail and forgotten.

Advantages Over Previous Classifications

Sinuhe Classification offers several advantages (Table 2). First, it provides the first‐ever classification for PHs, filling a significant gap in wound medicine. The three PH subtypes (open, closed and necrotic) correspond to clinically recognisable presentations and carry direct therapeutic implications. Second, by excluding the Type V category from laceration classifications, it resolves the diagnostic confusion between PLs and PHs. Third, it pairs each subtype with specific, evidence‐based treatment recommendations, making it a practical clinical tool rather than a purely descriptive taxonomy.

The PL subtypes in the Sinuhe classification are broadly consistent with the modified Dunkin Types I–IV, facilitating the transition from the existing system. The key modification is the removal of Type V and the addition of PH subtypes, which enrich the classification's clinical utility without fundamentally disrupting established practice for laceration management.

The Role of Dermatoporosis

Dermatoporosis is the central predisposing condition for both PLs and PHs [7, 8, 9, 10]. In PH patients, dermatoporosis was the strongest predictor of skin necrosis and the need for surgical intervention [15]. Recognising dermatoporosis at presentation is therefore critical, particularly in the management of closed PHs, where the decision between conservative management and prompt evacuation hinges on skin quality assessment. The Sinuhe Classification explicitly incorporates dermatoporosis as a criterion for surgical intervention in closed PHs, reflecting its prognostic significance.

The prevalence of dermatoporosis in the elderly population is estimated at 30%–38% in those aged 65 years or more [8, 42]. Despite this, the term ‘dermatoporosis’ was not used once in the medical records of the Finnish cohort studies; instead, descriptions such as ‘parchment‐like skin’ and ‘cortisone skin’ were used [15]. This underscores the need for education and awareness.

Standardisation of Terminology

Uniform terminology is paramount for clinical communication, research and epidemiological tracking. Sinuhe Classification proposes that ‘pretibial lacerations’ and ‘pretibial haematomas’ should be the standard terms for the two injury entities. Vague or overlapping terms such as ‘skin tears’, ‘deep dissecting haematomas’ and ‘tension subcutaneous haematomas’ should be avoided or used with reference to the specific subtype within the Sinuhe classification.

When addressing the broader patient population, including injuries occurring outside the pretibial region (e.g., forearms), the umbrella term ‘dermatoporotic wounds’ is suggested to describe the entire spectrum of cutaneous injuries associated with dermatoporosis (Figure 1).

Hierarchical overview of dermatoporotic wounds and Sinuhe classification. Dermatoporotic wounds are proposed as the umbrella term for cutaneous injuries arising from chronic skin fragility (dermatoporosis). Pretibial injuries, the most common and most studied subgroup, are classified by the Sinuhe Classification into pretibial lacerations (linear lacerations, flap lacerations and total skin loss) and pretibial haematomas (open, closed and necrotic). Similar injuries occurring at other anatomical sites (e.g., forearms and dorsa of hands) are also encompassed by the umbrella term.

Clinical Implications and Implementation

The Sinuhe Classification has been published as a treatment guideline in two Finnish medical textbooks [43]. Its adoption in clinical practice has required and continues to require education of emergency physicians, wound care nurses and primary care practitioners—the professionals who most frequently encounter pretibial injuries.

The classification is designed to be simple enough for use in emergency settings while providing sufficient detail to guide treatment decisions. A key practical message is that the clinician should first determine whether the injury is a PL or a PH and then classify the subtype. This simple dichotomy alone would represent an improvement over current practice, where the two entities are frequently conflated.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, it is a narrative review. Second, the evidence base for pretibial injury management is limited, consisting mainly of small retrospective studies and a few small randomised controlled trials. Third, the Sinuhe Classification has not yet been prospectively validated. Prospective studies evaluating outcomes when the classification is used to guide treatment decisions are needed. Fourth, the classification does not include a formal grading of severity or prognostic scoring, which could be developed in future iterations.

Conclusion

Pretibial injuries are common, costly and under‐recognised traumatic wounds that cause significant morbidity and suffering among the elderly. Despite being often considered trivial, they frequently result in chronic wounds, prolonged hospitalisation and mortality. The current state of diagnosis and treatment is non‐standardised, with persistent knowledge gaps among healthcare professionals.

Sinuhe Classification addresses these problems by providing a comprehensive, clinically practical framework that classifies both PLs and PHs into distinct subtypes with evidence‐based treatment recommendations.

The adoption of uniform terminology—‘pretibial lacerations’ and ‘pretibial haematomas’ under dermatoporotic wounds—and the Sinuhe Classification into clinical practice, medical education and future research is recommended to improve outcomes and reduce the healthcare burden of pretibial injuries. Finally, we recommend adopting the term ‘dermatoporotic wounds’ as its own category in the chronic wounds classification spectrum.
