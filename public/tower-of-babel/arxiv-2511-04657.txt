Photodetection of Squeezed Light: a Whittaker-Shannon Analysis
Jasper Kranias, Christian Drago, Colin Vendromin, J. E. Sipe

Abstract
The Whittaker-Shannon decomposition provides a temporally localized description of squeezed light, making it applicable in the CW limit and leading to a definition of squeezing strength based on the number of photon pairs at a time. We show examples of its usefulness by calculating quadrature variance in a homodyne detection scheme, coincidence detection probabilities in the continuous-wave limit, and analyzing the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect for strongly squeezed light. Quadrature uncertainty falls farther below the shot noise limit when squeezing is strong, but effects due to correlations between photon pairs are most significant with weak squeezing. Our analysis extends previous results to more general scenarios, and we leverage the Whittaker-Shannon formalism to interpret them based on the temporal properties of photon pairs.



I
Introduction

Squeezed states of light can exhibit interesting spectro-temporal behaviour that does not arise for ordinary light
13
, giving rise to applications in quantum computing
2
;
19
, quantum sensing
25
, high-precision optical interferometry
37
, and quantum key distribution
33
. Some of these require strongly squeezed light, and in those that utilize single photon pairs it is important to account for the chance of generating multiple pairs
33
;
32
;
1
;
18
;
3
. Therefore, a full analysis of squeezed light requires going beyond the single pair regime
29
, often done by taking a Schmidt decomposition of the joint amplitude. However, the Schmidt decomposition is less applicable to continuous-wave (CW) squeezed light, since the Schmidt number diverges. In the Whittaker-Shannon formalism, squeezed light is described instead using a set of modes localized in time
8
, allowing it to be applied in the CW limit since only the modes near the times of interest need be considered. The Whittaker Shannon formalism also leads to a definition of squeezing strength based on how many photon pairs exist near a given time; a very long pulse of squeezed light containing many photons can be thought of as weakly squeezed in the sense that there is only one photon pair at a time.

This work utilizes the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition to investigate the behaviour of squeezed light in multiple detection schemes, generalizing some results that previously were only known in the case of finite pulses to the CW limit, and providing an analysis of how squeezing strength affects the detection statistics of squeezed light. Throughout the paper, we use the Whittaker-Shannon formalism to build intuition on the behaviour of squeezed light based on the temporal correlations of photon pairs and the rate they are generated.

In Section
II
we outline the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition for nondegenerate squeezed light, show how it leads to a description of the state within a time window, and make comparisons with the usual method of discretizing the temporal modes. In the process of building the Whittaker-Shannon formalism, we produce a disentangling formula for the multimode nondegenerate squeezing operator. To our knowledge, this has not been correctly derived before; Ref.
28
provides a formula disagreeing with ours, though it appears not to be used elsewhere in the literature. In Section
III
we formulate homodyne detection in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition both in the continuous-wave (CW) limit and for finite pulses. In both cases, the quadrature variance is minimized further with increased squeezing strength, showing that our parameter for squeezing strength corresponds well with traditional measures
27
;
23
;
35
. In Section
IV
we extend Takesue’s work
33
on the coincidence visibilities of a polarization dependent detection scheme to a more general description of the squeezed ket. We find expressions for the photon number and coincidence probabilities in a time window by writing projection operators for detection inside the time window in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon modes within.
In Section
V
we derive expressions for the coincidence probability in a Hong-Ou-Mandel scheme for multimode squeezed. Unlike previous expressions that either only account for some higher order terms
11
, or are not applicable in the CW limit
32
, our formulae can be applied to CW squeezed light of arbitrary squeezing strength. The presence of multiple photon pairs reduces the visibility of the Hong-Ou-Mandel dip, agreeing with previous work
32
;
38
In contrast to homodyne detection where quantum effects were most significant for strong squeezing, the schemes of Sections
IV
and
V
display greater effects with weak squeezing.

II
Whittaker-Shannon Formalism

The Whittaker-Shannon decomposition describes squeezed light in terms of novel supermodes that are localized in time
8
. In this section, we outline the decomposition for nondegenerate squeezed light, and show how we can approximate the state in certain time windows using only the nearby supermodes.
From consideration of a finite time window, we provide a definition of weak squeezing that reflects the density of photon pairs within a range of time; “weakly squeezed” can then describe a ket in the CW limit, even though there are an infinite number of pairs. We also find the
\bm{N}
and
\bm{M}
moments in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon formalism, and compare the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition to a typical method of discretizing the temporal modes.

II.1
Whittaker-Shannon Decomposition

The Whittaker-Shannon decomposition was first introduced for degenerate squeezed light
8
. The notation for the degenerate regime can be used generally, but it will be convenient for us to introduce an explicit notation for the nondegenerate regime since commutativity of some of the operators simplifies certain calculations. Here we consider light propagation in one-direction, as in an optical fiber or waveguide mode. If the frequency ranges of
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
over which the joint spectral amplitude
\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is significant are far apart, we identify the first range with the “signal”, and the second with the “idler”, and then assign separate operators (with different center frequencies) to the signal and idler ranges. After shifting
\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
so that the center frequencies of the signal and idler modes are at zero, we can write a nondegenerate squeezed ket as

\ket{\psi}=e^{\beta\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})a^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})b^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac},

(1)

with

\displaystyle[a(\omega),b^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=0,

(2)

\displaystyle[a(\omega),a^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

\displaystyle[b(\omega),b^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

and
\ket{\vac}
the vacuum state
8
;
29
. We adopt the convention that integrals range from
-\infty
to
\infty
unless indicated otherwise. Eq. (
1
) can also apply if the signal and idler photons share the same center frequency, but are either spatially separated, or are associated with different transverse modes. The joint amplitude is not symmetric in general (
\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\neq\gamma(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})
), and is normalized by

\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}|\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})|^{2}=1.

(3)

Equivalently, we can write the squeezed ket in terms of the joint temporal amplitude

\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})=\int\frac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega_{1}t_{1}}e^{-i\omega_{2}t_{2}}\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

(4)

and the Fourier transforms of the annihilation operators

\displaystyle\overline{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega t}a(\omega),

\displaystyle\overline{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-i\omega t}b(\omega),

(5)

as

\ket{\psi}=e^{\beta\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac}.

(6)

We refer to the modes described by
\overline{a}(t)
and
\overline{b}(t)
as continuous temporal (CT) modes, and they obey commutation relations like Eq. (
2
). We take the ket in Eq. (
6
) to identify the state at
t=0
. The state at time
t
is then

\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{\beta\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\exp(i\omega_1t+i\omega_2t)a^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})b^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac},

(7)

or in terms of the temporal joint amplitude,

\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{\beta\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1}-t)\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t_{2}-t)-h.c.}\ket{\vac}.

(8)

Since the light propagates with velocity
v
— we neglect group velocity dispersion —
\overline{a}(t_{1}-t)
represents the field at
t_{1}
at a distance
d=vt
from the pulse center at time zero
8
. Therefore, the operators in Eqs. (
1
) and (
6
) represent the field at the detectors if they lie at position
d
and we shift the origin of time by
t=d/v
.

If
\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is approximately bandwidth limited by
\Omega
, significant only when

-\frac{\Omega}{2}\leq\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\leq\frac{\Omega}{2},

(9)

then we can perform a Whittaker-Shannon decomposition of the joint amplitude
8
to write

\ket{\psi}=S\ket{\text{vac}},

(10)

where

S=e^{\sum_{n,m}\beta_{nm}A_{n}^{\dagger}B_{m}^{\dagger}-h.c.}

(11)

is the nondegenerate squeezing operator,
\beta_{nm}=\beta\tau\overline{\gamma}(n\tau,m\tau)
,
\tau=2\pi/\Omega

and we define

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t),

(12)

\displaystyle B^{\dagger}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t),

where

\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\text{sinc}\bigg(\frac{\pi(t-n\tau)}{\tau}\bigg).

(13)

The Whittaker-Shannon timescale
\tau
is typically on the order of the coherence time, the range of
|t_{2}-t_{1}|
over which
|\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})|^{2}
is significant.
Unlike for degenerate squeezing,
\beta_{nm}
is not in general a symmetric matrix
8
. The Whittaker-Shannon modes
\{\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\}
are orthonormal
8
, which guarantees that the supermode operators obey the usual commutation relations

[A_{n},B^{\dagger}_{m}]=0,\quad[A_{n},A^{\dagger}_{m}]=\delta_{nm},\quad[B_{n},B^{\dagger}_{m}]=\delta_{nm}.

(14)

The supermodes associated with the operators
A^{\dagger}_{n}
and
B^{\dagger}_{n}
are localized around
n\tau
, in the sense that
A^{\dagger}_{n}
and
B^{\dagger}_{n}
are composed mostly of
\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t)
or
\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t)
near
t=n\tau
.
Since
\{\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\}
forms a complete set for expanding functions whose Fourier transforms are nonzero only for frequencies satisfying Eq. (
9
)
8
, and we are assuming that only these frequencies are necessary to describe the state, we can invert Eq. (
12
) to write the CT modes as

\displaystyle\overline{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)A_{n},

\displaystyle\overline{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)B_{n}.

(15)

If we let
\overline{\gamma}_{max}=max(|\overline{\gamma}(n\tau,m\tau)|)
and define

\mathring{\beta}=\beta\tau\overline{\gamma}_{max},

(16)

we can then write
\beta_{nm}
as

\beta_{nm}=\mathring{\beta}r_{nm},

(17)

where
r_{nm}=\overline{\gamma}(n\tau,m\tau)/\overline{\gamma}_{max}
, with
|r_{nm}|\leq 1
. Since
|\mathring{\beta}|
is the maximum of
|\beta_{nm}|
, it sets the magnitude of the matrix
\bm{\beta}
and other matrices we will define later. When
|\mathring{\beta}|
is small we can expand functions of those matrices to low orders. We argue in Section
II.2
that
|\mathring{\beta}|
quantifies the squeezing strength.

We shall now distinguish the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition from another way of decomposing the ket in terms of temporally localized modes. Consider discretizing the CT mode operators into time bins of size
T_{D}
by defining

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{D}}}\int_{D_{n}}dt\overline{a}(t),

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{D}}}\int_{D_{m}}dt\overline{b}(t),

(18)

where
D_{n}
indicates that the integral ranges from
(n-\frac{1}{2})T_{D}
to
(n+\frac{1}{2})T_{D}
. We refer to this as a
standard discretization
into discrete temporal (DT) modes, and the DT mode operators
\overline{a}_{n}
and
\overline{b}_{m}
obey commutation relations like Eq. (
14
). If we partition the integrals into time bins of size
T_{D}
and approximate the joint temporal amplitude in each time bin as taking the value at the center by assuming
\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})
varies slowly over
T_{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\beta\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t_{2})

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\beta T_{D}\overline{\gamma}(nT_{D},mT_{D})\overline{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\overline{b}^{\dagger}_{m}.

The squeezed ket then takes the same form as a Whittaker-Shannon decomposition with
\tau=T_{D}
:

\ket{\psi}=e^{\sum_{n,m}\beta_{nm}\overline{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\overline{b}^{\dagger}_{m}-h.c.},

(20)

but the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition remains distinct from this method, and holds some advantages over it. First, the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition allows for a timescale as large as the coherence time, where
\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})
is not slowly varying, so it requires less terms than Eq. (
20
) to cover the same time window. A timescale on the order of the coherence time also leads to a natural definition of squeezing strength. Moreover, Eq. (
18
) cannot be inverted to write the CT mode operators in terms of the DT mode operators, so we cannot use the standard discretization to calculate quantities with explicit time dependence (such as the moments in Eq. (
42
)). However, many calculations, such as those in Sections
IV
and
V
, are defined purely in terms of projection onto the Whittaker-Shannon supermode operators. Since Eq. (
20
) takes the same form as the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition, a calculation defined by projection of Eq. (
20
) onto DT mode operators will have an identical result to the corresponding calculation based on the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition.

II.2
Partitioning the Ket in Time

A model often used to qualitatively represent squeezed light is the double Gaussian joint amplitude:

\displaystyle\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{T_{p}T_{c}}{\pi^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T_{c}^{2}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{2}}{4\pi}}e^{-\frac{T_{p}^{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})^{2}}{4\pi}},

(21)

\displaystyle\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{p}T_{c}}}e^{-\frac{\pi(t_{1}-t_{2})^{2}}{4T_{c}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{\pi(t_{1}+t_{2})^{2}}{4T_{p}^{2}}}.

The length of the pulse is characterized by
T_{p}
, and
T_{c}<T_{p}
can be identified as a coherence time. The inverse of the coherence time
B_{c}=1/T_{c}
identifies the bandwidth of the joint spectral amplitude
29
;
9
.

It is natural to choose a bandlimit on the order of the bandwidth; we take
\Omega=2\pi/T_{c}
and
\tau=T_{c}
. In Fig.
1
we show the intensity of the spectral and temporal double Gaussian joint amplitudes and the matrix
r_{nm}
of its Whittaker-Shannon decomposition.

a) Normalized double-Gaussian joint spectral amplitude
|\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})|^{2}
with axes normalized by
\Omega
. b) Normalized double-Gaussian joint temporal intensity
|\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})|^{2}
with axes normalized by
T_{p}
. c) Amplitudes

t_{J}=7\tau
; any

outside of the box is either small, or has both
n\tau
and
m\tau
far from
t_{J}
. These plots were made with
T_{p}/T_{c}=15
.

Our choice of
\Omega
leads to

r_{nm}=e^{-\frac{\pi(n-m)^{2}}{4}}e^{-(\frac{T_{c}}{T_{p}})^{2}\frac{\pi(n+m)^{2}}{4}},

(22)

and we observe that
r_{nm}
is small far from the diagonal; off-diagonal terms will be a factor of at least
e^{-\pi}\approx 0.04
smaller than the maximum for
|n-m|\geq 2
. In the CW limit,
T_{p}\to\infty

7
, so we take

r_{nm}\to e^{-\frac{\pi(n-m)^{2}}{4}},

(23)

and can still use Eq. (
17
) since
|\mathring{\beta}|
remains finite in the CW limit even though
\beta
diverges
8
. Thus, the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition allows us to go to the CW limit analytically.

For the double Gaussian and other joint amplitudes with the property that
\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})
is small when
t_{2}
is far from
t_{1}
, we can follow earlier arguments
8
to approximate the ket locally near a particular time. If we are only interested in the squeezed light near some time
t_{J}
, then we can partition the matrix
\bm{\beta}
into

\bm{\beta}=\bm{\beta}^{J}+\bm{K}.

(24)

The matrix
\bm{\beta}^{J}
is centered at an index
n_{J}=[\frac{t_{J}}{\tau}]
, where
[\cdot]
denotes the nearest integer, and we choose
d_{J}
such that
\beta_{n_{J},m}
(
\beta_{n,n_{J}}
) can be neglected for
|n_{J}-m|>\frac{d_{J}}{2}
(
|n-n_{J}|>\frac{d_{J}}{2}
). We take
\beta^{J}_{nm}
to be equal to
\beta_{nm}
when
n,m
are within
(d_{J}-1)/2
of
n_{J}
and zero everywhere else; Fig.
1
c) shows a schematic of such a partition. The partition size
d_{J}
should be larger than the range of times in which we are interested, since the statistics at times close to the edge of the partition will be affected by elements of
\bm{\beta}
not included in
\bm{\beta}^{J}
.
When the coherence time is sufficiently smaller than the pulse duration,
d_{J}
can be chosen so that the time window is smaller than the pulse, and we can describe the state near
t_{J}
using only parts of
\bm{\beta}
that correspond to times near
t_{J}
. However, this is not possible for all joint amplitudes. For example, if we rotated the double Gaussian in Fig.
1
b) by
45\degree
in the
t_{1}-t_{2}
plane, then
|\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})|^{2}
would be significant over the entire range of significant
t_{2}
for most significant values of
t_{1}
and we could not choose a
d_{J}
that allows us to describe the state near
t_{J}
without considering the whole pulse. Later on, we derive a few results in terms of
\bm{\beta}^{J}
, and if the time window
J
is shorter than the pulse, then these expressions are only valid for joint amplitudes that are small far from the diagonal. However, the results apply to any joint amplitude if
J
covers the entire pulse so that
\bm{\beta}^{J}=\bm{\beta}
.

For the times of interest we can now approximate the squeezing operator as

S\approx e^{\sum^{J}_{n,m}\beta^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}-h.c.}\equiv S_{J}.

(25)

The superscript
J
on the sum indicates that we only sum over the nonzero elements of
\bm{\beta}^{J}
, and if we let
\ket{\vac}^{J}
be the vacuum state corresponding to the Whittaker-Shannon modes with indices inside the time window, the state near
t_{J}
can be approximately represented by

\ket{\psi_J}=S_{J}\ket{\vac}^{J}=e^{\sum^{J}_{n,m}\beta^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}-h.c.}\ket{\vac}^{J}.

(26)

Consider the left and right polar decompositions of
\bm{\beta}^{J}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{J}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{J}\bm{P}^{J},

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{J}

\displaystyle=\bm{Q}^{J}\bm{U}^{J},

(27)

respectively, where
\bm{P}^{J}=\sqrt{(\bm{\beta}^{J})^{\dagger}\bm{\beta}^{J}}
,
\bm{Q}^{J}=\sqrt{\bm{\beta}^{J}(\bm{\beta}^{J})^{\dagger}}=\bm{U}^{J}\bm{P}^{J}(\bm{U}^{J})^{\dagger}
, and
\bm{U}^{J}
is unitary. To gain some intuition on the state we use the disentangled form of the nondegenerate squeezing operator (Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle S_{J}

\displaystyle=|\bm{W}^{J}|e^{\sum^{J}_{n,m}T^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}}e^{\sum^{J}_{n,m}(L^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}+Y^{J}_{nm}B^{\dagger}_{n}B_{m})}

(28)

\displaystyle\times e^{-\sum^{J}_{n,m}V^{J}_{nm}A_{n}B_{m}},

where

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{J}

\displaystyle=\sech\bm{Q}^{J}

(29)

\displaystyle\bm{T}^{J}

\displaystyle=(\tanh\bm{Q}^{J})\bm{U}^{J}

\displaystyle\bm{L}^{J}

\displaystyle=\ln(\sech\boldsymbol{Q}^J)

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{J}

\displaystyle=\ln(\sech(\boldsymbol{P}^J)^T)

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{J}

\displaystyle=((\bm{U}^{J})^{\dagger}(\tanh\bm{Q}^{J}))^{T},

to find

\ket{\psi_J}=|\bm{W}^{J}|e^{\sum^{J}_{n,m}T^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}}\ket{\vac}^{J}.

(30)

If we are in the limit of weak squeezing (
|\mathring{\beta}|\ll 1
), the mean number of pairs in
\ket{\psi_J}
is

N_{J}\equiv\int dt\bra{\psi_J}\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t)\overline{a}(t)\ket{\psi_J}\approx\Tr((\boldsymbol{Q}^J)^2)+\mathcal{O}(|\mathring{\beta}|^{4}).

(31)

To leading order,
N_{J}
is proportional to
|\mathring{\beta}|^{2}
since
\bm{Q}^{J}
is proportional to
|\mathring{\beta}|
. However,
|\mathring{\beta}|\ll 1
does not guarantee that
N_{J}\ll 1
since the trace can be large if
\dim(\bm{Q}^{J})
is large. If
N_{J}\ll 1
, we can expand Eq. (
30
) to first order in
|\mathring{\beta}|
to find

\ket{\psi_J}\approx|\bm{W}^{J}|(\ket{\vac}^{J}+\sqrt{N_{J}}\ket{II}_{J}),

(32)

where
\ket{II}
is the normalized two-photon ket

\ket{II}_{J}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{J}}}\sum^{J}_{n,m}T^{J}_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}\ket{\vac}^{J},

(33)

and the prefactor

|\bm{W}^{J}|\approx 1-\frac{N_{J}}{2}

(34)

guarantees that the state is normalized to first order in
N_{J}\ll 1
.
Although the expansion required
N_{J}\ll 1
,
|\mathring{\beta}|\ll 1
is the only criteria we need to call the state weakly squeezed; small
|\mathring{\beta}|
means that we can write Eq. (
32
)
if
we choose a time window such that
N_{J}
is also small. We refer to a partition of weakly squeezed light where Eq. (
32
) holds as a
single pair window
. For light with a finite pulse length and mean pair number
N\ll 1
, the entire pulse can be considered a single pair window. In the CW limit where where
|\beta|\to\infty
and
N
diverges,
|\mathring{\beta}|
will remain finite and we can find a single pair window if
|\mathring{\beta}|
is small.

In a single pair window, we can see from Eq. (
33
) that the state is a superposition of pairs with probability amplitudes
|\bm{W}^{J}|T^{J}_{nm}
, and hence if the supermodes
x
in the signal and
y
in the idler are detected, they must be from the pair corresponding to
T^{J}_{xy}
. The next order in the expansion of Eq. (
30
) is

\sum_{n,m,j,k}T^{J}_{nm}T^{J}_{jk}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}A^{\dagger}_{j}B^{\dagger}_{k}\ket{\vac},

(35)

subject to an appropriate normalization. The detection of signal and idler supermodes
x
and
y
could result from the state
T^{J}_{xy}T^{J}_{pq}A^{\dagger}_{x}B^{\dagger}_{y}A^{\dagger}_{p}B^{\dagger}_{q}\ket{\vac}
, where
x
and
y
are from the “same pair”, but could also result from
T^{J}_{xq}T^{J}_{py}A^{\dagger}_{x}B^{\dagger}_{q}A^{\dagger}_{p}B^{\dagger}_{y}\ket{\vac}
, where
x
and
y
are from “different pairs”. Photons from the same pair will have a stronger dependence on the properties of
\bm{T}^{J}
. For example, consider a joint amplitude like the double Gaussian which is small far from the diagonal, so that
T^{J}_{nm}
is small for
n
far from
m
. Signal and idler photons from the same pair in supermodes
x
and
y
have a probability amplitude proportional to
T^{J}_{xy}
, so it will be unlikely to detect them in supermodes that are far apart in time. If the photons were from different pairs then their probability amplitude is proportional to
T^{J}_{xq}T^{J}_{py}
, which can still be large when
x
is far from
y
. Thus, the presence of multiple pairs can erode behaviour that is prominent in the single-pair regime; we will see this occur with polarization dependent coincidence detection and the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect.

II.3
Increasing the Whittaker-Shannon Resolution

Our discussion up to this point has assumed we have chosen a minimal bandlimit
\Omega
that is just large enough to contain all significant parts of
\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
. However, we could always increase
\Omega
by an arbitrary amount, and the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition would remain valid. Increasing the bandlimit decreases
\tau
, effectively increasing the temporal resolution of the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition, and in later sections we show many instances where this is useful. But one needs to be careful with the arbitrary nature of
\Omega
when discussing weak squeezing;
|\mathring{\beta}|
is proportional to
\tau
, so by increasing the resolution we could force
|\mathring{\beta}|\ll 1
for any joint amplitude. For a minimal bandlimit,
\tau
is on the order of the coherence time
8
, and it makes sense to call Eq. (
32
) the ket in a single pair window since the time window chosen will be at least on the order of
\tau
. On the other hand, if
\tau
is smaller than the coherence time, Eq. (
32
) could refer to photons in a time window smaller than that time, and we cannot claim there is at most one photon pair within the coherence time.
Therefore, the quantification of the squeezing strength by
|\mathring{\beta}|
requires the use of a bandlimit on the order of the bandwidth.

II.4
N and M Moments

So far we have dealt with nondegenerate squeezed states, but it will be useful to outline some results for both degenerate and nondegenerate squeezed states here. A degenerate squeezed ket can can be written as

\ket{\psi}=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}\int d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})a^{\dagger}(\omega_{1})a^{\dagger}(\omega_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac},

(36)

where the joint spectral amplitude is symmetric in its variables (
\gamma(\omega_{2},\omega_{1})=\gamma(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
). The Whittaker-Shannon decomposition follows analogously to that of nondegenerate squeezed light, and can be seen in full detail in
8
.

We define the
\bm{N}
and
\bm{M}
moments of the Whittaker-Shannon supermodes as

\displaystyle N^{d}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}\ket{\psi}

(37)

\displaystyle M^{d}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}A_{n}A_{m}\ket{\psi}

for degenerate squeezed light, and

\displaystyle N^{a}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}\ket{\psi}

(38)

\displaystyle N^{b}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}B^{\dagger}_{n}B_{m}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle M^{ab}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}A_{n}B_{m}\ket{\psi}

for nondegenerate squeezed light. As shown in Appendix
B
, they can be written in terms of Whittaker-Shannon matrices:

\displaystyle\bm{N}^{d}

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}\bm{P}^{J}

(39)

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{d}

\displaystyle=(\sinh\bm{Q}^{J})(\cosh\bm{Q}^{J})\bm{U}^{J}

in the degenerate regime, and

\displaystyle\bm{N}^{a}

\displaystyle=(\sinh^{2}\bm{Q}^{J})^{T}

(40)

\displaystyle\bm{N}^{b}

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}\bm{Q}^{J}

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{ab}

\displaystyle=(\sinh\bm{Q}^{J})(\cosh\bm{Q}^{J})\bm{U}^{J}

in the nondegenerate regime. Additionally, Eq. (
40
) provides the moments of the state given by Eq. (
20
)
29
.

The moments of the CT modes are related to those above by a sum over the Whittaker-Shannon modes. For degenerate squeezed light we have

\displaystyle N^{d}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t)\overline{a}(t^{\prime})\ket{\psi}=\overline{\chi}_{n}^{*}(t)N_{nm}^{d}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t^{\prime}),

(41)

\displaystyle M^{d}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\overline{a}(t)\overline{a}(t^{\prime})\ket{\psi}=\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)M_{nm}^{d}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t^{\prime}),

and for nondegenerate squeezed light,

\displaystyle N^{a}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{a}(t_{2})\ket{\psi}=\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n}(t_{1})N^{a}_{nm}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t_{2}),

(42)

\displaystyle N^{b}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{b}(t_{2})\ket{\psi}=\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n}(t_{1})N^{b}_{nm}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t_{2}),

\displaystyle M^{ab}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\overline{a}(t_{1})\overline{b}(t_{2})\ket{\psi}=\overline{\chi}_{n}(t_{1})M^{ab}_{nm}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t_{2}).

Homodyne Detection

Figure 2:
Homodyne Detection Scheme. The signal of interest (for us, a multimode degenerate squeezed state, represented by

To further justify the use of
|\mathring{\beta}|
as a quantification of squeezing strength, we investigate the quadrature variance of squeezed light in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition. Homodyne detection mixes a signal with a strong local oscillator to measure a photocurrent proportional to a quadrature operator of the electromagnetic field
26
;
10
; a schematic is shown in Fig.
2
. For squeezed light, measurements of the photocurrent variance will fall below the shot-noise limit for certain quadratures, and the minimum variance quantifies the amount of squeezing. In this section we investigate how homodyne detection can be formulated in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition. We find an expression for the frequency spectrum of the photocurrent variance in the CW limit, and solve for the minimum variance and optimal local oscillator for a measurement of the total homodyne current. For both pulsed and CW homodyne measurement, the minimum variance in dB decreases linearly with
|\mathring{\beta}|
.

Let us start by considering degenerate multimode squeezed light in the state

\ket{\psi}=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac}.

(43)

Given sufficiently fast detectors, the photocurrents are proportional to the photon numbers
24
, and the difference is represented by the operator

i(t)=\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t)\overline{c}(t)+\overline{a}(t)\overline{c}^{\dagger}(t).

(44)

Firstly, we will work in the CW limit where the local oscillator is also CW. Let the local oscillator be in the coherent state
\ket{\eta}
of
\overline{c}(t)
, where
\eta=|\eta|e^{i\theta}
for some phase
\theta
relative to the signal. In the CW limit we can measure quadrature squeezing by analyzing the frequency spectrum of the photocurrent variance (normalized by the local oscillator magnitude
|\eta|^{2}

\sigma^{2}_{CW}(\theta,\omega,t)=\frac{1}{|\eta|^{2}}\int d\tilde{\tau}\langle i(t)i(t+\tilde{\tau})\rangle e^{-i\omega\tilde{\tau}},

(45)

where

\displaystyle\langle i(t)i(t+\tilde{\tau})\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{\psi}\bra{\eta}i(t)i(t+\tilde{\tau})\ket{\eta}\ket{\psi}.

(46)

Assuming the local oscillator is much stronger than the signal, we find

\langle i(t)i(t+\tilde{\tau})\rangle=|\eta|^{2}\big(\delta(\tilde{\tau})+N^{d}(t,t+\tilde{\tau})+N^{d}(t+\tilde{\tau},t)+e^{2i\theta}M^{d}(t,t+\tilde{\tau})+e^{-2i\theta}\big(M^{d}(t+\tilde{\tau},t)\big)^{*}\big).

(47)

Although we should be in a stationary state because we are working in the CW limit, the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition introduced time dependence based on the Whittaker-Shannon modes, so we take an average over some time window
J=[t_{0}-T/2,t_{0}+T/2]

As a second example, we use the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition to characterize the coincidence detection probabilities of a two-polarization nondegenerate multimode squeezed state, with
H
(
V
) polarized signal and idler modes labeled by
a_{H}(\omega_{1})
(
a_{V}(\omega_{1})
) and
b_{H}(\omega_{2})
(
b_{V}(\omega_{2})
), respectively. Following Takesue
33
, we take the Hamiltonian to be

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{H}(t)+\hat{H}_{V}(t),

(60)

where the time evolution of
\hat{H}_{H}(t)
(
\hat{H}_{V}(t)
) results in a nondegenerate squeezed vacuum state in the
H
(
V
) polarization

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_H}=e^{\beta\int dt_{1}dt_{2}\overline{\gamma}(t_{1},t_{2})\overline{a}_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{b}_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{2})-h.c.}\ket{\vac_H},

(61)

and similarly for
\ket{\psi_V}
. Since the total Hamiltonian is a sum of those for each polarization, the resulting state is separable as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{HV}}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi_H}\otimes\ket{\psi_V}

(62)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{HV}}

\displaystyle=e^{\beta_{nm}{A_{n}^{H}}^{\dagger}{B_{m}^{H}}^{\dagger}-h.c.}\ket{\vac_H}

\displaystyle\otimes e^{\beta_{nm}{A_{n}^{V}}^{\dagger}{B_{m}^{V}}^{\dagger}-h.c.}\ket{\vac_V},

where we took the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition in each polarization using definitions of
\beta_{nm}
,
{A_{n}^{H}}^{\dagger}
, etc. analogous to those in Section
II
. We have assumed that both polarizations share the same joint amplitude, but the results can be easily generalized to the scenario where they are different. The detection scheme shown in Fig.
6
allows us to measure the coincidence detection probability between
H
photons in the signal and idler ranges
\mathcal{P}_{HH}
and the coincidence detection probability between
H
signal photons and
V
idler photons
\mathcal{P}_{HV}
. Since the
H
and
V
squeezed states are uncorrelated, comparing
\mathcal{P}_{HH}
and
\mathcal{P}_{HV}
tells us the degree to which coincidence counts are due to correlations between signal and idler photons.

Imagine turning on the detectors for a long time
T\gg\tau
, we consider a coincidence to occur if both detectors fire at least once within the time window, regardless of the time between each detector firing. If we take the detection probability of
x
photons incident on a threshold detector of efficiency
\alpha
to be
D_{x}\equiv 1-(1-\alpha)^{x}

33
, then we can find the coincidence detection probabilities by summing over the probability of projecting onto each possible photon number combination, weighted by their detection probabilities. Let
\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}^{J}
be the vacuum state corresponding to the
H
polarized signal modes within the time window
J=[t_{0}-T/2,t_{0}+T/2]
, and
\mathbb{I}_{a_{H}}^{{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}}
be the identity operator for the
H
polarized signal modes outside
J
, then
V_{a_{H}}^{J}\equiv\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}^{J}\bra{\vac}_{a_{H}}^{J}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{a_{H}}^{{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}}
is the operator that projects onto the vacuum state for modes within the time window, and onto the identity for modes outside it. The projector
P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}
for
s
signal photons within
J
can be written as (Appendix
E
)

P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}=\frac{1}{s!}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}\overline{a}_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots\overline{a}_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})V_{a_{H}}^{J}\overline{a}_{H}(t_{1})\ldots\overline{a}_{H}(t_{s}),

(63)

and similarly for
P^{b_{H}}_{J,s}
and
P^{b_{V}}_{J,s}
. We can then write the coincidence probabilities as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HH}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}D_{s}^{2}\bra{\psi_H}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}P^{b_{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H},

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s_{a}}\ket{\psi_H}

\displaystyle\times\bra{\psi_V}P^{b_{V}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_V}.

The expression for
\mathcal{P}_{HH}
has a single sum since there must be the same number of signal and idler photons.

Since
T\gg\tau
, the Whittaker-Shannon modes
\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)
will be approximately orthonormal within the time window and we can neglect the outside modes (Fig.
3
), allowing us to approximate

P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}\approx P^{A^{H}}_{J,s},

(65)

where

P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}=\frac{1}{s!}\sum^{J}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}V_{A^{H}}^{J}A^{H}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{n_{s}}.

(66)

The superscript
J
on the sum indicates that each sum over
n_{j}
ranges across the indices for which
n_{j}\tau
is inside the time window. Analogously to
V_{a_{H}}^{J}
,
V_{A^{H}}^{J}=\ket{\vac}_{A^{H}}^{J}\bra{\vac}_{A_{H}}^{J}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A_{H}}^{{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}}

n\tau
inside the time window, and onto the identity otherwise. Essentially we have approximated the set of CT modes in the time window as the set
J
of Whittaker-Shannon modes that are centered inside the window. To count coincidences in a time window where
T\gg\tau
does not apply we could increase the Whittaker-Shannon resolution.

Now the coincidence detection probabilities can be rewritten in terms of the Whittaker-Shannon projectors
P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}
,
P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HH}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}D_{s}^{2}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s_{a}}\ket{\psi_H}\bra{\psi_V}P^{B^{V}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_V}.

\mathcal{P}^{H}_{s}\equiv\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H}=\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{s}=\sum_{\{q_{n}\}\vdash s}\frac{|\bm{W}^{J}|^{2}}{1^{q_{1}}(q_{1}!)\ldots s^{q_{s}}(q_{s}!)}\prod_{u=1}^{s}\Tr((\tanh^2 \boldsymbol{Q}^J)^u)^{q_{n}},

(68)

where

V
Hong-Ou-Mandel Effect

Finally, we consider the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect, which occurs when indistinguishable photons are incident on a beam splitter and destructive interference occurs in one of the output ports. It is often demonstrated with a varying time delay on identical input states; when the time delay is zero they are indistinguishable and the probability to detect light in both output modes approaches zero
9
. In this section we apply the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition to the HOM effect and use the ideas we have built about squeezing strength to explain how the HOM effect differs for weakly and strongly squeezed light.

Figure 10:
Hong-Ou-Mandel scheme. The idler modes undergo a time delay
\tau_{H}
before they are mixed with the signal modes on a 50:50 beam splitter. We consider the probability for both threshold detectors to click, depending on the time delay.

Consider the scheme shown in Fig.
10
where we induce a time delay
\tau_{H}
on the idler modes and then mix them with the signal modes through a 50:50 beam splitter before they are incident on threshold detectors. Our input is a multimode squeezed state where the signal and idler modes share the same center frequency; however, we still label them with separate operators
\overline{a}(t)
and
\overline{b}(t)
since they exist in spatially separated channels. This can be written as a nondegenerate squeezed ket

\ket{\psi}=|\bm{W}|e^{T_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}}\ket{\vac};

(73)

we have used the disentangled form given by Eqs. (
29
)(
30
), but kept the full matrix
\bm{\beta}
instead of a partition
\bm{\beta}^{J}
.

The time delay transforms
\overline{b}(t)\to\overline{b}(t-\tau_{H})
, which shifts the Whitaker-Shannon mode operators to

\displaystyle B_{m}^{\dagger}\to\int dt\overline{\chi}_{m}(t)\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t-\tau_{H})=\int dt\overline{\chi}_{m}(t+\tau_{H})\overline{b}^{\dagger}(t).

(74)

If
\tau_{H}
is a multiple of the Whittaker-Shannon timescale
\tau
so that
\tau_{H}=q\tau
for some integer
q
, then
\overline{\chi}_{m}(t+\tau_{H})=\overline{\chi}_{(m-q)}(t)
, therefore,

B_{m}^{\dagger}\to B_{(m-q)}^{\dagger}.

(75)

The beam splitter causes the transformation

\displaystyle\overline{a}(t)

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\overline{c}(t)+\overline{d}(t)\right),

(76)

\displaystyle\overline{b}(t)

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\overline{c}(t)-\overline{d}(t)\right),

where
\overline{c}(t)
and
\overline{d}(t)
are the annihilation operators for the modes associated with the beam splitter outputs, and this transforms the Whittaker-Shannon operators as

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}_{n}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(C^{\dagger}_{n}+D^{\dagger}_{n}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle B^{\dagger}_{m}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(C^{\dagger}_{m}-D^{\dagger}_{m}\right),

where
C^{\dagger}_{n}
and
D^{\dagger}_{n}
are the natural extensions of the Whittaker-Shannon supermode operators to the beam splitter outputs:

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\overline{c}^{\dagger}(t),

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\overline{\chi}_{n}(t)\overline{d}^{\dagger}(t).

(78)

By defining the shifted matrix
\mathring{T}_{nm}\equiv T_{n,(m+q)}
, we can write the state in the time window
J
after the time delay and beam splitter as

\ket{\psi_{HOM}(\tau_H)}^{J}=|\bm{W}|e^{\frac{1}{2}\mathring{T}^{J}_{nm}(C^{\dagger}_{n}+D^{\dagger}_{n})(C^{\dagger}_{m}-D^{\dagger}_{m})}\ket{\vac},

(79)

where
\bm{\mathring{T}}^{J}
is equal to
\bm{\mathring{T}}
for indices inside
J
, and zero for indices outside of it. The matrix partition must be taken after applying the time delay, since it changes which Whittaker-Shannon modes are included in
J
.

As in the previous section, we consider a coincidence count to occur when both detectors register a click at least once. We show in Appendix
H
that with perfect detection efficiency the coincidence probability is

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HOM}(\tau_{H})=1+|\bm{W}^{J}|^{2}\left(1-2|\bm{I}^{J}-(\bm{\lambda}^{J})^{\dagger}\bm{\lambda}^{J}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(80)

where
\bm{\lambda}^{J}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\mathring{T}}^{J}+(\bm{\mathring{T}}^{J})^{T})
(the symmetrization of
\bm{\mathring{T}}^{J}
) and
\bm{I}^{J}
is the identity matrix of appropriate dimension. Although this calculation only works when
\tau_{H}
is an integer multiple of
\tau
, we can always choose a larger bandlimit
\Omega
in order to make
\tau
smaller, and therefore in principle evaluate
\mathcal{P}_{HOM}(\tau_{H})
for any value of
\tau_{H}
.

For weakly squeezed light in a single pair window, the coincidence probability has the usual Hong-Ou-Mandel dip; since the detected photons are from the same pair, they are indistinguishable (given a symmetric joint amplitude) if there is no time delay
9
. As squeezing becomes stronger, some of the contribution to the coincidence probability is due to photons from different pairs, and these will not necessarily destructively interfere in one of the beam splitter outputs. We see in Fig.
11
that as
|\mathring{\beta}|
increases (stronger squeezing), the minimum of the (normalized) dip becomes higher.

(82)

\displaystyle=1+|\bm{W}|^{2}\bigg(1-2\big|\bm{I}-\frac{1}{4}\tanh^{2}\bm{Q}\big|^{-1}\bigg).

VI
Conclusion

We have formulated the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition for nondegenerate squeezed light, found the disentangling formula of the multimode nondegenerate squeezing operator, and used the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition to analyze squeezed light in three detection schemes. The quadrature variance reduction measured with homodyne detection justified our use of
|\mathring{\beta}|
to quantify squeezing strength
27
;
23
;
35
. In a polarization dependent coincidence detection scheme introduced by Takesue
33
, the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition allowed us to find coincidence probabilities within a time window, even in the CW limit, and showed that entanglement between signal and idler photons has a greater effect on coincidence probabilities when squeezing is weak. Our expressions for the coincidence probabilities are more general than the previous analysis since they are compatible with any joint amplitude. The temporal properties of the Whittaker-Shannon modes also lead to expressions for the coincidence probability of multimode light of arbitrary squeezing strength in a Hong-Ou-Mandel scheme, and they can be applied in the CW limit where other results can not
32
;
11
. We find that the Hong-Ou-Mandel dip becomes shallower as squeezing strength increases.

Our examples show squeezed light exhibits its quantum nature in distinct ways. Effects due to the entanglement of photon pairs are strongest in the weakly squeezed regime, since any photons detected are likely to belong to the same pair. In contrast, quadrature noise can be squeezed the most when there are many photon pairs. Future work will investigate how the behaviour in these opposing regimes relates to different measures of nonclassicality. Negativity of the Wigner function does not detect any nonclassicality for squeezed light

36
. However, other measures indicate nonclassicality that increases with the strength of a single-mode squeezed state
14
;
20
;
15
, and future work will investigate these measures for multimode squeezed light. The presence of multiple photon pairs reduces entanglement
3
;
1
;
16
;
18
and degrades interference visibility in many schemes
38
;
5
;
40
;
22
. Thus, measures of entanglement could serve to describe regimes where quantum effects are greater for low squeezing strength.

A major advantage of the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition over the Schmidt decomposition is the ability to approximate the ket within the time window of interest. However, by reconstructing the joint amplitude within a finite time window, the Whittaker-Shannon decomposition could be used to define a temporally local Schmidt decomposition. This method may retain advantages of the Schmidt decomposition, including the Schmidt number as a measure of entanglement
4
, while also having the Whittaker-Shannon formalism’s ability to pick out the state at relevant times.

Appendix A
Disentangling of Nondegenerate Squeezing Operator

Here we outline the proof for the disentangling formula of the nondegenerate squeezing operator, which follows closely from the degenerate case in
21
. Define the operators

\displaystyle A(u)=\overline{b}u^{\dagger}a,\quad A^{\dagger}(v)=\overline{a}^{\dagger}vb^{\dagger},\quad B(w,x)=\overline{a}^{\dagger}(wx^{\dagger})a+\overline{b}(x^{\dagger}w)b^{\dagger},

(84)

where
u,v,w,x
are square matrices, but not in general symmetric as they are in the degenerate case. We also define
a=\begin{pmatrix}a_{o}&a_{1}&\ldots\end{pmatrix}^{T}
,
a^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\dagger}_{o}&a^{\dagger}_{1}&\ldots\end{pmatrix}^{T}
and let
\overline{a}
(
\overline{a}^{\dagger}
) denote the transpose of
a
(
a^{\dagger}
) (
b
and
b^{\dagger}
are defined analogously). To prove that
\mathcal{L}\equiv\{A(u),A^{\dagger}(v),B(w,x)\;|\;u,v,w,x=(zz^{\dagger})^{n}z,\;n=0,1,2,\ldots\}
forms a Lie group, we must show that
[a,b]\in\mathcal{L}
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{L}
. (The other Lie group axioms are satisfied automatically by the definition of the commutator.) Using Einstein notation to calculate the commutators:

\displaystyle=[b_{i}u^{\dagger}_{ij}a_{j},a^{\dagger}_{k}v_{kl}b^{\dagger}_{l}]

(85)

\displaystyle=u^{\dagger}_{ij}v_{kl}(b_{i}[a_{j},a^{\dagger}_{k}b^{\dagger}_{l}]+[b_{i},a^{\dagger}_{k}b^{\dagger}_{l}]a_{j})

\displaystyle=u^{\dagger}_{ij}v_{kl}(b_{i}b^{\dagger}_{l}\delta_{jk}+a^{\dagger}_{k}a_{j}\delta_{il})

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}_{k}v_{kl}u^{\dagger}_{lj}a_{j}+b_{i}u^{\dagger}_{ij}v_{jl}b^{\dagger}_{l}

\displaystyle=\overline{a}^{\dagger}(vu^{\dagger})a+\overline{b}(u^{\dagger}v)b^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[b_{i}u^{\dagger}_{ij}a_{j},a^{\dagger}_{k}w_{kl}x^{\dagger}_{lm}a_{m}+b_{k}x^{\dagger}_{kl}w_{lm}b^{\dagger}_{m}]

(86)

\displaystyle=b_{i}u^{\dagger}_{ij}w_{kl}x^{\dagger}_{lm}a_{m}\delta_{jk}+b_{k}u^{\dagger}_{ij}x^{\dagger}_{kl}w_{lm}a_{j}\delta_{im}

\displaystyle=\overline{b}(u^{\dagger}wx^{\dagger})a+\overline{b}(x^{\dagger}wu^{\dagger})a

\displaystyle=[a^{\dagger}_{i}v_{ij}b^{\dagger}_{j},a^{\dagger}_{k}w_{kl}x^{\dagger}_{lm}a_{m}+b_{k}x^{\dagger}_{kl}w_{lm}b^{\dagger}_{m}]

(87)

\displaystyle=-a^{\dagger}_{k}w_{kl}x^{\dagger}_{lm}v_{mj}b^{\dagger}_{j}-a^{\dagger}_{i}v_{ij}x^{\dagger}_{jl}w_{lm}b^{\dagger}_{m}

\displaystyle=-\overline{a}^{\dagger}(wx^{\dagger}v)b^{\dagger}-\overline{a}^{\dagger}(vx^{\dagger}w)b^{\dagger}.

Therefore we have the commutation relations

\displaystyle=B(v,u),

(88)

\displaystyle[A(u),B(w,x)]

\displaystyle=A(xw^{\dagger}u)+A(uw^{\dagger}x),

\displaystyle[A^{\dagger}(v),B(w,x)]

\displaystyle=-A^{\dagger}(wx^{\dagger}v)-A^{\dagger}(vx^{\dagger}w).

Now since
u,v,w,x=(zz^{\dagger})^{n}z
, then
xw^{\dagger}u=(zz^{\dagger})^{n}zz^{\dagger}(zz^{\dagger})^{m}(zz^{\dagger})^{l}z=(zz^{\dagger})^{n+m+l+1}z
, so
A(xw^{\dagger}v)\in\mathcal{L}
, and similarly for
A(uw^{\dagger}x),A^{\dagger}(wx^{\dagger}v),A^{\dagger}(vx^{\dagger}w)
. Therefore
\mathcal{L}
forms a Lie group. By Ado’s theorem
21
, we can construct a faithful matrix representation

\displaystyle A(u)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-u^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad A^{\dagger}(v)=\begin{pmatrix}0&v\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B(w,x)=\begin{pmatrix}wx^{\dagger}&0\\
0&-x^{\dagger}w\end{pmatrix},

(89)

which can be verified to obey the correct commutation relations. The operator we are interested in disentangling is

\displaystyle S=e^{A^{\dagger}(z)-A(z)},

(90)

and mapping to the matrix form of the operators we have

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=e^{\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
z^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}}

(91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
z^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
z^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}^{2n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)!}\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
z^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\begin{pmatrix}(zz^{\dagger})^{n}&0\\
0&(z^{\dagger}z)^{n}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)!}\begin{pmatrix}0&(zz^{\dagger})^{n}z\\
(z^{\dagger}z)^{n}z^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The polar decomposition
z=UP=QU
allows us to write

\displaystyle(zz^{\dagger})^{n}=(QUU^{\dagger}Q)^{n}=Q^{2n},

(92)

\displaystyle(z^{\dagger}z)^{n}=(PU^{\dagger}UP)^{n}=P^{2n},

\displaystyle(zz^{\dagger})^{n}z=Q^{2n}QU=Q^{2n+1}U,

\displaystyle(z^{\dagger}z)^{n}z^{\dagger}=z^{\dagger}(zz^{\dagger})^{n}=U^{\dagger}Q^{2n+1},

and we can write
S
as

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n)!}Q^{2n}&0\\
0&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n)!}P^{2n}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)!}Q^{2n+1}U\\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)!}U^{\dagger}Q^{2n+1}&0\end{pmatrix}

(93)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh Q&(\sinh Q)U\\
U^{\dagger}(\sinh Q)&\cosh P\end{pmatrix}.

We now seek a factorization of
S
of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cosh Q&(\sinh Q)U\\
U^{\dagger}(\sinh Q)&\cosh P\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&\alpha\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0\\
0&\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
\delta&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho+\alpha\gamma\delta&\alpha\gamma\\
\gamma\delta&\gamma\end{pmatrix},

(94)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\rho
are square matrices. Therefore

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\cosh P

(95)

which implies

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=(\tanh Q)U,

\displaystyle\delta=U^{\dagger}(\tanh Q)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\dagger},

(96)

and then

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\cosh Q-(\tanh Q)U(\cosh P)U^{\dagger}(\tanh Q)=(\cosh Q)^{-1}.

(97)

If we write the matrix factors as matrix exponentials, then

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&\alpha\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=e^{\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}},

(98)

and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
\delta&I\end{pmatrix}=e^{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\delta&0\end{pmatrix}}=e^{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\alpha^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}}.

(99)

For some
w,x^{\dagger}
, we should have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0\\
0&\gamma\end{pmatrix}=e^{\begin{pmatrix}wx^{\dagger}&0\\

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=e^{wx^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}\rho^{-1}U

\displaystyle\implies wx^{\dagger}

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=e^{\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\

\displaystyle S=e^{\overline{a}^{\dagger}zb^{\dagger}-\overline{b}z^{\dagger}a}=e^{\overline{a}^{\dagger}\alpha b^{\dagger}}e^{\overline{a}^{\dagger}(\ln\rho)a+\overline{b}(U^{\dagger}(\ln\rho)U)b^{\dagger}}e^{-\overline{b}\alpha^{\dagger}a},

(103)

or in terms of

Appendix B
Calculation of Moments

Here we explain how to obtain the
N
and
M
moments. The moments of degenerate squeezed light are found with the methods in
8
. Here we show similar derivations for nondegenerate squeezed light.

First we find the transformation induced by the nondegenerate squeezing operator
S=e^{\beta_{nm}A_{n}^{\dagger}B_{m}^{\dagger}-h.c.}
on the Whittaker-Shannon mode operators. Let
X=-\beta_{nm}A_{n}^{\dagger}B_{m}^{\dagger}+h.c.
, so that
S=e^{-X}
, and the operator expansion theorem tells us that
12

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}A_{r}S=A_{r}+[X,A_{r}]+\frac{1}{2!}[X,[X,A_{r}]]+\ldots.

(109)

Now compute the successive commutators:

\displaystyle=-\beta_{nm}B_{m}^{\dagger}[A_{n}^{\dagger},A_{r}]=\beta_{nm}B_{m}^{\dagger}\delta_{nr}=\beta_{rs}B^{\dagger}_{s},

(110)

\displaystyle[X,[X,A_{r}]]

\displaystyle=[X,\beta_{rs}B_{s}^{\dagger}]=\beta^{*}_{nm}\beta_{rs}A_{n}[B_{m},B_{s}^{\dagger}]=\beta^{*}_{nm}\beta_{rs}A_{n}\delta_{ms}=\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{as}A_{s},

\displaystyle[X,[X,[X,A_{r}]]]

\displaystyle=[X,\beta_{ra}\beta^{*}_{sa}A_{s}]=\beta_{ra}\beta^{*}_{sa}\beta_{nm}B_{m}^{\dagger}\delta_{ns}=\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{ab}\beta_{bs}B_{s}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle[X,[X,[X,[X,A_{r}]]]]

\displaystyle=[X,\beta_{ra}\beta^{*}_{ba}\beta_{bs}B_{s}^{\dagger}]=\beta_{ra}\beta^{*}_{ba}\beta_{bs}\beta^{*}_{nm}A_{n}\delta_{ms}=\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{ab}\beta_{bc}\beta^{\dagger}_{cs}A_{s}.

Each iteration switches between
A_{s}
(even) and
B_{s}^{\dagger}
(odd), and adds a factor
\beta^{\dagger}_{is}
(
\beta_{is}
) for even (odd) terms, where
i
is the last index of the previous factor. Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}A_{r}S

\displaystyle=\mu^{A}_{rs}A_{s}+\nu^{A}_{rs}B_{s}^{\dagger}

(111)

\displaystyle\mu^{A}_{rs}

\displaystyle=\delta_{rs}+\frac{1}{2!}\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{as}+\frac{1}{4!}\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{ab}\beta_{bc}\beta^{\dagger}_{cs}+\ldots

\displaystyle\nu^{A}_{rs}

\displaystyle=\beta_{rs}+\frac{1}{3!}\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{ab}\beta_{bs}+\frac{1}{5!}\beta_{ra}\beta^{\dagger}_{ab}\beta_{bc}\beta^{\dagger}_{cd}\beta_{ds}+\ldots.

The above can be expressed in matrix form with the polar decomposition of
\bm{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{A}

\displaystyle=I+\frac{1}{2!}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}+\frac{1}{4!}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}+\ldots=I+\frac{1}{2!}\bm{U}\bm{P}^{2}\bm{U}^{\dagger}+\ldots=\cosh\bm{Q}

(112)

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}^{A}

\displaystyle=\bm{\beta}+\frac{1}{3!}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}\bm{\beta}+\frac{1}{5!}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\dagger}\bm{\beta}+\ldots=\bm{U}\bm{P}+\frac{1}{3!}\bm{U}\bm{P}^{3}+\ldots=(\sinh\bm{Q})\bm{U}.

Note that
\bm{\mu}^{A}
is Hermitian, but
\bm{\nu}^{A}
is not symmetric since
\bm{\beta}
is not symmetric.
To find
S^{\dagger}B_{r}S
we can re-index the sum in the exponent of
S
to find

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}B_{r}S

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}-h.c}B_{r}e^{\beta_{nm}A^{\dagger}_{n}B^{\dagger}_{m}+h.c}

(113)

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta_{nm}B^{\dagger}_{m}A^{\dagger}_{n}-h.c}B_{r}e^{\beta_{nm}B^{\dagger}_{m}A^{\dagger}_{n}+h.c}

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta^{T}_{nm}B^{\dagger}_{n}A^{\dagger}_{m}-h.c}B_{r}e^{\beta^{T}_{nm}B^{\dagger}_{n}A^{\dagger}_{m}+h.c},

meaning that the transformation on
B_{r}
is the same as on
A_{r}
but with
\bm{\beta}\to\bm{\beta}^{T}
, which is equivalent to taking

\displaystyle\bm{U}\to\bm{U}^{T},\quad\bm{Q}\to\bm{P}^{T},\quad\bm{P}\to\bm{Q}^{T}.

(114)

Therefore,

\displaystyle S^{\dagger}B_{r}S

\displaystyle=\mu^{B}_{rs}B_{s}+\nu^{B}_{rs}A_{s}^{\dagger},

(115)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{B}

\displaystyle=\cosh\bm{P}^{T}=\cosh(\boldsymbol{U}^\dagger\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{U})^{T}=(\bm{U}^{\dagger}\bm{\mu}^{A}\bm{U})^{T},

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}^{B}

\displaystyle=(\sinh\bm{P}^{T})\bm{U}^{T}=\bm{U}^{T}(\sinh\bm{Q}^{T})=(\bm{\nu}^{A})^{T}.

Using these transformations, we calculate

\displaystyle N^{a}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}\ket{\psi}

(116)

\displaystyle=\bra{\vac}S^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}S\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\text{vac}}((\mu^{A})^{*}_{na}A^{\dagger}_{a}+(\nu^{A})^{*}_{na}B_{a}(\mu^{A}_{mb}A_{b}+\nu^{A}_{mb}B^{\dagger}_{b})\ket{\text{vac}}

\displaystyle=(\nu^{A})^{*}_{na}\nu^{A}_{mb}\delta_{ab}

\displaystyle=(\nu^{A})^{*}_{na}\nu^{A}_{ma}

\displaystyle=((\bm{\nu}^{A})^{*}(\bm{\nu}^{A})^{T})_{nm}

\displaystyle=((\sinh\bm{Q}^{*})\bm{U}^{*}\bm{U}^{T}(\sinh\bm{Q}^{T}))_{nm}=((\sinh\bm{Q}^{T})(\sinh\bm{Q}^{T}))_{nm}

\displaystyle=(\sinh^{2}\bm{Q}^{T})_{nm},

and similarly for the other moments. The moments of the CT modes are found in terms of those of the Whittaker-Shannon supermodes using Eq. (

\displaystyle N^{a}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\overline{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\overline{a}(t_{2})\ket{\psi}

(117)

\displaystyle=\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n}(t_{1})\bra{\psi}A^{\dagger}_{n}A_{m}\ket{\psi}\overline{\chi}_{m}(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n}(t_{1})N^{a}_{nm}(t_{2})\overline{\chi}_{m}(t_{2}).

Appendix C

This appendix shows the derivation of the quadrature variance of homodyne detection in the CW limit. Evaluating Eq. (

. This is a good approximation when

Appendix E
Time Window Projectors

Here we prove that the projector we defined in Eq. (
63
) is indeed a projection operator. We make use of the identity

\displaystyle\bra{\vac}a_{H}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t_{n})a_{H}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}})\ket{\vac}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma(\{t^{\prime}_{n}\})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta(t_{i}-\sigma_{i}),

(121)

where
\sigma(\{t^{\prime}_{n}\})
is a permutation of the set
\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}=\{t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n}\}
,
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
th element of
\sigma(\{t^{\prime}_{n}\})
, and the sum is over all permutations.

We can show that
P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s^{\prime}}=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}
as follows:

\displaystyle P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!s^{\prime}!}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}dt^{\prime}_{1}\ldots dt^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})V_{a_{H}}^{J}

(122)

\displaystyle\times a_{H}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t_{s})a_{H}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}})V_{a_{H}}^{J}a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!s^{\prime}!}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}dt^{\prime}_{1}\ldots dt^{\prime}_{s}a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})V_{a_{H}}^{J}\bigg(\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma(\{t^{\prime}_{s}\})}\prod_{i=1}^{s}\delta\big(t_{i}-\sigma_{i}\big)\bigg)a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}}{(s!)}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}dt^{\prime}_{1}\ldots dt^{\prime}_{s}a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})V_{a_{H}}^{J}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{s}\delta(t_{i}-t^{\prime}_{i})\bigg)a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t^{\prime}_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}}{(s!)}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})V_{a_{H}}^{J}a_{H}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t_{s})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s},

since there are
s!
permutations of the set
\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}
and we can exchange the order of the annihilation operators
a_{H}(t_{i})
.

Now we must show that
\sum_{s}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}
is equal to the identity operator. We can write an arbitrary state in the
a_{H}
Hilbert space as

\ket{\varphi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\ket{\varphi}_{n},

(123)

where
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|p_{n}|^{2}=1
. We set
\ket{\varphi}_{0}=\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}
, and for
n>0

\ket{\varphi}_{n}
is the
n
photon ket

\ket{\varphi}_{n}=\int dt_{1}\ldots dt_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{n})\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}

(124)

for some complex-valued function
\phi^{(n)}
normalized by
\int dt_{1}\ldots dt_{n}|\phi^{(n)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|^{2}=1
. The multiple integral over all time can be decomposed as combinations of integrals over the window
J
and the remaining times
{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}

\displaystyle\int dt_{1}\ldots dt_{n}=\left(\int_{J}dt_{1}+\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}dt_{1}\right)\ldots\left(\int_{J}dt_{n}+\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}dt_{n}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{n}\})}\int_{J}d\sigma_{1}\ldots d\sigma_{k}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma_{k+1}d\sigma_{n},

(125)

so
\ket{\varphi}_{n}
can expressed in a form where every creation operator is either in
J
or
{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}

\displaystyle\ket{\varphi}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{n}\})}\int_{J}d\sigma_{1}\ldots d\sigma_{k}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma_{k+1}d\sigma_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{n})\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}

(126)

\displaystyle\ket{\varphi}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{n}\})}\int_{J}d\sigma_{1}\ldots d\sigma_{k}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma_{k+1}d\sigma_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})

\displaystyle\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{k})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{k+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right).

We could exchange the order of the creation operators to write them in terms of
\sigma
, but the arguments of
\phi^{(n)}
stay as
\{t_{n}\}
since their order matters.

\displaystyle P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\varphi}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{k}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{k+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

(127)

\displaystyle\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\bra{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}a_{H}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}(t_{s})a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{k})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{k+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{k}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{k+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle\delta_{sk}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{s}\})}\prod_{i=1}^{s}\delta(\sigma_{i}-\sigma^{\prime}_{i})\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(t_{s})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{k+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right)

If

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(s!)^{2}(n-s)!}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{s}\})}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}d\sigma_{1}\ldots d\sigma_{s}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{s}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

(128)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{s}\delta(\sigma_{i}-\sigma^{\prime}_{i})\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{s})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(s!)^{2}(n-s)!}\sum_{\sigma(\{t_{s}\})}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{s}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{s})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!(n-s)!}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{s}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

\displaystyle\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{s})\ket{\vac}^{J}_{a_{H}}\right)\otimes\left(a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1})\ldots a_{H}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n})\ket{\vac}^{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}_{a_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle.

Acting with

on the arbitrary state results in

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\varphi}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\frac{1}{s!(n-s)!}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\{t^{\prime}_{n}\}}\int_{J}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots d\sigma^{\prime}_{s}\int_{J\mkern-8.0mu{\backslash}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{s+1}d\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\phi^{(n)}(t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{n})

Appendix F
Coincidence Probability Calculations

Here we show the calculation of coincidence probabilities when we have a large time window with
T\gg\tau
. Using Eq. (
15
), we can rewrite the projector
P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}
as

\displaystyle P^{a_{H}}_{J,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}}\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{s}}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}V_{a_{H}}^{J}A^{H}_{m_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{m_{s}}

(130)

\displaystyle\times\int_{J}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{s}\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n_{1}}(t_{1})\ldots\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n_{s}}(t_{s})\overline{\chi}_{m_{1}}(t_{1})\ldots\overline{\chi}_{m_{s}}(t_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}}\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{s}}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}V_{a_{H}}^{J}A^{H}_{m_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{m_{s}}

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\int_{J}dt_{1}\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n_{1}}(t_{1})\overline{\chi}_{m_{1}}(t_{1})\bigg)\ldots\bigg(\int_{J}dt_{s}\overline{\chi}^{*}_{n_{s}}(t_{s})\overline{\chi}_{m_{s}}(t_{s})\bigg).

Since
T\gg\tau
, the Whittaker-Shannon modes will be approximately orthonormal inside the time window, and we neglect the modes outside the window. Each integral over
t_{i}
results in
\delta_{n_{i}m_{i}}
, but only for the indices
n_{i}
where
n_{i}\tau
is inside the time window. Then we approximate the set of modes corresponding to
\ket{\vac}_{a_{H}}^{J}
as the set of Whittaker-Shannon modes for the relevant indices, and write the projector as

P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}=\frac{1}{s!}\sum^{J}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}V_{A^{H}}^{J}A^{H}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{n_{s}},

(131)

where the primed sum indicates we are only taking the indices which correspond to times within the Window, and
\ket{\vac}^{J}_{A^{H}}
corresponds to the Whittaker-Shannon modes labeled by those indices. By using the discrete analogue of Eq. (
121
)

\displaystyle\bra{\vac}A^{H}_{k_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{k_{n}}A^{H\dagger}_{k^{\prime}_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{k^{\prime}_{n\prime}}\ket{\vac}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma(\{k^{\prime}_{n}\})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{k_{i}\sigma_{i}},

(132)

we can prove with similar steps as the previous section that
P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s^{\prime}}=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}
and
\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}=\mathbb{I}_{A^{H}}^{J}
. Now to find the coincidence probabilities we sum over every combination of projectors where there is at least one signal and one idler photon, weighted by the detection probability of each number:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HH}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s_{a}}P^{b_{H}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_H}\approx\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s_{a}}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_H},

(133)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s_{a}}\ket{\psi_H}\bra{\psi_V}P^{b_{V}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_V}\approx\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s_{a}}\ket{\psi_H}\bra{\psi_V}P^{B^{V}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_V}

However, we can see from the disentangled form of
\ket{\psi_H}
that all terms have an equal number of signal and idler photons, therefore,
\bra{\psi_H}P^{a_{H}}_{J,s_{a}}P^{b_{H}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_H}
is only nonzero when
s_{a}=s_{b}
and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HH}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}D_{s}^{2}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{HV}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}=1}^{\infty}D_{s_{a}}D_{s_{b}}\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s_{a}}\ket{\psi_H}\bra{\psi_V}P^{B^{V}}_{J,s_{b}}\ket{\psi_V}.

(134)

The photon number probabilities can be calculated using the disentangled form of the ket. First let us consider
\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H}
. The only term in the expansion of
\ket{\psi_H}=|\bm{W}^{J}|e^{T_{jk}{A^{H}_{j}}^{\dagger}{B^{H}_{k}}^{\dagger}}\ket{\vac}
that contributes is the one with s signal and idler photons,
\frac{1}{s!}(T_{jk}A^{H\dagger}_{j}B^{H\dagger}_{k})^{s}\ket{\vac}_{H}^{J}
, where
\ket{\vac}_{H}^{J}\equiv\ket{\vac}_{A^{H}}^{J}\otimes\ket{\vac}_{B^{H}}^{J}
. Expanding, we find

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_H}P^{A^{H}}_{J,s}P^{B^{H}}_{J,s}\ket{\psi_H}

(135)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\bm{W}^{J}|^{2}}{(s!)^{2}}\sum^{J}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{s}}\sum^{J}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{s}}\bigg|\frac{1}{s!}\sum^{J}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{s}}\sum^{J}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{s}}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\ldots T_{j_{s}k_{s}}\bra{\vac}_{H}^{J}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{s}}A^{H}_{j_{1}}B^{H}_{k_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{j_{s}}B^{H}_{k_{s}}\ket{\vac}_{H}^{J}\bigg|^{2}.

Now compute

\displaystyle\bra{\vac}_{H}^{J}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{s}}A^{H}_{j_{1}}B^{H}_{k_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{j_{s}}B^{H}_{k_{s}}\ket{\vac}_{H}^{J}

(136)

\displaystyle=\bra{\vac}_{A^{H}}^{J}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}A^{H}_{j_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{j_{s}}\ket{\vac}_{A^{H}}^{J}\bra{\vac}_{B^{H}}^{J}B^{\dagger}_{m_{1}}\ldots B^{H\dagger}_{m_{s}}B^{H}_{k_{1}}\ldots B^{H}_{k_{s}}\ket{\vac}_{B^{H}}^{J}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\sigma(\{n_{s}\})}\prod_{u=1}^{s}\delta_{\sigma_{u}j_{u}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\pi(\{m_{s}\})}\prod_{v=1}^{s}\delta_{\pi_{v}k_{v}}\bigg),

and the term inside the absolute value becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s!}\sum^{J}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{s}}\sum^{J}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{s}}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\ldots T_{j_{s}k_{s}}\bra{\vac}^{J}A^{H\dagger}_{n_{1}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{1}}\ldots A^{H\dagger}_{n_{s}}B^{H\dagger}_{m_{s}}A^{H}_{j_{1}}B^{H}_{k_{1}}\ldots A^{H}_{j_{s}}B^{H}_{k_{s}}\ket{\vac}^{J}=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\sigma(\{n_{s}\})}\sum_{\pi(\{m_{s}\})}\prod_{u=1}^{s}T_{\sigma_{u}\pi_{u}}.

(137)
