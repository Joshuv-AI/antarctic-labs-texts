The weight of caring: emotional labour, burnout risk, and the need for supportive practice environments in Saudi Arabian mental health nursing
Authors: Alya Alghamdi, Bader M. Almutairy, Waleed M. Alshehri, Norah M. Alyahya, Abdulaziz M. Alodhailah, Faihan F. Alshaibany, Mohammed Almutairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health nursing involves significant emotional labour, yet the experiences of nurses in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) remain under-researched. This study explored nurses’ experiences of emotional labour when caring for women with psychosis in KSA, where unique cultural and organisational factors may compound emotional demands.MethodsA hermeneutic phenomenological design, guided by van Manen’s lifeworld existentials and emotional labour theory, was employed. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a purposive sample of 21 female nurses from two mental health facilities in Riyadh, KSA. Data were analysed using van Manen’s selective highlighting approach.ResultsOne overarching theme emerged: Nurses’ experiences of providing care to women with psychosis in KSA: understandings and emotions. This encompassed two key themes: (1) Fragmented knowledge in under-resourced environments, capturing how educational gaps and chronic staffing shortages shape nurses’ capacity to understand and respond therapeutically to psychosis; and (2) Carrying the weight without a net: emotional labour in the absence of support, illuminating the constellation of distress responses, including sympathy-driven over-caring, fear, sadness, and unrecognised transference, that accumulate when clinical supervision and formal psychological support are entirely absent. Participants reported intense feelings of sadness, anger, fear, and helplessness, exacerbated by a lack of clinical supervision or debriefing structures.ConclusionsMental health nurses in KSA perform substantial emotional labour without formal professional support systems. Notably, the collectivist cultural resources available to nurses, spirituality, family networks, and collegial belonging, provided informal coping, but did not substitute for structured clinical supervision or debriefing. Secondary stigma, manifesting as social distancing from colleagues upon disclosure of emotional difficulties, further limited help-seeking among some participants. The convergence of chronic staff shortages, limited specialist mental health nursing education (i.e., postgraduate or pre-registration psychiatric nursing preparation), and absent clinical supervision creates a high risk for burnout. To ensure nurse wellbeing and care quality, it is essential to establish supportive practice environments, including formal clinical supervision, resilience training, and protected time for specialist education.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04795-6.

Introduction

Mental health nursing is a profession characterised by sustained emotional engagement with individuals experiencing profound psychological distress [1–3]. Unlike many healthcare specialties where technical skills predominate, psychiatric nursing requires nurses to utilise themselves as therapeutic instruments [4], their presence, emotional responsiveness, and capacity for interpersonal connection are the primary vehicles of care [5, 6]. This relational intensity creates an inherent emotional labour burden that has been identified internationally as a significant contributor to occupational stress, compassion fatigue, and burnout among mental health nurses [7, 8].

Central to this study is the construct of emotional labour. Emotional labour, as conceptualised by Hochschild [9] and subsequently applied to nursing by Smith [10], refers to the effort involved in managing one’s own emotions to meet the emotional demands of the job, suppressing personal feelings, displaying appropriate emotional responses, and maintaining therapeutic composure in the face of behaviours that may be distressing, frightening, or emotionally provocative. In psychiatric nursing, emotional labour encompasses not only the general demands of caring for vulnerable individuals but also the specific challenges of maintaining therapeutic relationships with consumers who may be hostile, suspicious, withdrawn, or actively psychotic [11, 12].

Two psychodynamic phenomena are particularly relevant to the emotional labour of psychiatric nursing: transference and countertransference. Transference occurs when consumers unconsciously redirect feelings about significant others onto their nurse; countertransference occurs when nurses’ own unresolved feelings, biases, or emotional reactions are triggered by consumers [13, 14]. When unrecognised and unmanaged, these phenomena can compromise therapeutic relationships, contribute to inappropriate care decisions, and accelerate emotional burnout [15, 16].

Three structural features of mental health nursing in KSA converge to create a particularly acute emotional labour burden. The KSA context introduces additional factors that may compound the emotional labour of mental health nursing. First, as documented by Alyahya et al. [17], there is currently no postgraduate mental health nursing programme widely available across KSA institutions (notably, a Master of Mental Health Nursing programme exists at King Abdulaziz University); however, postgraduate specialist preparation remains inaccessible to the majority of practising mental health nurses, and none of the participants in this study held a postgraduate mental health qualification. Nurses begin their careers without specialist preparation for the relational and emotional demands of psychiatric care, as evidenced by the sample in this study (20 of 21 participants held only general nursing qualifications) and consistent with the documented absence of mandatory postgraduate entry requirements for mental health nursing roles across KSA facilities [17, 21]. Twenty of 21 nurses in this study held general nursing qualifications; only one possessed specialist mental health credentials. Second, clinical supervision, the structured reflective practice through which nurses internationally process emotional experiences, examine relational dynamics, and build professional resilience, is entirely absent across KSA mental health facilities [17]. Third, chronic staffing shortages create time pressures that prevent nurses from accessing available education and training, further limiting their capacity to develop the emotional coping resources necessary for sustainable practice [18–20].

Despite these converging risk factors, no previous study has examined nurses’ experiences of emotional labour when caring for women with psychosis in KSA [21, 22]. The aim of this study is therefore threefold: (1) to explore nurses’ understandings of psychosis and examine how these perceptions shape their embodied care experiences; (2) to investigate the specific emotional labour encountered when providing care to women with psychosis in KSA, including the dynamics of transference and countertransference; and (3) to identify the essential organisational and professional support structures required to sustain nurse wellbeing and ensure the quality of psychiatric care within the Saudi Arabian context.

Methods

Design and philosophical framework

This study employed a hermeneutic phenomenological design guided by the methodology of van Manen [23, 24]. The primary analytical lens was van Manen’s lifeworld existential of the lived body (corporeality), which examines how human beings inhabit their bodies relative to their experiences. In this context, the lived body illuminated how nurses physically and emotionally experienced the act of caring, particularly how their bodies registered exhaustion, tension, and distress. This was further enriched by other lifeworld existentials: the lived cyborg revealed the impact of workplace systems; the lived self-other explored relational experiences; the lived thing examined how resource limitations impacted embodied care; and lived time highlighted the consequences of under-staffing on the capacity to provide care.

Complementing this phenomenological approach, Emotional Labour Theory [9, 10] provided the conceptual framework for understanding the emotional demands of psychiatric nursing, specifically through the concepts of surface and deep acting. Furthermore, an Occupational Health Framework was integrated to contextualise these emotional demands within the broader landscape of workplace factors, such as staffing levels and organisational support, thereby positioning burnout prevention as an organisational obligation rather than an individual responsibility.

Setting and participants

The research was conducted at two major healthcare facilities in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia: Hospital A, a specialist mental health facility, and Hospital B, a general hospital featuring a dedicated psychiatric ward. A purposive sample of 21 female nurses participated in the study (Hospital A: n = 11; Hospital B: n = 10).

Data collection and analysis

Data were gathered through semi-structured in-depth interviews (the full interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1). The guide was informed by van Manen’s lifeworld existentials and emotional labour theory, and comprised four thematic areas: (1) nurses’ understandings of psychosis and its symptoms; (2) descriptions of a typical care interaction with a woman with psychosis; (3) emotional experiences and responses during care, including feelings of difficulty, satisfaction, fear, or connection; and (4) perceived support structures and their adequacy. Probing questions explored embodied reactions, relational dynamics, and the influence of the ward environment on care experience. lasting between 13 and 41 min. The variability in duration reflects the clinical reality of data collection in acute mental health settings: shorter interviews (13–20 min) occurred when staffing pressures required nurses to return to the ward; all participants were offered the opportunity to continue or return for a follow-up interview, and the brevity of some interviews therefore reflects organisational constraints rather than participant reluctance or data insufficiency. In van Manen’s hermeneutic phenomenological approach, analytic depth derives from the quality and richness of meaning units within interviews rather than from duration per se; even brief accounts yielded substantial phenomenological material through focused questioning on embodied experience. To accommodate participant preference and ensure depth of expression, 10 interviews were conducted in English (with the ten expatriate nurses at Hospital B, for whom English was the primary professional language) and 11 in Arabic; the latter were professionally translated and verified for accuracy. Data analysis followed van Manen’s [23] selective highlighting approach. Themes were identified and subsequently reviewed by research supervisors before being structurally analysed through the lived body existential to ensure a profound interpretation of the nurses’ experiences. Interviews were conducted between January and June 2017 at participants’ respective hospital sites, in a private room to ensure confidentiality. The researcher (NMA) conducted all interviews. Arabic transcripts were translated using a forward-back translation procedure: a bilingual translator produced an initial English version, which was then independently back-translated into Arabic by a second bilingual translator; discrepancies were reconciled by the research team before analysis proceeded. Data analysis was conducted by three researchers (AA, NMA, BMA) working independently before meeting to compare and reach consensus on coding and theme development. NVivo 12 (QSR International) was used to manage and organise the data. Data saturation was assessed iteratively: analysis of new transcripts was undertaken in parallel with ongoing data collection, and recruitment continued until no new themes or meaningful variations emerged across successive interviews, which occurred at n = 19; the final two interviews confirmed saturation.

Reflexivity

The lead researchers (AA and NMA) are Saudi female nurses with postgraduate training in mental health nursing research and prior clinical experience in psychiatric settings. This insider positioning facilitated rapport with participants and enriched interpretive sensitivity to the cultural and organisational pressures described [25]. At the same time, shared professional backgrounds risked confirmation of expected findings; to counteract this, the team maintained reflexive memos throughout data collection and analysis, documenting emerging assumptions and how these were challenged or refined. Interpretive decisions were regularly reviewed across the research team, and passages where the researchers’ language moved beyond participants’ own accounts (for example, the application of “stigma” to certain responses) were explicitly flagged as researcher interpretation rather than participant description.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was established through multiple strategies addressing Lincoln and Guba’s criteria. Credibility was supported by prolonged engagement with the clinical context (both interview researchers held prior psychiatric nursing experience), member checking (a sample of five participants reviewed their interpreted transcript summaries), and peer debriefing (regular team discussions interrogating interpretive moves). Dependability was addressed through an audit trail: all analytical decisions, code definitions, and theme revisions were documented in NVivo. Confirmability was supported by reflexive memoing (see above) and the retention of raw data, codes, and analytic memos for independent audit. Transferability is supported by thick description of the setting, sample, and context, enabling readers to judge applicability to other Saudi or similarly configured mental health nursing environments. The study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) framework.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health Research Committee, Institutional Review Board, King Fahad Medical City (IRB No. 17-127E), dated 02.05.2017, for both Hospital A and Hospital B. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. To ensure confidentiality, pseudonyms were assigned to participants, and all data were securely stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team.

Findings

Characteristics of participants

A total of 21 nurses participated in this study, representing two hospitals (A and B) and working across both inpatient and outpatient (OPD) mental health units. Participants ranged in age from 26 to 59 years, with varying levels of mental health nursing (MHN) experience, from 6 months to 31 years. The participants had an average age of 36.9 years and an average of 9.7 years of experience in mental health nursing. Regarding educational backgrounds, 13 nurses held diplomas while eight held bachelor’s degrees. Notably, 20 participants held general nursing qualifications, with only one possessing a specialist mental health qualification, although 19 had received some form of in-service mental health training.

The sample included both Arabic and non-Arabic nurses, reflecting the multicultural workforce within Saudi mental health services. Most participants held general nursing qualifications (Diploma, Bachelor, or Higher Diploma), while only a few had specialized mental health nursing qualifications.

Although the majority had received some form of in-service mental health training (comprising hospital orientation programmes, short courses on psychiatric medications, symptom recognition workshops, and/or mandatory mental health awareness modules, as opposed to formal postgraduate qualifications), there were notable variations in their knowledge of mental health principles, symptoms, psychosocial interventions, and medications, highlighting differences in preparation and professional development across the sample. Themes and sub-themes: nurses’ experiences of caring for women with psychosis in Saudi Arabia

Analysis of the qualitative data revealed one overarching theme: nurses’ experiences of providing care to women with psychosis in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), encompassing both their understandings and emotional responses. This overarching theme captures how nurses make sense of psychosis within their clinical practice while simultaneously navigating the emotional demands associated with care delivery.

Within this overarching theme, two interrelated themes were identified (Table 2). The first theme, understanding psychosis while providing care, reflects how nurses conceptualize psychosis, including variations between hospital settings and their perspectives on medication adherence and relapse. The second theme, emotional labour experienced when caring, highlights the emotional challenges nurses encounter, particularly in relation to staffing pressures and complex interpersonal dynamics such as transference and countertransference.

Together, these themes provide a comprehensive account of both the cognitive (understandings) and affective (emotional) dimensions of nurses’ experiences, illustrating the multifaceted nature of mental health nursing practice within the Saudi context. Themes and sub-themes

Theme 1: Understanding psychosis while providing care

This theme captures the cognitive dimension of nurses’ care experience: how they make sense of psychosis, what frameworks they draw on, and what gaps in understanding shape their clinical responses. It is defined here as the ways in which nurses conceptualise the nature and meaning of psychotic illness, including its symptoms, causes, trajectory, and treatability, and how those conceptualisations, whether sound or fragmentary, are enacted at the bedside. Analysis revealed that understandings differed markedly between the two hospital settings, shaped primarily by access to specialist education, in-service training, and the nature of nurses’ therapeutic role in each environment.

Sub theme 1. Hospital A nurses’ understandings

Hospital A nurses demonstrated limited and often fragmented understandings of psychosis. Afrah viewed psychosis as confusion of consciousness: ‘What I know about psychosis is that the consumer’s consciousness is different from usual; the consumer forgets some essential things, and she does not know how to wear her clothes, eat, or behave. She is not aware of reality, as if she was newly born’ (p. 1, lines 14–16). She then described her care routine: ‘We take care of a consumer from the morning…when she wakes up, we tell her to shower and eat [before] meeting the consultant. After that, she takes a break to pray and, in the afternoon, she mostly sleeps; that is usually the routine’ (p. 1, lines 21–24). This description reveals a predominantly custodial care orientation with limited therapeutic activity.

Fadwah did not initially recognise the term ‘psychosis’ and required the researcher’s explanation before she could respond. Despite this, she reflected a sound experiential understanding of caring for women with psychotic symptoms. Fadwah highlighted the limited number of courses available and the lack of time to attend: ‘The training and courses set [up] for the nurses are infrequent…[and] we have no time to attend. Most of us do not attend because of the lack of nurses. The number of nurses is not sufficient for taking care of thirty women [in the ward]. If two nurses attend [at once], it will cause trouble, which would be the nurse’s responsibility’ (p. 2, lines 63–67).

Saleha similarly noted time constraints: ‘I think that the nursing training provided in the hospital is…good. We have a day per week for training lectures, but the schedule and the lack of nurses can prevent us from attending such lectures. If we have the time, we will attend them’ (p. 2, lines 76–78). Rabab described training as ‘boring, as if they are explaining it on TV and someone is reading from a book’ (p. 5, lines 184–185). Majidah stressed reliance on self-directed learning, with knowledge gained through trial and error rather than evidence-based practice.

These accounts reveal a compounding structural gap: nurses who lack specialist education are denied access to even basic in-service training due to understaffing, resulting in care based on personal experience rather than evidence.

Sub theme 2. Hospital B nurses’ understandings

Hospital B nurses (predominantly expatriate, English-speaking) worked primarily in the OPD. Their role was limited to recording vital signs and initial assessment before sending women to their clinic session. Daisy, Samantha, and Anna described being unable to accompany women into clinical consultations, psychiatrists refused nurses’ attendance, women did not want nurses present (possibly due to distrust linked to language differences), and time was insufficient. Anna justified the limited engagement based on staff shortages: ‘We have a shortage of staff; our role as nurses here is [restricted to] taking the vital signs of the [consumers] and initial assessment’ (p. 2, lines 69–73).

These OPD nurses provided ‘physical care only’ and their understanding of women’s situations was ‘limited solely to their reading of diagnoses on the electronic system.’ This represents a systemic constraint on professional development, nurses who cannot observe, assess, or engage therapeutically with consumers cannot develop the advanced clinical understandings necessary for mental health nursing practice.

Sub theme 3. Understanding medication adherence and relapse

Nurses from both hospitals stressed the importance of medication adherence for preventing relapse. Several nurses recommended increased involvement in family psychoeducation to support adherence. However, hospital policies limiting nurses’ communication with families constrained this domain of practice. Nurses recommended that managers remove such restrictions to facilitate family education both during hospitalisation and after discharge.

Theme 2: Emotional labour experienced when caring

Sub theme 1. Emotional experiences related to staffing concerns

The shortage of nursing staff emerged as a primary source of emotional distress. Sophia from Hospital B articulated: the staffing situation created a constant sense of being overwhelmed, unable to provide the quality of care she aspired to deliver. Nurses described feeling guilty about the care they could not provide, frustrated by organisational indifference to their concerns, and exhausted by the relentless demands of under-resourced wards.

The interrelationship between staffing shortages and emotional labour was cyclical: understaffing increased workload, increased workload increased emotional exhaustion, emotional exhaustion increased sick leave, and increased sick leave further exacerbated understaffing. This cycle was compounded by the absence of food facilities, with nurses experiencing physical hunger alongside emotional exhaustion, a convergence of bodily and emotional depletion that the lived body existential powerfully illuminated.

Sub theme 2. Experiencing transference and countertransference

Nurses described complex emotional reactions triggered by their work with women with psychosis. These included:

Sympathy-driven over-caring, several nurses described developing strong emotional connections with women in their care that extended beyond therapeutic boundaries. They ‘sympathised with, and tended to over-care for, the women.’ While sympathy and compassion are essential qualities, unmanaged sympathetic over-engagement risks professional boundary violations, emotional exhaustion, and care decisions driven by the nurse’s emotional needs rather than the consumer’s therapeutic goals.

Fear and unease, some nurses described fear when confronted with acutely psychotic behavior, particularly aggressive or unpredictable conduct. This fear, often unprocessed due to the absence of debriefing structures, could lead to avoidance behaviours that further diminished therapeutic engagement.

Sadness and helplessness, nurses expressed sadness about the women’s circumstances, their family rejection, their confinement in non-therapeutic environments, and their limited recovery prospects. This sadness was compounded by feelings of helplessness: nurses knew what needed to change (healing spaces, better education, more staff) but felt unable to effect change. As the thesis documented: nurses felt ‘helpless to address the women’s poor position.’

Annoyance and stigmatising responses, Rabab from Hospital A expressed annoyance when hearing a woman hallucinating. While nurses did not use the term ‘stigma,’ the researcher interpreted some responses as reflecting stigmatising attitudes, an inability to relate to consumers with mental illness driven by lack of knowledge and skills. These stigmatising responses, when left unexamined, compromise person-centred care and nurse–consumer relationships.

The most consequential finding of this study is that these complex emotional experiences occurred in an environment in which participants reported a complete absence of structured professional support. As documented by Alyahya et al. [17], the absence of clinical supervision is a systemic issue across the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, representing a major concern for the nursing workforce. Without access to formal clinical supervision, the participants in this study lacked a dedicated forum to process their emotional reactions to distressing clinical events or to critically examine the intricate dynamics of transference and countertransference. Furthermore, this systemic void prevented nurses from reflecting on how their personal beliefs and cultural backgrounds influenced the care they provided, thereby hindering the development of advanced relational and therapeutic communication skills. In the absence of such structures, nurses were also deprived of essential peer support and validation, both of which are critical components for building the professional resilience necessary to navigate the emotional labour inherent in psychiatric care.

The combination of emotional demands with absent support structures creates conditions that international evidence identifies as high-risk for burnout [7]. Alenezi [27] specifically examined the effects of a burnout prevention programme on KSA mental health nurses, confirming the relevance of burnout as a concern in this context.

Discussion

The embodied experience of under-supported practice

The findings reveal a practice environment where, across both settings, participants described providing emotionally demanding care without adequate specialist preparation, professional support structures, or organisational resources that international evidence identifies as essential for sustainable mental health nursing practice. While the two hospitals differed in their structures and patient populations, nurses in both settings consistently reported the same core deficits. The lived body existential powerfully captures this reality: nurses inhabited bodies that were physically exhausted (from hunger and overwork), emotionally depleted (from unprocessed emotional labour), and professionally constrained (by limited knowledge and organisational barriers).

This embodied experience of under-supported practice extends the work of Thompson et al. [12], who examined inpatient nursing staff caring for young people with early psychosis and identified the need for structured support. It also extends Odeyemi et al. [11], who identified factors affecting mental health nurses working with consumers with first-episode psychosis. However, the KSA context reveals a more extreme version of these challenges: the complete absence (rather than inadequacy) of clinical supervision, the workforce composition (general rather than specialist nurses), and the cultural and linguistic complexities documented in this research.

Limited understanding as a source of emotional distress

The relationship between limited psychotic illness knowledge and emotional distress was bidirectional [25, 26, 28–30]. Nurses with limited understanding of psychosis were more likely to experience fear, annoyance, and avoidance when confronted with psychotic behaviours they could not understand or manage [31–33]. These emotional reactions then further limited their willingness to engage therapeutically, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of knowledge deficit and emotional withdrawal.

Hospital A nurses showed only limited understandings of psychosis, which might be linked to their lower educational qualification levels. Some revealed that they had no prior mental health training before beginning their careers. A further factor was that in-service training and education was limited, being either repetitive or inaccessible due to nursing staff shortages. Academic institutions must be the first to address this limited understanding by offering additional mental health education and training for undergraduate student nurses.

Transference and countertransference in the KSA context

The experiences of transference and countertransference documented in this study carry particular significance given the cultural context. It should be noted that nurses were not explicitly asked about transference or countertransference using those terms. Rather, the interview guide included questions about emotional reactions to patients, difficult relationships, and feelings triggered by care encounters. The constructs of transference and countertransference represent researcher-level interpretations applied analytically to the narratives, and are presented as such throughout this discussion. Nurses from diverse cultural backgrounds (Saudi, Filipino, Indian, and others) brought different cultural frameworks for understanding mental illness, emotional expression, and therapeutic boundaries [34, 35]. The intersection of these diverse cultural backgrounds with the emotionally charged dynamics of psychiatric care created complex relational landscapes that no nurse in this study had been specifically prepared to navigate.

Nurses are likely to develop better ways to deal with transference and countertransference when they are encouraged to develop resilience and offered the tools and training to develop coping mechanisms [36, 37]. Educating nurses to effectively communicate and build relationships based on trust and respect may also facilitate improvements in care that help overcome nurses’ own negative feelings. Mental health facilities would be well advised to consider enhancing the psychological support offered to nurses and both advocating for and promoting self-care among nurses.

From individual resilience to organisational responsibility

A critical contribution of this study is the repositioning of emotional labour management from an individual responsibility to an organisational obligation. The findings demonstrate that nurse emotional distress was not caused by individual deficiencies but by systemic failures: inadequate education, absent supervision, chronic understaffing, and non-therapeutic environments. Resilience-building programmes, while valuable, are insufficient without concurrent organisational change.

This aligns with Shirey’s [38] leadership practices for healthy work environments and with the broader occupational health literature that identifies organisational factors as the primary determinants of workplace wellbeing.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study necessitate several critical shifts in clinical practice and policy within the Saudi Arabian mental health sector. Most urgently, there must be an immediate establishment of clinical supervision as a mandatory component of nursing practice; as Alyahya et al. [17] suggests, collegial supervision should serve as the minimum standard to provide nurses with essential reflective space. To address acute emotional distress, healthcare facilities must implement formal post-incident debriefing protocols following aggressive incidents or the use of restrictive practices, ensuring nurses can process these events safely [39].

Furthermore, organisational changes are required to ensure protected time for education and training, which fundamentally depends on maintaining staffing levels sufficient to release nurses from clinical duties without compromising patient safety. Beyond professional development, the provision of accessible psychological support services and integrated resilience-building programmes is vital for long-term workforce sustainability. On a fundamental level, basic self-care promotion, including guaranteed access to adequate food, rest facilities, and welfare provisions, must be prioritised to support the physical “lived body” of the nurse. Finally, given the diverse workforce in KSA, the implementation of cultural orientation programmes for expatriate nurses is essential to address the unique emotional and relational dimensions of providing cross-cultural psychiatric care in the Kingdom.

Implications for policy

To ensure the sustainability of the mental health workforce and align with the strategic goals of Saudi Vision 2030 [40, 41], several high-level policy interventions are required. Central to this transformation is the mandating of clinical supervision as a national standard for all mental health nursing practices, providing a regulated framework for professional reflection and emotional support. This must be accompanied by the establishment and enforcement of minimum staffing ratios to alleviate the chronic pressures that currently hinder quality care and nurse wellbeing.

Furthermore, to address the specialist knowledge gap, there is an urgent need for the development of postgraduate mental health nursing programmes within the Kingdom, ensuring that future nurses are equipped with the necessary clinical and relational competencies. On an organisational level, investment must be directed toward nurse welfare infrastructure, including the provision of adequate food, on-site childcare, and dedicated rest facilities, to support the physical and emotional “lived body” of the nursing staff. Finally, the integration of burnout monitoring and prevention into organisational performance frameworks will ensure that nurse mental health is no longer treated as an individual concern, but as a key indicator of institutional success and care quality.

Implications for education

To bridge the current gap in specialist preparation, significant reforms are required within the nursing education landscape in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. First, there is an urgent need to expand specialist mental health nursing contentwithin undergraduate curricula, ensuring that all nursing students gain a foundational understanding of psychiatric care before entering the workforce. Education must move beyond technical skills to include intensive training in emotional intelligence and self-awareness, which are essential for managing the high emotional labour of the field.

Furthermore, curricula should explicitly address the psychodynamic phenomena of transference and countertransference, providing nurses with the theoretical tools to navigate complex therapeutic relationships safely. This should be supported by the formal development of reflective practice skills, enabling students to critically evaluate their emotional responses to clinical encounters. Finally, to ensure the long-term success of professional support structures, education must include specific preparation for clinical supervision, training nurses both in how to effectively receive supervision as a practitioner and, eventually, how to provide it as a clinical leader.

Limitations

Two-site study in Riyadh; may not represent all KSA settings. Phenomenological methodology does not seek generalisability. Interview duration variability due to staffing constraints. The study explored emotional labour through nurses’ accounts rather than direct observation or validated instruments, future research could complement these qualitative findings with quantitative burnout and emotional labour measures.

Conclusion

This study reveals that KSA mental health nurses caring for women with psychosis experience substantial emotional labour characterised by sadness, fear, frustration, helplessness, sympathy-driven over-caring, and unrecognised transference and countertransference dynamics, all without access to clinical supervision, debriefing, or structured professional support. The convergence of limited specialist education, chronic staff shortages, cultural and linguistic complexity, and absent clinical supervision creates conditions of heightened burnout risk that threaten both nurse wellbeing and care quality. Addressing these challenges requires a fundamental shift from expecting individual resilience to establishing organisational responsibility for supportive practice environments. The establishment of clinical supervision, protected time for education, adequate staffing, and psychological support services are not optional enhancements but essential prerequisites for safe, effective, and sustainable mental health nursing care in KSA. These findings directly inform the healthcare transformation agenda of Saudi Vision 2030 and provide an empirical basis for establishing the professional support infrastructure needed by the KSA mental health nursing workforce.
