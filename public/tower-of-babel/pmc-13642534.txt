Euler‐Top Gaussian Modes: Structured Beams From Quadratic Angular Momentum Dynamics
Authors: Mark R Dennis, Kerr Maxwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe propose a new family of paraxial Gaussian modes, the Euler‐top Gaussian modes. Motivated by SU(2) angular momentum spin algebra, these modes are defined as eigenfunctions of a natural quadratic combination of spin‐like Fradkin–Stokes parameters K=L2−M+2. Together with Hermite–Gaussian, Laguerre–Gaussian and Ince–Gaussian modes, these new modes complete the natural (quadratic) families of Gaussian eigenmodes, up to SU(2) transformations. Euler‐top Gaussian modes with positive eigenvalues have a real Laguerre–Gaussian appearance, and those with negative eigenvalues have a coupled Hermite–Gaussian appearance with fourfold symmetry. Certain other modes quantise a separatrix state between the two regimes. Their structure is motivated by paths on the Gaussian ray‐orbital Poincaré sphere, analogous to the polhode curves describing the angular momentum of an Euler top, that is, a classical rigid body with three different axes of inertia. Various properties of these new modes are considered, including their analogy to Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick modes from nuclear physics.

Introduction

Structured light has developed over the past few decades at the intersection of optical engineering, wave mechanics and topological optics [1, 2]. Families of paraxial scalar light beam wavefunctions are designed to exhibit topological and geometric structures, such as nodal lines and vortices; they are also eigenfunctions of well‐defined optical operators such as angular momentum [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The most familiar examples are structured Gaussian modes where solutions of the paraxial wave equation with Gaussian envelope are known to separate in exactly three transverse 2D orthogonal coordinate systems [8, 9]: Hermite–Gaussian (HG) modes in Cartesian coordinates x,y [4, 10], Laguerre–Gaussian (LG) modes in polar coordinates r,ϕ [3, 4] and Ince Gaussian (IG) modes in elliptic coordinates [11, 12].

Here we introduce a new mode family of structured Gaussian modes, motivated not by separation of variables, but by angular momentum algebra. We refer to these as ‘Euler‐top Gaussian’ (EG) modes because their definition is closely related to the classical dynamics of the Euler top: a rotating classical body whose three principal moments of inertia are all distinct [13, 14]. Because all paraxial structured Gaussian modes of fixed mode order share the same beam quality and Gouy phase, linear optical transformations map between them; algebraically, they form a finite‐dimensional family closed under quadratic optical observables/operators. Although not separable in a transverse coordinate system, the EG modes are exact eigenfunctions of a natural family of optical operators, and, up to a straightforward generalisation, complete the quadratic algebraic classification of Gaussian modes of fixed beam quality.

The explicit definition of the EG modes relies on the formal analogy between Gaussian modes, the 2D quantum harmonic oscillator and quantum spin states. These are all eigenfunctions of sets of angular momentum‐like operators (satisfying su(2) angular momentum commutation relations), underpinned by various Poincaré sphere‐like structures. For Gaussian modes, the sphere here does not represent polarisation, but rather the reduced phase space of the Hamiltonian ray dynamics, which, following the literature [12, 15, 16, 17, 18], we call the ray‐orbital sphere. We now summarise this picture, and define the EG modes in relation to the LG, HG and IG mode families.

Each structured Gaussian mode has a mode order N=0,1,2,… reflecting the fact that it is an eigenfunction of the beam quality.

The eigenvalue corresponding to each mode is proportional to N+1, that is, its well‐defined Gouy phase. For each N, the modes are N+1‐fold degenerate; the LG, HG and IG (and EG) basis sets are mutually related by inter‐basis expansions [4, 11, 12, 15].

The beam quality M2 is twice the 2D harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian HHO=Hx+Hy; as is well known, the structured Gaussian modes are equivalent to 2D quantum harmonic oscillator energy eigenstates [4, 6]. We note that there is some ambiguity in the notation: quadratic combinations of x,y,px,py can represent observables in 4D phase space, or operators acting on transverse beam amplitude wave packets. We will attempt to keep the meaning clear from context.

The N+1‐fold degenerate eigenspaces of the 2D quantum harmonic oscillator, correspond to the spin‐j irreducible representations of SU(2) with N=2j. This is the ‘Schwinger oscillator model’ [19], and we evidently have 12M22=HHO2=4J2, where J2 is the corresponding ‘total spin’ Casimir operator [1, 15, 16, 17].

This connection underpins the ‘Gaussian mode sphere’ [1, 4, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. LG HG, and generalised Laguerre–Hermite Gaussian (GG) modes (with mode order N=2j) are related by SU(2) rotations, with each N+1‐dimensional mode set represented by a point on the unit sphere. The N=1 j=12 ‘Poincaré sphere equivalent’ relates first‐order Gaussian modes, the Bloch sphere, and the standard Poincaré sphere (parametrising the classical 2D harmonic oscillator) [4, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20].

The classical ray‐orbital sphere itself underpins the Gaussian mode sphere. Each point on this ray‐orbital sphere is an elliptic orbit of rays crossing the focal plane with position x,y and direction px,py [15, 16, 17]. Its 1,2,3 directions correspond to angular momentum‐like Stokes–Fradkin parameters [12, 15, 16, 17, 18].

These are quadratic functions of optical phase space, analogous to half the Stokes parameters for the standard Poincaré sphere. As phase space observables (not operators), they satisfy the relation HHO2=M+2+M×2+L2, and they form a su(2) Poisson algebra, with Poisson brackets M+,M×=2L, M×,L=2M+, L,M+=2M× [15, 16, 17].

When M+,M×,L are considered as operators, each Gaussian mode can be considered as built up from these ellipses, on parametrised Poincaré paths on the ray‐orbital sphere [15, 16, 17]. This process of constructing Gaussian modes from ellipses of rays can be considered as a ‘quantisation’ [25] of this Poincaré path of ray‐orbital ellipses.

To avoid confusion, we will distinguish the dynamical, physical Stokes–Fradkin triplet M+,M×,L from the triplet of algebraic angular momentum‐like operators J=J1,J2,J3, although they have similar roles (and algebraically differ by a factor of 2). The latter triplet satisfies the usual angular momentum commutation relations: Jj,Jk=iεijkJi, for i,j,k=1,2,3. We refer to J1,J2,J3 as ‘pseudo‐AM’ operators rather than AM (angular momentum) operators: they encode the spin‐like algebra of the quadratic Gaussian structure, not physical angular momentum in real space (except for L and J3).

In this notation, LG modes LGℓ,p(r,ϕ) are eigenfunctions of the AM operator 2J3 with eigenvalues ℓ, the azimuthal quantum number p=12(N−|ℓ|) is the radial quantum number, corresponding to L. The HG modes HGm,n(x,y) with quantum numbers m,n in x,y, are eigenfunctions of 2J1, corresponding to M+, with eigenvalues m−n, and their 45° rotation to 2J2 and M×. Their eigenvalues are μ=−N,−N+2,…,N, with Poincaré paths on the ray‐orbital sphere that are circles centred on the 3, 1 and 2 axes respectively, enclosing proportional areas.

The IG modes arise slightly differently, determined by elliptic separability. The underlying dynamical quantity is the nonlinear combination I=L2+f2M+, where f≥0 is the focal parameter of the elliptic coordinate system. The IG modes are therefore eigenfunctions of the corresponding Ince operator [9, 12, 26],

as well as eigenfunctions of HHO=12M2 with eigenvalues N+1. As f→0, they become (real) LG‐like, and as f→∞, HG‐like. On the ray‐orbital sphere, Poincaré paths are the contours of the observable I. Geometrically, these correspond to the intersections of the sphere with an off‐centre cylinder [12, 26]. Due to the nonlinearity of I, the eigenvalues ιμ of I are nontrivial, but still labelled by μ, μ=−N,−N+2,…,N [12].

We are now in a position to define the Euler‐top Gaussian modes. They correspond to the observable andare eigenfunctions of the operator.

In 4D phase space, K involves a quadratic combination of x,y,px,py, and is not separable. In the spin analogy, Kˆ is quadratic in the Ji operators, so possesses similar simple but nontrivial algebraic structure as the Ince operator: algebraically tractable in terms of ladder operators, but more subtle than eigenfunctions of linear combinations of J1,J2,J3 such as LG, HG and GG modes.

The EG modes, as simultaneous eigenfunctions of the commuting operators HHO and Kˆ, have many attractive properties, and have well‐defined mode order N and Gouy phase. The operator Kˆ, similar to the Ince operator Iˆ, is a nonlinear quadratic combination of components of J, with eigenfunctions and eigenvalues which are algebraically tractable but has a more subtle structure than those of the spin‐like linear combinations of J underlying LG, HG and GG modes. In the ray‐orbital Poincaré sphere picture, their Poincaré paths are contours of K=L2−M+2: pairs of closed curves analogous to the precessing motion of the angular velocity vector of the Euler top [13, 14], which are called polhodes in Poinsot's classical theory [27].

In the next section, we develop an understanding of EG modes through polhode Poincaré paths on the ray‐orbital sphere, by analogy with those of the LG, HG, and IG modes, and through their connection with the Euler top. We then consider the spin algebra representation of Kˆ to calculate the EG modes directly, providing insight into their geometric and physical properties. We conclude with a brief discussion of their wider significance in optics and as a model wave‐mechanical system.

Gaussian Ray‐Orbital Sphere Geometry

We now motivate the Euler‐top Gaussian (EG) modes by comparing them with the separable HG, LG and IG families on the ray‐orbital Poincaré sphere. Each mode family is associated with a phase space observable, whose contours provide the modes' Poincaré path. Every mode is determined by one or more curves on ray‐orbital sphere, tracing out a family of elliptic ray orbits whose quantisation gives the mode [15, 16, 17, 18]. Although we focus here on the LG and HG modes, determined by L and M+ (and J3 and J1), respectively, any linear superposition of L,M+,M× determines a set of GG modes [1, 4, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24], which we emphasise less in this work as these are accessed by SU(2) rotations from the LG and HG mode sets. Readers uninterested in the elliptic ray orbit picture may largely omit this section.

From quantisation of the rays [16, 17, 18], the ray‐orbital sphere naturally has radius N+1, and each point on this sphere corresponds to an ‘elliptic orbit’ Gaussian coherent state ϒN,e(r) analogous to a spin coherent state. Each of these states is parametrised by a Jones‐like complex vector e=q+ip with |e|2=N+1. ϒN,e(r) itself is a smooth (integral) superposition of fundamental Gaussian beams with transverse centre (position) Re{eexp(−iτ)} and tilt (momentum) Im{eexp(−iτ)}, as τ varies from 0 to 2π, and weighted by amplitudes exp(i(N+1)τ). It does not play a fundamental role in our construction, but is included here for completeness.

A structured Gaussian mode of order N is constructed from a closed Poincaré path, which is a contour of an arbitrary function FM+,M×,L on the ray‐orbital sphere. Along this path, the coherent states ϒN,e(r) are smoothly superposed, weighted by a Pancharatnam–Berry phase proportional to the solid angle enclosed by the path [28]. This picture provides a geometric quantisation of the integrable invariant torus in the four‐dimensional phase space of the 2D harmonic oscillator [29, 30].

When Fˆ=2a1J1+a2J2+a3J3=2a⋅J for real unit 3‐vector a, the corresponding LG, HG and GG modes can be constructed directly from the spin coherent state Jones vector [17, 18] and quantisation is exact [31]. When FM+,M×,L is a higher‐order polynomial or a more general function, there is not such a clear connection in general between the modes and the contours of F as Poincaré paths.

Nevertheless, choices of F which are quadratic, such as I and K underlying the IG and EG mode families, respectively, have a simple enough contour structure to be identified with the exact eigenstructure of the corresponding Fˆ. In all these cases, the coherent state superposition helps the understanding of the wave structure: each mode is associated with a one‐parameter family of ellipses on the ray‐orbital sphere encoding the symmetry of the corresponding observable F. This provides a unified interpretation for the LG, HG, GG, IG and the new EG family of structured Gaussian modes. Up to SU(2) rotation, combinations of I and K generate the whole quadratic algebra of invariants of Gaussian modes [32].

LG, HG and GG Modes

For LG modes (HG modes), the operator Fˆ is 2J3 2J1. The Poincaré paths are contours of L M+: coaxial circles with axis along the 3‐ (1‐)axis, with latitudes set at μ/(N+2) from Ωμ,N. For LG modes, μ=ℓ (i.e., eigenvalue of 2J3, which is the usual azimuthal quantum number); for HG modes, μ=m−n (i.e., eigenvalues of 2J1).

Circular contours in the 1,2‐ and 2,3‐ planes give the LG and HG Poincaré paths shown in Figure 2a. Their projections into the 1,2‐plane, under a natural squaring conformal transformation [16], are related to the caustics of the corresponding ellipse families and modes, shown in Figure 2b. HG paths are straight lines in the 1,2‐plane, and the ellipse family caustics are rectangular (reflecting Cartesian separability). LG paths are circles, and the families of ellipses are enclosed by concentric circular caustics, reflecting polar separability.

When F is alternatively defined via a linear combination F=a⋅M+,M×,L, for real vector a, the Poincaré paths are circles with axis a subtending the same Ωμ,N areas as LG and HG modes [17, 18]. The GG modes are the eigenfunctions of the corresponding operator 2J⋅a, but outside the 1,2,3 axes, are not separable [1, 4, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. They can nonetheless be expressed as linear superpositions of LG or HG modes weighted by appropriate matrix elements of the SU(2) rotation; their ellipse families represent some arbitrary projection of the phase space torus [15, 17].

Squared LG and HG Modes

As an eigenfunction of 2J3, each complex LG mode LGℓ,p(r,ϕ) contains a complex phase term exp(iℓϕ). Real LG modes [10], which we here call squared LG modes, are proportional to cosℓϕ and sinℓϕ, and are eigenfunctions of the degenerate, quadratic J32. They have two circular Poincaré paths with equal and opposite ℓ2=μ2; J32 therefore couples the LG modes with labels N,±μ. The associated ray ellipse orbits have identical shapes but opposite orientations (analogous to left‐and right‐handed elliptic polarisations); the resultant modes are real‐valued interference patterns, as shown in Figure 2b, with fourfold rotational symmetry. Furthermore, the eigenvalues ℓ2 are no longer directly related to the areas Ωℓ,N.

Similarly, squared HG modes are eigenfunctions of the quadratic operator J12. They are of even and odd types, proportional to Hm(x)Hn(y)±Hn(x)Hm(y); squared HG modes couple the standard HG modes labelled by N,±μ. Again, they can be constructed from pairs of Poincaré paths, now along the 1‐axis. The squared HG modes are real‐valued, and have nodal patterns which are, approximately, circles and radial lines more commonly associated with real‐valued LG modes, as can be seen in Figure 2b.

IG Modes

As discussed above, the IG modes are separated in elliptic coordinates, and thus do not have the simple spin‐like SU(2) structure characterising the LG, HG and GG modes. The underlying Ince operator Iˆ in Equation (3) is a mixture of linear and quadratic pseudo‐AM operators. As Iˆ depends on the interfocal parameter f, the IG modes are in fact a 1‐parameter family interpolating between HG modes (f→∞) and real squared LG modes (f=0).

On the ray‐orbital sphere, the Poincaré paths are the contours of F=I=L2+fM+, which are intersections of the sphere with cylinders parallel to but offset from the 3‐axis. When f is small and eigenvalues ι of Iˆ are large, IG modes are squared LG‐like, corresponding to those intersections comprised of two loops; in contrast, in the HG‐like regime (not squared HG), there is a single loop intersection [12]. A notable feature of IG modes is a topological transition [26]: in between the two regimes, the contours of F have a self‐intersection, where the sphere is tangent to the cylinder.

The classical dynamics of I are those of the ‘Ince oscillator’: the phase portrait of a nonlinear pendulum wrapped around the sphere [12, 26]. The LG regime has two curves and is a ‘rotating’ pendulum orbit; the modes are perturbations of squared LG modes. The HG regime (one curve) is a ‘oscillating’ pendulum orbits, and the modes are perturbations of regular HG modes. The azimuth‐doubling inherent in the ray Poincaré construction implies that the IG Poincaré paths, displaced towards the +1‐direction, possess the characteristic π‐rotational symmetry of elliptic coordinates in the transverse plane.

However, due to nonlinearity, the eigenvalues of Iˆ do not correspond to the quantised areas subtended by contours of I [12]. An example of such contours for N=2 is shown in Figure 2a: as I decreases, pairs of LG‐like contours merge at a saddle point in the 1‐direction to a single HG‐like contour. The bottom row of Figure 2b shows the corresponding IG modes, with a reasonable qualitative match to the ellipse families from the contours.

EG Modes

The observable F=K=L2−M+2 is a quadratic form. On the ray‐orbital sphere, the 1‐ and 3‐axes are weighted equally but with opposite sign, and the 2‐axis is neutral. When a multiple of the constant HHO2=M+2+M×2+L2 is added to K, the contours correspond to the intersections of the sphere with a harmonically balanced ellipsoid.

This corresponds exactly to the interpretation of the path of the angular velocity vector of an Euler top in its body frame. The resulting polhode curves are found from the intersection of the energy sphere with the inertia ellipsoid [13, 14, 27]. The 3‐axis (1‐axis) corresponds to the largest (smallest) moment of inertia, and the 2‐axis to the intermediate, unstable axis. Polhodes are not perfect circles, giving the classical ‘wobble’ around pure circular precession about the stable axes. These polhode curves on the sphere, and corresponding ellipse families, are shown in Figure 3.

Each polhode pair corresponds to a family of ellipses along the Poincaré path: paths around the 3‐axis represent an LG‐like ‘squircle’ geometry (two upper insets), and those around the 1‐axis a HG‐like ‘Maltese cross’ (two middle insets). The ellipse family corresponding to the separatrix path sits between the characteristic squircle and maltese cross patterns (bottom inset).

Therefore K corresponds to a parameter‐free, balanced quadrupolar dynamical invariant encoding this anisotropic inertia structure. In 1,2,3‐space, the K contours have the dihedral symmetry of an ellipsoid, together with an inversion symmetry ±K when 1↔3 axes are exchanged.

When K>0, the loops lie in the upper and lower 3‐hemispheres around the 3‐axis. In this squared LG regime, these two polhodes on the ray‐orbital sphere correspond to ellipse families with nearly circular caustics. However, they are not squared LG functions: the polhode ‘wobble’ gives ellipse families with a fourfold symmetric ‘squircle’ structure [33, 34]. This suggests EG modes with positive eigenvalues κ>0 resemble squared LG modes, but deformed. The polhode symmetry endows them with a fourfold rotational symmetry, reflected in the plots in Figure 1.

For K<0, the polhode loops lie in the ±1‐directions. Each polhode loop gives an ellipse family bounded by a distorted rectangle (the distortion again due to the ‘wobble’). The net ‘Maltese cross’ structure has fourfold symmetry, again characteristic of azimuth doubling from the near‐elliptical polhodes around the 1 axis. This suggests EG modes with negative eigenvalue κ<0 are perturbations of squared HG modes, again reflected in the plots in Figure 1.

When K=0, the ellipsoid becomes tangent to the sphere at saddle points on the ±2‐axes, corresponding to meetings of a four‐arc separatrix: the net contour is a pair of geodesic circles intersecting on the ±2‐axis, and equidistant from the 1,3‐axes. In the classical intermediate axis theorem (also known as Poinsot instability, the ‘tennis racket theorem’ or the Dzhanibekov effect), rotation about the 2‐axis degenerates into a rotation about the 1‐ or 3‐axis. This reflects the instability of this separatrix contour, which for the EG modes is a topological transition between squared HG‐like and squared LG‐like regimes.

The balancing in K between L2 and M+2 guarantees that the separatrix occurs symmetrically at K=0. The ellipse family whose Poincaré path is the separatrix contour, shown in the lower inset of Figure 3, is a transitional pattern interpolating between the squircle‐like and cross‐like caustics characteristic of the two polhode regimes, again with fourfold symmetry. This transitional state is realised by EG modes with even N and κN,0=0, visible in the N=2,4 cases in Figure 1.

A detailed semiclassical analysis of the EG polhode caustics and coherent state superposition, analogous to that of IG rays [12], requires elaborate elliptic integrals and is outside the scope of this paper. As K is quadratic, the exact eigenvalues of Kˆ do not correspond to quantised polhode areas.

However, such an analysis is unnecessary to calculate the EG modes themselves. The EG operator Kˆ may be analysed directly using quantum spin algebra, yielding both κN,μ eigenvalues and coefficients for their expression as LG or HG superpositions (like the nonseparable GG mode). This will be considered in the next section.

On the ray‐orbital sphere, the Poincaré paths of the LG, HG, IG and EG modes are all the intersections of the sphere with quadric surfaces, aligned with the 1,2,3‐axes. In the 1,2‐plane, the Ince paths are off‐centre circles, mapping to ellipses and hyperbolas [12], but in the 1,3‐plane they are parabolas, reminiscent of the ray structure of Airy beams. Construction and Description of EG Modes

In this section, we construct EG modes as superpositions of LG modes. As with every Gaussian mode family, they are a set of eigenstates of an appropriate operator Fˆ=F2J1,2J2,2J3 corresponding to a function of Fradkin–Stokes parameters FM+,M×,L. Thus in the quantum spin language, the EG modes are eigenstates of the SU(2) Casimir operator J2, whose eigenfunctions are N+1‐fold degenerate for N=2j=0,1,2,…. The operator Kˆ in Equation (4) commutes with J2, and its eigenfunctions provide a basis for each of the N+1‐dimensional Gaussian mode spaces.

We choose LG modes as quantum algebra is more familiar in terms of eigenfunctions of the 3‐direction, though the same construction using a basis of HG modes (or, indeed, GG modes) is equivalent. Our derivation, though complete, will not describe all the algebraic steps in detail; these can easily be filled in using standard quantum spin algebra, whose background and techniques are described in standard textbooks [35, 36]. In the quantum spin algebra, the spin component labels in the 3‐direction are m=−j,−j+1,…,j. In optics language, the role of m is taken by the angular momentum number ℓ of the LG modes, with m=12ℓ.

The symmetries of K ensure the eigenmodes of Kˆ inherit features of the polhode contours. The 1↔3,±K symmetry ensures that positive and negative eigenvalues are paired, κN,μ=−κN,−μ, with even N having a zero eigenvalue κN,0=0 corresponding to the separatrix orbit.

For odd N, the N+1‐dimensional space splits into two independent but identical blocks because the Δℓ=±4 selection rule preserves the parity of ℓ/2=m. Each block contains (N+1)/2 states. As a result, in this case, every eigenvalue of Kˆ is twofold degenerate, corresponding to a Kramers‐like doubling arising from the ℓ=±N parity sectors of the Gaussian modes (rather than from time‐reversal symmetry of half‐integer spins in standard quantum mechanics [35, 36]). The higher, odd N HG‐like EG modes tend to have a checkerboard array of vortices close to the beam axis, as is visible in Figure 1.

Odd‐N EG modes necessarily carry nonzero orbital angular momentum. Near the beam axis, their intensity patterns form approximately square arrays of vortices reflecting the fourfold symmetry of the ellipse families from polhode Poincaré paths; further from the axis, the patterns become more intricate combinations of squircle‐like and cross‐like features. The orbital angular momentum and vortex circulations are reversed in the two Kramers‐like families of EG modes.

A distinctive feature of all the EG modes is that they inherit a fourfold azimuthal symmetry from the Δm=±2 coupling in the K matrix, geometrically related to the twofold dihedral symmetry of the polhode curves on the ray‐orbital sphere. This symmetry becomes especially transparent when the modes are plotted as functions of ϕ/2 rather than ϕ, as in Figure 5. The even‐N modes have nodal lines along the 1‐ and 2‐ axes, or along approximately elliptical or hyperbolic arcs; in addition, the odd‐N modes have rows of vortices of alternating sign (from the vortex arrays). This ‘half‐azimuth’ viewpoint gives a clear visual interpretation of how EG modes arise from quantising the classical polhodes curves, providing a natural hybrid of the real focal plane and ray‐orbital sphere representations in which their underlying symmetry becomes manifest.

The paraxial propagation of EG modes is straightforward, and similar to other structured Gaussian modes [16, 17]. As eigenfunctions of M2, they spread hyperbolically but with self‐similar intensity patterns, acquiring a Gouy phase of N+1 and, if N is odd, other phase terms consistent with conservation of orbital angular momentum.

Discussion and Outlook

We have introduced and described the Euler‐top Gaussian (EG) modes, a new family of structured Gaussian beams obtained from the simple quadratic operator Kˆ=4J32−J12. Unlike the well‐known HG, LG, and IG mode families, which arise from separation of variables in Cartesian, cylindrical, and elliptic coordinates, the EG modes are motivated algebraically from the operator structure of the 2D harmonic oscillator. Their amplitude structure reflects the quantisation of polhode curves of the Euler top: LG‐like (HG‐like) modes correspond to the stable 3‐axis (1‐axis) rotations and the κ=0 modes to the four‐branch separatrix associated with the intermediate‐axis instability.

In the semiclassical ray‐orbital sphere picture of structured Gaussian modes, each mode is a smooth superposition of SU(2) coherent states lying along a Poincaré path, weighted by the geometric phase. Gaussian mode families correspond to closed contours of functions FM+,M×,L on the Gaussian ray‐orbital sphere. Linear functions of the corresponding pseudo‐AM operators yield the LG, HG and GG modes (with quantised solid angle); the Ince operator Iˆ provides a more complicated, offset cylindrical quadratic. The EG operator Kˆ is the simplest balanced quadratic combination of Fradkin–Stokes pseudospin operators, and produces the most symmetric nonlinear family of modes, also realising separatrix states at the topological transition.

Among polynomial functions of the Stokes–Fradkin pseudo‐AM SU(2) generators, quadratic observables are the simplest whose contours remain simple closed curves on the ray‐orbital sphere. Up to SU(2) rotations and linear perturbations, the EG modes therefore complete the quadratic algebraic classification of families of structured Gaussian modes. Other choices of Poincaré paths are of course possible, but choices where F is a linear or quadratic combination of Stokes–Fradkin parameters are the only ones for which the structure of the quantised mode family is semiclassically related to the contours of F.

Other Gaussian‐related beams, such as Bessel–Gauss and Mathieu–Gauss beams, are not eigenfunctions of a natural operator related to SU(2) observables, are not eigenfunctions of the beam quality and have no well‐defined mode order, and therefore do not lie within this SU(2) hierarchy of Gaussian beams of fixed beam quality.

Although we have focused on EG modes with K balanced between the 3‐ and 1‐axes, with a transition between squared LG and squared HG modes, the calculations can easily be adapted to eigenfunctions of the operators J12−J22 and J22−J32 and beyond, by substituting the LG mode expansion with a suitable family of GG modes. These share similar features to the EG modes described here, such as the topological transition and fourfold symmetric modes which are deformations of squared GG modes, reflecting the polhodes about the rotated inertia ellipsoid in 1,2,3‐space.

Ince–Gaussian modes demonstrate the nonlinear pendulum dynamics of the Ince oscillator, corresponding to the Bose–Hubbard dimer model of condensed matter physics [26]. A similar analogy relates EG modes, through the balanced quadratic operator Kˆ, to a quadrupolar Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick model, with zero external field [37, 38]. Ref. [37] considers explicitly the case with a nonzero field term. In the language of this paper, adding J2 times a real constant to Kˆ, distorting the dihedral symmetry of the polhodes and destroying the fourfold symmetry of the pure EG modes (whilst retaining a 2‐fold symmetry). Its classical trajectories are precisely the polhodes of the Euler top, with stable and unstable axes and a separatrix [39]. Thus, IG and EG modes provide optical realisations of two of the most important integrable few‐mode many‐body models.

Separation of variables of the 3D Helmholtz equation in sphero‐conical coordinates yields Lamé functions connected with the classical Euler top [32, 40]. However, the 2D harmonic oscillator underlying paraxial Gaussian modes is only separable in Cartesian, polar and elliptic coordinate systems [8], corresponding exactly to the HG, LG and IG families. Therefore, no separable Lamé‐type Gaussian family exists, and the EG modes arise from operator algebra rather than coordinate separability.

EG modes should be straightforward to generate in the laboratory, using standard holographic methods such as computer‐controlled spatial light modulators as superpositions of LG modes. Unlike GG and IG modes [26, 41, 42], EG modes are not obviously generated as cavity modes or by the action of any natural optical element. Their inherent fourfold symmetry makes them an appropriate working eigenbasis whenever π/2‐rotation invariance is required; unlike the HGnn subfamily, EG modes form a complete basis of π/2‐symmetric beams. This might include square arrays of optical vortices (especially for odd N and κN,μ<0), stable modes of resonators with quadrupolar mirrors, and applications that require asymmetric orbital angular momentum distributions. Furthermore, their balanced quadratic structure also makes them promising for simulating LMG‐like spin models and topological transitions in tabletop optical experiments.

The EG modes highlight the possibility of new families of optical modes, motivated as eigenfunctions of operators arising in wave‐optical mechanics, beyond the separation of variables approach. Classical angular momentum systems provide a natural setting for the pseudo‐AM operators of structured Gaussian modes: various rotors and tops, mixed products such as J1J3 and higher‐order polynomial observables may yield further interesting mode families. More generally, our treatment of the EG modes is an example of a broader programme of structured light in terms of geometric quantisation.
