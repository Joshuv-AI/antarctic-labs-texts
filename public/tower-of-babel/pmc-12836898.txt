Integrating chest radiography-based TB screening and preventive treatment for household contacts in Pakistan: a pilot study
Authors: Nida Khan, Muhammad Ahmar Khan, Ahmad Ismail, Abdul Ghafoor, Alina Rashid, Farah Zafar, Razia Fatima, Bilal Afghani, Aamir Safdar, Corinne Simone Merle, Dennis Falzon, Avinash Kanchar, Christian Gunneberg, Muhammad Amir Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPakistan ranks fifth among countries with the highest tuberculosis (TB) incidence worldwide, yet only approximately 70% of TB patients receive treatment. The World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends active TB case finding and TB preventive treatment (TPT) for household contacts (HHCs) following exclusion of active TB disease. We piloted the integration of WHO-recommended chest X-ray (CXR) screening and TPT for HHCs, with data collection via PREVENT-TB app.MethodsA mixed-method pilot study was conducted in 19 healthcare facilities in Hafizabad and Nankana Sahib districts of Punjab, Pakistan, from December 2021 to September 2023. It aimed to assess the acceptability and feasibility of a CXR based TB screening among HHCs, the initiation of TPT, and the feasibility of using a mobile application to document the intervention and its completeness. Descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression were used to analyse quantitative data. Thematic analysis was used to analyse semi-structured interviews with programme staff, healthcare providers and patients. Digital and paper-based data collection approaches were compared.ResultsAmong 694 index TB patients, 2817 HHCs were identified, of whom 1421 (50%) underwent CXR and symptom screening. A total of 118 (8%) screened HHCs had TB symptoms and/or CXR abnormalities and 5 (4%) were diagnosed with TB. Of the 1416 HHCs eligible for TPT, 957 (68%) initiated TPT, and among these 903 (94%) completed their treatment. The most common reason for refusal of screening and TPT was perceived lack of need by HHCs. Digital data collection was found to satisfactorily support both implementation and monitoring.ConclusionsIntegrating CXR screening and TPT into routine primary healthcare with digital monitoring are feasible.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12378-0.Keywords: Integrated care, Household contacts of TB patients, CXR screening, TB preventive treatment, Prevent TB application

Introduction

Household contacts (HHC) of TB patients are at greater risk of TB infection and disease due to prolonged exposure. Recognising this risk, WHO strongly recommends TB screening of high-risk groups, especially household contacts [1, 2]. Providing TB preventive treatment (TPT) to HHC of pulmonary TB patients has been shown to reduce their risk of developing TB disease [3]. WHO recommends TPT to contacts of all ages after excluding TB disease [1].

Despite global commitments at the 2018 UN High-Level Meeting (UNHLM) to provide TPT to 30 million individuals by 2022, progress has been slow, with only 2.2 million household contacts (9%) receiving TPT worldwide [4]. In response to this limited progress, the 2023 UNHLM renewed commitments and set a target for 90% of eligible contacts to initiate TPT by 2027 [5].

According to Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, Pakistan ranks fifth worldwide, accounting for 6.3% of the global tuberculosis burden. In 2024, the TB incidence was estimated at 266 cases per 100,000 population, resulting in approximately 51,716 TB-related deaths. TB remains a leading cause of years of life lost in the country [6].

In Pakistan, integrated TB care is delivered by medical and allied staff working at designated Direct Observation Treatment Short-course (DOTS) clinics in all health centres and (sub)district hospitals. These publicly funded health facilities provide free-of charge basic diagnostics and treatment of TB.

In 2021, Pakistan’s National TB Programme (NTP), in collaboration with the Association for Social Development (ASD), a not-for-profit organisation based in Pakistan, initiated a research project to implement HHC TB screening strategy, as well as the implementation of TB Preventive Treatment (TPT), documented through a digital platform called PREVENT-TB. This study aimed to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and implementation of integrated CXR screening and TPT using the PREVENT-TB digital platform in Pakistan.

Methods

Study design and setting

A-mixed-method study was conducted at 19 public facilities in Hafizabad and Nankana Sahib districts, Punjab, Pakistan (Dec, 2021 to Sep, 2023).

Development of the intervention

The intervention was developed during December 2021 to August 2022, following WHO recommendations for CXR screening and TPT [2, 7]. The intervention package comprised clinical guidelines for physicians on implementing integrated chest radiography (CXR) alongside symptom-based screening for tuberculosis (TB), as well as the provision of tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT), using the PREVENT-TB platform to record data related to HHC screening and TPT [8]. Additionally, the research activity provided supporting materials such as CXR films for analogue X-ray machines, cost for printing digital X-rays, and brochures outlining screening procedures and TPT guidelines for health facilities. WHO assisted with the adaptation of the application by incorporating options to record data on treatment adherence and adverse events, adding alerts to prompt TB screening and treatment follow-up, and customisation of the dashboard for programme monitoring and response.

Conduct of the intervention and evaluation

The intervention was piloted in all 19 healthcare facilities, comprising 14 rural health centers (RHC), 3 tehsil headquarters and 2 district health quarters hospitals in the districts of Hafizabad and Nankana Sahib, Punjab province. The HHCs were registered from August 18th, 2022, till February 20th, 2023.

Two district level training events i.e., one in each district, were conducted through facilitation of the respective province and district level programme staff. At least one doctor and one allied staff, from each of the 19 public facilities, received one-day training. The training covered the whole set of tasks for screening and TPT care including use of PREVENT-TB App. The app was installed on the digital tablet devices that were being used by the healthcare staff at the respective facilities (already provided by NTP) and allowed both real-time data entry, as well as offline recording whenever internet was not available.

The material inputs at 19 facilities were managed mainly through mobilising the programme and district resources. These included conventional, analogue X-ray machines located at each RHC; digital X-ray equipment at each hospital supported by human interpretation of X-ray images (there was no access to computer-aided detection (CAD) software), sputum smear testing, Gene Xpert testing which was only available at one tertiary and one secondary level health facility - and medications for TPT regimens. These programme and district resources were supplemented by project inputs e.g., CXR films, digital tablets, the Prevent TB App., print materials, paper registers, and field staff support for each facility.

Intervention outline

During the study period, consecutive sputum smear-positive pulmonary TB patients were enrolled in the study and asked to list their HHCs. In terms of inclusion criteria, a HHC was defined as someone who resides in the same dwelling as the TB index patient. HHC already on TB treatment were excluded. Facility staff recorded the contact details in a paper screening register as well as on the Prevent TB app. The data entered in the app were cross-validated against paper registers.

TB patients were counselled about the important benefits of the intervention and asked to bring their HHCs for screening at the respective facility. CXR and symptom screening were offered free of charge to each HHC that visited the facility. HHCs were considered Presumptive TB cases if they had TB-suggestive symptoms or chest X-ray abnormalities suggestive of TB, or both. Adolescent or adult contact who was a presumptive TB case was offered smear examination at the respective facility, whereas a child contact under 5 was referred to the district headquarters hospital for further assessment. HHCs diagnosed with TB were offered treatment, as per NTP guidelines.

The HHCs in whom screening and/or further assessment excluded TB disease were offered a TPT regimen as recommended by the NTP. For those under 18 years of age, consent was obtained from parent/legal guardian. The verbal consent was recorded by the public health facility staff on routine record registers. Individuals aged 5 years or over were administered three months of once-weekly isoniazid plus rifapentine (3HP); and children under 5 years of age were prescribed six months of daily isoniazid (6H).

Everyone receiving TPT was required to visit the respective facility on monthly basis. The facility staff reviewed the adherence, by verbally asking about the number of days the drugs were taken during the month, and assessed for the adverse events (e.g., hepatic toxicity, peripheral neuropathy, and gastric upsets). If an individual did not complete 12 doses of the 3HP regimen within 16 weeks of beginning treatment or 182 doses of the 6 H regimen within 8 months of starting treatment, it was considered an interruption in TPT.

Verbal consent was obtained from all participants in this study. For participants under 18 years of age, consent was provided by a parent or legal guardian. The verbal consent was documented by public health facility staff in routine record registers. Those who did not consent to participate in study were not enrolled in the study, but the reason of refusal was recorded.

Process evaluation

A mixed method process evaluation was done to assess the acceptability and feasibility of TB screening among HHC, initiation of TPT, and the feasibility of using PREVENT-TB application for documenting the intervention and its completeness. The quantitative data regarding the records of HHCs were extracted from PREVENT-TB application, project records, telephonic surveys with index TB patients, CXR re-reading activity for verification.

The telephonic surveys were conducted with:

i)15% of randomly selected index patients to validate the listed number of household contacts; also, explore reasons for discrepancy in the listed number of HHCs.

ii)Index patients whose one or more HHCs refused CXR screening or TPT to explore the reasons for refusals.

Index patients whose one or more HHCs refused CXR screening or TPT to explore the reasons for refusals.

A sample of 20% of CXR was re-read by an expert radiologist. This sample included all those CXRs initially labelled as “Abnormality detected” (i.e., 12), and a random sample of 273 CXRs initially labelled as “Not Abnormality Detected”. The radiologist was kept blind to the original reported findings. The two readings were compared, and the discrepancy between BMU doctor and radiologist was analysed.

The qualitative data were collected using in-depth interviews (i.e., tailor-made interview guides) in national language (i.e., Urdu) with various stakeholders, after obtaining verbal informed consent (see Supplementary Files for the English version). A total of 12 interviews were conducted with index patients of HHCs. The sample selection was done to interview index patients of HHCs (adults and children) who completed CXR screening and TPT both, who were eligible but refused TPT, as well as who refused CXR screening. The qualitative interviews were conducted with index patients, so they could offer a broader perspective of various types of HHCs (i.e., old age, women, children, married, educated, employed, etc.) by reflecting on their varied insights within the household. Furthermore, 4 interviews were conducted with basic management unit (BMU) staff i.e., two staff members from each district. Two interviews with district-level TB programme staff (one from each district) and 2 field officers involved in implementation (one from each district) were also conducted.

Data analysis

The quantitative data taken from PREVENT-TB app and telephonic survey were analysed using SPSS Ver.25.0.

The proportion of HHCs listed, CXR screened, eligible for TPT, registered for TPT, eligible for further TB testing and registered as confirmed TB patient were analysed. The distribution of household contacts (HHCs) across facilities was analysed to determine relative contribution by each facility.

Associations between selected demographic characteristics (i.e., age, gender, years of schooling and marital status) and likelihood of screening, treatment initiation and treatment completion were explored using binary logistic regression and expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aOR). The regression models included gender of the index patient, gender and age group of HHC, years of schooling and symptomatic status for the relevant outcomes. Standard errors were not adjusted for clustering by facility due to the exploratory nature of the analyses. A p-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant.

For the telephonic survey data, refusal reasons for screening and TPT were investigated through open-ended questions. The responses were systematically coded and categorised into main themes, with the final categories reported in terms of frequencies and percentages.

Qualitative data from interviews were transcribed and analysed using thematic analysis.

Results

Cascade of care

A total 694 index TB patients listed a total 2817 household contacts, half (1421; 50%) of whom underwent CXR and symptom-based TB screening. Among those screened, 118 (8%) were identified as presumptive TB cases and tested, with 5 (0.3%) diagnosed with TB. Of the 1416 contacts eligible for TPT, 957 (68%) initiated TPT, and treatment completion achieved in 903 (94%) of those who started. Index patients

A total of 694 index TB patients (sputum smear-positive) from all 19 participating BMUs were included in the study. The mean age of index patients was 43 years (range:18–89); and 55% of them were men.

These 694 index patients listed a total of 2817 HHCs, with a mean of 4 HHCs per patient (interquartile range [IQR]: 3–5; median: 4.0). Only 97 index patients (i.e., 14%) listed any < 5-year-old HHCs.

Telephonic surveys were conducted with 106 index patients (15%) to verify discrepancies in the listing. Among them, 3 index patients and their family had migrated, 76 had no discrepancies, and 27 had discrepancies between the reported and actual number of HHCs. On average, 4 HHCs were missing from the list in these 27 cases, with discrepancies ranging from 1 to 7 HHCs.

Some of the family members no longer living with the index patient were listed erroneously because of misunderstandings on the definition of HHCs.

When I made follow-up calls, I came to know that some of the listed HHCs were not available because they had moved out of the house. (field officer)

The verification of HHCs listing found that only about one-fourth (i.e., 24 out of 106) of the surveyed index patients had incomplete listing of HHCs, missing a total of 97 HHCs.

We felt that some patients were reluctant to list their HHCs especially younger women and children. (Allied provider)

Many of the incomplete listings of household members were due to staff miscommunication.

It was not clear from the health staff instructions; so, I listed only some of my HHCs. (Male patient, District Hafizabad)

TB screening in household contacts

Among 694 index patients, 38% (i.e., 266) got all their HHCs screened; 30% (i.e., 209) index patients got at least one of their HHCs screened, whereas 32% (i.e., 219) got none of their HHCs screened.

Among the 2817 listed HHCs, around 50% underwent CXR-symptom screening; whereas the remaining half either refused (28%) or were pre-screening lost to follow up non-adherent/ pending (22%).

The index patient’s gender and HHCs’ age were associated with HHC screening. The likelihood of HHC getting screened was higher if index patient was male or HHC is older (Table 1). As compared to adults the likelihood of HHCs getting screened was nearly half if aged 12 to 17 years and one-third if aged less than 11 years.

Out of the 1421 screened HHCs, around 8% (118) were reported with TB suggestive symptoms and/or CXR abnormality; and the remaining 92% (i.e., 1303) had no symptom or CXR abnormality. Frequently reported symptoms were cough, fever, and weight loss. Only 12 of the symptomatic (i.e., 10.16%) were reported to have CXR abnormality. All 118 HHCs with a symptom or CXR abnormality got tested (GeneXpert: 24; and direct microscopy of sputum smear: 94); and 5 (4%) of these HHCs were diagnosed with TB (Fig. 1). The co-prevalence of TB in contacts was thus about 0.3%. One adult male HHC who was asymptomatic and had no CXR abnormalities at the time of initial screening developed symptoms after approximately one month of TPT and was diagnosed with TB.

Validity of CXR reading

It was found that less than 1% of HHCs had an abnormality on CXR. The expert re-reading of CXRs found that there was a 15% chance of technician incorrectly conducting CXR due to improper positioning or limited skills leading to poorer CXR quality (i.e., 36 of 285 readings were not readable). There was a 17% chance of BMU staff missing the CXR abnormality (i.e., 41 of readable 237 CXRs had missed an abnormality). Further analysis showed that the chances of missing CXR abnormality were slightly higher among asymptomatic individuals i.e., 19% (30 out 159 asymptomatic) as compared to symptomatic individuals i.e., 14% (11 out of 78 symptomatic). If the same proportion of mis-read was to be assumed for all 1409 CXRs (with no abnormality detected), then around 240 CXR with abnormality are estimated to have been misread as normal. This highlights a substantial operational gap in CXR interpretation within routine programmatic settings.

Among 796 HHCs who refused screening, the reasons for refusal were explored for 653 HHCs. The most common reasons for screening refusal included: healthy HHCs not perceiving the need, less-friendly working hours of RHCs i.e., available only during the working hours of either the HHC or the accompanying person, and perceived health risk of X-rays exposure (Fig. 2).

Barriers to screening uptake

The patient quotes from qualitative interviews presented below further elaborate their experiences:

No one in my family had cough or any other complaint. Therefore, we did not feel the need to expose family members to X-rays. (Male index patient, District Nankana Sahib)

The timing of RHC does not suit most of my household members as children are in school at that time and male members are at work. (Female index patient, District Hafizabad)

The health staff explained well to me the importance of HHC screening. However, I faced difficulty in communicating these messages to my family members in the same manner and answering their queries. (female index patient, District Nankana Sahib)

The main health staff reported reasons for missed screening included inadequate awareness, stigma, and time constraints. Health staff also reported occasional CXR interruption due to non-functioning machines and unavailability of technician.

Among the 413 TPT refusals, the most commonly reported reasons were; TPT deemed not to be necessary because of absence of symptoms and/or TB disease (60%; n = 248;), reluctance to take TPT because of other health conditions (32%; n = 131) and pregnancy (3%; n = 13), and formulation not friendly for child ingestion (5%; n = 21).

Barriers to TPT uptake

Some of the challenges faced by patients with children less than 5 years of age were expressed as:

After treatment of elders in the household was completed, it seemed difficult to make special visit for just one (child) HHC (male index patient, District Nankana Sahib)

My child was finding it difficult to swallow the medicines which further discouraged me to continue my child’s treatment

Children said that medicines are bitter. Children should get syrups instead of medicines (female index patient, District Hafizabad)

Some providers found that counselling the patients about the importance of TPT for HHCs by only delivering the written messages was insufficient and could be supplemented by a more interactive tool for counselling, such as a digital mobile app with voice recording for users with low literacy levels.

Some patients felt that despite information being provided by the healthcare staff, some easily accessible and comprehensible source of knowledge about the need and safety of TPT could play an important role in convincing HHCs especially those of female TB index patients. Prevent TB application

To ensure data completeness and accuracy, Prevent TB data were regularly cross-validated with hard copy HHC screening and tuberculosis preventive treatment registers available at each facility. The data on Prevent TB application was 99% complete whereas 91% accurate when cross verified from hardcopy registers.

The healthcare staff found the Prevent TB digital application user friendly and easy to comprehend. The data variables captured by Prevent TB app were aligned with those in the hardcopy programme registers.

Findings from healthcare providers interviews

Usefulness of digital alerts

The application had an alert feature that notified healthcare staff of delays in household contact screening or TPT initiation and follow-up. This function was reported by healthcare staff to be extremely helpful in managing their workload. The healthcare staff claimed that the alerts in the app played a huge role in improving the screening and treatment outcomes as compared to their usual way of working.

Every day we see the alerts to check how many HHCs are pending for screening and have their treatment follow-up due. The alerts helped us to contact the patient right away and remind them that their visit at BMU was due instead of referring back to registers to find pending HHCs. (Allied staff, District Hafizabad)

Offline data entry mechanism to address internet connectivity issues

The feature in the app that allowed offline data entry enabled healthcare providers to do real-time data entry despite irregular internet availability at the facilities.

The offline feature enabled us to update data even at times when there was no internet connection. When internet connection became available again, we could sync all the data in no-time, hence reducing our dependability on internet during the entire day.” (Field officer, District Hafizabad).

Use of dashboard for district-level monitoring

The programme and district level dashboards of the app offered a comprehensive tool to the programme and district level managers to monitor the screening and TPT performance of their respective province and district.

I found district-wise monitoring of TPT implementation very easy through the dashboard. The real-time updated numbers of screening and TPT were available. I could see the facility wise performance too. It takes time to generate a report from hard copy registers. If integrated with EMR, the app has potential to be expanded to other districts in Punjab (District Health officer, Hafizabad).

Discussion

We found integrating CXR screening and TPT of HHCs in routine primary healthcare setting in Pakistan is feasible. Screening uptake was more likely among adult and male HHC, while TPT initiation was more common among those under 17 years or who were symptomatic but found to be disease free. High completion of TPT was observed in shorter rifapentine-based regimens, whereas the lower completion among children on 6 H highlights the need to expand access to shorter, child-friendly regimens. Digital data collection via mobile phone was feasible and reliable in terms of completeness, presenting important advantages over paper recording.

Our coverage of CXR screening in HHC was comparable to a recent experience in Chennai, India [9]. However, CXR costs were covered by project resources. Under routine programmatic conditions, patients are required to pay out-of-pocket for their CXRs. This may significantly reduce screening uptake in standard care settings.

TPT completion (i.e., 94%) rates in our study were higher as compared to those reported in global reviews [10]. as well as WHO estimate of 84% among HHCs [6]. Several factors have been previously associated with higher TPT completion rates including low medication and transportation costs, shorter treatment regimen, directly observed therapy by either a healthcare worker or a community supporter, and initiating therapy at a rural health facility [11]. In our study, the higher completion rate could be attributed to use of shorter regimens among adults, as reflected in their comparatively higher completion rates. Additionally, use of Prevent TB app to systematically generate reminders for facility staff supported patient tracking leading to reduced loss to follow-up.

We found a lack of clarity in the definition of HHC used in primary care services which highlights the need for clear communication by healthcare provider and enhanced patient education. Achieving uniformity of definition of HHC was also found to be challenging in other similar settings in Karnataka, India [12]. The listing of HHCs in our study showed that the average household size is about 5 persons (i.e., an index patient and 4 household members), which is less than the reported national average of about 6.6 persons per household [13]. Similarly, the proportion of children < 5 years in the listed HHCs was less than 5%, which is notably lower than the national average of 15% [13]. These discrepancies highlight selective or incomplete enumeration of HHCs.

In our project, almost half of the listed HHCs got screened with both symptoms and CXR. This was higher than in other studies in LMIC countries, such as India, Africa and Ethiopia, ranging from 24% to 47% [14–16]. Inconvenient facility opening hours and concerns about the use of CXR in children were found to be common reasons for refusal to screen in our study, as elsewhere [9]. The screening uptake was especially low for children under 5 years old, which was not comparable to other studies conducted in Pakistan that found a high proportion of screening rates in children [17]. Similarly, a study in Ethiopia found the HHCs > 15 years of age were twice as likely to get screened. The inadequate screening of children HHCs was also found in other similar settings such in India where only 30% of the HHCs were counselled adequately on screening of children [14]. The prioritisation of children in the screening process was found to be a necessary step to achieve desirable results [18].

Our findings indicate suboptimal CXR performance and interpretation at primary healthcare level. A similar challenge has been reported in Zambia, where limited capacity for both CXR conduction and interpretation affected diagnosis [19]. The misclassification observed in our study may also have influenced the estimates of TB yield being an underestimate, as a proportion of true TB cases may have been missed due to poor image quality or errors in interpretation. This is particularly relevant for children, in whom the reading of CXR is important, and the detection of TB is often more challenging [20, 21]. Although CXR reading can now be done through computer-aided detection (CAD) software [22], it is important to uphold the quality of human interpretation of CXR through extensive training of staff. Moreover, appropriate operation of X-ray machines is required regardless of how the CXR will be read, and therefore rigorous training of X-ray technicians on the correct positioning of patient for optimal imaging is also needed.

The co-prevalence of TB among HHCs was approximately ten times lower than estimates from meta-analyses [23] suggesting a low sensitivity of the screening tools used, given that half those eligible did not receive a CXR. Our quality control on CXR suggests that the number of HHC screening positive could have been up to three times higher if the readings had been performed by better-trained personnel or supported by computer-aided detection (CAD) technology. Moreover, a substantial proportion of people screened positive were only tested with direct microscopy and access to more accurate molecular testing was limited explaining lower proportion of HHCs being screened positive.

The reasonably high TPT initiation and high treatment completion rates in adults are consistent with other studies in the region [12]. In a similar study in Karachi, Pakistan, high treatment initiation percentage of HHCs are reported (approx. 93%) [24]. However, our study found relatively low rates of completion in children under 5 years of age, in the extended period (i.e., after adult HHCs complete their TPT) which was also reported in a similar study in Karachi which also attributed failure to complete treatment to the extended duration required for treatment [25]. This may also be associated with the use of 6 months of daily isoniazid in this age group. TPT adherence could be further improved through provision of shorter rifapentine-based regimens and child-friendly dispersible and palatable formulations.

The digital application was found to be very helpful in the process of TPT administration. A study in Malawi found that the electronic management of records for people with TB/HIV improved patient care and provider decision-making [26]. EMR practice in TB care has up to now focused more on people with TB disease and operated using desktop platforms. As compared to traditional EMR, the mobile app provided clear advantages including its portability, its interactive features, dashboard analytics, the possibility to enter data offline, the options for patient care alerts on screening and TPT care delays, and the ability to quickly identify follow-up care challenges such as non-adherence and adverse drug reactions.

This study had several limitations. First, the lack of a control group undergoing regular passive screening in the study restricts the ability to compare results directly. Second, the interviews to understand refusal reasons for screening and TPT uptake were conducted almost 2 months after completion of pilot, introducing possibility of recall bias. Third, CXR coverage was relatively low, limiting the possibility of finding more people with TB. Finally, no testing for TB infection was carried out among HHC aged 5 years and more, and this may have lowered the motivation to start and complete TPT. The study also had several strengths. Firstly, it was conducted within the routine health care setting in Punjab province, Pakistan. Secondly, the use of a mixed-methods design provided better insights into the reasons for refusals on screening and treatment, identified gaps in TPT care cascades, and highlighted implementation challenges and enablers from both patients and providers.

In conclusion, this study highlights several key findings relevant to the expansion of TB preventive services in Pakistan. CXR screening for individuals at risk is feasible and likely to be accepted when offered free of charge. However, the sensitivity of CXR interpretation was found to be suboptimal, indicating a need for enhanced training of personnel and broader use of CAD. Providing culturally appropriate information may improve uptake of screening and initiation of TPT, particularly in households where the index patient is female. TPT initiation and completion rates could be further improved by offering shorter, child-friendly treatment regimen more widely. Finally, digital data collection was found to support community-based implementation and enabled programme managers to better identify challenges and respond in a timely manner.
